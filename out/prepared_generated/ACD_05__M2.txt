The rain in London did not fall so much as it was pressed down upon us by a low, leaden ceiling of cloud, saturating the very stones of the dockside until the air itself tasted of brine and rust. I stood beside the office of Mr. Silas Thorne, a retired Captain of the Merchant Navy, who was pacing the narrow room with the restless energy of a man whose anchor has dragged.

“He is not dead, Mr. Thorne,” I said, holding my hand out to catch the drop of water that had leaked through the cracked sash. “If he were, you would have received a telegram from the Coroner or a note from the Salvation Army. The silence is the anomaly. The silence implies a suspension of time, not a cessation of life.”

The Captain stopped. He was a lean, hard man, his face the color of old mahogany, his eyes still carrying the sharp, assessing gaze of one who had navigated by stars in a black sky. “My son, Arthur, went to the depot of Mr. Harkness three weeks ago. He spoke of wages paid weekly, in coin. He said the work was simple: counting crates, moving boxes. But he has not come home. He has not sent a letter. He has not even sent a word through the servant. I have searched the hospitals, the poorhouses, the jails. There is no Arthur Harkness. There is no Arthur Thorne. He has been swallowed by the earth.”

“Or by the ledger,” I replied, settling my hat on my knee. “Let us examine the facts, Captain. You state that the owner, Mr. Harkness, pays in cash and employs no clerk. This is unusual. A business of any magnitude requires an audit trail. If there is no clerk, there is no record. If there is no record, there is no accountability. And you mention the weight of the crates. You say they arrive lighter than they depart.”

The Captain leaned forward, his elbows resting on the scarred desk. “I am a sailor, Mr. Holmes. I know weight. I know buoyancy. I have weighed coal, iron, and timber. I have not seen crates become lighter in transit unless the contents are removed. If the crates arrive full and leave light, something has been taken out. If they arrive light and leave full, something has been added. But Harkness’s men swear the crates are sealed upon arrival and sealed again upon departure. They are never opened. And yet, the scales do not lie. The difference is slight—perhaps a pound or two per crate—but over thousands of crates, it is a fortune.”

“Or a crime,” I mused, striking a match against the heel of my boot. “A slight discrepancy in weight is often the hallmark of a sophisticated theft. But who is stealing from the crates? And why does the owner pay in cash to men who cannot be traced by a ledger? It suggests a desire to avoid the taxman, or perhaps the police. Let us go to the docks. The fog is thick, but it will suit us. We shall need a guide who knows the labyrinth of the wharves.”

We descended into the street, where the gas lamps struggled against the gloom. The air was thick with the smell of tar, rotting kelp, and the metallic tang of the Thames. We hailed a cab, which rattled along the cobblestones until the driver refused to go further, citing the mud and the likelihood of a carriage wheel becoming stuck in the mire. We alighted and walked the last stretch on foot, our boots squelching in the sludge.

The warehouse stood at the end of a blind alley, a structure of blackened brick that seemed to absorb the little light that remained. It was situated directly on the water’s edge, a jetty extending into the brown, sluggish current. The name HARKNESS & SONS was painted in peeling white letters above the main doors, but the ‘Sons’ part was faded, suggesting the business was older than the current proprietor.

A man stood by the door, a portly figure in a oilskin coat, holding a lantern. He looked at us with the wary suspicion of a dog hearing a stranger’s footstep.

“Closed,” he grunted. “No business after six.”

“I am not here for business, my good man,” I said, my voice calm and authoritative. “I am here to see Mr. Harkness. I represent a client who is concerned for the welfare of his son, Arthur Thorne.”

The man’s eyes flickered. He glanced at the Captain, then back at me. “No one by that name works here.”

“Perhaps he is not on the payroll,” I suggested. “But he was seen entering this facility three weeks ago. I trust that is a matter of public record, or at least of local knowledge.”

The man shifted his weight. The lantern swung gently, casting long, distorted shadows on the wet ground. “You’ll have to see the boss. But he doesn’t like visitors. And he doesn’t like questions.”

“We do not ask questions,” I said. “We observe. There is a difference.”

The man unlocked the door with a heavy iron key that clattered in the silence. Inside, the air was cool and dry, smelling faintly of pine resin and something else—something sharp and chemical, like the scent of a newly opened bottle of ether. The floor was concrete, stained with oil and dark moisture. In the center of the vast hall stood a series of wooden crates, stacked in neat rows. They were uniform in size, reinforced with metal bands, and bore no markings save a simple symbol: an anchor crossed with a key.

“Where is the boy?” I asked, my eyes scanning the room.

The guard shrugged. “I don’t know no such boy.”

I walked toward the nearest crate. It was solid oak, the grain tight and dark. I placed my hand on the wood. It was warm. Not the ambient warmth of the room, but a distinct, internal heat. I frowned. “These crates are warm.”
