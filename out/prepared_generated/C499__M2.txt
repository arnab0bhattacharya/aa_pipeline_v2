The carriage wheel had shattered on a gravel ridge three miles from the town of Ironbridge, leaving the *Theatrical Company of St. Jude* stranded in the mud of a Tuesday afternoon. The sound of the axle snapping had been a sharp, violent crack, like a bone giving way, and the horses had reared in panic before settling into a confused, snorting stillness. For Elias Thorne, the company’s manager and a man who usually thought in iambic pentameter, the situation was a tragedy without a prologue. He stood by the broken wagon, his boots caked in the grey sludge of the valley, and looked up at the town looming on the hillside. It was not a place of beauty. Ironbridge was a monolith of soot and brick, dominated by the blackened chimneys of the Vane Textile Mill, which belched a thick, oily smoke that turned the sky the color of a bruise.

To fix the axle, they needed a blacksmith. To pay the blacksmith, they needed coin. To get coin, they needed an audience.

Elias surveyed the company. There were twelve of them, huddled under a torn canvas tarp, shivering in the damp chill. There was Marta, who played the ingénues; young Julian, who did the acrobatics and the minor roles; and old Silas, who handled the props and the stage machinery. They looked at Elias with the weary resignation of men who knew that a broken wheel in a town with no theaters was a death sentence. But Elias saw something else. He saw the mill. He saw the rows of identical stone houses climbing the hill, the windows dark with coal dust, the people moving in stiff, synchronized lines toward the shift changes. He saw a population trapped in a rhythm as mechanical as the looms inside the mill.

"We have a play," Elias said, his voice cutting through the wind. "A play about a man who owns the air his workers breathe."

Marta looked up, her face pale beneath her bonnet. "You mean *The Iron King*?"

Elias nodded. "It’s short. It’s furious. And it fits the mood."

They worked through the night, the only light coming from a single tallow candle in the back of the wagon. They hammered the stage flat against the side of the carriage, using the broken axle as a prop for the set. Silas carved a crude crown from a piece of driftwood. By dawn, the carriage was a theater, the canvas peeled back to reveal a painted backdrop of a factory, the gears turning in a grotesque, eternal loop.

They set up their marquee in the market square, a stretch of cobblestones that smelled of horse manure and damp wool. Elias pinned a handbill to the town hall door. *The Iron King. A Play in One Act. Admission: Sixpence. Or a loaf of bread.*

The response was immediate, but it was not what Elias had hoped. The local magistrate, a man named Galloway, rode past on a black horse and stopped. He looked at the marquee, then at Elias, who was struggling to hoist the "Enter" sign.

"What is this, Thorne?" Galloway asked, his voice tight.

"A performance, sir. A bit of levity for the folks," Elias said, keeping his head down.

Galloway’s eyes narrowed. "There is no levity here. There is work. The mills run twenty-four hours. If your little show disturbs the peace, or causes a gathering that looks like a riot, I will have you arrested for vagrancy." He rode off, his spurs jingling like a warning bell.

But the real enemy was not Galloway. It was Silas Vane.

Vane owned the mill. He owned the houses. He owned the bakery that supplied the bread for the admission. He was a man who saw human beings as cogs, valuable only when they turned. When he heard that a traveling troupe had set up in the square, he did not send a messenger. He sent his foreman, a scarred man named Ketch, to the mill gates.

"Hands," Ketch bellowed, his voice amplified by the acoustics of the stone archway. "No one leaves the yard until the shift is done. No one attends the show. If you are seen in the market square, you will not be seen in the mill tomorrow. You will be seen in the turnpike."

It was a blunt instrument, but it was effective. The workers were afraid. Fear was the primary currency of Ironbridge, and Vane held the mint.

By noon, the square was empty. The wind whipped the empty seats of the canvas chairs. Elias felt a cold knot of despair in his stomach. They would have to move on, walking the miles to the next town, their money spent on the blacksmith’s fee, which they still hadn’t paid. They were trapped by poverty and by power.

Then, a single person walked into the square.

It was a woman. She was small, wrapped in a shawl that was too thin for the weather, and her hands were stained with the indigo dye of the looms. She walked with a limp, a common affliction among the weavers who stood for twelve hours. She stopped at the front of the stage, where Elias was sitting on a crate, staring at his shoes.

"Is it true?" she asked. Her voice was quiet, but it carried.

"True?" Elias asked.

"That the play is about him," she said. "About Vane."

Elias looked up. "It is a fictional character. But... yes. He is based on men like that."

The woman nodded slowly. She reached into her pocket and pulled out a sixpence. She placed it on the ground in front of Elias. "I have been working in that mill for ten years. My fingers are always cold. My back hurts when I sleep. I don’t have the time. I don’t have the strength. But I have this."

Elias picked up the coin. It felt heavy. "Are you the only one?"
