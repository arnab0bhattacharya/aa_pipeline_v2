The first thing that struck me about the island of Maroa was the sheer, unrelenting greenness of it. It was a green that seemed to have weight and substance, a dense, humid blanket of vegetation that muffled sound and pressed in upon one. As Resident Commissioner, a post I had accepted with visions of tropical languour and easy authority, I found it oppressive. My predecessor, a man named Prendergast, had lasted only two years before taking a nervous turn and shipping himself back to Suva. I now began to understand why.

The people were quiet, too. Not sullen, as I had seen in the Solomons, but possessed of a profound and unnerving placidity. They moved through the village with a slow, deliberate grace, their faces unlined and their eyes holding a strange, placid wisdom. It was an old man, Aroka, the headman, who first gave me the clue. He had the smooth skin and firm step of a man of forty, yet the council register, a water-stained ledger left by a missionary, recorded his birth some eighty years prior. I put it down to the exaggeration of a damp climate and poor record-keeping.

The truth, however, has a way of making itself known. It began with a fever. A week after my arrival, I was struck down with a malarial complaint of the most violent sort. For three days I burned in my cot, my mind a vortex of delirious fancies. I remember the faces of the islanders peering at me, their expressions serene and untroubled, and thinking they were angels of death come to collect me. Then came Aroka, with a small clay pot. The substance within was a thick, dark-green paste, smelling of crushed leaves and something else, something ancient and loamy, like the forest floor after a long rain. They forced a little of it between my lips. The effect was not gentle. It was a cold fire that scoured through my veins, and I felt a wrenching in my very bones. When I awoke, the fever was gone. But that was not all. The lines that had begun to etch themselves around my eyes were smoothed away. A persistent ache in my lower back, a souvenir of a polo injury in England, had vanished entirely. I felt, in a word, rejuvenated.

It was Aroka who explained it, in his simple, direct way. The plant, the *vina-lae*, or ‘life-leaf’, did not merely cure; it restored. It was not a medicine for the sick, but a sustenance for the well. They used it sparingly, a tiny dose every decade or so, to arrest the decay of the body. They did not seek to live forever, he told me, merely to live well for the time they were given. The secret was the island’s alone, a covenant with the forest that gave them the leaf. To speak of it to the outside world, he said, with a sudden, chilling gravity in his voice, would be to break that covenant and bring a ruin upon them all.

I, of course, did not heed his warning. A man in my position, with a discovery of this magnitude, has duties that transcend local superstition. I wrote to the Colonial Office, but it was my private letter to my uncle, a director at the Consolidated Mercantile & Colonial Company in London, that set the true machinery in motion. Six months later, the steamship *Cormorant* arrived, and with it, Mr. Silas Blackwood.

Blackwood was a man of the new century, all sharp angles and impatience. He wore a city suit of grey worsted that seemed to wilt in the humidity, and he looked upon the magnificent harbour of Maroa as if it were merely a poorly organised shipping depot. He did not care for the people, or the island’s strange harmony. He cared only for the formula.

“Vitality, Commissioner! Think of it!” he said, pacing the verandah of my bungalow, his voice cutting through the evening chorus of insects. “We’ll call it ‘Aethelgard’s Elixir’. The market is limitless. The aging aristocracy, the weary industrialist, the invalid with more money than hope! We’ll extract it, refine it, patent the process. You’ll be made, Hartnell. A baronetcy, at the very least.”

His talk was of yield per acre, of distillation processes and shipping logistics. He saw the *vina-lae* not as a sacred trust, but as a raw material. When I tried to explain Aroka’s warning, he laughed, a short, sharp bark.

“Covenant? Good God, man, don’t be a fool. These people are wards of the Crown. Their resources are, by extension, the Crown’s resources. And the Crown, in its wisdom, has licensed their exploitation to us. It is a matter of progress. Of economics.”

The decision fell to me. I was His Majesty’s representative, bound by duty and contract to the interests of the Consolidated Company. My career, my pension, my entire future back in England, hinged on my cooperation. Yet, I had looked into the clear, ancient eyes of Aroka. I had seen the children playing in the shallows, their lives stretching before them not as a frantic race against decay, but as a long, calm river. To unleash Blackwood and his chemists upon this place would be an act of profound and unforgivable vandalism. It would be like setting fire to the Library of Alexandria to boil a kettle.

The conflict raged within me for a week. I walked the jungle paths at night, the air thick with the scent of night-blooming flowers, and felt the pull of two worlds. One was the world of London, of clubs and committees, of honour and advancement, a world I understood. The other was this quiet, green world, which I was only just beginning to comprehend, and whose peace I was now sworn to shatter.
