It was a diagnosis delivered with the practised solemnity of a man who had never in his life been obliged to apologise for a headache, and young Lord Silverton received it with the perfect composure of one who had always believed that the Last Trump would sound at a decent hour and in a fashionable quarter. Dr. Abercrombie, a Scotchman of the old school, who regarded the human body as a regrettable piece of machinery that ought never to have been invented, informed him, in the flat accents of professional doom, that his heart was so fatigued by pleasure that it could not reasonably be expected to beat beyond the spring of the following year. The young man heard him out with the politeness of a host listening to a dull guest, and then, when the door had closed, he went to the window and looked down upon the grey London afternoon with an interest he had never before felt in it. The doctors, he reflected, had been wrong about everything else in his life — they had told him that champagne would ruin his digestion, and it had not; that the ballet would ruin his morals, and he had never noticed the ruin — but this, he perceived, was a different order of error. He had a year. It was a singularly awkward length of time. It was too short to be a career and too long to be a spree.

He resolved, that very evening, to spend it in doing good.

The resolution was made over a glass of a very excellent brandy, which he drank with a certain melancholy satisfaction, as a man might drink a toast to his own memory. He would become a benefactor. He would seek out the poor, the sick, the fallen, and he would lift them up; and the world, which had always found him amusing, would be compelled at last to find him admirable. The thought pleased him; it had the neatness of a well-turned epigram. Virtue, he had always suspected, was merely the last refuge of the man who had exhausted vice, and he was charmed to discover that the proverb, like most proverbs, was true, and that he was about to become its illustration.

His first attempt was made the following morning, upon a crossing-sweeper in the neighbourhood of Belgrave Square, a small and grimy child whose professional equipment consisted of a broom worn to a stump and a face that had never known soap. Lord Silverton approached him with the air of a man distributing alms and wisdom in equal measure, and pressed a sovereign into his hand. The child looked at the coin, then at his benefactor, then at the coin again, and, with the instinct of his class for detecting a counterfeit of any kind, he asked, in a voice of great suspicion, what the gentleman wanted.

‘I want nothing, my good boy,’ said Lord Silverton, much touched by the pathos of the situation. ‘I merely wish to make you happy.’

The child considered this for a moment, and then, with a suddenness that was almost miraculous, he burst into tears, and, flinging the sovereign into the gutter, he fled down the street, howling that a cove had been at him and would have his life.

Lord Silverton stood for some time upon the pavement, watching the sovereign roll into the drain, and he felt the first faint chill of a terrible discovery. Good deeds, it appeared, were not so simple as they looked. He had always supposed that the poor were grateful; it was, indeed, the one thing that had reconciled him to their existence. But this child had been ungrateful, and, what was worse, he had been ungrateful in the most inconvenient manner possible.

He persevered, however, with the courage of a man who has staked his reputation upon a system. He subscribed to charities with a lavish hand, and found that the secretaries of charities wrote him letters of thanks so beautifully phrased that he suspected them of having been written by the same man, and that they were, moreover, always accompanied by a fresh appeal. He visited a hospital, and distributed flowers and grapes among the patients, and discovered that the grapes were taken from him with a gratitude that was almost avaricious, and that the flowers were placed in water by the nurses with an indifference that was positively insulting. He attempted to reform a fallen woman, and found her so much more cheerful than himself, and so entirely unrepentant, that he was obliged to give her money to go to the music-halls, which she did with a readiness that suggested she had been waiting for nothing else. He called upon a poor curate, and offered to endow a mission, and the curate, a pale young man with a passion for the poor that was almost a vice, asked him whether he had considered the claims of the Church in the East, and, before he could answer, produced a subscription-list.

The winter he spent at the Villa of the Golden Pheasant, at Nice, where he had taken a house for the season, and where he found that the practice of virtue among the English colony consisted chiefly in the circulation of scandal and the avoidance of the Casino. He was, by this time, a good deal exhausted by his benevolence, and he found the society of the Riviera singularly restful, because it was the only society in Europe in which no one expected him to be anything but amusing. The Countess of Marlow, a widow of magnificent presence and uncertain age, informed him one evening that she had heard of his good works, and that she thought them a great mistake.

‘My dear Lord Silverton,’ she said, ‘you have been wasting your time. Good works are the last resource of the man who has no conversation, and you have always had conversation. Why should you wish to be better than other people? It is so very bad manners.’
