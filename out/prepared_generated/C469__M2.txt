The rain in Calderdale did not fall so much as it hung, a cold, damp shroud that clung to the wool of Josiah Thorne’s coat and seeped into the soles of his boots. He stood at the threshold of St. Jude’s, the new curate of the parish, watching the grey slurry of the Huddersfield road. To his left, the imposing brick face of Alastair Vane’s cotton mill loomed, its chimneys belching a thick, yellow smoke that tasted of sulfur and stale coal. To his right, the village stretched out in a jagged line of terraced houses, their windows dark and unlit, for the shift had just ended and the workers were either asleep or drunk in the public house.

Josiah had come here expecting a pastoral duty. He had expected sheep on the moors and farmers who knew the seasons by the turn of the apple tree. Instead, he had found a factory that consumed men and spat them back out with hollow eyes and aching lungs. The workers of Vane’s mill did not know his name. To them, he was merely another extension of the Master’s will, a man in a black coat who smelled of ink and church polish, standing in the same lane as the overseers who counted the hours and docked wages for a cough.

"Look at him," a voice had sneered the previous evening, as he had attempted to distribute his first set of charity blankets. "The parson’s come to count our sins along with our shifts. Does he want to see our hands, sir? Or just our souls?"

Josiah had said nothing, his face burning with a humiliation that was more profound than any physical pain. He had retreated to the rectory, a damp, two-story stone building that smelled of wet plaster and mildew, and had stared at the ceiling until his eyes ached. He was the master’s man, not because he owned the mill, but because he wore the same colors, spoke the same language of order, and demanded a respect that he had not earned in the mud and soot of this place.

The only person who treated him as a human being, rather than a symbol, was Elara Vance.

Elara kept the parish school in a room above the bakery on the high street. It was a small, chaotic space filled with the smell of yeast, chalk dust, and the sharp, metallic scent of fear. The children were not the innocent, blank slates of the diocesan reports; they were sharp-tongued, hungry, and watched with an intelligence that was terrifying in its maturity. Elara was a widow, young and sharp-featured, with hair pinned back severely and hands that were always stained with ink or flour.

"You are walking on water, Mr. Thorne," she said one morning, as Josiah entered the schoolroom to deliver a letter from the vestry. The children sat in rigid rows, their eyes wide and unblinking. "But the water here is ice. If you slip, you will drown."

Josiah blinked, taken aback. "I beg your pardon?"

Elara did not look up from the slate she was scrubbing. "You speak of grace as if it were a commodity to be dispensed. You speak of patience as if it were a virtue to be enforced. These children have been working in the mills since they were seven. They do not need your patience. They need your attention. But you cannot give them that, because you are too busy being angry at the world for not being holy enough."

It was a cruel assessment, delivered without malice, and it struck Josiah with the force of a physical blow. He looked around the room, really looked, for the first time. He saw the thinness of the children’s arms, the dust in the creases of their knuckles, the way they flinched when the door opened. He had been preaching to them from a distance, a safe, elevated distance where he could feel righteous without having to be responsible.

"I am trying," Josiah said, his voice quiet.

"Trying is not enough," Elara said, finally looking up. Her eyes were grey and hard, like the stones of the mill walls. "Vane pays you a stipend. He expects you to keep the peace. He expects you to tell the men that their suffering is a test of faith. That is your job. But it is not your conscience."

That night, Josiah did not go to the rectory. He walked to the edge of the mill, where the steam vents hissed like dying animals. He stood in the rain, letting it soak through his coat, until he was shivering violently. He thought of Elara’s words. He thought of the men who had spat at him. He realized that his anger was a shield. As long as he was angry at the workers for their lack of faith, he did not have to confront the reality that his faith was impotent in the face of their poverty.

The next Sunday, Josiah did not preach from the pulpit. He walked down from the altar and stood on the floor of the church, in the middle of the pews. He wore his black coat, but he had not polished his shoes. The church was half-empty, as usual. The men stood in the back, arms crossed, looking at him with suspicion. The women sat in the front, their faces hidden behind shawls.

"Brothers," Josiah said, his voice echoing in the high, vaulted ceiling. "I have no message for you today. I have no comfort. I have no promise of a better life to come. I am here because I am afraid."

A murmur rippled through the congregation.

"I am afraid because I have come to this parish and I have found nothing but misery. I am afraid because I have seen how the mills take your strength, your time, and your health, and give you nothing in return. I am afraid because I have been telling you that this is God’s will, and I am not sure I believe it anymore."
