The arrival of the Light Dragoons in the small market town of Farringford, situated in the pleasant but somewhat provincial county of Essex, was an event of such magnitude that it was discussed with a fervor which, in the season of 1805, could only be surpassed by the price of turnips. The town, which had hitherto known peace, and even a degree of dullness, found itself suddenly agitated by the presence of thirty-six officers, each possessing a uniform that dazzled the eye and a reputation that invited the hope of a match. Among these, Captain Arthur Pennington stood out with a distinction that was not merely due to his rank, but to a particular arrangement of features which the ladies of Farringford had unanimously declared to be the very image of a young god. He was tall, of a fair complexion, and possessed of a smile that seemed to promise everything and demand nothing.

It was therefore no surprise that every mother in the parish, who possessed a daughter of marriageable age, felt a sudden and urgent necessity to display her daughter’s charms. The drawing rooms, which had previously been reserved for the quiet consumption of tea and the mild exchange of neighborhood news, were now transformed into arenas of social strategy. Mrs. Gilt, the wife of the local magistrate, was particularly active in this endeavor. She had three daughters, all of whom were, in her estimation, possessed of such superior accomplishments and beauty that they could not possibly be overlooked by an officer of such distinction. She had arranged for Captain Pennington to be invited to every possible assembly, and she had instructed her daughters to wear their most advantageous gowns, to laugh at his jokes with appropriate timidity, and to display a lack of interest in any other man that was calculated to pique his curiosity.

Captain Pennington, for his part, was not unmoved by the attentions which were paid to him. He was a man of good sense, and he had no objection to the admiration of the fair sex, provided it was not accompanied by excessive demands upon his time or his purse. He found the company of the Farringford ladies agreeable, and he was particularly pleased by the way in which Mrs. Gilt’s eldest daughter, Miss Clara, seemed to fix her eyes upon him with an intensity that suggested a depth of feeling which he had not previously known to exist in such a small town. He was, however, a man of observation, and he soon perceived that the interest which Miss Clara professed was less a matter of the heart than of the mind. Her eyes might be fixed upon him, but her thoughts were clearly elsewhere, calculating the value of his regiment and the likelihood of his staying in Essex for the winter.

It was in this atmosphere of calculated affection and social maneuvering that Captain Pennington first encountered Miss Eliza Thorne. Miss Thorne was the only daughter of Mr. Thorne, a gentleman of independent means who lived in a quiet house on the edge of the town. She was not, by the standards of Farringford, a beauty. She was slender, with a face that was more interesting than lovely, and a manner that was marked by a certain quiet reserve. She did not seek the attention of the officers, nor did she make any effort to display her accomplishments. She was often seen walking alone in the town, or reading in the library, and she paid little heed to the buzz of conversation that surrounded the arrival of the militia.

Captain Pennington first noticed her at a dinner party given by Mrs. Gilt. He was seated at one end of the table, while Miss Thorne was at the other. He attempted to catch her eye, and she returned the look with a calmness that was almost insolent. There was no coquetry in her gaze, no invitation to converse, and no sign of the nervous excitement that usually accompanied the presence of a young officer. He was struck by this indifference, and he began to wonder if she were not, in fact, as amiable as she appeared. He made several attempts to engage her in conversation, but she answered his questions with brevity and accuracy, offering no more of herself than was strictly necessary.

It was this very indifference that captivated him. In a town where every woman seemed to be striving to please him, Miss Thorne appeared to be the only one who was not. He began to seek out her company, and he found that, when they were alone, she was far more interesting than he had first supposed. She had a sharpness of wit that cut through the small talk of the town, and a knowledge of the world that was unusual in a young woman of her age. She did not try to impress him, nor did she try to please him. She simply was herself, and in doing so, she attracted him in a way that none of the other ladies had.

Captain Pennington found himself spending more and more time in the company of Miss Thorne. He would walk with her in the fields outside the town, and they would talk of books, of politics, and of the folly of their neighbors. He was aware that he was falling in love, and he was surprised to find that he was not repelled by the fact. He had always prided himself on his ability to discern the true character of a person, and he believed that he had found in Miss Thorne a woman of superior understanding. He was, however, aware that his interest in her was not shared by the rest of the town.

Mrs. Gilt, in particular, was puzzled by the change in Captain Pennington’s behavior. She had expected him to be taken with her eldest daughter, and she was confused by his apparent disregard for Miss Clara. She made several attempts to draw him back into the orbit of her family, but he was not to be moved. He continued to seek out Miss Thorne, and he began to make plans to visit her father.
