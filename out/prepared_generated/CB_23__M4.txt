The letter came at dusk, when the mill-yard lay deep in that soot-coloured slush which the West Riding calls snow, and when the lamps in the counting-house burned with the sullen, guttering flame of tallow that has stood too long in a damp cellar. I had known Mr. Halgrave these five years: first as my father’s man of business, then as the master of Marrowgate Mill, and at last—since my father’s death left me sole mistress of Throstle Bank and its rents—as the suitor whose attentions had grown as regular as the striking of the mill bell. He was a man of forty, spare and dry, with a face like a well-planed board, and eyes that never softened but seemed rather to calculate the softness of others. Yet there was a vigour in him, a hard, bright, unyielding faculty that I had mistaken, in my loneliness, for strength of soul; and when he asked me, in his blunt fashion, to be his wife, I said yes, because I was weary of managing alone, and because I thought—God forgive the vanity and the folly—that I might warm him.

He came into my little parlour that evening with the snow on his shoulders and a paper in his hand, and I saw at once that something had gone wrong with the machinery of his composure. He stood before the fire and did not sit, though I bade him; he turned the paper over and over as if it were a thing he could not read, and at last he said—

“Miss Alderley, I must speak plainly. I am a ruined man.”

I confess my first thought was for him, not for myself. I rose and would have taken his hand, but he stepped back.

“The bank at Leeds has failed,” he went on, in the same dry voice. “The new looms are mortgaged twice over. Marrowgate is gone—gone as clean as if the river had swallowed it. I have nothing but the clothes on my back and the debts I cannot pay.”

“Then,” said I, “we must be married the sooner, and face it together.”

He looked at me then with a strange, cold steadiness, and I felt the first chill of a suspicion I could not name.

“You are generous,” he said. “Generous as you are rich. It is that generosity I have counted upon.”

“Counted upon?”

“Miss Alderley—Ellen—I will not deceive you. I asked you to marry me when I believed my fortunes sound. I ask you now because they are broken. Your rents are freehold; your property is not entangled with mine. If you wed me, my creditors can be stayed, my name kept above water, my credit patched with your gold. I do not pretend to a passion I have never felt. I offer you a bargain, and I am ashamed of it—but I am a drowning man, and I clutch.”

The room seemed to grow smaller about me, and the fire to burn with a thin, acrid smoke. I had listened to him in a silence that cost me more than any outcry; for I felt the blood leave my cheeks, and a great, cold, quiet anger take possession of my heart—not the wild anger of a girl, but the settled wrath of a woman who sees her whole hope dismantled in a single sentence.

“You have been honest,” I said at last, and my voice sounded strange to me, thin and hard as a wire. “It is the one thing you have given me that I can value. You have told me that I am not loved; that I am a sum of money, a freehold, a stay for your credit. You have told me that the man who sought my hand sought it as a speculator seeks a vein of coal. And you tell me this, and you look for—what? That I should weep? That I should rail at you? That I should cast you off in scorn, and let you go to your ruin?”

“I look for nothing,” he said. “I have no right to look for anything. I have put the truth before you because I could not play the hypocrite a day longer, and because—God help me—I would rather be despised by you than pitied.”

“Despised!” I cried, and now the anger rose and broke its bounds. “Do you think I am a child, to faint at a plain word? Do you think I have lived five-and-twenty years in this town, among mill-masters and money-lenders, and never learned that men marry for fortunes and women for homes? I am not a fool, Mr. Halgrave. But I will tell you what I am. I am a woman who keeps her word. When I said I would marry you, I said it before God, and I did not say it to your mill, nor to your credit, nor to your new looms. I said it to you—to the man I thought you were, and to the man you are, ruined and all. You have been honest with me; I will be honest with you. I do not release you.”

He stared at me as though I had struck him.

“You do not—you cannot mean——”

“I mean that I will hold you to your bond,” I said, “and I will hold myself to mine. You offered me a bargain; I accept the bargain. You shall have my money to stay your creditors, and I shall have your name to shield me from the world’s pity, and your hand to—no, I will not say to love me, for you have told me you cannot. But you shall have a wife who will not shame you, and you shall learn, before you die, what it is to be bound to a woman who does not break faith.”

He came a step towards me, and his face was working strangely—the hard lines loosening, the cold eye troubled.

“Ellen,” he said, and his voice was not quite steady, “you are cruel.”
