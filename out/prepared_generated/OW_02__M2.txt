Mr. Arthur Penrose was a man of such profound respectability that he often felt the weight of it pressing against his chest like a corset of lead. He lived in a villa in Clapham that was large enough to be imposing yet small enough to be safe, and he worked in the City of London, where he dealt in the invisible currency of other people’s hopes and fears. He was not a bad man, nor was he a good one; he was merely a stockbroker, which is to say that he was a man who had learned to value the stability of a ledger far above the turbulence of the soul. His life was a neat equation of debts and credits, of mortgages and margins, a balance sheet of the spirit kept strictly in the black.

It was on a Tuesday, a day of such unremarkable greyness that the sky seemed to have forgotten its duty to shine, that the letter arrived. It was not sealed with wax, nor did it bear the heavy crest of any known house. It was merely a plain envelope, cream-colored, smelling faintly of lavender and dust, slipped under the door by a boy who did not look at Mr. Penrose as he passed. Inside, upon paper of such delicate texture that it felt like touching a spider’s web, was a single sheet of writing. The script was elegant, flowing with the casual grace of a vine, and the ink was a deep, bruised purple.

It informed Mr. Penrose, with a politeness that bordered on the offensive, that he was the rightful heir to the Marquisate of Valverde, a title of ancient and noble lineage in Portugal, lost to the family line three generations ago due to a scandal involving a mule and a misunderstanding at a border crossing. The letter suggested that the current holder, a distant cousin of questionable habits, was willing to cede the title to a man of Mr. Penrose’s evident integrity and financial prudence, provided, of course, that the necessary papers were signed and the appropriate tribute was paid to the local church.

Mr. Penrose read the letter twice. The first time, he did so with the careful skepticism of a man examining a bond for forgeries. The second time, he did so with the detached curiosity of a man observing a strange insect in his study. He found the whole affair ridiculous. A marquisate! He, Arthur Penrose, who had never seen Spain, who had not even read a book about it, was to become a nobleman. It was absurd. It was the kind of thing one read in the yellow press, written by men who had nothing better to do than invent ghosts and genealogies. He folded the letter with precise, angular creases and placed it in the bottom drawer of his desk, next to his spare waistcoat and a collection of dead moths.

He told his wife, Mrs. Penrose, nothing. He knew that to mention it would be to invite her imagination, and Mrs. Penrose’s imagination was a garden overgrown with roses that had thorns. She was a woman of modest means but immense ambition, a creature who measured the value of a life not in years, but in titles and tablecloths. If she knew of the letter, she would not ask for it to be investigated; she would ask for it to be accepted. She would begin to dream of the library, the chandeliers, the servants who would know their place. She would look at the shabby carpet in the hall and see only the dust of the old country.

For three days, Mr. Penrose ignored the letter. He went to the City, he bought his lunch at the usual club, he sold his shares with the steady hand of a surgeon. But the letter waited for him in the drawer, and it had a way of seeping into his thoughts. In the boardroom, when he spoke of interest rates, he saw the purple ink of the signature. When he looked at the clock on the wall, he saw the face of the Marquis, which he had never seen, but which he imagined to be pale, weary, and perhaps a little bit wicked.

On the fourth day, he went home early. The villa was quiet. The sun was setting, casting long, amber shadows across the lawn, and the air smelled of cut grass and impending rain. He sat in his study, the letter in his hand, and for the first time in ten years, he felt a flutter of something that was not fear, but anticipation. It was a dangerous feeling. It was the feeling of a man who has been safe and is suddenly offered the edge of a cliff.

He did not write back. He was a practical man, and he did not believe in chimeras. But he could not throw the letter away. He kept it in his breast pocket, close to his heart, where it felt like a cold stone.

It was on the fifth day that Mrs. Penrose noticed the change in him. She had been in the hallway, holding a bundle of new stationery that she had ordered from a firm in Regent Street. It was a pale blue, with a watermark of a fern, and it had not been paid for yet. She had ordered it on a whim, a small act of rebellion against the beige paper that had ruled her life for so long. She was holding it up to the light, admiring the way the fibers caught the sun, when she heard the front door open.

Arthur came in, looking different. He looked taller, thinner, and his eyes had a strange, distant light in them, as if he were looking at something that was not there.

“Arthur,” she said, her voice soft, almost sweet. “I have just received a parcel from the stationer. Look.”

She held up the blue paper. It was beautiful, delicate, and utterly useless.
