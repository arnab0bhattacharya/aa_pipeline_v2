The news came upon me in the raw February of that year, when the sky over Heathdale was the colour of a bruised plum and the wind came down off the moor with a knife in its teeth. My husband was scarce three months in the grave—a good man, and a poor one, whose whole estate was a worn black coat, a shelf of sermons, and a half-share in the mill at Millbeck ford, which he had inherited from his father and never once visited. The letter that brought me word of it was from Mr. Ezra Garth, his partner; and it was couched in terms so smooth and so pious that I smelled the falseness on the page before I had read a line of the figures. He wrote that the concern was failing; that he had carried it upon his own shoulders these seven years while my husband dreamed in his study; that he would, out of Christian charity, relieve me of my burden for the sum of two hundred pounds, and that I should do well to accept it before the creditors descended.

I read it twice by the kitchen fire, and then I read it a third time, and I felt the old fierceness rise in me—the fierceness my father had beaten out of me with a strap and the Catechism, and which had gone underground in me and grown roots. Two hundred pounds! I had seen the mill-books once, by accident, in my husband’s desk, and I had noted the sums entered there, and I had asked him what they meant, and he had said, “I know not, Sarah; figures are Garth’s business.” Figures are Garth’s business. Yes—and Garth’s profit.

I wrote back that I thanked him for his charity, and that I would come to Millbeck ford myself and see the mill with my own eyes.

The answer came within the week, and it was shorter, and it was colder, and it said that the mill was no place for a woman, and that the market-hall was no place for a woman, and that I should find myself among men who would not spare my feelings. I replied that I had buried a husband and borne a child and kept a household on eleven pounds a year, and that I did not think there was much left in the world that could spare my feelings less than they had already been spared.

So it was that on a Tuesday in March, with the rain coming down in rods, I took the carrier’s cart from Heathdale to Millbeck ford, and I lodged myself in a small room above a chandler’s shop, and I asked the chandler’s wife for a slate and a piece of chalk, and I sat down that night and began to learn bookkeeping.

I will not weary you with the weeks that followed—the weeks of ciphering by a tallow candle until my eyes swam and my back ached, of asking questions at the mill-office until the clerk, a sallow young man named Nunn, learned to answer me with a kind of sullen respect, of walking the mill-floor and making the foreman, Hob Salter, show me the looms and the warps and the yarn, and the difference between a good piece and a bad. He was a Yorkshireman of the old stamp, blunt as a brick, and at first he would say, “Nay, missus, that’s man’s work,” and I would say, “Mr. Salter, it is my work now, and I will learn it or I will break,” and he would look at me sidelong and mutter, and then he would show me. By April I could read a pattern and price a piece, and by May I could tell when a warp was false by the sound of the loom, which is a thing Hob Salter himself could not do.

But the market-hall was another matter.

It stands in the middle of Millbeck ford, a great stone barn of a place, with a gallery running round it and a clock in the gable, and on market-days it is full of clothiers and merchants and factors, and the noise of it is like a kennel of hounds. I went in the first time with my samples under my arm and my heart in my throat, and I stood at the edge of the flags and waited to be spoken to. No one spoke to me. The men passed and repassed with their pieces on their shoulders, and they looked at me as they would look at a dog in a church, and one of them—a fat man with a watch-chain, Mr. Hiram Tuke, of Tuke and Sons—said aloud to his neighbour, “There’s the parson’s widow, come to sell her cloth. Lord help us, the trade’s gone to the women now.” And his neighbour laughed, and the laugh went round the hall like a ripple, and I stood there and felt the blood go out of my face and then come back into it hot as fire.

I did not go home. I stood there the whole morning, and I watched how it was done—how the pieces were handled and turned and held up to the light, how the prices were named and beaten down, how the factors wrote in their books. And at noon, when the hall was fullest, I walked up to Mr. Tuke himself, and I said, in a voice that I made steady, “Mr. Tuke, you have bought of Garth and Wainwright these ten years. I am Wainwright’s widow, and I have a share in that concern, and I have brought you a sample of our best black doeskin, and I will sell it to you at a penny under Garth’s price.”

He stared at me as if I had spoken in Greek. “Madam,” he said, “I do not deal with women.”

“Then you deal with a fool,” said I, “for the cloth is good and the price is fair, and if you will not buy it another will.”
