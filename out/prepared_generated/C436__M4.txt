The invitation had been a surprise, arriving as it did on a Tuesday morning in a thick, cream-laid envelope that smelled faintly of jasmine. Julian Ashcroft, who at nine-and-twenty had published a single volume of verse, *The Alabaster Sigh*, to a small but gratifying scandal, read it twice. Lady Ottilie Peverel, of Marchmont Hall in Leicestershire, had admired his work. She would be delighted if he would consent to spend a weekend with them. There would be shooting, she added, as if it were an afterthought, and a small dinner. He had written back at once, his pen scratching out a florid acceptance, picturing a house of soft light and softer conversation, a haven for a poet of delicate sensibilities.

The train deposited him at a station whose sign was half-swallowed by ivy. A brougham, smelling of damp leather and horse, conveyed him through lanes that were less roads than green tunnels. When Marchmont Hall finally appeared, it was not the ethereal retreat he had imagined. It was a vast, grey-stone edifice, its windows staring like eyes across a parkland shaved smooth by sheep. It had the solid, unpoetic look of a place built to withstand centuries of weather and dullness.

His hostess met him in the hall, a space of black-and-white tiles and the heads of deceased animals. Lady Ottilie was tall and pale, her beauty a fragile, porcelain thing, and her eyes, which were the colour of a winter sky, held a feverish light. “Mr. Ashcroft,” she breathed, extending a hand that was all bone and cold rings. “I feel I know you. Your ‘Ode to a Fading Rose’ is my constant companion.” He bowed over her hand, murmuring something about the honour being his. It was then that a heavy step echoed from a corridor, and her husband appeared.

Sir Godfrey Peverel was a man constructed on a different scale from his wife. He was broad and ruddy, his tweeds straining across a barrel chest, and his handshake was a brief, crushing ordeal. “Ashcroft,” he boomed, his voice filling the hall. “Heard of you. Write the stuff, do you? Good, good. Hope you don’t mind an early start. We’re after the birds at first light.” He clapped the poet on the shoulder, a blow that nearly sent him to his knees, and strode off towards the gun room, leaving a scent of wet wool and tobacco in his wake.

The weekend, Julian soon realised, was to be a campaign of attrition. The first battle was dinner. The table was a gleaming expanse of mahogany, lit by a forest of candles that did little to dispel the gloom. He was seated between Lady Ottilie, who spoke in low, thrilling tones of Baudelaire and the decay of empires, and a florid woman who talked of nothing but the distemper of her fox terriers. Across the table, Sir Godfrey presided, a great carnivore among his kind. He addressed his guests in a series of hearty pronouncements, pausing only to consume vast quantities of roast beef and claret.

“This poet fellow,” Sir Godfrey said suddenly, gesturing at Julian with a fork laden with potato. “Tell me, do you ever write anything cheerful? Something a man could whistle?” The table fell silent. Julian felt a flush creep up his neck. “I find, Sir Godfrey, that melancholy has a more profound music,” he replied, his voice sounding thin in the vast room. “Melancholy,” the squire repeated, as if tasting a bad wine. “I’ll stick to a good day’s hunting and a decent port. Does more for the soul than all that sighing.” He let out a laugh that was more like a bark, and the table, relieved, laughed with him. Lady Ottilie’s hand, resting on the cloth, clenched into a small, white fist.

The next morning was the shooting. Julian, who had not held a gun since boyhood, was given a position at the end of a turnip field. The sky was a low, bruised purple, and the air had the sharp, metallic taste of frost. He stood with the borrowed gun feeling absurdly heavy in his hands, watching the line of beaters advance, their shouts and the crack of their sticks shattering the country silence. Birds erupted from the cover, a frantic, feathered chaos. The air was torn by gunfire. Sir Godfrey, a few yards away, was a machine of destruction, his movements fluid and precise, his great frame absorbing the recoil as he brought down bird after bird with a terrifying efficiency. A pheasant, its plumage a jewel of copper and green, broke from the hedge in front of Julian. He raised the gun, his heart hammering, but he was too slow, too hesitant. The bird soared away, unharmed. He felt Sir Godfrey’s gaze on him, a look of profound and utter contempt.

The final assault came after dinner on the last night. The ladies had withdrawn, and the men were left to their port and their smoke. The conversation had turned, as it always did in that house, to blood and land. Julian, feeling the need to assert some small presence, made a remark about the cruelty of the hunt. Sir Godfrey set down his glass with a deliberate thud. “Cruelty?” he said, his voice dangerously quiet. “It’s the law of nature, Mr. Ashcroft. The strong survive. The weak are food. That’s the truth of it. It’s the same with men. You can write your pretty verses about fading roses, but the world is built on sterner stuff.” He leaned forward, his eyes fixed on the poet. “A man must be able to look at a thing and not flinch. To pull the trigger. I wonder, sir, if you have ever pulled a trigger in your life.”

Julian felt the room closing in on him, the heat of the fire, the scent of smoke and brandy, the expectant faces of the other men. He saw in Sir Godfrey’s eyes not just dislike, but a kind of pity, which was far worse. He wanted to speak of beauty, of art, of the delicate machinery of the soul, but the words shrivelled in his throat. He was a creature of the drawing-room, and he was in the den of a predator.
