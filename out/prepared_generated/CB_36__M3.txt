The rain lashed the narrow lattice of my chamber, driven in fierce, slanting volleys by a wind that roamed the moors like a famished beast. I stood at the casement, pressing my fevered forehead against the freezing glass, seeking a solace in the cold that the dim embers on my hearth could not bestow. Below me, the village of Scaurbrook crouched in the tempest—a huddled mass of grey stone, the distant clatter of the brook swollen with the night’s deluge reaching my ears like a ceaseless, mournful complaint. Tomorrow, the sun would rise upon a changed world. Tomorrow, I should be the wife of Mr. Eldridge.

I turned from the window and looked at the scanty preparations laid out upon the deal table: a grey silk gown, modestly trimmed, and a white bonnet that seemed to mock me with its unassuming innocence. My father’s chamber lay beneath my own. I knew I should find him there, absorbed in his ledger, his brow furrowed by the chronic anxiety of debt. He was a man worn thin by the world’s harsh usage, a scholar who had bartered his intellect for a pittance, until the very roof over our heads hung by the slender thread of a merchant’s forbearance. That merchant was Eldridge. I had been told, with a gentle yet unyielding gravity, that the sacrifice of my hand was the sole ransom for my father’s peace.

How could I refuse? The question had been put to me not as a command, but as a solemn necessity. Eldridge was twenty years my senior. He was a man of broad chest and rugged, impassive features, possessing a calm, elephantine patience. He spoke little, and when he did, his words fell with the measured dullness of dropping stones. Yet he was generous. He had cleared the heaviest of our encumbrances with a stroke of his pen, asking only for me. I had looked into his heavy grey eyes and seen no cruelty, but neither had I seen a spark of that vital fire which warms the human soul. There was only a vast, quiet possession, a settled expectation that took my consent as a foregone conclusion.

I paced the floor. The wind without shrieked with a sudden, piercing violence, rattling the frame until I thought it must yield. A passage of scripture swept through my mind, borne on the gale of my own tempestuous thought: *“Though I bestow all my goods to feed the poor, and though I give my body to be burned, and have not charity, it profiteth me nothing.”* Was gratitude synonymous with charity? Could I build a life upon the barren foundation of a thankful heart, when every pulse of my being cried out against the coldness of the union?

My father’s step creaked upon the landing. A low knock sounded at my door, and he entered. He looked frail, his face deeply shadowed in the candlelight. I felt a sudden, sharp pang of conscience—a fierce, protective love that momentarily silenced my rebellion. He had suffered much. I was young, strong; could I not endure?

“You do not sleep, Clara,” he said, his voice attuned to a weary tenderness. “The wind is wild. It will exhaust itself by morning.”

“I am not weary, father. I was listening to the storm.”

He sighed, moving to the hearth and stirring the dying embers into a brief, sullen flash. “Eldridge is a good man,” he murmured, gazing into the grate. “You will find his house warm, his table plentiful. He will not chide you. You will lack for nothing.”

“I shall lack for nothing,” I repeated. The words tasted like ash.

He turned, a sudden, searching keenness in his soft, scholarly eyes. “You are silent. You have been silent for days. Speak to me, Clara. Do you shrink from this? Is the burden too heavy?”

I looked at him. The lie trembled on my lips, the easy, soothing falsehood that would have sealed my fate and purchased his repose. But a stronger force surged within me—a desperate, agonizing honesty. I could not barter my soul for a quiet fireside.

“I shrink, father,” I said, my voice trembling. “I shrink from the deadness of it. Mr. Eldridge is kind—I know it; I feel it. He is bountiful. But he does not understand me. He looks at me as a man looks at a fertile field he has purchased. I am to be a possession, not a companion. Can I give him my hand, and withhold my heart? Is that a righteous exchange?”

He stepped back, his face paling. “You speak of a fire that burns but consumes, Clara. Life is not all romance. You are poor; we are poor. Eldridge offers us safety.”

“Safety!” I cried, the restraint snapping. “What is safety to a living soul pent in a tomb? I would rather face the moor in its fury, I would rather labour until my frame dropped into the earth, than bind myself to a man whose touch is ice, whose life is a stagnant pool!”

My father’s countenance fell, the lines of care deepening into furrows of acute distress. He covered his face with his hands. “The debts,” he whispered. “The bailiffs. I am old, Clara. I cannot begin again.”

The sight of his anguish struck me like a physical blow. I had wounded him. I crossed the room and knelt before him, clasping his knees.

“Forgive me,” I murmured. “Forgive my violence. I am wild tonight—wilder than the wind.”

He removed his hands, his eyes glistening. “I would not force you, child. God knows I would not. But what is there? The world is harsh to women who stand alone.”

“I will stand alone,” I said, the conviction gathering strength in my breast. “I can be a governess. I can endure the confinement of a schoolroom. I will write to Mrs. Thorne in Manchester tomorrow. She sought a companion for her daughters. I will not marry Mr. Eldridge.”
