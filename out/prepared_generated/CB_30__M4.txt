The rain had not ceased for three days. It fell upon the crooked roofs of Altenburg with a sullen, persistent malice, turning the cobbles to rivers and the gutters to cataracts, and penning me within the walls of the Englischer Hof as surely as if I had been a prisoner in a cell of my own making. I had come to this city to teach the daughters of a merchant how to pronounce the English tongue without strangling it, and to earn enough coin to keep body and soul together; but the vacation had emptied the schoolroom, and I found myself marooned in a foreign land with nothing to do but watch the rain and count the hours.

It was in this condition of enforced idleness that Mr. Edmund Harrowby found me.

He was a traveller of the kind that England produces in her abundance—a man of means and leisure, with no particular purpose beyond the gratification of his curiosity and the pursuit of his comfort. He had come to Altenburg, he informed me, to see the cathedral, which was said to possess a window of exceptional antiquity; but having seen it, and having found it, I suspect, rather less remarkable than he had been led to expect, he had fallen into a state of profound ennui, from which he sought to rescue himself by the exercise of a facile and somewhat reckless gallantry.

I had encountered him first in the public room of the hotel, where he had attempted to engage me in conversation upon the subject of the weather, a topic which, in Altenburg, admits of no variety. I had answered him briefly and turned away; but he was not to be discouraged. He had the assurance of a man who has never known rebuff, and the persistence of one who has nothing better to do. He sought me out at dinner, he waylaid me upon the stairs, he contrived to be present at every meal at which I appeared, until at length I found myself compelled either to retreat entirely to my chamber or to submit to his society.

I submitted. I am ashamed to confess it, but I submitted. The truth is that I was lonely—lonely with a loneliness so profound and so gnawing that it had eaten into the very marrow of my being. I had been six months in Altenburg, and in all that time I had not exchanged a word of genuine sympathy with any living soul. The merchant's daughters regarded me as a species of upper servant; the merchant himself was too absorbed in his ledgers to notice my existence; and the other English residents—such as they were—had their own affairs and their own circles, into which I had neither the means nor the inclination to intrude.

And so, when Mr. Harrowby spoke to me of England, of the green fields and the grey skies and the voices of my countrymen, I listened. When he jested with me, I smiled. When he brought me books from the circulating library, I read them. When he walked with me in the colonnade before the cathedral, sheltering me from the rain with his umbrella, I did not refuse his arm. I told myself that it was merely civility—that there was no harm in it—that I was not such a fool as to mistake his idle gallantry for anything more serious. But in my heart I knew that I was playing with fire, and that the flame, if it caught, would consume me.

It was upon the fourth evening of his acquaintance that he made his proposal.

We were sitting in the public room, before a fire that crackled and spat with the dampness of the wood, and he had been speaking—as he often spoke—of his estate in Devonshire, of its gardens and its orchards and its pleasant situation. I had listened with that mixture of interest and resentment which such descriptions always awakened in me; for I had no estate, no garden, no pleasant situation, and the contrast between his lot and mine was not flattering to my pride.

"And you," said he, suddenly turning to me with that air of easy assurance which I had come to recognize as his characteristic—"you, Miss Ashby, what do you propose to do with yourself? You cannot remain forever in this dreary place, teaching the alphabet to a parcel of German schoolgirls."

"I have no other prospect," I answered, briefly.

"No other prospect!" he repeated. "Come, now, that is too modest. A woman of your accomplishments—your understanding—your spirit—surely you might do better than that. You might"—he paused, and I saw a gleam of something that might have been mischief, or might have been something more serious, kindle in his eye—"you might marry."

I looked at him sharply. "Is that a proposal, Mr. Harrowby?"

He laughed—a light, careless laugh, that grated on my ear. "It might be," said he. "What would you say if it were?"

"I should say that you were pleased to jest," I replied, "and that I was not inclined to be your butt."

"Jest! By heaven, I never was more serious in my life. Come, Miss Ashby, let us be plain with one another. You are alone in the world, without means, without friends, without prospects. I am a man of property, with a house that wants a mistress and a heart that—well, that is not insensible to your merits. I offer you my name, my home, my fortune. What do you say?"

I was silent for a moment. The rain beat against the window-panes with a sound like the drumming of fingers upon a table. The fire hissed and flickered. I looked into his face—that handsome, careless, good-humoured face—and I asked myself what I saw there.

"You are bored," I said at last.

He started. "Bored?"
