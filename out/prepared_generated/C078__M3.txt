It was on a Tuesday in February of 1842 that Mr. Pellham, the senior partner of the firm of Pellham, Grint, and Hoyle, solicitors, of Chancery Lane, London, summoned me from the outer office where I spent my days copying depositions and engrossing affidavits. I was twenty-two years of age, a clerk of three years’ standing, and such a summons was uncommon enough to quicken my step along the uncarpeted corridor. I found Mr. Pellham at his desk, holding in his hand a small packet, no larger than a gentleman’s pocketbook, wrapped in oilcloth and tied about with twine. He regarded me over the rims of his spectacles in a manner I had come to understand signified that some matter of unusual delicacy was at hand. He told me that I was to carry the packet to a lady residing at Kelmarsh Hall, in the county of Essex, and that I was to place it into her hands and no other. He told me that I was to say nothing of its contents, for the very good reason that I knew nothing of them, and that I was to return with whatever reply the lady saw fit to give. He then wrote the direction on a slip of paper and dismissed me with a wave of his hand.

The journey was a long one. I travelled by coach from the Bell and Crown in Holborn as far as Chelmsford, and there engaged a gig driven by a man who knew the lanes. The February weather was raw, and a low mist lay over the fields so that the hedgerows seemed to float upon a grey sea. We came at last to a pair of iron gates set between brick pillars, and beyond them an avenue of limes, bare and dripping, at the end of which stood Kelmarsh Hall, a house of red brick in the Elizabethan manner, with many gables and tall chimney-stacks. I was admitted by a footman and shown into a panelled room that I took to be a library, for the walls were lined with books, though many of the shelves appeared to have been undisturbed for a generation, the volumes soft with dust. A fire burned in the grate, and near it, in a chair, sat the lady to whom the packet was addressed.

She was, I suppose, not yet forty, though she appeared older, for her face had the drawn and colourless look of one who sleeps badly or not at all. Her hair, visible beneath a lace cap, was dark, and her eyes were of a grey that seemed to match the weather beyond the long windows. She wore a dress of black stuff, though I observed no crepe, and I could not determine whether she was in mourning or had merely adopted the colour as a habit. She did not rise when I entered, but regarded me with an attention that I found disconcerting, as though she were attempting to read some letter written upon my face. I presented myself, gave the name of the firm, and placed the packet upon the table beside her. She looked at it for a long moment without touching it, and I observed that her hands, which rested on the arms of her chair, did not move. At length she drew the packet towards her and held it in her lap, still without untying the twine. She then asked me, in a voice that was low and even, whether I would call again in a week’s time. I said that I would. She thanked me, and the footman showed me out.

I reported all of this to Mr. Pellham, who heard me without expression and told me that I was to return to Kelmarsh Hall on the following Tuesday, and every Tuesday thereafter, until such time as the lady gave me other instruction or he, Mr. Pellham, directed me to stop. He offered no further explanation, and I was not in a position to demand one. And so began what I may call the Kelmarsh commission, which was to occupy me, in the manner described, for the better part of a year.

Each week I made the journey. The roads improved as spring came on, and the avenue of limes put forth its leaves, and the house, seen at the end of it, took on a less sombre aspect. I was always shown into the same room, and the lady was always waiting, or appeared to be waiting, in the same chair by the fire. On each occasion I placed before her a packet, identical in its wrapping to the first, which I had received that morning from Mr. Pellham’s own hand. And on each occasion she received it without opening it, holding it in her lap as she had done the first time, and asked me to call again the following week. She never varied the form of words, and I, for my part, never varied mine. We might have been actors in a small and rather tedious play, performing the same scene before an empty house.

I observed, over the weeks, certain small changes. In March she had moved the chair nearer to the window, and I found her sitting in the grey daylight rather than by the fire, which had been allowed to burn low. In April there were flowers on the table, a few daffodils in a glass, and I thought her face had a little more colour. In May she wore a dress of grey muslin, and I saw that she had once been handsome, or would be again if some weight were lifted from her. But the packets accumulated, I noticed, on a shelf behind her chair, a row of them, each wrapped in oilcloth and tied with twine, each unopened. They stood like a collection of small, uniform bricks, and I could not conjecture what they contained, or why they were delivered, or why she did not open them, or why, if she did not intend to open them, she continued to ask for them.
