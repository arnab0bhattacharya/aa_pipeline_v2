The road from Harrowden to the rectory at Letchmoor was three miles of cart-rut and flint, and Edmund Meacham walked it slowly, though the November air had already turned his breath to fog. He had walked this road twice a week for six years, first as a boy carrying his father’s old prayer book, then as a student home from the seminary at Whitford, and now as a man within a fortnight of ordination. He knew every gate-stile, every stand of beech, every dip where the mud would seize his boot in winter. Today the familiarity felt like the walls of a room closing in.

In his coat pocket, folded into a square no bigger than his palm, was the letter he had written to the bishop that morning. He had not yet sent it. The ink was dry, the words plain: he could not take orders. He had written them at the inn in Harrowden, sitting over a tea grown cold, and the pen had moved with a terrible ease, as if his hand had been waiting years for the rest of him to catch up.

The loss had not come as a single crack. It had been a long quiet ebbing, the way the Letchmoor brook withdrew from its banks in late summer, leaving the stones dry and pale. He had tried to hold it back. He had prayed for faith as a man prays for rain in a drought, with clenched hands and a desperate, angry hope. But the doubts had accumulated like silt. At Whitford he had read the required divines, had parsed the articles, had written his essays on grace and justification with a fluency that his tutors praised. And each phrase he committed to paper had felt like mortar laid into a wall that enclosed an empty field. He could defend the structure. He could not find the thing it was meant to protect.

The final week had undone him. He had been assigned his ordination sermon, taken his text from the sixth chapter of John, and sat down to compose. The words of the scripture lay on the desk before him, and he understood them as he always had, soundly and completely, and they were nothing to him. He read them again. They were marks on a page. He read them a third time and felt only the silence of the room, and the cold, and the dull certainty that he could not stand before a congregation and speak words he did not believe had been given to him to speak.

He had thought of his mother then, and the thought had driven him from the room into the street, walking fast, his collar up, as if he could outpace it.

The Meachams had lived in the shadow of the Church for as long as anyone in Harrowden could remember. Edmund’s grandfather had been a vicar in the next county. His father, John Meacham, had been curate at St. Aidan’s in Letchmoor until the fever took him in 1821, leaving his widow with two children and nothing but a small annuity from the parish society. Edmund had been eight. His sister, Clara, had been five. His mother, Margaret, had taken in mending, then boarders, then work as a seamstress for the draper’s wife, and all the while she had spoken of Edmund’s vocation as a fact already established, a stone set in the foundation of their lives before the boy could read.

She had not been wrong to see it there. Edmund had been a serious child, quiet, attentive to the services, able to recite the catechism before he understood half of it. The vicar, old Mr. Phipps, had noticed him, had given him books, had guided him toward Whitford. Margaret had wept with gratitude, and in the evenings after the boarders had gone to bed, she had knelt in the small kitchen and prayed that her son would be found worthy.

The living at Letchmoor was not wealthy. It was a curacy attached to St. Aidan’s, worth perhaps ninety pounds a year, but it came with the cottage near the churchyard gate, and the right to a portion of the glebe, and the parish’s respect. For the Meachams it would mean an end to the rented rooms and the needlework and the careful counting of every shilling. Margaret had spoken of it openly for the past year, not with extravagance but with the plain relief of a woman who has carried a heavy pot a long way and sees the table ahead.

Edmund passed the turn to the Whitford road and came into the fields above Letchmoor. The rectory was visible from the rise, a low stone building with a smoking chimney, set back from the church behind a bare garden. He could see the line of washing strung between the pear trees, white and grey against the brown grass. His mother would be inside. She would be preparing for his arrival, setting out the good cups, perhaps baking. She had been baking more often lately, as the ordination date approached, as though she could feed the future into being.

He stopped at the stile and took out the letter. He read it again. The words were the same. He folded it and put it back.

He thought: I can say it differently. I can say I need more time. I can say I am unwell, that the bishop has deferred me, that there has been a delay. He could buy months with such a story. But months would only bring him back to this stile, this letter, this silence in his chest where the words of the service should have been. And his mother would have prayed through every one of those months, on her knees in the kitchen, her hands clenched, her lips moving, and the deferral would only make the truth more cruel when it came.

He climbed the stile and walked down the path.
