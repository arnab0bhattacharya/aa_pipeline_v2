It is a truth generally acknowledged, though as constantly ignored, that the most dangerous of all errors is the one a clever woman commits to prove that she is not subject to error at all. The opinions of our friends, when they run counter to our own cherished inclinations, are seldom received as the disinterested counsel they may in fact be; rather, they are felt as a species of intellectual insult, a covert suggestion that our judgement is a thing to be guided and governed by external authorities. For Mrs. Adela Thorne, a young widow of six-and-twenty, possessed of a lively intelligence and a fortune of thirty thousand pounds settled entirely upon herself, the warnings of her aunt, Lady Jane Farringdon, and her late husband’s solicitor, the sagacious Mr. Penhaligon, had precisely this effect. They transformed what might have been a mere idle fancy for a handsome face into a solemn test of her own sovereign capacity to discern character.

The theatre of this trial was the spa town of Dunmere, a place where the tedium of the prescribed daily routine—drink the waters, walk the promenade, listen to the orchestra—provided a perfect, almost laboratory-like setting for the observation of human motive. It was here that Mr. Alistair Vance had presented himself. He was not, it must be admitted, a man to be easily overlooked. With a bearing that spoke of an older, more landed gentility than his circumstances could possibly warrant, and a conversational facility that could render the most trivial topic—a change in the weather, the quality of the biscuits at the Assembly Rooms—into an occasion for a quiet, flattering wit, he possessed all the outward signs of a man whose inner substance was worthy of his form. He was an artist, he said, a painter of landscapes who found the light of the western counties particularly inspiring. This profession, while romantic, conveniently excused any lack of a settled income or a clear lineage.

To Lady Jane, a woman whose seventy years had furnished her with a profound and unillusioned knowledge of the world’s small mercies and large cruelties, Mr. Vance was an object of immediate and severe suspicion. “My dear Adela,” she had said, her voice as dry as a winter leaf, “a man who speaks of painting the light and has no visible means of purchasing the canvas is not an artist, but a speculator. And his only commodity is charm.” Mr. Penhaligon, called down from London for a consultation in the chilly Dunmere lodgings, put the matter more plainly. “He is, in the common parlance, a fortune hunter, madam. His interest in your portfolio is far more ardent than his interest in the local scenery.”

These judgements, delivered with the weight of experience and professional duty, were as fuel to the fire of Adela’s resolve. She saw not a warning, but a challenge. To accept their verdict would be to confess herself a gullible girl, a mere cipher whose life was to be directed by the dreary calculus of prudence. To reject it was to affirm her own independence, her own penetration. And so, when Mr. Vance, with a lover’s eloquence that artfully blended passion with a seeming deference to her superior sense, declared that he sought not her fortune but the woman herself—whose mind, he swore, was a landscape more beautiful than any he had ever painted—Adela felt a triumphant certainty. She was choosing for herself. She married him in London three months later, in a quiet ceremony that was meant to signify her disdain for worldly opinion, but which in fact only isolated her further from the very counsel she might soon have occasion to regret spurning.

The first months of marriage were a vindication of her choice, or so it appeared. They took a handsome house in Mayfair, furnished with an elegance that seemed to belie any want of funds, for Alistair had a wonderful facility for acquiring beautiful things on credit, a talent he called an “artist’s eye for arrangement.” He was, as ever, the perfect companion: attentive, amusing, and possessed of an almost preternatural sympathy for her every mood. It was only with the slow, creeping onset of the new year that the first threads of the tapestry began to unravel. A bill for a set of silver-backed brushes, exorbitant in price, arrived and was paid. Then another, for a carriage he had leased, and then a request from his tailor, a man of forbidding aspect, for a settlement of a long-standing account.

Adela began to perceive that Alistair’s profession was less a vocation than a convenient fiction. His studio, which she had never been permitted to enter, was invariably locked; his canvases, when he did produce one, were hasty, derivative things. The money he spent so freely was not the product of sales, but an advance upon her own fortune, drawn in small, insidious amounts against their joint account—an account he had persuaded her to open as a symbol of their union. The charm, once the instrument of his courtship, was now the instrument of his evasion. When she, with a tremor in her voice, attempted to speak of economy, he would laugh, kiss her hand, and chide her gently for worrying her “pretty head” with sordid details, promising that a great commission was just around the corner.

The moment of decision arrived on a wet afternoon in November. A letter lay on the hall table, its address written in the familiar, upright hand of Mr. Penhaligon. Adela knew its contents before she broke the seal: it would be a formal notice of a third draught on her capital, a draught authorised by a signature she did not recall giving, and witnessed, no doubt, by some associate of her husband’s. She held the paper in her hand, the damp chill of the room seeming to emanate from it. She saw, with a clarity that was like a physical pain, the whole architecture of her folly.
