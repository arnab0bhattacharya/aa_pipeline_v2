The town of Brighton, with its expansive Marine Parade and seemingly inexhaustible supply of officers, was generally acknowledged to be a place of perpetual amusement; and of all the visitors drawn to its sea air and lively society, Mrs. Vernon was perhaps the most satisfied with her situation. She had taken a respectable house on the Steine for the summer, and having with her two daughters and the daughter of her particular friend, Mrs. Hayward, she considered herself perfectly equipped to enjoy all the rational and elegant pleasures the place could afford. Miss Clara Hayward, the young lady in question, was a girl of sensible disposition and some fortune, sent into Sussex chiefly to acquire that polish of manner which only a fashionable watering-place can bestow, and to be diverted, if possible, from a disappointment of the heart she had suffered the previous spring.

Miss Hayward was not wanting in understanding, but she possessed a warmth of feeling and a readiness to be pleased which her more cautious hostess viewed with some alarm. Mrs. Vernon had seen enough of the world—or at least, enough of assembly rooms—to know that an officer in a red coat is rarely a safe companion for a girl of nineteen, and she had taken it upon herself to point out the dangers of the military with a frequency that rather diminished its effect.

Among the various gentlemen then resident in the town, Captain Thorne of the 12th Dragoons had attracted particular notice—not wholly of the favourable kind. He was known to possess a very small estate in the north, a very large debt in the south, and a manner so generally agreeable that it was universally suspected of being insincere. Mrs. Vernon had gathered these particulars from a friend in Hampshire, whose cousin had been intimately acquainted with the family, and she lost no time in communicating them to Miss Hayward, with such additions of her own as her anxiety for that young lady's welfare naturally suggested.

"He is excessively agreeable, I allow," said she, one morning as they sat together after breakfast, "and he has a way of saying things that is very captivating; but I have it on the best authority that his conduct has been most reprehensible. He paid his addresses to Miss Belgrave last autumn, and deserted her within a fortnight—nay, it is even said that he was engaged to her, though the circumstance was never publicly acknowledged. I would not have you so much as dance with him, my dear Clara."

Miss Hayward listened with proper gravity, expressed her gratitude for the warning, and privately resolved to be very much upon her guard. She had no particular wish to be captivated by Captain Thorne; she had heard enough of his charms to feel a degree of resistance beforehand, which is perhaps the surest way of withstanding them. She was, however, not a little curious to see a man whose reputation was so formidable, and when she encountered him that very evening at the library, she observed him with an attention that she could not wholly justify to herself as mere caution.

She found him exactly as he had been described—agreeable, animated, and evidently very well satisfied with himself. He addressed her with an ease that bordered upon familiarity, and said several things which were clever without being wise. Miss Hayward, who had expected to be charmed against her will, was surprised to find herself merely entertained, and not always that. She replied with her usual good humour, but without any flutter of spirits, and retired to rest that night with the comfortable reflection that she had received a dangerous man's attentions and survived them entirely unaffected.

The following morning brought an unexpected addition to their party in the shape of Mr. Ellsworth, a gentleman of moderate fortune and considerable respectability, who had come down from London on business connected with the settlement of his late uncle's affairs. He was known to Mrs. Vernon through her husband's family, and was received with that sort of cordiality which is bestowed upon acquaintances who are not quite important enough to be troublesome. He was a man of five-and-thirty, rather grave in his appearance, and possessed of a countenance which, if it could not be called handsome, had at least the merit of being perfectly honest.

Miss Hayward had not been forewarned against Mr. Ellsworth, because no one had thought him of sufficient consequence to require a warning. He had no dangerous reputation, no history of broken engagements, no debts that were spoken of in whispers. He was, in short, a man whom nobody disliked because nobody feared him, and whom nobody admired because nobody thought about him.

It was therefore with some surprise that Miss Hayward found herself, after two or three days of his company, looking forward to his visits with a degree of pleasure which she could not entirely account for. He walked with them on the Parade, he attended them to the rooms, he sat beside them at concerts—and on all these occasions he said very little, but what he said was always sensible. He did not flatter her, he did not gaze at her, he did not say clever things for her particular benefit; but he handed her to her carriage with a steadiness that she found rather pleasing, and when she spoke, he listened with an attention that was at once modest and gratifying.

"I like Mr. Ellsworth vastly," she said to Miss Vernon, the elder of the daughters, as they were dressing for dinner.

"Do you?" replied Miss Vernon, with the indifference of a girl who has not yet made up her mind. "I think him rather dull."

"He is not dull—he is serious. There is a great difference."

"I am sure I do not know what the difference is, unless it is that a serious man may be dull with a better grace."
