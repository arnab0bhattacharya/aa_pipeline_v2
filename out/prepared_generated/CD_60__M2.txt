The smoke did not merely rise; it assaulted the heavens with a violence that seemed personal, a great, ragged, blackened hand clutching at the stars and squeezing them until they blinked out of sight. It was a Tuesday night in the town of Griston, a place so firmly planted in the mud of Yorkshire industry that even the sky seemed to remember it was there, but tonight the sky had forgotten its duties entirely. The Mill of Barnaby Vane, that great, grinding, iron-toothed beast which had chewed the lives of three hundred men and women into dust for forty years, was burning. It was not a polite fire, such as might warm a hearth in a gentleman’s study and crackle pleasantly while one reads a novel; no, this was a devouring, hungry, roaring thing, a monster of orange and yellow that licked the timber beams with tongues of flame and swallowed the very soul of the building whole.

Mr. Barnaby Vane, the proprietor, stood at the end of his own garden, a respectable distance from the inferno, holding a glass of port wine in one hand and a handkerchief to his nose with the other, though the wind was blowing the smoke away from him. He was a small man, Mr. Vane, with a face like a dried apple and eyes that were always calculating the weight of a penny. He watched the flames with the detached interest of a man observing a chess match in which he had already moved his king to safety.

"It is a great loss, certainly," he said to his steward, Mr. Pottle, who stood trembling beside him, his white cravat stained with soot. "But a loss, you must remember, is a thing that is insured. And as for the town... well, it has always been a bit damp. Perhaps this will dry it out."

Three hundred hands, they called them. Three hundred pairs of hands that had been stained with dye, broken by looms, and calloused by the relentless rhythm of the shuttle. Now those hands were idle, hanging limp at the sides of men and women who stood in the street, staring at the ruins with a blankness that was more terrible than anger. They were the mill-hands, the wives, the children, the entire fabric of Griston’s daily life, unraveled by the heat. The fire had taken not just the timber and the thread, but the future. And as the crowd milled about, a figure emerged from the smoke, a figure that seemed to have been conjured by the chaos itself.

It was Silas Crake, the foreman. Silas was a man of few words and many scars, a man who had been born in the mill and would likely die in it. He had not been in the office when the fire started; he had been on the floor, shouting for the workers to clear out, his voice drowned by the roar of the flames. He had not saved any lives, for the fire had been too fast, too sudden. But in the last few seconds, as the roof was coming down like a collapsing mountain, Silas had seen the desk. He had seen the iron-bound ledger, the book that contained the secrets of the mill’s soul. And he had taken it.

He held it now, wrapped in his own coat, a strange, warm object against his chest. He did not know why he had taken it. It was not his property; it belonged to Mr. Vane. But something in the fire, some kind of primal instinct, had told him that the truth was there, locked in those pages, and that the truth must survive.

Mr. Pottle saw him and ran towards him, his face a mask of panic. "Crake! What have you done? That ledger! It is the record of everything!"

Silas did not speak. He looked at the fire, then at the steward, and then at the small, smug figure of Mr. Vane. He walked past them, straight into the crowd, and the crowd parted before him like the Red Sea before Moses. They knew Silas. They knew his silence was a language more potent than any shout.

He stopped in the center of the square, where the fountain was dry and cracked, and he held up the ledger. The light from the fire illuminated his face, making his eyes look like coals. He opened the book, not to read, but to show. And in that moment, the fire seemed to pause, as if even the flames were holding their breath.

It was not the wages that caught the eye, though the wages were a story of poverty in themselves. It was the date. One week ago, Mr. Vane had taken out a policy. A full policy. On the mill. On the machinery. On the inventory. All of it. And the premium had been paid in cash, on a day when the mill had been inspected for fire hazards and found to be in perfect condition.

The silence in the square was absolute. The only sound was the crackle of the burning mill in the distance, a sound that now seemed less like a disaster and more like a performance. A performance for an audience of one, an audience that was currently enjoying a glass of port.

Mrs. Gable, the wife of the dyer, stepped forward. Her face was pale, her eyes wide with a dawning horror. "He knew," she whispered. "He knew it was going to happen. He knew the beams were rotten."

Silas nodded slowly. He did not say it, but the meaning was clear in the set of his jaw. He looked at Mr. Vane, who was now frowning, his glass of wine half-finished. The small man’s face, which had been so composed a moment ago, was now twisted into a mask of rage. He threw his glass at Silas’s feet, and it shattered, the red wine spreading across the cobblestones like a wound.
