The trouble began, as such troubles will, with a perfectly ordinary circumstance. I had come down out of the hills—where my machine had deposited me with a jarring suddenness I do not care to dwell upon—into a broad and shining city that called itself Vireo, and I had walked perhaps a mile along its glassy pavements before I remarked that the people about me were staring. Not the frank, nudging stare of rustics at a stranger, but a kind of frozen, courteous horror, as though I had committed some indelicacy too grave to be mentioned aloud.

I was tired, dusty, and in no humour for mysteries. I stopped a young fellow in a pale green tunic—everyone wore pale green, or a pale grey that was almost green—and asked him the way to an hotel. He recoiled a step, then recovered himself with an effort that was painfully visible, and pointed down the boulevard without once looking at my face. "The Meridian," he said, in a voice pitched too high, "is two squares hence. You cannot mistake it. It is the tall building with the—" and here his voice failed him, and he hurried away.

At the Meridian they gave me a room, and a mirror, and it was in the mirror that I first understood. I am not a vain man, but I own I was startled. My hair, which had been iron-grey when I set out, was now—well, it was still iron-grey; but it was the only grey head, I presently perceived, in the whole of Vireo. The bell-boy who brought my water had hair the colour of ripe wheat and a face as smooth as a boy's, and he was, I learned later, forty-one years of age. The manageress, a woman of commanding presence and not a line upon her brow, was sixty-three. They had, in this future of mine, found the trick of it. At thirty the body calls a halt; the clock is stopped; the years go by without the body's leave, and no one grows old because no one is permitted to.

I do not know how to convey to you the peculiar quality of that city. It was beautiful, clean, and brisk, and it was as though a whole population had agreed to pretend that nothing serious could ever happen to them. There were no old men in the parks, no grandmothers in the doorways. There was a great deal of laughter, and it had a brittle sound. And when I went down to the dining-room that first evening, the room fell silent, and a hundred pairs of unwrinkled eyes turned upon me, and I understood that I was not merely a stranger. I was an offence.

The waiter—a man of perhaps fifty, with the face of a youth of twenty—set down my soup with a trembling hand. "Sir," he said, "I must ask you—I am instructed to ask you—whether you are unwell."

"Unwell?" said I. "I am tired, and I am hungry, and I have walked a great distance. Otherwise I am in excellent health."

"But your—" he began, and could not finish.

"My hair," said I. "You mean my hair. It is grey. It has been grey these fifteen years. It is not a disease, my good fellow; it is a distinction. It is what happens to a man who has lived."

He looked at me as a man might look at a leper who had offered him a compliment. "I will fetch the superintendent," he said, and went.

The superintendent came, and after him a physician, and after the physician a polite but firm young official of the municipality, whose name was Rennick, and who sat down opposite me with the air of a man performing a disagreeable duty. He had a notebook, and he opened it, and he regarded me with a kind of pained benevolence.

"You are a stranger," he said. "We have no record of your arrival. That in itself is irregular. But we are not an inhospitable people, and we wish to help you. You are evidently suffering from a rare and distressing condition. There are, I believe, historical records of it. It is called—" he consulted his notebook—"senescence. It is a failure of the restorative treatment, is it not? A gradual decay of the tissues. We have not seen a case in Vireo in ninety years."

"It is not a failure of anything," I said, with some heat. "It is the natural course of a human life. I am sixty-seven years of age. I have earned every one of these grey hairs, and I am not ashamed of them. Do you suppose your smooth-faced young men have anything I lack? I have seen things they will never see. I have buried friends. I have made mistakes and mended them. I have grown."

Rennick wrote something down. "The delusion is characteristic," he murmured, to no one in particular. "The patient frequently insists upon the dignity of his condition. It is a compensatory mechanism, I am told."

"Compensatory!" I cried. "Man alive, look at me. Do you think I am compensating for anything? I am the only person in this city who has actually lived a full life, and you look at me as though I were a corpse that had learned to walk."

He was not offended. That was the dreadful part. He was patient, and kind, and utterly impervious. "You must understand our position," he said. "We have eliminated suffering. We have eliminated the decay of the body. We have made a society in which no one need fear the loss of beauty or strength or faculty. And you come among us displaying the very thing we have conquered, and you ask us to call it a virtue. Can you not see how disturbing that is? The young people—and they are all young, here—they have never seen a wrinkle. They do not know what to make of you. Some of them believe you are a ghost."
