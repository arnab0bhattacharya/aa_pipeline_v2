The wind, which had fretted the shutters all afternoon, now rose to a proper gale, and the rain lashed the panes of the library with a vehemence that seemed to me almost accusatory. I had come to this lonely house above the shingle at Fairhollow, armed with a letter of introduction and the ardour of a young man who believes that history is a ledger of noble deeds and that old men are merely its living pages. Captain Edward Ashcombe received me with a courtesy that was, I now perceive, the frosty politeness of a man who has spent forty years learning to keep his own counsel. He was tall and spare, his face weathered to the colour of old leather, and his eyes—those eyes that had looked upon Trafalgar and, as I had read in the gazettes, upon scenes of darker import in the Caribbean—were grey and still as a sea becalmed.

I stated my errand with what I hoped was becoming modesty. I was compiling a history of the navy’s lesser-known engagements, I said, and I had learned that he, as a young lieutenant aboard the frigate *Resolute*, had been instrumental in quelling a mutiny off the coast of San Cristóbal in the year ‘ninety-eight. A mutiny, I added, with perhaps too much eagerness, that had ended in the execution of three ringleaders and had been cited in certain quarters as a model of firmness and dispatch.

He did not speak for a space. He rose and went to the window, and stood with his back to me, watching the rain course down the glass. “I have nothing to say upon that subject,” he said at last, and his voice was low and flat, like a bell struck once beneath water. “The dispatches contain all that is fit for the public eye. I would be obliged if you would not press me.”

I confess I was stung. I had travelled far, and the ardour of youth is impatient of what it takes for mere obstinacy. “But Captain,” I persisted, “the dispatches are but a skeleton. They give the fact, not the feeling. Surely the historian may be trusted with the truth of a matter so long past? The men you hanged—did they not deserve their fate? Was it not a just and necessary severity?”

At that he turned, and I saw in his countenance a struggle that he strove in vain to master. “Just?” he repeated, and the word seemed to cost him a physical effort. “You speak of justice as a thing that may be measured out like powder and shot. You were not there, sir. You did not see the faces of those men when the sentence was read. You did not hear—” He stopped, and pressed his hand to his brow. “I will not speak of it. I have kept my silence these thirty years, and I will keep it still.”

I should have withdrawn. I know that now. But some demon of inquiry possessed me, and I drew from my satchel the packet I had carried with me from London—a packet I had hesitated to bring, and which I had told myself I would produce only if all else failed. “Captain Ashcombe,” I said, and my voice trembled with the import of what I did, “I have in my possession a letter, written by one of those men—by Thomas Rennick, the boatswain’s mate—upon the night before his execution. It was entrusted to a seaman who survived, and it has come at last into my hands. It tells the story differently.”

The effect upon him was extraordinary. He did not start, nor cry out; but his face went grey as ash, and he sank into his chair as though the strength had been cut from his legs. He stared at the packet in my hand as a man might stare at a serpent coiled in his path. “Rennick,” he whispered. “Rennick. I had thought that name buried with him.” He was silent a long while, and the storm without seemed to enter into the room and fill the space between us. Then he said, in a voice so altered I scarce knew it, “Read it. If you have come so far, you must read it. I would hear what he wrote.”

I broke the seal. The paper was brown and frail, the hand cramped and hurried, as of a man writing against time and in the shadow of the yardarm. I read aloud, and as I read, the captain sat motionless, his hands gripping the arms of his chair, his eyes fixed upon the fire.

The letter spoke of the *Resolute*’s cruise in the Caribbean, and of a fever that had swept the ship, and of a captain—not Ashcombe, but his commander, one Captain Hale—who had driven the men past endurance, withholding their grog and their rest, and flogging a boy of fourteen for a trifling offence until the lad’s back was raw as butchered meat. It spoke of a night when the men had gathered in the forecastle, not in mutiny, but in desperation, to petition for redress; and of how Hale, learning of it, had sworn to hang every tenth man. It spoke of Ashcombe himself—Lieutenant Ashcombe, as he then was—coming among them with a face of anguish, begging them to disperse, promising to intercede, swearing upon his honour that no harm should come to any man who returned to his duty. And it spoke of how the men, trusting him, had dispersed; and of how Hale, when the ship reached port, had broken the promise, and had ordered the courts-martial, and had selected three men to die as an example—Rennick among them.
