It is one thing to discover a substance that nullifies the most constant and universal of forces; it is quite another to discover it in a cluttered back garden in Wychford, and to have the demonstration witnessed by one’s housekeeper.

Professor Alistair Finch had been labouring at the problem for the better part of a decade. His colleagues at the University, men of sound but unimaginative minds, had long since dismissed his work as the hobby of an eccentric. They dealt in solid, observable facts—steam pressure, electrical resistance, the predictable orbits of planets—while Finch pursued the negative, the absence, the very thing that held it all together. His substance, which he had provisionally named Gravitite, was not a metal or a gas, but a crystalline precipitate of a peculiar and unstable nature. It did not repel gravity, as a magnet repels its opposite pole; it simply, and locally, cancelled its effect upon any object it touched.

On that particular Tuesday in late September, the air was crisp with the promise of autumn. Finch, his eyes gritty from a night spent hunched over a galvanometer, decided that the theoretical phase was complete. He required a practical demonstration. The subject he chose was a simple, wrought-iron garden chair, the kind one finds on a hundred suburban lawns. It was heavy, predictable, and utterly without sentiment.

Mrs. Gable, his housekeeper, was a woman of formidable practicality and a deep-seated suspicion of anything that could not be boiled, scrubbed, or accounted for in the weekly budget. She had arrived with his morning tea to find him dabbing a milky, shimmering fluid onto the chair’s legs with a small brush.

“Another of your potions, Professor?” she inquired, her voice dripping with the weary tolerance she reserved for his more outlandish schemes. “I do hope it won’t stain the flagstones. I’ve just had them scoured.”

“Far from it, Mrs. Gable,” Finch said, not looking up. “This is the culmination of my life’s work. This, I believe, will render the very concept of weight obsolete. Observe.”

He stepped back, gesturing towards the chair. It sat there, looking perfectly ordinary. Mrs. Gable, with a sigh that suggested she had observed many such disappointments, placed her tea tray on the garden wall and folded her arms.

“Well? It looks like a chair, Professor. A chair in need of a good coat of paint, I might add.”

“It is waiting,” Finch said, his voice taut with anticipation. “The effect is not instantaneous. The crystalline matrix must first attune itself to the local gravitational field. A few moments is all.”

Tired of standing, and with the mild impatience of a woman who had work to do, Mrs. Gable said, “If you’re quite finished with your magic, I shall sit down for a moment before I take the tea things in. My feet are killing me.”

Before Finch could utter a word of protest, she lowered herself onto the chair. She settled her considerable weight, adjusted her apron, and let out a small grunt of satisfaction. “Well, at least it’s comfortable.”

For a second, nothing happened. Finch held his breath. Then, a faint, almost imperceptible shimmer passed over the iron. The chair gave a little twitch, like a sleeping dog dreaming of a rabbit. Mrs. Gable looked down, annoyed. “Is it loose? I thought you said this old thing was sturdy.”

“It’s not loose, Mrs. Gable,” Finch whispered, his eyes wide with a mixture of terror and triumph. “It is… levitating.”

As he spoke the words, the chair, with Mrs. Gable firmly upon it, lifted a clear six inches from the flagstones. It hung there for a moment, perfectly level, before beginning a slow, majestic ascent. Mrs. Gable’s complaint died in her throat. She looked down at the ground, then at the Professor, her face a mask of utter disbelief.

“Professor Finch!” she shrieked. “What have you done? Put me down at once!”

“I cannot!” he cried, running about beneath her. “The effect is self-sustaining! I have to reverse the polarity, or dampen the field, or… oh, good heavens!”

The chair had cleared the top of the garden wall and was now drifting over the adjacent property, where old Mr. Henderson was meticulously pruning his roses. He looked up, adjusted his spectacles, and stared as the impossible sight of Mrs. Gable, seated primly as if for afternoon tea, floated silently above his prize-winning delphiniums.

“Morning, Mrs. Gable!” he called out, in the robust tone of a man greeting a neighbour across a fence. “Bit of a breeze up there, is it?”

Mrs. Gable, finding her voice, let out a string of epithets that would have made a docker blush. The chair, indifferent to her distress, continued its unhurried journey, rising higher and drifting towards the rooftops of Wychford. Below, the quiet hum of the town began to change. A postman, looking up from his round, dropped his bag. A dog began to bark furiously at the sky.

Finch, frantic, ran back into his laboratory. There must be a way to recall it, to create a counter-agent. But his mind, usually so clear and methodical, was a whirlwind of equations and panicked calculations. He knew the theory, but the application was proving to be terrifyingly absolute.

Meanwhile, Mrs. Gable was having her own crisis. The initial terror was subsiding, replaced by a cold, clear-headed anger. She was floating over the rooftops of the town she had lived in all her life, and it was an undignified and wholly unacceptable state of affairs. She could see the slate roofs, the chimney pots puffing out lazy plumes of smoke, the church spire of St. Giles’ rising like a stone finger in the distance. Down in the market square, a small crowd was gathering, all faces turned upwards. She saw the butcher, Mr. Davies, and the baker’s wife, their mouths agape.

“Put me down, you great galumphing man!” she roared, shaking her fist at the heavens, as if gravity itself were a misbehaving servant. “This is no way to treat a lady!”
