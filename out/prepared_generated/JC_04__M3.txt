It was in the narrow, funereal shadow of the Rue des Pénitents that the illusion took hold of him, or rather, that he allowed himself to be taken hold of by it—a distinction of immense, of terrifying, moral weight, which Arkady Elutin carried within his breast like a leaden stone. The afternoon, enveloped in a damp, grey mist that seemed to exhale from the cold waters of the lake, had darkened the city into a mere arrangement of spectral shapes. The towering masses of slate roofs and the angular pediments of the old town loomed through the vapor like the broken ramparts of a deserted world. He walked wrapped in his threadbare overcoat, his shoulders bent under the familiar, crushing burden of his exile—a desolate, voiceless creature thrown up by the great wave of autocratic repression upon this immaculate, heartless foreign shore.

From the mouth of an alleyway, a figure had emerged, blocking his path with a quiet, deliberate obsequiousness. It was Valentin Kosk, a man whose sallow, clean-shaven face bore the permanent imprint of sleepless nights and fanatical certainties. Kosk’s eyes, dark and glowing with a somber fire, searched Elutin’s features with a strange, almost tender, intensity.

"You are expected tonight, Arkady Vassilyevitch," Kosk had murmured, his voice barely rising above the whisper of the rain upon the cobblestones. "At the printing house behind the clockmaker’s. We have the proofs. The Committee requires your final approval before the sheets go north."

Elutin had stopped, his heart giving a sudden, violent lurch against his ribs. He had looked at Kosk with the heavy, uncomprehending stare of a man suddenly confronted with a reality entirely out of joint with his own. The Committee. The printing house. The sheets. What did these words mean, projected thus upon the blank canvas of his solitude? He was no emissary of their violent dawn; he was a student of dead languages, a useless, broken survivor of a university expulsion, possessing no fire for the future, only a melancholy reverence for the dust of the past. He had been mistaken for another—a man of action, a conspirator of the dark, perhaps merely because he possessed the same foreign accent, the same air of hunted reserve, the same habit of walking alone in the unfrequented quarters of the town.

He had opened his mouth to utter the truth, to disavow the monstrous, baseless assumption. But the words died upon his lips. The chill of the Geneva evening, the profound, freezing isolation of his life, struck him with a sudden, paralyzing force. To speak was to cast himself back into the abyss. The trust in Kosk’s eyes—a trust fierce, absolute, and unreasoning—was the first warmth that had penetrated the ice of his exile since he had crossed the frontier. It was a warmth he had not sought, but which, descending upon him like an unmerited grace, intoxicated his starved senses. He had bowed his head, a slow, almost imperceptible inclination, and murmured a halting, ambiguous assent.

That had been three weeks ago. And now, sitting in the dim, oil-lit room behind the clockmaker’s shop, Elutin felt the noose of his own cowardice tightening around his throat. The air was thick with the smell of ink, damp paper, and the acrid sweat of clandestine labor. A press stood in the corner, a dark, brooding monster crouching in the shadows. Around the table, four men were gathered, their faces pale blurs in the unsteady light, their voices dropped to the low, rhythmic cadence of men discussing the fate of empires.

Vetch, the oldest among them, a man with a magnificent, patriarchal beard that seemed incongruous with his shabby, threadbare collar, was speaking. His words fell into the silence with the heavy finality of stones dropped into a deep well. They spoke of a courier, of a route through the Baltic ports, of a manifesto that was to awaken the slumbering masses. And they looked to Elutin. They waited for his word, for the nod of the head, the quiet phrase of approval that would set the invisible, terrible machinery into motion.

What did they see in him? He could not tell. Perhaps they saw the shadow of a reputation he had never made; perhaps they mistook his profound apathy for a stoic calm, his mental vacuity for a deep, strategic reserve. They credited him with a wisdom he did not possess, a courage that made his bowels churn with a sickening shame. He was a hollow vessel, and into his emptiness they poured the fiery wine of their dangerous hopes.

He listened to the murmur of their voices, the earnest, halting phrases, the foreign idioms of their borrowed French mingling with the harsh consonants of their mother tongue. A sudden wave of nausea swept over him. It was not fear of the police, not the dread of the Siberian mines that might await them all, but a moral vertigo, a ghastly realization of his own imposture. He was living a lie—a lie that fed upon their trust, their devotion, their very lives. And the tragedy of it was that he could not, he dared not, break the spell. To confess his imposture now would be a cruelty more terrible than his silence. It would shatter their faith, it would make their sacrifice seem ridiculous, grotesque. They had built upon him; to remove him would be to bring down the whole fragile structure of their conspiracy in a ruin of disillusionment.

And so he sat, a silent conspirator, a false god in a temple of his own unwitting creation. He listened to Vetch outlining the route, to Kosk detailing the distribution of the manifestoes, to the low, eager comments of the others. He nodded when they looked at him, he murmured a vague, non-committal phrase when they pressed him for an opinion. His voice, when it came, was a deadened, exhausted whisper, which they mistook for the gravity of a man weighed down by the burden of command.
