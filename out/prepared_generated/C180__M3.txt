The chandelier in Mrs. Bracken's drawing room threw a hundred shards of light across the plasterwork ceiling, and standing beneath it, Clara Vernon felt every mile of the muddy lane that separated her from Hazelford. She had been in London eleven days, and still the city pressed upon her like a physical weight — the clatter of hooves on cobble, the smell of coal smoke and tallow, the unending procession of carriages that creaked past the tall windows of Upper Brook Street. Her aunt's house was a handsome one, tall-stooped and white-fronted, with a green door and a brass knocker that the maid polished each morning until it winked. But Clara had not yet grown accustomed to the way sound carried through the walls — the servants' feet on the back stairs, the piano from the parlour below, the perpetual murmur of the street beyond.

Her aunt, Mrs. Imogen March, had welcomed her with an embrace that smelled of violet water and a smile that seemed to Clara both warm and practised. "You shall have a season, my dear," she had declared, holding Clara at arm's length in the hallway, "such a season as Hazelford could never provide. I have connections enough to see you properly brought out." Clara, who had spent the carriage ride imagining a kind of fairy-tale transformation, had found the reality more bewildering than enchanting. The modiste who arrived the following morning spoke in a rapid French that Clara could not follow, and the bills — which she glimpsed only because the woman left them on the side table while Mrs. March was called away to a caller — made her stomach clench. Eight guineas for a morning dress. Fourteen for an evening gown of white sarcenet with pearls worked along the hem. She thought of her father's ledger at Hazelford, the careful columns in his blunt hand, and the way he had frowned when she had mentioned the visit to London, though he had not forbidden it.

It was at the Beresfords' musical evening, four days after her arrival, that Clara met the Misses Pellering. They arrived together — three sisters in pale dresses, their hair dressed in the Grecian style that Clara had seen only in the fashion plates her aunt kept stacked on the writing desk. The eldest, Miss Aurelia Pellering, was perhaps twenty, with dark eyes and a mouth that curved naturally into an expression of amused condescension. She looked Clara over with the frankness of someone accustomed to appraising dress and bearing, and then smiled in a way that was not unkind but carried the unmistakable weight of bestowal.

"You are Mrs. March's niece," she said. It was not a question. "We had heard she had brought a young lady to town. You must come driving with us tomorrow. Mama's barouche is open at two, and we are to go to the Park."

Clara accepted before she had time to consider whether she ought to consult her aunt. She told Mrs. March afterwards, expecting pleasure or at least approval, but her aunt's face had tightened in a way Clara did not entirely understand.

"The Pellerings," Mrs. March said, in the careful tone she used when selecting her words as one selects fruit at a market, pressing each before committing, "are very well connected. Their mother was a Haversham. But you must be careful, Clara. Young ladies of that set have expensive habits, and they will expect you to share them."

Clara promised to be careful. But the drive the following afternoon was so delightful, and the Misses Pellering so easy in their friendship — piling her into the barouche between the two younger sisters, Cecily and Maria, and plying her with questions about Hazelford that made country life sound charming rather than dull — that she forgot the warning entirely. Aurelia pointed out the houses of note along Piccadilly with an air of proprietorship, nodding to acquaintances as they passed, and when they returned to Upper Brook Street she leaned forward and pressed Clara's hand.

"We mean to have you fitted for hats at Madame Celeste's. You cannot be seen in that — do forgive me — that shapeless thing. It does nothing for your colour. We shall go on Thursday."

The hat cost six guineas. Clara had not brought six guineas of her own. She mentioned this to Aurelia with the awkward honesty that was all she knew, and Aurelia had laughed — a bright, dismissive sound — and said, "Oh, but your aunt will stand the thing, surely. She is your sponsor. It is quite the thing. And anyway, Mama says Mrs. March has always been wonderfully — comfortable." There was something in the pause before the word, something Clara could not identify but felt as a faint chill, that she let pass because she did not know how to hold it.

Over the following fortnight, the Misses Pellering carried Clara into a current of expenditure that moved faster than any river she had known. There were ribbons from a shop in Bond Street that Cecily declared essential. There were gloves — four pairs, because one could not wear the same gloves to the theatre as to a morning call. There was a morning at the bazaar in Soho Square where Maria pressed upon her a reticule of beaded silk that cost more than a quarter's income from the Hazelford dairy. Each time Clara hesitated, the sisters laughed with such easy affection that refusal came to seem a kind of rudeness, a provincialism she was meant to have left behind. And each time she mentioned the cost to her aunt, Mrs. March's mouth thinned and she said, "Very well," in a voice that grew tighter with each repetition.

It was Cecily, the middle sister, who said it first, in the milliner's shop on Oxford Street, while Clara was trying on a bonnet of pale blue silk that Aurelia had insisted upon. Cecily was standing near the looking glass, turning her own bonnet in her hands, and Clara thought she was not attending, but then Cecily spoke in a voice pitched low enough that the milliner, folding muslin at the back of the shop, could not hear.
