The hall was insufferable. It was not the warmth alone—though the warmth was considerable, rising in slow, thick waves from the press of serge and broadcloth and the breathing of so many hundred bodies—but the smell of it, the smell of camphor and cigarettes and wool that had travelled, and underneath, faint and persistent, the odour of carbolic, which seemed to have entered the very grain of the seats and the walls and the hangings. Dorothea sat with her hands folded in her lap and her eyes upon the speaker, though she had long since ceased to follow his argument, which concerned, so far as she could tell, the membranes of the throat and their several inflammations, illustrated by a diagram so minute that it might have been a map of some remote and unvisitable country. Beside her, her brother Frederick dozed with the upright, precarious stillness of a man who believes himself awake; his chin would descend, and jerk, and he would blink and frown and fix his gaze upon the lecturer as though he had caught him in an error, and then, in a moment, descend again.

They had taken her for his wife. It had happened at the very first dinner, a long table of men and a scattering of women, and the woman on her left—Madame something, the wife of a professor from Lyon, a large, handsome, decided woman with a cameo at her throat—had said, in her comfortable French, *And you accompany your husband, madame, to these solemnities? How devoted.* And Dorothea had felt the colour come into her cheeks and had opened her mouth to explain, and then—why, she could not have said—had not explained. *It is very interesting,* she had said instead, *though I understand so little of the medicine.* And Madame had laughed and patted her arm and said that that was the best way, and had told her, with a frankness that seemed to Dorothea extraordinary, of her own troubles with her husband’s liver, and of her daughter who wished to study the piano in Paris, and of the price of butter. It was not that the words were remarkable; it was that they were addressed to her, to Dorothea, who at home sat at the end of the table and was asked to pass the salt, and answered, when the talk turned on the parish, that she had heard so, or that it was very sad, and was not expected to say more.

Now she looked sideways at Frederick, at the fine grey hairs upon his temple and the small, unoffending paunch beneath his waistcoat, and she thought, with a tenderness that was almost a pain, how little he knew. He would have said—he had said, indeed, on the train—that she would find it dull, that the papers were dry, that the French were long-winded; he had brought her, she knew, out of a vague kindness, because she had never seen Paris, and because their mother, who had been dead these seven years, had always said that Dorothea should see something of the world. And here was the world, this hot hall with its diagrams and its smells, and here was she, transformed, a wife, a woman to be confided in, a person whose opinion was asked upon the rearing of children and the management of servants, as though she had not spent her whole life in a stone house at the end of a lane, with the rain upon the windows and the clock ticking in the hall.

The lecturer had finished. There was a rustling, a pushing back of chairs, a great explosion of talk. Frederick woke fully and said, *Well, well,* and began to gather his papers together, though he had taken no notes. *Did you follow it, my dear?* he asked, without looking at her. *The part about the—the membrane—was very clear, I thought. Very clear.* She said that it was very clear. And then a man was standing before them—a tall, stooping man with a grey beard and spectacles that caught the light so that his eyes were hidden—and he was bowing, and speaking to Frederick, and Frederick was introducing him, and the name, which she did not catch, was followed by *my sister, Miss Ainsworth,* and the man said, *Ah, indeed,* and his spectacles flashed, and he passed on.

So it was over, that little fiction, and she found that she was sorry. She rose and took her brother’s arm and went out into the corridor, where the air was a little cooler, and there was a table with glasses and a great bowl of some red drink, and the men were crowding about it. *We might walk a little,* she said. *Before dinner.* And Frederick said, *By all means, by all means,* and they went out through the great doors into the evening, which was the colour of pale smoke, with the lamps just beginning to prick the blue, and the trees in the square standing motionless and dark against the fading light.

They walked slowly, for Frederick was tired, and she looked at the shops, at the hats and the stuffs and the little boxes of sweets, and thought of the woman at dinner, of her large, kind, indiscreet face, and of how she had said, *Ah, the men, they are all the same, they must be managed,* and had leaned nearer and lowered her voice, and Dorothea had felt a warmth, a kind of expansion, as though some tight band about her heart had been loosened. It was not true, what she had said; she had no husband to manage; she had only Frederick, who managed himself, or rather who was managed by the routine of the practice and the habits of thirty years. But for a moment, in that hot room, with the wine before her and the woman’s hand upon her sleeve, she had entered into a life that was not hers, and it had seemed to her that she understood it, that she had always understood it, and that it was only the accident of circumstance that had kept her outside.
