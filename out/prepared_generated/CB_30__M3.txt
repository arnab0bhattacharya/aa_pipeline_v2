The wind swept down from the mountain passes and roamed the streets of Varenburg like a hungry wolf. It battered the tall, narrow windows of the great hotel, shaking the heavy velvet curtains that draped the saloon where the English travellers gathered to ignore the foreign dusk. I stood apart by the mantelpiece, my hands cold despite the fire burning fiercely behind the iron grate. I was an Englishwoman, wholly dependent on the precarious wages afforded by a foreign pensionnat, and the chill of that dependence seemed to seep into my very marrow, a frost no continental fire could thaw.

Into this warmth and noise strode Mr. Edgar Lyle. He was a man whom fortune had spoiled, possessing a face of striking, almost severe regularity, and a bearing that proclaimed a total ignorance of the stings of want. He had been in Varenburg a fortnight, lingering on his grand tour, and in that time had singled me out from the crowd of expatriates. He approached my solitary post, his step light and assured, his grey eyes dancing with a restless, almost combative fire.

"You are moping again, Miss Arundel," he said, not unkindly, yet with a bluntness that set my teeth on edge. "You look at those coals as though you expect them to yield up a prophecy of doom."

"I am looking at the fire, Mr. Lyle, because it is the only thing in this room that possesses true vitality," I replied. "It consumes, and is consumed. It does not pretend."

"A cynical speech for so young a woman. But you are unhappy here. I have watched you. These continental manners are a cage to you; you pace it like a creature robbed of its native heath. Why do you stay?"

"You know very well why I stay. I have my bread to earn, and my passage to pay. Those of us who must serve cannot simply wander the earth for our amusement."

He leaned an elbow on the high mantel, bringing his face closer to mine. "Then do not serve any longer. Leave this place. I am for England next week, and I am in earnest when I say you should return with me. As my wife."

I looked at him sharply, searching for the jest. But his features, though smooth, bore no trace of open mockery. Yet I saw the glint of boredom in his eyes, the restless craving for a new sensation that drove him from capital to capital. I felt a sudden, fierce resentment. Was I to be a diversion, a balm for his ennui?

"You are very gallant, Mr. Lyle," I said, my voice low but hard. "But you are also very reckless. You would offer me a home because you are presently at a loose end, and because it amuses you to play the knight-errant to a woman in threadbare gloves."

"Threadbare they may be," he retorted, his tone quickening with the sting of my defiance, "but they cover a hand of iron. I offer you my name because I find I cannot endure the thought of your pale face fading in this dreary place. I am bored, you say? Perhaps. But boredom has driven men to great deeds. Do not let your pride turn you into a martyr."

"Martyrdom is preferable to the pity of a wealthy man," I flashed back, feeling the heat rise to my cheek. "You think because I am poor and solitary, I must leap at the chance to be taken up. You do not know me. I might die at your feet, Mr. Lyle, and you would only sigh that the spectacle lacked sufficient theatricality."

He laughed, a short, abrupt sound. "You are a little fury. You think I do not know what loneliness is? You think because my purse is heavy, I do not feel the chill of an empty room? I am as solitary as you, Miss Arundel."

"Then you propose we marry out of mutual desolation? A fine foundation for a life! You would grow tired of me in a twelvemonth, and I should be left to bear the weight of your indifference."

"You wrong me," he said, the jesting vanishing from his voice, replaced by a sudden, urgent seriousness. "I am not a boy to be captivated by a doll of wax and kid, to be discarded when the spring is over. I see a soul in you that mirrors my own unrest. We are both exiles. Come with me."

I trembled. The offer was a sudden, dazzling light, blinding in its intensity. England! The thought of it struck my heart with a physical pang—green fields, grey skies, the language of my birth, the freedom from this perpetual alien tongue. And yet, beneath the temptation, a cold fear clutched at me. I knew myself. I knew the gnawing hunger of my own heart, how it leapt toward him even as my tongue denied him. I feared that I was only lonely, and that loneliness was a false prophet, whispering of love where there was only desperation.

I turned my face toward the window. The snow had begun to fall, great white flakes driven by the wind, swirling in the darkness beyond the glass. The glass was cold against my forehead.

"You ask me to trust my life to a passing mood," I whispered. "You are a traveller, Mr. Lyle. You gather experiences as other men gather estates. I cannot be an experience."

"By heaven, you are stubborn!" he cried, striking his hand against the wood. "You would rather rot in this decay than accept an honest hand! What do you want, then? A man of sorrows, acquainted with grief, who will weep with you over the injustices of the world?"

"I want truth," I said, turning back to face him. "And I fear you do not know your own heart. You see me here, a solitary figure against a foreign background, and you mistake the contrast for an affinity. When we are both in England, in your own world, the illusion will vanish."
