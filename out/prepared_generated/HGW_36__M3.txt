It is a curious thing, how readily an educated man, once he is settled comfortably into his middle age, will surrender his common sense to a botanical novelty. My cousin, Dr. Edward Lane, had lived alone in his tall, narrow house on Heathside Rise in Hampstead for nearly fifteen years. He was a man of precise habits, having devoted his life to the study of terrestrial magnetism, and his routine was as regular as the ticking of the clock in his study. It was, therefore, a considerable disruption to his quiet existence when his former pupil, the explorer Captain Redwood, returned from the upper tributaries of the Amazon and called upon him carrying a wooden crate.

Redwood was a hearty, sunburnt man, full of impatient energy and contempt for the ordinary conventions of English life. He had no interest in the scientific method, but he possessed an aggressive, practical curiosity. He had brought back a specimen, he said, that he thought would intrigue the Doctor. It was a plant, though it behaved more like an animal. Redwood had found it in a swampy clearing near the Rio Javary, trapping insects in its leaves.

The Doctor opened the crate in his study, finding inside a thick, tuberous root from which sprouted several long, hollow, trumpet-like leaves, a deep, unhealthy green in color. The leaves were faintly irritable, curling inward when Redwood prodded them with his walking stick. The Doctor was immediately fascinated. He had Redwood place the pot on the writing-table beside the magnetic instruments, and he listened with the keenest attention while the Captain described the plant’s natural habitat and its peculiar diet.

Redwood stayed for an hour, talking rapidly and drinking two glasses of the Doctor’s sherry, before leaving his former tutor to examine the specimen. The Doctor spent the rest of the afternoon peering into the trumpets of the leaves, measuring their length, and making notes in his precise, small handwriting. He fed it a common bluebottle, which he caught on the window-pane, and he watched with a quiet, scientific triumph as the leaf slowly closed over the insect and digested it.

From that day forward, the plant became the sole occupation of the Doctor’s mind. He abandoned his magnetic observations. He read no reviews of scientific progress. He spent his mornings in the study, tending to the specimen, which he named, in honor of its donor, the *Redwoodia*. He fed it at first upon ordinary house-flies, which he trapped upon the window-glass. Then, as the plant grew with quite extraordinary rapidity, he found it necessary to provide larger meals. The leaves, which had initially been no more than six inches long, were soon a foot in length, and the green of them became a deep, rich purple, glistening with a sticky, sweet-smelling secretion.

The Doctor’s housekeeper, Mrs. Hackett, was a stout, practical woman of fifty, who had kept the house on Heathside Rise in spotless order since the Doctor’s wife had died. She was a woman of robust common sense, and she viewed the *Redwoodia* with a profound and growing suspicion. She was a person who liked things to be clean and ordinary, and she did not like the smell of the plant, which had begun to fill the study with a heavy, faintly sweet odor that she found disagreeable.

Mrs. Hackett had her own observations to make, and she made them to the Doctor one morning as she brought in his tea. "If you please, sir," she said, standing squarely by the door, "I think there is something wrong with that plant."

The Doctor looked up from his notes. He was a trifle annoyed. "Wrong, Mrs. Hackett? In what way wrong?"

"It is growing too fast, sir," she said. "And it is making a mess upon the writing-table. The leaves are dripping."

"That is a natural exudation," the Doctor said, with the slightly superior air of a scientific man addressing an uneducated person. "It is a secretion to attract insects."

"But it is not just attracting insects, sir," Mrs. Hackett replied, firmly. "It is attracting mice. I found two dead mice upon the floor this morning, and the leaves were reaching down over the edge of the pot."

The Doctor stared at her for a moment, and then he rose and went to the table. He examined the plant with close attention. He did not, Mrs. Hackett noticed, look at the dead mice, which she had placed upon a sheet of newspaper. He looked only at the leaves. "The leaves are certainly extending," he said. "It is a remarkably vigorous specimen. I shall have to give it a larger pot."

"It is not a larger pot it needs, sir," Mrs. Hackett said. "It is a smaller appetite. I do not like the look of it. It seems to me to be growing quite out of hand."

The Doctor smiled, a thin, indulgent smile. "You must not let your imagination run away with you, Mrs. Hackett," he said. "It is a plant, not an animal. It has no will. It merely grows according to its nature."

Mrs. Hackett said no more, but she went away with a strong sense of dissatisfaction. She had observed, in her practical way, that the plant had grown another two inches in the night, and that the leaves were now nearly two feet long. They were thick and fleshy, and the trumpets of them were wide enough to take in a man’s fist. The exudation was stronger, and the flies in the study were so numerous that they were a positive nuisance.

The Doctor, however, was entirely absorbed in his observations. He had begun to feed the plant upon raw meat, which he purchased from the butcher’s shop in the village. He would cut the meat into small pieces and drop it into the trumpets, and he would watch, with a quiet, scientific pleasure, as the leaves contracted and the meat slowly disappeared. He was, he told himself, conducting a valuable experiment.
