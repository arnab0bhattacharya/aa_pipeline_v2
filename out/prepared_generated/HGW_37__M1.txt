The trouble began, as so much of my trouble has begun, with my own face. I had landed in the central plaza of a city I took to be called Yrth, a place of such astonishing cleanliness and uniform light that it seemed less built than grown. The buildings, of some seamless, milk-white substance, soared to giddy heights, and between them moved a populace of people who all appeared to be of an identical age—somewhere between twenty-five and thirty. Their faces were smooth, unlined, and possessed of a placid, almost vacant beauty. They moved with an unhurried grace, their clothes simple and colourless, and the air hummed with a gentle, unidentifiable music.

I, by contrast, was a ruin. I had come a vast distance in time and space, and the journey had left its mark. My hair, which had been a respectable brown when I left my own century, was now a fine, silvery grey. My face was a testament to fifty-odd years of weather, worry, and experience: a map of lines around the eyes, deeper furrows on the brow, and a network of them around my mouth. I was, in short, a man who had lived. To these people, I was a monstrosity.

At first, there was only a curious silence. The citizens of Yrth stopped their smooth, gliding movements and simply stared. A young man and woman, holding hands, saw me and the woman let out a small, sharp intake of breath. The man pulled her back, his eyes wide with a horror I could not fathom. I had expected confusion, perhaps, or scientific curiosity. I had not expected revulsion.

“Good day,” I said, my voice sounding rough and cracked in the placid air. “I am a traveller. I mean you no harm.”

My words seemed to break the spell of silence and replace it with a growing panic. A child pointed a trembling finger. “The grey one! The ruin-face!” he cried.

An older man—or rather, a man who seemed to hold some authority, though his face was as smooth and unwrinkled as the rest—stepped forward. He held up a small, silver disc that emitted a soft chime. “Remain still,” he commanded, his tone not angry but profoundly disturbed. “You are… unwell.”

“I am quite well,” I said, trying to smile. “I am merely older than you. It is the natural way of things. We are born, we age, and we learn.”

A shudder went through the assembled crowd. “Learn?” the authority figure repeated, as if the word were a foul taste. “We learn through the Lattice. We do not learn through… decay. What is this condition? Is it a contagion?”

“Condition? Contagion? My dear sir, this is simply life! It is the dignity of experience written upon a man’s face. These lines,” I said, tracing a deep furrow on my brow, “are from years of concentration. These,” I gestured to my eyes, “are from laughter, and from sorrow. It is the record of a life fully lived.”

They recoiled as if I had brandished a weapon. The concept was utterly alien. A woman with hair the colour of polished copper clutched the arm of her companion. “It is a horror,” she whispered, loud enough for me to hear. “He has let himself become a ghost.”

A ghost! The word struck me with the force of a physical blow. They did not see a man; they saw a phantom, a walking corpse, a thing that ought to have been erased. I saw their own faces, beautiful but static, like portraits that had never known the passage of a single day. Theirs was a world without grandparents, without the softening of features that comes with a long marriage, without the proud, craggy visage of a man who has worked the land for a lifetime. They had traded the entire symphony of a human life for a single, perfect, unending note.

“You must understand,” I pleaded, my voice rising with a passion that only seemed to terrify them further. “You have halted the process, but you have halted the meaning as well! What is the point of a life that never changes? A face that never tells its story? You are not living; you are merely… persisting!”

The authority figure gestured, and several others moved forward, their movements suddenly sharp and purposeful. They carried silver discs like his own. “You are a disruption to the civic harmony,” he said, his voice now cold and clinical. “Your presence induces fear and cognitive dissonance. You must be purified.”

“Purified? You mean cured of my own life?” I cried, backing away. “You see age as a deformity, but it is the very shape of wisdom! You see these white hairs as a curse, but they are the crown of a life’s journey! You have conquered death, perhaps, but in doing so, you have murdered time itself!”

They did not listen. How could they? I was speaking a language they had forgotten, a poetry written in the flesh and bone of their ancestors. As the silver discs began to emit a low, insistent hum, I realised the true horror of their perfect world. They were not the ghosts. They were the statues. And I, with my wrinkles and my grey hair and my fifty years of living, was the only real man in a city of phantoms. I turned and ran, the cries of “The ghost!” echoing behind me, a man haunting a future that had no place for the past.
