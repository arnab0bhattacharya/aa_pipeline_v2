The heavy breath of the Malayan Sea, creeping up the winding reaches of the Pulau Mas estuary, carried with it the decay of submerged forests and the faint, acrid smell of smoke from the hillside. It was an atmosphere that seemed to weigh upon the very soul of the island, pressing a damp and oppressive finger upon the two white men who found themselves stranded there, looking at each other across the splintered boards of the dilapidated verandah with the bitter recognition of men irrevocably chained to a shared and inescapable destiny. For nearly six months the sea had remained an empty, glittering expanse of cruel indifference, yielding up neither the expected smoke of the supply steamer nor the slightest whisper of the outer world, leaving them alone in a silence that was profound, heavy, and thick with unspoken condemnations.

Mr. Warborough, the agent of the Bera Trading Company, stood with his hands resting upon the railing, his gaze fixed upon the muddy sweep of the current as it eddied past the rotting stakes of his private jetty. He was a man of considerable bulk, though the flesh seemed to have fallen away from him in those months of isolation, leaving behind a heavy, flaccid frame wrapped in a soiled suit of white duck. He had the appearance of a man who had been gradually crushed by the overwhelming futility of his commercial existence, his small, red-rimmed eyes staring out from beneath swollen and hairless lids with a sort of weary cynicism that seemed to penetrate the very pretenses of civilization. He was thinking, perhaps, of the thirty tons of gutta-percha and damar resin lying under the palm-leaf roof of his godown, of the unpaid drafts and the broken credit that must surely be ruining his reputation in the counting-houses of Singapore, but behind those material anxieties there was the dark, brooding consciousness of another presence upon the island, a presence that irked him with its silent persistence, its unyielding and abstract faith.

For beyond the strip of yellow sand, where the decaying piles of the jetty cast their lengthening shadows into the water, stood the mission house—a miserable structure of bamboo and attap, built upon a slight elevation of the shore, as if to keep its spiritual occupant physically removed from the sordid traffic of the coast. The Reverend Mr. Marten had been established there for some obscure number of years, sent out by a society of obscure enthusiasts whose subscriptions, gathered in the bleak chapels of the north, maintained his precarious foothold upon the edge of the heathen world. He was a man of fragile build, with a pale, ascetic face and large, dark eyes that seemed to look out upon the world with a sort of terrified wonder, as if he were perpetually confronted by some vast and incomprehensible iniquity which he was powerless to resist. His life was a silent, unending protest against the materialism of the trader, against the smoke of his opium, against the clink of his dollars, against the whole sordid mechanism of barter and exchange that seemed to him to be the very devil's work, carried on under the very shadow of the cross.

They had lived, for these long years, in a state of armed neutrality, their mutual dependence acknowledged but never spoken of, their mutual contempt hidden beneath a thin veil of formal civility that was often broken by sudden gusts of irritability and dark, brooding silences. Warborough, with the practical cynicism of his kind, regarded the missionary as a useless and interfering fool, a man who wasted his life in a vain attempt to uplift a race that was irrevocably committed to its own dark and primitive impulses. Marten, on his part, looked upon the trader as the embodiment of all that was greedy and predatory in the white man's character, a man who corrupted the natives with his rum and his firearms, who exploited their ignorance and their vices, and who, in the end, would probably die of his own excesses, leaving nothing behind him but a pile of empty cases and a lingering memory of evil. And yet, in the evenings, when the sun had set and the darkness had fallen upon the island, they would drift together, as if drawn by some invisible and irresistible force, to the verandah of the trading post, where they would sit in silence, listening to the murmur of the sea and the distant throbbing of the drums from the kampong, their two solitary figures huddled in the gloom like the last survivors of a doomed expedition.

Now, in the sixth month of their isolation, the tension between them had reached a pitch that was almost unbearable. The supply boat had failed, and with it had failed the thin thread that connected them with the world of their own kind. The rice had given out, and they were living upon the produce of the island—sago, fish, and the occasional tough and stringy flesh of a wild pig. Warborough’s stores of liquor were exhausted, and he sat now with a cup of black coffee in his hand, his nerves quivering with the want of his accustomed stimulant, his eyes roving restlessly over the empty sea. The missionary, too, felt the strain, for his supply of canned milk and tea, those small luxuries that made his exile endurable, had disappeared weeks ago, and he was reduced to a diet that was little better than that of the natives he had come to save.

“It is useless,” said Warborough, at last, breaking a silence that had lasted for nearly an hour, “to expect the steamer. She is not coming. There has been some trouble in the straits, or the company has failed, or the skipper has gone ashore in some devil's hole of a port and forgotten us. I tell you, Marten, we are forgotten men.”
