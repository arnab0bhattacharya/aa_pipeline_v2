It was upon the evening of Tuesday, the fourteenth of November, that the singular business of Lady Roderick Vane came to our attention. The autumnal fog, a yellow, choking presence, pressed against the windows of our Baker Street lodgings, and the fire had burned low. Holmes was engaged in a silent, abstracted study of his violin, drawing the bow across the strings in fitful, mournful sounds that spoke of a mind far from the music. My own engagement with a monograph upon the poisons of the East had been interrupted by the arrival of the evening post, which lay now upon the mantelpiece.

I was about to resume my reading when Holmes, with a sudden, impatient gesture, laid his instrument aside.

“It is no use, Watson,” he said, his voice sharp with a restless energy. “The mind will not be quieted. It is like a hound which has caught a scent but cannot yet discern the quarry. I am conscious of some factor, some trifling element, which eludes me.”

I did not venture to question him, for I knew that such confidences were forthcoming only when he was ready to impart them. I merely turned a fresh page, and in so doing, my eye fell upon the post. There was a letter upon a heavy, cream-coloured stock, addressed in a bold, feminine hand. Holmes, following my gaze, rose and took it.

“Lady Roderick Vane,” he read aloud. “A lady of some social prominence, I fancy. Let us see what she has to say.”

He slit the envelope and drew out a single sheet. As he read, his expression, which had been one of mere curiosity, hardened into a look of intense concentration. He held the paper closer to the lamp, his eyes scanning the lines, and then, with a sharp exclamation, he thrust it towards me.

“Read that, Watson. And observe the handwriting.”

The note was brief, written in a state of evident agitation.

“Mr. Sherlock Holmes,” it began. “I am in a position of the utmost perplexity and, I fear, of some danger. An event has occurred this evening which is so utterly inexplicable that I dare not confide in the police. A dinner party, of which I have no memory whatsoever of planning, is now taking place in my own house in Belgrave Square. The guests have arrived, the table is laid, and the invitations, Mr. Holmes, are in my own handwriting—a hand so perfect that even I am deceived by it. Among the guests is a man who is, to my certain knowledge, undergoing a five-year sentence for fraud at Dartmoor. I have sent my footman to you. I beg you, come at once, before the soup is served. I am in the library. Yours in desperation, Eleanor Vane.”

Holmes was already reaching for his coat.

“An invitation in one’s own hand to a dinner one has not planned!” he cried, his eyes alight with the thrill of the chase. “A guest from Dartmoor! This is a problem of a most fascinating character. Come, Watson, there is not a moment to lose. The soup waits for no man, and it would seem that neither does the solution to this mystery.”

A hansom cab rattled us through the fog-shrouded streets to the imposing facade of number seventeen, Belgrave Square. A flustered footman, his face pale in the gaslight, admitted us and led us at once to the library. Lady Vane rose to meet us. She was a handsome woman of perhaps forty-five, with a proud bearing that was now marred by an expression of acute distress. She was dressed for dinner in a gown of black silk, but her hands trembled as she gestured for us to be seated.

“Mr. Holmes, thank heaven you have come!” she said, her voice low but clear. “The situation is becoming unbearable. They are all in the drawing-room, behaving as if this were the most natural thing in the world. And I—I feel like a ghost at my own feast.”

“Pray compose yourself, Lady Vane,” said Holmes, in a soothing tone. “Your note was most explicit. Let us begin at the beginning. You say you have no memory of issuing these invitations?”

“None, I swear it! I awoke this morning with a headache and spent the day in this very room, seeing no one. At seven o’clock, my maid began to dress me for dinner. I asked her for whom, and she looked at me as if I were mad. ‘For your own party, my lady,’ she said. I was dumbfounded. I came downstairs and found the table laid for eight, the silver polished, and the cook in a state of high excitement. Then the guests began to arrive, each presenting an invitation card.”

“And the cards?” Holmes asked, leaning forward.

“Here.” She passed him a small pile from the blotter. “They are perfect. The wording, the paper, the script—it is all mine. But I did not write them.”

Holmes examined one closely, holding it to the light. “Remarkable,” he murmured. “The ink is fresh. There is not the slightest tremor of forgery. It is your hand, Lady Vane. And the guests? You say one is a convict?”

“A Mr. Silas Croft. I knew him in society some years ago, before his disgrace. He is here now, large as life, with a new name—Mr. John Carrington—and a story of having made a fortune in the Americas. But I saw his face in the papers when he was sentenced. It is he.”

“And the others?”

“The Marchioness of Blackwood, Sir Giles Pardoe, Mr. and Mrs. Alistair Finch—who are my dearest friends—and a Mr. Hargrave, a financier of some repute, whom I have met but once. And then there is you, Mr. Holmes. My guests believe you are the final member of the party.”

Holmes’s eyes gleamed. “A most audacious scheme. Someone has gone to extraordinary lengths to place us all in this room. Tell me, Lady Vane, has anything been taken? Any papers disturbed?”
