Young Henry Fearon woke on the morning of the twelfth of September with a tickle in his throat, and by noon he had begun to sneeze. This in itself was no great matter, for the men and women of the twenty-fourth century sneezed as readily as their ancestors had done, upon the inhalation of dust or pepper; but Henry Fearon sneezed thrice in succession, and then complained of a heaviness in the head, and then, most alarming of all, of a chill.

His mother, a brisk and competent woman of the sort that the new age produced in abundance, felt his forehead with the back of her hand and drew it away as though she had touched a hot iron.

“You are feverish,” she said, and her voice had a quality in it that Henry had never heard before—a note of something between horror and pity.

“I am sure it is nothing,” said Henry, who was twenty-three and worked in the Bureau of Atmospheric Regulation, and had never in his life experienced a day of sickness. “Perhaps I have eaten something disagreeable.”

But his mother was already at the telephone, and in a few minutes the face of Dr. Pemberton—who was not a doctor at all, but a Physiological Referee of the District Health Commission, a title that had replaced the older and more ominous one—appeared upon the little screen.

“Fever?” said the Referee, and his eyebrows went up. “You are certain?”

“His forehead is like a stove,” said Mrs. Fearon.

“Keep him isolated. I will come at once.”

The Referee arrived within the hour, a tall, clean-shaven man in the grey uniform of the Commission, carrying a small black case that had not been opened, so far as anyone could remember, in the whole of his professional career. He examined Henry with a kind of reluctant curiosity, taking his temperature with an instrument that had been designed for the purpose but never, in the Referee’s experience, called upon to register anything above the normal. The mercury—for the instrument was a quaint survival of the older physics—shot up to a hundred and one.

“This is extraordinary,” said the Referee. “Most extraordinary. I must communicate with the Central Board.”

“What is it?” asked Henry, whose head was now aching abominably. “What have I got?”

“That,” said the Referee, with an attempt at cheerfulness that sat ill upon him, “is precisely what we must determine. You are not to leave this room. You are not to admit anyone. Your food will be left at the door. I shall return within the day.”

He departed, and Henry, lying upon his bed with the afternoon sun upon the counterpane, felt himself the victim of some vast and incomprehensible joke. He had read of illness in the old books—the books that were still printed, though no one read them—and it had seemed to him a thing as remote as the siege of Troy or the Black Death of London. The human race, it was universally agreed, had conquered disease. The conquest had been so complete, so gradual, and so long ago that no one now living could remember the methods by which it had been achieved. There were no doctors, because there was no doctoring; there were no hospitals, because there was nothing to hospitalise. The great sanitary reforms of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, the elimination of the disease-bearing insects, the purification of water, the perfection of nutrition, the systematic breeding-out of hereditary weakness—these things had done their work so thoroughly that the very vocabulary of sickness had decayed. Men spoke of being “out of sorts” or “below par,” but of disease, of infection, of contagion, they spoke as they might have spoken of dragons.

And now Henry Fearon lay in his bed with a fever of a hundred and one, and the news of it ran through the district like a flame through dry grass.

By evening the street outside his window was empty. The neighbours had drawn their blinds. The grocer at the corner had put up his shutters. It was reported that the Committee of the Local Council had met in special session, and that a motion had been carried to petition the Central Board for the immediate removal of the patient to the Isolation Station—a grim brick building on the outskirts of the town that had been erected in the twenty-first century and had stood empty ever since, a monument to a terror that men had thought extinct.

“They mean to take you away,” said Mrs. Fearon, who had contrived to speak to him through the door, her voice muffled by the wood. “They say you are a danger to the community.”

“I am no danger to anyone,” said Henry, and sneezed violently.

“They say it is contagious. They say it is the old plague come back. Henry, Henry, what have you done?”

“I have done nothing,” said Henry, with a touch of petulance. “I have eaten my meals and done my work and breathed the air like anyone else. If I am ill, it is no fault of mine.”

But the machinery of the state was already in motion. The Referee returned at dusk with two assistants in white overalls, and they stood at the foot of the stairs and shouted up to Henry that he must prepare himself for removal.

“I will not be removed,” said Henry.

“You have no choice,” said the Referee. “It is the law. You are a focus of infection, and you must be isolated.”

“Then isolate me here. I am comfortable here.”

“The law is the law,” said the Referee, and there was a tremor in his voice that betrayed the uncertainty beneath his official manner. “We do not know what you have. We do not know how it is communicated. We do not know—forgive me—whether you will recover. The Isolation Station is equipped for the treatment of infectious disease.”
