There are, in the great and teeming hive of London, certain darker cells, certain forgotten combs, where the light of day and the light of law penetrate but seldom and with a feeble, sickly ray. Of such a nature was that ragged colonnade of brick and soot which supported the railway viaduct in Bermondsey, where the monstrous trains, those iron dragons of the modern age, roared overhead with a noise like the grinding of giant’s teeth, and shook down a perpetual dust of cinders upon the world below. Here, in this arched and clammy twilight, lived a society of outcasts; a brotherhood of outcasts, bound together not by the ties of blood, but by the stronger, coarser ties of hunger and of cold.

Their chief, their patriarch, their captain of this ragged band, was a boy of some fourteen years, though he had the knowing, wizened face of a man of forty. He was known to the constables and the pawnbrokers as Wizened Jem, and he wore his rags with the air of a monarch in his coronation robes. His subjects were the Screeching Rat, a small, quick creature with eyes that were always looking for the policeman; and Silent Sam, who was deaf to the cries of the world but could smell a dropped halfpenny from a hundred yards. And then there was the child.

She had come to them in the winter, a waif of the fog and the frost, a little creature with hair the colour of dust and eyes as wide and blue and uncomprehending as the sky in summer. She could not tell them her name, nor where she had come from, nor how she had come to be lost. She knew nothing, save that she was cold and that she was hungry. Jem, whose heart was a hard nut with a small, soft kernel at its centre, had taken her in. “She’s a good-luck charm,” he had declared to the Screeching Rat. “A girl-brat brings in the pennies. The toffs and the old women, they pity a girl-brat.” And so she had become one of them, a silent, wide-eyed member of their strange family, whom they called the Little Un.

They fed her with the proceeds of their industry—a beggar’s whine, a boot-black’s rag, a quick-fingered pilfering of a baker’s tray. They clothed her in the cast-off rags of their own cast-off rags, and they slept around her in a heap of humanity, a pile of breathing, shivering warmth against the damp brickwork of the arch. She was their treasure, their doll, their good-luck charm. They did not love her, for they knew not the word for it, but they kept her, and they fed her, and no boy of their gang was permitted to lay a rough hand upon her.

One afternoon, when a watery sun was trying to burn a hole through the London smoke, a disruption occurred in the settled economy of the arches. A lady, a real lady, from the great world of bonnets and carriages, was seen to be making enquiries in the neighbourhood. She was a tall, elegant person, all in black silk, with a face as pale and sad as a winter moon. She stopped the Screeching Rat as he was scuttling from the fishmonger’s, and the boy, overcome by the sheer magnificence of her, could only point a trembling finger towards the viaduct.

The lady came. She stood at the mouth of their cathedral of soot, a vision of impossible finery, her skirts brushing against the filth of the pavement. The boys, who had been engaged in the division of a stale loaf, froze in their places, their mouths full, their eyes wide with a mixture of terror and awe. The lady’s gaze fell upon the Little Un, who was sitting on a pile of sacking, her dusty hair catching a stray beam of light.

“Oh, my dear!” the lady cried, her voice a melody of sorrow and of hope. “My lost little darling! Is it you?”

The child looked up, her blue eyes clouded with the same blank confusion that always filled them. She did not move. The lady took a step forward, her gloved hands outstretched. “Do you not know me, my pet? I am your mama!”

The word, ‘mama,’ fell upon the ears of the boys like a stone into a still pond. It was a word from another world, a world of parlours and prayers and pillows. Wizened Jem, his small, hard face a mask of suspicion, stepped forward, placing his ragged person between the lady and the child. “She’s ours,” he said, his voice a low growl. “We found her. She’s our Little Un.”

The lady’s sad face was now a study in bewilderment and distress. “Yours? But she is my child! Her name is Eleanor! She was lost from her nursemaid in the great crowd at London Bridge. I have searched and searched! I have not slept! Oh, you poor, poor boys,” she said, her pity flowing over them like a warm, unwelcome bath, “you must have cared for her. And I will reward you. I will give you each a sovereign. A golden sovereign, to do with as you will!”

A golden sovereign! The words were a magic incantation. The Screeching Rat’s eyes grew as round as shillings. Silent Sam, who could smell a halfpenny, could practically taste the gold. A sovereign was a fortune. It was a kingdom. It was a new life.

That night, a council was held under the arch. The Little Un was asleep, her small form curled in the nest of rags. The three boys sat in a circle, the phantom gold glittering in their minds.

“A sovereign each!” whispered the Screeching Rat, his voice trembling with avarice. “We could live like lords! We could have meat pies every day! We could buy coats! Real coats, with buttons!”

“And what of her?” Jem asked, nodding towards the sleeping child. “She’d be gone. Off to her own kind. To a life of fireplaces and milk.”
