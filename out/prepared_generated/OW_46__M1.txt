Lord Arthur Devereux had always considered virtue to be a form of ill-health, and philanthropy a very tedious species of self-advertisement. His life had been a masterpiece of exquisite uselessness, devoted entirely to the pursuit of the beautiful, the expensive, and the utterly immoral. However, when Dr. Arbuthnot, a man with the face of a melancholic undertaker and the bedside manner of a bailiff, pronounced the sentence of death upon him—giving him, with a precise lack of optimism, exactly twelve months to live—Arthur’s views underwent a sudden and violent revolution. He resolved, in a fit of tragic grandeur, to die as a saint, since he had lived entirely as a sinner. The difficulty, he soon discovered, was that good deeds are far more difficult to execute than bad ones. Bad deeds require only a lack of scruple and a certain amount of loose change; good deeds demand a degree of tact, patience, and social courage that Arthur had never been called upon to cultivate.

He began his crusade in London, where the winter fogs provided a suitably gloomy backdrop for his spiritual renunciation. He decided to start small, with a gesture of pure, unadulterated benevolence towards a lady of the stage. Miss Fanny Featherstone, a creature of pink satin and painted smiles, had long been Arthur’s most expensive amusement. He resolved to set her up in a respectable trade, thereby saving her soul at the cost of his own pleasure. He had heard that the manufacture of jam was a pious and profitable occupation.

‘Fanny,’ he said, as he sipped a pale green liqueur in her dressing-room, surrounded by the wreckage of a bouquet of orchids, ‘I have decided that you must leave the stage.’

‘Oh, Arthur, don’t be absurd,’ she replied, examining a diamond bracelet with a critical eye. ‘I have just been offered a new part. I am to play a duchess who elopes with a bishop. It is sure to be a scandal.’

‘That is precisely why you must leave it,’ said Arthur, with the gravity of a martyr. ‘I intend to buy you a shop. A jam shop. In Kensington. You will wear a neat black dress and a white apron, and you will sell preserves to the clergy. It is the only way to ensure your future.’

Miss Featherstone looked at him as though he had suggested she take up the occupation of a charwoman. ‘A jam shop? And what am I to do with my diamonds? Wear them in the sugar-boiling? Arthur, you are demented.’

‘It is for your good,’ he insisted.

‘My good?’ she cried, flinging a powder-puff at him. ‘My good lies in being admired, not in being sticky! If you wish to be a grocer, Arthur, do it yourself. I shall remain an artist.’

Arthur left the theatre in a state of profound depression. He had offered a woman the pearl of respectability, and she had preferred the paste of pleasure. It was a most discouraging beginning to a life of virtue.

He next turned his attention to his cousin, Cecil Vane, a young man of exquisite sensibilities and appalling debts. Cecil was currently languishing in a sponging-house, having been arrested for an unpaid bill for a set of Sèvres porcelain. Arthur, determined to be a saviour, rushed to the prison with a cheque-book and a sermon.

‘Cecil,’ he said, as he bailed the trembling youth out of the gloom, ‘I have saved you. But you must reform. You must give up your extravagance and marry a woman of solid worth. I have arranged a match for you with Miss Amelia Pringle, the daughter of the soap manufacturer. She is plain, she is pious, and she has a fortune of fifty thousand pounds.’

Cecil shuddered, his delicate hands clutching the iron railings of the prison gate. ‘Arthur, you cannot be serious. Miss Pringle has the figure of a bolster and the conversation of a Methodist tract. To marry her would be a fate worse than death.’

‘Death is what awaits me,’ said Arthur, with a tragic sigh. ‘And I wish to leave the world better than I found it. You will marry her, Cecil. It is your duty.’

‘My duty is to my own aesthetic instincts,’ cried Cecil, with a flash of spirit. ‘I would rather rot in prison than live in a house that smells of soap and sanctity. You have not saved me, Arthur; you have merely proposed a different kind of cell.’

Once again, Arthur’s philanthropy had been spurned. He was beginning to find that the world was strangely attached to its own imperfections. The poor refused to be uplifted, the wicked refused to be reformed, and the beautiful refused to be made useful. It was a very selfish age.

Disheartened by the ingratitude of London society, Arthur decided to seek a more receptive audience for his benevolence among the peasantry of the Riviera. He travelled to a small village near Nice, where the sun blazed down upon the lemon groves and the poverty was picturesque. There he found a young fisherman named Jean, who was in love with a girl named Marie, but could not marry her because he lacked a boat.

‘I shall buy you a boat,’ announced Arthur, scattering gold coins like confetti. ‘I shall buy you a boat, and nets, and a cottage with blue shutters. You shall be the happiest fisherman in France.’

Jean looked at the gold, then at Arthur, and then at the sea. ‘Monsieur,’ he said slowly, ‘if I have a boat, I must work. If I have nets, I must mend them. If I have a cottage, I must repair the roof. Today, I have nothing, so I lie in the sun and watch the waves. It is the only thing I am good at. Your gift would be a burden.’

‘You are lazy,’ said Arthur, annoyed.

‘I am free,’ corrected the fisherman. ‘And I am happy. You wish to make me rich so that I may be as miserable as you. Please, Monsieur, keep your gold. I prefer my hunger to your dinner.’
