The rain had stopped by the time Miss Fuller reached the bottom of the lane, leaving the cobbles slick and the air sharp with the smell of wet stone and coal smoke. She carried a basket on her arm, its contents wrapped in a clean cloth—a small jar of calves’-foot jelly, a quarter-pound of tea, and two oranges that had cost her more than she cared to admit. The oranges were for old Mrs. Pemberton, who had a cough that would not shift and a fondness for anything that came from abroad.

Miss Fuller had been visiting the poor of St. Cuthbert’s for three years now, ever since her father’s death had left her with a modest income and an excess of time. She was not a sentimental woman, but she had a conscience that operated with the precision of a clock, and it had told her, quite clearly, that a person of her means and education had no business sitting at home with a novel when there were children in the courts off Mill Street who had never tasted an orange in their lives.

The parish was not a large one, and its affairs were conducted from a small vestry room behind the church, where the Reverend Mr. Ellery could usually be found on Tuesday and Thursday afternoons, surrounded by ledgers and the accumulated dust of forty years’ ministry. He was a widower, childless, and had baptised, married, and buried most of the families in the town. He was also, as everyone knew, a little vague. He forgot names, forgot faces, forgot whether he had already visited a sick parishioner or merely intended to. But he was kind, and his kindness was of the sort that did not require remembering—it was simply there, like the church itself, a fact of the landscape.

The curate, Mr. Wickham, was a different matter. He was thirty-five, thin, with a way of smiling that suggested he knew something you did not. He had come to St. Cuthbert’s six years ago, and in that time he had made himself indispensable. He kept the accounts, managed the poor relief, corresponded with the bishop, and saw to the fabric of the church. The Reverend Mr. Ellery, who had never had a head for figures, had handed over the ledgers with relief and never asked to see them again.

Miss Fuller had never liked Mr. Wickham. She had told herself it was a prejudice, the sort of irrational dislike that arises between two people of similar temperament, and she had done her best to ignore it. But on a Tuesday in late October, she had found herself in the vestry room at an hour when she had expected it to be empty, and she had seen something that she could not ignore.

The ledger was open on the table. Mr. Wickham was not there—she had heard him in the church, arguing with the sexton about a broken window—and she had only meant to wait for the Reverend Mr. Ellery, who had promised her a list of the parish’s widows. But the ledger was open, and her eye fell on it, and she read, quite without meaning to, the entry for the previous quarter’s poor relief.

She knew the sum. She knew it because she had helped to raise it. There had been a subscription in the spring, and she had gone from door to door with a list, and the total had been entered in the churchwardens’ book in her presence. The figure in the ledger was less than half of that.

She stood very still. The rain dripped from the eaves outside. She turned the page. There were other entries—for coal, for blankets, for the burial of a pauper child—and the figures were wrong. Not wrong in a way that could be explained by carelessness. Wrong in a way that suggested a hand had been at work, subtracting here, adding there, making the columns balance at the bottom while the money itself had gone somewhere else.

She closed the ledger. Her hands were shaking. She put on her gloves, though she had not taken them off, and she went out into the rain.

For three days she said nothing. She visited Mrs. Pemberton and gave her the oranges. She attended the Thursday service and sat in her usual pew, and she watched Mr. Wickham read the lesson in his clear, confident voice, and she thought about the burial of the pauper child, whose name she had found in the register, and who had been buried in a plain deal coffin because there had been no money for anything better.

She thought about the Reverend Mr. Ellery. She thought about how he would look when she told him. She had seen that look before—on the face of a woman whose husband had been taken for debt, on the face of a man whose son had run away to sea. It was the look of a person who has believed in something and has been told, gently and with evidence, that the belief was misplaced. It was not a look she wished to cause.

But she thought, too, of the widows who had received less coal than they had been promised, and of the children who had gone hungry in a parish that had raised money to feed them. She thought of the ledger, and the careful, patient hand that had made the columns balance.

On the fourth day, she went to the vicarage.

The Reverend Mr. Ellery received her in his study, which was warm and cluttered and smelled of old books and tobacco. He was a small man, stooped, with white hair that stood up in tufts, and spectacles that were always slightly askew. He offered her tea, which she refused, and then he sat down in his chair and looked at her with an expression of mild and expectant kindness.

“Miss Fuller,” he said. “You have the look of a woman with something on her mind.”
