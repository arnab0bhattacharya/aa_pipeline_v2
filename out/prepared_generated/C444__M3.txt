The chandelier threw a thousand fractured lights across the shoulders of the guests, and the Hon. Philip Bassett wished, for the dozenth time that evening, that the floor might open and swallow him into the sewers. He stood beside a marble column in Lady Whitchurch's drawing room, holding a glass of claret he had not tasted, watching the great machine of society turn. He was twenty-six, a curate of no particular distinction from a parish in Hampshire, and he had been invited to this gathering only because his uncle, the Bishop, had felt it politic that someone from the family attend Lady Whitchurch's reception for the returning African explorer, Captain Conrad Glanville.

Philip had met the Captain once, years ago, at a garden party. He had thought him a boor. But he had also thought, standing in the warm light of the afternoon, that anyone who could command such attention simply by entering a room must possess some quality he himself entirely lacked. He had thought about it often since.

A woman appeared at his elbow. She was handsome, perhaps thirty, with dark hair and the expression of someone who has been bored for a very long time and has begun to find it amusing.

"You are he, aren't you?" she said. "Captain Glanville?"

Philip opened his mouth. What he meant to say was: *No, I am the Bishop of Winchester's nephew, a curate, entirely unremarkable, please forgive me.* What he said was nothing. The woman took his silence for confirmation.

"I thought so. I recognized you from the sketch in the *Graphic*. Lady Whitchurch said you would be here, though she is so busy tonight she has quite forgotten to announce you." She extended her hand. "I am Mrs. Aldous Crane. My husband has interests in the Gold Coast. I have been desperate to meet you."

Philip took her hand. It was gloved. He released it quickly, as if it might burn him.

"Madam," he said.

"Come," she said. "There are people who will want to hear your account. Lady Whitchurch has been promising it all week."

Before he could protest, she had drawn him into the center of the room, where a cluster of chairs had been arranged around a handsome fireplace. Several guests were already seated. They looked up at him with expressions ranging from curiosity to a kind of hungry expectation. Philip recognized among them Sir Clement Ffoukes, a man whose name was attached to half the railway schemes in the colonies, and Lady Whitchurch herself, vast and corseted, presiding like a galleon in silks.

"Captain Glanville," Lady Whitchurch said. "How splendid. We were just speaking of the interior. Mrs. Crane tells us you have penetrated further than any European alive. Is that so?"

Philip felt the room contract around him. The claret in his glass trembled. He set the glass on the mantelpiece and found his hands were shaking, so he pressed them together, as if in prayer, which was, he supposed, what they were.

"I have been fortunate," he said.

"Fortunate!" Sir Clement leaned forward. "Come now, Captain. We want more than that. Lady Whitchurch has promised us wonders."

Philip looked at their faces. They wanted a story. He was a curate. He knew about stories. Every Sunday he stood before a congregation and gave them one, and they believed it, or pretended to, which amounted to the same thing. He took a breath.

"The river," he said, "which the Portuguese call the São Bonifácio, but which the people of the interior call by a name I cannot pronounce without offending Lady Whitchurch's guests—" Here he paused, and Mrs. Crane laughed, and the laugh gave him courage. "The river leads inland for perhaps six hundred miles before it reaches a series of cataracts. Beyond the cataracts the country changes. The forest gives way to a kind of high plateau, very green, very cool, unlike anything one imagines when one thinks of Africa. It is like Surrey, if Surrey were infinite."

"Surrey," Sir Clement repeated, disappointed.

"But populated," Philip continued, warming to his own voice now, feeling the strange thrill that came sometimes in the pulpit when he knew the sermon was working. "The people of the plateau call themselves the Maravi — no, that is not quite right — the Mavari. They are tall. Extraordinarily tall. And they have built a city there, of stone, not unlike something one might see in the Levant. Terraces. Gardens fed by aqueducts. I confess I was not prepared for it."

"You are describing a civilization," Lady Whitchurch said.

"I am describing what I saw," Philip said, and was surprised to find that this felt true, in some way, as if the place existed not in the world but in the space between his words and their belief, and was real precisely because they wanted it to be.

"Their king," he went on, "is a man called Odibo. He is old. He has ruled for forty years. When I was brought before him, he asked me — through an interpreter, one of the coastal men who had traveled with me — he asked me what I wanted. And I said, because I did not know what else to say, that I wanted nothing. That I had come only to see."

"And what did he make of that?" Mrs. Crane asked. She was leaning forward now, her earlier boredom quite gone.

"He thought it was very strange. He said that in forty years no man had come to his court wanting nothing. He said he did not believe me. But he gave me leave to stay, and I stayed for — I stayed for some months."

The room was very quiet. Philip could hear the clink of glasses from the far side of the drawing room, the murmur of other conversations, but here, in this circle of chairs, there was only his voice and the fire.
