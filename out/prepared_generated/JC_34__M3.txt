There are moments in the sun-smit, stagnant waters of the world when the very air we breathe seems heavy with the emanations of a blind, senseless fatality. It was in such a moment that I found myself lingering on the decaying masonry of the custom-house wharf at Sulaco, watching the last British tramp steamer of the month preparing to warp out into the blinding glare of the Gulf. The heat was immense, a palpable oppression that seemed to stifle not merely the breath, but the moral capacity of man. The town behind me—the white, staring houses, the heavy church towers, the crumbling fortifications—lay prostrate under it, as if drained of life by the relentless light. One might have thought it a city abandoned to the silence of death, had it not been for the faint, irregular popping of musketry far away in the hills, where the shadows of the Sierra de la Mina gathered a purple, sullen density.

I had been sent out there by the company to superintend the harbour scheme—the dredging of a deep-water channel and the construction of a massive concrete pier intended to break the fury of the northern gales. It was a work of civilization, of progress, of that stubborn, material optimism which we of the Northern latitudes carry with us into the tropics, as though a few thousand tons of imported cement could bind the passions of men to the service of a peaceful commerce. I had believed in it, in my own sober, mechanical fashion. But the scheme, like everything else in that feverish republic, had fallen into the shadow of a sudden political convulsion.

General Guzman, the Minister of Public Works who had signed our concession with a flourish of grandiloquent rhetoric, had raised the standard of revolt against the central authority in the capital. The harbour works were suspended. The labourers had vanished into the hills, carrying with them the picks and shovels of the company, to dig, I suppose, the trenches of a different sort of enterprise. And Guzman’s daughter was in the town.

Her name was Senorita Rosa, and she had a face of a tragic, austere pallor, with eyes that held the profound, unquiet stillness of deep water. I had met her first in the dim, shaded coolness of the Intendencia, when her father’s star was at its zenith, and I had been foolish enough, or blind enough, to imagine that the shadowy, enigmatic glance she turned upon me from beneath the black lace of her mantilla held a meaning that transcended the mere exchange of common civilities. She had spoken little, but her silences had seemed to me charged with a delicate, appealing confidence. I had constructed a romance of it—a Northern engineer, grave, competent, bringing order to a chaotic land, and the daughter of the South, looking upon him as the harbinger of a better, more stable era. It was a presumptuous illusion. She was the daughter of her race, and her quietness was but the mask of a profound, ineradicable fatalism.

Now her father was an outlaw, and the town, held by a meagre garrison of government troops, waited in a breathless, cowering suspense for the inevitable crash of the revolutionary storm. The streets were empty; the foreign residents had either fled to the shipping or barricaded themselves in their warehouses. I alone lingered, ostensibly to safeguard the company’s property, but in reality because I was under the spell of an obscure, fascinating infatuation. The steamer was going. The captain, a gruff, red-faced man from Cardiff, had sent me a laconic message that he would wait no longer than four o'clock. It was now half-past three.

I walked slowly along the wharf, my footsteps echoing hollowly on the sun-baked planks. The water below was thick, dark, and still, exhaling a faint, noisome smell of decay. Suddenly, I saw her. She was standing at the corner of the ruined guard-house, dressed entirely in black, as if mourning for something not yet dead. She did not move as I approached; she seemed to be gazing out over the glittering, desolate expanse of the Gulf, as though she expected something to arise from its depths.

"Senor," she said, before I could speak, her voice carrying that harsh, vibrating quality I had learned to listen for with a strange, painful eagerness, "the ship is leaving."

"Yes," I said, haltingly, like a man confronted with an accusation. "In half an hour. I came to tell you. I came to say goodbye."

She turned her head slowly and looked at me. Her eyes were dry, luminous, and terribly searching.

"You go," she murmured. "It is well. You go back to your country, where things are safe, where men build their walls against the sea and do not fear the breaking of them. You leave us to our fate."

There was no bitterness in her tone, only a profound, weary resignation. Yet, for me, it was as if she had struck me. The blood rushed to my face.

"I am an engineer," I protested, weakly, as though defending my virtue. "My work is here. The pier is not half finished. The channel—"

"Your work," she repeated, and a faint, ironical smile touched the corners of her lips—a smile of infinite sadness, of a wisdom older than the stones of the city. "Your work is of the sea, and of the rock. But we are of the earth, Senor. We are rooted in the blood, and the mud, and the fire. Your father—" I stopped, appalled at my own banality.

"My father is a man of destiny," she said, with a sudden, fierce pride that flared up and died in an instant, leaving her face more desolate than before. "He fights. He suffers. It is our way. But you—you are not of us. You must not stay. It is not your quarrel."
