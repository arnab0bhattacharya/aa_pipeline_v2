The fog came upon London not as a visitor but as an occupier. It arrived on a Tuesday in the third week of November, and by the Friday it had ceased to be weather at all and had become a condition of existence. It was not the yellow, greasy fog of tradition, the sort that bites the throat and blackens the handkerchief, but something paler and more obstinate, a vast, slow precipitation of opacity that seemed to have its source not in the Thames or the chimneys but in some profound and general failure of the air itself. It filled the streets to the height of the first-floor windows and lay upon the city like a blanket laid upon a cage. The lamps burned in it all day, small, useless haloes of orange that served chiefly to make the darkness visible. Traffic crawled; then, in the second week, it stopped. The ’buses ceased to run, the hansoms vanished, and the great railway termini stood silent, their signals invisible, their engines cold. Trade, which is the blood of London, thickened and then congealed.

It was into this white and stifling silence that Mr. Alfred Pettigrew, a junior clerk of the Consolidated Provincial and Westminster Bank, wandered on the evening of the ninth day, and it is to his eternal credit—or perhaps his eternal shame—that he did not at once turn back. He had been sent out, in the general confusion, upon an errand of no importance whatever, and having lost his bearings at the corner of a street he could not name, he had struck out with the blind confidence of the utterly unimaginative. He was a small man, neat, with a face like a startled rabbit and a mind that ran in grooves as narrow and as deep as the ruts of a country lane. He had no opinions, only habits; and his habit, when lost, was to keep moving. Thus it was that he came, by degrees, to the neighbourhood of Clerkenwell, and thus it was that he heard, through the muffling fog, the sound of voices raised in argument.

The sound came from a grating at the level of the pavement, and from the grating a faint, warm light ascended. Mr. Pettigrew, whose curiosity was as timid as his person, paused. He heard a word—"proletariat"—and then another—"barricade"—and these words, though he did not understand them, had about them a quality of urgency that drew him. He looked about him. The street was empty; the fog was absolute. He knelt, not without a certain difficulty, for his trousers were tight, and peered through the grating. He saw a cellar, lit by a single gas-jet, and in the cellar some seven or eight men seated upon boxes and upturned crates about a deal table. They were of the sort that Mr. Pettigrew, in his own mind, classified as "not quite"—not quite gentlemen, not quite working men, but a sort of shabby-genteel, bearded, argumentative class that he had encountered occasionally in the reading-room of the British Museum and had always avoided. One of them, a large man with a mane of grey hair and a voice like a foghorn, was speaking.

"The moment is upon us," he was saying. "The bourgeoisie are blind, literally and figuratively. The whole machinery of their commerce is stopped. Their police are helpless, their soldiers cannot march. What is to prevent us, I ask you, from seizing the instruments of production and establishing the Cooperative Commonwealth?"

"Everything," said a thin man with spectacles. "The fog will lift. It always lifts. And then we shall be hanged, and very properly."

"Coward!" said the large man.

"Realist," said the thin man.

Mr. Pettigrew, listening, felt a strange and uncomfortable sensation. It was not fear, exactly, though fear was present; it was rather the sensation of a man who has inadvertently stepped into a play and finds himself upon the stage. He was about to withdraw, to creep away into the fog and forget the whole affair, when his foot slipped upon the wet stone. He made a small, involuntary sound—a sort of squeak—and the voices below ceased instantly.

"Who's there?" cried the large man.

Mr. Pettigrew, paralysed, said nothing.

"Come down and show yourself!" said the large man. "We are all friends here."

This was so evidently untrue that Mr. Pettigrew, with a desperate courage, rose to his feet and began to walk away. But he had gone no more than three paces when a hand gripped his arm. He turned and found himself looking into the face of the large man, who had ascended the cellar steps with a speed surprising in one of his bulk.

"A spy!" said the large man.

"I—I assure you—" stammered Mr. Pettigrew.

"Come down," said the large man, and Mr. Pettigrew, feeling that resistance was futile, came down.

The cellar was warm and smelt of damp and tobacco. The men about the table regarded him with a mixture of suspicion and curiosity. Mr. Pettigrew, standing in the midst of them, his bowler hat in his hands, felt himself to be the most conspicuous object in London.

"Who are you?" said the thin man with the spectacles.

"Pettigrew," said Mr. Pettigrew. "Alfred. I am a clerk. At a bank. I was lost in the fog."

"A bank clerk!" said the large man, with a wealth of contempt in his voice. "A lackey of the bourgeoisie!"

"I—I only keep the ledgers," said Mr. Pettigrew.

There was a pause. The men looked at one another. Then the thin man, whose name was Mr. Wainwright, said: "He is evidently harmless. Let him go."

"No," said the large man, whose name was Mr. Blunt. "He has seen us. He knows our faces. He might betray us."

"I wouldn't dream of it," said Mr. Pettigrew, with perfect truth.

"Then you must join us," said Mr. Blunt. "You must be one of us. It is the only security."
