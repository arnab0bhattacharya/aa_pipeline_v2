The shop of Messrs. Crumble and Crumble—for so the faded gilt letters above the door declared it, though the second Crumble had never in his life weighed out a pound of tea nor set his hand to the bacon-knife—stood at the lower end of Chatham High Street, in that part of the town where the pavement is worn into hollows by the tread of sailors and the shop-windows are dim with the breath of the river. It was a little, low-browed, bow-windowed establishment, of the sort that seems to stoop forward as if to peep at the passers-by, with a door that had a bell above it which rang with a cracked and confidential tinkle, as though it were whispering a secret to the whole street. Within, the air was compounded of coffee and candle-grease, of cheese and brown soap, of herrings and hartshorn; and the shelves were so closely packed with jars and canisters and boxes that the whole shop wore the appearance of a stout old gentleman buttoned to the chin in a many-pocketed waistcoat.

Here dwelt Tobias Crumble, the elder brother, a man of five-and-forty, round-shouldered from much stooping over ledgers, with a face as mild and as wrinkled as a winter pippin, and a habit of rubbing his hands together as though he were perpetually washing them of some imaginary stain. He rose at five, swept the shop himself, took down the shutters with a clatter that was the earliest sound in Chatham, and kept his accounts in a hand so neat that it might have been engraven on a tombstone. He was, in short, a man who had made a religion of the grocery business, and who regarded a well-kept ledger as a sort of moral exercise, and a bad debt as a sin against Providence.

His brother Nathaniel—called Nat by everybody, and Nat by himself most of all—was a young gentleman of two-and-twenty, who had been indulged in childhood because he was the younger, and in youth because he was the handsomer, and in manhood because nobody could make anything of him. He had a fine open countenance, a curling head of hair, a blue coat with brass buttons, and a way of leaning over the counter with his chin upon his hand and his eyes fixed upon the ceiling, as though he were expecting a vision to descend through the plaster. He had been intended for the law, and had spent six months in a solicitor’s office, where he had learned to imitate his master’s signature and to smoke cigars; he had been intended for the sea, and had gone one voyage to Gravesend, where he had been so violently ill that he declared the ocean was a mistake; and he had finally been received into the shop, where he stood behind the counter like a picture in a frame, and sold nothing, but talked to the young ladies who came for a pennyworth of pins in a manner that made them blush and forget their errands.

Now it is a melancholy fact, and one that I record with a sigh, that Nathaniel Crumble was in the habit of borrowing. He borrowed from the till, from the till’s master, from the neighbouring tradesmen, from the young gentlemen at the Mitre, from anybody who had a shilling and a good opinion of him; and he borrowed not in the manner of a rogue, but in the manner of a poet—with a smiling confidence that the future would somehow provide, and a cheerful forgetfulness that the past had ever been. Tobias, who loved his brother with the fond, foolish, unreasoning love of a man who has no children of his own, paid these little sums as they came in, and entered them in a private book which he kept in the drawer with the nutmegs, and which he never spoke of, but which grew fat and greasy with the years.

It was on a November afternoon, when the fog had come up from the Medway and hung about the shop-windows like a wet blanket, and the gas-lamp at the corner had been lit at three o’clock, that Nathaniel came in from the street with an air of such extraordinary importance that the bell above the door seemed to ring a note higher. He flung his hat upon the bacon-block, ran his fingers through his curls, and stood before his brother with his legs apart and his hands behind him, like a man who has just been elected to Parliament.

“Tobias,” said he, “I am engaged.”

Tobias, who was weighing out a quarter of a pound of moist sugar, set down the scoop and looked up.

“Engaged, Nat? To be married?”

“To be married,” returned Nathaniel, with a flourish of his hand, “to Miss Sophia Wellerby, daughter of Mr. Alderman Wellerby, of Wellerby and Wellerby, wholesale grocers, of Thames Street, London. The wedding,” he added, “is to be in the spring.”

Tobias came round the counter and took his brother’s hand. His mild face was all aglow.

“Nat,” said he, “I am glad of it. I am heartily glad of it. She is a good girl, I hope?”

“She is an angel,” said Nathaniel, “and her father is an alderman. But, Tobias—” here he paused, and looked at the ceiling, and then at the floor, and then at the sugar, and then at his boots—“Tobias, there is a little difficulty.”

“A difficulty, Nat?”

“The wedding,” said Nathaniel, “is to be—that is to say, the alderman insists—that is, it would be expected—in short, Tobias, it must be a handsome wedding. The Wellerbys are people of consideration. There will be a breakfast, and a carriage, and—and—in point of fact, Tobias, it will cost money.”

Tobias rubbed his hands slowly.

“How much, Nat?”

“Two hundred pounds,” said Nathaniel, “at the least.”

There was a silence in the shop, broken only by the ticking of the Dutch clock and the faint hiss of the gas-jet. Tobias went back to the counter and took up the scoop again, and turned it over and over in his fingers.
