It was the custom of the Cathedral of St. Aethelred to be silent, and the Bishop of Verran had ever been a man for whom custom was a kind of second religion. His sermons were masterpieces of the commonplace, and his theology was as neat and unadorned as his linen. To him, faith was a matter of discipline, not of delight, and a cathedral was a house of order before it was a house of God. It was therefore with a certain grim satisfaction that he learned, on the evening of Christmas Eve, that the great organ of St. Aethelred, which had been mute for a hundred years, had begun to play of its own accord.

The instrument was a monstrous relic of a forgotten age, a towering edifice of carved oak and gilded pipes that rose into the cathedral’s gloom like a petrified forest. Its bellows, stiff with a century of dust, had groaned to life, and from its silent throat poured a music of such strange and unearthly beauty that the very stones seemed to listen. It was not the jubilant carol of angels, nor the solemn hymn of the faithful; it was a melody of pure, abstract sorrow, a lament for something lost long before the world was made.

The Bishop, summoned from his study where he had been composing a sermon on the virtue of tidiness, heard the first bars as he crossed the cloister. He stopped, his face a mask of austere disapproval. “A demoniacal trick of the wind,” he pronounced to the terrified verger. “Or some prank by a student. Find the source and silence it.”

“It is the organ, my lord,” the old man whispered, his face pale in the lantern light. “It plays by itself.”

“Nonsense,” the Bishop snapped. “Nothing plays by itself. That is the province of God, and He, I am confident, has more respectable taste in music. The thing is a blasphemy, a distraction from the holy purpose of this night. No one is to listen to it. To listen is to entertain a profane curiosity. I forbid it. Go to the bell-tower and ring the peal for the Midnight Mass. Let the sound of proper, man-made bells drown out this pagan noise.”

And so, as the strange, melancholic music filled the nave, the great bells of St. Aethelred began their clamour, a furious and pious assault upon the delicate melody. The city’s burghers, arriving for the service, heard only the familiar clangour and nodded in approval. But the sound of the organ seeped through the bells like perfume through a wall, and a few, pausing on the steps, felt a sudden, inexplicable sadness.

In the choir stalls, the boys were restless. Among them was Julian, a child of ten with the face of a Botticelli angel and a soul that was entirely his own. He was not a pious boy, though he sang the pious words with a voice of pure silver. His piety was for things the Bishop did not understand: the way the winter sun struck the coloured glass, turning the dust motes to a swirl of tiny jewels; the scent of the old hymnals, which smelled of beeswax and forgotten prayers; and now, this music.

The music spoke to him. It did not tell of sin or salvation, but of a lonely star, of a rose blooming in the snow, of a beauty so profound it was almost a wound. While the other boys thought of gingerbread and the morning’s presents, Julian thought only of the sound that was weaving itself through the bell-tolls. The Bishop’s prohibition, delivered in a stern whisper before the service, had only sharpened his desire. To forbid a thing, Julian felt, was to confess its power.

He could not sleep. The memory of the melody was a key turning in a lock within him. Slipping from his pallet in the choir dormitory, he crept through the silent corridors, a small, white-robed ghost in the vast, sleeping cathedral. The service was over, the city was quiet, and the bells had ceased. And in the silence, the organ was still playing.

He pushed open the heavy oak door to the nave. The air was cold and still, thick with the scent of incense and stone. Moonlight, the colour of a pale sapphire, fell through the high windows, illuminating the motes of dust that danced in the air like tiny, silent spirits. And there was the music. It was no longer a lament. It had become a narrative, a story told in sound. It spoke of a deep and abiding love, not for a person, but for the very texture of the world—the coldness of marble, the warmth of candlelight, the fragile perfection of a single snowflake. It was the sound of a soul that had loved the world so intensely that it had become one with the stone and the silence, and now sang of that love to the empty air.

Julian crept forward, his bare feet making no sound on the cold flags. He did not go towards the organ loft. To see the pipes would be to reduce the miracle to mechanics. Instead, he knelt before the great, gilded statue of the Madonna in the Lady Chapel, its serene face illuminated by the shafts of moonlight. From there, he closed his eyes and simply listened. He was not listening to an instrument, but to the cathedral’s own heart, which had, for one night of the year, remembered how to beat. The music filled him, not with the fear of God the Bishop preached, but with a sense of exquisite, unutterable joy. It was a joy that had no place in a sermon, a beauty that was its own justification.

The music swelled, a cascade of notes like falling stars, and then, as the first grey light of dawn began to touch the eastern windows, it faded. It did not stop abruptly, but receded, note by note, into the silence from which it had come, until the only sound was the beating of a small boy’s heart.
