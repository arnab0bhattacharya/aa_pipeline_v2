The intelligence which reached Delvyn Hall on a Tuesday morning in November was of a nature so extraordinary that Mrs. Ashworth was obliged to sit down twice before she had heard it to the end. Her son Thomas, who had been but six months returned from Winchester, had formed an attachment—a violent attachment, as the servant reported, for Miss Grey, the governess at Annesworth Park.

That a boy of seventeen should know his own mind was a proposition Mrs. Ashworth had never entertained; that he should know it so disastrously was a grievance she entertained at once and at length. Miss Grey was a young woman of irreproachable character, considerable accomplishment, and no fortune whatever; and Mrs. Ashworth, who had four other children to establish, felt that the disproportion of the match was such as no prudence could reconcile and no love excuse.

Her daughter Sophia, who was three years the elder and a good deal the wiser, received the news with a composure that did her credit, though it cost her something to maintain it. She liked Miss Grey very well—better, indeed, than she liked most of the young women of the neighbourhood, whose conversation was confined to the weather and their acquaintance, and whose accomplishments, when they had any, were displayed at the pianoforte with a pertinacity which made music a punishment. Miss Grey read French and drew in watercolours and had, moreover, the still rarer talent of listening as though she heard; and Sophia could not but think that if Thomas must fall in love, he had at least chosen a person of taste.

Yet the amusement of the thing was considerable, and Sophia did not deny herself the enjoyment of it. Thomas had always been a creature of impulse, governed by whatever enthusiasm possessed him last; and the spectacle of him in love—with the gravity he assumed, the sighs he practised, the air of melancholy importance with which he entered a room—was such as might have furnished a comedy, had not the author been her brother and the subject so nearly touched the peace of her family.

Mrs. Ashworth resolved to speak to him; and Sophia, who foresaw the conference with a mixture of dread and expectation, took care to be within hearing of the library, where the interview was appointed.

"You are very young, Thomas," said his mother, in the tone of one who states an axiom.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Too young, certainly, to think of marriage."

Thomas was silent.

"Miss Grey is a very respectable young woman—I have nothing to say against her—but she has no connexions and no means; and you have your way to make in the world."

"I do not care for that," said Thomas.

Mrs. Ashworth paused. It was one of the misfortunes of her situation that her son's sincerity, which in a better cause would have been admirable, was employed only to defeat her arguments. She had expected to be met with evasion or denial, and had prepared herself to triumph over both; but to be met with a direct avowal of indifference to interest was a difficulty she had not foreseen.

"You do not care for it now," she said, more gently, "but you will care for it very much when you are five-and-twenty and have nothing to live on. Believe me, my dear, these feelings, however violent—"

"They are not violent," interrupted Thomas, with a dignity which Sophia, listening without, thought extremely well performed. "They are the most rational feelings I ever had. Miss Grey is the most sensible woman of my acquaintance."

"She is seven years older than you."

"That is nothing. She is all that is amiable and good; and I would rather have her without a farthing than any woman in England with ten thousand pounds."

Mrs. Ashworth, finding that argument was of no avail, had recourse to authority; and the conference ended with a command that Thomas should not visit Annesworth for a fortnight, and a declaration on his part that no command should prevent him from thinking of Miss Grey every hour of the day.

Sophia, when her mother had retired, took the opportunity of speaking to her brother alone; and she did it with more address than Mrs. Ashworth had been able to employ, for she began by allowing the whole of what he felt.

"She is a very superior person," said Sophia, "and I do not wonder at your admiring her. Only, my dear Thomas, admiration and love are very different things; and you have known her but three months."

"I have known her long enough to know that she is everything."

"You have known her at a dinner party, and at a walk in the shrubbery. There is a great deal of a person which does not appear in such circumstances."

Thomas looked at her with the impatience of one who believes that the whole world is blind except himself; and Sophia, perceiving that he was not to be convinced, changed her ground and spoke of Miss Grey's situation—how precarious it was, how dependent, and how impossible it must be that she should encourage the addresses of a boy who was not his own master.

At this Thomas coloured violently and declared that Miss Grey had never encouraged him; that he had forced his attentions upon her; that she had, on the contrary, desired him to think no more of her, with a delicacy and a propriety which had only increased his regard.

Sophia was struck by this account, which bore the marks of truth, and felt for Miss Grey a respect which her brother's passion had not been able to inspire. She resolved to call at Annesworth the next morning and to judge for herself how the matter stood; for she was not without a suspicion that Thomas's imagination had been more busy than Miss Grey's heart, and that the governess, whatever her merits, might be less deeply engaged than he supposed.
