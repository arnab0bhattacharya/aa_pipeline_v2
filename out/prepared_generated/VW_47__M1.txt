The light, this morning, had a particular quality, a thin, watery gold that fell through the plane trees and lay in dappled coins upon the pavement, as if the whole street were a riverbed and she were walking, not on stone, but on the slow, sunken treasures of the past. Forty years. The words had no weight, no shape; they were merely a sound, a hum in the air like the sound of the electric lamp overhead, a thing one lived inside without perceiving it, until the day came—this day—when the walls were to be dismantled, the furniture dispersed, and the whole fabric of a life, spun so finely and so long, was to be gathered up and put away. Tomorrow. But today, there was only the street, the light, and the long, slow procession of the houses, each with its own face, its own story murmuring behind the glass.

Number 12, of course, was the beginning. Not her beginning, but the street’s, for her. The Mertons. Their brass knocker, a lion’s head, had been polished to a fierce, silent roar. Mrs. Merton, with her high collar and her air of having just come from a committee meeting, her voice a dry rustle like fallen leaves. “A new neighbour! How delightful!” And she had felt, then, so impossibly young, her own hands, in their new gloves, feeling clumsy and raw against the polished wood of her own door. She had been a bride then, a creature of silk and uncertainty, and the Mertons had seemed to her like figures from a history book, solid and settled, their lives already complete. Now the lion’s head was gone, replaced by a simple bell, and a young man with paint-stained trousers was washing a window, his movements quick and thoughtless, his life not yet begun to accumulate its layers of dust and memory.

She walked on. The sun shifted, and a shadow, long and blue, fell across the pavement from the ash tree that had grown so tall. The Pastons’ tree. They had been gardeners, of a sort; that is, they had loved the earth in a quiet, desperate way, and Mrs. Paston, a woman with soil permanently lodged beneath her fingernails, had given her cuttings—geraniums, a straggling rose—which she had planted in her own small yard. And for a time, she had been a gardener too, kneeling on the damp ground, feeling the cool, dark life of the soil, a different woman entirely from the one who worried over dinner parties and the state of the linen. Then Mr. Paston died, and Mrs. Paston moved to the country to be with her sister, and the garden, untended, had returned to a wildness that felt like a judgement. The young man who lived there now had a bicycle propped against the wall. He would never know the particular weight of a trowel in the hand, the satisfaction of a weed pulled clean.

At Number 18, the curtains were drawn, a heavy, dark velvet that seemed to absorb the light. The Penroses. A hush fell over her thoughts here, a contraction, as if a cold draught had found its way through the solid brick. Mr. Penrose, a scholar of some obscure period, who had been found one morning in his study, his head upon a book. The doctor had said it was his heart. But she had always felt it was the silence of that house, the weight of all those unread pages, that had finally pressed the life from him. Mrs. Penrose had not spoken for a week afterwards. She would stand at the window, her face a pale oval behind the glass, and watch the street as if it were a foreign country. For months after, walking past, she, too, felt the silence, felt the house holding its breath. It was a silence that had its own texture, its own colour—a grey, velvety silence, thick with the dust of scholarship and grief. Now, a child’s hoop lay abandoned on the step, a bright, circular shout against the dark.

The street curved, and the light changed again, becoming warmer, catching the red brick of the end house, where old Miss Ainsworth had lived. Ah, Miss Ainsworth! The memory came not as a picture but as a scent—lavender, and beeswax, and the faint, sharp tang of the strong peppermints she always kept in a silver dish on her hall table. She had been ancient then, or so it seemed, a creature of shawls and creaking corsets, who had lived in that house since she was a girl. She had known the street when it was new, when the trees were saplings and the air smelled of horses and coal smoke. “It all goes by so quickly, my dear,” she had said once, her voice a dry whisper, her eyes, like faded blue beads, fixed on some point far beyond the window. “Like a train. You look out, and the fields are gone.” At the time, she had found it a morbid thing to say. Now, she felt the truth of it in her very bones. The train was pulling into the station. The fields were gone.

She reached her own door. Number 22. The paint was peeling on the window frame, a detail she had meant to attend to for years. Her hand, with its familiar ring, felt for the key in her pocket. The metal was cold. Inside, the hall was dim, and the air was thick with the dust of packing, the ghostly shapes of furniture shrouded in sheets. The silence here was different from the Penroses’; it was not an absence of sound, but a waiting for it. It was the silence of an empty room after a party, when the glasses have been cleared and the chairs pushed back, and only the memory of voices remains. She stood there for a moment, her hand on the door, and looked back down the street. The light was fading now, the gold turning to a bruised purple.
