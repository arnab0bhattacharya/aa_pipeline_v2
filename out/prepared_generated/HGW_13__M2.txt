The heat in the basement office of the Local Government Board was a physical weight, a thick, stagnant thing that seemed to press against the eardrums and dull the edges of thought. It was late July, the kind of London summer that turned the air into a soup of dust and decay, and the gas lamps hissed faintly in the corners, casting long, trembling shadows across the piles of blue-bound returns. Arthur Pym sat hunched over his desk, a magnifying glass in one hand and a ledger in the other, his spectacles sliding down his nose. He was a small man, unremarkable in every way that mattered to the civil service, a clerk of the fourth class whose life was measured in ink stains and the rhythmic clatter of typewriters. But Pym had a habit, a dangerous habit, of looking at the numbers.

The figures for the district of Hackney were wrong. Not wrong in the sense of a clerical error, a transposed digit or a missing decimal, but wrong in a way that suggested a fundamental misunderstanding of the nature of the data. The mortality rates for influenza had dropped by three percent, while the cases of "fever of unknown origin" had spiked by forty. It was a statistical anomaly, a hiccup in the machine, something that would be smoothed over by the next quarterly review. Or so Pym intended to believe. He made a note in the margin, a small, dry comment: *Verify source data. Possible misclassification.*

He looked up. The room was empty, save for the janitor, a heavy man with a mop, who was wrestling with a clogged drain in the corner. The janitor grunted, a sound of pure physical effort, and Pym felt a sudden, inexplicable chill. It wasn’t the temperature. It was the silence. Outside, London was roaring, a vast, churning beast of steam and iron, but down here, in the belly of the bureaucracy, the world had stopped.

The news had been out for three days. The *Times* had run a small item, buried on page four, under the heading "Minor Disturbances in the East End." The *Pall Mall Gazette*, however, had seen the opportunity. They had printed a headline in bold red type: "THE GHOST PLAGUE: WHAT IS THE SECRET ILLNESS THAT STRIKES THE POOR?" They spoke of spectral figures, of a miasma that drifted from the cellar doors of the tenements, of a demon that fed on the sweat of the laborer. It was sensationalism, pure and unadulterated, but it was not entirely baseless. Pym knew, because he had seen the reports from the medical officers. They spoke of a rash that appeared like burnt skin, of a cough that sounded like tearing cloth, of a delirium that lasted only a few hours before ending in death or a strange, absolute silence.

The authorities were in denial. The Minister of Health had given a speech that morning, a polished, oily thing full of assurances about the efficacy of the sanitation squads and the purity of the water supply. He had spoken of "exaggerated reports" and "hysterical tendencies in the press." But Pym had seen the raw data. He had seen the graphs, the sharp, jagged lines that climbed like vines up the paper. He knew that the denial was not a mistake. It was a strategy.

He picked up the ledger. The weight of it was significant, a physical manifestation of the lie. If he reported this upward, it would vanish. It would be absorbed into the bureaucracy, diluted by committees, buried in sub-files. The Minister would not want to alarm the markets. The stock exchange was already nervous; the Bank of England was watching the gold reserves. A panic would be catastrophic. The economy must be stable. The health of the individual was a minor variable in the equation of national stability.

Pym thought of his sister, Elspeth, who lived in Bermondsey. She was a nurse, a practical woman who smelled of carbolic and boiled wool. He had not spoken to her in two days. She had been quiet on the phone, her voice thin and distant. "It’s not flu, Arthur," she had said. "It’s something else. It moves fast." He had dismissed it as fear. Fear was a common ingredient in the epidemic of rumor. But now, looking at the numbers, he felt a different kind of fear. A cold, rational dread.

He stood up. His knees creaked. The janitor was gone, having finished his task, and the smell of sewage was still strong, a pungent reminder of the city’s underbelly. Pym walked to the window. It was a small, high window, looking out onto a brick wall. Through the narrow gap between the bricks, he could see a sliver of the sky, a pale, washed-out blue. It looked clean. It looked safe.

He made his decision. It was not a brave decision. It was a practical one. He could not fix the machine. He could not stop the machine. But he could warn someone who could. He thought of the journalist from the *Pall Mall Gazette* who had come to the office last week, a loud man with a loud tie, who had asked Pym for the "big picture." Pym had given him the official figures. He had lied by omission. Now, he had to make up for it.

He packed the ledger into his bag. It was a bold move, technically a breach of protocol. The documents were the property of the Crown. To remove them was to court dismissal, or worse. But the ledger was not just paper. It was a map of a disease that was eating the city from the inside out. It was a weapon.

He left the office without looking back. The stairs were steep, and the air grew cooler as he ascended. When he reached the street, the noise of London hit him like a wall. The clatter of hansom cabs, the shouting of newsboys, the hiss of steam from the manholes. The newsboys were crying the same headline: "GHOST PLAGUE!" People walked past him, their faces tight with a new, unspoken anxiety. They touched their arms, checked their throats. The disease was in the air, not just in the bodies, but in the minds.
