The dining room at No. 12, Cadogan Square was a temple of good taste, and Eleanor Vance was its high priestess. The gaslight, softened by silk shades, glinted on the silver and the crystal, casting a warm, expectant glow over the assembled faces. Every detail was immaculate: the damask napkins folded into perfect lilies, the decanters of claret breathing on the sideboard, the subtle scent of beeswax and hothouse roses. Eleanor surveyed her domain with a quiet pride. Tonight was to be her masterpiece. Tonight, the great Mr. Silas Croft would dine at her table.

Croft was not a statesman or a general, but something far more revered in the drawing rooms of Belgravia: a celebrated talker. His monologues, delivered at dinners across London, were legendary. He could discourse on the fall of Rome, the nature of boredom, or the precise flavour of a winter pear with equal and astonishing brilliance. To have Croft at one’s table was a social coup, a guarantee that the evening would be remembered, discussed, and envied for weeks. Eleanor had spent a month on the guest list, another on the menu. She had chosen her companions with the care of a curator selecting pieces for an exhibition: the witty Lady Beatrice, the sharp-minded politician Mr. Albright, the quiet but appreciative poet, Mr. Finch.

The door to the dining room opened and the butler, Graves, announced, “Mr. Silas Croft.” A hush fell. All eyes turned to the doorway, where the great man stood. He was smaller than Eleanor had imagined, a slender man in an impeccably tailored evening suit. But it was his face that held the room. It was a landscape of experience, etched with fine lines around the eyes and mouth, the face of a man who had not only seen the world but had found the perfect words for it. He smiled, a brief, charming twitch of the lips, and took his seat at Eleanor’s right hand.

The first course, a consommé with shreds of chicken, was served. A pause ensued, the customary, reverent silence as the guests waited for the evening’s performance to begin. Eleanor, her heart fluttering with anticipation, turned to her guest. “Mr. Croft, we are so very honoured to have you with us. I confess, I have been looking forward to this evening for a month.”

Mr. Croft inclined his head. “You are too kind, Mrs. Vance.”

The silence returned. It was not the comfortable silence of a moment’s thought, but a thin, brittle thing. Eleanor felt a prickle of unease. She tried again. “We were just speaking of the new exhibition at the Grosvenor Gallery. The Post-Impressionists are causing quite a stir. Do you have any thoughts on the matter?”

Mr. Croft stirred his consommé with a silver spoon. “A stir,” he repeated, as if tasting the word. “Yes. A stir.” He said nothing more.

A dreadful coldness began to seep into the room, chilling the warm gaslight. Lady Beatrice, ever the social diplomat, leaped into the breach. “And what of the political situation, Mr. Croft? The Prime Minister’s latest pronouncements on the Empire have certainly given us all a great deal to… discuss.”

Mr. Croft looked up from his bowl, his famous eyes seeming strangely vacant. “The Empire is… vast,” he offered, his voice flat. He took a sip of his soup. The silence that followed was now a physical presence, a heavy, suffocating blanket. Mr. Albright coughed. Mr. Finch studied the pattern on his plate as if it held the secrets of the universe. The entire elaborate structure of the evening, built so carefully around the expectation of brilliance, was collapsing into ruin.

Eleanor felt a hot flush of panic and shame. This was her party, her failure. The great Silas Croft, the celebrated talker, had arrived with nothing to say. He was an empty vessel, a beautiful, hollow gourd. Desperation, sharp and cold, seized her. She looked at the faces of her guests—confused, disappointed, pitying. She could not let it end like this. She would not.

Taking a deep breath, Eleanor felt a strange, reckless energy surge through her. If the master would not perform, then the apprentice must. She turned to Lady Beatrice, a determined smile fixed on her face. “It reminds me,” she began, her voice a little too bright, “of my cousin, Theodora. She married a man from the Diplomatic Service and was posted to Constantinople. She wrote to me in a state of absolute despair, not because of the political intrigues or the heat, but because of the Turkish Delight.”

A flicker of interest in Lady Beatrice’s eyes. “The Turkish Delight?”

“Oh, it was everywhere,” Eleanor continued, warming to her theme. “Piled high in silver bowls in every room. The trouble was, Theodora had a phobia of powdered sugar. It was the texture, she said. Like fine dust. She was convinced it was slowly coating her lungs. She spent her entire tour politely declining the national confection, terrified she would choke to death on a sweetmeat. She wrote to me, ‘My dear Eleanor, I am representing Her Majesty’s Government in one of the great cities of the world, and my greatest challenge is avoiding dessert.’”

A ripple of laughter went around the table. It was a small sound, but to Eleanor, it was a symphony. Mr. Finch, the poet, was smiling. Mr. Albright chuckled. Even Lady Beatrice looked genuinely amused. Eleanor felt a surge of power, as if she had seized the reins of a runaway carriage and brought it safely under control. She glanced at Mr. Croft. He was looking at her with a faint, unreadable expression.

Emboldened, she launched into another story, this one about a disastrous attempt to host a séance with a fraudulent medium who was exposed when the floating trumpet was revealed to be suspended on a wire from the chandelier. She described the outrage of the guests, the frantic scrambling of the medium, and the horrified expression of her late husband, who had believed he was about to receive a message from Napoleon. She did not do impressions or use extravagant gestures. Her delivery was calm, precise, her timing impeccable. She let the absurdity of the situations speak for themselves, her own dry wit providing the commentary.
