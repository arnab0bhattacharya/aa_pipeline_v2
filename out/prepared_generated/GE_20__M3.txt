The apprehension of foreign notions in a quiet English market-town is a sentiment that gathers force in a ratio far exceeding the actual evidence of danger, much as a small quantity of leaven works upon a bulky mass of meal, producing a fermentation altogether disproportionate to its visible quantity. In the town of Treddleston, where the ordinary course of thought ran in a deep, sluggish channel of constitutional loyalty, the presence of Herr Elias Brunn, a professor of music, had long been viewed with a compound of respect and suspicion. He was a man who had left his native Saxon village under circumstances that implied a certain impatience of arbitrary rule, bringing with him a broad forehead, a deeply-lined brow, and a fundamental consciousness that the world’s injustices were not merely to be accepted with a patient, Teutonic shrug, but were rather to be intellectually contested.

It was not his musical abilities that were in question, for he played the pianoforte with a precision and feeling that drew reluctant admiration even from the wives of the town’s chief maltsters. Rather, it was the slow, deliberate fashion in which he was observed to read certain foreign journals at the window of the reading-room, and the occasional, half-muttered remarks concerning the dignity of the laboring classes, which had caused the germs of suspicion to take root in the conservative soil of Treddleston society. For it is a well-observed phenomenon in provincial life, that any deviation from the customary round of thought is immediately translated from the intellectual sphere into the moral one; a man who questions the political constitution is quickly believed to be deficient in the domestic virtues, and a foreigner who entertains abstract theories of liberty is presently suspected of designs upon the very poultry in the yard. The recent agitation of the manufacturing districts had thrown a tremor of alarm into the rural counties, causing the local magistracy to look upon all strangers, and particularly those of German extraction, as so many combustible elements only waiting for a spark.

The chief instrument of this uneasiness was Mr. Poyser, a local magistrate and wool-stapler, whose mind was as accurately fenced against novel ideas as his meadows were against the encroachment of the river. He was a man who held the social order to be a fixed and sacred edifice, wherein each man’s duty was to remain in the room assigned to him, and he regarded the political discontent of the times as a sort of moral disease, highly infectious, to be suppressed by the immediate application of authoritative plasters. His daughter, Miss Poyser, was among Herr Brunn’s most promising pupils, and the magistrate, having persuaded himself that the music-master’s lessons were subtly imbued with republican poison, determined to apply a definitive test to his loyalty.

It was on a damp Tuesday afternoon in late October that Mr. Poyser walked up the creaking stairs to Herr Brunn’s sitting-room, carrying in his hand a printed paper. The room was of that mingled character which belongs to the apartments of men who live by their wits in a provincial town: a spinet stood in the corner, a shelf of German philosophers occupied the wall, and a faint odor of cold coffee and wood-smoke pervaded the atmosphere. Herr Brunn, who had been correcting a manuscript sonata, rose with that grave and slightly melancholy courtesy which never quite deserted him.

“Sit ye down, Mr. Poyser, sit ye down,” said the magistrate, waving his hand in a manner that was meant to be jocular but which fell short of its intended ease. He was a man whose inward apprehension of danger was always clothed in a robust, outward confidence. “I am come to have a bit of a talk with you, Herr Brunn, concerning a matter that is giving some uneasiness to the parents of your young scholars. There is a notion about that you are a man of what may be called advanced opinions, and in these ticklish times, when the very foundations of order are threatened by disaffected persons, we, the magistrates of the county, think it prudent to require a declaration of loyalty from all foreigners in positions of trust.”

Herr Brunn’s dark eyes rested quietly upon the magistrate’s face, and there was a slight tightening of the muscles about his mouth, as when a man perceives that the moment for a painful but inevitable decision has arrived. “A declaration?” he said, in his slow, carefully articulated English. “Of what nature is this declaration, Mr. Poyser?”

The magistrate unfolded the paper, which had been drawn up with the combined legal acumen of the town clerk and the rector. It affirmed the signer’s unfeigned loyalty to the Crown, his abhorrence of all republican or revolutionary principles, and his solemn promise to abstain from the circulation of seditious literature. It concluded with a blank space for the signature.

“I have brought it for your signature,” said Mr. Poyser, laying the document on the table between them with an air of finality. “It is but a form, you’ll understand, a mere form; but it will set the minds of the parents at rest, and your situation in the town will be, so to speak, confirmed. You can still give your lessons to the young ladies, and we shall all know where we are.”

For a few moments Herr Brunn did not speak. He was not unaware of the consequences of refusal. He knew that his livelihood in Treddleston depended upon the goodwill of a dozen families whose political sympathies were as narrow as their domestic virtues were robust. He thought of his daughter, little Minna, who was at that moment in the adjoining room, practicing her scales with the patient, uncomprehending perseverance of childhood; and the thought of her future, of the bread that might be taken from her mouth by a stroke of his pen, or by the withholding of it, pressed upon him with a heavy, paternal weight. The temptation to sign was strong, for it is the peculiar trial of conscientious men that their scruples often appear, even to themselves, in the guise of mere self-will, and that a concession which involves no overt wrongdoing seems, at first sight, a reasonable concession to the prejudices of others.
