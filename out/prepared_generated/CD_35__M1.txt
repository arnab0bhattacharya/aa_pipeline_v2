It was a day of dripping skies and smutty fog upon the Grand Western Canal, that watery thoroughfare which, in its muddy and industrious fashion, connects the potteries to the black country, and carries upon its back a floating population of barges, boats, and bargemen, as the great roads of England carry their coaches and their carts. The fog, that greasy and all-pervading vapour, had descended in the night, and now clung to everything with a tenacious familiarity: to the dripping branches of the willow-trees, to the cold iron of the lock-gates, to the very faces of the men who stood about the wharf at Nether Pegglesworth, their breath pluming in the air like so many little, transient spirits. The air was thick with the smell of wet coal, of damp earth, and of the indefinable, ancient odour of the canal itself—a compound of mud, rotting weed, and the ghosts of a thousand cargoes.

Upon the deck of the good barge *Water Sprite*, a vessel of stout oak and honest tar, stood a young man, though to call him a mere youth would be to do a disservice to the breadth of his shoulders and the quiet confidence of his bearing. His name was Tom, or so he had been called for as long as he could remember; Tom the Sweep, Tom the Chimbley, and, latterly and most happily, Tom of the *Water Sprite*. His hands, which had once been blackened beyond all remedy by soot and despair, were now calloused and brown from the tiller and the tow-rope. His face, which had been pinched and pale with hunger and fear, was now open and tanned by the sun and the wind. He was a creature of the canal, and the canal had remade him, washing away the grime of his first years and feeding him with the simple, wholesome fare of potatoes, bacon, and the great, quiet dignity of the water.

Beside him, her small hands busy with the needle and thread upon a piece of sailcloth, sat Eliza, the daughter of the barge-master. Her hair was the colour of a ripe hazelnut, and her eyes, when she looked up from her work to smile at him, held the clear and placid light of the canal on a summer’s day. It was she who had taught him his letters, scratching them with a piece of chalk upon the lid of a cargo barrel; it was she who had opened for him the great, silent book of the world, which he had previously known only as a place of darkness and hard, unfeeling stone. He owed her more than he could say; he owed her the very words in his head.

‘You are quiet, Tom,’ she said, her voice soft as the rustle of the reeds. ‘Is the fog making you sad?’

‘Not sad, Liza,’ he replied, his own voice a low and pleasant rumble. ‘Only thinking. Thinking of the great distance between the boy I was and the man I am. It is a longer voyage than any we have made upon this cut.’

Their conversation was cut short by the grinding of chains and the shout of the lock-keeper, a man whose face was a map of the waterways he commanded, all wrinkles and tributaries. ‘*Water Sprite*! Lock’s ready! Come on, then, don’t dawdle all day!’

As Tom took up the pole to fend off from the lock wall, a commotion arose on the tow-path above. A gentleman, or at least a man dressed in the shabby finery of a gentleman’s servant, was berating a poor fellow who was tending to a donkey. The servant was a weasel of a man, with a face that seemed to have been squeezed into its present shape by a lifetime of resentment. His voice was a high, thin scrape, and he had a habit of punctuating his sentences with a sharp, dry cough.

‘Dawdle! You dally, you dawdle, you dally and dawdle! It is the plague of my existence!’ the weasel-man shrieked, kicking at a loose stone. ‘Get a move on, you idle beast! The master waits for no man, and he waits for no donkey, neither!’

Tom glanced up, a flicker of irritation crossing his features. But as the man turned, his small, peering eyes fell upon the barge, and upon Tom, and a strange, terrible light of recognition dawned in them. The weasel-man stopped dead. His mouth fell open, and his weaselly face, for a moment, looked almost human in its astonishment.

‘Well, I never!’ he gasped, scuttling closer to the lock gate. ‘If it ain’t! Strike me down if it ain’t the little soot-sprite! The little climbing-boy! Scrambled out of the flue and run off to play at boatswain, have we?’

A cold dread, a sensation he had not felt in a decade, seized Tom’s heart. It was a feeling of soot in the lungs, of darkness, of a small, hard hand gripping his ankle. He knew that voice. He knew that face, though it was older, thinner, and more sourly etched with the lines of servitude. It was Snively. Snively, the assistant to the cruel Master Grimstone, the man who had held him fast at the top of a hundred chimneys, who had beaten him for a whim, who had called him ‘Cinder’ and ‘Dirt’ until he had almost forgotten he had any other name.

‘I do not know you,’ Tom said, his voice hard and flat, the words falling like stones into the water.

‘Don’t know me!’ Snively cackled, his high voice grating on the ears. ‘Don’t know me! Oh, that’s rich, that is! That’s a rum go! Look at him, Liza!’ he cried, though he did not know her name. ‘Big and bold as brass, but I knew him when he was no bigger than a broom-handle and twice as black! Master Grimstone would be glad to see his old apprentice, I’ll be bound! He’d have you back up a flue before you could say Jack Robinson!’
