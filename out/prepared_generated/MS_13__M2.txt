The rain lashed against the leaded panes of my sitting room in a rhythm that seemed less like the falling of water and more like the frantic tapping of a thousand hidden fingers, seeking entry into the sealed vaults of my memory. It was a grey, melancholy afternoon in Bath, where the very stones of the city wept with the dampness of the season, and the air hung heavy with the scent of wet leaves and extinguished hopes. I sat by the hearth, where the fire had dwindled to a sullen red glow, watching the shadows lengthen and distort upon the walls, until they resembled the gaunt, elongated forms of those I had lost. I had built my life here, in this respectable lodging house near the Pump Room, upon a foundation of silence and strict propriety. For seven years, I had been Mrs. Elspeth Thorne, a widow of modest means and unimpeachable virtue, caring for my lodgers with a quiet, efficient hand that brooked no curiosity and accepted no pity. I had succeeded in burying my former self so deep that even the memory of the name I once bore—Elspeth Vance—felt like a phantom limb, aching with a pain that had no physical source, yet was more real than the flesh and blood that now composed my being.

It was as the first strokes of evening darkness began to veil the city that a knock sounded at the door, sharp and insistent, breaking the monotony of the rain. My housekeeper, a stout woman named Martha, appeared in the doorway, her face pale and her hands trembling as she whispered that a gentleman wished to see me. I commanded her to show him in, for I had long since ceased to fear the approach of strangers, having learned that the world outside my walls was merely a stage upon which men performed their various disguises. The man who entered was tall and gaunt, wrapped in a coat of dark, damp wool that clung to his frame, revealing the haggard lines of a face that had been carved by hardship and perhaps by a life spent in the harsh climates of distant penal colonies. He did not sit. He stood by the fireplace, watching the embers with a fixed, predatory intensity, his eyes, though dimmed by age, holding a light that I recognized with a shock that went through me like a bolt of lightning.

"Mrs. Thorne," he said, and his voice was low, hoarse, and strangely familiar, like the sound of a rusted hinge forced open. "Or shall I say, Mrs. Vance?"

The name struck me with the force of a physical blow. I felt the blood drain from my face, leaving me cold and numb, as if I had been plunged into an icy stream. I tried to speak, to deny, to plead, but my tongue was heavy and unresponsive, paralyzed by the sheer magnitude of the past he had unearthed. I could only stare at him, my heart pounding in my chest with a rhythm that threatened to burst the confines of my ribs. He smiled, a thin, cruel expression that did not reach his eyes, and turned to me with a slow, deliberate grace that was more terrifying than any threat of violence.

"I am Edward Vance," he continued, his tone conversational, as if we were discussing the weather or the price of tea. "I was transported for the crime of forgery, a crime for which I was made the scapegoat by men of greater power and less conscience. I have returned, Mrs. Vance, and I have come to collect my due."

My mind reeled. I had spent seven years constructing a fortress of respectability, brick by brick, hiding my past behind the facade of a respectable widow. I had married a kind, if dull, man who died young, and in his death, I had found a second life, a chance to be someone other than the wife of a convict. I had loved him, or what remained of my capacity for love, and I had mourned him truly. Now, this specter from my past stood before me, demanding that I acknowledge the rot at the core of my current existence.

"What do you want?" I managed to whisper, my voice barely audible above the roar of the rain.

"Money," he said simply. "A substantial sum. Enough to secure my passage to the colonies, or to buy me a new identity in the East. I know you have savings, Mrs. Vance. I know the value of your reputation, and I know how much you have invested in the lie that you are a respectable widow. I am not here to expose you to the public, for I am too old and too weary for such vengeance. But I am here to remind you that you owe me for the years I spent in chains, for the years I spent in a hell that you walked past in the sunlight. You had the choice then, to stand by me, or to flee. You chose to flee. Now, you must pay."

I looked at the fire, watching the ashes curl and turn to dust. The thought of my lodgers, my clean linen, my quiet evenings, the small, hard-won happiness I had found in this respectable life, rose up within me with a fierce, desperate passion. Was it worth giving up? Was it worth surrendering to this man, this broken remnant of my former self, who represented everything I had worked so hard to escape? I thought of the shame that would follow if he were to speak, the gasps of my lodgers, the pity of my neighbors, the collapse of the world I had built. But I also thought of the years of silence, the lies I had told, the weight of the secret that had sat upon my chest like a stone.

"I have no money," I said, my voice gaining a strange, cold strength. "I am a widow, living on a small pension. I cannot give you what you ask."
