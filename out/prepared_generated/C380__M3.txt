The frost on the inside of the bedroom window had not melted in three weeks. Elara drew her shawl tighter across her shoulders, listening to the rattling breath of the woman in the bed. Aunt Marguerite was a landscape of bone beneath the counterpane, her face a map of veins and shadow. Outside, the Yorkshire moors stretched in every direction, vast and white and indifferent. Blackthorn Farm had always been isolated, but this winter it had become a country unto itself, sealed away from the world by drifts that reached the lower windowsills.

For four months Elara had nursed her aunt through the decline. She had spooned broth past cracked lips, changed linens soiled by a body surrendering its dignity, and sat through the long nights when Marguerite's breathing grew shallow and erratic, when each exhalation seemed it might be the last. She had done this not out of obligation, though obligation played its part, but because there was nowhere else for her to go. Her father had died owing money to men who did not forgive debts. Her mother had followed him within the year, leaving Elara with nothing but a trunk of clothes and the name of an aunt in Yorkshire who might take her in. Marguerite had taken her in. That was five years ago.

Now the solicitor had come, or rather had sent word, for the roads remained impassable for carriages. The letter had arrived carried by a boy on a moor pony, the paper damp and the ink blurred but legible enough. Marguerite's will, drawn up two years prior, left the farm and all its contents to her son, Caleb. Caleb, who had not crossed the threshold of Blackthorn Farm in seven years. Caleb, who lived in York and had married a merchant's daughter and had no interest in sheep or turnips or the woman who had raised him alone after her husband's death. Elara read the letter twice, folded it, and placed it in the drawer of the kitchen dresser beside the candle snuffers. Then she went upstairs to change her aunt's bandages.

She told no one. There was no one to tell. The farmhand, Job, who looked after the few remaining sheep, was half-deaf and kept his own counsel. The neighbour's wife, Mrs. Thistle, had stopped visiting in December when the snow began in earnest. Elara was alone with the dying and the knowledge that when the dying was done, she would have nothing.

The will's existence did not surprise her, not truly. Marguerite had spoken of Caleb often in the early days of her illness, before the laudanum clouded her speech, before the pain made her cruel. She spoke of him as though he were a boy still, as though his absence were a temporary misunderstanding that would resolve itself any day. Elara had listened and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would not wound. She had understood that Marguerite's love for her son was the one unassailable thing in her life, a love that had survived his silence and his neglect and his failure to write even when word came that his mother was dying. It was a love Elara could not compete with and had never tried to.

What surprised her was the smallness of her own grief at the news. She had expected to feel anger, or at least the bitter sting of injustice. Instead she felt something closer to the sensation of stepping off a curb in the dark and finding the ground sooner than anticipated. A jolt, and then solid earth beneath her feet. She was penniless, and she was free. These two facts existed simultaneously, and she could not yet determine which weighed more heavily.

Marguerite died on a Tuesday, or perhaps it was Wednesday. The days had blurred together. Elara knew only that she woke from a doze in the chair beside the bed and found her aunt's chest still, her mouth slightly open, her hands cool beneath the blankets. She sat for a long time, holding the thin fingers, listening to the wind against the stone walls. The clock in the kitchen ticked on, indifferent to the fact that it now counted the hours of an empty house. Eventually Elara rose, drew the sheet over her aunt's face, and went downstairs to build up the fire.

Job helped her dig the grave in the frozen earth behind the byre, a terrible labour that took the better part of two days. There could be no question of a church burial. The village was six miles away and the roads were still deep. They lowered Marguerite into the ground wrapped in her best blanket, and Elara read what prayers she could remember, her voice swallowed almost at once by the wind. Job removed his cap and stood with his head bowed, then put it on again and went to see to the sheep. Elara remained at the graveside until her feet went numb, then returned to the house and sat at the kitchen table and allowed herself, for the first time, to consider what came next.

The letter from Mrs. Hester Thornfield arrived the following week. Elara recognized the hand on the direction, the careful copperplate of a woman who had been educated beyond her station and never forgotten it. Hester Thornfield was the widow of a mill owner in Halifax, a woman of property and rigid principle. She had known Marguerite in the years before her marriage, when both were young women in service, and the connection had been maintained through occasional letters that grew less frequent and more formal as their lives diverged. Elara had met her twice. The first time, Hester had inspected her as one inspects livestock at market, pronouncing her passable. The second time, two years ago, Hester had offered her a place in her household as a companion to herself and tutor to her two young daughters. The offer had carried conditions that Elara understood perfectly. She would be neither servant nor family. She would occupy the liminal space that such women inhabited in respectable houses, expected to be grateful and useful and invisible in equal measure. She had declined, and Marguerite, to her credit, had said nothing in reproach.
