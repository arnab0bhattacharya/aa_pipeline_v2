The letter from Blackwood & Vance sat folded on the desk, its wax seal broken like a dark bruise against the cream paper. Elias Merrick had read it four times since morning, and the words had not changed. The mill owners, in their boundless charity, had decided that the night-school at Ironbridge must confine its curriculum to arithmetic and the catechism. These subjects alone were suitable for the moral and practical improvement of the workers. Any instruction beyond these limits would be considered a misuse of the company’s funds and the company’s goodwill.

Elias pushed the letter beneath a stack of well-worn primers and looked up at the schoolroom. It was a narrow building of blackened brick, wedged between a chandler’s shop and the mill wall, which rose beyond the window like a cliff face. Twenty-odd chairs were arranged in uneven rows. The slate blackboard behind him still carried, from the previous evening, a line of long division and the opening words of the Westminster Shorter Catechism. He had not yet wiped them clean.

The workers began arriving just after the six o’clock bell, their faces still carrying the grime of the shift. They came in stomping, shaking the damp from their coats, and took their seats with the careful quietness of men who had spent the day in noise. Most of them were young, between fifteen and twenty-five, though a few were older—men who had come to the school late and with a stubbornness that Elias found humbling. They worked the looms, the carding engines, the roving frames. Their hands were rough and their eyes were tired, but they came.

Samuel Rudd came last, as he always did, because the boilers were at the far end of the works and it took him longer to walk the length of the yard. He was a broad man of perhaps thirty-five, with a beard that was going grey at the chin and hands that were perpetually marked with the rust of the furnace doors. He had been coming to the school for two years. In that time he had mastered the basic arithmetic and could recite the catechism with a fluency that was mechanical and complete. He could also read, which set him apart from most of the class. He had taught himself, laboriously, by candlelight, working through a battered copy of the book of Psalms that had belonged to his mother. When he read aloud, his voice was low and deliberate, each word handled like something fragile.

Elias began the lesson as he always did, with a problem on the board. A calculation of wages: so many hours at so many pence, minus the cost of lodgings and the price of coal. The men copied it down, some of them gripping their pencils in fists, others with a more practised hold. Elias moved between the rows, correcting, guiding. When he reached Samuel, he found the problem already finished and correct, the figures written in a small, exact hand.

"You've done it," Elias said quietly.

Samuel looked up. His eyes, beneath the grime, were a pale and startling blue. "I want to ask you something," he said, in a voice pitched low enough that the other men would not hear. "Not now. After."

Elias nodded and continued down the row.

The arithmetic portion ended, and Elias turned to the catechism. He read the questions and the class responded in unison, their voices a rough, blended chorus that filled the small room. "What is the chief end of man? Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever." The words rose and settled like dust. Elias watched their faces as they spoke. Some of them mouthed the words without thought, as one might wind a clock. Others, Samuel among them, spoke with a weight that suggested they had turned the sentences over in their minds during the long hours at the mill, examining them for cracks.

When the lesson ended and the men began to gather their things, Samuel remained in his seat. Elias returned to the desk and waited. The last of the others left, pulling the door shut behind them, and the room was quiet save for the distant, perpetual murmur of the mill.

"I want you to teach me to read poetry," Samuel said.

Elias sat down slowly. "Poetry."

"Aye." Samuel's hands rested on the desk in front of him, large and still. "I've been reading the Psalms, as you know. And there's something in them—the way the words turn. I don't know how to say it. They go in a different way than other words go. I want to understand that. I want to read other things that go that way."

Elias looked at him. The lamp on the desk threw a yellow light across the man's face, catching the lines around his eyes, the grey at his jaw. He was not a romantic figure. He was a boiler-tender, a man who fed fires and watched gauges, and who came to school after twelve hours of heat and iron. But his expression was open and exact, as though he had thought about this for a long time and had arrived at it not through fancy but through the same patient logic he applied to long division.

"You know what the letter said," Elias replied. "Arithmetic and the catechism. Nothing else. Blackwood & Vance were very clear."

"I know."

"If I teach you poetry and they find out, they'll close the school. Or they'll dismiss me and put someone in who'll follow the letter."

Samuel considered this. "Then teach me here. After the others have gone. I'll come late and leave late. No one will know."

"It's not that simple."

"It is, though." Samuel leaned forward slightly. "I'm not asking you to put it on the board for the lot of them. I'm asking you to sit with me and a book of poems, once a week, and help me read them. That's all. You can keep teaching the sums and the catechism to the rest. I'll still be here for that. I'll still learn it. But I want more than that, and I think you can give it to me."
