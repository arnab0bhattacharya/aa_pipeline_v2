The lamp in Mrs. Ellerby’s parlour burned with that steady, unadorned flame which seems the very emblem of a life regulated by duty, and it shed its light upon a scene of quiet, concentrated preparation. Young Theodore Ellerby sat at the table, his books spread before him in a manner that suggested not the eager, desultory reading of a scholar, but the grim, methodical advance of a man who must master a subject against his will. There was a commentary on the Thirty-Nine Articles, a volume of Paley’s Evidences, and a manuscript book in which he had been wont to inscribe, with a neatness that now seemed to him a species of irony, the heads of his sermons. His pen lay idle by his hand, and his eyes, instead of following the close-printed lines of the text, were fixed upon the window, against which the wind of a bleak March evening was driving the rain in fitful, passionate gusts.

He was a young man of a fine, thoughtful countenance, in which the lines of a natural gentleness contended with the marks of an inward struggle that was fast assuming the character of a settled gloom. The living of Beckworth, a comfortable rectory with its acres of glebe and its modest but certain income, had been destined for him from his cradle. It was the one fixed star in the somewhat clouded firmament of the Ellerby fortunes. His father, a clergyman of the old school, had died when Theodore was but a boy, leaving his widow and children with a small independence, quite insufficient to maintain the position of gentility to which they had been born. The living, in the gift of a distant cousin, was to be the family’s salvation; and Mrs. Ellerby, a woman of strong affections and a piety that was the very substance of her life, had woven the hope of it into every prayer she had offered for twenty years. To see her son ordained, to see him installed in the rectory of Beckworth, to have him near her, a guide and a comfort, was the crown of her earthly desires.

Theodore had accepted the prospect with the docility of a loving son. He had gone to Oxford, had distinguished himself, had taken his degree, and had returned to the little town of Millcote to prepare for ordination under the eye of the venerable Archdeacon Grantly, a family friend. But the process of preparation, which should have been the confirmation of his faith, had proved its solvent. The very studies which were meant to arm him for the defence of the Church had, in his hands, become instruments of doubt. He had followed the stream of German criticism in its subterranean course; he had weighed the evidences of miracles and found them wanting; he had examined the history of the Church and found it a record of human passion and human error as much as of divine guidance. The figure of Christ, which had been to him the object of a childlike adoration, had receded into the mists of a remote and unverifiable antiquity. He was no longer a believer. The discovery had come upon him slowly, like a creeping sickness, and the struggle to retain his early faith had been long and agonising. But the struggle was over; and he was left with the cold, clear, terrible conviction that he could not stand before a congregation and profess to be a minister of a creed he had ceased to hold.

The thought of his mother was the sharpest thorn in his side. He could picture her now, as she sat in her own room, her Bible open on her knees, her lips moving in that prayer which had been the daily incense of her life—a prayer for her son’s prosperity in the sacred office to which he was dedicated. To tell her the truth was to break her heart. It was to shatter the fair edifice of hope which she had been building for twenty years. And there was the money too—the miserable, necessary money. What was to become of them? The small income would not suffice. His sisters must go out as governesses, or they must all sink into that genteel poverty which is the most pitiable of all the forms of penury.

The door opened, and Mrs. Ellerby entered, carrying a small tray with a basin of gruel. She was a woman of fifty, with a face that had once been handsome, and was still pleasing from its expression of serene and settled goodness. Her hair was grey, and her dress was of a plain, dark stuff, but there was a certain dignity in her carriage, a certain refinement in her manner, which spoke of a life lived in the atmosphere of gentle and cultivated ideas. She set the tray down on the table beside her son, and looked at him with a tender anxiety.

“You are pale, Theodore,” she said, laying her hand on his shoulder. “You study too closely. You must not let the archdeacon’s examinations wear you to a shadow. There is no need to be so anxious. You know your subject well.”

He started at her touch, and the pen he had taken up fell from his fingers. He rose from his chair, and stood before her, his face working with emotion. He felt that he could not endure the suspense another moment. He must speak, even if the words were as a sword in her heart.

“Mother,” he said, in a voice that he hardly recognised as his own, “I have something to tell you. It is not about my studies. It is something more serious—far more serious. I cannot be ordained.”

Mrs. Ellerby’s face grew white. She put out her hand as if to steady herself against the table. “Cannot be ordained, Theodore? What do you mean? You are not ill? You have not lost your voice?”
