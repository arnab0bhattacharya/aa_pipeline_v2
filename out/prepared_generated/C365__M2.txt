The announcement had rippled through the corridors of Ashcroft Hall like a shockwave, shattering the polite, suffocating silence that had defined its final days. Lord Alistair Thorne, eighty-two and brittle as dry kindling, had stood at the head of the long mahogany table in the dining room, his voice surprisingly steady as he declared that his entire estate would be bequeathed to the St. Jude’s Orphanage in Norwich. The reaction had been immediate and visceral. His cousin, Sir Julian, had turned a shade of purple that matched the velvet drapes. His niece, Lady Elara, had fainted, or appeared to, into the arms of a footman who looked terrified to catch her. The other relatives, a constellation of vultures who had gathered in the manor like crows on a wire, had erupted into a cacophony of sputtering protests, accusations of madness, and veiled threats.

Alistair had merely watched them. He had watched the greed strip away the veneer of family affection, leaving only the raw, ugly architecture of their desires. He had signed the papers in front of his solicitor, Mr. Halloway, a man whose eyes were always hidden behind thick lenses, and then he had dismissed the room. He did not look back as the doors swung shut. He was tired. He was tired of the perfume, the forced laughter, the endless recitations of lineage that served only to remind him of what they thought he owed them. He was tired of being a wallet with a pulse.

In the weeks that followed, the house emptied out. The carriages rolled away one by one, taking the relatives back to their own estates, their own debts, and their own resentments. They promised to return, of course. Letters arrived daily, thick with ink and honeyed lies, begging for a reconsideration, a "mistake," or at least a modest stipend. Alistair read them with the detached interest of a man reading the weather report. Then, he threw them into the fireplace.

Only one visitor remained.

His name was Thomas. He was twenty-two, quiet, and possessed of a face that seemed to have been eroded by time before it had fully formed, giving him an air of permanent, gentle apology. Thomas was Alistair’s ward, a boy taken in after his father, a minor poet and a major drunk, had drowned in the Wenside River. Thomas had no claim to the fortune. He had no claim to the name. He was, in the eyes of the relatives, a stray dog that the old man had picked up out of pity. Consequently, Thomas was the only one who had not been disappointed by the will. He was the only one who did not need to apologize for his existence.

On a grey Tuesday in November, Thomas arrived at Ashcroft Hall. He did not announce himself. He simply walked up the gravel drive, his boots sinking slightly into the wet earth, and let himself in through the side door that the gardener used. The house was cold. The fires had been let die in the sitting rooms, a symbolic death of the household’s spirit. The air smelled of damp stone and decay, of old paper and the faint, sweet rot of flowers left too long in vases.

Alistair was in his study, a room lined with books he had never read and maps of places he had never visited. He was sitting in a wingback chair, a blanket over his knees, staring at the window where the rain lashed against the glass. He did not turn when the door opened. He knew the weight of Thomas’s footsteps; they were light, hesitant, as if the boy feared his presence might disturb the dust motes dancing in the shafts of afternoon light.

"Good afternoon, my lord," Thomas said. His voice was soft, barely rising above the sound of the rain.

Alistair closed his eyes. "You need not come, Thomas. They are all gone. There is no one left to disappoint."

"Disappointment is a luxury, sir, I find I cannot afford," Thomas said. He moved to the fireplace, where a single log lay unburned. He did not light it. He simply stood there, looking at the hearth. "I brought tea. It is still warm. I brewed it in the kitchen."

Alistair opened his eyes. He looked at the young man. Thomas was wearing a coat that had seen better decades, the elbows frayed. He held a silver tray with the care of a surgeon holding a scalpel. On the tray sat two cups, simple porcelain, and a small teapot.

"You should not be here," Alistair said, though his voice lacked its usual edge. "There is nothing to gain. Nothing to salvage. I am a ruined man, Thomas. A fool who has thrown away his legacy to satisfy a whim."

Thomas set the tray on the low table beside Alistair’s chair. He did not sit. He stood by the window, watching the rain blur the view of the estate. The trees, once manicured into perfect spheres, now looked like grasping hands.

"Legacy," Thomas mused. "Is it the gold, sir? Or is it the story?"

Alistair frowned. "It is the gold. It is the power. It is the ability to command. Without it, I am nothing. A shell."

"Then perhaps you were a shell before," Thomas said gently. "I remember when I first came to you. I was twelve. I was afraid of the house. I was afraid of the silence. You told me that the house was not the master. The house was the servant. You told me that if I stopped trying to impress the walls, I would stop being afraid of them."

Alistair turned over in his mind. He had said that. He had said it to comfort a frightened child. He had not realized it was a confession.

"They want to take me," Alistair said, his voice dropping. "Not you. They want to take me. They want to strip me of my dignity before they strip me of my coin. They call me mad. They call me senile. They do not speak to me as a man, but as a failing mechanism."
