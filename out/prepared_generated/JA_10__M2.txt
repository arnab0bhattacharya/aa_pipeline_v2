It was a truth universally acknowledged, at least in the watering-places of the west of England, that a mother in possession of a good complexion and a moderate degree of vanity must be in want of a remedy for a constitution that was decidedly too robust to require it. Mrs. Hartley, a woman of forty-five, possessed both these attributes in such abundance that her daughter, Miss Eliza Hartley, had long since despaired of finding any medical authority capable of convincing the world that her mother was not merely well, but thriving. And yet, here they were, established in a set of rooms at the Crown Hotel in Cheltenham, the windows thrown wide to admit the damp October air, and Mrs. Hartley seated by the fire, looking as if she had been expecting a guest of some consequence rather than the inevitable chill of the season.

Eliza, who had been educated in the belief that her mother’s ailments were the result of a nervous constitution susceptible to the slightest change of weather, had accompanied her with the resigned curiosity of one who knows the truth but chooses to maintain the fiction for the sake of family harmony. She had expected to spend the weeks in a state of benign inactivity, taking the waters with a skeptical eye and observing the local gentry with the detached amusement proper to a young lady of twenty who had seen enough of the world to be unimpressed by its superficialities. But the first morning had brought a letter, delivered by a footman with an air of excessive deference, which Mrs. Hartley had read with a tremor of the hand that Eliza had initially mistaken for a return of her supposed nervous debility.

The letter was from Mr. Arthur Penhaligon.

The name struck Eliza with the force of a revelation that was both startling and deeply inconvenient. Mr. Penhaligon had been the gentleman who, three years prior, had paid such assiduous attention to her mother at the previous summer’s assembly in Bath. He had been handsome, certainly, and possessed of a fortune that was respectable without being ostentatious, but Eliza had always regarded him as a creature of polished manners and limited imagination. He had been, in the eyes of the family, a suitor of the second tier—safe, sensible, and utterly lacking in the spark of genius that Eliza, with her superior intellect, had always deemed essential to a true match. When Mrs. Hartley had rejected him with a delicacy that Eliza had privately admired for its firmness, it had been understood that the matter was closed, and that Mrs. Hartley’s preference for the quieter, more manageable Mr. Sterling, a lawyer of no particular distinction, was the final verdict.

And yet, here was Mr. Penhaligon, now a widower, and here was Mrs. Hartley, now a “patient,” seemingly ready to reopen the correspondence of a past that Eliza had thought safely buried.

Eliza watched her mother fold the letter with a care that suggested it contained not merely an invitation to tea, but a proposition of some gravity. Mrs. Hartley’s eyes, usually bright with a mischievous glint that Eliza had learned to interpret as an impending scheme, were now fixed on the fire with an intensity that bordered on the devout.

“Eliza,” her mother said, her voice taking on that particular tone of serene confidence that Eliza knew meant a change of plans was imminent. “I believe I must accept Mr. Penhaligon’s invitation to dine this evening. The waters have done me such good, you know, that I find myself in a state of spirits that would be ill-advised to resist. One cannot be too particular in one’s engagements when one is recovering, can one?”

Eliza felt a chill that had nothing to do with the October wind. She looked at her mother, who was already reaching for her shawl, her movements precise and practiced. There was no fever in Mrs. Hartley’s cheek, no pallor in her lips, only a heightened color that Eliza now recognized as the blush of anticipation, not of illness.

“Mother,” Eliza said, trying to keep her voice light, “I had not thought you possessed the energy for a formal dinner. You were complaining of fatigue only this morning.”

Mrs. Hartley turned to her with a smile that was too sweet to be entirely comforting. “Fatigue is a relative term, my dear. And Mr. Penhaligon is a most agreeable companion. I find I am quite eager to renew our acquaintance. It has been a long time since we last met, and I confess I have been thinking of him with some pleasure.”

The words hung in the air, absurd and undeniable. Eliza felt a surge of emotion that was a complicated mixture of indignation and a reluctant, dawning delight. Indignation, because her mother had been playing a farce upon the public, and upon herself, for weeks, solely to manufacture the opportunity to see a man she had previously dismissed with such finality. But delight, because if Mrs. Hartley was indeed going to marry Mr. Penhaligon, Eliza would be free of the suffocating supervision of a mother who treated her life as a medical case to be managed.

Eliza looked at the letter in her mother’s hand. It was a simple sheet of paper, but it represented a shift in the entire architecture of their lives. Mrs. Hartley had not come to Cheltenham for her health; she had come for her heart. And in doing so, she had displayed a cunning that Eliza had never previously credited her with. Her mother had used her own supposed frailty as a shield, a justification for the very activity of courtship, while Eliza, the supposed guardian of the household, had been left in a state of helpless observation, her superior intellect rendered useless against the sheer force of her mother’s will.

“Very well, Mother,” Eliza said, forcing a smile that felt brittle on her face. “I hope you will find the company agreeable. I shall remain in the room, I fear my constitution is not yet strong enough for the exertions of a dinner party.”
