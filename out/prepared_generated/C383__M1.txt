The proposal had been made in the library of the house in Kensington, a room that smelled of leather, beeswax, and the dry, papery scent of history. Alistair Finch, his hands clasped behind his back in a posture of deferential strength, had looked not at her, but at the portrait of her great-grandfather, a judge of formidable repute.

“My dear Miss Vance,” he had begun, his voice as earnest and level as a perfectly drafted deed of trust. “For some time, I have felt a… a profound admiration. I believe we are well-suited. My position is now secure, thanks to your father’s mentorship. I can offer you a life of stability and… and quiet respect.”

Eleanor Vance, then, had looked at Alistair’s face. It was a good face, symmetrical and pleasant, with clear eyes and a jaw that spoke of a steady, unshakeable nature. He was her father’s star pupil, the young man who could already parse a Torts case with the weary precision of a barrister twice his age. He was kind. He was reliable. He was, she thought with a clinical detachment, the logical conclusion to the life she had been raised to expect. There was no reason to say no. She had accepted.

Now, three months into the engagement, the search for a reason to have said yes felt like a lonely archaeological dig. She was excavating her own life, brushing away the dust of routine, hoping to find some shard of passion, some fossil of a feeling she might have overlooked.

Her days had a rhythm as predictable as a grandfather clock. Mornings were spent at the Kensington house, with her mother, overseeing the menus and the household accounts with a brisk efficiency. Afternoons were often spent at Lincoln’s Inn, where her father, Silas Vance, presided over his chambers like a god in a small, paper-strewn universe. It was here that Alistair thrived. He moved through the ancient, hallowed grounds with a proprietary air, his briefcase held like a shield, his conversations peppered with Latin phrases and legal precedents.

One Tuesday, she sat on a bench in New Square, the heart of the Inn. The plane trees cast a dappled shade over the manicured lawn. Alistair was beside her, having just come from a consultation. He was explaining, with gentle enthusiasm, the nuances of a recent ruling on property law.

“The principle of *caveat emptor*, you see, is being subtly redefined,” he said, his gaze fixed on the fountain in the centre of the square. “It’s fascinating, really. The implications for future conveyancing are… significant.”

Eleanor watched a pigeon land near the fountain, its head cocked. She tried to feel what he felt. She tried to see the beauty in the logic, the poetry in the precedent. But all she saw was a pigeon. All she felt was the cool stone of the bench through her skirt.

“It sounds very clever, Alistair,” she said, and the words felt like flimsy, inadequate currency.

He turned to her then, his admiration a tangible thing, a warm blanket she felt smothered by. “You always understand the heart of things, Eleanor. It’s one of the things I…”

He didn’t finish. He never did when it came to the truly personal. The sentiment hung in the air, unspoken but understood. She looked away, towards the grand, soot-stained brick of the old buildings. They were magnificent, certainly. They held centuries of argument and ambition, of lives built and unmade on the strength of a clause. But they were also cold. They were designed to contain, to define, to restrict.

Her search for a reason took her to odd places. She began reading novels not from her father’s shelves of legal biographies, but from the circulating library: stories of impossible love, of grand passions, of women who threw everything aside for a single, burning moment. The characters felt alien to her, their choices reckless and illogical. She could not find herself in them.

She found herself, instead, listening more closely to the world around her. In the evenings, as she and Alistair sat in the drawing-room after dinner, he would read the *Times* aloud, his voice a steady drone, while her father smoked his pipe and her mother did her needlepoint. But sometimes, from the street below, she would hear it: the clip-clop of a horse-drawn cab, a snatch of laughter from a passing couple, the distant, mournful call of a newspaper boy. These sounds were like fragments of a different play, one she was not in.

One afternoon, walking through the Inn with Alistair, they passed a young barrister she didn’t recognise. He was leaning against a stone archway, a book of poetry open in his hands. He wasn’t reading it, though. He was just looking up at the slice of sky visible between the rooftops, a faint, crooked smile on his face. As they passed, his eyes met Eleanor’s for a fraction of a second. There was no admiration in them, no assessment. There was only a shared, fleeting awareness of the sky, of the moment. Then he looked back at his book, and the moment was gone.

Alistair, oblivious, was talking about the Lord Chancellor’s latest speech. Eleanor felt a pang of something sharp and unfamiliar. It was not love for the stranger, but a sudden, piercing grief for the life of the mind that was not bound by precedent. A life that could just stand and look at the sky.

That evening, she stood at her bedroom window, looking out over the quiet Kensington square. The gas lamps cast long, lonely shadows. She thought of Alistair’s face, so earnest and clear. He was a good man. He would be a devoted husband. He would provide the stability her family prized, the respectable future they had mapped out for her. He was the correct answer to the question of her life.
