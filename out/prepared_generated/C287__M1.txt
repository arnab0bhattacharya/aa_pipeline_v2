The dust of the revolution had a taste, Padre Tomás knew. It was the taste of dry earth, of gunpowder, and of fear. It coated the tongue and settled in the soul. From the bell tower of the Church of San Miguel, he watched the column of men snake up the mountain road. They were not the polished, blue-coated soldiers of the capital’s garrison, but a ragged river of brown and grey, their rifles slung over shoulders that were more accustomed to the weight of a machete. They were the army of the General, and they had come for the silver of the saints.

He descended the winding stone steps, his old knees protesting with a familiar ache. Inside, the church was cool and silent, the air thick with the scent of beeswax, old stone, and the faint, sweet smell of wilting marigolds from the altar. The light, filtering through the high, grimy windows, illuminated motes of dust dancing in the stillness. It was a holy peace, one he had tried to preserve for fifty years. Now, it was a fragile thing, about to be shattered.

He walked to the small sacristy behind the altar. The true treasure of San Miguel de las Cumbres was not the gilded monstrance or the silver chalice, though those were valuable enough. It was the hidden cache, a legacy of the old viceroyalty, donated by a pious mine owner two centuries ago. A chest of silver bars, a crucifix studded with emeralds, and a collection of gold coins that had never seen the light of day. For generations, the priests of San Miguel had guarded this secret, a fund for the poor, for times of famine or plague. This was a plague of a different sort.

He had already done the work in the dead of the previous night. With a strength born of desperation, he had pried loose the heavy flagstone before the altar of the Virgin of Sorrows. He had lowered the chest, wrapped in burlap and leather, into the cold, dry earth of the crypt below. He had covered it, replaced the stone, and scattered dust to hide the seams. But he knew the ways of men with guns. They would find it. They would tear the church apart stone by stone until they did.

The great wooden doors groaned open. The sound echoed in the nave like a death knell. The soldiers filed in, their boots scuffing the stone floor, their eyes wide and restless, taking in the faded glory of the place. At their head walked a man in a dusty officer’s uniform. He was lean and hard, his face tanned and lined by the sun and the wind of the high sierras. A single scar cut through his left eyebrow, a white slash against his dark skin. He stopped just inside the doorway, letting his gaze sweep over the altar, the statues, the flickering candles.

Then his eyes found the priest.

Padre Tomás felt his breath catch in his throat. He knew that face. Beneath the lines of command and the shadow of the scar, it was the face of a boy he had known. A boy with quick, dark eyes and a restless energy, who had served his mass with clumsy reverence and listened to his sermons with an unnerving intensity.

“Padre,” the officer said. His voice was deeper now, roughened by command, but the cadence was the same.

“Colonel,” Padre Tomás replied, his own voice a dry rasp. He made the sign of the cross over his chest. “You have come for the silver of the saints.”

The officer, Colonel Valerio, took a step forward. His spurs clinked softly on the stone. “I have come for the resources of the Republic, Padre. The Church has hoarded the wealth of the people for too long. We will put it to better use. For the cause of liberation.”

“The liberation of souls is my cause,” the priest said, his hands clasped before him. “The silver belongs to God, and to the poor of this village.”

Valerio gave a short, harsh laugh. “The poor of this village will be better served by a rifle than by a prayer, old man. Where is it? Do not make my men search. They are not gentle.”

“I do not know what you mean,” Padre Tomás lied, his heart hammering against his ribs. “There is the chalice, the censers… what you see is what we have.”

Valerio’s gaze was piercing. It was the same look the boy had given him when he’d asked a difficult question about the nature of sin or the existence of hell. “You were never a good liar, Father. Even when I was a boy, I could tell when you were hiding something. You would look at the floor, just as you are doing now.”

Padre Tomás forced himself to meet the Colonel’s eyes. “I am an old man, Valerio. I have nothing left to hide.”

The name hung in the air between them. A flicker of something—surprise, perhaps even pain—crossed the Colonel’s face before being suppressed. “So you remember me.”

“I baptized you,” the priest said softly. “I taught you your catechism. I gave you your first communion. You were a good boy. Fervent. You loved God.”

“I loved the stories,” Valerio corrected him, his voice flat. “Stories of martyrs and saints who stood up against the tyrants of Rome. You taught me to question authority, Father. You just never imagined I would question yours.”

He turned to his men. “Search the sacristy. Then the vestry. Check behind every painting, under every pew. Break what you must.”

The soldiers moved with practiced efficiency. The sounds of splintering wood and tearing fabric filled the church. A statue of Saint Joseph was knocked from its niche, shattering on the floor. The silence that followed was heavier than the noise. Valerio walked slowly down the aisle, his boots echoing in the cavernous space. He stopped before the altar of the Virgin of Sorrows.
