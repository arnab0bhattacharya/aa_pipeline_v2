The shrubbery at Delaford Place was allowed, by common consent, to be the most retired and the least frequented corner of the whole estate; and it was precisely upon this reputation that Miss Harriet Ellwood had come to depend for the preservation of her peace of mind, and Mr. Frederick Vaughan for the indulgence of his hopes. To say that they met there by accident would be to do them both an injustice; for the walks were intricate, the hedges high, and the hour invariably that which separated the conclusion of morning calls from the summons to dinner. That they should have met at all was a circumstance of which neither could give any satisfactory account, except that the one had a book to finish and the other a great desire to know how it ended; and as Harriet was the sort of young woman whose countenance expressed every feeling before her tongue could be brought to confess it, and Frederick the sort of young man who mistook the warmth of his own admiration for a proof of its reasonableness, their conversation had for some weeks past been of a nature to render the shrubbery exceedingly agreeable to them both, and the approach of the dinner hour exceedingly unwelcome.

It was on a Thursday, and the weather had been uncommonly fine, that they were thus employed. Harriet sat upon a rustic bench, her work lying neglected in her lap, and her eyes fixed upon the gravel with an attention that the gravel could hardly deserve, while Frederick, leaning against the trunk of an ancient thorn, spoke with that happy fluency which is the gift of a man who has never yet been contradicted in a matter of importance. He had just reached the interesting particular of his expectations from an uncle in the navy, when the sound of a foot upon the path behind them produced an effect which no eloquence of his could have achieved. Harriet started; Frederick stood upright; and Mrs. Ellwood, the aunt with whom Harriet resided, and to whose care she had been committed by a dying sister, advanced into the little circle with the air of a woman who had long suspected that walks in shrubberies were not always taken for the sake of exercise.

“I beg your pardon,” said she, with a composure that was far more alarming than any anger. “I had no notion the shrubbery was so much frequented. Mr. Vaughan, I believe. I have heard your name very often from my niece, and I am glad to see you. Pray do not let me interrupt you. Harriet, my love, you look pale; you had better walk a little.”

Frederick, who had a man’s natural dread of being thought to have done something wrong, and no very distinct idea of what that something might be, bowed and attempted a sentence upon the fineness of the evening, which ended where it began. Harriet gathered up her work with a trembling hand, and would have spoken, but her aunt, with a smile of the most gracious description, took her arm, and, turning to the young man, observed that she hoped he would do her the honour of calling at the house on the following morning, as she had a few words to say to him which might as well be said in comfort.

That night was passed by Harriet in a state of mind which it is not pleasant to describe, and by her aunt in a state of perfect tranquillity. Mrs. Ellwood was a widow of small fortune and great decision, who had governed her own household for many years, and had never yet discovered the necessity of consulting anybody in the management of it. She was not a hard woman; she was, on the contrary, sincerely attached to her niece, and sincerely desirous of her happiness; but she had a theory of her own upon the subject of young women, which was, that they were the best judges of their own inclinations, and the worst judges of everything else; and she had a theory of her own upon the subject of young men, which was, that they required to be managed with a firm hand, and that the firm hand should be hers. To forbid the match never entered her head. Mr. Vaughan was rich, or would be rich; his manners were agreeable; and Harriet, who had nothing but a pretty face and a good disposition, might reasonably think herself fortunate. But that such a matter should be left to the discretion of two young people, who had been meeting in a shrubbery without the sanction of their elders, was a proposition which Mrs. Ellwood regarded as altogether inadmissible. She would manage it herself; and she would manage it so well that everybody should be satisfied, and Harriet should have reason to thank her.

The following morning brought Frederick, who had passed the night in a condition between hope and apprehension, and who entered the drawing-room with the look of a man who has come to be hanged and hopes for a reprieve. Mrs. Ellwood received him with the utmost affability. She spoke of the weather, of the roads, of his uncle the admiral, of whom she had made inquiries, and whose character she had no doubt was excellent; and then, having thus disarmed him, she proceeded to the business of the morning.

“I am not blind, Mr. Vaughan,” said she, “to what has been going forward. I have seen it for some time, and I may say I have expected it. You admire my niece; my niece, I believe, is not insensible to you. Nothing could be more natural, and nothing, I may add, could be more agreeable to me. But you will allow that there are forms to be observed, and that a young woman who has been left to my care is not to be disposed of in a shrubbery, like a stray kitten. You will do me the justice to believe that I have no objection to you personally. On the contrary, I approve of you entirely. But I must insist that everything be done properly, and that I be allowed to conduct it.”
