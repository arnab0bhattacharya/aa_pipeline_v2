The summons came upon me at the close of the afternoon lesson, borne by the little serving-maid who was wont to move through the corridors of the Pensionnat with the noiseless celerity of a mouse. Mademoiselle Dufour desired my presence in her cabinet at six o'clock precisely. I dismissed my class with a composure I did not feel, and stood at the tall window of the deserted schoolroom, watching the rain drive in grey sheets across the courtyard, while the words of the message worked upon my spirit like a ferment.

I knew what the interview portended. For a fortnight past, Mademoiselle Dufour had regarded me with a benevolence that was almost alarming in its intensity; she had praised my method of instruction before the assembled staff; she had invited me to her private table on three separate occasions, where I had eaten my portion of carbonade in a silence rendered awkward by the scrutiny of her pale, prominent eyes. And then, but two days since, the directress—a woman whose tongue was as sharp as her nose and whose benevolence, when it appeared, was always the prelude to some demand upon one's conscience—had hinted, with a smile that displayed her yellow teeth, that the position of sous-directeur, about to become vacant through the retirement of the worthy Monsieur Legrand, might be conferred upon a man whose fidelity to the establishment was beyond question; a man, moreover, who should be willing to ally himself with the family of Dufour.

The niece, Mademoiselle Célestine, was a person of unimpeachable respectability. She was thirty years of age, of a full and solid figure, with hair of an indeterminate brown and eyes that held no mystery. She played the piano with mechanical precision, spoke French with the accent of Liège, and possessed a dowry of fifteen thousand francs. She had never, in my hearing, uttered a sentence that betrayed either folly or malice; indeed, she had never uttered a sentence that betrayed anything at all. She was, in short, the most estimable and the most unendurable of women.

And I—I was a teacher of English, thirty-two years old, without fortune, without family, without prospects save those which my own labour might carve from the flint of circumstance. I had come to this city of Belgic commerce five years since, a fugitive from a hundred disappointments, and had found in the Pensionnat Dufour a refuge and a pittance. The pittance was small; the refuge, until of late, had been sufficient.

But there was that in the house which had made it more than a refuge. There was Mademoiselle Verhoeven.

She taught German and history to the younger pupils; she was twenty-eight years old, and she had no connexions whatsoever. Her father had been a clerk in a counting-house at Antwerp; he had died of a fever contracted in the damp cellars of his employer, leaving his daughter with nothing but her education and her pride. She was not beautiful—at least, the world would not have called her so. Her figure was slight to the point of fragility; her face was pale, with high cheekbones and a mouth that seemed made for silence rather than for speech. But her eyes—her eyes were of a grey so deep and clear that they seemed to hold within them the reflection of some inward fire, banked and restrained, yet capable of leaping into flame at the breath of injustice. And when she spoke—which was seldom, and never of herself—her voice had a low, vibrating quality that stirred the heart like the note of a 'cello.

We had never exchanged a word that could be construed as a declaration. We had spoken of books, of the revolutions of '30, of the poverty of the Flemish weavers, of the poetry of Heine. We had walked together, once, in the garden of the Place Verte, on a Sunday afternoon in autumn, when the leaves were falling and the bells of the cathedral were tolling the Angelus; and she had said, looking not at me but at the grey tower against the grey sky, that she thought the lot of woman in this world was to wait, and to be patient, and to hope for that which never came. I had not answered her. What could I answer? I had no home to offer her, no name, no security. I had only a heart that beat fiercely against the bars of its cage whenever she entered the room.

At six o'clock I presented myself at the cabinet of Mademoiselle Dufour. She received me with a graciousness that was more terrible than her accustomed asperity. She motioned me to a chair; she spoke of my services in terms that made my cheek burn; she touched upon the approaching retirement of Monsieur Legrand; she dwelt, with a significant pause, upon the advantages of a permanent alliance with the house of Dufour.

"Célestine," she observed, "is not a woman of brilliant parts; but she is good, she is docile, she has a heart of gold. She would make you an excellent wife, Monsieur Ashford. And the position—the position would be assured to you for life."

I sat silent. The rain beat against the window; the fire crackled on the hearth; the clock upon the mantelpiece ticked with a deliberation that seemed to mock the tumult of my thoughts.

"Mademoiselle," I said at last, "I am deeply sensible of the honour you do me. But I cannot accept it."

Her countenance changed. The benevolence vanished; the sharpness returned.

"Cannot? And why cannot?"

"Because I do not love Mademoiselle Célestine."

"Love!" She uttered the word as though it were an indecency. "Love! And what has love to do with marriage? You are not a schoolgirl, Monsieur Ashford; you are a man of thirty-two, without fortune, without family. You speak of love as though it were a commodity to be weighed in the balance against bread and shelter. I offer you a home, a position, a wife who will respect you and obey you. And you reply that you do not love her!"
