The coach from London set him down at the crossroads just as the bells of St. Aidan’s struck vespers, the sound rolling over the wet rooftops of Thornbury like a tide. Dr. Julian Hale stood in the mud, his medical bag in one hand and his leather trunk at his feet, and listened to the bells he had not heard in three years. The cathedral spire, pale against a bruised evening sky, looked exactly as it had the morning he left for Calcutta, when the world had seemed a simple place and his path as clear as the river below the close. He had gone out to the East India Company’s hospital to save the dying, and to forget a girl. He had succeeded only in the first.

Julian took lodgings above a seed merchant in the lower town, two rooms with a sloping ceiling that smelled of damp plaster and linseed. The practice he had purchased with what remained of his savings was in the same street—a narrow-fronted premises that had belonged to an apothecary who died in the spring, leaving behind a brass plate, a pestle, and a ledger of accounts most of which were marked unpaid. The people of the lower town were hop pickers, tide-workers, and the families of journeymen. They could not pay a guinea for a visit. They paid in eggs, in kindling, in promises made over the bedsides of children whose fevers would not break. Julian worked from dawn until the candles burned low, and he told himself it was enough.

He had been in Thornbury a fortnight when the boy was brought to him. Edward Marsh, eleven years old, thin as a willow switch, carried in the arms of his mother because he could no longer walk the length of the room. His face had the translucent, blue-veined pallor Julian had learned to read in Calcutta, though the disease was different here. The lungs were involved. He listened with his ear to the boy’s narrow chest and heard the wet, rattling commerce of fluid where air should have moved freely. The mother, Mrs. Marsh, stood against the wall with her hands pressed together, and Julian knew her at once though she had aged. She had been a handsome woman once. She was handsome still, in the way of stone that has weathered.

“You are Dr. Hale,” she said. It was not a question. She had known he was here. The whole close would have known within a day of his arrival. He was the boy who had been apprenticed to Dr. Aldous in the cathedral precincts, the boy who had read Virgil in the cathedral library and walked with Margaret Aldous among the tombs. He was the boy who had left without a word of farewell because there was no farewell adequate to the news that Margaret was to marry Sir Henry Coltrane.

“He will need to stay in bed,” Julian said. “For a long time. I will come each day.”

Each day became a geography. The Marsh house stood in a row of grey brick dwellings at the edge of the cathedral green, and the walk from Julian’s rooms took him past the very gates of the close, past the house where Dr. Aldous had lived and where Margaret had grown up, past the canon’s house where she now resided as Lady Coltrane. He kept his eyes forward. He carried his bag and he did not look at the windows.

But Edward was her brother, and the house was the house of her childhood, and Margaret was there on the third morning when Julian climbed the narrow stair to the boy’s room. She stood at the landing with a cup of broth in her hands, and the years between them collapsed into the space of a breath. She was twenty-four. She had been twenty-one when he left. Her hair, which had been the colour of dark honey, was pinned close and severe, and she wore the grey dress of a woman who keeps her own counsel. She looked at him with an expression he could not catalogue—relief, perhaps, or the particular pain of being grateful to someone one has wounded.

“Dr. Hale,” she said. “My mother tells me you have been very kind to Edward.”

“I have done what any physician would do.”

“No physician in Thornbury has done what you have done. Dr. Pell came twice and left a bottle of laudanum. You have come every day. You sat with him through the night when the fever returned.” She paused. She held the cup as though it were a fragile and necessary thing, which it was. “Julian,” she said, and the use of his name was like a key turning in a lock he had believed rusted shut.

He took the cup from her. He did not touch her fingers. He went up the stairs and he sat with the boy and he listened to the ruined lungs and he did not come down for an hour.

The illness ran its course the way such illnesses do in children—not in a straight line toward death or health, but in a zigzag of crises and remissions that wore grooves in the days. Julian bled the boy once, cautiously, and regretted it. He applied blistering resin to the chest and stopped that too. What he did, in the end, was what he had learned in Calcutta from an old Bengali vaidya who worked in the hospital courtyard: he kept the boy warm, he kept the air moving through the room, he gave him teas of marshmallow root and thyme, and he stayed. He stayed when the coughing was so violent that Mrs. Marsh wept in the passage. He stayed when Edward, delirious, called him father. He stayed because staying was the thing he knew how to do, and because the boy’s life had become the single bright wire on which his own was strung.
