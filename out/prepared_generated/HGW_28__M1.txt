It was on a Tuesday in June that the Age of Motoring came to its abrupt and inexplicable end. Mr. Arthur Penrose, proprietor of Penrose Motors & Cycles of Thatcham Cross, Berkshire, was at the time engaged in the delicate business of persuading a farmer that the internal combustion engine was a more reliable companion for a wet October than any number of horses. The farmer, a Mr. Grubb, was sceptical. “It’s all very well, Mr. Penrose,” he was saying, “but a horse knows its way home. This contraption of yours just stops and sulks.”

As if to prove the man’s point with an almost supernatural punctuality, the brand-new 12-hp De Dion-Bouton they were admiring shuddered, gave a final, gaseous sigh, and went silent. Not just the engine, but every light on the dash, every spark of electrical vitality, simply ceased to be.

“Ah!” said Grubb, with immense satisfaction. “You see?”

But it was not merely the De Dion. From the High Street came the sound of a motor omnibus grinding to a halt, followed by a chorus of confused exclamations. A delivery van across the square had stopped dead. Even the ancient, rattling steam traction engine belonging to the council, which had been chugging up the hill, had fallen silent. A strange and profound stillness descended upon the town, a silence broken only by the clop of a single, bewildered pony and the rising murmur of human voices.

Penrose, a man whose entire fortune and future were staked on the noisy, oily, and magnificent promise of the motor car, felt a cold dread creep up his spine. This was not a simple mechanical failure. This was a judgement. He rushed to his workshop, where his two apprentices, young Jenkins and Carter, were staring at a half-disassembled Humber in a state of utter confusion.

“It’s no good, Mr. Penrose,” said Jenkins, holding up a sparking plug. “There’s no current. Not a whisper. It’s as if the electricity’s been drained out of the world.”

Penrose seized a magneto from the bench and spun its flywheel with all his might. Nothing. He checked the battery of a nearby electric brougham. Dead as a stone. The science of it, the clear and practical physics that he had so enthusiastically sold to his customers, had simply packed up and gone home. For a man who had built his life on the predictable behaviour of pistons and volts, this was a form of madness.

The first wave of creditors arrived within the hour. They did not come with lawyers’ letters, but with the much more immediate problem of being stranded. Farmer Grubb was at their head, his face a thundercloud. “My prize bull is due at the Newbury auction tomorrow, Penrose! I’ve got him loaded up in my new Thornycroft truck, and the blasted thing is a metal coffin! You sold me that machine! You and your ‘modern progress’!”

The town was in an uproar. People gathered in knots, their faces pale with a peculiar, modern anxiety. The doctor could not reach a patient in the next village. The post was undelivered. The entire intricate, clockwork mechanism of the new century had seized up. And at the centre of it all stood Arthur Penrose, the prophet of this now-silent age, feeling the weight of their collective failure pressing down upon him.

His wife, Eleanor, found him in the stockroom, staring at a wall of tyres. “Arthur,” she said, her voice tight with fear, “they’re talking about the bank. Mr. Henderson from the bank was here, asking about your overdraft. He says if you can’t liquidate your assets, he’ll have to foreclose.”

The word ‘foreclose’ had a chilling finality. His assets were the silent, useless machines filling his showroom and yard. His fortune was a mountain of scrap iron.

“We have to go,” Eleanor whispered, her practical mind already at work. “Tonight. We can take the pony and trap. We can be in Reading by morning, get the first train to London. We could go to your cousin’s. Start again.”

It was the sensible course. The only course. To flee the wreckage of his ambition and the men who had trusted him. He could picture it: the quiet escape, the anonymous life in the capital, the slow, painful process of forgetting Thatcham Cross and the grand folly of Penrose Motors. He was a ruined man, and a ruined man must think of himself first.

But then he looked out of the grimy window, across the yard to the main road. A family was struggling along the verge, the father carrying a heavy case, the mother trying to comfort a crying child. They were not his creditors; they were simply people whose world had stopped. Further down the road, old Mrs. Gable, the vicar’s widow, was leaning heavily on her cane, trying to walk the two miles to the doctor’s surgery. She moved with a painful, arduous slowness that struck Penrose with a sudden, sharp force.

He thought of the map of the county spread out on his office wall. Thatcham Cross was an island in a sea of countryside. The nearest town of any size, Newbury, was a good seven miles away. A distance easily covered in a quarter of an hour by motor car. On foot, for an old woman or a man with a heavy burden, it was a formidable journey.

The thought came to him not as a noble inspiration, but as a cold, clear observation. He was a dealer in transport. His entire purpose, the very justification for his business, was to move people and goods from one place to another. The mechanism had failed, but the need remained. The problem had changed, but the principle was the same. To run away now would be the ultimate failure, a retreat not just from his creditors, but from the very idea of his trade.

He turned from the window. “Eleanor,” he said, and his voice was firm in a way it had not been all day. “Get the big handcart from the workshop. And the long tarpaulin.”
