The invitation to Berkeley Square, when it came, was received by the two Miss Wrayfords with very different sensations. For Harriet, the elder, it was a mark of family condescension not to be lightly refused; for Sophia, the younger, it was an insult so artfully disguised as a kindness that its very elegance made it more offensive. The letter, from their cousin Mrs. Anstruther, spoke of the delights of the season, the desirability of having their “dear cousins’ faces” about her, and the slight inconvenience of a full house, which she was sure their good nature would overlook.

“Good nature!” cried Sophia, pacing the small parlour of their Bath lodgings. “She calls it good nature to be lodged in the attics, while half the town is received in her drawing-room. We are to be the poor relations, Harriet, kept to be looked at and to be pitied. I can see her now, introducing us to some dowager: ‘My cousins, the Miss Wrayfords. So sensible, so unaffected. They do not mind at all.’”

Harriet, who was mending a glove, did not look up. “We are to be lodged and fed in a fine house in London for three months, and to see all the society it can offer. If the price of such advantages is a little condescension from a woman who has married a Baronet, I am sure we can afford to pay it. Eleanor is our cousin. It is not for us to question the manner of her kindness.”

“The manner is the substance!” Sophia retorted, stopping before the window. “Her kindness is a sort of tyranny. She means to make us feel our dependence at every turn. I should rather stay here and mend my own clothes than be given a new bonnet by her and be told how well it becomes a person of my ‘quiet pretensions.’”

But the matter was not to be decided by Sophia’s pride. Their situation, though respectable, was narrow, and a season in London, however compromised, was not to be thrown away. A week later they found themselves rattling over the stones of Berkeley Square in a hired coach, their own modest trunks following in a cart, and were ushered into the grandeur of Anstruther House.

Lady Anstruther, their cousin Eleanor, received them in a saloon of such magnificence that Sophia’s resentment was for a moment silenced by awe. The ceilings were painted with allegorical figures, the walls hung with silk, and the air was thick with the scent of hothouse flowers and beeswax. Eleanor herself, splendid in a morning dress of figured muslin, rose to meet them with a smile that was perfectly correct and entirely devoid of warmth.

“My dear Harriet. My dear Sophia. How good of you to come at such short notice. You must be fatigued. The journey from Bath is not a short one. I trust you found the coach comfortable?” Her eyes, as she spoke, took in their travelling dresses, and a flicker of something—pity, perhaps, or satisfaction—passed over her features.

“It was a tolerable journey, thank you, Cousin,” said Harriet, with a proper degree of gratitude. “We are very sensible of the honour of your invitation.”

“The honour is mine, I am sure,” Eleanor replied, in a tone that suggested the honour was a very trifling one indeed. “We are such a party that I fear we cannot offer you much quiet. But I thought the change of scene might be of service to you. Sophia, my dear, you are grown. I declare I should not have known you.”

Sophia, who felt she was being assessed like a piece of furniture, could only curtsey. She was resolved to say as little as possible, and to let her silence be a reproach.

Their rooms, when they were shown to them by a silent footman, were in the very top of the house, as Sophia had predicted. They were clean, but small and plainly furnished, looking out over a vista of chimney-pots. The contrast with the state rooms below was a deliberate one, and it was not lost upon either sister.

“This will do very well,” said Harriet, calmly removing her bonnet. “We are not come to be great ladies, but to be guests.”

“We are come to be reminded we are neither,” Sophia muttered, throwing herself into a chair. “Did you see the look she gave my pelisse? She thinks us a pair of dowds from the country. Well, let her. I shall not be made to feel ashamed of what I cannot help.”

For the first week, their life at Anstruther House followed a pattern of elegant neglect. They were included in the family’s plans when it suited, and forgotten when it did not. They were taken to the park in the carriage when there was room, but were left to walk when Lady Anstruther had more favoured companions. At dinner, they were seated at the far end of the great table, near some elderly baronet or a dull country cousin, and were expected to be grateful for the conversation. Sophia bore it all with a bitter, silent grace, her every look a declaration of her wounded pride. She saw insults in every civility and slights in every omission.

Harriet, however, observed more than she felt. She watched their cousin not with resentment, but with a growing and curious pity. She saw that Eleanor’s triumph was a strangely joyless affair. The great house did not seem to be a home; it was a stage, and her cousin its chief actress, forever performing the part of Lady Anstruther. Her smile was a fixed thing, her conversation a series of polished phrases, her authority over her household absolute and cold. Her husband, Sir Arthur, was a man of immense fortune and no conversation, who seemed to think his duty was done in providing his wife with an establishment, and in ignoring her existence within it.
