There are few histories more inscrutable to the ordinary eye than the slow, stealthy shifting of rural hierarchies, where the solid landmarks of three generations are dissolved not by sudden catastrophe, but by the quiet, corrosive action of debt, inheritance, and the municipal appetite for country sport. Such was the case with the Phipps estate. For fourscore years the broad acres of Wanstone Hill had belonged to a family who, residing continually in their Wiltshire manor, had known their shepherds and their ox-herds as familiarly as the seasons themselves. But the third Squire Phipps, inheriting the convivial temper of his father without the corresponding acres, had found it expedient to let the land to a stranger from Hampshire, while he himself migrated to Bath to take the waters and seek a wealthy bride. Thus it happened that the tenantry of Wanstone, accustomed to a landlord whose eye was as familiar as the village church-spire, found themselves suddenly subject to the critical surveillance of Mr. Evelyn—a gentleman whose notions of agriculture were drawn from the pages of Mr. Coke of Norfolk, and who consequently regarded the immemorial customs of the Wiltshire downland as so many proofs of a sluggish and unenlightened husbandry.

It was upon this new order of things that old Abel Grover found himself suddenly, if not unexpectedly, cast adrift. Abel had entered upon the Wanstone farm as a boy of ten, learning to fold the sheep under the tuition of his father; and for nearly fifty years he had traversed those swelling chalk hills with a step as measured and unvarying as the constellations. His face, weather-beaten to the hue of the local flint, bore the deep furrows of a hundred winters; his eye, though somewhat dimmed by the gathering mists of age, still held the patient, far-looking gaze of one accustomed to watch the heavens rather than the earth. He had outlived his wife, his sons, and even the old Squire; and now, in the late afternoon of his life, he retained but one anchorage in the shifting tides: his grandson, Jabez. Jabez, a sharp-featured boy of thirteen, possessed that restless, acquisitive intelligence which is sometimes found among the sons of the soil, and which, lacking the appointed channels of education, is apt to run into odd and self-taught courses. He had learned his letters from the parson’s daughter, and had advanced so far in writing as to be able to compose a missive of no mean pretension—a fact which filled Abel with a wonder that was not unmixed with awe, as of a man who beholds a supernatural feat performed in his own kitchen.

It was on a raw November morning, when the mists lay heavy in the combes, that the new tenant, Mr. Evelyn, walking the farm with an eye to improvements, encountered Abel superintending the folding of the ewes. The old man’s movements, it must be owned, were not characterized by the alertness which Mr. Evelyn had observed in his Hampshire shepherds; his dog, too, was somewhat deaf and slow to obey. Observing these deficiencies, Mr. Evelyn, who was not a man to mince matters, addressed the shepherd with a directness that was perhaps more kind than tactful.

"Look ye, Grover," said he, "I have no mind to see the flock suffer for the sake of old acquaintance. You have served the farm well, I make no doubt, in your day; but your day is drawing to a close. You must be nearing seventy, I should think, and a man of that age has no business on the hills in such weather as this. I shall want a younger hand before lambing-time."

Abel, who had straightened himself to his full, though diminished, height, received this intimation in silence. His eye wandered from the tenant’s face to the distant line of the downs, where the grey sky met the greyer earth, as if he sought in that familiar prospect some argument against the decree. But the downs, for the first time in his memory, offered him no counsel. He merely said, in his slow Wiltshire speech, "I be old, zur, I alloo; but I knaws the sheep, and they knaws me. Ther’s not a ewe on the hill but what’ll come to my call; and as for the lambing, I’ve seen fifty seasons of it, and never lost a one as I could save."

Mr. Evelyn, though not entirely unmoved, was inexorable. "I dare say, I dare say," he replied; "but we must have new blood, new methods. You had better speak to the steward about a cottage. You shall not want a corner to die in, I’ll warrant that." And so saying, he strode on, leaving Abel standing by the hurdle, with his crook in his hand and the weight of half a century pressing upon his heart.

The old man returned to his cottage that evening with a step heavier than usual. Jabez, who had been sent home from the plough with a bruised foot, was sitting by the fire, conning over a tattered almanac by the light of a dip. He looked up as his grandfather entered, and reading in the old man’s face something of what had passed—for the boy had a quick perception of feeling, and could read the lines of sorrow as readily as the printed page—inquired what had befallen.

Abel, seating himself heavily, recounted the interview with the tenant. The boy listened in silence; but his dark eyes kindled, and a flush rose to his thin cheeks. He was silent for a moment, his mind evidently working with unusual speed; then he looked up with an air of resolution.

"Grandfather," said he, "we’ll write to th’ Squire."

Abel stared. "To the Squire? And what should a letter say to him? He’s a deal too grand to mind the likes of us."
