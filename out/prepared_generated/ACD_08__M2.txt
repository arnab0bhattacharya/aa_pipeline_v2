The rain lashed against the soiled panes of the window in Mrs. Pritchard’s sitting room, a relentless, drumming hiss that seemed to amplify the silence within. She sat rigid in her velvet armchair, her hands clasped tightly in her lap, the white knuckles betraying a tremor that she could not quite suppress. Across from her, Mr. Arthur Sterling sat with that peculiar, still intensity of his own, his dark eyes fixed not on the landlady, but on the small, brass-bound trunk that stood in the corner of the room like a monument to a crime not yet committed.

“You say, Mrs. Pritchard,” Sterling began, his voice low and precise, cutting through the sound of the storm, “that he was a man of very few words.”

“None, sir, really,” she replied, her tone sharp with residual fear. “He did not speak to the other lodgers. He did not speak to me. He paid his rent in three months’ advance, in gold sovereigns, counted out on the table with a dexterity that suggested he handled money, if not people, with great ease. He was Mr. Silas Vane. That was the name he gave me. A quiet man. A pale man. And he burned a candle in his window every single night until the hour of two.”

Sterling raised an eyebrow. “A candle until two. In the gaslit age of London? That is a peculiar habit.”

“It was more than a habit, sir. It was a ritual. And it was the last thing I saw of him. This morning, when I went up to collect the rent for the next month, his door was locked. I knocked. I waited. I called out. There was no sound from within. I then used the master key, which he had allowed me to keep in the drawer, and entered the room. It was empty. His bed was made with military precision. His clothes were hanging in the wardrobe. But he was gone. And the candle, sir, was still burning. The wick had burned down to the last inch, but the flame was still alive, though the room was cold and the window was wide open.”

Sterling turned his head slowly toward the window, where the rain still streaked the glass. “A strange ending for a routine,” he murmured. “And the trunk?”

“Left behind. He took nothing else. No hat, no coat, no bag. Just the trunk, which he had unpacked on the first day and never touched again. It is heavy, sir. I could not move it from the landing. I had to bring it down here for you to inspect, for I knew the police would only see a vagrant or a madman. But I knew, Mr. Sterling, that this was no vagrant. He had a room with a view of the garden, he ate well, and he paid without a murmur. There is a mystery here, and I do not trust the constables to find it before the truth is lost.”

Sterling stood up and walked toward the trunk. He did not open it immediately. Instead, he walked around it, observing its position relative to the door, the window, and the center of the room. He was a man who believed that objects, like people, left footprints.

“May I?” he asked, gesturing to the latch.

Mrs. Pritchard nodded, stepping back as if the trunk itself were a venomous creature. Sterling lifted the lid. The interior was lined with a faded blue silk, now discolored by time and damp. Inside, there was no clothing, no jewelry, no books. There was only a single, heavy object wrapped in a white cloth.

Sterling unwrapped the cloth with careful fingers. Beneath it lay a large, iron-bound key, and next to it, a small, leather-bound notebook. The key was heavy, its teeth jagged and complex, the kind of mechanism used for strong bank vaults or perhaps the doors of a private study. The notebook was thick, its pages dense with a cramped, slanted script.

“A key and a journal,” Sterling observed, turning the key over in his hand. “And yet no map, no address, no name other than the one you gave me. Mr. Vane was a man of contradictions, Mrs. Pritchard. He paid in advance, suggesting a desire for anonymity or a long absence, yet he burned a candle until two, a time when most respectable men were asleep. Why?”

“I do not know, sir. I thought it strange. I thought he was waiting for someone. Or perhaps he was keeping watch.”

“Waiting for whom?”

“I cannot say. But I did see a man come to the door on the night he vanished. It was late, past midnight. I was in the kitchen, and I heard the lock turn. I heard two voices, low and urgent. One was Mr. Vane’s. The other was deep, rough, and foreign. I crept to the window, but I could see nothing but the rain. When I looked again, the candle in Mr. Vane’s window was out. I assumed he had gone to bed. But the next morning, he was gone.”

Sterling opened the notebook. The handwriting was consistent, meticulous. He skimmed the first few pages. “Here,” he said, pointing to a date three days before Vane’s disappearance. “‘The cipher is complete. The location is secure. I must ensure the transmission is made before the deadline. If I fail, the consequence is beyond reckoning.’”

“Transmission?” Mrs. Pritchard whispered. “Of what?”

“I am not yet sure. But look here.” Sterling turned the page. “‘The candle is the signal. When the flame dies, the window must be open. He will come then. I must be ready.’”

Sterling looked up at the landlady, his expression grave. “Mrs. Pritchard, you said the candle was still burning when you found the room empty. But the window was open. If the candle was the signal, and the window was the condition, then the signal was given. But if the candle was still burning, it means the signal was not completed. Or, perhaps, it means that Vane did not intend for it to die. He intended to stay until the end.”
