It was on the third of October, a blustery and rain-swept afternoon, that I received a telegram from my old friend Sir Percival Renshaw, of Wychwood Hall in Sussex, requesting my immediate presence at that house upon a matter of the utmost delicacy. His message was characteristically brief, yet I detected in its terse phrasing a note of genuine agitation which was foreign to his usual bluff composure. Accordingly, I caught the afternoon train from Victoria, and by four o’clock found myself being driven in a dog-cart through the dripping beech-woods that screen the village of Felpham from the Downs.

Wychwood Hall is a long, low house of grey stone, built in the reign of the second Charles, and it wears its years with a certain dignified indifference. I was shown at once into the library, where Sir Percival stood before the fire, a tall, spare man of seventy, with a face like a weathered hawk and a manner that spoke of a lifetime of command. Beside him, her hands clasped nervously before her, stood his housekeeper, a small, sharp-eyed woman of middle age named Mrs. Tregellis, who had served the family for thirty years.

“You are very good to come, Doctor,” said the baronet, motioning me to a chair. “I find myself in a position which requires a cooler head than my own. The fact is, I have received a visit from a young man who claims to be my nephew, and I am at a loss to know whether to believe him or to have him thrown into the road.”

“A nephew?” said I. “I had understood that you were the last of your line.”

“So I believed. My elder brother, George, was lost at sea some five-and-thirty years ago, upon a voyage to the Cape. He was unmarried when he sailed, or so we all supposed. Now comes this young fellow, with a story of a secret marriage and a childhood spent in the colony, and with such a knowledge of our private affairs as would stagger the most sceptical man in England.”

Mrs. Tregellis made a small sound of protest. “I beg your pardon, Sir Percival, but I must speak. I knew Mr. George as well as I know my own hand. He was a wild one, I’ll not deny it, but he was no deceiver. If he had married, he would have written to his father. He was not a man for secrets.”

“You see how it is,” said the baronet, with a faint smile. “My good Mrs. Tregellis is the very soul of loyalty, but she would have it that the young man is an impostor. I desire you to meet him and to form your own judgement. He is in the morning-room at this moment.”

I asked that the claimant be brought before me, and in a few minutes the door opened to admit a young man of perhaps eight-and-twenty, well-knit and sunburnt, with a frank, open countenance and a manner that was respectful without being servile. He was dressed in a suit of good but well-worn tweed, and he carried his hat in his hand with the air of one who had been bred in the colonies.

“This is Mr. Henry Renshaw,” said the baronet, “or so he calls himself. Mr. Renshaw, this is Doctor Merivale, an old friend of mine, who has kindly consented to hear your story.”

The young man bowed. “I am glad to tell it, sir, for I have nothing to conceal. My father was George Renshaw, and my mother was Maria Ferreira, the daughter of a wine-merchant at Cape Town. They were married there in the year ’sixty-one, and I was born the following year. My father was drowned in ’sixty-four, when the *Perseverance* went down off the Needles, and my mother died when I was ten. I was brought up by her people, and it is only now, upon the death of my last surviving relative, that I have thought it right to come to England and make myself known to my father’s brother.”

“And how do you explain,” said I, “that your existence has remained a secret for so many years?”

“My father, sir, was a proud man, and he had quarrelled with his own father over his marriage. He swore that he would never set foot in England again until he could do so with a fortune. He died before he could make his peace, and my mother, who was a simple, trusting soul, never thought to write. She had no reason to believe that the family would care to hear of a colonial wife and a half-caste child.”

Here Mrs. Tregellis could contain herself no longer. “Half-caste, indeed! And what would you have us believe next? That Mr. George, who was as fair as a lily, should have a son with hair like a gypsy and a skin like a sailor?”

The young man turned to her with a smile that was not without dignity. “My mother was of Portuguese descent, madam, and I have spent my life in the open air. I cannot help my complexion, but I can prove my birth. I have my parents’ marriage-lines, and I have letters from my father to my mother which bear his private mark.”

He produced from an inner pocket a bundle of papers, yellowed with age, and handed them to the baronet. Sir Percival examined them with a troubled brow, and then passed them to me. The marriage certificate was in the form used in the Cape Colony, and the letters, though faded, bore the signature of George Renshaw and a small monogram which the baronet confirmed was his brother’s private seal.

“These are very convincing,” said I, “but they are not conclusive. A clever forger might have obtained the seal, and the certificate is easily imitated. You will pardon me, Mr. Renshaw, if I put a few questions to you. You say you were brought up by your mother’s people. What was the name of the street in which they lived?”
