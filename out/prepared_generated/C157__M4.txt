The rain had not ceased for three days, and the grounds of Wrenfield Hall had turned to a sullen, sucking clay that pulled at the boots of every servant who crossed the yard. It was on such a morning that Ellen Marsh was given her wages and told to be gone before the clock struck noon. She stood in the back kitchen with her box at her feet and her bonnet in her hands, and she did not cry, though her chin trembled like a plucked wire. Mrs. Trant, the housekeeper, would not meet her eye. “It is not for me to say,” Mrs. Trant murmured, “but her ladyship has made up her mind, and Mr. Rusk agrees the matter is plain.” The matter, as everyone in the house now knew, was the sapphire necklace—the great blue stone set in diamonds that Lady Ashbury had worn to the Hunt Ball and had not worn since—which had vanished from its velvet bed in the dressing-room on Tuesday last, and which had been found, or so it was said, in the lining of Ellen’s own workbasket.

Ellen had sworn until her voice cracked that she had never touched the thing, that she had not so much as opened the case since the evening she had helped her ladyship out of her gown, and that anyone might have put it there. But the case was locked, and only two keys existed, and her ladyship’s was on the chain about her neck, and Mrs. Trant’s was in the housekeeper’s cupboard, and Ellen’s own key—for the girl was trusted with the smaller jewel case—was in the pocket of her apron where it had always been. Mr. Rusk, the agent, had made a great show of examining the lock and had pronounced it untampered. The constable from the village had written it all down in a slow, laborious hand. And so Ellen Marsh, who had been in service since she was thirteen and who had never taken so much as a pin, walked out through the tradesmen’s gate in the rain with her box and her ruined name.

Her brother, Daniel Marsh, was a police constable of four years’ standing, attached to a division in south London, and it was to his lodgings in Lambeth that she came that evening, soaked to the bone and too proud to ask for more than a night’s shelter. Daniel listened to the whole of it twice over, once while she wept and once while she did not, and by the second telling he had begun to feel the cold prickle of doubt at the back of his neck. He knew his sister. He knew, too, the way of country houses, where a missing trinket must be given a thief before the week is out, and where the thief is always the one whose accent is thinnest. But he was a constable, and a constable does not accuse a baronet’s household of carelessness or worse on the strength of a brother’s feeling. So on the Thursday he took the train to the city and climbed the stair to a door in Furnival’s Inn, where a man named Aloysius Pike kept rooms and a reputation.

Pike was a small, dry fellow of perhaps fifty, with spectacles perched on the end of his nose and the manner of a solicitor who had long ago given up expecting clients to tell the truth. He had once been a clerk to the Thames Police and had a genius for the dull particulars that other men overlooked. He heard Daniel out without interruption, turning his pen between his fingers, and when the constable had finished he asked only one question. “Who benefits if the necklace is gone?”

“Her ladyship, sir, in the way of insurance,” said Daniel. “Though she is rich enough not to need it.”

“And who arranged the insurance?”

“Mr. Rusk, the agent. He handles all the estate business.”

Pike nodded slowly and reached for his hat. “Then let us go down to Wrenfield and look at a policy.”

They went on the Friday, in a trap hired from the station, and Pike spent the afternoon in the estate office with the ledgers while Daniel waited in the yard and watched the rain. When Pike came out his spectacles were smudged and his mouth was set in a thin line. “The necklace was insured in her ladyship’s name for eight hundred pounds,” he said. “A week before it vanished, Mr. Rusk had the sum raised to two thousand five hundred. The premium was paid from the estate account, and the endorsement was signed by her ladyship, who, I am told, signs whatever is put before her at breakfast.” He tapped his pocket. “There is also a letter in the file from a jeweller in Hatton Garden, one Mr. Voss, offering to purchase the stone outright for nine hundred, which offer Mr. Rusk declined on her ladyship’s behalf. Curious, is it not, that a man should refuse nine hundred in cash and then arrange to be paid two thousand five hundred for a loss?”

They found Mr. Rusk in the gun-room, cleaning a fowling piece with the air of a man who has nothing to fear. He was a heavy, florid person with a manner of hearty condescension, and he greeted Pike as though he were a tradesman come about a bill. Pike was patient. He spoke of the weather, of the price of coal, of the difficulty of keeping good servants in these modern days, and then, quite gently, he asked whether Mr. Rusk had ever met Mr. Voss of Hatton Garden.

The effect was not dramatic. Rusk did not start or blanch; he merely paused in the wiping of the barrel, and his eyes went flat and still, like a pond in winter. “I have had correspondence with a number of jewellers,” he said. “It is my business to know the value of her ladyship’s things.”
