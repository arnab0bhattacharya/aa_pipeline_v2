The wind from the moors came down upon Stonecroft Parsonage like a summoned spirit, rattling the wainscoting and driving the rain in sheets against the diamond panes. It was the wild equinox, stripping the bleak Yorkshire hillsides to their bones. I stood in the narrow kitchen garden, the wind pulling at my bonnet and my grey stuff gown, watching the clouds tear across the face of the livid sky. I was thirty years old, and my life was ending.

I do not mean my natural life, which was sturdy enough—I had never been ailing or fragile—but the life of purpose, the quiet routine that had been my sustenance for nine years. I had kept house for Arthur. He was my elder by five years, the curate of this damp, stone-heavy parish, left a widower two winters past. I had stepped into the void left by Martha’s passing. I had ordered the meals, managed the recalcitrant servants, visited the sick of the parish, and copied his sermons. I had been useful. I had been necessary. But necessity, I was to learn, is a shifting sand.

I turned and went into the house, shaking the wet from my shawl. The parlour fire was low; I knelt on the hearthrug and thrust the iron poker deep into the coals, rousing a sudden, angry flare of crimson light. The room was spare and cold, smelling of damp plaster and old paper. Arthur sat at his desk by the window, a folio of parish registers open before him. He was a handsome man, though worn, with a high, pale brow and eyes of a washed-out blue that could look gentle in the pulpit but often looked evasive in private converse.

"Eleanor," he said, without looking up. "You are soaked. You should not expose yourself to the gale."

"The gale does not trouble me," I replied, standing and warming my hands at the blaze. My voice was steady, though my heart beat with a heavy, muffled stroke against my ribs. "It is better than the stagnation of the house."

He turned a page. The scratch of the stiff paper was the only sound for a minute. Then he spoke again, and his tone was measured, cautiously pleasant, which struck a sudden chill through me. "I have had a letter from Agnes Thornbury. She writes that she will return to Halifax next week."

Miss Thornbury. The daughter of a wealthy mill-owner, a woman of high spirits and independent means. She had been a frequent visitor in Martha’s time, but since my sister-in-law’s death, her presence had become, to my mind, a trifle too frequent. I had said nothing. It was not my place. I was only the sister, only the drudge who kept the hearth swept.

"She will be welcome," I said. The words tasted like wood-ash.

Arthur closed the register and leaned back in his chair, lacing his fingers together over his waistcoat. He looked at me then, and I saw a certain firmness, a resolve, in his jaw. "I am glad you think so, Eleanor. For I have asked her to be my wife. She has accepted."

The fire hissed. A log shifted and fell with a clatter into the grate. I did not move. I stood perfectly still, feeling the heat scorch the hem of my skirts, feeling the wind shriek against the outer wall like a lost soul. I looked at my brother, and a fierce, hot indignation surged up in my throat. I swallowed it down. I locked my teeth.

"You have done well for yourself, Arthur," I said at last. The words were plain, but they carried the weight of a sudden, crushing realization. "And for the parish."

"She will make an excellent mistress of this house," he continued, misreading my silence for acquiescence. "She has means, Eleanor. The living is poor, you know that. The roof is rotting, and the damp is ruinous. Her fortune will repair Stonecroft. And you—Agnes is a generous girl. She would not wish you to leave. You must stay and help her. She knows nothing of the poor."

"Stay," I repeated. The word was a stone in my mouth. "Stay and be the dependent relation. Stay and be grateful for the scraps of authority she chooses to throw me. Stay and see another woman rule the kitchen and the parish, while I fetch and carry at her bidding."

Arthur frowned, his brow clouding. "You are unjust. You are bitter, Eleanor. It is unseemly."

"I am honest," I flashed, the restraint breaking. I took a step toward him, my hands clenched at my sides. "You speak of generosity, Arthur, but you speak of what is convenient for you! You have had my labour for nine years. I have cooked your meals, I have sat up with your sick, I have copied your dull, lifeless sermons when my eyes ached! I did it because I was your sister, and because I had no other sphere. But I will not be a servant in my brother’s house to a woman who buys the right to rule it. I will not stay."

He looked genuinely shocked. He had expected me to bow, to weep, to accept. "And where will you go?" he asked, his voice rising with the instinctive, defensive anger of the selfish. "You have no money. You have no training. You are thirty years old."

"I know it," I cried, and the tears, hot and scalding, sprang to my eyes, though I furiously blinked them back. I would not weep before him. "I know my destitution. I know that society has no use for a plain woman of thirty without a fortune. You remind me of my prison-house, Arthur. But I tell you, I would rather go out into the storm and beg my bread from door to door on the moors, than sit at this table and eat the bitter bread of dependence."

He stood up, his face pale, his hand outstretched. "Eleanor, you are hysterical. This is unwomanly."
