The rain had not ceased for three days. It drummed upon the high, narrow windows of the school-room in Sint-Amandsberg, a relentless tattoo that seemed to beat time to the dry scratching of thirty pens upon paper. I stood before the class, my back to the gray Flemish sky, and heard in that monotonous sound the very rhythm of my life for five years past: the steady, unremitting fall of duty, day after day. I had come here an exile, my heart a raw and humbled thing; I had built a fortress of discipline and learning, and in its walls I had found a peace that was not happiness, but something more durable. The girls, with their round Flemish faces and their earnest, obedient eyes, were my garrison.

Madame de Winter, the Directress, summoned me to her study at noon. The air there was close, smelling of beeswax and dried lavender. She sat behind her great mahogany desk, a formidable woman whose severity was the school’s bedrock. In her hand she held a letter, the paper thick and creamy, its seal a crisp impression of a crest I did not know.

“Miss Vance,” she began, her voice as dry as the rustling of the letter itself. “I have received a communication that concerns you. It is from the headmistress of the Ladies’ Academy at Highgate, in London.” She paused, letting the word ‘London’ fall between us like a stone into a deep well. “You have been recommended to her in the highest terms. She offers you the post of First Assistant Mistress. The salary is… considerable. Double what you receive here.”

My own heart gave a strange, hard thump against my ribs. England. The very word was a spectre of a life I had left behind—the damp green lanes, the familiar cadence of the language spoken without effort, the society I had once known. It was an escape, a vindication. For a moment, I was seized by a fierce, ungovernable desire to snatch the letter, to cry out that I would go. To return was to prove I was not a failure, that my exile had been a choice, not a punishment.

“You have until Saturday to consider,” Madame de Winter continued, her gaze unwavering. “I will not stand in the way of your advancement. You have been an asset to this school, Miss Vance. But a woman of your talent is wasted here.”

I took the letter, my fingers trembling slightly. “Thank you, Madame.”

The news, as such things will, escaped. By the afternoon recess, a whisper had become a murmur, and the murmur a palpable sorrow in the schoolroom. When I entered for the final lesson of the day, the usual crisp “Good afternoon, Miss Vance” was a ragged, mournful chorus. Little Annette, whose English was the weakest and whose devotion the strongest, began to weep silently into her copybook, her tears making watery circles on the page.

“Miss Vance,” she stammered when I approached her desk. “Is it true? Are you leaving us for England?”

The question hung in the air. I looked at their faces—some defiant, some pleading, all of them wounded. These were not the daughters of aristocrats, but of merchants and minor officials, girls for whom a good education was a hard-won prize. I had given them that prize. To leave now felt like a betrayal of a sacred trust.

“It is an offer I must consider, Annette,” I said, my voice sounding unnaturally calm, a foreign thing in my own ears. “A great opportunity.”

“But we need you,” she whispered, and the other girls nodded, a silent, powerful appeal. “No one teaches us as you do.”

I turned away, lest they see the sudden, fierce stinging in my own eyes. I was their fortress, yes, but who was to be mine? In that moment, I felt the terrible solitude of my position more acutely than ever before. I was respected, I was necessary, but I was alone. And the offer from London was a promise of an end to that loneliness.

At the close of the day, as the girls filed out, their steps unusually quiet, I remained at my desk. The rain still fell. From the corner of my eye, I saw that I was not alone. Mr. Thorne, the master of mathematics and natural philosophy, stood by the window, ostensibly examining the deluged garden. He had been at the school longer than I, a quiet, reserved Englishman of whom I knew little beyond his reputation for brilliant, if severe, instruction. We had exchanged perhaps a hundred words in five years, all of them concerning timetables and examination papers. He was a man of granite, my equal in solitude, and I had always felt a slight, unspoken antagonism between us, a clash of two proud, unyielding natures.

He did not turn, but spoke to the glass. “The girls are in great distress.”

“It is a change,” I replied, gathering my books. “They will accustom themselves.”

“Will you?” The question was quiet, yet it struck me with the force of a physical blow. He turned then, and his gray eyes, usually so cool and distant, held a look of intense, probing scrutiny. There was no pity in it, but a kind of grim challenge.

“I have not yet decided,” I said, my own voice hardening. “It is a question of duty, Mr. Thorne. And of advancement.”

“Advancement,” he repeated, and the word sounded like an indictment. “You are respected here, Miss Vance. You have built something. In London, you will be one of many. A well-paid functionary.”

“And what is it I am here?” I retorted, the injustice of his judgment stinging me. “Am I not a functionary, however well-regarded? A governess to other people’s children, in a land not my own?”

“You are a pillar,” he said, his voice low and fierce. “You are the standard by which these girls measure themselves. Can you not see the good you do? Is that not a kingdom, however small?”
