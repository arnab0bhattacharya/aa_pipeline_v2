The city of Ostrava was not beautiful, in the way that old, decaying capitals are beautiful; it was efficient. It rose from the flat, sterile plains of the northern continent in a grid of white concrete and glass, punctuated by the rhythmic, humming pulse of the Great Sorter. I had traveled there from the old, messy, chaotic world of the south, where men still chose their trades by accident, ambition, or the sheer inertia of habit, to see this utopia of perfect placement. I was a journalist, a title that meant little here, but I carried my notebook and my skepticism like a man carries a rifle into a jungle.

I arrived at the station on a Tuesday, the air smelling faintly of ozone and polished steel. The citizens of Ostrava moved with a peculiar, synchronized grace. There was no shuffling, no bumping of elbows, no idle chatter. Each person walked with the purpose of a mechanism, their eyes fixed on the next step, the next task, the next beat in the great industrial symphony. I hired a guide, a young man named Kael, whose uniform was the standard grey of the administrative class, to show me the heart of the city: the Hall of Allocation.

"It is simple, sir," Kael said, his voice devoid of inflection. "We do not guess. We do not hope. We measure."

We walked through streets that were so clean they seemed to vibrate with a negative charge. There were no dumpsters, no stray papers, no stains. The sweepers moved in silent, rhythmic pairs, their brushes striking the pavement in unison, a soft *shhh-shhh-shhh* that sounded less like labor and more like the breathing of the city itself.

The Hall of Allocation was a massive cylinder of glass and steel, towering over the central square. Inside, the machinery was a dizzying array of sensors, cameras, and calculating engines. Here, at the age of eighteen, every citizen underwent the Test. Blood samples, nerve response metrics, linguistic pattern analysis, physical endurance trials. The machine did not care for your dreams, nor your family's wishes, nor your own desperate desires. It cared only for the data. It assigned you to the slot where your aptitude was highest, and where the city’s need was greatest. It was, they claimed, the end of wasted talent and the end of unemployment.

I was shown the records. I saw the smooth flow of human resources into the economy. A boy with a high spatial intelligence and steady hands became a surgeon. A girl with a low tolerance for noise and a high sensitivity to texture became a textile inspector. It was logical. It was elegant. It was, I thought, a prison dressed in white linen.

It was in the lower galleries of the Hall, where the rejected or the reassigned were briefly held before their induction, that I met Elara.

She was sitting on a bench, a small, unassuming woman in the blue tunic of the sanitation corps. She was not sweeping; she was sitting, her hands clasped in her lap, staring at the floor with an intensity that suggested she was seeing something I could not.

"You are the new arrival from the South," she said, without looking up. Her voice was quiet, but it cut through the hum of the machinery. "I am Elara. I am a Sweeper."

"I see," I said, sitting beside her. "And you are unhappy with your assignment?"

Elara smiled, a thin, sad expression that did not reach her eyes. "Unhappy is a word for those who have something to lose. I am not unhappy. I am... misplaced. The machine says I have a high sensitivity to pattern recognition and a low tolerance for repetitive physical motion. It says I am best suited for cleaning the intricate filigree of the central data banks. But I do not want to clean. I want to write."

"Write? Poetry?"

"Yes. I hear the words in the rhythm of the brushes. I see the shapes in the dust. I want to give them meaning. But the machine says that my linguistic pattern analysis is... adequate. Not exceptional. And my physical endurance for sustained standing is average. Therefore, I am a sweeper. It is efficient. It is what I am."

I looked at her hands. They were rough, stained with the grey dust of the city, but the fingers were long and delicate, the kind of hands that might have held a quill or a pen with great dexterity. "Do you write?"

"Every night," she whispered. "In my quarters, in the dark, when the monitors are off. I write verses about the wind that does not blow, about the stars that are hidden by the light of the towers. I write about the feeling of being a part of a whole, but not a part that fits. When I write, I am not a sweeper. I am not a unit. I am Elara."

I asked her if she had considered appealing to the machine. Re-taking the test.

She laughed, a sharp, brittle sound. "The machine does not make mistakes, sir. It makes decisions. If I appeal, they will re-test me. And the machine will find the same patterns. It will see the inefficiency of my desire. It will see that my poetry does not feed the city, does not build the towers, does not clean the streets. It will see that I am a waste of potential. And so, I will be reassigned to something even less suited to my soul. Perhaps a data entry clerk. Perhaps a waste reclaimer."

I thought of the streets outside, the perfect, spotless, silent streets. I thought of the sweepers moving in their synchronized rhythm, their faces blank, their movements precise. They were not slaves, for they were free to complain, to appeal, to resign. But they were not free to be themselves. They were free to be their most efficient selves.
