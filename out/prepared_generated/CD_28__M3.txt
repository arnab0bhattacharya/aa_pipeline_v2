If any pedestrian, hurrying along the rain-smeared pavements of Chatham High Street in the autumn of eighteen hundred and forty-something, were to cast a furtive glance through the fogged and streaked glass of a certain small grocery establishment, he might have beheld a curious, not to say affecting, tableau of fraternal industry. The shop in question belonged—or rather, had belonged, in the equitable but bewildering manner of deceased parents—to the brothers Mould: Mr. Nathaniel Mould, the elder, and Mr. Barnabas Mould, the younger. It was a shop of such profound and settled respectability that it seemed to draw its very breath from the smell of moist sugar, Dutch cheeses, and East India raisins. Nathaniel, a man whose countenance was as round, pale, and expressive as a highly polished Dutch oven, stood behind the counter, measuring out half a pound of best Patna rice with a care that bordered upon the devotional. He was a man who wore his clean apron as a bishop wears his vestments, and who possessed a calm, unshrinking faith in the sovereign virtue of a small but certain profit.

Barnabas, on the other hand, stood at the window, gazing through the misty glass at the muddy traffic of the street, with his arms folded and his thoughts—if one might judge from the vagueness of his eye—wandering in a region of very tall castles built upon very slender foundations. Barnabas was the dreamer, the borrower, the restless genius of the family. He had a handsome face, a persuasive tongue, and a pocket that was forever yawning open to receive the loans of acquaintances who were destined, by a strange fatality, never to be repaid.

It is not to be supposed, however, that Barnabas was without resources. He had recently achieved a triumph of which he spoke so incessantly that the very canisters of tea upon the shelves might have been heard to echo it. He was engaged to be married to Miss Clementina Toppington, the only daughter of a retired hop-merchant of considerable means, dwelling in a large stuccoed house at the genteel end of the Rochester road.

‘A most enchanting creature, Nathaniel,’ Barnabas was in the habit of observing, as he abstracted a fig from the nearest box. ‘A creature fashioned by the Graces for a higher sphere than this. Her father, Toppington, is a man of weight, Nathaniel—of immense weight. He looks upon me with a paternal eye. “Barnabas,” he said to me only yesterday, clapping me upon the shoulder with a hand like a padded sledgehammer, “you are the son of my old age. I ask but one thing: that you appear before the world in a manner befitting the husband of my Clementina.”’

To which Nathaniel, weighing his rice with a slight tremor of the scales, would mildly reply, ‘A wedding is a very solemn thing, Barnabas. And a grocer’s shop, though humble, is a very certain thing.’

‘Certain!’ cried Barnabas, with a theatrical wave of the hand that nearly swept a row of candles into the sawdust. ‘You speak of certainty, Nathaniel, as if it were the summit of human ambition! Is certainty to deck the altar of Hymen? Is certainty to provide the silver forks, the ice-pails, the champagne, the mourning coaches for the Toppington connection? No, my dear brother. I have a conception of the nuptials—a grand, comprehensive, and splendid conception—that requires a more elastic medium than the weekly takings of rice and soap.’

The grand conception, as Barnabas presently unfolded it, was nothing less than the absolute sale of the grocery shop, the stock-in-trade, the goodwill, and the shining brass scales themselves, to provide the necessary capital for a wedding that was to astonish the whole Medway valley.

Nathaniel, upon hearing this proposition, became so deeply agitated that he dropped the scoop into the rice-bag, and stood staring at his brother with his mouth slightly open, looking, in his helpless stupefaction, very much like a fish that has unexpectedly found itself in a tea-cup.

‘Sell the shop!’ gasped Nathaniel. ‘The shop left to us by our father! The shop in which you and I, Barnabas, were rocked in their respective sugar-crates when we were infants! Sell the goodwill—the good, solid, unromantic, paying goodwill!’

‘I do not say sell it for a caprice,’ returned Barnabas, with a dignified air. ‘I say, sell it for an investment. An investment in the future. An investment in the Toppington connection! Think of it, Nathaniel. When I am married to Clementina, I shall be introduced to men of mark. Men of commerce. Men who deal, not in pounds of tea, but in tons of—of something vastly more extensive! I shall be in a position to place you, my dear brother, in some tremendous undertaking. A brewery. A railway. A bank!’

Nathaniel’s face, which had been pale, now flushed with a feeble glow of temptation. ‘A bank?’ he repeated, in a hushed voice, as if the very word were too sacred to be spoken aloud above the smell of pitch and treacle.

‘A bank!’ affirmed Barnabas. ‘Or, at the very least, a wholesale establishment. But the wedding must come first. Toppington insists upon it. The settlements require it. The bride demands it. “Barnabas,” said Clementina to me, her eyes swimming in a gossamer of tears, “I cannot be yours if the world is to point its finger at a grocer.” The child is right, Nathaniel. She is sensitive, refined. She is made of porcelain, and we are made of common clay.’

Nathaniel sighed. He had no answer for porcelain versus common clay. He looked at the canisters, at the scales, at the amber-colored liquids in the window-bottles, and saw in them no longer the humble means of a respectable livelihood, but the grim, unpoetic barriers to a railway directorship and the smiles of Miss Clementina Toppington.
