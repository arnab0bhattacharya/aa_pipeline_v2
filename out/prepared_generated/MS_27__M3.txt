The tempestuous surges of the Atlantic, lashed into a fury by the equinoctial gales, beat upon the shingles of Selsea Haven with a savage and unyielding despair. It was late in the twenty-first century, an epoch when the old world lay stricken and ruined, its ancient continental heart consumed by a pestilence both of body and of body politic; and the dark, heaving waters seemed to mirror the chaos of a collapsing humanity. Through the murky twilight and the scudding rain, a vessel—a battered, solitary bark, freighted with the miseries of a lost population—was driven toward our shores. She bore the remnants of a fleeing multitude, refugees from a continent whose cities were now but charnel houses, whose fertile plains were become vast, unburied graves. They came to England as to a sanctuary, their eyes strained through the gloom to catch a glimpse of that white cliff which, in happier days, had been the beacon of the oppressed; but the England that now looked upon them was shrunken in her sympathies, and trembled only for her own immediate safety.

The alarm spread through the town of Selsea Haven with the rapidity of a contagion. The fishermen and townsfolk gathered upon the quays, their faces hardened by the bitter blast, their hearts steeled by a terror that wore the mask of righteous prudence. They beheld the fevered and dying emigrants borne to the vessel's side, and they saw in those wasted, supplicating figures not fellow-creatures, but the ministers of their own destruction. "Send them from us!" cried the multitude, their voices rising above the howling wind, in a paroxysm of selfish fear. "We will not have our hearths defiled, nor our children's lives jeopardised by the maladies of a ruined world!" And thus, with imprecations and menaces, they forbade the stricken ship to cast her anchor in the harbour, or to send a single boat to the land. The wretched exiles, who had escaped the sword and the pestilence of their native shores, were now to be murdered by the cold charity of their fellow-men.

In the midst of this tumultuous and hard-hearted assembly stood Alistair Vaughan, a young physician, whose ardent soul and profound sensibility rendered him a stranger among the calculating and the cold. He had mingled in the scenes of devastation abroad, and his heart bled at the spectacle of woe which the vessel presented. He gazed, with an anguish that words were powerless to convey, upon the crowd of fugitives, whose hollow eyes appealed to the humanity of those who denied them a resting-place for death. He felt that compassion was not merely a virtue, but a duty, the first and most imperative of our mortal obligations; and he shrank with horror from the selfishness that could bar the door against the perishing. Turning to his fellow-townsmen, he addressed them with an impassioned eloquence, which, for a moment, suspended their fierce resolves.

"You call yourselves men," he cried, "and yet you would shut your ears to the groans of the dying! You tremble for your own lives, and in your selfish terror, you would consign hundreds of your fellow-creatures to the mercy of the tempest. Are we not all bound by the same chain of destiny, and is not he who refuses aid to the distressed a murderer in the sight of Heaven? If infection there must be, let us meet it with the remedies of science and the courage of a righteous heart; but let us not stain our souls with the guilt of a cruelty more fatal than any pestilence. I adjure you, by the sacred ties of our common nature, to open your arms to these houseless wanderers, and to prove that, even in this late and fallen age, the heart of England is not utterly dead to the claims of suffering humanity!"

His words were listened to with a sullen silence, but they awoke no responding echo in the breasts of his hearers. The fear of infection had extinguished every generous sentiment; and the multitude, unmoved by his appeal, insisted that the council should be immediately assembled to decree the banishment of the vessel. The mayor and the principal magistrates, themselves infected by the general panic, convened in the town-hall, a gloomy and dilapidated edifice that overlooked the raging sea. Through the long hours of the night, they sat in deliberation, while the storm raved without, and the murmurs of the populace, gathered in the streets, penetrated the walls of their chamber. The candles flickered in the agitated air, casting a lurid and uncertain gleam upon the countenances of the councillors, whose visages were stamped with the workings of fear, rather than the gravity of wisdom.

Vaughan was summoned to their presence, and again he urged his plea with all the fervour of his soul. He spoke of the duties of hospitality, of the sacredness of human life, and of the degradation which a denial of those duties would bring upon their common nature. He painted the horrors of the vessel, where the dying and the dead were huddled together, and where the living were famished with hunger and consumed with fever. He described the despair of the mothers, who, with their dying infants in their arms, implored but a handful of earth wherein to lay their bones; and he asked if England, the ancient refuge of the oppressed, would now become their tomb. His voice trembled with emotion, and his eyes were suffused with tears, as he thus pleaded the cause of the friendless and the forsaken.

But his appeals were vain. The councillors, with a few trembling and hesitating exceptions, were resolved upon their course. They argued of the public weal, of the necessity of sacrificing the few to save the many, and of the paramount duty of preserving the health of the community. They spoke of laws, of quarantine, and of the imperative demands of self-preservation; and they turned a deaf ear to the voice of humanity, which pleaded in vain against the dictates of a selfish policy. The decree was issued that the vessel should be compelled, by force if necessary, to put to sea, and that any attempt of the refugees to land should be resisted by the armed force of the town.
