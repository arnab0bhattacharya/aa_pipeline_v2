It was in the second week of a hard January, when the fens lay under a sky the colour of slate and the dykes were frozen to a dull pewter, that Mrs. Hollis, the rector’s wife, first took notice that no smoke came from the chimney of the cottage at Gallows End. The cottage stood a little apart from the village street, at the tail of a furlong of sodden grass, and it had about it that air of sullen independence which its mistress, Dame Crick, had worn like a second shawl for the twenty years since her husband’s death. She was a woman who had buried a man and three children and had never once asked the parish for a penny, though the penny had been offered more than once, and by none more officiously than by Mrs. Hollis herself, whose sense of Christian duty was apt to outrun her sense of delicacy.

The quarrel between them—for it had been a quarrel, though it never came to loud words—had its root in that very matter. Some three winters before, when the fever had gone through the village, Mrs. Hollis had come to the cottage door with a basket and a text, and Dame Crick, standing in the doorway with her arms folded, had said, in a voice as dry as the rushes on her floor, that she thanked the lady kindly but she had never yet eaten the bread of charity and did not mean to begin at her time of life. Mrs. Hollis had coloured, for she was a woman in whom benevolence was mixed with a considerable quantity of pride, and she had answered that charity was not a thing to be ashamed of, and that the poor should remember who had said that the poor should be always with us. To which Dame Crick had replied that she had no doubt the rector’s lady knew her Bible, but that she herself had found it better to be beholden to nobody but God Almighty, who had never yet failed her. And Mrs. Hollis had gone away with her basket, and had said to the rector that evening that she washed her hands of that woman, who was eaten up with a pride that was a sin against providence. Since then the two had met in the lane and had nodded, and the nod had been of that species which is more cutting than a cut direct.

Now, when the frost had held for a fortnight and the birds were found stiff in the hedgerows, Mrs. Hollis, going her rounds with a basket of broth and flannel, observed the cold chimney at Gallows End and felt a stir of something that was not quite Christian satisfaction. She said to herself that the woman would be ill, and that pride would have to swallow itself at last. She went home and sat by her own fire and tried to read a sermon, and found that the words would not hold together. The wind came off the fens and shook the casements, and she thought of the cottage at Gallows End, where the thatch was old and the door ill-fitting, and where an old woman lay perhaps alone with no one to bring her a cup of water. At last she rose, and put on her cloak and her pattens, and took a small bottle of cordial and a blanket from the chest, and went out into the dark, feeling that she was doing a thing that would be thrown back in her teeth, and yet unable to stay.

The door was on the latch, and she pushed it open and went in. The fire was nearly out, and the room was cold with that particular cold of a cottage where the hearth has been let die. Dame Crick lay in the bed in the corner, her face turned to the wall, and she did not move when Mrs. Hollis came in. Mrs. Hollis stood a moment, and then she said, in a voice that she tried to make firm, “Dame Crick, it is I, Mrs. Hollis. I saw no smoke from your chimney, and I have brought you a blanket and a little cordial.”

There was a silence, and then the old woman said, without turning, “I want nothing, ma’am. I thank you.”

“You are ill,” said Mrs. Hollis, coming nearer. “You cannot deceive me. You have not been out these three days, and your fire is out.”

“I have been worse,” said Dame Crick. “It will pass. It has passed before.”

“It will not pass if you lie here in the cold,” said Mrs. Hollis, with some of her old impatience. “I shall make up your fire, and you will drink this cordial, and then we shall see.”

She set about it without waiting for leave, and the old woman lay still and watched her with a kind of wonder, as if she were seeing a strange thing. Mrs. Hollis knelt and blew the embers and put on sticks and then coal, and the flame leapt up and cast a wavering light over the poor room, over the deal table and the two chairs and the old clock that had stopped, and over the face of Dame Crick, which was very thin and very grey, and had in it a look that Mrs. Hollis had not seen there before—the look of a woman who has been strong and is now afraid of her own weakness.

“You should have sent for me,” said Mrs. Hollis, when the fire was burning.

“I have never sent for anybody,” said Dame Crick. “I have managed my own affairs since my man died, and I have not been beholden to the parish, and I did not mean to be beholden to you, ma’am, who have been kind enough to think me a heathen.”
