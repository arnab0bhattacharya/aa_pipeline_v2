It was upon a night of such melancholy and tempestuous splendour as might have awed the most stoic soul into a sense of its own insignificance, that the last of the elders, a man whose name, Adelard, had for years been synonymous with wisdom and caution, yielded up his spirit to the great, unknowable Power that governs all things. He was gathered to his fathers, or rather, to that silent earth which had become the sole repository of the past, and we, the remnant of humanity in the Severn valley, were left as children without a guide upon a vast and treacherous shore. The sickness, that spectral visitor which had emptied the great cities and silenced the clamour of a world now forgotten, had at last retreated, but it had left behind it a desolation more profound than any plague, for it had robbed us of our memory of what we once were.

In the hollowed shell of the old abbey at Wychford, whose stone arches framed the raging sky like the ribs of some colossal beast, we gathered. The wind shrieked through the vacant windows, a lament for all that was lost, and the rain fell in torrents, as if heaven itself were weeping for our orphaned state. It was I, Elara, whom they turned to. I, who had known only the quiet tending of the herb-gardens and the careful preservation of the few books that had survived the damp and the decay. I had never sought dominion over my fellows; the very notion was as alien to my nature as the sun is to the depths of the sea. Yet, in their desperate faces, illuminated by the fitful glare of the central fire, I read a plea that was more compelling than any command. They looked to me, perhaps because I was young enough to still hold a flicker of hope, yet old enough to have felt the full weight of our collective sorrow.

“You must lead us, Elara,” a woman named Brenna said, her voice hoarse with a grief that was not only for Adelard, but for us all. “Your mind is clear. Your heart is true. Choose for us, and we shall follow.”

Oh, what a cruel burden was laid upon my soul in that moment! To be the arbiter of the fate of these last forty souls, to have the lives of men, women, and children—those dear, trusting faces of the little ones who knew no world but this valley—rest upon my own uncertain judgment. I felt my spirit quail beneath the terrible responsibility. My mind, which had been wont to soar in the tranquil realms of thought and contemplation, was now dragged down and chained to the earth, to the mud and blood of our survival. The choice they presented to me was a chasm, and on either side lay ruin or salvation, and I could not see which was which.

The first path led north, to the coast. The old maps, their paper brittle as autumn leaves, spoke of a port town named Saltmere. The elders had often debated it, their voices low and earnest by the firelight. They argued that the sea would provide a bounty of fish, that the air of the coast might be more pure, that perhaps, in the great, empty world beyond our valley, other bands of survivors might have gathered, and we might not be the last flickering candle of mankind. To go north was to embrace the unknown, to venture forth with our children and our frail into a wilderness of ruin, driven by a hope that was little more than a whisper carried on the wind. It was an act of faith, of audacious daring, that seemed to me both magnificent and mad.

The other choice was to remain. To stay here, in the Severn valley, which we knew as intimately as a lover knows the face of the beloved. Here were the fields we had coaxed back to life, the river that gave us water and fish, the sturdy walls of the abbey that sheltered us from the storms. Here lay the graves of our dead, a silent congregation that bound us to the very soil. To stay was to accept the limits of our existence, to cultivate our small Eden and pray that the sickness did not return, that the winters did not grow too harsh, that our numbers might slowly, painstakingly increase. It was an act of prudence, of preservation, but was it not also an act of cowardice? Was it not to condemn our children to a life of quiet desperation, hemmed in by the ghosts of a greater world?

All that night, while my companions slept the troubled sleep of the grieving, I walked beneath the storm-lashed heavens. The wind tore at my hair and garments, and the rain chilled me to the bone, yet I felt nothing but the fire of my own inner torment. I looked upon the valley, a dark and familiar shape under the fleeting glimmers of moonlight that pierced the clouds. It was a place of safety, but was it a cage? Then I turned my gaze northward, towards the black and featureless horizon, where the land met the sky in a line of utter uncertainty. That way lay the future, a vast, unreadable page. The sublime terror of that emptiness filled me with a dreadful awe. I felt myself to be a mote of dust, a fleeting breath, caught between the silent, sorrowful past that lay buried in the valley and the roaring, unknown future that awaited beyond the hills.

A great conflict raged within my breast. The instinct for preservation, the deep love I bore for this familiar earth and these my people, cried out to me to stay, to build our walls higher and hold fast to what we had. But another voice, a wilder, more desperate longing, urged me to dare, to seek, to believe that humanity was meant for more than a slow fading in a forgotten corner of the world.
