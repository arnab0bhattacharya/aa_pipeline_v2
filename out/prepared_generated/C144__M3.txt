The rain had followed them from Portsmouth, a persistent, sullen drizzle that seemed determined to rob the city of its promised healing waters. Lieutenant Thomas Vane stood beneath the portico of the York House hotel, watching the carriages splash through the puddled cobblestones of Pulteney Street. He had been at sea for eighteen months without respite, enduring Atlantic gales and the tedious blockade of the French coast, only to find himself anchored in the most landlocked, artificial place in all of England.

"I am perfectly certain it has stopped, Thomas, or very nearly." Clara appeared at his elbow, adjusting the clasp of her spencer. At twenty-two, she possessed the same dark eyes as their late mother, though hers held a brightness that Thomas associated more with stubbornness than with sensibility. She had been pale when he collected her from their aunt's house in Dorset, worn down by a winter of being treated as a glorified nursemaid, and he had hoped the change of scene would restore her spirits. It had, perhaps, too well.

"You will catch your death," he said, though he offered his arm.

"I will catch my cure, according to Dr. Merewether. Three weeks of the waters and I shall be entirely myself again."

They descended to the street and picked their way toward the Pump Room, Clara leaning on his arm with a familiarity that reminded him how little of her life he had been present for. He had joined the Navy at fourteen. She had been a child of six. In the intervening years she had grown into a woman he understood only in fragments, through letters that arrived months late and spoke of parish concerts and new bonnets as though these were matters of genuine consequence.

The Pump Room was already crowded when they arrived, the morning's invalids and socialites circulating in their slow orbits around the fountain. Clara's face took on the expression of determined hope that Thomas had learned to dread, the look of a woman who expected society to provide what nature had denied her. She was not wealthy, not accomplished in any way that drew notice, and not possessed of those connections that might smooth the path of a young woman without a substantial dowry. Their father had left them barely enough to keep Clara respectably situated with their aunt, and Thomas's prize money, while not inconsiderable, was tied up in shares and expectations.

He left her at the fountain with a glass of the dreadful water and retreated to a bench near the door, where he could observe without participating. He was nursing a cup of coffee and a general resentment toward the entire enterprise of leisure when he saw the man approach.

Captain Edmund Morley, retired. Thomas recognised the walk before the face, the particular stride of a man who had spent his life on quarterdecks and carried the habit into civilian life as though the pavement might rise beneath him at any moment. Morley had been commander of the Hyperion during the action off Cape Ortegal three years prior, the same engagement in which Thomas's captain had been killed and Thomas himself had assumed command of a shattered frigate with a third of his crew dead or dying. Morley had received the surrender of the French vessel and, with it, the credit for the engagement. Thomas had received a mention in dispatches that misspelled his name and a promotion to lieutenant that had come two years later than it should have.

Morley was perhaps forty, tall, with the weathered complexion of a man who had served in tropical stations. He wore civilian clothes with the air of a man who considered them a costume. Thomas watched him cross the room, watched him pause at the fountain, watched him address Clara with an easy smile that seemed to illuminate the entire corner of the room.

Clara laughed. Thomas set down his coffee.

He did not move immediately. He told himself he was observing, gathering intelligence, assessing the threat. Morley had left the service after the Cape Ortegal affair, citing wounds that Thomas suspected were more political than physical. He had settled into Bath society with the ease of a man who had been preparing for it his entire life, and his reputation, as far as Thomas could determine from discrete inquiries, was that of a gentleman of unimpeachable address and moderate fortune.

None of this changed what he had done, or what he was.

"He seems very agreeable," Clara said on their walk back to the hotel. She had not stopped smiling since they left the Pump Room, and Thomas felt the familiar tightening in his chest that preceded a difficult conversation.

"He is a fraud."

Clara's step faltered. "I beg your pardon?"

"Morley. The man you were speaking with. He took credit for an action he did not earn. Captain Aldiss died in that engagement, Clara. I was the one who brought the Calypso home. Morley was nowhere near the action until it was over, and then he arrived in time to accept the French captain's sword and claim the prize."

Clara was quiet for a moment. The rain had begun again in earnest, and they hurried beneath an awning. "You have told me this before," she said. "In your letter, after the action."

"Then you understand why I cannot countenance the acquaintance."

"I understand that you were wronged. That is not the same thing." She looked at him directly, and he saw in her eyes the same steel that had been in their mother's when their father had proposed moving the family to Jamaica. "He was perfectly civil. He asked after our journey, and whether we intended to take the waters regularly. He mentioned that his sister keeps house for him in a property on Brock Street."

"Of course he was civil. He is always civil. Civility is the only weapon a man like that needs."
