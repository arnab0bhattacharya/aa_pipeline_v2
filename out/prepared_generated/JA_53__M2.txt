The morning light in the breakfast room of Harrowden Hall was pale and diffuse, casting a melancholy dignity over the silver and china that reflected the faces of the three women present. Eliza Wentworth sat at the head of the table, her posture rigid as if she were propped upon a cushion of invisible needles, her eyes fixed not upon the porridge before her, but upon the lady who had recently entered her family’s orbit. It was Mrs. Arthur Wentworth, née Clara Vane, a woman of twenty-four, possessing a complexion of such unblemished fairness that it seemed to demand the attention of every eye in the room, and a smile so precisely modulated that it might have been calculated by an actuary.

Eliza had long entertained the notion that her brother Arthur’s marriage was an act of such profound imprudence that it required a daily, vigilant examination to ensure the catastrophe had not yet fully unfolded. Arthur, a man of modest means and even more modest imagination, had wed a girl who had no known estate, no substantial dowry, and whose father, a retired colonel, had died owing debts to his creditors. To Eliza, this was not merely a lack of financial prudence; it was a red flag of such vivid hue that it threatened to stain the entire fabric of their household. She was convinced, with the certainty of a prophet who has studied the weather enough to predict rain, that Clara Vane was a creature of ambition, a fortune hunter who had ensnared Arthur not with love, but with the insidious allure of his name and his modest but respectable estate.

And yet, as Clara sat down and poured herself a cup of tea with hands that trembled not at all, Eliza found herself in a state of peculiar frustration. For if Clara had been rude, or if she had hinted at the desire for new furniture, or if she had looked with obvious longing at the gold-framed portraits of the Wentworth ancestors, Eliza’s suspicions would have been confirmed with the satisfaction of a case solved. But Clara Vane was perfect. She was so aggressively, so relentlessly perfect that her propriety became an offense in itself. She did not eat too much; she did not laugh too loudly; she did not ask for money. She spoke only when spoken to, and when she did speak, it was in tones of such gentle deference that Eliza felt, for a moment, a pang of unwarranted guilt, which she swiftly suppressed as a weakness of character.

“Your tea, Mrs. Wentworth?” asked the old housekeeper, Mrs. Gable, a woman whose loyalty to the Wentworth name was matched only by her suspicion of all newcomers.

Clara turned to her, her eyes wide and innocent. “Oh, thank you, Mrs. Gable. It is most kind of you. I had not noticed the temperature, but I am sure it is perfect.”

Eliza watched the exchange with narrowed eyes. *Perfect,* she thought. *Always perfect. It is the mask of the viper, soft and cold to the touch.* She took a sip of her own tea, which was lukewarm, and decided that the day would require more effort than usual to maintain the appearance of domestic harmony.

After breakfast, the household dispersed to its respective duties, but Eliza did not join her mother in the drawing room. Instead, she retreated to the library, a room she had chosen for its silence and its distance from the main corridors, from which she could observe the comings and goings of the household. She opened a book of poetry, but the words swam before her eyes as she listened for the sound of footsteps.

It was an hour before she heard them. Clara was walking in the garden, Eliza deduced, for the soft crunch of gravel was audible through the open window. Eliza set down her book and rose to the window, peering out through the lace curtain. There was Clara, strolling alone among the rose bushes, her blue dress a splash of color against the grey sky. She was not looking at the flowers, Eliza noted; she was looking at the house. Specifically, she was looking at the window of the study, where Arthur kept his accounts.

*There it is,* Eliza thought, a small, cold triumph settling in her chest. *She is spying on his finances. She is calculating her future income. How bold she is, to think she can fool me with her demure air.*

But then, as Eliza watched, Clara stopped walking. She stood still for a moment, her hand resting on a rose bush, and then she turned away from the house, walking instead toward the small greenhouse at the end of the garden. Eliza frowned. This was not what she had expected. If Clara were a fortune hunter, she would have lingered, perhaps even approached the window, or sent a maid to ask Arthur about the state of the accounts. Instead, she was tending to the plants.

Eliza felt a sudden, irritating doubt. Perhaps she had misjudged her sister-in-law. Perhaps Clara was simply a woman of simple tastes, content with flowers and quietude. But then, as Clara entered the greenhouse, she saw her speak to the gardener, Mr. Pym. She said something, her head tilting slightly, and Mr. Pym nodded, looking somewhat abashed. Eliza strained to hear the words, but the distance was too great. She could only see Clara’s hand moving, pointing to something on the ledger she held in her other hand.

*Ah,* Eliza thought, the suspicion returning with renewed vigor. *She is asking for more. She is asking for the keys to the safe, or the deed to the farm. She is charming the servants to get what she wants. It is a subtle game, but I shall see it through.*
