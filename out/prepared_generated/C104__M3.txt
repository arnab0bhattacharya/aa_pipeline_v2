Miss Euphemia Clawthorne came to my office in Tooley Street on a Tuesday in November, bringing with her the damp chill of the river and a countenance marked by sleepless dread. She was a woman of perhaps eight-and-twenty, plainly dressed, her hands reddened from lye and cold. Her father, the Reverend Josiah Clawthorne, was the incumbent of St. Bede's, a crumbling parish of grey stone and damp mortar tucked into the lanes behind Bermondsey Wall, where the Thames at high tide sent its breath of sewage and coal tar through every cracked window. For eleven years he had preached there with a fury that shook the rafters, a man of iron constitution and unshakable conviction. Now he sat in his study like a figure carved from tallow, and would not speak of what he had seen.

She laid the matter before me with the practised economy of someone who had rehearsed the telling many times. Three weeks prior, on the last night of October, her father had gone to visit a dying parishioner in Nag's Head Court, a narrow rookery of lodging-houses and chandler's shops that bent toward the river along a lane called Saviour's Stairs. He had gone at the request of a note delivered to the vicarage door, written in a hand he did not recognise, begging him to come and administer the last rites to a sick man named Trundle. He had returned two hours later than he should have, and when Euphemia opened the door to him, he walked past her as though she were a stranger. His face was the colour of unwhitewashed plaster, his lips grey, his clerical collar dark with sweat. He went directly to his study and locked the door. He had not ascended the pulpit since. The curate, a timid fellow named Mr. Pellow, had been conducting the services, and the parishioners were beginning to talk.

Each night, she told me, the same ritual unfolded. At half past ten her father would light the lamp at his desk, take out a sheet of the heavy cream paper he used for parish correspondence, and write. She had once crept to the door and listened, and heard only the scratching of the nib, steady and unbroken, for upwards of an hour. Then would come the scrape of a match, and the particular silence that attends the consumption of paper by fire. By morning the grate would contain only a fine grey ash, indistinguishable from any other, and her father would be sitting in his chair by the window, staring at the wall of the churchyard with eyes that seemed to see nothing at all.

I asked her whether she had read any of the letters before they were burned. She hesitated, and a flush rose in her cheeks. Once, she admitted, she had intercepted the letter before he could set it alight, by walking into the study on the pretence of bringing him tea. She had seen only a few lines before he snatched it from her hand. The words she recalled were these: "I beg you to understand that I did not go down those stairs of my own choosing, and that what I found at the bottom was not what I had been told I would find. It was a face, and the face knew me." She had not been able to glean more. He had taken the letter, burned it then and there in the hearth, and afterward locked his door each night.

I agreed to make enquiries, though I told her plainly that I held little hope of discovering what had passed in a court of lodging-houses by the river, where people kept their own counsel and distrusted the questions of strangers. She pressed a sovereign into my hand, more money than I expected her to possess, and left without further ceremony.

The following evening I crossed Tooley Street and made my way into the lanes behind Bermondsey Wall, where the buildings lean together overhead and the cobbles run with water that has no certain source. Saviour's Stairs was a narrow passage, barely wide enough for two men to walk abreast, descending in broken steps toward the foreshore. The houses on either side were ancient, their timbers swollen and blackened, their upper stories jutting out over the lane so that the sky was reduced to a thin grey ribbon. At the bottom of the stairs, where the passage opened onto a small court, I found a public house called the Broken Anchor, its sign hanging from a single rusted chain. Inside, the air was thick with tobacco and the smell of boiled beef. A few men sat drinking in silence. The woman behind the bar, a heavy-featured person with arms like a stevedore, watched me without expression.

I ordered a glass of gin and sat near the fire. After a time I made enquiry about a man named Trundle, and was met with the particular silence that I had been advised to expect in such places. The woman behind the bar wiped the counter with a rag that had seen longer service than the British Empire. She said there was no Trundle in Nag's Head Court, nor had been in the fifteen years she had kept the house. I pressed her, and she repeated herself with a flatness that discouraged further questioning, but added, as though it were an afterthought, that the Reverend Clawthorne had been seen on Saviour's Stairs that night, coming out of number four, which was the last house on the east side of the court, the one whose back wall formed part of the old river wall. She said this as though it were a detail of no consequence, and turned to serve another customer.
