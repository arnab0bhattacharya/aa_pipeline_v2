If there be any spot on the feverish map of London where the very mud appears to labour under a heavy debt to society, it is that dreary stretch of foreshore adjoining the river at Grimes’s Vat, hard by the rotten piers of Battersea. Here, the waters of the Thames, thick with the refuse of a thousand up-stream kitchens, crawl sluggishly past a low, soot-weeping building from which arises, day and night, a stupendous belching of white steam and a sickly, suffocating savour of tallow. It was into this fragrant Purgatory that young Master Tommy Trunnion was thrust, a slender and pale-faced child of twelve, to stir the boiling vats with a wooden paddle taller than himself.

Tommy’s true father was not a mariner, but a bankrupt clerk from a counting-house in Cornhill; a mild, watery-eyed gentleman of letters, who had lent his signature to a friend and, in the doing of it, had drowned himself in a sea of promissory notes, leaving Tommy to the tender mercies of his uncle, the proprietor of the soap boilery. But the gloom of a counting-house and the disgrace of a debtors’ prison make but a poor figure in the conversation of a boy surrounded by rough and brawny men; and so, finding himself among the giants of the vat-house, Tommy Trunnion did, in his childish wisdom, resolve to be the orphan of a hero.

He did not boast. He merely suffered a fatal fact to escape him, during the half-hour allowed for the chewing of bread and fat bacon, that his late father (may the heavens protect him!) had been a Commander in the Merchant Marine, lost in a terrible typhoon off the coast of Sumatra. The men of the boilery, who were accustomed to deal only in solid weights and measures, and whose notions of geography were chiefly confined to the public-house signs of the Borough, received this intelligence with a respectful grunt. But Tommy, finding the seed cast upon such stony ground to sprout wonderfully, watered it with a daily dew of invention.

He spoke of the gallant frigate, the *Saucy Salamander*—a name which, to the men of the vats, carried a pleasing flavour of the amphibious and the fiery. He described a cabin lined with cedar and mahogany, where his father, a man of a majestic, weather-beaten countenance and a pigtail, would sit reading aloud from the Book of Psalms, while the parrot, a bird of exceedingly high principle, squawked the responses. He told of a mother, a lady of surpassing gentleness, who had died of a broken heart upon receiving the news of the shipwreck, communicated to her in a bottle sealed with black wax. He even detailed the exact nature of the cargo: three hundred bales of the finest Chinese silk, a cage of monkeys for a nobleman’s menagerie, and a mysterious golden idol, stolen from a temple, which was said to bring a curse upon any ship that carried it.

So vividly did Master Trunnion paint this scene, and so feelingly did he narrate the tempest, that he would frequently pause, his bread and bacon suspended in mid-air, to dash an imaginary tear from his eye; and the rough men about him, their own faces shining with the unguents of their trade, would mutter, “Poor lad!” and “A brave skipper, and no mistake!”

And here lies the strangest part of this small history: that a fiction, told often enough, and with sufficient passion, will sometimes take root in the very soul of its creator. Tommy Trunnion, stirring his viscous, bubbling vat, came, in the course of some months, to believe in the *Saucy Salamander* as firmly as he believed in the soap. The golden idol, the squawking parrot, the majestic pigtail, and the cedar cabin became to him as real as the steam that scalded his face. He forgot the pale clerk of Cornhill; he forgot the ink-stained ledger and the bailiffs; he saw only the broad, blue Pacific, and his father, waving a tarpaulin hat from the quarter-deck of a doomed ship.

It was upon a Saturday evening, in the month of December, when the fog lay upon the river like a wet, grey blanket, that the reckoning came. Tommy was seated in the low, smoky tap-room of the Jolly Tarbucket, a tavern much frequented by the men of the boilery. He was enjoying a half-pint of mild ale, purchased for him by his admiring workmates, and was just reaching the climax of a new invention—how his father had fought a duel with a Spanish don on the beach at Valparaiso—when a stranger entered the room.

He was a very ancient mariner, with a face like a shrivelled apple, and a nose that glowed with the perpetual fire of strong drink. He wore a pea-jacket so patched and tarred that it resembled a geological map of the British Isles, and he carried a stout oak stick, which he planted upon the brick floor with a heavy thump. He ordered a gill of rum, warm, and sat himself down in the chimney-corner, listening with a half-drunken gravity to Tommy’s recital.

The boy, stimulated by the ale and the presence of a new auditor, waxed exceedingly eloquent. He spoke of the coral reefs, and the flying-fish, and the manner in which a true British tar may be known by his rolling gait. The ancient sailor nodded, slowly, his eyes fixed upon the fire. When Tommy had finished, and the men had murmured their approbation, the stranger turned his head.

‘A pretty story, my lad,’ he said, in a voice that creaked like a block in a rusty sheave. ‘A very pretty story. And a brave man was your father, I make no doubt.’

‘He was, sir,’ said Tommy, with a proud, though melancholy, air. ‘There was not a bolder commander in all the China trade.’

‘And what might be the name of his ship?’ inquired the ancient mariner.

‘The *Saucy Salamander*, sir,’ said Tommy, without a moment’s hesitation.
