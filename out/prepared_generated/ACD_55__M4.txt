The letter which I received from the Reverend Septimus Harding of Stoke Prior, in Worcestershire, reached me upon a Tuesday morning in the October of ’93, and its contents were of sufficient interest to draw me down into the country upon the following day. The reverend gentleman wrote in a hand that betrayed considerable agitation, and his account was this: that the silver chalice of his church, a piece of no great value in itself but of some antiquity and much reverenced by the parish, had been stolen from the vestry upon the night of Sunday last; that suspicion had fallen with the usual promptitude upon a family of gypsies encamped upon the common beyond the village; and that he, being unconvinced of their guilt, desired a private inquiry before the matter went further.

“I am aware,” he wrote, “that the constable has already made up his mind, and that the village is of one accord with him. But I have seen these people, and I cannot think them guilty. There are circumstances which I shall explain when we meet.”

I arrived at the vicarage in the early afternoon, and was shown into a study whose shelves were crowded with volumes of sermons and antiquarian lore. Mr. Harding was a spare, grey-haired man of perhaps sixty years, with the gentle, abstracted manner of a scholar, and he received me with a warmth that spoke of genuine relief.

“You are very good to come, sir,” said he. “I have read of your methods, and I confess that I am at my wits’ end. The matter stands thus. On Sunday evening, after the evening service, I locked the vestry myself, and the chalice was then in its place within the oak cupboard. The key I retain upon my person. Upon Monday morning, when the curate went to prepare for the early celebration, the cupboard was open and the chalice gone. There was no sign of violence upon the door or the lock.”

“And the gypsies?”

“They are encamped upon the common, some half-mile from the church. There are five of them—an old man, his two sons, and their wives. They have been here these three weeks, and have done no harm that I know of. They mended a kettle for my housekeeper and were paid honestly for it. But the constable, Mr. Prewitt, has found that one of the younger men was seen upon the church path upon Sunday evening, and he has taken that as proof enough.”

“What of the key?” I asked. “You are certain it never left your possession?”

“I would have sworn so,” he replied, “but here is a circumstance that troubles me. Upon the Monday morning I found my keys upon the table in the hall, where I had not left them. My housekeeper, Mrs. Bream, had dusted the study upon the Sunday afternoon, and she may have moved them, but she is positive she did not. Yet they were there, and I cannot account for it.”

I made a note of this, and then inquired as to the churchwarden, for I had observed that the vicar had mentioned him in his letter with a certain hesitation.

“Mr. Amos Craddock,” said he, “is a farmer and a man of some substance in the parish. He has been churchwarden these twelve years, and is much respected. But I must tell you, sir, that he has lately been in difficulties. He had a mortgage upon his land, and there was talk in the village that he would lose the farm. Yet within the last month he has paid off the whole of the debt—some eight hundred pounds—and no one knows whence the money came. He is a close man, and I would not have mentioned it, but that you asked me to conceal nothing.”

“And the bell-ringers?”

The vicar smiled faintly. “They are three old men who ring the bells upon Sunday and for weddings. They were in the tower upon the Sunday night, practising, as is their custom, until near eleven. They all agree that at midnight they heard a horse upon the path outside the churchyard, and that it stood for some minutes, and then went away. They saw nothing, for the night was dark, but they are honest fellows and their testimony is not to be disregarded.”

I asked to be shown the vestry, and we walked across the churchyard together. The church was old, with a squat tower and a porch of Norman work. The vestry was a small room at the north side of the chancel, with a single window, iron-barred, and a door that opened upon the churchyard path. The lock was of the old-fashioned kind, and showed no marks of forcing. The cupboard within was of black oak, and its door hung open.

“You observe,” said I, “that the lock of the vestry door is not damaged. It was opened with a key. And the cupboard likewise. You have but two keys to the vestry, I presume?”

“Two only—my own and that of Mr. Craddock, who as churchwarden holds a duplicate.”

“And the cupboard key?”

“That also is upon my ring, and there is no duplicate.”

I examined the window. The bars were sound, and the dust upon the sill was undisturbed. I looked next at the floor, which was of stone flags, and here I found what I had hoped for: a faint trace of damp earth, such as might be left by a boot, leading from the door to the cupboard and back again. The marks were not those of a labourer’s boot, but of a smaller and neater foot.

“The gypsies,” said I, “are they shod?”

“The young men wear boots of a rough kind,” said the vicar. “I have seen them.”

“These marks,” I returned, “were made by a boot of good leather, well kept, and of a size that would fit a man of middle height and slender build. Your churchwarden, Mr. Craddock—is he such a man?”
