The Valley of Sarn lies at the extreme edge of the great plain, where the land folds inward upon itself, as if weary of its own monotony, and seeks refuge in a sudden depression of soft, green hills. It was in this valley, on a morning after a tempest of quite unnecessary violence, that the shepherd Adrian found the bell.

Adrian was a young man of remarkable simplicity, a quality he shared with the pebble, which is doubtless why he had been chosen by Nature to guard sheep. The sheep themselves were exceedingly beautiful, being dressed in a uniform of perfect grey, but they possessed that profound stupidity which is the peculiar privilege of the entirely innocent.

The storm had passed, leaving the world washed to a silver brightness, and in the wet grass, gleaming like a fallen star, lay the bell. It was wrought of a metal as pale as moonlight, and hung from a handle of twisted ebony. Adrian picked it up, and, moved by that curiosity which is the ruin of shepherds and the making of philosophers, he rang it.

The sound was singularly clear, possessing a resonance that seemed to belong more to the air than to the metal. At the first stroke, every sheep upon the hillside sank upon its forelegs in an attitude of profound and sudden devotion. The larks in the air faltered in their ascent. A passing butterfly alighted upon a thistle, and remained there, its wings quivering in an ecstasy of stillness.

Adrian rang it a second time, and a third, and the entire hillside became a prostrate congregation. He was a youth of little imagination, but he recognized that he possessed a power over living things. He did not, however, attach any moral significance to the phenomenon. He merely concluded that the bell was a very good bell, and that the sheep were, for once, behaving with a becoming propriety.

Word of this miracle, as such things will, crept down the hillsides and through the hedgerows, until it reached the ears of Lord Valerius, who ruled the valley from a house of white stone that glittered with a certain aggressive opulence on the opposite ridge. Lord Valerius was a man of immense wealth and corresponding unhappiness, having discovered, somewhat late in life, that the only thing one can do with a great deal of money is to be miserable in a great many places.

He was, besides, a collector of beautiful objects. He collected enamels, and tapestries, and first editions, and he had recently begun to collect religious reliquaries, which he found aesthetically satisfying, as they combined gold with a touch of the grotesque. When he heard of the shepherd’s bell, a consuming desire took possession of his soul. To own a bell that could command the knee of creation seemed to him the ultimate ornament for a drawing-room.

He sent for Adrian.

The shepherd arrived at the white house, the bell hanging from his belt, looking entirely out of place among the bronzes and the Persian rugs. Valerius, who was lolling on a divan of yellow silk, regarded him with an expression of weary courtesy.

"So you are the boy with the bell," he said. "Ring it for me."

Adrian rang it, and the two greyhounds sleeping by the fire-place rose instantly and knelt, their slender bodies curved in a posture of supplication. Valerius laughed with an unmixed delight.

"It is a charming toy," he cried. "I must have it. What is your price?"

"It is not for sale, my lord," Adrian answered, with a simplicity that was entirely without insolence.

Valerius smiled, as one smiles at a child who has used a long word without knowing its meaning. "Everything is for sale, my dear boy," he said. "That is the one solid foundation of modern society. The only things one cannot buy are the things one does not want, and I want this bell. I will give you a hundred golden crowns."

"I cannot sell it," Adrian repeated.

"Two hundred," said Valerius, a slight irritation crossing his fine, pale features. "You are foolish to refuse. Money is the only thing that understands us all."

"The bell does not belong to me," Adrian replied. "I found it."

"Exactly," said Valerius, smoothly. "And I wish to find it. Possession, my dear shepherd, is not a moral state; it is merely an accident of geography. I propose to alter the geography. Come, I will give you three hundred crowns, and a new cloak."

"I will not sell it," said Adrian, and there was a pause.

Valerius looked at him with a cold, hard stare. He was not accustomed to resistance, for the poor are generally too polite to be sincere. "You will bring it to my hall tomorrow evening," he said, "or my men shall take it. A shepherd without a pasture is a mere philosopher, and philosophers, you know, are only tolerated in marble."

Adrian returned to the hills, his heart heavy. He sat among his kneeling sheep, and pondered the nature of power. He knew that Valerius would take the bell, and that the bell would be locked away in a glass case, a mere ornament for the amusement of idle guests. He thought of the hall, with its polished floors and its brilliant lights, and a singular idea entered his mind.

The next evening, as the sun was setting in a sky that looked like a bruised plum, Adrian descended to the white house. He was admitted without question, for the servants had been instructed to let him pass. He found Valerius in the great hall, surrounded by his friends—a collection of men and women who were considered brilliant because they said things that were entirely devoid of meaning with an air of profound conviction.

"Ah, the shepherd!" cried Valerius. "You have come to your senses. Give me the bell, and I will have the gold brought to you."

"I have come to ring it," Adrian answered.
