I had taken the rooms at the "Copper Kettle" in Saltcomb for the sea air and the isolation, being engaged upon a line of inquiry into the behavior of certain alkaloidal salts when subjected to a weak electrical current. It was a dull season. The east wind blew off the Channel, rattling the shutters, and the landlady, a Mrs. Gussage, was a woman of a narrow, domestic mind, perpetually suspicious of any gentleman who kept late hours and smelt of chemicals. She was a decent, hard-working woman, I admit, but her mental horizon was bounded by the parish church and the price of mutton.

My experiments were conducted in a small shed in the garden, which Mrs. Gussage called her "wash-house," though I believe no washing had been done there since the Crimean War. I had secured a quantity of common cress, some mustard seed, and a few hardy ferns from the beach, to serve as test subjects. The compound I was perfecting was a fluid of a pale, greenish hue, derived from a phosphate of ammonia treated with a novel catalyst of my own discovery. I had been applying it, in varying dilutions, to the roots of my specimens.

It was upon the Tuesday that I first noted the extraordinary effect. A single drop of the fluid, diluted in a half-pint of water, had been administered to a pot of common cress. By evening the cress had burst from its pot, its stems thick as my thumb, its leaves a dark, viridian green, and reaching to the roof of the shed. I was astounded, as any man of science must be. I had unlocked some fundamental mechanism of growth, some secret spring in the plant's biology that, once released, refused to wind down.

By Wednesday I had extended my trials to the garden. Mrs. Gussage had a miserable strip of lawn at the back of the inn, bordered by a few stunted laurels and a privet hedge. I injected a small quantity of my solution into the roots of the privet. By Thursday morning the hedge had become a wall of glossy, green leaves, reaching to the second-floor windows, and the laurels were thrusting out great, succulent limbs that darkened the kitchen windows. The sheer, raw vitality of the stuff was terrifying and magnificent. I was like a man who has invented a new explosive; I was proud, but I handled the bottle with extreme caution.

Mrs. Gussage was the first to register alarm. She came to my room on Thursday afternoon, her face pale beneath her bonnet.

"If you please, Mr. Pendel," she said, "but the privet is coming in at the scullery window. I cannot shut the casement. And the roots of it have cracked the brickwork of the drain."

I made some evasive reply about the unusual mildness of the season, but she was not to be put off. She was a practical woman, and she knew very well that privet does not grow six feet in a night, however mild the weather. She went down on her knees and implored me to tell her what I had done. I confessed that I had been experimenting with a new fertilizer, but I kept the exact nature of it to myself. I was afraid, I think, of the thing I had let loose. It was no longer a matter of test tubes and measured grains; it was a gross, physical fact, asserting itself in her garden with a kind of vegetable ferocity.

By Friday the situation was hopeless. The shed was entirely swallowed up. A great mass of green tendrils, thick as cables, had burst through its roof and were reaching across the yard. The lawn had vanished under a sea of rank, wet grass, a foot and a half high. The laurels had become trees, their branches heavy with a dark, unnatural foliage. The whole garden was a jungle, a piece of the tropics set down in the middle of a dull Kentish winter. The smell of it was overpowering, a thick, sweet, earthy smell that made the head swim.

It was on Saturday that Constable Bottley appeared. He was a large, slow man, with a red face and a profound respect for the law, which he conceived as a bulwark against anything he did not understand. He had been summoned by Mrs. Gussage, who was now in a state of mind bordering on hysteria. The plant life had begun to invade the house. A root of the privet had come up through the floor of the pantry, and the kitchen was dark with the shadows of the leaves.

"Morning, Mr. Pendel," said Bottley, in his slow, heavy way, touching his helmet. "This is a queer go, this is."

He stood in the yard and looked at the green wall before him with a kind of bovine wonder. He took out his notebook and sucked his pencil.

"Mrs. Gussage says you've been putting something on the plants," he said.

I admitted that I had.

"And what might that be, sir?" he asked.

I told him, as well as I could, that it was a chemical compound that stimulated growth. I used no technical terms, for I knew he would not understand them, but I tried to convey to him the scientific significance of what had happened. He listened with an air of patient, obstinate suspicion.

"And you've no license for this here compound?" he asked.

I explained that there was no license required for a fertilizer.

"Fertilizer," he repeated, slowly. "Well, I don't know about that, Mr. Pendel. But I do know as the Drainage Act of '78 gives me the right to report anything as is a nuisance to the parish. And this here," he said, waving his hand at the mass of green, "is a nuisance."
