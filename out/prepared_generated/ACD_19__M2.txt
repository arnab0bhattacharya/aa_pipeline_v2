The rain lashed against the windowpanes of my lodgings in Baker Street with a persistent, rhythmic fury that seemed to mimic the agitated tapping of Miss Elara Vance’s fingers upon the table. She sat before me, a slender woman of perhaps thirty-five, dressed in mourning black that spoke not of recent bereavement but of a long-standing, habitual sorrow. Her hands, white and trembling, rested upon a sheet of manuscript paper covered in musical notation.

Mr. Sherlock Holmes had retired to his room, leaving the investigation of this peculiar musical anomaly to my less acute faculties, though I suspected his interest had been piqued by the sheer absurdity of the situation. I examined the paper, finding it to be a transcription of a waltz, simple in structure but haunting in its melody.

"I must insist, Watson," Miss Vance said, her voice trembling slightly, "that I am not mad. I am a rational woman, a teacher of the conservatory for twenty years. Yet, I have never in my life encountered a student who alters the very essence of her performance so drastically from one session to the next, nor one who attributes this change to such a fantastical source."

"And what is this source?" I asked, leaning forward.

"A stranger," she replied, her eyes wide and unfocused. "He hums beneath the window of her practice room. A low, droning hum, yet it is precise. When I correct her, she insists that she is merely following the tune he sings. When I ask for the name of the composer, she shrugs. When I ask for the title, she says it has none. It is a new song, born of the air, dictated by the man in the garden."

I frowned, trying to reconcile this romantic nonsense with the cold facts of the case. "The pupil in question is Miss Constance Halloway, if I am not mistaken?"

"Yes. The daughter of the late Lord Halloway, currently under the guardianship of her uncle, Mr. Julian Halloway. He is a man of considerable wealth and, I am told, a temperament of considerable volatility. When I refused to cease my lessons, on the grounds that the girl was showing a decline in her technical proficiency, he forbade me from ever setting foot in the Halloway estate again."

"And you believe this is due to the stranger?"

"I believe the stranger is the cause of her distraction. Or perhaps, Mr. Holmes, as I have heard, the stranger is the result of her delusion. But there is a third possibility, one that chills me. I believe the stranger is real, and that he is using music as a code. A cipher."

I looked at the manuscript again. It was a waltz, yes, but the tempo markings were erratic. *Allegro*, then *Adagio*, then *Presto*. It was not a song of joy, nor of sorrow, but of command.

"You say he hums beneath the window?" I asked.

"Every evening at seven o'clock. For the last month. The groundsman swears he has seen a figure in a grey coat standing by the rose hedge. But when they approach, the figure is gone. The girl, however, is certain of his presence. She claims he wears a top hat and carries a cane with a silver head."

I stood up and walked to the window, looking out at the grey London street. The fog was rolling in, thick and yellow, obscuring the lamps. It was the sort of weather that encouraged shadows to stretch and lie.

"Miss Vance," I said, turning back to her, "if this man is real, and if he is communicating with a minor girl in secret, we are dealing with a breach of trust of the most serious nature. But if he is not real, then we are dealing with a young woman who is either deeply ill or deeply manipulated. In either case, the guardian’s reaction is not merely protective; it is punitive. He has cut her off from her only intellectual connection."

"He is a miser," Miss Vance said, her voice hardening. "He sees the music as a frivolity. He sees the girl as an investment to be preserved, not a mind to be cultivated. I fear, Mr. Watson, that if I do not intervene, Constance will be locked away in a sanitarium or a convent, her spirit broken."

I took the manuscript from her hands. "You must allow me to examine this more closely. The notation is standard, but the dynamics are unusual. Look here. These marks are not just instructions for volume; they are instructions for rhythm. *Staccato* here, *legato* there. It is not a melody to be played; it is a message to be decoded."

Miss Vance stared at me, her fear momentarily replaced by curiosity. "A message?"

"Music is a language, Miss Vance. And like any language, it can be used to say things that cannot be said in words. If this man is a gentleman, as the top hat suggests, he may be a suitor. But if he is a gentleman, he would not hide in the hedges. He would call. Therefore, he is not a suitor. He is a spy, or a thief, or a madman."

"And what do you propose to do?"

"I propose to go to the Halloway estate. I shall not go as a detective, for that would alarm the guardian. I shall go as a friend of yours, a fellow musician who has heard of the girl’s unique gift. I will offer to transcribe the 'stranger's' waltz for her, so that she may have a written record. If the guardian agrees, I will have access to the room. If he refuses, I will know that his refusal is not about the music, but about what the music hides."

Miss Vance nodded slowly. "And if the man is there? If he is waiting beneath the window?"
