The fog from the lake had crept up into the old streets of the Eaux-Vives quarter, a cold, clinging exhalation that seemed to swallow the gas-lamps before they could properly burn, leaving them to glow like the faint, smouldering wicks of a dying fire. Inside the austere walls of the Calvinist chapel, the damp had settled into the very stone, a chill that was not merely of the air but of the place’s stern, forgotten soul. I, David Ravel, stood at the lectern, my fingers resting upon the great, brass-clasped Bible, looking down into the nave where the shadows gathered thickly, as if the night outside had poured its heavy, viscous substance through the cracked doors and accumulated in the silent aisles.

Before me stood the man who called himself Stepan Kovalenko, though I knew, with the sudden, crushing certainty of a dream, that no such man had ever existed in the eyes of God or the police of the Russian Empire. He was one of the exiles, those brooding, specter-like men who drifted to the shores of the Leman, fleeing the vast, frozen silence of their own land only to carry its heavy gloom with them into our bright, orderly Swiss sunlight. He was tall, gaunt, with a sallow cheekbone catching the dim reflection of the single lamp I had lit. His eyes, dark and restless, held the furtive, burning glance of a hunted animal, and his clothes, though brushed with a desperate care, hung upon him with the shabbiness of an utter, tragic destitution. Beside him, veiled and trembling very slightly, was the woman. She was a compatriot, one of the flock I had inherited, a silent, pale girl whose name, I had been assured by the elders of the Russian colony, was Sofia. A name, a veil, a trembling—nothing more was vouchsafed to me.

I had been asked to perform the marriage without asking questions. The request had come from him, transmitted through a third party, an old Nihilist with a cough who drank his tea in the gloomy café down by the landing-stage, a man whose eyes held the profound, weary scepticism of one who has seen too many of his comrades vanish into the snows of Siberia. "Pastor," the old man had said, his voice a harsh, rasping whisper, "they have suffered enough. The paper is in order. You will not look too closely at the paper, eh? For the sake of Christian charity, you will not look."

And I, a shepherd of souls, had agreed—had agreed to this monstrous, this terrible, thing, in the name of a charity I did not feel, seduced by the somber romance of their misery, by the very silence that surrounded them.

I looked at the man, at the false name he had written in my registry, a name that was a lie, a cold, deliberate invention to conceal whatever crime or flight or abandonment lay behind him. The sacrament of marriage, I had been taught, was a covenant before God, a union of truth, a sacrament that rested upon the honest confession of two souls. Could a lie support the weight of divine grace? Could the holy mystery be built upon a deception, however pitiful?

The silence in the chapel was immense, oppressive, broken only by the faint, rhythmic drip of water from the eaves. It seemed to me that the shadows themselves were waiting, holding their breath, for my decision. I saw, as in a vision, the vast, desolate plains of Russia, the grey skies, the troikas plunging through the snow, the prison walls—and then, with a painful, ironic clarity, the neat, orderly, sunlit streets of Geneva, where nothing was ever concealed, where every soul had its number, its registered place, its unblemished, documented truth.

I began to speak, my voice sounding hollow and uncertain in the empty vault. "Dearly beloved," I said, and the words seemed to fall flatly, to lose themselves in the fog that pressed against the windows. I looked at the woman. Her veil was thick, but I could feel her eyes, wide, terrified, fixed upon me. She was not trembling now; she was rigid, frozen into an attitude of suspense, as if her whole life, her whole future, hung suspended upon the next word I would utter. And the man beside her, his face was set, his jaw clenched, the muscles of his cheek working nervously. He was staring past me, at the dark cross behind the altar, with the intense, consuming gaze of a man who has forgotten God and sees only his own desperate need.

I thought of my duty, of the stern, uncompromising faith of my fathers, of the incorruptible truth that must reside in the heart of the priest. And then I thought of the world they had fled, the world of arbitrary power, of secret police, of names erased and identities destroyed, where a man was nothing but a number, a unit of suffering, and where the only truth was the truth of pain. What was a name, in the face of that? What was a lie, if it was the only shield left to a soul hunted to the edge of the world?

I paused. The silence had become a living thing, a presence, a third figure standing beside them in the gloom. I felt the weight of the Bible beneath my hand, the weight of centuries of doctrine, of unyielding moral law. And I felt, too, the weight of their eyes, the weight of their desperate, unspoken need. The fog pressed closer, as if the night itself were listening.

I spoke at last, my voice dropping to a tone that was almost a whisper, yet which carried, in the stillness, with the force of a cry. "I must ask you," I said, turning to the man. "I must ask you to tell me your true name. For the sake of the sacrament, for the sake of the truth that must precede grace, I must know who you are."
