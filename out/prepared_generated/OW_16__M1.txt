It is a curious fact, though one sufficiently verified by the experience of every man who has ever dined out in Mayfair, that the passion for beauty is the most tedious of all the virtues. It demands a perpetual vigilance, a constant adjustment of the critical lens, and an inexhaustible supply of adjectives. To be an aesthete is, in short, to be a martyr to one’s own eyesight.

No one understood this better than Lord Evelyn Vane. He had been educated at Oxford to admire the Greek line, and in Paris to admire the Japanese curve, and he moved through the drawing-rooms of London with the weary air of a man who has seen everything worth seeing, and found it slightly out of focus. He was the president of the Society for the Preservation of the Pale, and he held strong views on the incorrectness of auburn hair. His soul, he told his friends, was a sensitive plate, over-exposed to the glare of the commonplace.

It was therefore with a sense of profound relief, mingled with a certain aristocratic horror, that he found himself seated next to Miss Elinor Vance at the Duchess of Chiltern’s country house, Highwood Hall. Miss Vance was, by the merciless standards of the London season, a disaster. She possessed a nose that defied the laws of classical proportion, a complexion that suggested the pale, unwholesome life of the library rather than the healthy glow of the tennis court, and a mouth that was formed, it seemed, solely for the utterance of inconvenient truths. She was, in a word, plain.

And yet, she was the only person in the room who did not bore him.

“Your lordship is staring at me,” she said, without looking up from her plate. “I am afraid I have committed some social solecism. Have I used the wrong fork, or merely the wrong adjective to describe the Duchess’s peacocks?”

“Neither, Miss Vance,” said Evelyn, recovering his composure. “I was merely struck by the extraordinary intellectual vitality of your profile. It is a face that demands a second look, not because it is beautiful, but because it is… articulate.”

“How delightful,” she replied, a flicker of amusement in her grey eyes. “Most men prefer a face that is merely decorative. A vase, after all, is judged by its curves, not by the quality of the emptiness inside it.”

Evelyn laughed. It was a genuine sound, a rarity in a house where laughter was usually a polite signal that a witticism had been made. “I have spent my life collecting vases, Miss Vance. I find them increasingly heavy to carry.”

“And I,” she said, “have spent my life cultivating weeds. They are so much more interesting than roses. Roses have only one thing to say to the world, and they say it so very loudly. Weeds, on the other hand, have a philosophy.”

From that moment, Evelyn was lost. He abandoned the Duchess’s peacocks, he abandoned the discussion on the correct shade of teal for a waistcoat, and he surrendered himself to the conversation of a woman who looked like a governess and thought like a philosopher.

His friends, of course, were appalled. Sir Reginald Fitzherbert, a man whose soul was entirely composed of cravats and cricket scores, found Evelyn in the library the following afternoon.

“Vane,” he said, adjusting a monocle that magnified his concern, “you are making a spectacle of yourself. You have been closeted with that… that bluestocking for three hours. She has a face like a forgotten Greek tragedy.”

“She has a mind like a Greek comedy, Reggie,” Evelyn replied, pouring himself a sherry. “She has just informed me that the only difference between a hypocrite and a philanthropist is that the hypocrite is at least honest about his desire for applause. I haven’t been so entertained since the night the Bishop fell into the goldfish pond.”

“She is plain,” Sir Reginald insisted, as if this were a fatal medical condition. “She has no conversation on the weather. She wears shoes that are designed for walking, not for display. What will people say?”

“They will say I am in love,” said Evelyn. “And for once, they will be right.”

“Love?” Sir Reginald shuddered. “With a woman who reads newspapers? It is a perversion of the natural order. Beauty is the food of the soul, Vane. You are trying to subsist on… on dry toast.”

“Dry toast,” Evelyn murmured, smiling, “is so much more sustaining than the sugared almonds you serve up as wisdom. Besides, I find that I have lost my appetite for sugar.”

He returned to London with her. They walked in the park, ignoring the scandalized glances of the dowagers. They sat in the British Museum, discussing the bankruptcy of the Roman Empire while staring at broken pottery. To Evelyn, the world had suddenly acquired a sharpness, a focus that it had never possessed when he was looking at a Botticelli. The Botticelli was lovely, certainly, but it had nothing to say about the price of coal or the absurdity of marriage.

It was a Tuesday evening in November, the fog pressing against the windows of Evelyn’s study, when the crisis occurred. They were drinking tea—Evelyn hated tea, but he found he did not mind it with her—and she was dismantling the character of a mutual acquaintance with the precision of a surgeon.

“He is a man,” she said, “who believes that a well-tied cravat is a substitute for a conscience. He is, in fact, a masterpiece of upholstery.”

“And what am I?” Evelyn asked, suddenly anxious. “I, too, have been a slave to the superficial. I have judged men by their boots and women by their earrings.”

Miss Vance looked at him. It was a look that stripped away the velvet, the silk, and the practiced ennui.

“You,” she said, “are a man who has finally realized that the most beautiful thing in the world is a thought that has never been thought before. You are not ugly, Evelyn, but you were terribly boring until you learned to listen.”
