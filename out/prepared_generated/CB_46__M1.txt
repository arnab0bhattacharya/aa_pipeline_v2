I had been three years in V—, a city of grey stone and greyer skies, and in that time I had learned to prize the small, warm corner of life I had carved for myself. My schoolroom, smelling of chalk and the leather of well-thumbed grammars; my two rooms on the fourth floor of a crooked house in the Altstadt; the society of books—these were my world. It was a world without England, without the green of home, and the want of it was a dull ache that never quite left me. So when the card was brought up, bearing the name *Miss Eleanor Vance*, and the servant murmured of an English lady, a cousin, I felt a sudden, foolish leap of the heart, as if a door had been flung open on a landscape I had thought lost forever.

She entered like a breath of May wind into my close, book-lined room. She was not beautiful in the way of the statues in the Hofgarten, but she had a vitality, a quick, intelligent charm that was far more captivating. Her eyes, the colour of a summer sky, seemed to miss nothing, and her smile was so frank, so full of warmth, that my habitual reserve melted under its gaze. “My dear cousin!” she cried, holding out her hands. “I knew I should find you! I have heard so much of you from our great-aunt in Shropshire. She speaks of you as the clever one, the one with the fire in her.”

We talked for an hour, and I was a willing captive. She knew it all—the name of my childhood dog, the particular slant of the roof on the old vicarage, the gossip of our remote branch of the family. She painted a picture of England so vivid I could almost smell the damp earth and the roses. I, who had starved for such news, devoured every word. I watched her, this living link to all I had lost, and felt a surge of affection so intense it was almost painful. She was charming, yes, but she was more than that; she was proof that I was not alone in the world.

It was only when the teacups were empty and the light from the window had begun to fail that a shadow passed over her bright face. “I have a dreadful liberty to ask,” she began, her voice dropping, her eyes fixed on a crack in the floorboards. “I have come to V— on a matter of some delicacy, a family affair that requires a small sum to settle. I am stranded here, and I am ashamed to say it, but I am quite without funds. I would not dream of asking, but you are my only friend in this foreign land.”

The request, when it came, was for fifty florins—a sum that represented a full month’s careful living for me. My first impulse was to give it to her without a moment’s hesitation. But a colder voice, the voice of hard-won experience in a city that was not always kind, spoke up within me. I looked at her fine travelling-dress, at the gold locket at her throat. “I am sorry for your trouble, Eleanor,” I said, and the use of her Christian name felt both a comfort and a risk. “But fifty florins is a great deal to me. It is my rent, my bread.”

A flicker of something—annoyance? disappointment?—crossed her features before the charm settled back into place. “Of course, of course. I am a fool to have asked. It is only… the shame of it. To be an Englishwoman, and to be in such a fix.” She spoke of honour, of family obligation, of a debt that must be paid to preserve a relative’s good name. Her words were a torrent of persuasive sentiment, and I felt myself being drawn back into the current of her belief.

“Tell me truly,” I said, leaning forward, my voice harder than I intended. “Is this matter what you say it is? For I have little to spare, but I would give it freely to a kinswoman in honest need. I will not give it to a stranger in a pretty dress who knows the name of my old dog.”

The silence that followed was a physical thing, heavy and charged. Her smile vanished. For a moment, she looked at me not as a cousin, but as a stranger, and her gaze was as sharp and assessing as a hawk’s. “You doubt my word?” she asked, her voice low and trembling with what might have been wounded pride or cold fury. “After I have sat here and opened my heart to you? After I have told you of your own mother’s wedding-veil?”

“I doubt the convenience of your need,” I retorted, my own heart hammering against my ribs. “You speak of England, of home, of ties of blood. But you are here, in a strange city, asking for money from a poor teacher. What am I to think?”

She rose then, a queenly figure in the fading light. “I see I have made a mistake. I thought I would find a friend, a kinswoman. Instead, I find a suspicious creature, hoarding her pennies and judging my misfortunes. Well, keep your money. I shall manage.”

She swept towards the door, and in that instant, a terrible conflict raged within me. The loneliness of three years cried out to me to call her back, to believe, to pay any price for the illusion of home. But my reason, my pride, the very integrity of my small, self-ordered life, forbade it. I watched her go, and the door clicked shut behind her, leaving a silence more profound than before.
