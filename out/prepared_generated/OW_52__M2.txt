Lady Mabel Vane had been educated in the belief that money was a form of dirt that one must wash off before one could be considered polished. It was a peculiar philosophy for a woman who owned three houses in London and a castle in the Highlands, but Mabel possessed that rarest of qualities, a genuine inability to be impressed by anything that could be bought at a department store. She viewed her bank balance with the same detached horror a surgeon views an abscess; it was necessary for the health of the body, perhaps, but certainly not something one discussed over dinner.

She met Julian Thorne in a gallery in Chelsea, on a Tuesday afternoon, which is the only day of the week when art is safe from the interference of the public. Julian was painting a portrait of a dog, or so he claimed, though the canvas suggested a study of the human soul rendered in shades of ochre and despair. He was young, and he was poor, and he had the kind of face that looks as if it has been waiting for centuries to be scandalized by the world.

"You are looking at my dog," Mabel said, with the calm authority of one who has never once been wrong.

"No," said Julian, without turning from the easel. "I am looking at the poverty of the brush. The dog is merely an excuse for the colour."

Mabel laughed. It was a bright, sharp sound, like a silver spoon dropping onto a marble floor. "You are either a genius or a lunatic," she said. "I suspect you are both, which is the only combination that survives in this town."

They fell in love with the speed of a chemical reaction, which is to say, instantly and with considerable heat, but without the smell. They spent their evenings in his studio, which was warm because of the fire and cold because of the wind, and they spoke of nothing that mattered, which is the only way to speak of anything that does. Mabel found in Julian the aesthetic of want. His threadbare coat was not shabby; it was a canvas for the dust of the age. His empty pockets were not voids; they were receptacles for dreams. She adored him precisely because he could not afford to be vulgar.

But Julian, who had been raised by a mother who sold her hair to pay for his tuition in drawing, did not share Mabel’s romanticized view of destitution. To him, poverty was not a muse; it was a landlord. It was a constant, nagging presence that smelled of boiled cabbage and desperation. He wanted to be rich, not for the sake of wealth, which he considered as tawdry as a peacock’s tail, but for the sake of silence. He wanted to buy his own space in the world, a space where he could think without the intrusion of hunger.

The discovery came on a Sunday, in the park, under a tree that was dropping its leaves with a melancholy that matched the mood. Mabel had brought a picnic of strawberries and cream, wrapped in silk that cost more than Julian’s annual rent.

"You look tired," she said, offering a strawberry. "Is it the light? The light here is too grey for your complexion. It makes you look like a statue that has been left in the rain."

"I am tired of being poor, Mabel," he said. He did not take the strawberry.

She blinked. "Of being poor? But that is the only thing that keeps you honest. It is the anchor that holds you to the earth while the rest of us are floating away in the air of our own vanity."

"It is not an anchor," Julian said, and his voice was hard, like a stone dropped into still water. "It is a chain. I want to be rich. I want to have a house with high ceilings and no leaks. I want to buy paint without counting the pennies. I want to be free."

Mabel withdrew her hand as if he had touched her with a diseased limb. "Free? You are free now, Julian. You are free to starve, which is the highest form of freedom. Once you have money, you will be owned by it. You will become a slave to your own comfort. You will lose your soul."

"My soul is safe enough," Julian said, standing up and brushing the grass from his trousers. "It is my body that is in danger. And if I must sell a little of my soul to keep my body warm, I will do it. It is a fair trade. The world is made of trade."

He left her there, sitting on the bench, holding her box of strawberries, which began to bruise and brown in the afternoon sun. She felt a profound sense of betrayal, not because he had left, but because he had been realistic. Realism, she decided, was the ugliest of vices. It was the vulgarity of fact.

She resolved then to cure him. She would teach him the beauty of poverty. She would show him that want was a kind of spiritual wealth, that the empty stomach was a temple for the mind, and that the cold was a purifier of the spirit. She would strip away his desire for comfort and replace it with the desire for truth.

She began her instruction the next day. She wore a dress of plain grey wool, which was the most expensive wool in London, and she ate only bread and water, which was the most expensive bread and water in the world. She spoke to him of the nobility of the peasant, the wisdom of the beggar, and the holiness of the debt. She told him that his desire for wealth was a symptom of a diseased imagination, and that if he would only look inward, he would find that he already had everything he needed.
