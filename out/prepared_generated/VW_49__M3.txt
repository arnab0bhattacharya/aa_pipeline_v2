The house, when she pushed the heavy oak door, exhaled the same damp, slightly fusty breath of coal-dust and dried lavender that it had breathed for twenty years. Eleanor stood for a moment in the narrow hallway, her suitcase resting against her boots, and felt the extraordinary sensation of having been erased. There was the same faint stain on the wallpaper near the hat-stand, branching upward like a dark, subterranean river; there was the same muffled ticking of the grandfather clock, measuring out the seconds with a ponderous indifference that suddenly struck her as almost monstrous. She had returned. The train journey from Cambridge had unspooled in a flurry of soot and flying fields, carrying her backward, backward, through the landscape of her girlhood, until the train had drawn into the station with a sigh that seemed to say, *Here you are, then; here you always will be.* And now, the house, which should have been smaller, which should have shrunk under the magnificent weight of the ideas she had accumulated in her mind, remained obstinately, insolently, the same.

She moved into the drawing-room. The light fell in long, slanting shafts through the mullioned windows, illuminating the dust motes that danced above the worn Persian rug. The rug was faded exactly where the sun struck it, a pale ghost of its former crimson, and the piano, with its pile of yellowing sheet music—Schubert, Schumann, and a tattered copy of *The Maiden’s Prayer*—stood against the wall like an animal waiting to be fed. Eleanor touched the keys; a single, isolated note sounded, vibrating in the quiet air, hanging there, then dissolving into the general hum of the afternoon. It was the note her mother had played a thousand times, always slightly out of tune, always pausing at the same bar to turn a page with a soft, moistened finger.

In the kitchen, she could hear the clatter of cups, the sharp, domestic percussion of her mother’s domain. Eleanor moved to the window and looked out over the garden. The laburnum was in bloom, its yellow tassels hanging heavy and still in the windless air; the stone birdbath, cracked and leaning slightly, held a shallow pool of rainwater in which a single, drowned wasp floated. She had watched that wasp, or another exactly like it, ten years ago. Nothing had changed. Nothing had moved forward. And yet, she thought, pressing her fingers against the cold glass until the blood left them, she was altered, radically, irrevocably altered. It was as if a lens had been slipped over her eyes at Cambridge. She had read Plato; she had read Aristotle and Descartes; she had walked under the vast, gothic arches of the college library and felt the thrill of pure abstraction, the severe, cold beauty of an idea pursued to its logical end. She had learned to think. And now, standing in her parents’ drawing-room in Richmond, thinking felt like carrying a concealed weapon.

“Eleanor? Is that you, dear?”

Her mother’s voice floated in from the doorway, sweet and vague, carrying with it the scent of boiling jam and fresh linen. Mrs. Vance appeared, wiping her hands on her apron, her face wearing an expression of fond, slightly startled welcome, as if Eleanor were a guest who had arrived a day early.

“We didn’t expect you until the six train,” her mother said, pressing a cool, dry cheek against Eleanor’s. “But it’s lovely to have you. Your father is in the study, doing the crosswords. He’s been struggling with the Latin all morning.”

Eleanor looked at her mother. She saw the silver threads in the dark hair drawn back so neatly, the slight stoop of the shoulders, the absolute, unassailable contentment of a life lived entirely within the confines of this house, this garden, this unvarying routine. A wave of tenderness, sharp and almost painful, washed over her, followed immediately by a suffocating panic. If she stayed, she would become her mother. She would boil jam; she would play Schubert slightly out of tune; she would grow silver threads in her hair and her shoulders would stoop under the invisible weight of the ticking clock.

“I took an earlier train,” Eleanor said. Her own voice sounded strange to her, too measured, too resonant for the small room. “I wanted to be home.”

“Well, you must be tired. Shall I put the kettle on? Mary has the afternoon off, but I can easily manage.”

Eleanor watched her mother move toward the bell-pull, her hand already rising to summon the absent maid out of sheer habit, then falling, remembering. The gesture, so small, so automatic, struck Eleanor with the force of a revelation. They were all sleepwalkers here, moving through a choreography they had learned by heart, never questioning the steps. She thought of the advertisement she had seen in the newspaper a week ago, tucked between the agony column and the shipping news: *Junior Assistant required for the Borough Library, Cataloguing Department. Apply in writing.* She had torn it out, folded it into the lining of her pocket, and carried it there ever since, a secret talisman against the encroaching silence.

“Mother,” Eleanor began, and then stopped. The word hung in the air, inadequate, a mere pebble tossed into the abyss that separated them. How could she explain that the house was a tomb? That the ticking of the clock was not a comfort, but a death sentence? That she wanted to earn money, to live in a rented room with a gas-fire that smelled of sulphur, to eat her dinner alone on a tray, if only she could be free of this suffocating, beautiful inertia?

Mrs. Vance looked at her daughter, her eyes softening with a sudden, piercing intuition. She saw the young woman standing by the window, her hands clenched at her sides, her chin lifted in that stubborn angle she had inherited from her father. Mrs. Vance felt a tremor of fear, a cold premonition of loss. She knew, without being told, that Eleanor had outgrown them. The house, with its faded rug and its out-of-tune piano, was no longer large enough to contain whatever it was that had blossomed in her daughter’s mind during those years away.
