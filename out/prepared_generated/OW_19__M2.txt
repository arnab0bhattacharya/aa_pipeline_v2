The carriage stopped before the oak doors of Ashby Hall, and Julian Vane stepped out into the damp chill of the Leicestershire evening, feeling that he had arrived not merely at a house, but at a trap. The gravel crunched beneath his boots with a sound that seemed grotesquely loud, as if the earth itself were protesting his presence. He was a poet of some repute, or at least of considerable pretension, and his latest volume, *The Dying of the Light*, had been praised by the critics with the specific, deadly sincerity that is reserved for works which are too beautiful to be useful and too dangerous to be ignored. Lady Hester Ashby, who had commissioned the invitation, loved this poetry with the fervor of a convert who has found a new god in the shadows. Her husband, Sir Percival, loved the land, the hounds, and the heavy, unpretentious silence of men who have never had a thought that did not fit into a bridle.

Julian smoothed his cravat, a thing of pale blue silk that looked impossibly delicate against the grey sky, and ascended the steps. He was greeted by Lady Hester, who was dressed in emerald velvet that shimmered like the wings of a large, benevolent insect.

"You are late, Mr. Vane," she said, her voice low and musical, like a cello string plucked in an empty room.

"I am early, Lady Ashby," Julian replied, bowing with a grace that was more theatrical than genuine. "The sun has not yet set, and it is not until the sun sets that the day is truly over. One must not be in a hurry to finish what is so beautifully begun."

Sir Percival stood by the door, a broad man with a face like a slab of unpolished granite. He looked at Julian with the detached curiosity one might show a butterfly that had flown into a gun room.

"Ah, the poet," Sir Percival said. "I hope you are not too tired. We ride at seven. The hounds are eager, and so am I."

"I do not ride," Julian said, smiling. "I read. And I listen to the wind in the elms, which is far more honest than the barking of dogs."

Sir Percival’s brow furrowed, a tectonic shift in his facial geography. "The wind is cold," he grunted. "The dogs are warm."

The first evening was a study in contrasts. The dining room was a cavern of red leather and dark mahogany, lit by candles that burned with a steady, indifferent flame. The food was excellent, heavy with the richness of the local soil, yet to Julian it tasted of earth and iron. Lady Hester sat at Julian’s left, her eyes bright with a dangerous intelligence, while Sir Percival sat at the head of the table, dissecting a pheasant with a knife that looked too sharp for its task.

"You have written a poem about a rose," Sir Percival said, cutting a slice of breast meat with surgical precision. "It was in the *Athenaeum*. It took three pages to say that a flower is pretty and then it dies."

"It was not about a rose," Julian corrected gently. "It was about the memory of a rose, which is a much more tragic thing. A living rose is merely a vegetable. A dead rose is an idea."

Sir Percival chewed slowly, his jaw working like a millstone. "Ideas do not fill the belly," he said. "And they do not keep out the foxes."

Lady Hester laughed, a soft, tinkling sound. "You are too literal, Percival. Julian sees the soul of things. You see only their utility."

"Utility is the only soul that matters," Sir Percival replied, pouring himself more claret. "If a thing is not useful, it is a nuisance. And if it is a nuisance, it should be shot."

Julian felt a chill that had nothing to do with the draft coming through the tall windows. He realized then that he was not merely a guest; he was an object to be hunted. Not with guns, perhaps, but with a more subtle weapon: the crushing weight of practical indifference. He was a sparrow in a cathedral of stone, singing his intricate song to an audience that cared only for the sound of the stone settling.

The next morning, as promised, the household rose at dawn. The sound of the huntsmen’s horns drifted across the moor, a brassy, urgent noise that shattered the stillness of the garden. Julian woke to the smell of tobacco and horse sweat. He dressed slowly, choosing a waistcoat of black velvet embroidered with silver threads, a garment that was as unsuited to the morning as a peacock in a pigsty.

He went down to the breakfast room, where the fire was roaring and the coffee was black and bitter. Sir Percival was already gone, leaving behind a half-eaten marmalade toast and a pair of mud-caked boots by the door. Lady Hester was waiting, her hair pinned up in a style that suggested she had not slept, or had slept only in fragments of dream.

"He is gone," she said. "To the wood. He says you are to stay in the library. There is a book there that he has not read, and he thinks you will enjoy it."

"Which book?" Julian asked, pouring himself coffee with a hand that did not tremble.

"The Bible," she said, her eyes narrowing. "He thinks it is the only book that is not pretentious."

Julian laughed, a dry, brittle sound. "He is right. It is the only book that tells the truth about men: that they are weak, and that they are loved despite it. But I do not think I shall read it. I think I shall sit by the fire and watch the smoke rise. It is a kind of poetry in itself."

Lady Hester sat down across from him. "You are not afraid of him?"
