The heat upon the marble was a thing one felt through the thin leather of one’s boots, radiating upward, entering the blood; and Eleanor, standing where the great columns tapered into the blue, found herself thinking not of Pericles, nor of the scholia, nor of the dialectical gymnastics to which Edmund had subjected her over the breakfast coffee, but of the yellow flower, the low, scrubby, nameless thing that had pushed its way up between the cracked flags at her feet. It was entirely itself. It had no history. It was not, as Edmund would say when she pointed it out, a variety of *Erysimum* mentioned by Theophrastus, but simply a flap of yellow silk trembling there in the glare, and it was beautiful, and she was suddenly, quite foolishly, near tears.

Edmund had gone ahead, his grey hat bobbing among the German party. She could hear the measured cadence of his explanations travelling back to her—how the metopes, how the pedimental figures—she could not catch the nouns, only the rhythm, which was the rhythm of the lecture hall, of the study at midnight, of thirty years of conversation conducted always on his terms. He was explaining Greece to a young man in spectacles who was writing it down. The young man nodded. Edmund took out his handkerchief and pressed it to the back of his neck. She had pressed his neck for him, once, when they were children and he had a headache, and the gesture had become, over the decades, a kind of signature—Eleanor, hovering; Edmund, attended to. She stood very still now and watched the two figures dwindle along the path, and the sound of his voice became the sound of a bee in a flower, a continuous vibration that signified nothing, that was only the noise a mind makes when it is consuming, and she thought, *I am forty-three years old, and I have never until this moment stood in a place where he was not speaking.*

The sea below was not blue, or not only blue; it was violet where it lay in the shadow of the island, and elsewhere a kind of hammered tin, and the light struck it and broke into pieces that seemed to her like the pieces of a life she had not lived. She had read Sophocles; she had read, at Edmund’s insistence, the whole of Thucydides in the original, taking three winters over it, and could parse every participial construction; but it came to her now, standing in the wind on the sacred rock, that she had read him as one reads a railway timetable, for information, and had missed the cry. There was a cry in it. The cry of a woman trapped in a stone house, or a man blinded by birds. She had not heard it because Edmund had stood between her and the page, explaining the optative.

A lizard shot across the stone and was gone, leaving only a small quivering of the air where it had been. She watched the space it had occupied. Everything here was precise and bright and cruel. The goats on the hillside opposite moved like punctuation across the brown slope, and the sound of their bells came up faintly, a thin music that seemed to belong to another century, and she thought how in London at this hour the maid would be drawing the curtains in the parlour and the kettle would be making its small preliminary sighs, and she did not want to be there. She did not want the parlour. She did not want the curtains drawn against the dark, nor the muffling, nor the cushions, nor the endless evening of reading aloud while Edmund turned the pages of whatever edition he was preparing, his pencil making its tiny scratches in the margin, and she sitting opposite, her hands idle, her mind drifting out into the street, listening to the cab wheels on the wet stones, thinking, *Is this all? Is this all?*

She had thought it was idleness, that restlessness. She had thought it was a failure in herself, a want of concentration, a want of the scholar’s temperament. But standing here, where the light was like a blade, she saw that it was not idleness but life, pressing up through the crust of his preoccupations, the way the yellow flower pressed up through the marble, asking only to be, and she had muffled it, year after year, because he was clever and she was not, because he had needed someone to listen, and she had been taught that listening was a woman’s genius. But it was not her genius. Her genius, if she had one, was this: to stand in a high wind and feel the salt on her lips and the sun on her arms and to know, without words, without Greek, without commentary, that the world was beautiful and terrible and that she was part of it, not its auditor but its witness, and that the cry which Sophocles had heard was the same cry she heard now, which was the cry of being alive and knowing that you must die, and she had almost missed it, almost let it go by, wrapped in Edmund’s learning like a shawl.

The young man in spectacles was writing still. Edmund had moved to the edge of the precipice and was gesturing towards the harbour. She could see his profile, sharp and confident, the profile of a man who has always known where he is and what century he belongs to, and she felt for him a sudden tenderness, as for a stranger one has sat opposite in a railway carriage, because he was small against the sky and the sea was very large, and his explanations, which filled rooms and hours, were nothing here, were a whisper against the noise of the wind and the goats and the water breaking far below.
