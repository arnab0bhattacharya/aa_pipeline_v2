The fever had consumed her by degrees, draining the vital warmth from her veins as a relentless sun parches a summer brook, until at length the last faint flicker of her spirit expired upon the midnight air, leaving me alone—utterly and irrevocably alone—in a world that seemed suddenly stripped of its light and joy. I was but nineteen, a desolate orphan gazing upon the ruin of all my earthly hopes; the cottage, which had so lately been a sanctuary of murmured prayers and agonized watchings, was now a silent sepulchre, and the heavy, suffocating stillness that succeeded the restless tossings of the sick-bed weighed upon my bursting heart with a tenfold cruelty. How dreadful it is to emerge from the oblivion of a brief and troubled slumber, to receive as a good morrow the mute wailing of one's hapless heart—to return from the land of deceptive dreams, where the beloved dead smile upon us with their accustomed grace, to the heavy, crushing knowledge of unchanged, irremediable disaster! Thus was it with me, now, and for ever; the sting of other griefs might be blunted by time, and the world might whisper of consolation, but I never looked first upon the morning light but with my fingers pressed tight upon my throbbing brow, my soul deluged by an interminable flood of hopeless misery.

They bore her to the cold, damp earth beneath the weeping yews of the churchyard, where the mountain winds swept down with a mournful cadence through the barren branches, and I stood by the newly turned sod, a lonely, unsheltered reed, shivering beneath the tempest of my own despair. It was then that Mr. Llewellyn, a distant relative, who possessed estates in the more fertile vales of Glamorganshire, approached me with words of measured kindness, offering me a home beneath his hearth. He spoke of the duties of society, of the solace to be found in the companionship of his daughters, and of the impropriety of a young woman dwelling solitary in the wilds of Merionethshire; but his voice, though eloquent of human prudence, fell upon my ear like a profanation. To leave the cottage where every stone was sanctified by her memory, to abandon the room where her frail hand had last guided mine, to mingle in a society that knew not the profound and sacred woe that had seared my soul—this I could not, would not, endure. I rejected his proposal with an impassioned firmness that perhaps bordered on the ungrateful, for I was wholly possessed by a holy, melancholy madness, a determination to preserve the scene of my bereavement as a temple consecrated to my undying grief. Let the world call me strange, let the village whisper of a mind unsettled by sorrow; I heeded them not. I would dwell alone with the shades of my departed happiness, and feed my spirit upon the recollections of the past.

The cottage stood on the verge of a rugged mountain-track, overlooking a narrow valley, through which a mountain-torrent rushed with a perpetual and hoarse murmuring, that seemed to the ear of my desolation like the continuous wail of a suffering spirit. Around it, the wild hills lifted their bare and heath-covered summits into the grey Welsh sky, and when the storms gathered, as they often did with a sudden and awful fury, the thunder rolled in long, continuous peals among the crags, while the lightning illuminated the black and yawning chasms with a vivid and terrible splendor. It was in such scenes that I found a fearful yet soothing sympathy; the sublime and terrible aspects of nature chimed with the tempestuous anguish of my own heart, and I would stand for hours at my casement, my hair drenched by the driving rain, watching the conflicting elements with a wild, intense delight, feeling that in the grandeur of desolation I was not utterly alone. The villagers, indeed, regarded me with a mixture of pity and superstitious awe; they crossed themselves when I passed, and whispered of the strange maiden who held commune with the spirits of the storm, and whose solitary walks among the crags at midnight seemed to them an evidence of a mind estranged from the common paths of humanity. But what knew they of the agonizing struggles of a heart that seeks in the immensity of nature some counterpart to its own boundless suffering? They were wedded to the earth, and their joys and sorrows were bounded by the narrow horizon of their daily toil; but I, cast forth from the paradise of domestic love, wandered an exile upon the mountains, seeking in the voice of the tempest some echo of the tones that were forever hushed.

I had no companion but my own thoughts, and they were thoughts of such overwhelming bitterness that I often marvelled they did not shatter the frail tenement they inhabited. I read again the books she had loved; I pressed my lips to the pages she had turned; I sat in the chair where she had been wont to sit, and strove to fancy her presence beside me, till the illusion became so powerful that I started at the sighing of the wind, half-expecting to hear her gentle voice breathing my name. But the illusion vanished, and the cruel reality remained: I was alone, and the years stretched before me like a vast and sunless desert, through which I must journey without a guide, without a friend, without a hope.

Sometimes, in the quiet dusk of evening, when the shadows lengthened across the valley and the owls began their mournful note, a feverish restlessness would seize me, and I would wander forth, unheeding of the gathering dews, climbing the steep ascents with a strength and agility born of despair. I would reach some lonely summit, and, gazing upon the wide expanse of rolling hills, veiled in the purple mist of twilight, a cry of involuntary anguish would escape my lips—a cry that was swallowed up by the void around me, and met with no response save the echo of my own wretchedness.
