The cathedral of Saint Veraine stood at the heart of the city of Aurelia, and its western gate, where the three roads met, was the particular province of a beggar named Corvin. He was a man of no discernible age, for his face had been weathered by sun and rain into a kind of map of endurance, and his rags, which were many and various, had achieved a sort of harmony in their decay. He had sat at that gate for so long that the stones themselves seemed to have grown accustomed to him, and the pious, passing in and out to their devotions, had come to regard him as a fixture of the place, as much a part of the architecture as the gargoyles who leered from the parapets, though considerably less demanding.

He was not, it must be said, a beggar of the ordinary sort. He did not wail, nor display his sores with a theatrical insistence, nor call down the blessings of heaven upon the heads of the charitable in a voice calculated to extort. He merely sat, with his bowl before him, and his silence was so profound and so absolute that it had the quality of a rebuke. It was a silence that made men feel, for a fleeting moment, the inadequacy of their own noisy virtues.

One evening, as the sun was setting and the great rose window of the cathedral burned like a coal of fire against the darkening sky, a stranger came to the gate. He was young, and his garments were of a whiteness that seemed to hold a light of their own, and his face was so beautiful that Corvin, who had seen all the ugliness the world could offer, felt his heart contract with a strange, sweet pain. The stranger did not pass by, as the others did, but stood before the beggar, and in his hands he carried a crown. It was not a crown of gold, such as the kings of the earth wear, but a circlet of woven light, from which there fell a radiance that made the beggar’s rags look like the robes of an emperor.

“Corvin,” said the stranger, and his voice was like the sound of a harp string plucked in a quiet room, “I have watched you at this gate for many years. Your patience has been noted, and your silence has been heard. I am come to offer you a gift.”

The beggar looked up, and his eyes, which were the colour of a winter sea, met the stranger’s gaze without flinching.

“And what gift is that?” he asked, though he knew already, for the light from the crown was falling upon his face, and it was warm, and it was sweet, and it promised an end to all his weariness.

“This crown,” said the angel, for such he was, “is the crown of a kingdom that is not of this world. It is the reward of those who have endured. Take it, and you shall sit at a table that is always full, and you shall know a joy that has no ending. There is but one condition.”

The beggar’s hand, which had been resting on his bowl, tightened upon it. “And what is that?”

“You must give up your bowl.”

There was a long silence. The cathedral bells had begun to ring for vespers, and their sound came down upon the two figures at the gate like a benediction. The angel waited, and his patience was the patience of eternity.

“No,” said Corvin.

The angel did not seem surprised. “You refuse?” he asked, and there was no anger in his voice, only a kind of sorrowful curiosity. “You refuse a kingdom for a bowl?”

“It is my bowl,” said the beggar, simply.

It was at this moment that the bishop of Aurelia, a man named Anselm, who had been passing through the gate on his way to the episcopal palace, stopped in his tracks. He was a learned man, and a proud one, and he had a great reputation for the subtlety of his theological disputations. He had heard the angel’s offer, and he had heard the beggar’s refusal, and he was filled with a righteous indignation that was not entirely unmingled with a sense of professional grievance. It seemed to him that a great opportunity was being wasted, and that the beggar, in his stubbornness, was bringing discredit upon the whole economy of divine reward.

“Fool!” cried the bishop, advancing upon them. “Do you not know what you are refusing? This is an angel of the Lord, and he offers you the crown of eternal life! And you would cling to a piece of filthy wood?”

The angel turned his gaze upon the bishop, and the bishop, who had faced down kings and emperors in his time, felt a sudden chill at the roots of his soul.

“It is not filthy,” said Corvin, quietly. “It is mine.”

“Yours?” scoffed the bishop. “What is yours? You have nothing! You are the poorest man in Aurelia! Your rags are a scandal to the city, and your bowl is a thing of no value whatsoever! I have seen better bowls in the scullery of my own palace!”

“And yet,” said the beggar, “it is the only thing I have ever owned. I did not inherit it. I did not steal it. I did not receive it as a gift. I carved it myself, from a piece of wood that I found by the river, on the day that I first came to this gate. It is not beautiful, and it is not valuable, but it is the work of my own hands, and it is the only thing in all the world that is truly mine.”

The bishop was not a man to be silenced by a sentiment. “But the crown!” he protested. “The crown is of infinite value! It is the reward of faith! It is the prize for which all men strive! And you would give it up for a bowl?”
