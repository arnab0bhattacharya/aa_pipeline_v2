At the edge of the great northern forest of Aethelwood, where the pines stood like a congregation of severe and silent monks, there was a small cottage. In it lived a little boy named Corvin, whose left foot was twisted in a way that denied him the swift, thoughtless grace of other children. His world was a world of observation. From his window, he watched the seasons change the forest’s attire, from the heavy green velvets of summer to the austere white ermine of winter. His lameness, which others might have called a misfortune, had given him the gift of stillness, and in that stillness, he saw things that hurried folk missed.

Each night, when the moon was a silver coin tossed into the velvet purse of the sky, a white stag would come to the cottage clearing. It was a creature of impossible beauty, its coat the colour of new snow, its antlers like branches of pure ivory on which the moonlight snagged and glittered. It did not speak, but its dark, liquid eyes held a summons that was more potent than any command. Corvin felt no fear, only a profound and silent communion. The stag was the soul of the forest, and it had chosen him.

One evening, the summons was irresistible. As the stag turned to melt back into the shadows of the trees, Corvin, leaning on a staff of gnarled hazel, followed. The forest, which had always been a barrier, now opened before him. The path was invisible to him, yet the stag’s hooves made no sound on the mossy earth, and Corvin’s own limping gait seemed to find a strange, compensating rhythm in the gloom. They travelled for hours, or perhaps for moments; time in the deep woods is a liquid thing. The air grew colder, smelling not of pine and damp earth, but of something ancient and sterile, like polished stone and frozen metal.

At last, they emerged from the tree line into a wide, manicured park, and beyond it lay a city. It was a marvel of white marble and crystalline spires, all sharp angles and perfect symmetry. There were no gargoyles to frighten, no softening ivy to cling, only the severe, unassailable beauty of a flawless jewel. The stag paused at the city gates, which were carved from a single piece of moonstone, and then dissolved into a shimmer of light.

Corvin entered. The city, which he would later learn was called Aethelgard, the City of Unblemished Forms, was populated by people of breathtaking perfection. Men and women of statuesque build and harmonious features glided along the polished streets. Their clothes were of strange, shimmering fabrics in hues of peacock blue, deep amethyst, and blood-red silk. Yet, as Corvin watched, a chill crept over him that had nothing to do with the night air. No one was old. There were no silver-haired grandmothers, no stooped grandfathers. More strangely still, no one was lame, or scarred, or bent by toil. Every face was a mask of serene, untroubled beauty.

And no one was happy.

They moved with a languid, listless grace, their conversations a murmur of exquisite, empty pleasantries. They praised the colour of a sunset that was not yet visible, the scent of a flower that was not there, the intricate design on a neighbour’s sleeve. It was a city dedicated to the worship of the surface, a living gallery where every exhibit was perfect and every spectator was bored. Their eyes, beautiful as they were, held the flat, incurious gleam of polished agates.

An old man—or what seemed an old man, though his face was as unwrinkled as a youth’s and his hair a perfect, unchanging silver—detached himself from a group and approached Corvin. His name was Lord Valerius, and he regarded the boy’s twisted foot with a look of profound, aesthetic horror.

“You are a flaw,” he said, his voice as smooth and cold as marble. “A discordant note in a perfect chord. How did you come here?”

“I followed a white stag,” Corvin answered simply.

Valerius sighed, a sound like wind through a hollow tomb. “The Stag of Memory. It is a cruel creature. It brings us reminders of the world we have forsaken. Here, in Aethelgard, we have perfected the art of living. We have banished age with its dreadful wrinkles, and decay with its vulgar imperfections. We have traded the chaos of nature for the serenity of art.”

“But you are not happy,” Corvin observed, his child’s honesty a sharper weapon than any adult’s wit.

A flicker of something—annoyance, perhaps, or a distant memory of pain—crossed Valerius’s perfect face. “Happiness,” he declared, with the air of a man quoting a well-worn epigram, “is a thing of moments, a fleeting and untidy emotion. We have exchanged it for beauty, which is eternal. To be happy is to be subject to change. To be beautiful is to be a god. Is it not better to be a flawless statue in a silent gallery than a laughing, weeping, aging creature in a muddy field?”

Just then, a woman approached. She was the most beautiful of them all, her hair a cascade of spun gold, her gown the colour of a twilight sky. But as she drew near, Corvin saw a single, perfect tear trace a path down her flawless cheek. It was a tear of glass, and it did not fall, but remained, a permanent, crystalline ornament of sorrow.

“This is Lyra,” said Valerius. “She has wept that same tear for a hundred years. It is considered our city’s finest work of art.”

Corvin looked from the glass tear to the woman’s empty eyes. He thought of his own small cottage, the scent of woodsmoke, the rough wool of his blanket, the ache in his leg on a cold morning. He thought of the joy of seeing the white stag, a joy made sharp and precious by the long, silent wait. These people had traded all that—the messy, painful, glorious tapestry of life—for a single, perfect, unchanging thread.
