The air in the counting-house of Mr. Silas Croft was as thin and sharp as the man himself. It tasted of dust and the acid tang of vinegar used to scrub the ink stains from the floorboards. Elias Thorne sat at his high stool, the nib of his pen scratching a precise, elegant script in the ledger. He was a creature of this room, all angles and shadows, his face a pale, unlined mask of concentration. He was twenty-three, but his work had carved the years from him, leaving only a relentless, meticulous efficiency. Mr. Croft, a man whose bulk seemed to consume the very light in the room, had finished his dictation with a grunt. “The Gages, number four, Saffron Hill. Three weeks in arrears. The father’s a tanner, or was. No work for him now, by the smell of the place. They’re out. Today. Take the constable if you must.”

Elias dipped his pen. “Yes, Mr. Croft.” There was no more to be said. An eviction was not a matter for sentiment; it was a simple subtraction. A column of figures that no longer balanced. He blotted the ledger, the small, final gesture of a concluded matter, and rose to fetch his hat and coat. The task was a familiar one, a grim pilgrimage through the city’s tangled veins to where the money ran thin and the desperation pooled in the gutters. Saffron Hill was a place he knew well, a labyrinth of brick and misery that stank of boiled cabbage, gin, and the faint, ever-present sweetness of decay.

The tenement was a leprous giant, its brickwork stained with weeping soot and its windows like vacant eyes. The air on the staircase was a physical presence, thick with the smells of a dozen cramped lives. Elias moved through it with a practiced economy, his boots making little sound on the treads. He was halfway to the third floor when he saw her.

She was sitting on a step, a small figure lost in the gloom. A girl of perhaps ten or eleven, with hair the colour of weak tea and eyes that held a weary knowledge far beyond her years. She was mending a tear in a rough woolen sleeve, her small fingers working a needle with a deftness that spoke of long practice. She did not startle at his approach, but simply looked up, her gaze direct and unnervingly steady. This, he knew, must be the eldest Gage girl.

He stopped, his hand resting on the banister. “I am looking for the Gage family,” he said, his voice flat, devoid of inflection. It was the voice he used for these errands, a tool as blunt and effective as a crowbar.

The girl set down her sewing. “My father is not well,” she said. Her voice was quiet, but clear. “He is sleeping.”

“Your family is three weeks behind on the rent,” Elias stated. It was not a question. “My employer has instructed me to see you removed from the premises.”

The girl’s composure did not break. There was no plea in her eyes, no sudden welling of tears. There was only a deep, quiet stillness. She looked at his clean, dark coat, his polished shoes, the leather satchel at his side that held the instruments of her family’s ruin. Then, she reached into the pocket of her thin dress.

Her hand emerged, closed in a small, pale fist. She held it out to him. “This is all we have,” she said.

Elias looked at her hand, then back at her face. He was not accustomed to being addressed as a person. He was a function, a harbinger, the walking embodiment of a debt. He hesitated for a fraction of a second before extending his own hand. Her fingers were cold as she placed the coin in his palm.

It was a single, grubby farthing. The copper was worn smooth, the profile of the monarch almost rubbed away. It was warm from her grasp. He stared at it, this tiny, pathetic piece of metal. It was an insult to the sum owed, a laughable gesture against the tide of their poverty. It would buy a crust of bread, perhaps. It would not delay their eviction for an hour. It was, in the cold calculus of Mr. Croft’s world, worth less than the effort of taking it.

But as Elias stood there on the grimy staircase, the farthing seemed to gain a strange and terrible weight in his hand. It was not money. It was not a payment. It was a story. It was the last defence of a child against an uncaring world. It was her entire fortune, offered not to buy a debt, but to buy a moment. To buy a chance. The girl’s steady, unblinking gaze held him fast. In it, he saw not a debtor, but a custodian. She was guarding her sleeping father, her sick mother, her younger siblings. She was the wall between them and the street. And she had just offered him the only stone she possessed.

Elias’s mind, usually a well-ordered ledger of figures and dates, was thrown into chaos. He saw the coin, and he saw the tenement, and he saw the family huddled in their single room. He saw the stark, clean lines of his own life, the solitary room he rented, the quiet meals, the ordered existence. It all seemed brittle, a thin veneer over a chasm of meaninglessness. This girl, with her single farthing, possessed a gravity he lacked.

Slowly, deliberately, he closed his fingers over the coin. He did not put it in his satchel with the other receipts. He slipped it into the waistcoat pocket of his shirt, where it rested against the steady, unfamiliar beat of his own heart.

He looked at the girl one last time. “Stay here,” he said, his voice still flat, but now it was a mask for something else entirely. He turned and descended the stairs without another word, leaving her sitting in the oppressive silence.
