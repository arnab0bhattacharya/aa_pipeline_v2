The rain had come in from the river, a fine grey mist that blurred the edges of the world, and it was this mist, or something like it, that seemed to have settled in the room at Cheyne Walk. The young man, Mr. Thistle, sat on the edge of a chair whose damask had faded to the colour of old roses, and he felt that to breathe too deeply would be to disturb the dust of a thousand conversations. His notebook lay open on his knee, its pages a startling, virginal white against the gloom. He was very young; his hands, holding the pen, were smooth and unmarked, and he had that earnest, almost painful desire to capture truth which is the particular affliction of the young.

Mrs. Vane, who was the centre of this room as a stone is the centre of a ripple, watched him from the depths of a winged chair. She was very old, and her face, in the firelight, was a web of fine lines, but her eyes were sharp and bright, like a bird’s. They took in the cut of his collar, the shine on his shoes, the desperate hope in his expression, and they twinkled. A story, she thought, is not a fact. A story is a shape you give to the chaos of things so that a young man might sit in a chair and feel that the world is ordered, and knowable. And so she began.

‘It was in the winter of eighty-nine, or perhaps it was ninety,’ she said, her voice a dry rustle, like leaves skittering across pavement. ‘We were all at Rodmell, at the Lewes’s place. The wind came down from the Downs and you felt it could scour the very soul from you. And there was young Aubrey, so brilliant, so full of a kind of cold fire. He was writing his great poem, the one that was to have been his masterpiece. He read us the first canto one evening. The words… they were like icicles. Beautiful, but they would freeze the tongue that spoke them.’

Mr. Thistle’s pen scratched. *Aubrey. Winter, 1889. Canto One. Icicles.* The words became a little island of fact in the swirling fog of her memory. He did not know that the Lewes’s place was in Sussex, not Norfolk, or that the wind had been a mild autumn breeze. He saw only the icicles.

‘And then,’ Mrs. Vane continued, her gaze fixed on a point somewhere above the young man’s head, where the shadows of the dripping leaves outside trembled on the ceiling, ‘there was the affair of the peacock feather. It was a silly thing. A trifle. But it is the trifles, is it not, that hold the whole structure together?’

She paused, and in the pause, the whole of that imaginary evening assembled itself. The gaslight, the scent of coal smoke and damp tweed, the tension that vibrates in a room when genius is present. Mr. Thistle felt it too. He leaned forward.

‘Aubrey had given it to poor Gwendolen. A single feather from a bird he had shot. A gesture, you see. Of possession. Of… well. And Gwendolen, who was all warmth and colour, like a piece of stained glass, she wore it in her hair that night. And when she came into the drawing-room, where we were all talking of Nietzsche and the death of God, Aubrey… he stopped. He just looked at her. And in that look was everything. The poem, the passion, the terrible, doomed future of it all. He saw the end, right there, in the iridescent eye of the feather.’

She stopped. The fire settled with a soft sigh. Outside, a cab splashed by, its wheels tearing the wet silence of the street. Mr. Thistle had not written a word for a full minute. He was lost. He saw Gwendolen, a woman he had never met, standing in a light he had never witnessed, and he felt the tragic weight of a glance that had never been exchanged. He believed it. He believed it utterly, for it had the shape of a story, and his young mind was hungry for shapes.

‘And what happened to the poem?’ he asked, his voice hushed.

Mrs. Vane smiled, a small, secret curve of her lips. The firelight caught in her eyes, and for a moment she looked not old at all, but ageless, like a spirit who tells tales to travellers. She looked at the boy with his pristine notebook, his belief shining on his face like a lantern in the fog, and she felt a profound and tender satisfaction. The truth of what had happened—that Aubrey was a mediocre poet who died of influenza, that Gwendolen was his cousin who disliked him, that there was no feather—was a dead thing. But this, this shimmering fiction, this was alive. It was in the room now, between them. It was as real as the teacup on the table, the rain on the window, the boy’s eager, believing heart. And in the grey, swirling quiet of the Chelsea afternoon, that was the only truth that mattered.
