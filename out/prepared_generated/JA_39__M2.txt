The arrival of Mrs. Armitage at Highbury Grove was an event which, in the estimation of the elder Mrs. Hargrave, was calculated to bring about the immediate ruin of the family’s comfort, if not its reputation. Her son, Arthur, had married a woman of such evident refinement and urbanity that it was impossible not to admire her appearance, though equally impossible to endure her company. She possessed the air of a creature who had never known a draught, nor suffered from a wet floor, and who viewed the rustic simplicity of the Northamptonshire hills with a polite, yet unfeigned, distaste.

Arthur Hargrave, a man of amiable disposition but limited imagination, had prepared for his wife’s visit with the anxious care of a general expecting a siege. He had polished the silver, aired the bedchambers, and instructed the servants to suppress their usual habit of laughing at the cat. He was resolved to present his family in their best light, for he believed that a little tact could bridge the chasm between the provincial and the metropolitan. His mother, however, held a different opinion. Mrs. Hargrave had raised three sons and seen two of them through marriages that were, by all accounts, satisfactory. She trusted not in tact, but in the immutable laws of nature, which she believed to be superior to any fashion devised by the witty ladies of London.

When the carriage drew up to the door, Mrs. Armitage descended with a grace that seemed to defy the uneven gravel. She wore a gown of pale blue silk, trimmed with lace that cost more than the rent of the farm next door, and her bonnet was a structure of such architectural complexity that it seemed designed to shelter her from the very idea of the outdoors.

"Your mother, I am sure, will be most kind," said Mrs. Armitage, as she was handed the tea tray in the drawing room. Her voice was light, clear, and possessed of that particular softness which serves to mask a sharpness of judgment. "I confess, I had a dreadful apprehension of the country. One reads so much of the mud, the damp, and the utter lack of conversation."

Mrs. Hargrave, seated by the fire in her best morning gown, which was of a sensible brown and had been mended twice, smiled with the serene composure of a woman who had long since ceased to be offended by the observations of the young.

"I hope, my dear, you will find Highbury Grove quite dry," said Mrs. Hargrave. "We have had a very moderate autumn, and the air is remarkably pure. It is said to be good for the lungs."

Mrs. Armitage raised her eyes, which were dark and quick, and fixed them upon the old woman with an expression that suggested she was examining a specimen of insects. "Pure air," she repeated. "Yes, I am sure it is. I shall try to breathe it, though I must confess I find the scent of the hearth a little overpowering. I do so hope we may have the windows open, if the weather permits. I am quite fond of a draft, provided one is warm enough to bear it."

Arthur, standing by the table, intervened with a hasty smile. "The windows are always open, my dear, in the sitting room, if you wish them. The air is quite refreshing."

"I am sure it is," said Mrs. Armitage, not looking at him, but at his mother. "I am most particular about fresh air. My late father, who was a man of great sensibility, always said that a room without circulation was a tomb for the mind. I find that stale air has a way of making one feel quite stupid. And I am sure I am the last person in the world who wishes to be stupid."

Mrs. Hargrave sipped her tea, a beverage of such dark, robust strength that it would have turned the stomach of a lady of fashion, but which she consumed with the regularity of a clock. "I am glad to hear you value the mind, my dear," she said mildly. "It is a thing we try to cultivate here, though perhaps not with the same... vigor as in London."

"Oh, no, no," said Mrs. Armitage, waving a hand dismissively. "We are simple people here, I daresay. I have been told so by Mrs. Gable next door. She spoke of your family as being 'very decent' and 'unpretentious.' I think 'unpretentious' is a word I shall have to learn to pronounce with the right emphasis. It sounds so much like 'unintelligent.'"

Arthur shifted his weight, uncomfortable in his own home. He glanced at his mother, who was now arranging her shawl with a precision that spoke of inner calm. He saw in her face not the offense he expected, but a kind of benevolent amusement, as if she were watching a kitten attempt to catch a fly.

"Mrs. Gable is a very charming woman," said Mrs. Hargrave. "She makes a most excellent plum cake. I am sure you will find it to your taste, if you have a sweet tooth."

"I have no sweet tooth," said Mrs. Armitage. "I find sugar to be a great enemy to the complexion. I live almost entirely on water and air. And a little chicken broth, perhaps, if the kitchen is clean. I am told that the servants in the country are rather... rustic. I hope they will not be too noisy. I find that noise destroys the faculty of thought."

"The servants are very quiet," said Arthur, feeling the need to defend his household. "They are all old servants. They have been with us for years."

"Old servants," said Mrs. Armitage, with a slight shrug. "They are often the most difficult to manage. They think they know everything. I find that a new maid, with a fresh mind, is much more amenable to instruction. But I am sure you will not wish to disturb your establishment on my account. I should be the last to wish to cause any inconvenience. It is so tiresome to be the cause of any trouble, is it not?"
