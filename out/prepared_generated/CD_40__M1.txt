Of all the fogs that ever descended upon the ancient city of Minsterbridge—fogs that crept up from the River Stour, fogs that muffled the cathedral bells in a woollen wrap of sound, fogs that turned the gas-lamps into so many weeping, luminous ghosts—the fog that met Dr. Elias Thorne upon his return from the burning plains of India was the most oppressive, the most suffocating, and the most symbolically appropriate that could possibly be imagined. He had left a land of blinding, unrelenting sun, a land where the very air shimmered with heat and the dust of a thousand years, to find his own heart, and his own future, shrouded in a damp, grey, and impenetrable murk.

Elias Thorne, who had been a lanky, hopeful boy of nineteen when he sailed away, was now a man of thirty, with a physician’s steady hands and a gaze that had seen too much cholera, too much sunstroke, too much of the swift and merciless finality of the grave. He had come back to Minsterbridge with little more than his diploma, a few books, and the burning, foolish, unquenchable hope of Miss Rosalind Vane. Rosalind, who had been his childhood’s companion, his confidante, his very reason for striving! He pictured her still as the girl with the ribbon in her hair, who had dared him to steal apples from the Deanery garden, whose laughter was like the chiming of silver bells. It was for her he had endured the privations of a ship’s surgeon, for her he had studied by the light of a sputtering oil lamp until his eyes burned, for her he was coming home, a man made, to claim the promise that had passed between them in a thousand unspoken glances.

He found his old home, a small house in the shadow of the great Minster, now occupied by strangers. A few enquiries, made with a heart that beat a frantic tattoo against his ribs, directed him to the Vane residence on the Cathedral Close—a handsome, gabled house that spoke of comfort, of established position, of everything he did not possess. And there, as he stood on the gravel path, the fog seemed to part for a moment, and he saw them. There was Rosalind, more beautiful than his fondest dreams had painted her, her face alight with a gentle happiness. And beside her, his hand resting with proprietary ease upon her arm, was Mr. Cuthbert Pemberton, the very man for whom Elias himself had once toiled as a junior clerk. Pemberton, who was now not a clerk, but a man of substance, a partner in the great malting firm of Pemberton & Sons, a man with a waistcoat that strained against its buttons and a watch-chain that hung like a golden fetter.

The meeting was a masterpiece of social agony. Rosalind’s cry of “Elias! Can it be?” was a gasp of genuine surprise, which quickly curdled into a look of such profound pity that it was worse than any scorn. Pemberton, for his part, was all affable condescension. “Thorne! My dear fellow! Back from the Indies, eh? Looking as brown as a berry! We heard you’d taken up the medical line. A noble profession, a very noble profession.” He said it with the air of a man congratulating a dog on learning a new trick. The engagement, it transpired, was a thing of six months’ standing. It was announced with blushes and stammered explanations, a sorry little tableau of present happiness built upon the ruins of a forgotten past. Elias, with a voice he did not recognise as his own, offered his congratulations, his felicitations, his every good wish, the words tasting like ash in his mouth. He felt as though he were watching his own life from a great distance, a play in which he had been cast, without his knowledge, as the fool.

He took the only practice available to a man of no name and less money: a dreary set of rooms in a narrow, weeping street called Pilgrim’s Row, where the houses leaned together as if for warmth or in shared despair. His patients were the poor, the forgotten, the men and women who lived in the shadow of the Minster’s magnificence but shared none of its light. They paid him in ha’pennies, in kind, or not at all, and Elias Thorne, who had once dreamed of a practice among the gentry, found a grim satisfaction in tending to their fevers and their ailments. He was a good doctor, a careful doctor, and his kindness was as much a part of his physic as the powders he dispensed from dusty bottles. He thought he had built a wall around his heart, a wall of duty and professional detachment, and believed he could endure the sight of Rosalind Vane’s happiness from a distance.

But the wall was not built strong enough. The summons came not from the grand house on the Close, but from its back door, delivered by a terrified scullery maid whose apron was stained with tears. It was for young Master Arthur, Rosalind’s brother, a boy of ten with a constitution as fragile as spun glass. He had been seized by a fever of such virulence that the family’s own physician, the pompous Dr. Grantly, had already washed his hands of the case. “The boy is in God’s hands, Mr. Pemberton,” Grantly had pronounced, with a gravity that was more an absolution of his own failure than a statement of faith.

Elias Thorne went. He entered the sick-room, a place of shadows and the smell of camphor and despair, and found the boy small and still beneath the covers, his face flushed with a terrible heat. For the next three weeks, Elias lived in that room. He became a phantom of Pilgrim’s Row, seen only when he hurried back for fresh supplies, his face gaunt with exhaustion. He fought the fever not with grand pronouncements, but with a quiet, stubborn persistence—a cool cloth on the brow, a drop of water on cracked lips, a murmured word of encouragement in the dead of night. He and the boy’s life were locked in a desperate wrestling match, and Elias would not let go.
