The brass eyepiece was cold against Arthur Vane's brow, but the sweat on his skin made it slip. He adjusted the dial—a fraction of a turn, no more—and the image behind the glass sharpened. He had been calibrating for three hours. The gas lamps in his Chelsea laboratory hissed and flickered as though protesting the late hour, but Arthur did not notice. He was looking at Westminster.

Or rather, he was looking at something above Westminster. The angle was wrong. He had set the machine's focal pointer at street level, targeting the junction of Bridge Street and Parliament Square, just as he had done a dozen times before. But what he saw through the aperture was not the cobbled road or the familiar shadow of the Clock Tower. It was sky. A pale, bruised sky, streaked with black trails that moved and curled.

He pulled back and checked his settings. The resonator hummed steadily. The crystal array was aligned. He pressed his eye to the glass again and this time tilted the focal lever downward—slowly, carefully—and the image descended through clouds into the city itself.

What he saw made no sense.

The streets were there. He recognised the shape of them, the curve of the Embankment, the silhouette of the Abbey. But the buildings along Whitehall were gutted, their façades peeling away like skin from fruit. Rubble choked the roadways. Over the Thames, the bridge—he could not tell which one—had lost its middle section, and the water beneath it was dark with debris. There were no people. No carriages, no constables, no newsboys. Just stillness, and above it all, those black trails in the sky, drifting like smoke from a fire that had no source he could find.

Arthur stepped back from the machine. His hands were trembling. He checked the time dial on the resonator's face: it read the current date, the fourteenth of March, nineteen hundred and three. He checked it again. He wiped the dial's glass with his sleeve and checked it a third time.

The image remained.

He spent the rest of the night running tests. He redirected the focal pointer to Hyde Park and saw trees stripped bare, their branches snapped and pointing upward as though the destruction had come from above. He redirected it to the docks at Wapping and saw ships half-submerged, their masts broken. He redirected it to King's Cross and saw the station's great arched roof collapsed inward, the tracks beneath it twisted into shapes that reminded him, absurdly, of the wire frames he used to shape his daughter's hair ribbons.

Everywhere the same. Ruin. Absence. Those black marks in the sky.

By dawn he understood. The machine was not showing him what was there. It was showing him what would be there. Some fault in the resonator's timing circuit—some harmonic misalignment he had not anticipated—had shifted the aperture forward. He did not know how far. Weeks, perhaps. Months. But the image was not a hallucination. He had built the machine to see at a distance, and it was doing exactly that. It was seeing across distance of a kind he had never intended.

He went to his friend Edmund Lyle first. Edmund was a physicist at King's College, a man whose respect Arthur had earned over fifteen years of correspondence and collaboration. They met at a café on Sloane Street, and Arthur told him everything. He spoke carefully, laying out the mechanism, the tests, the images. He did not embellish. He used numbers and observations, the language they shared.

Edmund listened with his hands flat on the table. When Arthur finished, there was a silence that lasted long enough for the waiter to refill their cups and withdraw.

"You understand what you're saying," Edmund said.

"Yes."

"You're saying you've seen the future."

"I'm saying the machine showed me an image of London in a state of destruction that does not currently exist. The most likely explanation is a temporal displacement in the resonator's field."

Edmund's mouth twitched. "The most likely explanation."

"I have considered the alternatives. Lens corruption, crystal fatigue, operator error. None of them account for the consistency of the image across multiple target sites."

"Arthur." Edmund leaned forward. "You have built a remarkable device. I have no doubt of that. But what you are describing is not science. It is prophecy. And I do not deal in prophecy."

"I am not asking you to believe in prophecy. I am asking you to come to my laboratory and look through the aperture."

Edmund came, two days later. He looked through the aperture for forty minutes. He adjusted the dials himself. He checked the crystal array, the resonator frequency, the focal calibration. When he finally stepped back, his face had taken on a grey, closed quality that Arthur recognised. It was the face Edmund wore when he had decided not to believe something he could not explain.

"There must be a fault in the lens system," Edmund said. "Some kind of composite artefact. The images are detailed, I grant you, but they are not real. They cannot be."

"They correspond to real geography."

"So do the paintings of Mr. Martin. That does not make them true."

Arthur went to others after that. He wrote to the Royal Society and received a polite reply suggesting he submit his findings for review. He did. The review committee returned his paper after six weeks with a note thanking him for his interest and advising him that the society did not currently accept submissions pertaining to prognosticatory devices. He contacted the Home Office and was referred to the Metropolitan Police, who sent an inspector to his laboratory. The inspector looked through the aperture, thanked Arthur for his time, and left. Arthur heard nothing further.

He began to notice the black trails more clearly when he returned to the machine. They were not smoke. They were too regular, too evenly spaced. They cut across the sky in parallel lines, converging toward the east. He followed them as far as the focal range allowed—out past the estuary, over the grey water—and there, at the edge of the image, he saw something that made him grip the edge of the workbench.
