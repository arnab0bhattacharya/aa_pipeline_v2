The rain lashed against the leaded panes of the study, a ceaseless, grating percussion that seemed to mock the stillness within. I sat at the desk, the ink in the pot black as a bruise, my hand trembling not from the chill that seeped through the walls of this damp Flemish house, but from the fury of a spirit forced to watch its own dignity trampled. Outside, the street was a grey smear of mud and misery; inside, the air was thick with the scent of stale tobacco and the heavy, cloying perfume of Mrs. Van Houten’s lavender water, which clung to my clothes even after she had departed, a scent of cloying deception that I could not shake.

I had been employed by Mr. Van Houten, a merchant of considerable repute in Antwerp, to instruct his two sons, Casper and Willem, in the English language. It was a task that required patience of a stone, and I had possessed, in my younger, more hopeful days, a patience that might have rivaled the granite of the harbor walls. But patience, like faith, is a combustible substance; it burns, and when it burns, it leaves ashes.

Casper, the elder, was a boy of fifteen with eyes that held a dull, watery vacancy, as if he were looking not at the text before him, but at some distant, unattainable horizon. Willem, his brother, was younger, sharper, but possessed of a cunning that made him a master of evasion. They did not read; they stared. They did not speak; they waited. And when I, driven by the imperative duty of my station, attempted to draw forth their tongues, they offered me silence, or worse, a smug, insolent half-smile that felt like a slap.

“The English tongue,” I had said that morning, my voice steady, though my heart hammered against my ribs like a trapped bird, “is not merely a tool of commerce, gentlemen. It is the key to the mind. Without it, you are locked in a cellar, seeing only the dark.”

Casper had yawned. Willem had picked at a loose thread on his sleeve.

It was then that Mrs. Van Houten entered. She did not knock. She never knocked. She moved through the room with the fluid grace of a cat that knows it will never be shooed away, her face a mask of serene, maternal concern that masked a cold, calculating indifference.

“Mr. Thorne,” she said, her voice soft, honeyed, yet carrying an edge of steel. “My husband is away in Brussels, on matters of great importance. He has entrusted us to your care, but I fear the instruction is too severe. The boys are weary. They are children, not machines to be wound and set ticking.”

I looked up from the open book in my hands. I looked at the two boys, who had ceased their fidgeting to watch this exchange with an alertness that surprised me.

“Madam,” I said, rising to my feet, “severe is the word for truth when it is unpleasant. If I were to teach them only what is pleasant, I would be teaching them nothing. Their father expects progress. I intend to give him progress, or I will tell him exactly why it is not forthcoming.”

Mrs. Van Houten’s eyes narrowed. “You are a young man, Mr. Thorne. You speak with the fire of youth, but you lack the wisdom of experience. My husband is a busy man. He does not wish to hear complaints. He wishes to hear that his sons are becoming men of the world. Please, be more... gentle.”

I felt a surge of heat rise in my cheeks. It was not the heat of shame, but of righteous indignation. I was a bookkeeper, a man who lived by the precision of ledgers and the clarity of accounts. To be told to be "gentle" with the truth was an insult to the very fabric of my being. Truth is not gentle. Truth is a blade. It cuts. And if it cuts, it is because the flesh was rotting.

“I will do my duty, Madam,” I replied, my voice low, dangerous. “I will not lie to Mr. Van Houten. Nor will I lie to these boys. If they wish to remain in the dark, they may do so. But I will not help them to keep the curtains drawn.”

She left then, her heels clicking on the floorboards like the ticking of a clock counting down to a disaster. The silence that followed was heavy, oppressive. I turned back to the boys. They were staring at me, their expressions unreadable. For the first time, I saw not just idleness in their eyes, but fear.

“Open your books,” I said.

They did not move.

“I said, open your books. Now.”

Casper opened his. Willem did not.

I crossed the room. I did not shout. I did not raise my hand. I simply placed my hand on the closed cover of Willem’s book and pressed down, hard. The sound of the spine cracking was sharp, final.

“You will open it,” I said. “And you will read. And if you cannot read, you will spell. And if you cannot spell, you will count the letters. You will do it until your hands bleed, or until your mind clears. I will not stop until you see the light.”

It was a cruel sentence, I knew. It was a sentence born of desperation, of the sheer exhaustion of fighting a battle against the tide of apathy. But as I watched Willem’s face flush, watched the defiance waver into confusion, and then, slowly, into a begrudging compliance, I felt a strange, cold satisfaction settle in my stomach.

That evening, as the rain continued its relentless assault on the house, I sat in my small, cold room above the shop. I took out my pen. I uncapped the ink. I began to write to Mr. Van Houten.
