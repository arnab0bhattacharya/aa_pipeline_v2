The path, a pale scar of dust and loose stone, wound upwards through the scrub. The heat was not a presence but an absence, an exhaustion of the air itself, so that the cicadas’ strident, unending argument seemed to rise not from the thyme and rock-rose but from the very substance of the afternoon. Above them, the summit of Mount Koryphos was a shimmer of white against a sky bleached of all but the most stubborn blue. Clara, lifting her parasol to shield her eyes from the glare that bounced from the limestone, felt the day dissolve into a sequence of small, sharp sensations: the grit of dust on her damp ankles, the prickle of sweat at the nape of her neck, the rhythmic, protesting thud of her own heart.

“It is rather a brute of a climb, isn’t it,” said Alistair, not turning. He had forged ahead, his tweed jacket slung over his arm, a man compelled to conquer, even a mountain of sun-baked rock. His voice was a small, dry puff of sound, quickly absorbed. Beside Clara, Elinor laughed, a short, breathless sound. “He thinks it’s a fell in the Lakes. He’ll be wanting a flask of tea at the top.” But there was a tension in her face, a tightness around the mouth that belied her lightness. She had quarrelled with Alistair at breakfast over a map, a trivial thing about the route, but the map had become a battlefield, its contours the lines of a deeper disagreement that now hung between them, unspoken and heavy as the heat.

Alistair stopped and waited for them, his silhouette sharp against the immense, hazy bowl of the sky. As they drew level, he pointed. “Look. The whole coast.” Below them, the island fell away in folds of grey-green and ochre, and the sea was a sheet of polished metal, so bright it hurt the eyes. The white cubes of the town, Praxos, clung to the shore, and the long, dark fingers of the cypress trees stood sentinel in the valleys. The sight was immense, a panorama that dwarfed their small human efforts, their breathlessness and their bickering. For a moment, they were all silent, held by the sheer scale of it. The silence was a shared thing, a momentary truce. Clara felt the argument between Alistair and Elinor recede, becoming as small and distant as the fishing boats leaving silver threads on the water. It was nothing, really. A map. A word. Here, under this vast, indifferent sky, how could such things matter?

“It’s all very well to say that,” Elinor said at last, her voice thin, as if she had read Clara’s thought and resented it. “But one can’t live on a view.” She turned away, fiddling with the clasp of her watch, and the truce was broken. The air was thick again with all that was unsaid. Alistair’s face hardened. He looked at Clara, not at his wife. “She thinks I drag her to these places to prove a point,” he said, his voice low, a confidence that felt like a betrayal. “She doesn’t understand that there’s more to it than that. There’s… a clearing of the mind.” He looked out at the sea, but Clara knew he was seeing not the water but the dusty confines of his London office. She felt a sudden, sharp pity for him, and an equal, uncomfortable pity for Elinor, who stood a little apart, her shoulders rigid, looking at a single, twisted pine that had forced its way through the rock. The pine was a testament to survival, but it looked, Clara thought, terribly lonely.

They reached the summit, a small plateau of sun-bleached rock. The wind, which had been absent on the climb, was here, a thin, dry whisper that tugged at Clara’s hair and cooled her burning cheeks. The world was spread beneath them, but it was a different world now. The details had been lost in the glare; the town was a white smudge, the fields a coloured quilt. It was a map without names, a land without history. Elinor sat down on a rock, her back to them both, and began to draw in the dust with a twig. Alistair stood at the edge, his hands on his hips, a conqueror surveying his domain. But he looked diminished, somehow, not larger. The silence was no longer a truce but a void, a space where their separate thoughts could echo without meeting. Clara thought of the letters waiting for her at the villa, the ink black on the creamy paper, the words that would pin her down again. Here, on the roof of the island, she felt a terrifying, exhilarating sense of being unmoored.

The descent was always quicker, more treacherous. The stones, which had been obstacles on the way up, now slid underfoot, and the joints, which had merely ached, now screamed in protest. The sun, lower in the sky, cast long, distorted shadows that made the familiar path seem strange and menacing. The light was different, too; it was a softer, golden light that gilded the edges of the rocks and the brittle leaves of the thyme. But it was the light of an ending. Nothing was quite the same. The argument that had seemed so fierce at breakfast was now just a foolish thing, a crack in a teacup. The view, which had promised so much clarity, had offered only a vast, impersonal silence. As they walked, Alistair reached out and took Elinor’s arm to steady her on a steep patch. She did not look at him, but she did not pull away. It was not forgiveness, not resolution, but a kind of acknowledgement, a recognition of the shared difficulty of the path. Clara watched them, her own feet finding their way, and felt that they had all been changed by the ascent, not by what they had seen, but by the simple, shared, exhausting fact of the climb itself. The day had left its mark on them, like a fine layer of dust that would not easily be brushed away.
