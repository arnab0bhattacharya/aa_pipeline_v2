It was a Sunday morning in May, and London lay under a sky of polished pearl, a city scrubbed and sanctified by the Sabbath. For Lord Vivian Ashworth, however, the day presented not a spiritual problem, but an aesthetic catastrophe of the first order. The catastrophe, a matter of inches and of hue, was a single, wilting gardenia.

The flower, a perfect specimen of creamy white, had been the cornerstone of his costume for the wedding of the Duke of St. Albans’s eldest daughter. But a cruel gust of wind in Mayfair, a puff of atmospheric impertinence, had torn it from his lapel and sent it tumbling into the gutter, where it was promptly crushed beneath the wheel of a hansom cab. To be seen without a buttonhole was, for Lord Vivian, a state of undress more scandalous than being caught in his pyjamas. It was to be incomplete, a sentence without its full stop.

“Fergus,” he said, his voice a study in calm despair, as he surveyed the carnage from his drawing-room window. “The gardenia is no more. It has joined the angels, or more likely, the sewer rats.”

His valet, a man whose discretion was matched only by his ability to find a decent claret at an unreasonable hour, offered a consoling thought. “A tragedy, my lord. Perhaps the white rose on the hall table would suffice?”

Lord Vivian turned, a look of profound pity on his face. “Fergus, my dear fellow, a white rose is a statement of innocence. Today, I require a declaration of passion, tempered with a certain worldly cynicism. I require a scarlet carnation.”

It was then that the true horror of the situation revealed itself. It was Sunday. The great temples of floral commerce—Twining’s, Floris, the emporiums of Bond Street and Piccadilly—were sealed tight, their steel shutters down like the eyes of the pious. London, that great marketplace of all things beautiful and unnecessary, had for one day in seven declared itself a desert.

“One must,” Lord Vivian declared, seizing his silver-headed cane, “become a pilgrim.”

His first port of call was the quiet, scholarly street where Mr. Penrose, his own florist, resided. The blinds were drawn. A small, handwritten note on the door read, ‘For Worship and Rest.’ Lord Vivian considered the priorities of the modern world to be sadly misplaced. Next, he tried the fashionable establishments, their windows displaying silent, refrigerated tributes to beauty, locked away behind glass like saints’ relics. The city was a gallery of things he could not have.

His pursuit led him from the grand squares of Belgravia to the tangled lanes of Covent Garden, where the flower market lay in a state of post-Sabbath exhaustion. The air, thick with the ghosts of lilies and the scent of damp earth, was a taunt. A few porters were sweeping up the detritus of Saturday’s trade—bruised petals, broken stems, the sad, discarded finery of the week.

“Carnations, sir?” a man in a grubby apron asked, seeing his fine coat. “All gone, sir. Nothing but rubbish left.”

Lord Vivian looked at the man’s face, a map of early mornings and heavy loads, and felt a sudden, unexpected pang of sympathy. “It appears,” he said, “that the pursuit of beauty is the most exhausting of all trades.”

From Covent Garden, his search became a pilgrimage to the city’s more eccentric fringes. He took a cab to Chelsea, to the studio of his friend, the painter Sebastian Croft, hoping the artist’s hothouse might yield a prize. Croft, a man who believed breakfast was an interruption to the day’s first drink, greeted him at the door in a magnificent silk kimono.

“Vivian! You look positively distraught. Has someone written a bad review of your waistcoat?”

“I am a man undone,” Lord Vivian replied, accepting a glass of sherry. “I have lost my flower. I require a scarlet carnation.”

Croft laughed. “My dear boy, this is a matter for a milliner, not a philosopher. But come, let us see what the glasshouse has produced.”

The glasshouse, however, was a riot of artistic disorder. Vines choked the panes, and the air was heavy with the scent of damp soil and turpentine. There were orchids of impossible, predatory shapes, and tulips the colour of a bruise. But not a single carnation.

“The trouble with carnations,” Croft mused, swirling his sherry, “is that they are too sincere. They lack the delightful decadence of the orchid or the tragic fragility of the lily. They are a flower for grocers and soldiers.”

“Today,” said Lord Vivian, “I would settle for a grocer’s passion.”

His quest now carried him across the river, over the grimy, magnificent span of a bridge, into the labyrinthine streets of Southwark. Here, London was not polished pearl but a palette of brick and soot. The quiet reverence of the West End gave way to the cheerful clamour of life. Children played in the streets, and the air smelled of roast beef and coal smoke. It was in a small, forgotten square, wedged between a public house and a cobbler’s, that he saw it: a tiny shop, its window filled not with fresh blooms, but with wax flowers and funeral wreaths.

On impulse, he pushed open the door. A small bell tinkled mournfully. An old woman with a face like a dried apple looked up from her work, her fingers expertly wiring a wreath of artificial lilies.

“We are closed for the Sabbath, sir,” she said, her voice raspy.

“I can see that,” Lord Vivian replied, his gaze sweeping over the funereal wares. “I am not here for the dead, but for the living. Or, to be more precise, for my own social life. I am in desperate need of a scarlet carnation.”

The woman stopped her work. She looked at him, at his immaculate suit, his polished shoes, his air of worldly ennui, and a slow smile spread across her face. It was a smile of pure, unadulterated commerce.
