The card lay on the silver salver like a pale, accusing hand. It was of the finest cream stock, the kind she favoured, and the address, *Mrs. Eleanor Vance, 11 Belgrave Square*, was written in a looping, confident script she knew as intimately as her own reflection. Because it was her own. The invitation within, for a dinner party on the following Thursday, was penned in the same unmistakable hand, detailing a menu of consommé, turbot, and roast duck, and listing a guest list of London’s most notable and desirable names. Yet, Eleanor Vance had planned no such dinner. Her engagement book for that Thursday was conspicuously, comfortably blank.

For three days, the card haunted her. She held it to the light, scrutinising the ink, the subtle dip of the nib where she had supposedly written ‘Lord Ashworth’. It was a perfect forgery of her own self, an intimate violation that chilled her more than any anonymous threat. Her husband, Arthur, away on business in Edinburgh, dismissed it as a prank. “A jilted suitor, my dear, or one of those dreadful society journalists trying to catch you out. Simply ignore it.” But Eleanor could not. The elegance of the deception was too pointed, too personal. And so, driven by a strange cocktail of dread and a hostess’s unshakeable sense of duty, she gave orders to the cook and the footman. If the ghosts were coming to dinner, she would serve them the finest consommé in London.

Thursday evening arrived with a damp chill that crept through the window frames of the grand Belgravia house. By eight o’clock, a fine drizzle had begun to mist the gaslights, blurring the edges of the square outside. Eleanor stood in the hall, a statue in dove-grey silk, her hand resting on the cool marble of a console table. The house was a picture of perfection. The silver gleamed, the roses in the crystal vases were a deep, velvety crimson, and the scent of beeswax and lilies hung in the air. It was a stage set, and she was waiting for the actors she had never hired.

The first to arrive was Lady Gwendoline, a woman whose gossip was as sharp as the diamonds at her throat. She swept in, shaking rain from her sables. “Eleanor, darling! Such a delightful surprise to receive your invitation. I was certain you were still mourning Arthur’s departure.” Before Eleanor could formulate a reply, Lord Ashworth, a portly man with a magnificent moustache, was announced. He kissed her hand with a flourish. “A triumph, Mrs. Vance! To organise such a gathering at a moment’s notice. You are the jewel of Belgravia.” They all spoke of it as a surprise, a last-minute whim she had conceived. They spoke of *her* invitation. The knot of dread in Eleanor’s stomach tightened.

She was greeting Sir Alistair Finch, a Member of Parliament with a perpetually worried expression, when the final name was called by the butler, a man of such profound composure he was nicknamed ‘Stone’ by the staff. “Mr. Julian Croft.”

The name did not register at first. Then, a face emerged from the shadows of the doorway, and the world tilted on its axis. He was thinner than she remembered, the boyish charm of his youth scoured away by something hard and weary. His evening clothes were impeccable, but they could not hide the new severity in his bearing, or the pale, thin scar that traced a line from his temple to his jaw. Julian Croft. The man who, according to *The Times*, was meant to be rotting in Pentonville for the next decade, his name a byword for financial ruin and social disgrace.

A collective, subtle intake of breath went through the room. Lord Ashworth’s moustache seemed to droop in shock. Lady Gwendoline’s eyes became round, predatory ovals. Julian Croft ignored them all. His gaze found Eleanor’s, and in it she saw not a plea, but a challenge. He smiled, a faint, cold curve of his lips. “Mrs. Vance. How kind of you to invite me. I believe I am the last to arrive.”

Eleanor felt a strange calm descend upon her. The absurdity of the situation had passed through fear and emerged on the other side as a grim, icy resolve. She was the hostess. This was her home. She would not be a pawn in this bizarre game. “Mr. Croft,” she said, her voice steady and clear. “Welcome. We were just about to go in to dinner.”

She took Sir Alistair’s arm, her mind racing. This was not a prank. This was a performance, and she had been cast as the unwitting producer. The guests filed into the dining room, their earlier chatter replaced by a strained, heavy silence. The soup, a clear, fragrant consommé, was served by Stone, his face a mask of impassive duty. Eleanor watched as spoons dipped into the pristine white bowls. Julian Croft sat opposite her, his hands resting on the damask cloth. He did not touch his soup. He simply watched her, his eyes holding a story she could not yet read. It was a story of prison walls and forged letters, of a debt settled and a new one incurred.

The sight of him, so calm and deliberate in her dining room, sent a fresh wave of clarity through her. This was a crime. An act of impersonation and trespass, orchestrated with terrifying precision. The soup was a line in the sand. She would not allow this charade to proceed to the fish course.
