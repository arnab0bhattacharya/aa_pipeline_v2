The silence was the first thing that truly frightened the people of Cinderford. Not the quiet of a Sunday, with its gentle tolling of church bells and the clip-clop of a single carriage, but a profound, dead absence. It was the silence of a heart that had stopped beating. For three weeks, the great ironworks of Gable & Sons had stood cold, its chimneys weeping ghosts of grey smoke instead of the thick, black columns that had for a century marked the city’s progress. The train yard was a cemetery of silent, sweating boilers. The foundries were cavernous and chill. The coal trains, the city’s lifeblood, had stopped coming from the north, and the Midlands were choking on their own cold air.

In the centre of this frozen world sat Silas Croft, a man who had made his fortune from that very black dust. His office, above the wharf, was warm, heated by a generous fire burning the finest anthracite. Outside, the city was a portrait of misery. Women with shawls pulled tight over their faces queued for hours at bakeries for bread that was growing more expensive by the day. Children, their faces pale with the cold, huddled in doorways. The workhouses were overflowing. And all the while, the mountains of coal on Croft’s wharf, vast and black and glistening in the weak winter sun, sat untouched. He had seen the crisis coming, a rumour from a contact in Cardiff, and had bought up every last sack he could find. Now, he was the only man in Cinderford with a fire that didn't cost a shilling to light.

The government man, Mr. Abernathy from the Ministry of Supply, had been the first to call. He was a man of soft hands and a stiff collar, and he smelled of London. He’d sat opposite Silas, his teacup rattling in its saucer, and made his offer. "A patriotic duty, Mr. Croft. The nation is at a standstill. We will purchase your entire stock. We can offer you, let us say, five pounds a ton. A princely sum. You will be a hero."

Silas had stroked his grey moustache, a slow, deliberate motion. "Five pounds is a fine price for common steam coal, Mr. Abernathy. But this," he’d said, gesturing towards the window and the wharf beyond, "this is premium Welsh anthracite. It burns hotter and cleaner. The price is twenty pounds a ton."

Abernathy had paled. "That is… extortionate. It's profiteering!"

"It is business," Silas had replied, his voice as flat and hard as a shovel blade. "The price is what the market will bear. And right now, the market is desperate."

Now, the second visitation was upon him. It did not knock at the door but hammered on it, a sound like the breaking of a ship’s hull. Silas rose from his desk, his heart thumping a nervous rhythm against his ribs. He opened the door to a crowd of men, a dozen or more, their faces grim and smudged with the memory of their trade. At their head was Tom Reilly, a foreman from Gable & Sons, a giant of a man whose hands were like two shovels.

"Croft," Reilly growled, his breath pluming in the cold air of the hallway.

"What is the meaning of this?" Silas demanded, trying to inject authority into his voice. "This is my private property."

"Your property is freezing our bairns to death," a man from the back of the crowd shouted.

Reilly took a step forward, forcing Silas back into the office. "We know what you've got, Croft. We can see it from the bridge. Enough coal to keep this city warm for a month, and you're sitting on it like a dragon on its gold. We've come to ask for what we need. A fair price. Enough to keep the fires going."

"The price is twenty pounds a ton," Silas said, his chin lifted. "For everyone."

A collective, ugly murmur went through the men. Reilly’s face darkened. "My wife is sick with the lung-rot. The doctor says she needs a warm room. You'd let her die for your profit?"

"I am not a charity," Silas said, his voice trembling slightly. "I have a family to provide for. A future to secure."

"We are your neighbours," Reilly said, his voice dropping to a low, dangerous whisper. "We've bought your coal for twenty years. We've made you rich. And now you'd starve us." He took another step, and his huge hand shot out, grabbing the lapels of Silas's wool jacket. "You have one last chance. A fair price. Or we will take what we need, and God help you."

Before Silas could answer, a voice cut through the tension, sharp and clear as a shard of ice. "Let him go, Tom."

Elspeth Croft stood in the doorway to the back rooms, her arms crossed. She was a slight woman, with her father’s sharp features but none of his hardness. Her eyes, the colour of a winter sky, were fixed on Reilly’s hand. She was a teacher at the board school, and a known member of the socialist reading circle that met above the bookbinder’s. To the men of Cinderford, she was a curiosity, a strange mix of her father’s blood and a fire they didn’t understand.

"Elspeth, go to your room," Silas commanded.

She ignored him, her gaze still on Reilly. "Beating him won't get you coal, Tom. It will only get you a prison cell. He’s a stubborn, greedy old man, but he’s still my father."

Reilly, shamefaced, released Silas’s jacket and stepped back. "We're desperate, Miss Croft. You must see that."

"I see it," she said, her voice softening a little. "I see it every day at the school. I see it in the children’s hollow eyes. I know you are desperate. But this is not the way."
