The air in Xylos tasted of ozone and sterilised dust, a sharp, synthetic flavour that clung to the back of my throat. I stood in the centre of the Grand Concourse, a river of polished obsidian that flowed between towers of spun glass and humming metal. The people of Xylos moved around me in a silent, seamless ballet. They were all beautiful, all identical in their perfection. Their skin was smooth and unlined, their hair a uniform shade of chestnut or gold, their bodies lean and strong. They had not passed the age of thirty, and they never would. The city’s great secret, the Chrono-Stasis, had been perfected centuries ago. It was a gift, a blessing, an end to the tyranny of decay.

To them, I was a walking blasphemy. A ghost.

My hair, a stark and shocking white, was the first thing they noticed. In a world of uniform youth, my silver locks were a shock of unnatural colour. Then there was my face, a roadmap of a life they could not comprehend. The deep lines etched around my eyes, the furrows on my brow, the slight sagging of my jaw—these were not signs of wisdom or experience to them. They were deformities. Horrors. Evidence of a disease they had eradicated.

I had been walking for an hour, trying to find the Archive of Histories, a place my ship’s logs had indicated held records of pre-Stasis life. But my journey had been halted. A circle had formed around me, a silent, wide-eyed congregation of the perpetually young. A child, no older than six or seven in appearance, pointed a trembling finger.

“Look, Mother,” the child whispered, the sound unnervingly clear in the hushed air. “The Dry Man. His skin is like old paper.”

The mother pulled the child back, her perfect face a mask of fear and revulsion. She did not look at me, but at my hands, which rested on the gnarled head of my walking stick. My knuckles were swollen with arthritis, my skin a thin, mottled parchment over blue veins. To her, it was the most grotesque thing in the universe.

A man, his face a placid, youthful mask, stepped forward. He wore the silver tunic of a Civic Warden. “You are… a phenomenon,” he said, his voice betraying a sliver of awe beneath his official tone. “An aberration. The systems did not flag your arrival.”

“I am not an aberration,” I said, my voice a low rasp, another sound alien to their smooth, clear tones. “I am a man. My name is Kaelen.”

“Kaelen,” the Warden repeated, as if tasting a strange and unpleasant fruit. “You are afflicted. The Wasting Sickness. We have texts on it, from the Before Times. A terrible plague of entropy.”

“It is not a sickness,” I said, leaning heavily on my stick. The effort of standing was a familiar ache in my bones. “It is time. It is life. It is the process of living.”

I tried to explain. I spoke of the sun, not the calibrated light of Xylos, but the real, wild sun that warmed the skin and left its mark. I spoke of seasons, of the cycle of growth and harvest and dormancy. I described the joy of watching a child grow into an adult, the quiet comfort of holding a hand that had grown wrinkled alongside your own. I spoke of lines on a face not as flaws, but as a history. A testament to laughter, to sorrow, to worry, to love. Each one a story.

They listened with the morbid fascination of a crowd watching a disaster. My words were nonsense. A child’s face, to them, was meant to be smooth. An elder’s hand, meant to be firm and strong. The idea of a body changing, of it recording the passage of years, was as terrifying to them as a sudden, violent death.

“You speak of it as if it has value,” the Warden said, his brow furrowed in genuine confusion. “But you are… broken. You are tired. Your body is failing you. We have ended that. We have perfected the human form. We are eternal.”

“Eternal is not the same as immortal,” I countered, my voice gaining a little strength. “You are frozen. A perfect, beautiful statue in a museum of your own making. You have traded the texture of life for the smoothness of a pebble. You have no stories written on your skin. You have no memories of a first love that faded, of a friendship that deepened over decades, of a loss that carved a canyon in your soul but taught you the shape of your own heart. You have only this moment, repeated forever.”

A murmur went through the crowd. My words were a foreign language, concepts so alien they bordered on the profane. To speak of loss as a teacher? To speak of fading love as a memory? These were glitches in the perfect system, errors to be corrected.

“The Archive,” I said, changing my tack. “I must go to the Archive. There, you will see. You will see the faces of the Before Times. You will see the dignity in age.”

The Warden considered this. He saw me not as a man, but as a problem to be solved, a specimen to be studied. “The Archive is a place of record, not of… this.” He gestured vaguely at my entire being. “But perhaps it is for the best. To see the source of the plague. To understand what we have escaped.”

He turned, and the crowd parted for him. “Follow me, Kaelen of the Wasting.”

He led me through the silent, sterile city. The people we passed stared, then quickly looked away, as if my very presence might somehow contaminate their perfect youth. I felt like a walking wound, a reminder of a past they had violently amputated from their own history.
