Miss Rowan stood in the middle of the great floor where the looms had once rattled and sung. Now the silence of the place was an accusation. The rain, driven by a wind from the west, lashed against the high, broken windows, and through the gaps in the slates the November sky showed a bruised and sullen grey. Her own heart felt much the same: a bruised, aching thing, yet still, she thought, full of a stubborn life.

She had bought the ruin of Ravenscroft Mill with the last of her patrimony, a sum her guardian had called "a lady's foolish fancy" and her suitor, Mr. Thorne of Ashfield Park, had termed "a most unbecoming and desperate venture." He had withdrawn his offer for her hand the very week the deed was signed, his handsome face a mask of pained astonishment. "You cannot mean it, Eleanor," he had said, his voice smooth as polished mahogany. "To cast your lot in with these people? To soil yourself with trade and with their squalid troubles? It is beneath you."

"And what is beneath me, Mr. Thorne?" she had retorted, her own voice hard and clear in the drawing-room of her aunt's house. "Is it beneath me to see a parish full of honest souls with empty bellies and hollow eyes? Is it beneath me to try and put bread in their mouths? If so, then I am content to be soiled."

He had looked at her then, not with anger, but with a kind of cold pity. "You will regret this," he had said. "You are a woman of feeling, not of business. The world will break you." He had bowed, and she had not seen him since. The gentry of the valley, a small, tight circle of squires and clergymen, predicted the same disaster with a relish that was almost cheerful. They spoke of her "unfeminine ambition" and "the ruin that awaits the poor Rowan girl."

Now, in the dripping, echoing shell of her mill, she felt the chill of their prophecy creep into her bones. For the great beam engine, the heart of her enterprise, had seized. A vital connecting rod, forged in some forgotten foundry, had cracked under the strain of the first hard frost. A new one must be cast, and the man from Halifax who could do it spoke of weeks, and of a price that made her stomach clench.

The door opened, letting in a gust of wind and a tall, gaunt man named Silas Croft. He was her foreman, the most skilled of the out-of-work mechanics she had hired, and the most sceptical.

"Well, Miss Rowan," he said, pulling off his cap. His Yorkshire speech was broad and blunt. "It's done for, then. The engine's dead."

"It is not dead, Silas," she replied, forcing a steadiness into her voice. "It is injured. It will be mended."

"Mended? Aye, when? Winter's set in proper now. There's no work, and there'll be no wages. The men are talking. They're saying this was all a wild-goose chase."

The injustice of it stung her more than the cold. "A wild-goose chase? Have I not given them hope? Have I not given them work these three months past?"

"Hope don't fill a belly, Miss. Nor do promises. My own bairns are looking at me with eyes that ask questions I can't answer. They're asking what sort of fool their father is to follow a lass with more money than sense, who's bought a heap of stones and a broken machine."

His words were stones thrown at her soul. She turned away to the window, her hands clasped tight behind her back. Below, the river, swollen and brown, churned through the valley. The wind moaned in the empty rafters like a spirit in torment. She felt a surge of fury—at the machine, at the weather, at the fickle hearts of men. But beneath the fury was a deep and terrible fear. Was Mr. Thorne right? Was she merely a foolish girl playing at Providence?

"I have not been idle, Silas," she said, her back still to him. "I have written to the bank. I have written to the foundry. I will not let these people starve. I will not."

"Words, Miss. Just words," he said, and his voice was not cruel, only tired. "The men won't believe 'em much longer."

He left her then, and the great door boomed shut, leaving her alone with the dripping water and the groaning wind. For a long time she did not move. She felt the weight of all their doubt, of all the valley's scorn, pressing down upon her. She was a single woman against the world, and the world was vast and cold and hard. A sob rose in her throat, but she choked it down. She would not weep. Tears were a currency she could not afford.

Then, a memory came to her, unbidden and clear as a bell. Her father, a plain country parson, reading to her from the Book of Job. *'Behold, I go forward, but he is not there; and backward, but I cannot perceive him.'* And then his own quiet voice, adding, "The Lord tries us to the utmost, my Eleanor. But He does not leave us without a path, however narrow."

The scripture steadied her. It did not offer a solution, but it offered a companion in her trial. She was not alone in this wilderness of doubt. Her grief and her fear were not signs of her failure, but part of the human condition, a fire through which she must pass. She lifted her head. The rain had lessened. A pale, watery gleam of light struck through the broken roof, illuminating the dust motes dancing in the air. It was not sunshine, but it was light. It was a beginning.
