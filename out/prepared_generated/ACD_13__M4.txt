The 9.40 down goods was already twenty minutes late, and the fog which had been threatening all evening had begun to thicken in earnest, when I received the telegram at my lodgings in Croydon. It was from my old friend, Inspector Mallett of the South-Eastern Railway police, and it was characteristically brief: “Come at once to Ashcombe Junction. Matter of a vanishing parcel and a ghost light. Bring your bag.”

I had known Mallett for some years, ever since the affair of the counterfeit tickets at London Bridge, and I had learned to trust his judgment in matters where the ordinary constable would see nothing but a drunken porter or a broken lamp. He was a man of slow speech and slower temper, but his eyes missed nothing, and he had a way of worrying at a problem, as a terrier worries at a rat, until it yielded up its secret.

The fog was so thick by the time I reached Ashcombe that I could scarcely see the gas-lamp above the station door, and the guard who met me at the barrier had to lead me by the sleeve along the platform. Mallett was waiting in the stationmaster’s office, a small, close room smelling of coal-smoke and wet wool, with a fire burning sulkily in the grate. He rose as I entered and shook my hand with a grip that was warmer than his usual manner.

“I am glad you have come, Doctor,” said he. “This is a queer business, and I confess it has got the better of me. Sit you down and hear the facts.”

I sat, and he pushed a tin of tobacco across the table.

“The facts, then, are these. On Tuesday night last, the 9.40 goods from Hatherley Street to Dover was stopped at this junction, as is usual, to take on water and to shunt a couple of trucks of coal for the gasworks. The train consisted of the engine, tender, twelve wagons of general merchandise, and a single passenger carriage—an old four-wheeler, coupled on behind, which is used for the guard when the weather is bad, though on this occasion the guard, a man named Pemberton, chose to ride in the brake-van. The carriage was empty and locked. It had been examined at Hatherley Street by the head porter, and was found to contain nothing but a quantity of sacking and a smell of fish.”

“A smell of fish?” said I.

“It had been used the day before for the conveyance of a consignment of kippers from Yarmouth. That is by the way. The train arrived here at five minutes past eleven, and was shunted into the sidings to allow the 11.15 express to pass. While it was standing there, the signalman in the north box—a man named Wren, who has been in the company’s service for fifteen years and bears an excellent character—observed a light moving in the window of the empty carriage. He describes it as a small, steady flame, like that of a hand-lamp, and it moved from one window to the other, as though someone were walking up and down within. He watched it for perhaps a minute, and then it vanished. He immediately telephoned to the station, and a porter was sent down the line to investigate. The carriage was found to be locked, as before, and empty. There was no lamp in it, no sign of any person having entered, and the sacking lay undisturbed upon the floor.”

“And the bonds?” I asked.

Mallett’s face grew graver.

“Ah, the bonds. That is the kernel of the matter. In the third wagon from the engine there was a sealed box containing fifty thousand pounds in bearer bonds, consigned from the City to the Credit Lyonnais at Paris. The box was of iron, bound with steel hoops, and it was locked with a patent lock, the key of which was carried by the guard. When the train reached Dover, the box was found to be missing. The lock was untouched, the seals were unbroken, and the wagon was otherwise in perfect order. The guard, Pemberton, is an old servant of the company, and I would stake my reputation on his honesty. He swears that he never left his van from the moment the train left Hatherley Street until it arrived at Dover, and that he saw no one approach the wagons.”

“Then the bonds were removed before the train left London?”

“That was my first thought. But the box was seen and its seals examined by the head porter at Hatherley Street not five minutes before the train started, and the wagon was locked and sealed in his presence. The thief, therefore, must have operated between London and Dover, and the only opportunity he could have had was during the five minutes the train stood at this junction.”

“And in those five minutes,” said I, “a light was seen moving in an empty carriage, and a sealed iron box vanished from a locked wagon without leaving a trace.”

“Precisely.”

I sat for a while in silence, watching the fire. The fog pressed against the window like a living thing, and somewhere in the distance I heard the mournful whistle of an engine.

“You have examined the carriage?” I asked at last.

“I have. There is nothing in it but the sacking and the fishy smell. The floor is of plain boards, the seats are of the old-fashioned kind, upholstered in horsehair, and there is a small ventilator in the roof. The windows are of the usual pattern, and they were all fastened on the inside.”

“And the wagon from which the box was taken?”

“It is a covered goods wagon, of the ordinary type, with a sliding door on either side. The door was sealed at Hatherley Street and found intact at Dover. There is no other opening.”

“Then the thief must have entered through the roof.”

“The roof is of wood, covered with tarpaulin, and there is no sign of its having been disturbed.”
