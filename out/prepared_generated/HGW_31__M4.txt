I had been three weeks at Kerguelen’s Rest—a name that flattered the place, for there was neither rest nor welcome in it—when the thing came out of the ice. The station itself was a huddle of tarred sheds and try-works upon a black beach, with a perpetual reek of blubber and the grinding of the flensing-gear, and behind it a wilderness of glacier and moraine that ran back to the mountains and was silent. My own position was a humble one. I was ship’s doctor to the *Petrel*, put ashore with a wrenched knee while she went south for the season’s kill, and I had nothing to do but mend the men’s cuts and watch the ice and think of the warmer latitudes I had left behind me.

The manager of the station was a man named Hasketh, a hard, red-faced colonial with a great capacity for gin and a greater for profit, and it was Hasketh who brought the news to me one evening in the third week of my exile. He came into my little room by the infirmary with his coat still crusted white, and his eyes had the shine of a man who has found money in the street.

“Doctor,” said he, “put on your boots and come with me. There’s a thing on the floe that will make your fortune and mine.”

I asked him what thing, and he would not say, only that I must see it with my own eyes and tell him what the learned men would call it. So I wrapped myself in my oilskins and went out with him along the shore, where the pack-ice had driven in and lay in a grey confusion under the last of the light. Half a mile along, in the lee of a stranded berg, a knot of men stood about something in the ice, and they drew back as we came up, as men draw back from a corpse.

It was a corpse, in a sense, or had been. It lay half-embedded in the floe, and the whalers had cut it clear with their axes without touching it, so that it lay in a kind of ice coffin of their making. It was perhaps twelve feet from the snout to the tail, and it was neither seal nor walrus nor any whale that I had ever seen or heard of. Its body was long and sinuous, with a hide of a curious drab colour, mottled darker along the back, and it had four limbs, or the relics of four, the hinder pair being no more than flippers, while the fore pair ended in something that was not a paw and not a hand, but a hooked and clawed extremity such as I had never seen on any beast. The head was the worst of it. It was not the head of any animal that swims; it had a brow and a muzzle, and the jaw was furnished with teeth that were neither the even saw of the whale nor the fangs of the seal, but a mixed and ugly set, and the eyes—the eyes were closed, but the lids were heavy and folded like a man’s.

“Well?” said Hasketh, at my elbow. “What is it?”

I told him honestly that I did not know. I told him that in my opinion it was a new thing, or an old thing that had outlived its fellows, and that the learned men at the museums would give a great deal to have it. At that his eyes shone the brighter.

“Then it’s worth money,” said he. “It’s worth money, Doctor, and we’ll have it into the shed before the tide turns.”

I was about to protest that the thing should be preserved in the ice and kept whole for the men of science, but Hasketh was already shouting his orders, and the men were laying hold of the coffin and dragging it, with a great scraping and sliding, up the beach towards the station. I followed, and I confess that my scientific curiosity was stronger than my prudence. For I had noticed a thing that Hasketh had not, and that the men, being busy with their hauling, had not either. Where the axe had grazed the creature’s flank, the ice had not run with the clean water of the floe. It had run with a thin, dark trickle, and the trickle had not frozen. The thing was beginning to thaw.

They got it into the long shed by the try-works, and there, under the lamps, we had a better view of it, and a worse. For the thawing had gone further than I had thought. The hide, which had been stiff with frost, was softening, and there was a smell in the shed that was not the smell of blubber, but something older and more disagreeable. I put my hand upon the flank, and it was cold, but it was not the cold of the ice; it was the cold of a thing that has been dead a long while and is not yet done with dying. And as I stood there, I felt, or fancied I felt, a faint and irregular tremor under the hide, such as one feels in the flank of a horse that is standing still and dreaming.

I said nothing to Hasketh of this, but I said that the thing must be packed in salt and sent north at once, and that until then it must be kept cold. He would not hear of the salt—it would spoil the hide, he said, and the hide was the best part of the value—but he agreed to have the windows opened and the door left ajar, for the night was bitter and the shed was on the windward side of the works. Then he went off to his gin, and I went back to my infirmary, and I did not sleep well.
