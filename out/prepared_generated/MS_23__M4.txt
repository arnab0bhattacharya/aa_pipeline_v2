The bell had been cast in the year of our Lord seventeen hundred and ninety-four, and its voice, though cracked by the frosts of more than two centuries, still carried across the empty green with a mournful clarity that pierced the morning mist. Miss Honora Ashby pulled upon the rope with the same steady, deliberate motion her mother had taught her, and her mother before her, watching the rope's fraying hemp rise and fall, rise and fall, until the echoes of the final stroke had faded into the silence of the shire. The sound was, she thought, the only living thing abroad in Wexcombe St. Mary, for the cottages stood with their windows dark and their doors barred against the pestilence, and the lanes were overgrown with nettle and willowherb, and the church itself, whose spire had once summoned the faithful from three parishes round, now kept a silent and a broken watch above the tombs of its last congregation.

Three children awaited her within the schoolroom. They sat upon their benches in the wan light that struggled through the leaded panes, their slates before them, their faces pale and grave as those of little mourners at a funeral, which indeed they were, for each of them had seen the sickness take a mother, a father, a sister, or a brother, and each of them had learned, in the terrible months of that long winter, a silence that belonged to no child. There was Thomas Ledbury, twelve years old, who had buried his father and his elder brother in the same week and now kept his mother's cottage and chopped the wood and walked two miles each morning to learn his letters; there was his sister, Prudence, nine years old, who had not spoken a word since the day of her father's death but who learned her sums with a desperate and silent diligence; and there was little Clara Finch, seven years old, the daughter of the miller, whose parents had fled the village in the first weeks of the sickness and had never returned, and who was kept by the charity of the parish, such as it was, and by the kindness of Miss Ashby, who had taken her into her own small house and fed her from her own diminishing stores.

The lesson that morning had been the parable of the sower, and Miss Ashby had read it aloud in a voice that trembled despite her will, for she could not speak of seed cast upon stony ground and among thorns without thinking of the little flock before her, and of the fields of England lying fallow, and of the harvest that would not come. "But other fell into good ground, and brought forth fruit, some an hundredfold, some sixtyfold, some thirtyfold." She closed the book and looked upon her three scholars, and her heart swelled within her with a grief that she dared not show, for she was all the world to them, and they were all the world to her.

It was while she stood thus, in the midst of that silence which had become the natural element of their lives, that she heard the sound of a motor upon the lane, and going to the window she beheld a black carriage of the government, such as had once been seen in those parts only upon the business of the assizes, drawing up before the schoolhouse gate. A man descended from it, wrapped in a greatcoat against the damp, and stood a moment looking about him at the empty green, the shuttered windows, and the unkempt hedgerows, with an expression which she could not read but which seemed to her to mingle astonishment with a kind of sorrow. He was a tall man, past the middle of life, with grey upon his temples and a countenance worn by much study and by the fatigues of a long and thankless office.

"Miss Ashby?" he inquired, when she had opened the door to him. "I am Mr. Josiah Pemberton, of the Board of Public Instruction. I am come, madam, upon a melancholy errand."

She bade him enter, and he stood within the schoolroom and looked upon the three children, and upon the worn benches, and upon the map of England that hung upon the wall, its corners curling with damp, and his countenance grew yet more grave. "You have my letter?" he said. "The Board has determined that the school at Wexcombe St. Mary must be closed. The population of the parish is reduced to—" he consulted a paper in his hand—"to eleven souls, of whom three are children of school age. The expense of maintaining a schoolmistress and a building for so small a number cannot be justified, madam, in these times. The children may be conveyed to the school at Chipping Norton, where the master still receives a few pupils. I am come to make the necessary arrangements and to offer you the thanks of the Board for your faithful service."

Miss Ashby stood very still, and the children looked up from their slates with wide and frightened eyes, for they understood, in that way that children do, that the black carriage and the grave man in the greatcoat portended some new and terrible loss. "And what," she said at length, and her voice was low but steady, "is to become of the school bell?"

Mr. Pemberton looked at her in some surprise. "The bell, madam? The bell will be taken down and sold, I imagine, with the other effects. It has some value as old metal."

"Sir," said Miss Ashby, and now her voice rose with an earnestness that she could not restrain, "you speak of closing a school, as though it were a shop or a mill that had failed for want of custom. But this school is not a place of trade; it is the last light burning in a house that the darkness has all but swallowed.
