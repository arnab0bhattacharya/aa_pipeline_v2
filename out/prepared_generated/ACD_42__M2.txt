The fog in Liverpool that November was not the thin, maritime mist of the coast, but a dense, yellowish curtain that smothered the streets and turned the gas lamps into diffuse, glowing orbs. It was a fitting atmosphere for the peculiar state of mind in which I found my client, Mr. Arthur Pendelton, a retired tea planter who had spent the better part of three decades in the highlands of Ceylon. He sat in the armchair by the fire, his hands clasped tightly over the back of his knees, his eyes fixed upon the grate with an intensity that suggested he was watching for the flame to speak to him.

"You say, Mr. Pendelton," I began, leaning forward in my chair, "that the man you believed to be dead is not only alive, but has been spotted in this very city within the last forty-eight hours."

"He is alive," Pendelton replied, his voice dry and brittle as autumn leaves. "I have seen the photograph. It is not a ghost, but a man. And yes, he is here. But that is not the matter that brings me to you, Mr. Holmes. No, the matter is the figure in the doorway."

I raised an eyebrow. "A figure in the doorway?"

"A sailor," he said, and for the first time a tremor ran through his composure. "He has been there for three days. I wake at dawn, and he is there, standing beneath the awning of the tea shop. I return from my walks in the afternoon, and he is there, leaning against the iron railing of the gate. He wears a red cap and a heavy blue coat, and he carries a coil of rope over his shoulder. He does not approach. He does not speak. He simply watches. And as I watch him, I feel that I am being watched in return, by something far more terrible than the man himself."

It was a curious account. A retired gentleman, isolated in his quiet home in Aigburth, harassed by a silent observer. It sounded like the work of a debt collector or a scorned suitor, yet the demeanor of my client suggested a fear that went deeper than mere annoyance. He spoke of a partnership, a venture in the tea estates near Kandy, which had ended in a tragedy so profound that he had sworn never to speak of it again. But now, the return of his former partner, Mr. Elias Thorne, had torn the scab off a wound that had long since healed.

Thorne, as Pendelton described him, had been a man of brilliant intellect but volatile temperament. They had made a fortune together, only to fall out over the management of the estate and the treatment of the laborers. The details were hazy in Pendelton’s memory, or perhaps he was deliberately withholding them. He refused to explain how Thorne had disappeared, or why the local authorities had declared him dead after a storm on the hills.

"Why do you believe this sailor is connected to Thorne?" I asked, studying the planter’s face.

"Because Thorne was a man who believed in messages," Pendelton said slowly. "And because the sailor is a Lascar, a sailor from the coast of India. Thorne had a friend, a man of considerable influence in the shipping trade, who was known to employ such men for discreet errands. If Thorne is here, he does not come to make friends. He comes to settle accounts."

I had no time to ponder the implications, for the telephone on the desk began to ring. It was a rare device in those days, and its shrill trill cut through the silence of the room like a knife. Pendelton flinched, his hand flying to his pocket as if expecting a blow.

"Answer it," I said.

"I cannot," he whispered. "It is him. It must be him."

I picked up the receiver. "Mr. Pendelton’s residence."

"Ay, Mr. Holmes," a voice said. It was low, guttural, and accented with the musical cadence of the East. "You are the man who helps the planter?"

"I am the man who answers the phone. Who is this?"

"My name is Iqbal. I am the man in the blue coat. I have watched your client for three days. He is a proud man, but he is afraid. I do not wish to hurt him. I am only a messenger. But the man who sent me says that if the planter does not come to the Pier Head by ten o’clock tomorrow, the message will be delivered in a different manner. A manner that will not be pleasant."

"Who sent you?" I demanded. "Who is this partner of his?"

"The name is Thorne," the voice said, and then the line went dead.

I replaced the receiver, my heart beating with a sudden surge of excitement. There was a pattern here, a logic beneath the apparent chaos. The fear of Pendelton, the silence of the sailor, the cryptic threat. It was not a simple case of blackmail. It was a negotiation, conducted through intermediaries, with a man who was unwilling to show his face.

"Ten o’clock at the Pier Head," I said, turning to Pendelton. "You must go. You cannot hide in your house any longer. The man in the blue coat is not the threat; he is the warning. The threat is the silence."

Pendelton stared at me, his face pale. "You ask me to walk into a trap?"

"I ask you to step into the light," I said. "Thorne has been dead in the eyes of the law for years. If he is alive, he has no protection. He is a fugitive, or a ghost. But he is also a man who wants something. He has sent a warning, not an attack. That suggests he is not a murderer, but a man with a grievance. And grievances can be discussed."
