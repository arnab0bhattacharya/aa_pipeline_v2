It was the custom of Abel Tuckett, the carrier, to set forth from the village of Cottesby at six of the clock on Tuesday and Friday mornings, his wagon laden with such produce as the farms about sent up to the Lutterworth market, and to return by the same road in the evening, when the light was beginning to fail and the hedgerows to look like solid walls of shadow. He was a man of fifty, broad in the shoulders and slow in speech, with a face that seemed to have been weathered into its present state of brown composure by the same winds that had bent the oaks along his route; and he was known, in all the hamlets between Cottesby and the market-town, less as a carrier of goods than as a carrier of news, of messages, of small commissions, and of those confidences which people find it easier to entrust to a man who is going away than to a neighbour who is staying.

The wagon was a kind of moving parliament, wherein every passenger had a voice, and the subjects debated were the price of cheese, the delinquencies of the overseer, the likelihood of rain, and the private histories of families, which were discussed with a freedom that would have astonished the families themselves. Abel sat upon the box, his whip idle in his hand, and listened; and if he sometimes smiled, it was a smile that betrayed nothing. He had learned that a carrier’s business is to carry, and that a man who carries too much talk is like a wagon overloaded on a bad road—he is apt to stick fast, or to break an axle.

Now, among the passengers who occasionally rode with him was a young woman named Ruth Colby, the daughter of a small farmer at the end of the lane beyond the mill. She was a quiet girl, with a pale face and dark eyes that looked as if they were always asking a question which she had not the courage to put into words; and Abel had observed that for some weeks past she had been less ready to talk than formerly, and that when she did speak, it was of indifferent things, and with a little nervous haste, as if she were afraid of a pause. He had known her from a child, and had carried her to market in the days when she sat beside him and asked a hundred questions about the horses; and he felt that sort of paternal interest in her which a man of his calling cannot help feeling for the young people who have grown up under his eye, as it were, upon the road.

It was on a Friday evening in October, when the leaves were falling and the air had that smoky smell which belongs to the season, that Ruth came to the door of the carrier’s cottage, and stood there with a letter in her hand, and a face that was white even in the dusk. Abel had just unharnessed the horses, and was rubbing down the near leader with a wisp of straw, when he heard her step, and turned.

“Why, Ruth,” said he, “what brings you here at this time? Is your father worse?”

“No, he is not worse,” she said, in a low voice. “I came—I came to ask a favour of you, Abel.”

He laid down the straw, and looked at her attentively. She was holding the letter as if it were something she was ashamed of, and her fingers trembled.

“You know,” she said, “that you go to Lutterworth to-morrow.”

“I do,” said Abel. “I go every Saturday, as you know.”

“I want you to take this letter,” she said, “and—and to give it to some one there—not at the post-office—to some one who will be waiting for it—by the church—at the corner of the lane that goes down to the mill—at twelve o’clock.”

Abel was silent for a moment. He looked at the letter, and then at the girl’s face, and then at the letter again.

“Ruth,” said he, “you are asking me to carry a letter in secret.”

“Yes,” she said, “in secret. It must be in secret, Abel. You will not betray me?”

“I am a carrier,” said Abel, slowly. “I carry what I am paid to carry. But I am not a carrier of secrets. There is a difference, Ruth.”

“I will pay you,” she said, eagerly. “I will pay you whatever you ask.”

“It is not the pay,” said Abel. “It is the thing itself. If I carry this letter, I am doing what your father would not like, and what your mother, if she were alive, would not like. I am doing what I have no right to do, for I am not your father, nor your brother, nor your husband. I am only the carrier.”

Ruth’s eyes filled with tears. “I have no one else,” she said. “I cannot trust anyone in the village. They would talk. They would tell my father. And if my father knew—” She stopped, and her voice broke.

Abel was a man who had seen a great deal of the world in his slow way, and he knew that a girl’s secret is a delicate thing, and that to crush it roughly is to crush the girl herself. He was not a man of many words, but he had a conscience that was as tough as his own sinews, and it would not let him do a thing merely because it was easy.

“Who is the letter to?” he asked.

“To—to a young man,” she said, “who is going away. He is going to London, and he will not come back. He has asked me to write to him, and I have written. And if I do not send it, he will think I have forgotten him, and he will go away unhappy. And I shall never see him again.”
