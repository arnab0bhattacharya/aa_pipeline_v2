It is a fact that there are two sorts of men in this world: those who look up at the sky and see only the weather, and those who see a vacant territory awaiting conquest. I was, and I suppose I remain, of the latter sort. But I must begin my account not with the sky, but with the earth—specifically, the damp, musty earth of my father’s barn at Bishop’s Down, which smelled of generations of hay and, latterly, of the acrid, modern perfume of dope and castor oil.

It was there, in the spring of 1903, that I completed my apparatus. I called it the *Albatross*, though it bore no more resemblance to that bird than a bicycle does to a horse. It was a triplane of my own design, driven by a converted motor-car engine and steered by a system of warping wings that I had calculated would give it a stability far beyond the flimsy kites the Wrights were tinkering with in America. When the engine was started, the whole structure quivered with a tremendous, suppressed energy, like a leashed hound. The noise of it—a staccato, rhythmic coughing—could be heard across the entire village, and it was this noise that first drew the attention of my neighbours.

At first, they came to gawk. The children of the village would line the fence of the farmyard, their faces pressed to the slats, watching me oil the great spars or adjust the wire bracing. The men of the village, a practical and unimaginative lot concerned chiefly with the price of barley and the health of their sheep, came to shake their heads. "Young Ashton's at it again," they would say, as if I were engaged in some slightly eccentric but ultimately harmless hobby, like collecting beetles.

But the moment I actually took to the air, the village’s mood shifted from curiosity to a kind of superstitious dread. My first flight was a short, bumpy affair, scarcely clearing the tops of the elms before I brought her down again in a nearby pasture. But it was enough. The sight of a man-made machine, roaring and snorting and rising against the will of gravity, was a profound shock to the settled order of things.

The first to articulate this shock was the Reverend Mr. Croft. He was a man of the old school, who saw in every new invention a subtle attack on the divine plan. He preached a sermon on the text, "Their line is gone out through all the earth," and twisted its meaning to suggest that my flying machine was an instrument of pride, a modern Tower of Babel. "We are not meant to flit about like the sparrows, my brethren," he thundered, his face flushed with righteous indignation. "We are creatures of the earth, bound by our duties and our station. To seek to abandon these things is to invite a terrible judgment!" I attended the service, and I must confess I found his performance more amusing than alarming. He saw my engine as the work of the Devil; I saw it as a triumph of the internal combustion principle.

A more pragmatic, if equally unwelcome, response came from Squire Hardcastle. He was a man whose family had owned the best land in the parish since the Conquest, and he viewed the world as a collection of assets to be acquired. He came to the barn not with a sermon, but with a chequebook. "A remarkable toy, young Ashton," he boomed, clapping me on the shoulder with a hand accustomed to the firm grip of a hunting crop. "A novelty, eh? The county set will pay a fortune for rides in this… this kite of yours. I'll buy the whole concern from you. Name your price."

I explained to him, as patiently as I could, that the *Albatross* was not a toy, but a prototype; that its purpose was not to ferry the idle rich about for amusement, but to demonstrate a principle. He looked at me with the blank incomprehension of a man who has never understood that some things are not for sale. He could not grasp that the value was in the doing, not the owning.

Throughout all this, the one person who seemed to understand was Eleanor Vance. She was the daughter of the schoolmaster, a girl with a sharp mind and eyes the colour of a winter sky, who read books that were not on the recommended list and was not afraid to ask questions no one else thought of. I had admired her from afar for a year, and the thought of her watching me from the farmyard fence had been a powerful, if unacknowledged, motive in many a late night spent over blueprints.

It was she who saw the true meaning of the machine. While the vicar saw sin and the squire saw a commodity, Eleanor saw freedom. She came to me one afternoon as I was making final adjustments to the carburettor. "They don't understand, do they?" she said, her voice quiet but clear. "They think you're just making a loud noise and frightening the horses. They can't see that you're making a new kind of silence, up there above the wind."

I was so taken aback I could barely stammer a reply. Before I could, she fixed me with her steady gaze. "When will it be ready to carry a passenger?"

My heart gave a most unscientific lurch. "It is ready now," I heard myself say. "The controls are sensitive, but I am confident of its stability."

"Good," she said, with a decisive little nod. "Then I shall be the first."

I must confess that a wave of sheer terror washed over me. The thought of this girl, this perceptive and remarkable creature, strapped into my fragile collection of wood and wire, subjecting herself to the risks I took as a matter of course, was almost more than I could bear. The vicar's talk of judgment suddenly seemed less absurd. But to have refused her would have been an insult. It would have meant I saw her not as a partner in discovery, but as a fragile ornament to be protected. And Eleanor Vance was no ornament.
