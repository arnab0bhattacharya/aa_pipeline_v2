The light at the window had thickened, slowed, and turned the colour of weak tea; it lay in long, quivering lozenges across the oak floor of the library at Bretby Hall, illuminating the dust that lifted, drifted, and settled again with a motion so idle it seemed the very breath of the old house. Helena stood on the steps of the rolling ladder, one hand resting on the leather spine of a calf-bound Burke, and looked down the long avenue of shelves. The books, in their faded ranks, appeared to her not so much objects as sleeping creatures, coated in the fine, grey ash of decades. She had been sent down from London to catalogue them—a task, the new owner had said over the telephone, with that odd, detached voice that seemed to reach her from a vast, windswept moor of money, that would take perhaps a fortnight. But Helena, brushing her thumb across the foxed edges of a 1798 edition of Cowper, knew that a fortnight was nothing. Time, in this room, had ceased to be a matter of days or hours. It had become a substance, a sediment, laid down leaf by leaf, page by page, in the dark.

She opened the Cowper. The spine cracked with a sound like a small bone breaking, and a smell rose up—must, dried leather, and something else, something sweetly decaying, like pressed violets left too long between paper. And there, in the margin beside a poem about a country churchyard, was a small, precise annotation in pencil. *The thrushes build in the ivy now. Mother says it is unlucky, but I have seen four blue eggs.* Helena read it twice. The handwriting was a girl’s, round and slanting, the letters pressed deep into the soft paper as though the writer had leaned upon them with the whole weight of a small, eager body. Below it, in a different hand—one that was angular, hurried, the loops pulled thin as if the pen had been snatched along by impatience—someone had written: *Nonsense. Thrushes are bold creatures. They fear nothing.*

Helena held the book closer to the window. The two hands, the round and the angular, seemed to her to be speaking not about thrushes at all but about something else, something that pressed and trembled beneath the words and could not be said outright. She felt, with a sudden, involuntary tightening of the throat, that she had interrupted a conversation which had been going on quietly for a hundred years and which would go on long after she had packed her notebooks and caught the train back to King’s Cross. She set the Cowper aside, gently, as one sets down a cup that is too full.

The afternoon wore on. The lozenges of light shifted, narrowed, and climbed the opposite wall, and Helena moved along the shelves, pulling volumes from their places with the slow, absorbed deliberation of a swimmer moving through deep water. Each book was a small, sealed chamber, and within each chamber a life had left its trace. Here, in a history of the Peninsular War, the pages were marked with scraps of paper, and on the scraps, in a hand that shook—whether from cold or age or some inward trembling she could not tell—were lists: *Blankets. Linen for bandages. Write to Margaret. God preserve us.* The handwriting was a soldier’s; she thought so because of the way the capital letters were formed, upright and military, each one standing to attention. But between the lists, in the white spaces, the same hand had written fragments of verse—unrhymed, broken, seeming to Helena to hang in the air like smoke: *the river runs red / and the olives are black / and I have forgotten the sound / of my own name.* She read it and felt the hair rise on her forearms. Who had written this? Some Bretby, some younger son perhaps, sent out to Spain with a commission and a case of books, who had sat in his tent and tried to make words out of the thing he saw, and failed, and left these fragments here, in the house, for her to find.

She turned a page and found, laid flat between the leaves, a pressed flower, so thin and brittle it was scarcely there. A crocus, she thought, from the yellow ghost of its throat. And beside it, in yet a third hand—this one large, loose, generous, the handwriting of a woman accustomed to writing letters and making lists—a single line: *Eleanor’s first flower, April 3rd, 1841. She is well today.*

Helena closed the book and held it against her chest. The room was very quiet now. The light had gone the colour of ash, and the shelves had retreated into shadow. She could hear, far away, the sound of a clock striking five, and then, closer, the sound of footsteps on the gravel outside the window—crisp, purposeful footsteps that stopped and then, after a pause, turned and went back the way they had come. She did not need to look to know whose they were. Mr. Ashby, the new owner. He had come down from London for the weekend, and he walked the grounds of his inherited house with the brisk, slightly bewildered air of a man who has acquired a landscape he does not know how to inhabit. She had met him once, on the first morning, when he had shown her the library and stood in the doorway with his hands in his pockets, looking at the shelves with an expression that was not quite dismay and not quite indifference but something between the two—the expression, she thought, of a man confronted with an embarrassment of things that meant nothing to him.

“The books are rather mouldy, I’m afraid,” he had said. “My grandfather never threw anything away. Do what you can with them. I shall be in the study if you need anything.”
