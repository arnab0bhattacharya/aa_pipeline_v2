Mrs. Arabella Thorold, widow of the late respected surgeon of Whitbridge, was a woman whose mental constitution demanded that her observances should justify themselves by some appeal to the general fitness of things. She had been left, by the provident care of her husband, in the enjoyment of a clear three hundred a year—a competence which, in the Midlands town of Whitbridge during the autumn of 1837, permitted a genteel economy without the vulgarity of positive privation. Her house in the High Street, a tall, narrow-bricked edifice with a handsome mahogany door and a knocker of exceptional prominence, was a monument to that precise and unimpassioned orderliness which, in the absence of strong joys, constitutes for many worthy persons the chief bulwark against despair. It was, therefore, with a considerable trepidation of spirit that she had admitted, as a lodger, a foreign gentleman of whose antecedents she knew nothing beyond his own brief and slightly foreign statement that his name was Monsieur Laval, that he was a native of Brussels, and that his commercial affairs required a temporary residence in the neighborhood of Whitbridge.

The commercial affairs of Monsieur Laval were, however, of a nature so profoundly nocturnal as to awaken in the mind of Mrs. Thorold’s single servant, Bessy, a suspicion that the gentleman’s trade was conducted chiefly with the powers of darkness. He occupied the two best rooms on the second floor, paid his rent with a punctuality that was almost aggressive in its disregard of the customary fortnightly delays, and kept hours that seemed to have no relation to the solar economy of Whitbridge. He was seen abroad at dawn, when respectable householders were merely turning in their beds; he was invisible throughout the entire diurnal stretch, when the sun lit up the gray stone of the parish church and the market carts jostled in the square below; and his candles, Bessy reported with a bated breath that savored of the marvellous, were frequently burning at two o’clock in the morning.

To a mind constituted like Mrs. Thorold’s—a mind in which the strictly provincial love of known biographies was curiously blended with a fatalistic submission to the pressure of monetary circumstance—this irregularity was a source of profound uneasiness. But the rent was high, and the alternative had been the letting of the rooms to a family from Birmingham-ward, whose children and all. So she stifled the misgivings that naturally sprang from an unknown tongue and a locked portmanteau, reflecting that Providence sometimes sends its blessings in strange grammatical cases.

But the social economy of a provincial town is an engine of exquisite sensitiveness; it registers the slightest variation in the routine of its recognized units, and it translates that variation into a moral hypothesis with an alacrity that puts the inductive sciences to shame. It was not long before the whispering gallery of Whitbridge had taken hold of Monsieur Laval. At the shop of Mr. Goby, the draper—a man whose intellect moved in a perpetual atmosphere of surmise, and who found in theFailures of his neighbors a rich compensation for the monotony of measuring cambric—the foreign gentleman’s nocturnal habits were subjected to a rigorous interpretation. Mr. Goby was of the opinion, freely communicated over the counter to any lady of mature years who did not cut him short, that the stranger was a Jesuit in disguise, sent to reconnoiter the Protestant Midlands for a Papal invasion. Mrs. Patten, the rector’s wife, thought it more probable that he was an exiled French aristocrat, a supposition which had the merit of romantic charity but lacked the robust evidence of probability. The lower orders, represented chiefly by the grooms and stable-boys who congregated at the Dragon Inn, held a simpler view: they believed him to be a smuggler, or, at the very least, a horse-thief of continental cunning.

But of all the minds upon which the presence of Monsieur Laval cast its disturbing shadow, none was more curiously affected than that of Mrs. Thorold’s daughter, Letitia. Letitia was twenty-three, and she possessed that most dangerous of all intellectual endowments in a young woman of narrow means and narrower opportunities—a mind conscious of its own emptiness, and athirst for the dramatic. Her days were passed in a round of small domestic duties, in the practice of a not very brilliant pianoforte, and in the perusal of circulating-library novels, which, while they nourished her imagination, gave her no clue to the conduct of a life so essentially devoid of incident as hers. She was a girl of dark, heavy features, with a complexion that spoke of suppressed energy, and a pair of gray eyes that had a disquieting habit of fixing themselves upon objects as if they were attempting to penetrate their molecular constitution.

To Letitia, the lodger was not merely a source of social scandal; he was an event, a phenomenon, a problem presented to her faculties for solution. And the problem began to exercise that fascination which the unknown always possesses for the inactive. She found herself, in the long blank afternoons, listening for his footfall on the stairs; she invented pretexts for passing through the hall when his door was shut, in the hope of catching some sound that would reveal the nature of his occupations. She observed, with a minuteness that would have done credit to a naturalist, that his boots were always polished to a mirror-like brilliance, that his greatcoat was of a fashion unknown in Whitbridge, and that he carried a cane with a head of carved ivory which she suspected to be of Oriental workmanship.

But it was one thing to observe these exterior facts, and another to penetrate the secret of that locked room, of those nocturnal absences, of that persistent silence concerning his past. Letitia’s curiosity, at first a mere fluttering of the imagination, began to take the form of a purpose. She reasoned with herself—and the reasoning was not without a certain logical force—that her mother had taken the stranger in on trust, and that it was the duty of someone in the household to verify the trust.
