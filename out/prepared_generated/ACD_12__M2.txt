The letter arrived at my lodgings in Baker Street on a Tuesday morning, smelling faintly of tobacco and old parchment. It was from Mr. Arthur P. Halloway, the secretary of the Strand Chess and Whist Club, a gentleman of unimpeachable character and considerable nervousness.

"I am instructed by the Committee," wrote Mr. Halloway, "to request your presence at our weekly tournament on Friday. We are faced with a situation of the most baffling and disquieting nature. Our member, Mr. Silas Vane, has won every game for the past four weeks. Not merely won, sir, but with a style of play that defies all known theory. More alarming still, three of his opponents have suffered sudden and severe collapses following their defeat. The last of these, Mr. J. B. Thorne, fell senseless at the board after drinking his tea and has not yet regained his wits. The Committee is divided. Some believe it is a matter of cheating; others whisper of poison. We desire you to observe Mr. Vane and, if possible, ascertain the nature of the threat he poses to the health of our members."

I showed the letter to Holmes, who was seated by the fire, a violin tucked loosely under his arm. He read it with that peculiar, quick glance that missed nothing, then tossed it onto the heap of discarded newspapers.

"A chess player who wins by moves no one can predict, and his opponents collapse after drinking tea," he mused, striking a match. "It sounds like a trivial domestic squabble, Watson, unless one looks beneath the surface. But why should a man who can beat the board need to harm the man across it? Unless the board is not the only arena. When do you go?"

"Friday," I said.

"Then I shall come with you. I have a notion that Mr. Vane’s victories are not merely intellectual, but chemical. And I have a fancy that his tea is not the only vector."

The Strand Chess and Whist Club was a sombre establishment, lined with dark oak and the smell of stale pipe tobacco. It was a place where men of moderate talent and large egos went to feel superior. I was introduced to the committee members, a group of stout, flushed men who looked upon us with a mixture of suspicion and desperate hope.

"Mr. Vane is expected any moment," said Halloway, wringing his hands. "He is a man of very few words, Dr. Watson. He plays fast, and he plays hard. When he wins, he smiles. But it is not a pleasant smile."

We took our seats in the gallery, from which the boards were clearly visible. The room was dim, lit by gas jets that flickered uncertainly in the draught from the street. The players were a collection of white-haired veterans and eager young men, all hunched over their boards with the intensity of men defusing a bomb.

Mr. Silas Vane entered quietly. He was a tall, thin man, with a face that seemed to have been carved from pale marble, all sharp angles and cold, bright eyes. He wore a high collar and a dark coat, and he moved with the silent precision of a predator. He took his seat at the board opposite Mr. Thorne, the man who had collapsed the previous week, though Thorne was not present. Today, Vane’s opponent was a young solicitor named Mr. Fenn, who was visibly trembling.

"Observe him," Holmes whispered, leaning forward. "Note his hands. Note his eyes. And above all, note what he does when he moves a piece."

The game began. I had played chess myself, but I was no master. I saw only a shuffle of pawns and knights. Holmes, however, watched Vane with the intensity of a surgeon watching a heart.

Vane moved quickly. He did not tap his board, nor did he lean back in contemplation. He simply looked at the board, moved a piece, and sat still. His opponent, Fenn, sweated profusely. He moved slowly, agonizing over every step.

"Look at the tea," Holmes said softly. "The boy is drinking. Watch the cup."

I looked. Fenn sipped from a small, white cup. It was a modest cup, of plain porcelain. But as he drank, I noticed a fine, almost invisible residue on the rim. It was not tea, or at least, not entirely tea. It had a slightly iridescent quality in the gaslight.

Vane moved a knight. Fenn gasped. He moved a bishop. Vane’s lips curved into that thin, cold smile.

"He is winning," I said. "But how?"

"He is not winning by strategy, Watson. He is winning by exhaustion. The tea is a stimulant, or perhaps a depressant. It is altering the player's perception. Look at Fenn’s eyes. They are glazed. He is not thinking; he is reacting. Vane is playing against a man who is half-asleep, or half-mad. It is the easiest thing in the world to predict the moves of a man whose mind is clouded."

Just then, Fenn made a blunder. A catastrophic one. He moved his queen directly into the path of a bishop. Vane did not hesitate. He took the queen.

Fenn stared at the board. His face turned a shade of grey. He reached for his tea, his hand shaking violently. He raised the cup to his lips.

"Stop him!" Holmes cried.

But it was too late. Fenn drank. He set the cup down and looked at Vane. "I resign," he said, his voice slurred. "I cannot see the board. It is spinning."

Before Vane could speak, Fenn swayed. He clutched the edge of the table, his knuckles white. A low moan rose in his throat. He collapsed forward, his head striking the wooden surface with a dull thud. He did not move.

The room erupted in chaos. Men shouted, men ran. Halloway rushed down the stairs, his face a mask of horror.

"Get a doctor!" he screamed. "Quickly!"

Holmes stood up, his face pale but his eyes sharp. "Do not let him leave," he said, his voice cutting through the noise. "Secure the door."
