It is a curious thing to observe the profound suspicion the ordinary Englishman harbours toward anyone who attempts a systematic study of the weather. My name is Arthur Pendel, and I reside in the Surrey village of Chilworth Green. I am not a man of independent means; I keep the local chemist’s shop. But my true passion, sustained at considerable expense and inconvenience, is amateur meteorology. I had fitted up my back garden with a barometer of my own design, an anemometer, and a series of thermometers that recorded the diurnal range with what I believed to be a very fair degree of accuracy. For three years I had observed them without attracting anything more than mild ridicule. Then came the great storm of October 1894.

It was on the Tuesday that I first noticed the extraordinary and unprecedented drop in the barometric pressure. It was falling, not gradually as it does before a common rainstorm, but with a steady, headlong plummet that I had never seen recorded in any almanac. My anemometer, meanwhile, registered a strange, oppressive stillness in the upper air, a sort of sultry hush that made the very leaves on the elms hang limp. I did not know it then, but we were about to experience a cyclonic vortex of a sort exceedingly rare in the English climate. I calculated the trajectory of the low-pressure system, and the mathematics, so far as my humble instruments could provide them, pointed to a direct and devastating impact upon Chilworth Green.

Filled with that earnest, argumentative zeal which afflicts the amateur scientist, I went directly to the parish council. They were convened in the vestry room of St. Agnes’s, debating the purchase of a new watering-trough for the village green. I interrupted the chairman, a Major Bellingham, to state my case. I told them, plainly and with a good deal of energy, that a storm of immense magnitude was approaching, that the wind would be of a velocity to strip roofs, and that they must secure their properties and seek substantial shelter immediately.

They received my announcement with that infuriating, heavy-lidded patience reserved for the mildly insane. Major Bellingham, a man of red complexion and absolute intellectual inertia, puffed his cheeks and laughed. "My dear Pendel," he said, "your glass tube has gone to your head. The sky is as mild as a May morning. We have a trough to discuss, and I won't have the council's time wasted by your hobby-horses."

The others murmured their agreement. The vicar suggested I take a holiday by the sea, and Mr. Griggs, the grocer, made a coarse jest about my barometer being stuck in a drainpipe. I left the vestry in a fury, swallowing that bitter pill of middle-class impotence, and returned home to make my own preparations.

I had, some years prior, bricked up the old coal cellar beneath my shop, reinforcing the heavy oak door with iron bands and lining the walls with mortar. It was a cool, dry place where I kept my more volatile chemicals. I judged it the only structure in Chilworth Green capable of withstanding the atmospheric violence I knew was coming. I moved my provisions—tinned meats, water, and a spirit lamp—down into the dark, and I waited.

The storm broke at half-past four. It did not arrive as an ordinary storm arrives, with a splattering of rain and a rising wind. It came with a sudden, awful greenish light in the western sky, followed by a roaring sound that I can only compare to the approach of a great number of railway trains. Then the wind struck.

It was a monstrous, invisible weight. I heard the slates of my roof leap from their fastenings like playing cards scattered by a giant, and a moment later the shop window above me shattered with an explosive crash. The sound of Chilworth Green being torn apart was indescribable. There was the shrieking of wind through a thousand new cracks, the splintering of timber, and above it all, a deep, guttural roar that seemed to press upon the very earth itself. I sat in my cellar, gripping the edge of my table, my lamp guttering in the draughts that seeped under the door, feeling a strange exultation mixed with terror. My science had been correct; my instruments had not lied. I had predicted the great event, and the council had been fools.

For an hour and a half the tempest raged. Then, about six o'clock, I heard a new sound above the howling of the wind. It was the clatter of iron upon iron, and a frantic, human screaming.

I took my lamp and went to the foot of the cellar steps. Someone was beating upon the outer hatch of my reinforced door, which opened onto the alley beside the chemist’s. I drew back the heavy bolts. The wind tore the door from my hand, and a figure came tumbling down the steps in a wet, shivering heap. It was Major Bellingham. His coat was torn to ribbons, and his face was bleeding from a cut on his forehead.

"The church!" he gasped, wiping the blood and rain from his eyes. "The steeple is down! Griggs is buried! For God's sake, Pendel, let us in!"

I stood at the foot of the steps, the lamp in my hand, looking up at him. I felt a sudden, intense desire to shut the door in his face. I wanted to remind him of the watering-trough, of his heavy-lidded patience, of his contempt for my science. I wanted to tell him that my glass tube had gone to my head, and that he had best seek shelter elsewhere. It was a primitive, vindictive impulse, and I confess it took me a moment to master it. The man was terrified, and his terror was a very ugly thing to see.
