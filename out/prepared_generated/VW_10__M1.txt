There was the light on the water, and it was always that first: a shivering, a trembling of silver coins cast and recast upon the grey-green swell of the sea. She was seven, and the world was the size of the cove at Porthgenna, hemmed in by granite cliffs that smelled of damp and gorse. Her father’s hand was a warm, rough weight in hers. The roar of the surf was a constant presence, a great beast breathing in its sleep, and the cry of gulls was the thread that stitched the sky to the land. She had a small bucket, red as a berry, and a spade with a wooden handle worn smooth by her own small grip. The moment was whole, entire. It was not a memory of a summer; it was the summer itself, the sun on the back of her neck, the grit of sand between her toes, the cold shock of the water as a wave ran up the beach to lick at her ankles. She would hold a shell to her ear, and the roar of the sea would be inside it, smaller and more secret. That was all. That was everything.

Years fell away like petals from a rose, and the light was different now, filtered through the lime trees in the garden at Highwood. It dappled the lawn where Edmund stood, his hands clasped behind his back, his shadow long and thin beside the sundial. The air was thick with the scent of roses and cut grass, and the sound of a distant lawnmower was a sleepy, mechanical hum. He was speaking, but the words came to her in pieces, detached from their meaning. “…a partnership… mutual esteem… the future…” His face was earnest, his brow slightly furrowed, as if he were reading a difficult passage aloud. She looked at the play of light on the silver head of his cane, leaning against the stone rim of the fountain. It seemed more real than his proposal. She felt a strange detachment, as if she were watching a scene from a play in which she had been given the wrong part. She was to say yes; she knew that. The whole garden, the house, the quiet certainty of the afternoon, seemed to expect it of her. “Yes,” she said, and the word was a small, flat stone dropped into the deep well of the afternoon. He smiled, a release of tension in his face, and reached for her hand. His touch was dry and warm. She looked past him, at the bees drunkenly circling the lavender, and wondered if this was the beginning, or the end of something she had never known.

The light was gone. It had been replaced by a low, yellowish glare from a lamp in the corner of the room, a light that made the shadows long and the air feel close. The smell was of carbolic soap and boiled linen. Her husband’s face, on the white pillow, was a landscape of ruin she no longer recognized. The strong jaw was slack, the eyes, once so full of quiet inquiry, were closed. All that was left was the sound of his breathing, a shallow, rattling thing that seemed to tear at the quiet of the room with each effort. It was an intrusion. She sat in the hard-backed chair, her hands folded in her lap, and watched the small, grey world of the nursing home shrink to this one point: the rise and fall of the sheet. She tried to summon the garden at Highwood, the boy on the beach at Porthgenna, but they were flimsy things, painted backdrops against this stark and present reality. There was only the ticking of a clock on the mantelpiece, each second a small, hard bead of time falling into a void. She felt a vast, hollow space opening inside her, not of grief, not yet, but of absence. The man who had shared her life was disappearing with each shallow breath, and she was left behind, a witness to the unraveling.

And then, another light, the clean, sharp light of a Tuesday morning in a London park. A small, hot hand was thrust into hers, sticky with the residue of a sweet. “Again, Nana, again!” cried a voice, and she looked down at the bright, impatient face of her grandson, Leo. He was a creature of pure energy, all forward momentum and bright questions. He pointed a chubby finger at a pigeon. “Is it sad?” he asked. She laughed, a sound that felt rusty and unfamiliar in her own throat. “I don’t think so, my dear. I think it’s looking for crumbs.” They walked on, under the plane trees whose leaves were just beginning to turn. The life of the park swirled around them: the distant shout of children, the clip-clop of a horse-drawn cab, the scent of roasting chestnuts from a vendor’s cart. She looked at the boy, at the complete and utter absorption with which he watched a squirrel dart up a tree, and felt a sudden, piercing clarity. It was all here. The boy on the beach was Edmund, was Leo, was every child who had ever felt the cold shock of the sea. The proposal in the garden was the quiet turning of a key in a lock. The death in the nursing home was the closing of a door. They were not a story. They were not a chain of cause and effect. They were just moments, held up to the light like sea-glass, worn smooth and strange by the tumbling of the years. They did not make a life. They were a life. She squeezed the small, sticky hand in hers. The sun was warm on her face. It was enough. It was everything.
