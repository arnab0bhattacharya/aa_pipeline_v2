Silas Vane had always been a man of impeccable taste and abysmal patience, two qualities that seemed to exist in his constitution in direct opposition to one another. He possessed a soul that craved the eternal, the symmetrical, and the gold-leafed, yet he lived in a world that offered him only the temporary, the lopsided, and the damp. It was a peculiar tragedy, really, for a man who had built his life upon the worship of Beauty to find that his most ardent devotions were directed toward a woman who possessed none of the conventional attributes of the goddess he had so long invoked. Her name was Elara, and she was, by the strictest and most unforgiving standards of society, plain. Not merely unremarkable, but actively resistant to the eye’s desire for harmony; her nose had a certain obstinate geometry, her skin held the pallor of old parchment, and her hair, a dark and unruly thing, refused to be coaxed into the fashionable curls that adorned the heads of the young wives of the county. Yet, when Silas looked at her, he did not see a failure of nature, but a triumph of the spirit over the mere animal. He saw a mind of such crystalline sharpness that it cut through the velvet fog of polite conversation like a diamond through glass.

Their meetings took place at Ashcombe Hall, a sprawling country house of stone and ivy that smelled perpetually of damp wool and old books. It was there, in the library with its dust motes dancing in the shafts of afternoon light, that Silas first discovered the true nature of his infatuation. It was not the body that attracted him, for the body is merely the vehicle, often a rickety one, for the soul’s journey. It was the mind. Elara spoke with a cadence that was musical not because of its melody, but because of its precision. She had a way of taking a commonplaceness—a remark about the weather, a comment on the price of silk, a gossip of the local squire—and inverting it until it revealed the absurdity at the heart of the thing.

One afternoon, as the rain lashed against the leaded windows, turning the garden into a grey blur of misery, Silas found himself trapped in a conversation with her about the nature of boredom. It was a subject she treated with the gravity of a theologian discussing sin.

“Boredom,” she said, her voice low and steady, turning a page of a book she was not reading, “is the only true state of existence. Everything else—love, hate, joy, grief—is merely a reaction to the cessation of boredom. We do not love because we are happy; we love because we are bored with being alone. We do not hate because we are righteous; we hate because we are bored with being indifferent.”

Silas smiled, a rare and genuine expression that softened the sharp angles of his face. “You are dangerous, Elara. You dismantle the furniture of life while I am still sitting in it.”

“I am merely a carpenter,” she replied, her eyes, which were a startlingly clear grey, meeting his. “And you, Silas, are a man who has been sitting in the same chair for so long that he has grown into the woodwork. You mistake your habits for your character.”

It was a stinging remark, delivered with the casualness of a passing bird, yet it landed with the force of a hammer. Silas felt a flush of heat rise to his neck, not of anger, but of a strange, electric delight. In London, in the drawing rooms of Mayfair where the air was thick with the scent of perfume and the sound of lies, conversation was a game of mirrors, reflecting nothing but the vanity of the speaker. One spoke to be heard, to be admired, to be pitied. Here, in the shadowed quiet of Ashcombe, one spoke to be understood, which was a far more terrifying and exhilarating risk.

His friends, however, were not so charitable in their assessment. Lord Henry Ashworth, a man who wore his cynicism like a well-tailored suit, visited Silas in London shortly after the summer solstice. They sat in Silas’s club, surrounded by the usual array of mahogany and brass, drinking brandy that was old enough to have opinions.

“You have gone native, Silas,” Lord Henry said, swirling the amber liquid in his glass. “I saw you at the garden party. You were standing by the side of the pond, ignoring the Duchess of Frobisher, who was wearing a dress that cost more than your annual income, and talking to that... woman. What was her name? The one with the face of a dried apple and the mind of a lawyer?”

“Elara,” Silas corrected gently.

“Elara,” Lord Henry repeated, as if tasting the word and finding it lacking in sugar. “You are a romantic, Silas. You always were. You worship at the altar of the Ideal, and when reality fails to live up to the Ideal, you simply change the name of the object of your worship. It is a cheap trick. Beauty is the only thing that matters. It is the only thing that justifies our existence. Why else do we paint, why else do we write, why else do we die? To preserve the beautiful. To preserve the perfect. And she...” He waved his hand dismissively. “She is a bore. She is a dullness that has learned to read.”

Silas shrugged, a gesture of profound indifference. “You are mistaken, Henry. Beauty is not the only thing that matters. It is merely the most convenient. It is the mask we wear to hide the fact that we are fundamentally empty. Elara does not need a mask. She is empty in a different way. She is empty of pretense. And that is far more beautiful than any face I have ever seen.”
