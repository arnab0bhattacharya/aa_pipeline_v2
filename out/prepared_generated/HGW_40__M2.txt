The rain in Camden Town did not fall so much as it existed, a persistent, grayish fog that soaked into the bones of the victorian terraces and turned the cobbles into treacherous slicks of mud. It was in this atmosphere of damp and decay that I first understood the peculiar nature of Mr. Arthur Vane. He was a physicist, or so he claimed, though he wore a tweed suit that had seen better days and possessed the distracted, shabby air of a man who had forgotten to eat since breakfast. His lodgings were on the top floor of a narrow house in Inverness Street, a place where the air smelled perpetually of boiled cabbage and coal dust.

My name is Mrs. Hester Prynne, and I was there to do the washing. I had been employed to look after the rooms for Vane when he was not in them, a task that required a certain stoicism, for Vane was a difficult tenant. He did not pay on time, he forgot to trim his lamps, and he spoke to himself in a low, muttering tone that suggested either madness or profound boredom. But he was quiet, which was more than I could say for the other lodgers, and the rent, though irregular, was sufficient to keep the roof over my head.

It was a Tuesday when the incident occurred. I was standing in the kitchen, staring at the mountain of soiled linens on the table, feeling the weight of the day’s labor pressing against my temples. Vane appeared in the doorway, holding a glass of water that trembled slightly in his hand.

"You look distressed, Mrs. Prynne," he said. His voice was thin, reedy, like a flute played in a drafty room.

"The washing, sir," I replied, wringing my hands. "It is a Tuesday. The water is cold, the soap is hard, and the day is short. I fear I shall not finish before dark, and the sheets will be stiff as board by morning."

Vane set his glass down on the table with a sudden, sharp *clack*. "That is a problem of magnitude," he said. "A problem of entropy. Of the relentless forward march of seconds."

"I am afraid I do not follow, sir. I am merely a washerwoman."

"Come to the study," he said. It was not a request.

I followed him out of the kitchen and down the narrow hallway. The study was a small, cramped room, dominated by a heavy oak desk buried under stacks of papers and strange brass instruments. In the center of the room stood a device I had not seen before. It was a large, circular frame, perhaps four feet in diameter, made of copper and glass, with a series of rotating dials and a humming coil at its center. It looked like a machine that had been pulled out of a dream and dropped into reality.

"Sit," Vane said, gesturing to a chair. "I have been working on a theory of temporal displacement. Or rather, a theory of temporal compression."

"Compression," I repeated, feeling a chill that had nothing to do with the damp stone walls.

"Yes. Imagine that time is a river. We are all carried along in its current, from birth to death. But what if, in a small pool, we could slow the current? What if, while the rest of the world moves forward, the water in the pool remains still?"

I looked at the machine. The air around it seemed to shimmer, like heat rising from a summer road. "And what does that have to do with my washing?"

Vane smiled. It was a thin, eager smile that did not reach his eyes. "Everything. I have constructed a field. Within the boundaries of this device, time moves at one-thousandth its normal rate. To an observer outside, the process takes a minute. To an observer inside, it takes an hour. Or a day. It is a bubble of preserved moments."

He moved to the device and began to turn a key. The humming grew louder, a deep, resonant thrum that I felt in my teeth. The air inside the circle grew still, heavy, and thick.

"Step in," he said. "Place your hands on the table. I will activate the field for sixty seconds. When it deactivates, the washing will be done. You will have had an hour of subjective time to scrub, rinse, and dry. You will be tired, perhaps, but the work will be finished."

I looked at him. I looked at the machine. I thought of the cold water, the aching shoulders, the long, gray evening ahead. It was a absurd proposition, but it was also the only solution to a problem that had plagued me for years.

"Is it safe?" I asked.

"Scientifically, yes. Philosophically, who knows? Time is a strange thing. But I have tested it with a clock. It worked."

I stepped into the circle. The moment my feet touched the floor inside the boundary, the world changed. The sound of the rain outside ceased. The smell of coal dust vanished. There was only a profound, ringing silence. I looked at my hands. They were moving, but slowly, as if submerged in honey.

I picked up a basin of water. It did not splash. It moved in a slow, deliberate arc. I began to scrub a shirt. The motion was laborious, heavy. I could feel the fabric beneath my hands, the grit of the dirt, the texture of the cotton. I worked for what felt like an hour. I scrubbed, I rinsed, I wrung out the water. I hung the shirts on the line. I folded the towels. I washed the dishes. I swept the floor. I even dusted the shelves.

When I looked up, the room was exactly as it had been, but the shirts were dry, crisp, and white. The time had passed.

"Excellent," Vane said, stepping out of the circle. He looked at me, his eyes bright with a feverish intensity. "You see? The physics holds. The energy expenditure is minimal. It is a triumph."
