The wind that swept down from the moors above Birstwith was a thing of malice. It found out every crack in the stone of the old mill, and it whined through the window-frames like a hungry soul. Inside, the air was thick with the ghosts of industry: the phantom roar of the looms, the faint, acrid scent of grease and dye, a silence more oppressive than any noise. I sat at the broad oak table, my late husband’s ledger open before me, its columns a foreign language of debits and credits I was only just beginning to decipher. My fingers, stained with ink instead of embroidery silk, traced the lines that told the story of our ruin.

It was a story of trust betrayed. Mr. Silas Croft, my husband’s partner, had been a friend of his at Cambridge, a man of smooth words and a hard heart. He had drawn my gentle Edmund into this venture, promising prosperity and a legacy for our future children. But Edmund, God rest his soul, understood sermons, not schemata. He understood the soul of man, but not the soul of a balance sheet. And Croft, with his calculating eyes and his talk of ‘market fluctuations’, had bled the concern dry, leaving Edmund to face the creditors and the shame. When the fever took him, it took a man already broken by injustice.

Now Croft came again, his carriage wheels crunching the gravel of the yard with an insolent sound. He did not dismount, but sent his man to the door. The message was the same as it had been for weeks. “Mr. Croft wishes to purchase your share of the mill, Mrs. Armitage. He is prepared to offer a final, generous sum. He urges you to consider your position.”

I sent back my reply, my voice steady though my heart hammered against my ribs. “My position is that of a partner. I have no wish to sell.” I saw Croft’s face through the carriage window, a pale, impatient oval. He believed me a fool, a woman clutching at a dead man’s folly, waiting to be worn down. He did not know that the injustice of it all had forged a new resolve in me. I would not be another of his victims. I would learn the language of his world and use it to fight him.

And learn I did. I sat up with candles guttering in their sockets, my head aching with figures. I taught myself the art of double-entry, the tyranny of the profit and loss account. I learned the weight of a yard of broadcloth, the meaning of ‘combing’ and ‘scribbling’, the relative merits of Cheviot wool against that from the Leicester flocks. My mind, once filled with poetry and scripture, became a repository of thread counts and shipping rates. It was a hard education, a bitter medicine, but I swallowed it down. Each column of figures I mastered felt like a stone added to a wall I was building against him.

The day of the cloth market at Leeds was a trial I had not fully imagined. The great hall was a roaring cavern of commerce, a pandemonium of shouting men. The air was thick with the smell of raw wool and the sweat of men who worked for their bread. They stood in their rough coats and wide-brimmed hats, their faces ruddy with ale and the sharp northern air, their voices a torrent of Yorkshire dialect that was almost unintelligible to me. And I, in my plain black bombazine, my bonnet a stark mark of my widowhood, was a heron in a flock of crows.

I made my way to the stand for the Birstwith concern, a small, sad-looking display of our mediocre cloth. The men fell silent as I passed, their conversation dying in their throats. They stared, their eyes hard and curious. I felt their contempt like a physical blow. Here was the parson’s widow, out of her element, meddling in men’s affairs. I heard a low murmur, a stifled laugh. “Poor fool,” one man said to his neighbour, not troubling to lower his voice. “Tha’lt learn nowt here but how to lose what little tha’s got left.”

I did not flinch. I stood my ground, my hands clasped before me, and watched the trade flow around me. I saw the buyers from Manchester and Bradford, their shrewd eyes assessing every bolt of cloth. I saw Silas Croft, holding court not twenty yards from me, his voice booming with false bonhomie. He caught my eye and smiled, a thin, triumphant curl of the lip. He thought to see me humbled, to see me run. But the scorn of these men, the pity in their eyes, only fed the fire in my own breast. They judged me by my bonnet and my sex. They did not see the ledger in my mind, the figures I had memorised, the knowledge I had clawed from the pages of my husband’s books. I was not a fool. I was a widow, and I had come to claim my own.

Croft, unable to resist the temptation to torment me, detached himself from his companions and approached. He doffed his hat with a flourish that was more mockery than courtesy.

“Mrs. Armitage,” he said, his voice smooth as oil. “This is a most unexpected pleasure. I had not thought to see you in this rough-and-tumble world.”

“I am a partner in a mill, Mr. Croft,” I replied, my voice clear and carrying. “It is my world now, as it was my husband’s.”

“Ah, yes. A noble sentiment. But sentiment, I fear, makes for poor weaving. The trade requires a certain… hardness. A masculine grasp of realities.”
