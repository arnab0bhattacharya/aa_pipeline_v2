The sun, that ancient and dependable furnace, had grown chary of its heat. It was not a matter of clouds or of the customary Kentish mists, but a subtle, persistent failing in the light itself. A man might look at the sky at noon and see a clear, pale blue, yet the warmth that should have accompanied it was absent. The shadows remained sharp, but the air carried a December chill well into what ought to have been June. The barley stood thin and sickly, the wheat refused to fill its ear, and the great orchards of the Weald, which should have been heavy with blossom, were barren and silent.

Thomas Hargreaves, a farmer whose family had tilled the soil at Nettlestead for three generations, was a man of practical disposition and little patience for fads. He watched the failing season with a grim, calculating eye. He had seen bad years before—wet summers, late frosts, a blight of this or that—but this was different. This was a fundamental refusal of the world to behave as it ought.

“They say in London,” remarked his neighbour, Jim Pardoe, leaning over the gate with a look of bovine perplexity, “that it’s a temporary atmospheric phenomenon. Something to do with volcanic dust from the East Indies. The Prime Minister himself said it was all under control.”

Hargreaves snorted, a sound like a bull clearing its throat. “The Prime Minister ain’t got a field of potatoes dying on the vine, has he? Temporary? It’s been five months. My seedlings are turning blue. Dust from the East Indies don’t explain why the sun feels like a farthing candle. It’s the sun itself that’s gone cold.”

It was this idea, baldly stated, that first took hold of him. He was not a man of science, but he was a man of observation. He had a barometer and a thermometer and he knew how to use them. He’d been keeping a log since February. The temperature was consistently, unnervingly, eight to ten degrees below the seasonal average. The sun’s radiation, as he’d read in a borrowed copy of *Nature*, seemed to be diminished.

His neighbours thought him a crank. They preferred the government’s explanation of dust, or the parson’s talk of a divine test. But Hargreaves was not interested in explanations; he was interested in potatoes. And potatoes, he knew, required warmth.

The notion came to him one evening as he stared at the magnificent, useless glasshouses of the gentry at Penshurst Place. “We’re trying to grow summer in winter,” he muttered to his wife, Martha. “It’s daft. The heat’s all up there, and we’re down here freezing. What we need is to bring the summer down to us.”

The plan, when he laid it out at the Bell Inn the following Friday, was met with a mixture of ridicule and despair. “Glasshouses?” scoffed a hop-grower named Cobb. “On a farm scale? Tom, that’s for earls and orchids, not for cabbages and turnips. The cost alone would ruin us!”

“I’m not talking about hot-houses for pineapples,” Hargreaves retorted, his voice cutting through the pipe smoke. “I’m talking about cold-frames. Big ones. Long rows of them, right across the south-facing fields. A simple timber frame, a good bed of manure underneath for what little heat it’ll give off, and a roof of the cheapest horticultural glass we can buy. It’s not a perfect summer, but it’ll be ten degrees warmer in there than it is out here. It’s a chance.”

He was a stubborn man, and his practicality had a certain force. He’d done the sums. He’d written to glass merchants in Maidstone and Tonbridge Wells. He spoke of crop rotation, of soil temperature, of the specific needs of the potato and the hardy cabbage. He did not speak of the sun’s failing light, but of the necessity of putting food in their children’s mouths come winter. Slowly, grudgingly, they began to see it not as a madman’s fancy, but as a desperate, shared enterprise.

Meanwhile, in the Houses of Parliament, the problem was being managed. A Select Committee had been formed. Papers were laid before the House. The President of the Board of Agriculture gave a reassuring speech, full of phrases like ‘unseasonable meteorological conditions’ and ‘a temporary aberration in solar output.’ He spoke of government seed-potatoes being made available and of the resilience of the British agricultural sector. He did not speak of the cold, which was now a palpable presence in the nation’s bones.

The men of the Weald paid no mind to the speeches. They paid with their savings and their credit. They felled timber from their own copses and carted it to Nettlestead, where Hargreaves, with the air of a field-marshal, directed the construction. It was a strange, frantic industry. Men who had only ever dug and sowed were now measuring and sawing, hammering and glazing. The air filled with the smell of sawdust and putty.

The first great glass-house was erected in Hargreaves’ own Long Meadow. It was not a thing of beauty—a long, low tunnel of timber and glass, squatting on the Kentish soil like some great, blind insect. But when the first hardy seedlings were transplanted into its sheltered beds, a murmur of wonder went through the assembled farmers. Inside, the air was still and noticeably warmer. The weak sunlight, filtered through the glass, seemed to gather and hold. It was a fragile, man-made pocket of a season that had been lost.

Others followed. Soon, the landscape around Nettlestead was dotted with these low, glinting structures. They were ugly, practical, and utterly unlike the rolling green fields of a Kentish picture postcard. They were the work of men who had stopped waiting for the world to be put right and had decided to put a small piece of it right themselves.
