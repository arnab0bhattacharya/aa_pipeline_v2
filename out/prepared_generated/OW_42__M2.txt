The cottage of Elowen stood at the very lip of the Blackwood, a place where the civilized world ended in a tangle of brambles and the wild world began with a sigh. Elowen was a boy of twelve, or perhaps thirteen; it was difficult to say, for time had a habit of becoming slippery in the presence of one who moved with a slight, persistent limp. He had been born with a twist in his left ankle, a small mechanical error in the architecture of his body that made him a figure of pity to the villagers and a subject of profound indifference to himself. He preferred the company of his books to that of men, for books, unlike men, did not look at one’s feet with such eager curiosity.

Each night, when the moon hung low and pale like a silver coin dropped into a dark well, the white stag came. It did not bark or whinny, nor did it demand to be fed. It simply stood outside the window, its antlers branching out like the frozen roots of a lightning strike, its eyes holding a stillness that was more terrifying than any wildness. Elowen would watch it from his bed, wrapped in a quilt of patchwork blues and greys, and feel a strange, cold pull in his chest, a gravity that had nothing to do with the earth.

One night, when the frost lay thick upon the panes, Elowen rose. He did not know why. He only knew that to stay in the warm, stifling silence of his room was to betray a promise he had not made but felt he had kept for years. He dressed in his finest clothes—a waistcoat of faded velvet and boots that had seen better days—and stepped out into the night. The air was sharp enough to cut the breath from his lungs. He followed the stag. It did not run; it walked with a deliberate, aristocratic slowness, as if it were leading him on a tour of its own estates.

The forest swallowed them. The trees here were ancient and tall, their branches interlacing overhead to form a vault of black stone. There was no sound, save for the crunch of Elowen’s uneven step and the soft, silent padding of the stag’s hooves. After what seemed like an hour, or perhaps a century, the trees parted, and the city of Aethelgard revealed itself.

It was a city of impossible white marble, gleaming under a sky that had no stars, only a faint, diffuse luminescence that seemed to come from the buildings themselves. The streets were wide and clean, swept free of even the memory of dust. Elowen walked into the main square, his limp drawing the eyes of the inhabitants. They were all young, impossibly so, with skin the smoothness of polished alabaster and hair the colour of spun gold or midnight. None of them had a scar. None of them had a wrinkle. None of them had a flaw.

And none of them were happy.

Elowen observed them with the detached interest of a naturalist studying rare insects. They moved with a grace that was mechanical, their smiles fixed and identical, their conversations a low hum of meaningless pleasantries. He watched a young woman pour wine for her companion. The wine was the colour of deep garnet, but the woman’s face was a mask of such serene, hollow emptiness that Elowen felt a shiver run down his spine.

"You are lame," said a voice beside him.

Elowen turned. A young man stood there, holding a lyre that he had not played. His eyes were the pale, watery blue of winter ice.

"I am," Elowen replied, surprised by his own calm. "And you are perfect."

The young man smiled, but the smile did not reach his eyes. "We are all perfect here. We are all young. We are all beautiful. Is that not enough?"

"It is not enough," Elowen said. "I have read of such places. They are prisons made of mirrors. You reflect only what you wish to see, and in doing so, you lose the ability to see anything else."

The young man frowned, a small crease appearing between his brows, a flaw that seemed to pain him. "We do not suffer here. Pain is a thing of the past. We have transcended the body’s limitations."

"But you have also transcended the soul’s capacity for joy," Elowen countered. "Joy requires contrast. It requires the knowledge of loss, of time, of imperfection. You have no shadows, so you have no depth. You are flat, like a painting in a gallery. I would rather be a sketch, rough and unfinished, than a masterpiece that tells me nothing."

The young man looked at Elowen with a mixture of horror and fascination. "You speak like a philosopher, yet you walk like a cripple. How can you know such things?"

"Because I have felt the weight of the world," Elowen said softly. "You have only felt its surface. My limp is my anchor. It reminds me that I am here, that I am real, that time is passing. Without it, I might float away, like you, into this grey eternity."

The stag appeared at the edge of the square. It looked at Elowen, then at the young man, and then back at the city. Its gaze was not one of malice, but of a profound, tragic pity. It turned and began to walk back toward the trees.

Elowen followed. The young man called out to him, his voice thin and reedy. "Will you not stay? We can cure you. We can make you walk straight. We can make you young forever."

Elowen paused, his hand on the bark of the first tree of the forest. He looked back at the city, at the gleaming, joyless faces that stared up at him. He thought of his cottage, of the smell of pipe smoke and old paper, of the ache in his ankle that was as familiar as his own name.
