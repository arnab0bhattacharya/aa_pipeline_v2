Mr. Wilberforce Pegg was a man whose entire existence might be summed up in a single, resolute principle: the avoidance of anything resembling a fuss. His world was one of ledgers and lamb's-wool, of neatly stacked quires and the gentle chink of the till. He was a bookseller, not of the romantic, starving variety, but of the prosperous, portly sort, whose shop on Ludgate Hill was as orderly as his own person, and whose soul was bound as tightly as his own volumes. He had journeyed to Paris with a heart full of righteous indignation and a pocket full of unpaid invoices, his mind fixed upon a sum of three hundred and seventeen pounds, four shillings and sixpence, owed to him by the celebrated Parisian printer, Monsieur Étienne Dubois.

It is one thing to travel to a foreign land with a clear and singular purpose, and quite another to find that land has, in the interval of your journey, lost its collective wits. Mr. Pegg had expected to find Monsieur Dubois amidst the clatter of his presses, a man of business to be reasoned with, or at the very least, intimidated by a stern English glare. Instead, he found Paris. He found a Paris that was not a city of art and cuisine, but a great, roaring, boiling cauldron of human passions, in which all reason and regularity had been flung to bubble and burst. The streets, which his guidebook had promised were lined with elegant shops and stately hotels, were choked with a surging, shouting, singing multitude. Tricolour cockades bloomed on every breast like a feverish rash. The air, which should have smelled of baking bread and roasting chestnuts, was thick with the acrid smoke of torches and the sour tang of unwashed bodies and unwashed ideas.

Mr. Pegg, clutching his hat to his head as if it were the last remnant of his sanity, was borne along on this human tide until he was deposited, gasping and bewildered, before the printer's establishment. The great doors were splintered, the windows shattered into a thousand jagged teeth. Inside, the magnificent printing presses, those engines of order and enlightenment, stood silent and derelict, their iron limbs twisted like the victims of some mechanical torture. The floor was a sea of scattered paper, trodden into a damp and formless pulp. It was a scene of such utter desolation that Mr. Pegg felt the sum of three hundred and seventeen pounds, four shillings and sixpence, recede into a distant and improbable fantasy. His quest, he saw with a sinking heart, was not merely difficult; it was absurd.

It was from a dark corner of this ruined workshop that a figure emerged, a wraith of ink and anxiety. He was a boy, no more than seventeen, with a smudge of black across his cheek that might have been a tear or a thumbprint of despair. His eyes, large and dark in a pale, thin face, held the frantic look of a creature cornered. "Monsieur?" he stammered in a whisper, as if afraid the very walls had ears. "You are English? You have come from the bookseller in London?"

Mr. Pegg, drawing himself up with what little dignity he could muster, confirmed his identity and his purpose. The boy, whose name, he would learn, was Jean-Luc, wrung his hands together. "Then you are too late, monsieur. Or perhaps, you are just in time. They have taken him. The Comité. They came at dawn. They took the master, and they took the books, and they took the type. They call it sedition to print a word of doubt. They have arrested the alphabet itself!"

The boy's voice rose in a pitch of hysteria, and he clutched at Mr. Pegg's sleeve. "Monsieur, you must help me. You must." From beneath a loose floorboard, he drew a small, oilskin-wrapped parcel, no larger than a brick. "It is all that is left. The proofs of the master's great work, his 'Vindication of Liberty'. The only complete set. If they find it, he will go to the guillotine for certain. You must take it. To England. To safety."

Mr. Pegg stared at the parcel as if it were a coiled serpent. To carry papers? Unidentified, possibly treasonous, papers, out of a city in the grip of a murderous mob? He, Wilberforce Pegg, whose greatest act of daring had been to serve a customer a slightly foxed edition of Cicero at the price of a pristine one? The thought was so monstrous, so far beyond the pale of his carefully circumscribed life, that he felt a sudden, violent urge to laugh. "My dear boy," he began, in the soothing tone he used for difficult customers, "you misunderstand my purpose here. I am a man of commerce. I have come to settle an account, not to embroil myself in… in foreign politics."

But the boy would not be soothed. "Politics? This is not politics, monsieur! This is a man's head! They will take him to the Conciergerie, and from there it is a short walk to the Place de la Révolution. Please! I have no one else. You are a stranger. They will not suspect you. Just a bookseller, come to buy books, yes?" He pushed the parcel into Mr. Pegg's hands. It felt unnervingly warm, as if it contained a living heart.

As Mr. Pegg stood there, paralyzed with a terror so profound it was almost comical, the roar of the street outside swelled to a new crescendo. There was a rhythmic, heavy thudding of boots, and a guttural chant that rose and fell like the breath of some great beast. Through the broken window, he saw them: the sans-culottes, marching in a ragged but terrifyingly unified column, their pikes and scythes bristling against the grey sky. Their faces were a blur of fanaticism and hunger. They were the very embodiment of the chaos he so abhorred, a physical manifestation of a world turned upside down.
