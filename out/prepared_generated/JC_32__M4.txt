The order had come up with the last of the river steamers, a single folded sheet of paper whose brevity was more eloquent of the Company’s mind than any length of argument could have been. It said that the station at Nsanga was to be abandoned, its stores and trade goods brought down, its personnel withdrawn, and that this was to be accomplished with all dispatch, in view of circumstances upon which the circular did not condescend to enlarge. I read it in the shadow of the verandah, with the afternoon sun striking through the thatch in thin blades of light, and I remember that the paper was still damp from the river, and that it smelled faintly of the hold of the steamer, of tar and bilge and the sour breath of the cargo. The words were few and clean, as words are when they have been written far away by men who have never seen the thing they order, and who are therefore able to be tidy.

I did not read it a second time at once. I stood looking out over the clearing, where the plantain leaves hung motionless in that heavy air which seems to have been breathed already by the earth itself, and where the red dust of the compound lay in the long, still patches of light, and I was aware of a certain reluctance, a heaviness of the spirit, which I set down at first to the heat and to the fever that had been in my blood the week before. But I knew, even as I framed the thought, that it was not the fever. The station had grown about me in the four years of my charge, as a tree grows about the stake that supports it, until I could no longer say with any honesty where the one ended and the other began. I had seen the first huts raised; I had laid out the compound with my own hands, and the row of mangos beyond the store was of my planting. There is a vanity in such things, and I do not deny it, but there is also a kind of love, which is the more difficult to confess because it has no object that a man may name with pride.

It was while I stood thus that Nkosi came across the compound. He was an old man then, though his age was of that kind which seems to have been always the same, and he walked with the deliberate gait of one who has learned that haste is a confession of weakness. He was the chief of the village beyond the stream, and for these four years he had been to me what the Company’s circulars would have called, in their careful and evasive English, a friendly native; but what he was in truth I had come to understand slowly, and with a certain shame at my own slowness, as a man who had set his hand to my safety as another sets his hand to a bargain, and who would not let go.

He stopped at the foot of the steps and looked up at me, and I saw that he had already heard the news. It is a strange thing, and one that I have never grown used to, how the word of a paper that has come a thousand miles by river will be known in the villages before the ink is dry. He did not speak at once. He stood with his hands folded before him and his eyes upon the paper in my hand, and there was in his face that patient, weighing look which I had learned to dread more than any anger, because it was the look of a man who is considering what he must do, and not what he wishes.

“The steamer will go back tomorrow,” he said at last. It was not a question.

“It will go back,” I said.

“And you will go with it.”

I folded the paper and put it in my pocket, and I heard my own voice, as though it belonged to another man, saying that the orders were the orders of the Company, and that a servant of the Company had no choice but to obey. The words sounded thin and foolish in the heavy air. He heard them out with the same patience, and then he said:

“When the traders came first to this river, they came with guns, and we met them with guns, and there was much killing. Then there came a man who sat down and talked, and he was not killed, and the killing stopped. That was before your time. When you came, the killing had been stopped for many years, and you did not know what it had cost. But I know. I have kept the peace in this country with my own hands, and I have kept it because I believed that the man who sat down and talked would not go away and leave the guns behind him.”

He was silent for a moment, and the shadows of the plantains had not moved, and somewhere beyond the huts a woman was pounding grain with a slow, regular stroke that seemed to measure out the silence.

“If you go,” he said, “the people will say that the talk was a trap, and that the guns will come again. And they will not be wrong. There are men up the river who have waited for this. They will come down when the station is empty, and they will say that they have driven the white man out, and the young men will believe them, because it is easier to believe a boast than a promise. I am an old man, and I cannot hold them alone.”
