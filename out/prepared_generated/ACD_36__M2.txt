The fog that evening had settled over the city of Eldenwick with the tenacity of a damp wool, clinging to the spires of St. Jude’s Cathedral and muffling the distant bells of the evening prayer. I stood beside Mr. Arthur Pendelton, the cathedral’s Chief Investigator, as he adjusted the collar of his tweed coat against the chill. We had been summoned by the Dean, a man of considerable piety and even greater anxiety, to address a peculiar duality of disturbances that had thrown the choir into a state of near-hysteria.

“Mr. Holmes,” the Dean had said, his voice trembling slightly in the gloom of the chapter house, “we are beset by two anomalies. The organ plays itself, and the altar plate is missing. The musicians swear they have seen no one, yet the notes rise from the keys as if guided by an invisible hand. And then, there is the matter of the silver.”

Holmes had listened with that peculiar, still intensity which I had come to associate with the early stages of his deductions. He had not asked for the police. He had asked for a lantern, a length of string, and a key to the organ loft.

Now, as we climbed the narrow, spiraling staircase that led to the choir stalls and the organ gallery above, the air grew stale and thick with the scent of old wood and beeswax. The cathedral below was silent, save for the occasional drip of condensation from the vaulted ceiling.

“Do you believe in ghosts, Watson?” Holmes asked, his voice low and crisp, cutting through the silence.

“I believe in evidence, Holmes. Ghosts are merely evidence we have not yet categorized.”

“Precisely. And this ‘organ ghost’ is likely to prove to be a rather mundane creature, albeit one with a mechanical aptitude.”

We reached the top of the stairs. The organ, a massive instrument of oak and brass, dominated the space. It was a magnificent piece of engineering, with its pipes rising like a forest of silver and gold into the shadows. Holmes set the lantern on the floor and examined the keys. They were cold. The pedals were untouched.

“Mr. Ashcroft,” Holmes said, addressing the organist who had been waiting anxiously at the foot of the stairs, “you claim that the instrument plays a few notes by itself at night. Is this accurate?”

Ashcroft was a man of slight build, with nervous hands and eyes that darted to the shadows. “Yes, sir. Last Tuesday, and again last Thursday. Always around midnight. It is a short phrase—three notes, then a rest, then two more. It is distinct. I have tried to determine the source, but the mechanism is locked.”

“Locked,” Holmes repeated, turning his gaze to the organist’s face. “And you have not reported this to the authorities? You chose to speak to the Dean first.”

“It was... it was frightening, sir. The choir is superstitious. I did not wish to cause a panic.”

“A prudent decision, perhaps,” Holmes said, though his tone carried a sharp edge. “But tell me, Mr. Ashcroft, where were you on these occasions?”

“In my lodgings, sir. I lock my door. I hear nothing until the next morning, when I find the keys have been depressed.”

Holmes nodded slowly. He walked to the side of the organ, where a narrow maintenance panel had been removed. Inside, the complex system of levers and cables was visible. He traced the path of the action from the key to the pipe valve.

“Here,” he said, pointing to a small, rusted bolt. “This is old. And here, a fresh scratch. Someone has tampered with the linkage.”

I leaned in, examining the scratch with my pocket watch glass. It was indeed fresh, the metal bright against the rust.

“But how?” I asked. “If the keys are locked and the room is sealed...”

“The room is not sealed,” Holmes said, his eyes narrowing as he looked toward the high, arched window at the far end of the gallery. “Look at the sill.”

I moved closer. The window was narrow, barely wide enough for a man to squeeze through, but it was open. A thin wire, almost invisible in the dim light, stretched from the window frame to a hook on the organ case.

“A string and pulley system,” Holmes murmured, a smile playing on his lips. “Simple, elegant, and entirely effective. A weight attached to the other end of the string, dropped from the outside or from within a concealed space, would depress the keys. But why?”

“It is a ghost,” Ashcroft whispered, his face pale.

“No,” Holmes said firmly. “It is a signal. Or a test. And it is connected to the other matter. The altar plate.”

We descended the stairs, the lantern swinging gently in the draft. The nave was empty, the pews dark shadows in the gloom. The altar stood at the far end, the silver plate missing from its place. In its stead lay a copy, gilt in brass, identical in size and shape but lacking the heavy, solid weight of the original.

The Dean was waiting for us, his face drawn with worry. “The police say it is a simple theft,” he said. “But why leave a copy? Why the noise?”

Holmes picked up the brass plate. It was warm to the touch. “It was placed here recently. The brass has not had time to cool. And the ‘noise’... the organ notes... were likely a distraction. Or a mark of ownership.”

Holmes turned to the Dean. “Who has access to the altar? Who has access to the organ loft?”

“The sacristan,” the Dean said. “And the organist.”

Holmes looked at Ashcroft, who had turned a shade whiter. “Mr. Ashcroft, you have been at the cathedral for twenty years. You are respected. You are paid fairly. What motive could you have for stealing the plate and creating a ghost story?”

Ashcroft’s hands began to tremble. “I have no motive, sir. I am a musician. I do not steal.”
