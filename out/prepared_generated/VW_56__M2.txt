The light in the drawing room did not fall so much as it pooled, a thick, amber viscosity that caught the dust motes suspended in the stagnant air. It was the hour of siesta, that long, suspended breath of the afternoon where the world outside seemed to dissolve into the heat, leaving only the interior life to churn and bubble. Elara sat by the window, her fingers tracing the rim of a porcelain cup that had long since gone cold, watching the water in the lake below shimmer and break, break, and shimmer again, a rhythmic pulse that seemed to synchronize with the beating of her own heart, or perhaps it was the other way around, her heart merely echoing the lazy lapping of the water against the stone embankment.

Her mother, Mrs. Halloway, was at the desk, the nib of her pen scratching a faint, dry sound against the paper, a sound like the scuttling of small insects. She was writing to her sister in London, no doubt detailing the quality of the linen, the humidity of the air, the price of the lemonade, and the general excellence of the villa’s management. She wrote with a serene, untroubled face, her lips pursed in concentration, unaware that the truth of their summer was bleeding out of the house through the open shutters, ignored by the breeze. She did not write of the Count. She did not write of the way the light caught the silver in his hair when he leaned over the garden wall, or the way the air seemed to thicken around them when he spoke, his words sounding like the plucking of a cello string, low and resonant, vibrating in the chest. To write of it would be to give it form, to make it solid and therefore mortal, and Elara had a suspicion, a deep, intuitive dread, that if she named him, if she pinned him to the page with the same casual precision with which her mother pinned the weather, he would vanish, like smoke into the blue Italian sky.

And yet, he was everywhere. He was in the shadow of the arbutus tree, where the leaves rustled with a dry, papery sound. He was in the silence that followed the bell at the villa, a silence that was not empty but full of expectation. Elara felt a strange detachment from her own body, as if she were watching herself from a great distance, a figure in a painting, posed and static, while the life of the room flowed around her in a current she could not quite catch. She thought of her brother, Julian, who had run off this morning with a group of local boys, their laughter echoing back from the cliffs like a jagged, bright sound. He was gone into the hills, into the wild, unmanaged spaces where the path ended and the rock began. He was becoming something else, she realized, something hard and angular, like the stones at the bottom of the lake. He no longer looked at her when he passed; his eyes were fixed on the horizon, on the escape, on the freedom of being alone with the wind and the stone.

The scratching of the pen stopped. Mrs. Halloway looked up, her face smooth, a mask of polite interest.

“The light is very strong, my dear,” she said, her voice light and airy, like a leaf caught in a draft. “I am sure you will find it better to rest now. The heat is oppressive.”

Elara did not answer. She was thinking of the Count’s hand, which had rested on the back of her chair, a weight that was barely there, yet everything. She remembered the way he had looked at her, not with desire, but with a kind of sorrowful recognition, as if he knew what she was thinking, what she was feeling, and found it both beautiful and tragic. He had said, *La vita è breve*, life is short, and the words had hung in the air, heavy and sweet, like the scent of the orange blossoms in the courtyard.

But her mother was already turning back to her letter, the nib moving again, a steady, rhythmic scratching that covered the sound of the wind, the sound of the lake, the sound of Elara’s own breathing. She was building a world of paper, a safe, ordered world where nothing happened, where the sun shone and the water was calm, and where her daughter was merely tired from the heat. It was a kind of cruelty, this silence, this refusal to see. It was a wall built of good intentions, of propriety, of the belief that if one did not speak of a thing, it did not exist.

Elara rose from the chair. The movement was slow, deliberate, as if she were moving through water. She walked to the window, pushing aside the heavy curtains. The view opened up before her, vast and indifferent. The lake was a sheet of hammered gold, reflecting the sky, which was a pale, washed-out blue, devoid of clouds. In the distance, the mountains rose, their peaks sharp and white against the sky, immutable, eternal. They did not care about the love that burned in the drawing room, or the wildness that roamed in the hills, or the letters that were being written to a sister who would never know the truth. They simply were, a fact of nature, as solid and real as the stone beneath her feet.

She leaned her forehead against the cool glass. The heat pressed against her skin, a physical weight. She felt a sudden, sharp pain in her chest, a tightening, a constriction. It was not sorrow, exactly, nor joy, but a mixture of both, a complex emotion that had no name. It was the feeling of being alive, painfully, fully alive, in a moment that was slipping away, too fast to hold, too fast to capture.
