It is one thing to be poor, and quite another to be unnoticed, for poverty is a misfortune, but obscurity is a relief. Little Ilka, who sold matches at the corner of the Rue of the Frozen Fountain in the city of Vinterhavn, had discovered this distinction early. She possessed nothing that was beautiful save her own hunger, which was a thin and elegant thing, and her matches, which were but little sticks of wood tipped with the promise of a momentary sun. The city itself was a masterpiece of austerity, a collection of silver spires and alabaster domes that glittered under a sky the colour of a faded violet, and the snow lay upon its rooftops like ermine trimmed for a coronation that would never come. To Ilka, the palace of the Grand Duchess was not a place of residence but a work of art, a jewel-case of white marble and malachite in which the ruling family were kept like precious stones.

One evening, when the snow was falling in great, silent flakes that muffled the world in a shroud of white velvet, Ilka saw a glint of colour against the monochrome of the street. It was a small thing, lying half-buried near the palace gates, and she knelt to retrieve it with the instinct of one who knows that even the smallest shadow may cast a long darkness. It was a comb, carved from a single piece of ivory and set with stones that seemed to have captured the very essence of the winter twilight: a central sapphire of a blue so deep it was almost black, surrounded by a constellation of pearls and tiny, vicious emeralds. It was a thing of terrible beauty, and Ilka felt a sudden and profound kinship with it. It was the first beautiful thing she had ever owned, and she knew immediately that she would not sell it.

To sell it would have been to admit that she was merely a pauper, a creature of commerce. To keep it was to become a connoisseur, a collector of rarities. She slipped it into the pocket of her thin coat, where it rested against her ribs like a frozen heart. For three days, the city buzzed with a scandalous rumour. The Princess Elara, a young woman of legendary beauty and legendary wilfulness, had lost her favourite ornament. She was searching the city herself, not with the police, but with a troop of her ladies-in-waiting, their silks a riot of colour against the grey stones. Ilka watched them pass, and felt a strange, detached amusement. She did not fear discovery; she felt that the comb had chosen her, and that fate, that most aesthetic of forces, would not allow its choice to be overturned by a mere princess.

On the third day, the Princess Elara stopped before Ilka’s little stand. She was, Ilka admitted, a masterpiece of nature, with hair the colour of spun gold and eyes the colour of the sapphire in the comb. She looked at Ilka with a gaze that was both imperious and strangely weary. ‘You sell matches,’ said the Princess, her voice like the chime of a silver bell. ‘I sell warmth, Your Highness,’ replied Ilka, with a composure that surprised them both. ‘The matches are merely the medium.’ The Princess smiled, a slow, enchanting curve of her lips. ‘A philosopher in rags. How delightful. I am looking for a thing that is lost. A comb, set with stones. It is of no value, of course, save to me. It is the only thing I own that is not a crown or a command.’ ‘Many things are lost in Vinterhavn, Your Highness,’ said Ilka, her hand resting on the lump in her pocket. ‘Often, they are better for the losing. Possession is the tomb of beauty.’ ‘And what do you know of beauty, little match-seller?’ asked the Princess, leaning closer. Her breath smelled of cloves and winter roses. ‘I know that it is a burden,’ said Ilka gravely. ‘It requires an audience, and an audience is a tyrant. I imagine a princess feels this more than most.’ The Princess Elara looked at Ilka for a long moment, and in her eyes, Ilka saw a flash of recognition, a sudden, shocking understanding. ‘You are right,’ the Princess whispered. ‘I am tired of being looked at. I want to be the one who looks. But without my comb, I feel… incomplete. It is a part of my soul, you see, that I wear in my hair.’ ‘A soul is a heavy thing to carry in one’s coiffure,’ observed Ilka. ‘You are very impertinent,’ said the Princess, but there was no anger in her voice. ‘Give it to me. I will give you a thousand crowns. You will never be hungry again.’ ‘Hunger is a fire that keeps the soul warm,’ said Ilka. ‘Your gold would put it out. Besides, I have grown fond of the sapphire. It is the colour of a bruise on a winter sky. It reminds me that even pain can be beautiful.’ The Princess sighed, a sound of genuine frustration. ‘Then you are a thief.’ ‘I am a curator,’ corrected Ilka. ‘I have rescued the object from the indignity of being a mere accessory. In my pocket, it is a secret. In your hair, it is a spectacle. There is a great deal of difference.’ ‘I must have it,’ said the Princess. ‘Name your price. I will give you my palace. I will give you my title.’ ‘I have no use for a palace,’ said Ilka. ‘It is too much trouble to dust. And a title is merely a word, and words are the cheapest things in the world.’ The Princess looked at the girl, at the matches, at the falling snow, and then she began to laugh.
