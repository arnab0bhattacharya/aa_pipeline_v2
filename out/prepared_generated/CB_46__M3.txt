The rain had beaten against my window all morning with a savage persistence, and the wind, sweeping down from the distant mountains, howled through the narrow streets of V—— as if in pursuit of some invisible prey. I had been sitting for two hours over my books, attempting to fix my mind on the conjugation of German verbs, but the gloom of the weather had infected my spirits. Solitude is a discipline to which I had long submitted myself, yet there are days when the chain galls, when the blank walls of a foreign lodging-house seem to contract around one like the walls of a prison, and the heart cries out for some familiar voice, some token of that old English life which I had left behind me, perhaps for ever.

I was roused from my abstraction by a knock—a single, clear, decided knock, quite unlike the hesitating taps of my landlady or the clumsy battering of the butcher's boy. I opened the door myself, and there stood a woman—tall, dressed in black, with a cloak dripping wet, and a bonnet that had known better days. Her face was striking rather than beautiful; pale, with dark eyes that held a singular mixture of audacity and melancholy, and a mouth whose lines suggested both humour and resolve. She looked at me with a directness that disconcerted me; I felt, in that first instant, as if I were being read, weighed, and judged.

"Mr. Locke?" she said. "Henry Locke?"

"That is my name."

"Then I have found you. I am your cousin—your distant cousin, if you will have it so—but your cousin nonetheless. I am Sophia March."

She entered with a quiet confidence, laying her wet cloak across the back of a chair, and seated herself before I had quite recovered from my surprise. I observed that she carried herself with an air which spoke of better circumstances, of a breeding that had survived the wreck of fortune; her hands, though gloveless and reddened by cold, were slender and well-shaped. She looked about the room with a swift, comprehensive glance, taking in the bare floor, the deal table piled with books, the meagre fire, and—I could have sworn—noting each particular with a precision that made me uneasy.

"You will think me bold," she continued, fixing those dark eyes upon me; "and so I am. I have need to be. When one has no protector, no friend, no resource but one's own wits, one learns to be bold, or one perishes. I have come a long way to find you, Mr. Locke, and I have not come without reason."

"What reason?" I asked. I had recovered myself somewhat, and spoke, I flatter myself, with composure; but inwardly I was stirred. The word cousin had touched some deep-buried chord; I had not heard it spoken in relation to myself for years—not since my mother's death, not since the day when, standing by her grave in the wet churchyard at Haworth, I had turned my back upon England and all its associations.

"Your mother," she said, "was a March. Her father—your grandfather—was my great-uncle. I have the papers; I have the family Bible, with the entries in his own hand. I will show them to you, if you doubt me. But I did not come to bore you with genealogies. I came because I am alone, and because I believed that somewhere in this great Continent there lived a man connected to me by blood, who might not be wholly indifferent to the claim of kinship."

She paused. Her voice had dropped; there was a tremor in it that I could not feign—and I believe I know feigned emotion from true, having witnessed both in abundance among the pupils and masters of this establishment. I looked at her, and something within me responded. She was, by her own account, of my mother's house; she bore the name that my mother had borne before her marriage; she had, perhaps, sat by the same hearth, looked upon the same hills.

"You speak of being alone," I said; "so am I. This is not a country where an Englishman finds fellowship easily. I am tolerated here; I am paid to teach; but I am not understood, and I do not pretend that the deficiency lies only on their side."

"Exactly," she replied, with a smile that was both sad and quick; "you are starved—I see it in your face. Starved for the sound of an English voice speaking without constraint, for the freedom of one's own tongue. I know the feeling. I have lived three years in Brussels, and I am famished with it."

We spoke then for an hour. She told me of her father, a clergyman who had died insolvent; of her mother, who had followed him to the grave within a twelvemonth; of her own struggles to maintain herself by teaching music. She spoke with a frankness that was almost startling, concealing nothing of her poverty, extenuating nothing of her necessity. I found her conversation at once stirring and soothing; she had read, she had thought, she had suffered, and suffering had not soured her but deepened her. There were moments when her eye flashed and her lip curled with a scorn that I recognised—as one recognises a feature in a strange mirror—for it was the very expression my mother had worn when she spoke of injustice and wrong.

At length she came to the matter. She did not approach it obliquely; she was too proud and too honest for that.

"Mr. Locke," she said, "I must ask you for help. I need fifty francs. I would not ask it if I had any other resource. I have pawned everything that would bring a price; I have eaten once a day for a fortnight; I have walked the streets in shoes that let in the rain at every step. I am not ashamed to beg of a kinsman what I would not accept from a stranger. You may refuse me; I shall not reproach you. But I tell you plainly—I am at the edge."
