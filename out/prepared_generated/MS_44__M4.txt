The air within the ward was a thing one could taste—a miasma compounded of antiseptic and despair, of the faint, sweetish odour of fever and the sharper note of fear that clung to the very fabric of the curtains. I had breathed it for so many weeks that it had become the element of my existence, and I moved through it as a creature native to some dim, subterranean world, my steps muffled, my voice hushed to a whisper. They called it the Grey Fever, a name of no scientific precision, but one that spoke with a poet’s truth of the pallor it stamped upon the countenance and the slow, creeping twilight it brought to the mind. It had come upon London like a thief in the night, and now the great city, with all its clamour and its pride, lay prostrate beneath a visitation it could neither comprehend nor stay.

I was a nurse at St. Cuthbert’s, a vast and ancient pile whose stones seemed to weep with the damp of centuries, and whose corridors, though lit by the cold, unwavering glare of electric lamps, retained a gloom that no light could wholly vanquish. We were overwhelmed. The beds were filled to overflowing, the wards choked with the dying and the dead, and we who tended them were as mariners clinging to the wreck of some proud vessel, battered by a storm that would not cease. Sleep was a forgotten luxury, and hope a fragile flower that withered each morning as we counted our losses.

It was in this season of universal woe that I first beheld him. He had been brought in upon a Tuesday, in the third week of my service, a young man of perhaps eight-and-twenty years, whose name, they told me, was Julian Ashworth. He was a musician, they said—a composer of some renown—and he had been found insensible in his rooms, his violin clasped to his breast as though it were a talisman against the darkness that had claimed him. When first I saw him, he lay in a stupor, his dark hair matted upon a brow of marble whiteness, his breathing shallow and irregular, his hands, those delicate instruments of his art, lying limp and useless upon the counterpane. There was about his countenance, even in its prostration, a refinement so exquisite, a sensibility so palpable, that I felt my heart stir with a pity that was not wholly of the professional kind.

I tended him through the long watches of the night, bathing his fevered brow, moistening his parched lips, and listening to the broken murmurs that escaped him—fragments of melody, perhaps, or the ghosts of words half-formed. In his delirium, he spoke often of music, of harmonies that eluded him, of a symphony he had heard in a dream and could not recapture. His voice, though weakened by sickness, possessed a timbre that thrilled me to the core, and I found myself lingering by his bedside long after my duties had called me elsewhere, straining to catch the import of his ravings.

On the fifth day, he awoke. His eyes, when they opened, were of a grey so deep and luminous that they seemed to hold within them the shadows of all the sorrows of the world, and yet, too, a light that was not of this earth. He looked at me with a gaze of such piercing inquiry that I felt my very soul laid bare before him, and I, who had grown accustomed to the indifference of the dying, blushed like a girl.

“You are an angel,” he whispered, his voice a thread of sound, “or I am dead, and this is some fair spirit sent to guide me hence.”

“You are not dead,” I replied, striving to maintain the composure befitting my station, “though you have been very near it. You must not speak; you must conserve your strength.”

But he would not be silent. He asked my name, and when I told him, he repeated it softly, as though it were a line of some cherished poem. “Elara,” he murmured. “It has a music of its own.”

From that hour, a bond was forged between us that I could neither explain nor resist. Each day, as his strength flickered like a candle in a draught, I found myself drawn to his bedside, and each day our conversation deepened, touching upon subjects that I had never discussed with any living soul—the nature of beauty, the mystery of suffering, the strange consolations of art. He spoke to me of his music, of the visions that haunted him, of the symphony that he was certain would be his masterpiece, though he knew, as I knew, that he would never live to complete it. His voice, when he spoke of these things, rose above the common register of speech into something like a song, and I listened, entranced, forgetful of the moans of the other patients, the summons of the doctors, the relentless approach of death.

I knew, from the first, that he would not recover. The fever had taken too deep a hold upon his frame; his lungs were ravaged, his heart enfeebled, and the physicians, in their hushed conferences, spoke of weeks, perhaps days. I knew it, and yet I loved him. I loved him with a passion that was all the more fierce for its hopelessness, a love that burned in the darkness like a solitary star, shedding its light upon a landscape of ruin. I knew what I was doing—I, who had seen so many die, who had held so many hands as they grew cold, who had closed so many eyes—I knew that I was binding my heart to a corpse, that I was pouring out my soul upon the sands of a desert that would never bloom. And I did it anyway.
