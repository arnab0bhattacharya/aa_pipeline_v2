The rain had stopped, leaving the pavement outside the chapel to gleam with a wet, metallic brilliance that seemed to slice the morning into sharp, separate fragments. Eleanor stood beneath the stone archway, her black gloves pulling at the damp hem of her coat. She had become a Frenchwoman in her posture, she thought, feeling the weight of her shoulders settle differently than they had twenty years ago. She smoothed the leather over her knuckles, pulling the fine kid down so that it lay flat and tight. She did this with the meticulous care of a woman who has spent two decades in a house where the shutters are closed at noon and the meals are served at times that her sisters would consider barbaric. The chapel door was propped ajar, exhaling the dense, floral scent of lilies and damp plaster. It was not the smell of her mother, she thought; it was the smell of London in October, which is the smell of extinguished coal and wet wool, and of something else, something that had no name, but which settled on the skin like a fine, cold dust.

Her sisters were already inside, standing in a row before the altar like three dark, unyielding pillars. Eleanor had watched them through the glass of the side door before she entered, their backs rigid, their hatpins catching the gray light that filtered through the high, narrow windows. They belonged here, in this specific, unyielding arrangement of stone and grief. They moved through the ritual with the certainty of people who have never been required to translate themselves. She felt, standing a little behind them, that she was a draft from a foreign room. The words of the service, which should have been her words, fell upon her ears with the muffled, distant quality of a language learned in childhood and half-forgotten. She found herself listening to the vicar’s diction, the round, soft vowels of the Home Counties, and contrasting them with the sharp, nasal clarity of the curé at Saint-Aubin, who would have said the words differently, with a rapidity that swept over the meaning and left only the sound, like water over stones.

Cecily turned her head slightly, just enough to catch Eleanor’s eye, and there was in the movement something so restrained, so carefully polite, that it felt like the turning of a key in a lock. A lock that shut her out. They had kissed, of course, in the hallway of the house in Maida Vale, the house where they had all been born and where their mother had died with the curtains drawn against the afternoon sun. They had touched cheeks, left to right, a gesture Eleanor now performed automatically, having learned it in a country where kisses are currency, where they are spent freely in markets and doorways. But Cecily had stiffened; Eleanor had felt the minute withdrawal, the tightening of the jaw, the instinctive flinch from a familiarity that was not English. It was the gesture of a stranger.

The service concluded. The organ wheezed into life, the notes hanging in the damp air like wet laundry. They walked out into the thin sunlight, and the gravel crunched beneath their feet with a sound that was shockingly familiar, a sound that belonged to the summers of their childhood, to the pushing of dolls’ prams and the scraping of sticks along the iron railings. Eleanor looked at the railings. They were painted black, and the paint was chipped, revealing a dull, rusted brown beneath. She thought of the ironwork on the balcony in Lyon, the intricate, swirling leaves of wrought iron that Jean-Louis had painted green to match the shutters. The green had faded in the sun, leaving a powdery bloom on the surface that came off on her dresses if she brushed against it. She had scrubbed those dresses in the sink, her hands red with the cold water, while the church bells rang across the river. Here, there were no bells, only the distant, hollow roar of the city, a sound that seemed to have no beginning and no end.

They were to go back to the house for the reading of the will, or perhaps simply for tea. Eleanor was not sure. The arrangements had been made in a flurry of letters, the tone of which had been uniformly brisk and faintly accusatory, as if her absence of twenty years had been a deliberate slight, a failure to participate in the family’s slow, inevitable decline. She sat in the back of the motorcar with Margery, who was the youngest and who had been a baby when Eleanor left. Margery sat with her hands folded in her lap, her face turned toward the window, the reflection of the passing trees sliding across her pale cheek like a film of water.

"You’ll find the house changed," Margery said, without turning her head. Her voice was flat, devoid of the rising and falling inflections that Eleanor had grown accustomed to, the musical rise of French, which turns every statement into a question, every assertion into a song.

"Will I?" Eleanor said. She looked at Margery’s hands, which were large and red, the knuckles prominent. They were not the hands of a woman who had played the piano, as their mother had. They were the hands of a woman who had scrubbed floors, perhaps, or who had kneaded dough. Eleanor felt a sudden, sharp pity for her, and then, immediately, a sense of her own falseness, for the pity was tinged with the condescension of the outsider, the one who has escaped and looks back.

"Mama had the conservatory pulled down," Margery continued. "After Father died. She said it was too much trouble to keep the glass clean."

Eleanor nodded. The conservatory, with its lush, overheated air, its scent of earth and decay, its panes of glass that were always misted, had been her favorite place. She had sat in there, reading, her legs tucked beneath her, while the rain drummed on the roof. It had been her refuge, her private jungle. Now it was gone, and with it, some essential part of her childhood, the part that had made the house hers.
