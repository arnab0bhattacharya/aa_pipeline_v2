The light was going; it drained from the corners of the Bloomsbury room, pooling for a moment in the glass of the window before sinking into the grey, damp shroud of the December afternoon. Eleanor sat on the floor, surrounded by the dispersed fragments of a woman’s life. They lay in heaps around her—silks that had slithered to the carpet, heavy wools holding the stiffness of old boards, and fine muslins that seemed barely to tether themselves to the earth. The room was growing cold, the fire in the grate having collapsed into a mound of white ash and occasional, startling sparks, and the gaslight from the streetlamps began to throw long, watery reflections against the ceiling. She pulled a coat towards her; it was a heavy thing, dark green, almost black in the dusk, smelling of damp wool, of damp London evenings, and of the faint, persistent scent of violet cachous that had always seemed, to Eleanor, to be the very odor of her mother’s secrecy.

How odd, she thought, handling the lapel, feeling the thickness of the embroidery that branched over the collar in tight, dark vines, that a woman should be reduced to this. To textiles. To the smell of dried flowers. She had spent the morning sorting the heavy silks, the cashmeres, the shawls that were fringed with tiny, broken threads, and now, as the winter evening shut them in, she felt that she was not merely sorting clothes, but gently unspooling the thread of a life that had ended, leaving her, the daughter, to wind it up. It was a profound unity, this common blood, and yet how little she actually knew of the woman who had worn these garments. She had known her as a mother—majestic, occasionally flustered, always vaguely present in the background of Eleanor’s own frantic childhood—but who had she been before? Before the children, before the illnesses, before the slow accumulation of domestic gravity that had pressed her into the armchair by the fire?

Eleanor thrust her hand into the deep pocket of the green coat. Her fingers met not the smooth lining, but a crumpled, sharp-edged rectangle. She pulled it out. It was a small piece of pasteboard, slightly yellowed, its edges soft from friction. A theatre ticket. The print was faint, smudged by a year of lying forgotten in the dark; *The Lyceum*, it read, and then a date—November the fourteenth, but the year was obscured by a blur of blue ink, looking for all the world like a small, crushed beetle. Eleanor held it to the dying light. 1903? 1908? It might have been any year in that dim, pre-war epoch before she herself was born; an era that seemed now, looking back, to be composed entirely of gaslight, rustling skirts, and a certain breathless, impending joy.

She sat back on her heels, the ticket resting in the palm of her hand, feeling the chill of the floorboards seep through her skirt. A profound silence enveloped the room, punctuated only by the distant, muffled cry of a vendor in the street below, selling something, chestnuts perhaps, his voice rising and falling in a melancholy, rhythmic cadence that seemed to beat against the fog. Eleanor stared at the ticket, and the ticket became a portal; the green coat dissolved, and she was no longer in the Bloomsbury flat, but out in the city, out in that vanished winter.

She tried to imagine the evening. It must have been raining, or perhaps there was a heavy, yellow fog, the kind that turned the city into a sequence of isolated lamps and looming, phantom shapes. Her mother—Clarice, she must force herself to think of her as Clarice—would have been young then. Not the Clarice of the invalid’s sofa, nor the Clarice who presided over the teacups with a slightly distracted, weary grace, but a Clarice who moved. A Clarice who stepped into the light. What had she worn beneath this very coat? Something pale, Eleanor thought, something that would have caught the glare of the carriage lamps as they swept past the Lyceum’s pillars. She imagined the fabric shifting, a glint of satin, the delicate, precarious arch of an ankle as she stepped from the kerb.

And who had she been with? Eleanor’s mind, feeling its way through the dim, unrecorded past, conjured a figure. A man, certainly; perhaps it was Eleanor’s father, but if so, he was a father stripped of his paternal authority, a father reduced to a young man in evening dress, perhaps holding an umbrella at a rakish angle, his face flushed with the cold and the excitement of the play. Or—and here Eleanor’s thought paused, turning aside to peer into a darker, more intriguing alley of speculation—perhaps it was not her father at all. Perhaps it was someone else, someone whose name had never been spoken in the bright, over-exposed daylight of the nursery. There might have been a moment, as the crowd surged through the foyer, when Clarice had looked up, her eyes—were they grey, or that deep, unsettling brown that Eleanor saw in her own mirror?—meeting the eyes of this unknown companion with a look of absolute, unspoken understanding.

They would have pushed through the swing doors, leaving the damp night behind them, entering that cavernous, gilded space that smelled of orange peel, stale perfume, and the heat of a thousand human bodies. Eleanor could almost hear the murmur of the orchestra, the rustle of programs, the sudden, sharp clearing of a throat from the gallery. Clarice would have settled into the velvet seat, the coat folded in her lap, and for three hours she would have been someone else, absorbed into the tragic, magnificent lives of the stage. She might have wept, her tears falling silently, unnoticed by the man beside her, who was perhaps watching the stage, or perhaps, just perhaps, stealing a glance at the pale profile beside him, illuminated by the faint, ghostly glow of the footlights.
