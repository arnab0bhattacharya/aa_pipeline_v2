Lady Evangeline Vane had always held that a dinner party was a work of art, and, like all works of art, it should be appreciated by a public that knew nothing whatsoever about it. To this end, she conceived of a supper that would be, in its own way, as audacious as a novel by Zola and as exclusive as the innermost circle of the Vatican. She would invite only strangers. Not merely people who had not been introduced, which is a simple matter of book-keeping, but souls who had never, in all their lives, cast eyes upon one another. It would be a symphony of first impressions, a table of beautiful, articulate, and entirely unknown quantities. Society, she declared to her butler, was a stagnant pond; she intended to introduce a new and dazzling fish.

The guests arrived at the house in Grosvenor Square under a sky the colour of a bruised plum. First came the Right Reverend Arthur Penhaligon, Bishop of Carthage *in partibus*, whose reputation for asceticism was as luminous as a stained-glass window and whose sermons were known to move men to tears and, occasionally, to subscriptions. He was followed by Miss Lillian Lyle, the actress, a creature of such volatile beauty that she seemed to be composed of equal parts silk, scandal, and sorrow. Then, Mr. Silas Croft, the explorer, entered, bringing with him the scent of camphor and distant continents, his face a mask of sun-cracked leather over a skeleton of formidable will. Finally, there was Julian Alistair Finch, the poet, whose slim volume, *Orchids of the Flesh*, had caused a sensation by suggesting that the only true morality was to be found in the perfect curve of a boy’s neck and the languid scent of a hothouse flower.

Lady Vane, a masterpiece in emerald-green satin, greeted them with the serene confidence of a deity arranging the stars. ‘My dear Bishop,’ she cooed, ‘how good of you to descend from the celestial to the merely fashionable.’ To Miss Lyle, she murmured, ‘Your reputation, my dear, precedes you like a trumpet, but I see it has been beaten from gold leaf.’ And so she went, weaving her web of polished flattery, seating her four perfect strangers as if they were jewels in a new and diabolical crown.

The first course, a consommé of turtle, was consumed in a state of exquisite, wary politeness. The conversation was a marvel of surface tension, each guest skirting the depths with the grace of a dragonfly on a summer pond. The Bishop spoke of the moral decay of the East End; Miss Lyle spoke of the moral decay of the West End. Mr. Croft described the unutterable beauty of a Saharan sunrise, while young Mr. Finch opined that all sunrises were fundamentally vulgar, being a form of public spectacle. They were, as Lady Vane had intended, a collection of beautifully framed portraits, each hung in its own private gallery.

It was the arrival of the turbot, dressed in a delicate shrimp sauce, that proved the undoing of this perfect arrangement. The Bishop, in a moment of pastoral concern, turned to the actress. ‘I trust, Miss Lyle, that the life you lead, for all its glitter, does not leave your soul… unquiet.’

Miss Lyle’s lips, painted the colour of a crushed cherry, curved into a smile that was both a confession and a challenge. ‘My Lord Bishop,’ she replied, her voice a low thrum of amusement, ‘my soul is only unquiet when the house is not full. But you need not trouble yourself. I have found a most excellent confessor in London. A Father Michael, at a small church in Soho. He is remarkably understanding of the… artistic temperament.’

A silence fell, as sudden and as heavy as a velvet curtain. The Bishop’s hand, which had been reaching for his wine glass, froze in mid-air. His face, usually the colour of pale parchment, took on the hue of old rose. For a fleeting, terrible instant, the man of God and the man of the world were at war upon his features. The war was won by the man of the world, who forced his lips into a thin, bloodless line. ‘Indeed,’ he said, his voice a dry rustle of leaves. ‘One hears such names.’

The web was broken. The first thread had been pulled. Mr. Croft, who had been observing the Bishop with the keen eye of a man accustomed to tracking rare and frightened animals, saw his opening. ‘A small church in Soho,’ he mused, as if to himself. ‘A curious place for a great man of the cloth to be found. Almost as curious as finding a man of the cloth in the diamond fields of Kimberley, some ten years ago, when he was not a Bishop at all, but a very persuasive gentleman with a new name and a proposition concerning a certain mine.’

Now it was the Bishop’s turn to be silent. He looked at the explorer as if seeing a ghost risen from a red-earth grave. The explorer, in turn, looked at Miss Lyle. ‘And I must confess, Miss Lyle,’ he said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, ‘that I saw you long before I saw you tonight. Not on the stage of the Lyceum, but in the stateroom of a P&O liner. You were travelling to Calcutta with a certain lord, and you were not known as Lillian Lyle, but as the wife of a rather dull manufacturer from Manchester.’

Miss Lyle did not even have the grace to blush. She simply laughed, a silvery, reckless sound. ‘My dear Mr. Croft, a girl must have a past. It is the only thing of value she can acquire for herself. But do tell me,’ she added, her eyes glittering with malice, ‘was it in Kimberley that you lost the fingers on your left hand? Or was that a more recent adventure, say, in the private study of a certain Royal Duke, whose collection of… unique artefacts you were so keen to liberate?’
