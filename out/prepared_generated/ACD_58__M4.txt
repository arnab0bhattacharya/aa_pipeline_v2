The rain had ceased but the pavements still shone like wet slate, and a thin yellow fog was curling up from the river to meet the dusk as I sat before the fire in our sitting-room in Baker Street, turning over the leaves of a medical journal which had long since ceased to interest me. Holmes, who had been silent for the better part of an hour, was engaged in the delicate operation of transferring a pinch of some grey powder from a folded paper to a glass slide, and the stillness was broken only by the hiss of the coals and the distant rumble of a cab in the Marylebone Road.

It was upon this quiet that there came a ring at the bell, sharp and peremptory, followed by a heavy tread upon the stairs and a tap at our door so timid that it seemed curiously at odds with the urgency of the summons below.

“Come in,” said Holmes, without looking up.

The man who entered was of middle age and stoutly built, dressed in decent black, with a tall hat which he held before him in both hands as though it were an offering. His face was ruddy and honest, but there was about his eyes a troubled, sidelong look, and his collar was damp with the sweat of a man who has walked far and fast upon an errand he dreads.

“Mr. Holmes?” said he. “Mr. Sherlock Holmes, the inquiry agent?”

“I am Sherlock Holmes, and this is my friend and colleague, Dr. Watson. Pray be seated, Mr. —”

“Cribb, sir. Josiah Cribb, of Cribb and Sons, undertakers, of the Essex Road, Islington. I would not have troubled you, sir, only I am at my wits’ end, and there is no one else to whom a man in my trade can go without making himself the laughing-stock of the parish.”

“You interest me already,” said Holmes, laying aside his slide and waving our visitor to the basket-chair. “An undertaker who fears ridicule is a novelty. Tell me your story from the beginning, and spare no detail, however trifling it may appear to you.”

Mr. Cribb sat forward upon the edge of the chair, turning his hat slowly in his fingers. “It is about a funeral, sir — a funeral that I conducted this very afternoon at the cemetery off the Holloway Road. The deceased was a lady, a Mrs. Emmeline Voss, widow, of no great age, who had lived very retired in a house in Barnsbury. She died a fortnight since, and the funeral was arranged by her nephew, a Mr. Lambert, who is her only surviving relative and who has been staying at the house. There was no doctor in attendance at the end, and the certificate was given by an elderly physician who had not seen her for a month — but that is common enough, and it is not my business to question it.”

“Go on.”

“The coffin was ordered of plain oak, and I measured the lady myself before she was laid out. She was a small woman, sir, and lightly built — I should not have said above seven stone. Yet when the coffin was brought down and set upon the trestles in the hall, I give you my word, Mr. Holmes, I could scarce lift the head of it with my own hands, and my son, who is a strong lad of two-and-twenty, staggered under the foot. I have handled a mortuary these five-and-thirty years, and I know to a pound what a coffin should weigh.”

Holmes’s languor had fallen from him as a cloak falls from the shoulders. He sat erect, his thin fingers pressed together at his lips, his grey eyes fixed upon the undertaker with an intentness that made the man falter.

“You weighed it?”

“I did not weigh it in scales, sir, but I have a practised arm, and I will swear before any magistrate that the coffin held near upon three hundredweight. It was all my son and I could do to get it upon the bier.”

“And you spoke to the family?”

“I did, sir, and that is the part that troubles me. I suggested, respectful, that if the lady had been ailing long there might be some dropsy or swelling of the tissues, and that perhaps the family would wish the lid unscrewed for a moment, that I might satisfy myself and them that all was as it should be. Mr. Lambert turned upon me very sharp and white in the face, and said that his aunt was to be buried without delay, that the weather was warm, and that he would have no prying into what did not concern me. And the funeral, which was set for eleven this morning, was hurried forward to nine, before the other mourners — there were but two, a man and a woman in deep black, whom I had never seen before — had well arrived. We were at the graveside by half-past nine, and they would not even wait for the parson to finish the committal before they were moving away.”

“And the grave is filled?”

“It was filling when I left, sir. The sexton is an old man and slow, and I came straight from the cemetery to you. I have heard of you, Mr. Holmes, from my brother-in-law, who was in the force at Bow — and I thought that if there was any wickedness, it was better that it should be looked into before the earth was trodden down and the whole thing made fast for ever.”

Holmes sprang to his feet and took down his hat and his heavy walking-stick from the rack.

“You have done rightly, Mr. Cribb — very rightly indeed. Watson, your coat and your revolver, if you please. There is not an instant to be lost. The sexton may be slow, but the nephew may be quicker. We shall take a hansom, and I pray that we may find the grave still open.”
