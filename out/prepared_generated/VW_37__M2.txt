The light came in through the dormer window, slanting and dusty, a pale blade that cut the air of the attic studio into two distinct worlds: the one where Maud stood, holding her brush, trembling with the weight of the unsaid, and the one where the canvas waited, white and absolute, demanding something that did not exist. It was a Tuesday, or perhaps a Wednesday; time had become a viscous thing up here, thick and slow, pooling in the corners like the varnish she had spilled the day before. Below, in the kitchen, the clatter of plates and the sharp, rhythmic chopping of Mrs. Halloway’s knife against the wooden board rose up to her, a sound so domestic, so final, that it felt less like a meal being prepared and more like the closing of a door.

"You are wasting your time," Mrs. Halloway had said that morning, her voice muffled by the thickness of the wool shawl she always wrapped around her neck, as if the damp of Hampstead were a personal enemy she had to fend off with fabric. She had been standing in the doorway of the attic, looking up at Maud with an expression of such profound pity that it was worse than anger. "Mr. Thorne says you have no eye. And I say you have no future. It is better to marry, Maud. It is better to be a wife. A wife does not have to be right; she only has to be present."

Maud had not answered then. She had only looked at her hands, stained with cadmium yellow and ultramarine blue, the colors of a life she was not permitted to live. Mr. Thorne’s face had flashed before her now, in the grey light of the attic. He had been sitting in the studio at the Royal Academy, his back straight, his mouth thin and severe. "You paint like a child," he had said, turning the small sketch she had offered him over in his fingers, inspecting it as one might inspect a broken hinge. "The lines are hesitant. The form is lost. There is no structure here, Miss Maud. There is only... noise. You must stop."

*Stop.* The word hung in the air, heavy and suffocating. It was the same word as *marry*, the same word as *obey*. It was a command to cease, to fold oneself into a smaller shape, to fit inside the confines of a marriage bed or a domestic role, rather than the vast, terrifying expanse of the canvas. And yet, here she was, in the attic, where the air smelled of turpentine and old dust, painting.

She dipped her brush. The bristles were stiff, dry, but when they touched the wet paint, they softened, becoming a part of her, an extension of her nerve endings. She began to paint the window, not the window as it was, but the window as it felt. The light was not merely white; it was a presence, a warm, golden pressure against the glass. She mixed the colors, adding a touch of vermillion to the yellow, a whisper of violet to the blue, until the paint on the palette swirled into a new, impossible hue. It was the color of memory, she thought. The color of the afternoon she had spent in the park, watching the leaves turn, when the world had seemed infinite and she had felt, for the first time, that she might be able to hold it in her hands.

But the world was not infinite. The world was Mrs. Halloway’s chopping board, the sound of the rain against the roof, the ticking of the clock in the hallway below. The clock was a relentless, mechanical heartbeat, counting down the seconds until the engagement was announced, until the dress was bought, until the life she had dreamed of in the quiet hours of the night was buried under layers of lace and silence.

Maud painted faster. Her hand moved across the canvas, a blur of motion, the brush a bird in flight. She was painting the view from the window, but it was not the street below, with its grey pavements and huddled figures. It was a landscape of her own making, a place where the trees were made of light and the sky was a deep, velvety blue. It was a place where Mr. Thorne did not exist, where Mrs. Halloway’s pity was a distant, irrelevant sound. It was a place where she was free.

The light changed. The slant of the sun shifted, moving across the floorboards, illuminating the dust motes that danced in the air. Maud paused, her brush hovering above the canvas. She looked at the painting. It was not good. It was not what Mr. Thorne would call good. It was not structured, not precise, not correct. But it was alive. It breathed. It was the only thing in this house, in this city, in this world, that was truly real.

A knock at the attic door. Sharp, impatient.

"Maud!" Mrs. Halloway’s voice, muffled but urgent. "Come down. The car is here. We are going to the shop."

The car. The shop. The dress. The future, arriving in a leather-wrapped box, ready to be unwrapped and worn. Maud looked at the canvas. The paint was still wet, glistening in the dim light. It was a secret, a small, rebellious act of existence. She could not show it. She could not let them see it. If they saw it, they would see the truth of her, and the truth was too much for them to bear.

She turned away from the window, her back to the light, facing the darkness of the room. She put down her brush. The silence of the attic was not empty; it was full of the sound of her own breathing, of the rain, of the clock below. She walked to the door, her feet bare on the cold floorboards. She opened it.
