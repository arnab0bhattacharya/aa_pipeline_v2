The rain had ceased, but the pavements of Kensington still shone like wet slate, and a thin, chill vapour rose from the roadway as though the very stones were breathing. It was upon such an evening that Mrs. Eleanor Vane was shown into our sitting-room in Baker Street, and I remember that Holmes, who had been scraping at his violin with an air of profound melancholy, laid the instrument aside with a readiness which told me that he had been awaiting her coming with some impatience.

She was a woman of perhaps five-and-forty, dressed in the deep mourning which becomes a widow, and she carried in her hand a small morocco case such as a lady might use for her visiting cards. Her face was pale and composed, but there was about her eyes that strained, watchful expression which I have learned to associate with a mind long exercised by a single anxious thought.

“You are very good to receive me, Mr. Holmes,” said she, when she had taken the chair which he pushed forward. “I am aware that my business must seem trifling to a man accustomed to great affairs, but I have no one else to whom I can turn.”

“Madam,” said Holmes, “there is no case so trifling that it may not repay attention. Pray let me hear the particulars.”

She opened the morocco case and laid upon the table before him a sheet of white blotting-paper, upon which reposed a number of pressed flowers, perhaps a dozen in all, each flattened and faded to a delicate brown, yet preserved with evident care.

“These have come to me,” said she, “by the post, one every week for the past twelve weeks. Each is enclosed in a plain envelope, addressed in a hand I do not know, and each bears the postmark of a different county. At first I believed it to be some foolish hoax, or perhaps the kindness of an old friend of my husband’s who did not choose to give his name. But they have continued without intermission, and I confess that I have come to dread the day of their arrival.”

Holmes had drawn the paper towards him and was examining the flowers through his lens with the swift, methodical scrutiny of a naturalist.

“You are a botanist yourself, madam?” said he.

“I know a little of the subject. My late husband, Colonel Vane, was a keen collector. It was his chief pleasure in his retirement. He had a great herbarium, and these—” she indicated the paper with a slender finger—“these are all of them specimens which he himself gathered and pressed. I recognise his method of mounting them. He used always this particular grey paper for the drying.”

“Indeed.” Holmes’s eyes had brightened. “And you say that each comes from a different county?”

“Yes. I have kept the envelopes.” She produced them from her reticule and spread them out upon the table. “There is one from Devon, one from Somerset, one from Dorset, one from Wiltshire, and so on. They form a most irregular line.”

Holmes sprang from his chair and went to the great map of England which hung upon the wall. He took the envelopes one by one and, with a pin, marked the postmark upon the map. I rose and stood beside him, and I saw that the pins, when they were all placed, described a curious and unmistakable track—a wandering course from the western coast of Cornwall, northward and eastward through Devon, Somerset, and Wiltshire, then into Berkshire and Oxfordshire, and so at last into the home counties.

“You observe, Watson,” said Holmes, with something of his old eagerness. “The route is not haphazard. It follows the great western road, and it turns aside only where a town of any size offers a post office. It is a journey, madam—a journey taken on foot or by the slower carriers, and made in stages of some twenty miles a week.”

Mrs. Vane had risen and was gazing at the map with a face from which every trace of colour had fled.

“That was his journey,” she whispered. “The year before he died, he was persuaded by his physician to take a walking tour for his health. He was away six months, and he wrote to me from every place at which he stopped. He was a great walker, Mr. Holmes. He said that a man who walks sees what a man who rides never dreams of.”

“And did he travel alone?”

“He took with him a young man, a Mr. Edgar Prendergast, who had been his orderly in the regiment and who afterwards acted as his secretary. He was devoted to my husband—or so I believed.”

Holmes was silent for a moment, his fingers pressed together, his eyes fixed upon the map.

“Tell me, madam,” said he at last, “how your husband died.”

“He died of a seizure of the heart, in his own study, upon the fourteenth of October last. The physician was quite clear as to the cause. He had suffered from a weakness of the heart for some years.”

“And Mr. Prendergast?”

“He left us a month after the funeral. He said that he could not bear the house without the Colonel. I have not seen him since.”

“Did your husband make a will?”

“He did. He left me the house and a life interest in his estate, with the reversion to his nephew, a young man for whom he had no great affection. But there was one clause which I did not understand at the time, and which has troubled me since. He left to Mr. Prendergast a sum of five hundred pounds, and also—and this is the strange part—‘the box of botanical specimens marked with the letter P, now in the cabinet in the study.’”

Holmes turned sharply from the map.

“And have you examined this box?”

“I have not. It is sealed, and I had not the heart to open it. It lies still in the cabinet where he placed it.”
