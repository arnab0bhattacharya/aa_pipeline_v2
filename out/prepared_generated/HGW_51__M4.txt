My dear Father,

I write to you from London at last, having survived the railway journey from the coast, and I must tell you at once that I am convinced the English are a race of sorcerers who have enslaved a tribe of docile and invisible giants. I do not say this in jest. I have seen the thing with my own eyes, and I am not ashamed to confess that I was compelled to sit down upon a wooden bench in the station and hold my head in my hands until the trembling passed.

The train itself is a case in point. You will recall how we laughed, you and I, over the traveller's tale of the iron horse, and how you said that men who lived in stone boxes and ate their bread from metal plates would believe anything. I owe you an apology, Father. The horse is real, and it is not a horse at all, but a sort of elongated kettle upon wheels, which breathes fire and shrieks like a woman in labour. It drew us through the countryside at a speed I should not have credited had I not counted the hedgerows myself. The noise is the chief thing. It is not a noise one can describe by comparing it to anything in the valley. It is a noise that gets inside your chest and shakes the bones of you, and when it stops, the silence is more alarming still, for you fancy your ears have been struck dead and will never recover.

I reached the house of Mr. Hartley, the solicitor, who is to be my guardian in matters of business, at about four in the afternoon. He is a small, brisk man with a face like a well-scrubbed potato, and he greeted me with a great deal of hand-shaking and a stream of questions which he did not wait to have answered. He has a wife and two daughters, who looked at my clothes with an expression I can only describe as the expression our people wear when they are shown a thing that is not quite clean. I have since learned that this is the English manner of being polite.

But I must tell you of the machines, for they are the thing that has most disturbed me. In the valley, when we wish to lift a stone, we lift it. When we wish to grind grain, we grind it. Here, it seems, no man will do anything for himself if he can persuade a machine to do it for him. There is a machine in the Hartley kitchen that turns a handle and beats eggs; there is another that boils water by passing a current of the invisible fire they call electricity through a wire; and in the street there is a great clattering apparatus that sweeps the mud from the road by means of revolving brushes, drawn by a horse who seems as weary of it as I am. The people walk about among these contrivances as calmly as our women walk among the goats, and they do not seem to hear the din at all. I asked the younger Miss Hartley how she could endure it, and she looked at me as though I had asked her how she could endure the sky.

The streets are worse than the machines. I went out this morning with the intention of finding the postal office, and I had not gone fifty paces before I was obliged to press myself against a wall to avoid being run down by a carriage. Then a cart came, and then a great red omnibus with an advertisement for soap upon its side, and then a man on a bicycle, ringing a bell with the fury of a madman. I stood there for some minutes, I am afraid, with my mouth open, and I am conscious that several persons turned to stare at me. One of them, a boy of perhaps twelve years, called out something which I did not catch, but which the other boys found very amusing. I have since been told that it is a form of greeting.

The English, Father, are a tribe of extraordinary customs. They do not, for instance, greet a stranger by offering him food. They offer him a piece of paper with their name upon it, which they call a card, and they seem to attach great importance to the manner in which it is presented. They eat their principal meal at an hour when we should be thinking of sleep, and they eat it in a room so brightly lit that one might suppose the sun had been captured and imprisoned in glass globes. They have a curious horror of silence. At the Hartley table, if there is a pause of more than a moment, someone will remark upon the weather, or ask whether the trains are running to time, as though silence were a wild beast that must be kept at bay with a constant throwing of words.

I have begun my studies at the college, and here I find a different sort of Englishman. These are the men who make the machines, or rather who understand them, for I am told that the actual making is done by a class of men who are never seen in polite society. My tutor is a Mr. Ellsworth, a thin, dry man with spectacles and a habit of tapping his teeth with his pencil while he thinks. He asked me what I knew of mathematics, and I told him what you had taught me, and he seemed surprised. He said that it was a very sound foundation, and that I had been fortunate in my instruction. I did not tell him that you had taught me by drawing figures in the dust with a stick, for I feared he would not understand.
