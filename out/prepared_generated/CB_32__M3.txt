The town of Keldmore lay in a hollow of the West Riding, a bleak and stony place, hedged about by moors whose heather was beaten flat by ceaseless winds. It was a town of grey stone and blackened brick, where the rain seemed to fall with a peculiar slant, driving into the very mortar of the walls. In this unpropitious spot I had planted my standard. I had taken a lease on a narrow house in Silver Street, and with the small hoard amassed through five years of governessing, had opened a day-school for girls. I had no patron but my own resolution; no friends but the few books and meagre furniture I had conveyed thither. I was but four-and-twenty, plain of face, and wholly unprovided with those arts of pleasing which win favour for softer natures. Yet I knew my own capability, and I relied on it. My scholars were few at first—only six, the daughters of tradesmen and the smaller yeomen—but they were taught with a rigour and a care that I knew would tell in time.

For a brief season, I believed the frost had broken. The spring brought warmer light into my schoolroom, and with it came two more pupils. I permitted myself to hope. I had tasted the bitter bread of dependence in other households; I had felt the degradation of being an appendage to arrogant wealth; and now, in this humble station, I felt the dignity of self-reliance. I was no longer a servant, but a teacher—accountable to none but God and my own conscience.

It was in the month of June that the blow fell. I was returning from the market, carrying a parcel of slates and copybooks, when I perceived a notice affixed to the door of the vacant house directly opposite mine. I crossed the street, my heart beating with a presentiment of evil, and read the words: *Keldmore Church School for Young Ladies, under the Patronage of the Reverend Edmund Vane, Rector.* Below, in smaller text: *Miss Arabella Vane, Principal.*

I stood as one stricken. The house opposite had been empty since March; I had rejoiced in its emptiness, for it afforded me a clear view of the hill beyond, and some illusion of space. Now it was to become the theatre of my ruin. I knew Mr. Vane by reputation—a man of cold intellect and warm ambition, who ruled his parish as a little kingdom, and brooked no rival in any enterprise that touched his influence. That his sister should open a school across the way was no coincidence. It was a deliberate act of war.

Within a fortnight, the house was fitted with new desks, fresh curtains, and a handsome pianoforte. A board was erected above the door, painted in fair letters. Scholars began to arrive—drawn not merely by the Vanes' consequence, but by the promise of instruction in music, drawing, and French, accomplishments which I could not pretend to offer with any elegance. My own scholars began to look across the street with wistful eyes. I read their thoughts as plainly as if they had spoken: *Why should we sit in this narrow room, copying sums and parsing English, when yonder there are songs and pictures and a young lady who wears ribbons?*

I will not say I was calm. I was not constituted for calmness in the face of injustice. A fire rose in me—a fierce, scorching indignation that I had to choke down with both hands, lest it consume all restraint. I had built this thing from nothing. I had denied myself warmth, meat, even adequate fire in winter, to save the money that now stared back at me in the form of slate and plaster. And now, because a rector willed it, I was to be starved out.

I resolved, at first, to hold my ground. I would not beg. To beg of Mr. Vane would be to acknowledge his right to crush me, and that right I denied with every fibre of my being. I would not close. To close would be to confess defeat before the battle was joined. I would compete. I retrenched my expenses yet further. I dismissed the charwoman who cleaned the stairs and undertook that labour myself before dawn. I sat late into the night preparing lessons of such thoroughness that no scholar under Miss Vane's roof could match them. I told myself that solid learning would prevail over showy accomplishments, as light prevails over darkness.

But I had not reckoned with the power of patronage. One by one, my pupils withdrew. The mothers came to me with soft words and averted eyes, speaking of "advantage" and "connection" and "what Mr. Vane so strongly advises." Each departure was a small death. I stood at my window and watched them cross the street—literally cross the street, with their satchels and their tidy pelisses—and enter the door of my rival. I pressed my forehead against the cold glass and felt the chill sink into my skull. I did not weep. I had trained myself against weeping years ago, in a darker place, under a crueller sky. But the dryness of my eyes was more terrible than any flood.

It was October when I at last acknowledged that competition was futile. Three scholars remained, and of these, two paid only a pittance. I sat at my desk after they had gone for the day, and looked at the empty room, and listened to the wind moan down the chimney. The fire was low; I could not afford more coals. A Bible lay on the shelf beside me, and my eye fell upon a passage I had marked in earlier days: *The foxes have holes, and the birds of the air have nests; but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head.* I was not the Son of man. I was a poor, proud, solitary woman, and I had nowhere else to lay my head but here, in this failing school, among these bare walls.
