The steamer shuddered, a low, guttural groan that seemed to rise from the belly of the earth itself, and then the world tilted. It was not a violent motion, but a suggestion, a leaning of the horizon line, as if the fjord were a great green eye narrowing in the light. The water, that deep, untroubled slate-grey, held the cliffs in a mirror so perfect, so silent, that one could not tell where the stone ended and the sky began. It was a trick of the light, a dissolution of boundaries that made the mind feel as though it were floating, untethered from the floorboards, from the smell of coal smoke and damp wool, from the specific, heavy reality of the body.

Arthur sat by the window, his hands resting on the back of the empty seat beside him. He was a man of a certain age, his face a map of lines that had been carved not by wind and sun alone, but by the long, quiet hours of a life lived in the shadow of another. He watched the water. It was not blue, never truly blue; it was the color of old iron, of bruised plums, of the depth of a well. And in that depth, he saw, or thought he saw, the ghost of a face. It was not a face, of course. It was merely the reflection of the cloud, the distortion of the wave, the play of light upon the dark. But for a moment, the illusion held. It was a momentary suspension of time, a breath held in the lungs of the afternoon.

Across the aisle, separated by a row of stiff-backed chairs and the rustling of program notes, sat Miss Elspeth Vance. She was a woman who had never married, a fact that hung in the air between them like a scent, distinct and unexplained. She was reading a volume of poetry, her lips moving slightly, shaping the words into silence. Arthur had noticed her before, in the way one notices a stone in a stream—not with intent, but because it catches the light. She was small, precise, her hair pinned up in a manner that suggested a mind tightly ordered, a life carefully curated. There was a stillness about her that mirrored the fjord, a stillness that was not empty, but full.

The younger members of the party, a cluster of bright, restless energy, were engaged in a dispute that echoed through the cabin. It was about poetry. One of them, a young man with a sunburned nose, was declaring that verse was a dead language, a relic of a time when men wore swords and spoke in riddles. Another, a girl with large, questioning eyes, was defending the old forms, arguing that rhythm was the heartbeat of the soul. Their voices were high, shrill, cutting through the heavy air of the cabin. They did not know that their words were already fading, that the moment they were spoken was already gone, dissolved into the steam and the noise of the engine.

Arthur turned his head. He had not meant to look at Miss Vance, but his gaze drifted, drawn by the stillness of her. She had closed her book. Her eyes were fixed on the water, but they did not seem to see it. They were looking inward, into some deep, private well. And then, as if by some invisible pull, she turned her head. Their eyes met. It was a brief, electric connection, a spark in the dark. She looked at him with an expression that was not quite surprise, but a recognition, as if she had been waiting for him to look.

"Is it beautiful?" she asked. Her voice was soft, low, barely above a whisper. It cut through the noise of the young people, through the groan of the engine, and landed in Arthur’s heart with the precision of a stone dropped into still water.

He did not answer immediately. He looked back at the water. The cliffs were moving, slowly, inevitably. The glacier, that white, silent sentinel at the end of the fjord, was coming into view. It was vast, terrible, indifferent. It had stood there for millennia, watching the water change, the sky turn, the light fade. It did not care for the young people’s arguments, nor for the widower’s grief, nor for the spinster’s solitude. It simply was.

"Yes," Arthur said. "It is beautiful."

Miss Vance nodded. She did not smile, but her face softened, the lines around her eyes relaxing. "But it is cold," she said. "Isn’t it? That is what I think. It is beautiful, but it is cold."

Arthur looked at her. He saw, in that moment, a kinship. They were both observers, both standing on the bank of the river of life, watching the current flow. They had not been swept away. They had remained, fixed, watching. And in that watching, they had found a kind of peace.

The steamer turned, the water churning beneath the hull, and the glacier loomed larger. It was a wall of white, jagged and ancient. The light caught its surface, turning it into a diamond, a crystal, a thing of pure, cold light. The young people fell silent. Even the sharpest of them stopped speaking, awed by the scale of the thing. The noise of the engine seemed to fade, replaced by the sound of the water lapping against the stone, a soft, rhythmic sound, like a heartbeat.

Arthur and Miss Vance did not speak again. They did not need to. The conversation had reached its limit, its natural end. Words were clumsy tools, inadequate for the task of describing what they felt. What they felt was not a feeling, exactly. It was a state of being. A presence. A shared silence.
