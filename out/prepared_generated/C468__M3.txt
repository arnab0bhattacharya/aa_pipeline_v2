The letter arrived not by post but by the hand of a footman in the Duchess of Sutcliffe's private livery, a quiet arrogance of delivery that Arthur Pendelton noted and admired even as he broke the black wax seal. The invitation was to take tea the following afternoon at Sutcliffe House, and though it bore no explicit purpose, Arthur knew precisely what it portended. For six months he had charmed, argued, and maneuvered his way into the fringes of the Duchess's political circle, that shadow cabinet of drawing rooms and dinner tables where the real governance of England was decided over cold pheasant and warm port. He was twenty-eight, the youngest barrister in recent memory to argue before the Court of Appeal, and he had done it not by any brute brilliance of legal reasoning alone but by a quickness of manner, a certain grace under cross-examination that made judges feel clever for agreeing with him. He had risen, in short, by making himself indispensable and delightful in equal measure, and now the Duchess, who collected promising young men the way other ladies collected Sèvres porcelain, had apparently decided he was ready for a higher shelf.

He dressed with care for the visit, not in his courtroom blacks but in a well-cut grey frock coat that a colleague had once told him made his shoulders look broader than they were. The hansom cab rattled through the November drizzle past the great facades of Mayfair, and Arthur sat with his gloves folded in his lap and his heart beating with an agitation he refused to name. Sutcliffe House rose behind its iron railings like a small and magnificent government building, all Portland stone and tall windows that looked out upon the square with an expression of serene and unassailable authority. He was shown not into the drawing room but into the Duchess's private study, a room he had entered only once before and which still struck him with the force of its intimacy. The shelves were lined not with decorative bindings but with real books, Hansard volumes and legal reports and the unbound pamphlets of reform societies, and the desk was scattered with correspondence in a hand so sharp and angular it seemed to cut the paper. The Duchess herself stood by the fire, and the sight of her struck him as it always did, with a quality of recognition that was almost painful.

Cordelia, Duchess of Sutcliffe, was forty-three years old, a widow of seven years, and one of the two or three women in England whose opinion the Prime Minister was known to solicit before reshuffling his cabinet. She was not beautiful in the fashionable sense. Her face was too angular, her jaw too decided, her dark hair too severely arranged for the soft prettiness that the illustrated papers admired. But she possessed a quality of attention that was almost physical in its intensity, a way of looking at a person that made them feel, for the duration of her gaze, that they were the only object in the world worth examining, and when she turned that gaze upon Arthur, as she did now, he felt the familiar vertigo, the sense of standing at the edge of a great height and wanting very much to fall.

"Mr. Pendelton," she said, and extended her hand. "Thank you for coming."

"Your Grace," he said, and bowed over it, and the brief pressure of her gloved fingers against his own sent a current through him that he was certain she must have felt, though her expression did not change.

They sat. Tea was poured and dismissed with a wave that sent the maid from the room. The Duchess regarded him across the small distance of the tea table with an expression that combined warmth with the practical directness of a general reviewing a map.

"I have a proposition for you," she said. "The seat for East Holcombe will be vacant by spring. Alderman Priddy is retiring on account of his lungs, and the association is mine to manage. I can place you there, Arthur. I can place you in Parliament by the summer recess, and from there, with the right alliances and a moderate share of luck, I see no reason you should not be in ministerial office within three years."

Arthur set down his cup with a care that belied the trembling in his wrist. He had imagined this conversation, rehearsed it in the private theatre of his bath, but the reality of it was different, was larger, was lit by the firelight that played across the Duchess's face and caught the faint lines at the corners of her eyes, lines that he loved because they deepened when she smiled, and she was smiling now, a little, with the pleasure of offering a gift she knew he wanted.

"Your Grace," he began, but she raised her hand.

"There is a condition." She paused, and in that pause Arthur felt the room contract around him. "You must marry."

The word hung in the air between them like a held breath.

"Marry," he repeated.

"My niece, Helena. You have met her." It was not a question. He had met Helena Marchmont at three dinners and one garden party in the course of the summer, a girl of twenty with fair hair and wide eyes and a manner of agreeable docility that he found neither appealing nor repellent but simply inert, like a portrait one acknowledges and passes. She was pretty in the way that porcelain figurines were pretty, flawless and still, and Arthur had thought of her not at all between meetings, except to note that she watched her aunt with an adoration that bordered on worship.

"She is a charming young woman," he said carefully, and the Duchess nodded as though he had answered a question in an examination.
