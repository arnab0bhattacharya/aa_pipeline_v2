The news of Mr. Wynford’s misfortune reached his family not by direct confession, but by letter. It was delivered from London by his man of business, and opened by Mrs. Wynford at the breakfast-table, under the very eyes of her three daughters and a visiting cousin. The letter was long, and the perusal of it drew from that lady a succession of sighs so artfully modulated as to command the full attention of the room without quite descending into the vulgar spectacle of distress. When she had finished, she laid the paper down with an air of grave resignation, and observed that they must all prepare themselves for a very material change in their style of living.

Mr. Wynford, it appeared, had entrusted a considerable portion of his capital to a canal venture in the north, a scheme promising unparalleled returns, and had been rewarded with a bankruptcy that swallowed up a third of his annual income. He was already on his way to Barrow Park, their seat in Wiltshire, bringing with him the melancholy conviction that some retrenchment was unavoidable.

Mrs. Wynford heard the word retrenchment as a lady of her sensibility might hear the name of a distant, disreputable relation—she acknowledged the connection, but refused to receive it into her house. She bore her husband’s absence with a composure that spoke well of her nerves, if not of her heart, and applied herself at once to the immediate and pressing question of what was to be kept, and what might, with a very good grace, be parted with. The carriage, she declared, was not to be thought of. She could not, she would not, give up the carriage. A family of their standing in the county, with three daughters unwed, must keep up its appearance. To be seen walking into Devizes, or to be obliged to borrow a neighbour’s gig, was a degradation she could not endure.

“My dear Mrs. Wynford,” said Mrs. Leake, the visiting cousin, a widow of narrow means and comfortable self-importance, who was staying with them for a fortnight, “you are perfectly right. The carriage is the last thing one ought to part with. People judge so much by these things. If they see you without a carriage, they will suppose the whole family is ruined, and then where will your girls be?”

Mrs. Wynford looked gratified, and said she was sure Mrs. Leake felt for her situation as a mother.

Mr. Wynford arrived the following evening, looking harassed and older than his years, but disposed, to his credit, to face the matter with less equivocation than his lady. He spoke of the canal with a bitterness which the consciousness of his own imprudence rather heightened than relieved, and entered at once upon the subject of their debts. He had been sanguine, he acknowledged; the prospectus had been flattering, and Sir Henry Vane, whose judgement in such matters was generally trusted, had been deeply concerned in the undertaking. But the canal had failed, the shares were worthless, and a third of his income was gone. He must sell some land, perhaps discharge a servant or two, and certainly live more narrowly.

“And the carriage?” said Mrs. Wynford, in a tone which, if not peremptory, was at least very far from submissive.

Mr. Wynford hesitated. He was a man who could face a creditor with tolerable courage, but who quailed before his wife’s displeasure. He looked at his daughters, as if hoping one of them might speak, and relieve him of the necessity of deciding.

“The carriage,” he said at last, “is a heavy expense. The horses, the coachman, the keeping—”

“It is not to be thought of,” said Mrs. Wynford, with a finality that closed the subject as neatly as a well-constructed sentence. “We may give up the London season, if we must. We may reduce our table. We may even, if your father insists,” she added, turning to her daughters with a smile that cost her something, “dispense with the upper housemaids. But the carriage remains.”

Mr. Wynford said no more, and the carriage was, for the present, preserved.

The eldest Miss Wynford, a young woman of seven-and-twenty, whose good sense was rather more relied upon than acknowledged by her family, had listened to the whole discussion in silence. Charlotte Wynford was not handsome, and had long since ceased to expect to be thought so; but she possessed a clearness of head which, had she been a man, would have made her an excellent manager of an estate, and which, being a woman, made her an excellent manager of a household. She had already, before her father had finished speaking, begun to calculate. The carriage cost, she knew, upwards of two hundred pounds a year, including the coachman’s wages, the horses’ keep, and the repairs which a vehicle of some age and little merit required with increasing frequency. Two hundred pounds was a sum not to be disregarded, when one’s income was reduced by a thousand. But she said nothing. She had lived long enough in her mother’s house to know that opposition, however reasonable, was seldom so effectual as compliance, followed by a quiet and gradual arrangement of circumstances to one’s own design.

She began, that very evening, to consider which of the family’s expenses might be reduced, without exciting her mother’s alarm or her sisters’ resentment. The London season she dismissed at once; it was the most obvious sacrifice, and the one least likely to be regretted by anyone but Mrs. Wynford, who valued it chiefly for the visits it enabled her to pay, and the visits she received in return. The servants might be reduced by two, without any great inconvenience, provided the dismissal was managed with discretion, and the remaining maids were not given to gossip. The wine cellar, which Mr. Wynford had stocked with more liberality than judgement, might be drawn upon more sparingly; and the education of the youngest girl, who was still at a rather expensive school in Bath, might be completed at home, under Charlotte’s own superintendence.
