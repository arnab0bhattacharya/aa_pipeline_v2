The grey light of a lowering afternoon fell upon the harbour of Penhale, and found no vessel to gladden with its beams; for the boats lay upturned upon the shingle, their keels bleached and cracked by many winters, and the water itself, which had once been the highway of a thousand keels, now crept sluggishly among the blackened ribs of ships that would never sail again. It was a place of silence and of graves; and yet, upon this day, there moved among the ruins a little company of the living, who had come down to the water's edge with a purpose so desperate, so improbable, that the very gulls, wheeling above them, seemed to scream their derision at the folly of mankind.

Elinor Vane stood apart from the others, her cloak drawn close about her slender form, and her eyes fixed upon the western horizon, where the sun, sinking through a bank of sullen cloud, cast a path of blood-red light across the heaving waters. She was young—scarce three-and-twenty—yet sorrow had already set its seal upon her countenance, and there was in her gaze that mingled tenderness and resolution which belongs to those who have loved much and suffered more. Beside her stood her husband, Adam, a man of grave and earnest aspect, whose hand rested upon the gunwale of the little vessel that was to bear them across that vast and unknown expanse which had once been called the Atlantic, but which now, in the wreck of all human knowledge, was known only as the Dark Water—a name that carried with it a terror beyond words, for none who had ventured upon it had ever returned to tell of what they found.

"Thou art silent, Elinor," said Adam, in a low voice, and his tone was tender with that anxious affection which fear makes doubly dear. "Dost thou repent thee of our enterprise? It is not yet too late to turn back; and though I should grieve to see thee suffer, yet I would rather endure the pang of disappointed hope than behold thee exposed to perils which I cannot share."

She turned to him with a smile so sad, so full of lofty courage, that it might have inspired a coward to heroism. "Nay, Adam," she answered, "repentance is for those who have acted against the light of their own consciences; and I have no such cause. When I look upon this desolate land, where our fathers perished and our children shall never be born—when I behold the ruins of all that was great and good, and feel that we alone, perchance, of all the human race, survive to perpetuate the name of man—I am ready to encounter any danger, to brave any tempest, so that I may learn whether there yet live others who may share our solitude, and help us to rebuild what has been destroyed."

Her voice trembled as she spoke, and the tears stood in her eyes; for she was a creature of deep feeling, and the thought of the great calamity that had befallen the world was ever present to her mind, filling it with images of horror and desolation. She thought of the cities that had once teemed with life, now silent as the tomb; of the fields that had waved with golden harvests, now choked with weeds and briars; of the countless multitudes who had been swept away by pestilence and famine, by war and fire, until the earth was left a wilderness, and the few who remained wandered like spectres among the tombs of their kindred.

Old Matthew Trelawny, who had been a sailor in his youth, and who alone of all their company had knowledge of the sea, now approached them, and his weather-beaten face was grave with thought. He was a man of few words, but his counsel was valued, for he had weathered many a storm, and his heart was as stout as his frame was hardy. "The wind favours us," he said, "and the tide will serve within the hour. If we are to go, it must be now; for the clouds are gathering in the south-west, and there will be a heavy sea before nightfall."

"And the child?" asked Adam, glancing towards a little figure that stood at some distance, watching with eager eyes the preparations that were being made. "She will not be left behind; she clings to us with a pertinacity that baffles all persuasion; and though I would fain spare her the perils of our voyage, yet I know not how to refuse her, for she is alone in the world, and her little heart is set upon accompanying us."

The child was named Lucy—a small, fragile creature, with hair of palest gold and eyes of that deep blue which seems to hold within its depths the mystery of the sea. She had been found, three years since, wandering on the shore, the sole survivor of a shipwreck that had cast her upon the coast; and from that day she had attached herself to Elinor with a devotion that was touching to behold. She now came running towards them, her little hands outstretched, and her face bright with an animation that seemed strange in that place of gloom.

"I am ready," she cried. "I have brought my doll, and the picture-book that Elinor gave me, and the little wooden horse that Adam carved for me. I shall not be afraid, for the sea cannot hurt me; it has been my friend, and it will carry us safely to the land where the other people live."

Elinor stooped and caught the child to her bosom, and her tears fell fast upon the golden head. "My poor Lucy," she murmured, "thou knowest not what thou askest. The sea is a cruel master, and those who trust it too fondly are often betrayed. Yet if thou wilt go with us, I will not leave thee; for I have promised to be a mother to thee, and a mother's love is stronger than the fear of death."
