The rain came down over Clerkenwell in a steady, greasy drizzle that turned the cobbles of Sekforde Street into a slick of reflected gaslight. Inspector Aloysius Finch, a man whose coat seemed to have been tailored from the very soot of the city, ducked under the lintel of number fourteen and knocked with the brass head of his cane. The door was opened by a constable with a face like a worried potato.

“Inspector. He’s in the back. Mr. Tillinghast.”

The workshop smelled of hot shellac, ozone, and the sharp, coppery tang of anxiety. Workbenches were cluttered with the flotsam of a dozen aborted experiments: coils of wire, glass tubing, the brass guts of clocks. In the centre of this mechanical graveyard stood a man, thin as a drawn wire, his fingers stained with nitrate of silver. Mr. Ambrose Tillinghast, inventor of the Tillinghast Arc, a lamp that promised to make night itself obsolete.

“Inspector Finch,” Tillinghast said, his voice a high, taut string. “You’ve come. They told me you would. You must understand the gravity of this. It is not merely theft; it is the violation of a mind.”

Finch removed his hat and placed it carefully on a stool. “Tell me about the lamp, Mr. Tillinghast.”

“The lamp is everything,” Tillinghast said, gesturing wildly at a metal cylinder clamped to a stand, its carbon electrodes cold and lifeless. “A self-regulating arc. The mechanism, you see, is in the clockwork. It maintains the precise gap between the carbons as they burn, a problem that has baffled the finest minds for a decade. I solved it. And Silas Rook stole it.”

“Silas Rook. Your rival.”

“My shadow. My parasite. He runs a workshop off Coppice Row. He has been circling my work for years, sniffing at the edges of my genius. Three weeks ago, he came to me, all smiles and false admiration, asking to see my progress. Like a fool, I showed him. The principle, the elegance of the regulator. And now he has a lamp. A working lamp. He’s been showing it to the investors, Finch. To the very men who were to fund my own production.”

“And the drawings for your lamp? The plans?”

Tillinghast’s face went pale. “In my safe. Where they have always been. Where they remain.” He led Finch to a corner of the workshop, where a heavy iron safe sat bolted to the floor. He spun the dial, his hands trembling, and pulled open the door. Inside, on a velvet cushion, lay a thick sheaf of papers. He lifted them out, his eyes wide with a kind of terrified triumph. “You see? Untouched. No man has been near this. I have the only key.”

Finch took the drawings. They were meticulously detailed, every gear and spring rendered in precise ink. “So Rook, in a single afternoon, committed your design to memory and reproduced it perfectly?”

“He is a cunning devil. He must have. There is no other explanation.”

“There is always another explanation, Mr. Tillinghast,” Finch murmured. He handed the drawings back. “I will need to visit Mr. Rook’s workshop.”

The drizzle had hardened into a proper downpour. Coppice Row was a narrow, crooked lane, its gutters choked with refuse. Rook’s workshop was above a chandler’s shop, the sign on the door, ‘S. Rook, Electrical Innovations’, already peeling. The door was unlocked. Finch pushed it open, the constable at his heels.

The scene inside was one of frantic, chaotic departure. A chair lay on its back, a half-eaten meal of bread and cheese congealed on a plate. The air was thick with the smell of dust and something else—the faint, acrid whiff of a recently extinguished fire. The workbench was a mirror of Tillinghast’s, but where Tillinghast’s was a controlled mess, this was the aftermath of a panicked pillage. Tools were scattered, drawers hung open, their contents rifled. A single, heavy bootprint was stamped in a pile of spilled plaster.

Finch walked slowly through the room, his eyes taking in every detail. He stopped before a small, cast-iron stove in the corner. The door was ajar. Inside, nestled in a bed of cold ash, were the curled and blackened fragments of papers. He took a pair of tweezers from his pocket and gently lifted one out. It was a corner of a drawing, the ink bubbled and destroyed by heat, but a single, elegant curve of a gear-tooth was still visible.

“He burned his plans,” the constable observed.

“Did he?” Finch asked softly. He turned the fragment over in the tweezers. “Or did he burn someone else’s?” He looked at the bootprint, then at a coat rack near the door, from which a single, cheap wool scarf hung, forgotten. He brought it to his nose. It smelled of cheap tobacco and sweat. He knew for a fact, from the constable’s report, that Tillinghast did not smoke.

Finch returned to Sekforde Street, his mind a whirl of gears and motives. He found Tillinghast pacing the floor of his workshop, a glass of brandy untouched in his hand.

“Rook is gone, isn’t he?” Tillinghast said, a note of triumph in his voice. “Fled! The coward’s confession.”

“He is gone,” Finch agreed. “His workshop is in chaos. He left in a hurry.”

“You see! The guilt! He knew you would uncover his perfidy.”

Finch ignored him, his gaze drifting to the workbench. He picked up a small, discarded component—a delicate brass screw with an unusual, left-handed thread. “You make your own screws, Mr. Tillinghast?”

“I make everything. I trust no one.”

“A wise policy,” Finch said. He walked over to the safe, still open. He ran his finger along the inside edge of the door, then held it up to the light. A faint, dark smudge. He then walked to the small stove in Tillinghast’s own workshop, the one used for heating solder. It was clean. Too clean. He lifted the lid. Inside, barely visible against the black iron, was a tiny, melted bead of silver solder and a speck of brass.
