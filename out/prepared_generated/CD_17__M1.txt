It is a truth not sufficiently acknowledged in the bustling, self-important thoroughfares of London, that the greatest revolutions—the most violent overthrows of established order—do not always announce themselves with the clamour of cannon or the crash of toppled thrones. No! They come, more often, in the form of a quiet word in a quiet room; a word which, being spoken, changes everything and nothing at all, leaving the world to go on spinning in its accustomed, and now utterly unendurable, course.

Such a word had been spoken to Mr. Timothy Tibbott, in the gas-lit, paper-scented labyrinth of Messrs. Pratchet, Vane, & Co., Solicitors, of Lincoln’s Inn Fields. For thirty years—thirty years, which is to say, since the days of his own whiskerless and hopeful youth—Mr. Tibbott had occupied the same high-backed stool, at the same sloping desk, which bore the same two circular ink-stains where his forearms had rested, worn into the seasoned oak like the twin orbits of some tiny, clerical planet. His world was one of vellum, of sealing wax, of the dry rustle of parchment and the satisfying *thump* of the red-taped docket. He was a copying clerk, and in his copying there was a perfect, a beautiful, a monotonous fidelity. His hand, though now gnarled and stiffened by the passing of the seasons, formed letters as neat and as regular as if they had been struck from a machine; his ‘a’s were all of one family, his ‘g’s all descended from the same respectable line. He had not missed a day. Not for influenza, not for the death of a relation (there being few left to die), not for the great cholera that had emptied streets and filled graves. He was as constant a feature of the office as the blackened grate and the dusty portrait of the firm’s founder, who stared down from the wall with a perpetual expression of mild financial surprise.

And now, it was over. Mr. Vane, the junior partner, a young man with a startlingly white cravat and an air of brisk, forward-moving impatience, had called him in. There had been no cruelty in it, which was perhaps the cruellest part. There had been talk of ‘a new system,’ of ‘celerity,’ of ‘a young man from the Telegraph office who writes with astonishing speed.’ The words had tumbled out, all reasonable and modern and terrible, and had piled up around Mr. Tibbott’s ears like snowdrifts. He had stood before the polished mahogany desk, his hat in his hand, and had felt the thirty years of his life rise up and choke him. He had bowed, and mumbled something about being ‘much obliged,’ and had walked out, the great oak door of the office closing behind him with a sound of such finality that it seemed to echo in the very marrow of his bones.

The walk from Lincoln’s Inn to his lodgings in Pentonville was one he had performed twice a day for more than half his life. But this evening, the city was a madhouse. The omnibuses, which he had always thought of as lumbering, patient beasts, now seemed to be charging at him. The newsboys, with their piercing cries of ‘War in the East!’ and ‘Murder in Whitechapel!’, were shrieking the end of the world. Every face in the crowd was a mask of purpose, of destination, of a life that was going somewhere, while he, Timothy Tibbott, was going home for the last time as a man of employment. He, who knew the value of a steady wage and a fixed routine, was now cast adrift upon the great, roaring ocean of London, with no compass and no shore in sight.

He let himself into the small, neat house in a quiet street off the Caledonian Road. The smell of boiled cabbage and beeswax, the familiar and comforting scent of home, struck him with a physical force. In the parlour, by the window where the last of the daylight was fading, sat his sister, Prudence. She was darning a stocking, her spectacles perched on the end of her nose, her lips pursed in concentration. And above her, in a cage of shining brass wire, was the source of Mr. Tibbott’s only, his entire, pleasure in life: a canary of a particularly cheerful and optimistic yellow, named Pip.

“Timothy!” said Prudence, looking up. “You are late. The kettle has been on the boil this half-hour. I was about to think you had been run over by an omnibus, which is a fate I have often pictured for you, what with your wandering mind.”

“No, Prudence,” he said, hanging his hat on its peg with a tremor in his hand. “No omnibus. I am perfectly well.”

“You do not look it,” she observed, with the penetrating gaze of a sister who has known one since the cradle. “You look as though you have seen a ghost. Or, more likely, a solicitor’s bill. Come, sit down. Tell me of your day. Was Mr. Vane in a temper? Did he demand more copies of the Clutterbuck conveyance?”

Mr. Tibbott sank into his armchair. He looked at his sister’s kind, inquisitive face, and the words he had rehearsed all the way home—that he was finished, that he was replaced, that their small world was about to be turned upon its head—stuck in his throat like a fishbone. He could not say it. To speak the words would be to make them real.

“It was… a quiet day, Prudence,” he managed at last, his voice a dry whisper. “A very quiet day.”

“Quiet?” she said, putting down her needle. “Quiet is not the word for your face, Timothy Tibbott. Your face is as long and as mournful as a Lenten sermon. Something has happened. Out with it.”
