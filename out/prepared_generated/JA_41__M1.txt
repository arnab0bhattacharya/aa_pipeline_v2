It was a truth not universally acknowledged, that a young man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife; but it was a truth perfectly well understood by Mrs. Augusta Pemberton, who had observed Mr. Evelyn Ashworth’s attachment to her niece with a vigilance that nothing but the deepest sense of duty could have sustained. That the attachment had been cultivated in the privacy of the south shrubbery, and had attained, under her very nose, to a degree of understanding far beyond the reach of polite conversation, was a circumstance which Mrs. Pemberton felt she might reasonably have been expected to discover earlier. But she had been engaged in the important business of arranging the linen press, and one cannot be everywhere at once.

Her discovery was made on a Tuesday. The weather being fine, and the shrubbery offering a pleasant screen of laurel and holly, Miss Clara Fairweather and Mr. Ashworth had found it an agreeable spot for a conversation which had begun with the cultivation of geraniums and had ended with the cultivation of their hearts. It was at this tender juncture that Mrs. Pemberton, armed with a pair of secateurs and a pressing desire to prune the box hedging, came upon them. The scene that met her eye was one of mutual regard, expressed with a simplicity which was, to her mind, as shocking as it was unadorned.

Mr. Ashworth, a young man of twenty-five, whose fortune was as ample as his manners were unassuming, rose with a colour that was quite sufficient confession. Clara, whose fortune consisted of a hundred pounds a year and an excellent pair of eyes, looked as though she would have preferred the earth to swallow her whole. Mrs. Pemberton, however, was not a woman to be defeated by mere embarrassment. She had, in her youth, managed the courtship of three sisters and two cousins, and she viewed the present crisis not as a catastrophe, but as an opportunity. To forbid the match was the work of a moment’s thought and a lifetime of regret; to manage it, on the other hand, required skill, judgement, and a series of carefully worded letters. She therefore chose the latter course.

“My dear Clara,” she said, closing her secateurs with a decisive snap, “and Mr. Ashworth. This is a matter of some importance. We will not discuss it further in this… public setting. You will both attend me in the drawing-room in precisely one quarter of an hour. There is much to be arranged.” She then turned and walked back towards the house with an air of such profound purpose that the young couple felt less like lovers than like errant servants summoned to an accounting.

In the drawing-room, Mrs. Pemberton wasted no time. “The engagement,” she declared, taking her seat in the largest armchair, “is of course a settled thing. It is a connexion of which I entirely approve. Mr. Ashworth’s character is unexceptionable, and Clara’s conduct, while perhaps a trifle precipitate, shows a discernment I cannot but applaud. But an understanding reached in a shrubbery is not an engagement. An engagement requires form. It requires introductions, announcements, settlements, and, above all, a proper sense of occasion.”

Mr. Ashworth, who had hoped for a quiet engagement and a marriage by special licence, found himself agreeing to everything. He would wait upon his own relations in Bath. He would engage his solicitor. He would, at Mrs. Pemberton’s insistence, purchase a new carriage, as his present one, though perfectly sound, was deemed insufficiently impressive for the bride’s family.

Clara, meanwhile, found her own role rapidly diminishing. She was to be fitted for a wardrobe of such magnificence as would have suited a duchess. She was to learn the management of a household with a staff of twelve. She was to be presented to the neighbourhood not as a young woman in love, but as a future Mrs. Ashworth, a title which, her aunt explained, carried with it a weight of social obligation that must be borne with dignity. The tender, secret understanding that had flourished between her and Evelyn was now a public commodity, to be packaged and presented according to Mrs. Pemberton’s exacting standards. The very qualities she had admired in him—his quiet preference for books over society, his lack of ostentation—were now deemed defects to be corrected. He was to become a man of consequence, and she a woman of fashion.

A month passed in a whirlwind of appointments. The solicitors exchanged letters of such length and complexity that the simple matter of a hundred pounds a year and a fine estate in Derbyshire seemed to require the settlement of a European treaty. Mrs. Pemberton held two dinner parties in their honour, at which Clara was required to smile until her face ached, and Mr. Ashworth was required to discuss the state of the turnip crop with gentlemen he had never met. He bore it all with a patience that was becoming strained, while Clara watched him grow more and more distant, his eyes holding a weary confusion where once they had held only warmth.

The breaking point arrived on a Friday evening. Mrs. Pemberton had summoned them to hear the final draft of the marriage articles, which she had composed herself with a lawyer’s zeal and a general’s authority. She read aloud a clause concerning the settlement of pin-money, her voice ringing with the triumph of a battle won. Mr. Ashworth, who had been silent for some time, suddenly rose from his chair.

“Mrs. Pemberton,” he said, and his voice, though quiet, carried a new and terrible finality. “I believe we have gone far enough.”

The room fell silent. Mrs. Pemberton looked at him over her spectacles. “I beg your pardon?”

“This engagement,” he continued, turning to Clara, who looked pale and alarmed, “was founded upon a mutual understanding. It was simple, and it was ours. It has since been made complicated, and it has become yours. I find I have no wish to proceed with it on these terms.”
