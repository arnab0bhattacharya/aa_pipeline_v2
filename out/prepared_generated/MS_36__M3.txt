The autumn of eighteen hundred and thirty-five had descended upon the county of Kent with a melancholy, pervading sweetness, stripping the ancient oaks of Ashgrove Manor of their summer pride, and scattering their brown and withered leaves across the damp, moss-covered terraces where I had been accustomed to wander. I was very young—scarcely past my twentieth year—and my heart, nurtured in solitude and steeped in the deepest gratitude, beat with a fervour that seemed too vast for the narrow confines of my breast. I was an orphan, cast upon the world’s bleak shore without a beacon, until the benevolent hand of Mr. Edric Lyndmore, my late father’s most cherished friend, had gathered me from the obscurity of a distant and penurious relation’s roof, and established me beneath his own noble and hospitable roof. He was a man whose expansive soul was written in the majestic lines of his countenance, and whose wealth was perpetually flowing in the channels of benevolence; yet, beneath his grave and somewhat melancholy aspect, I had early discerned a profound and solitary sadness, a secret wound which I, in the artless vanity of my youth, imagined I might one day be permitted to heal.

For five years I had been the inmate of his household, and the recipient of a tenderness so unwearied, so delicately exercised, that it seemed less like the guardianship of a mortal man than the protecting shadow of an invisible spirit. He had supplied the place of both parents to my desolate heart; he had watched over my fragile health with an anxiety that never slumbered, and had cultivated my mind with a care that seemed to anticipate my every wish before it was formed into thought. I looked up to him with a reverence that partook of the nature of idolatry, and a fondness that was as unbounded as it was sincere. It was, therefore, with a strange and tremulous commingling of surprise, gratitude, and overwhelming emotion, that, in the waning light of a tempestuous October evening, he had suddenly knelt at my feet, and, seizing my trembling hand, implored me to become his wife. He spoke of his long and silent attachment—of how he had watched the dawn of my intellect, and the expansion of my character, with a hope that he had scarcely dared to acknowledge to himself—of his deep, his absorbing devotion; and he concluded with an impassioned appeal, so eloquent in its simplicity, so overwhelming in its earnestness, that I was completely unmanned, if I may use the expression, by the tide of conflicting sentiments it awoke within me.

I did not, I could not, answer him then; I pleaded for a respite, a few hours of solitude, in which to collect my scattered thoughts and to examine the nature of my own heart. He granted my request with a tenderness that almost reproached me for my hesitation, and I fled to my own apartment, to weep, to pray, and to ponder on the strange destiny that seemed unfolding before me. I was grateful—deeply, unfeignedly grateful; I was fond of him—fond with the fondness of a child for its protector; but was this love? Was that intense and all-absorbing passion, which I had read of in books, and which my own ardent imagination had painted as the indispensable condition of a happy union, to be found in my bosom for Edric Lyndmore? I knew not; I only knew that to reject him would be to plunge a dagger into my own heart, and to embitter the remainder of his life. I resolved to consent; I would dedicate my existence to the happiness of the man who had preserved it from obscurity and want.

But a mysterious and fatal fatality seemed to preside over my destiny, and to interpose its dark and impenetrable veil between me and the consummation of my wishes. In the course of the evening, while wandering in a distant part of the house, I chanced to enter the picture-gallery, a long and shadowy apartment, lined with the portraits of the Lyndmore family. I had often been there before, but tonight, by the flickering light of a solitary lamp, my attention was singularly attracted to a portrait which I had hitherto passed without particular notice. It was the likeness of a young and beautiful woman, with dark, expressive eyes, and a smile of inexpressible sweetness playing about her lips. I stood before it, fascinated; I seemed to recognize those features, to have seen them in a dream, or in some forgotten reality of my earliest childhood. A vague and thrilling recollection flashed across my mind; it was my mother!

With a beating heart, and a hand trembling with eagerness, I sought out the old housekeeper, Mrs. Wilmot, a woman who had lived in the family for more than forty years, and who was known to be in the possession of many of its most private traditions. I found her in her cozy sitting-room, and, without preface, demanded the history of the portrait I had just beheld. The good woman started, changed colour, and, after a long and evident struggle with herself, yielded to my importunity. She told me, in a voice often interrupted by emotion, that the portrait was indeed that of my mother, the beautiful and unfortunate Eleanor Vane; that Mr. Lyndmore, in the early years of his manhood, had been deeply, passionately attached to her; that they had been betrothed, and that the wedding-day was fixed, when my father, a young officer of captivating address and brilliant prospects, had crossed their path. My mother’s heart, too susceptible, too easily swayed by the sudden and impetuous impulses of passion, was turned aside; she broke her engagement with Mr. Lyndmore, and, in defiance of the entreaties of her friends, and the representations of her own judgment, she eloped with my father, and they were married in London.
