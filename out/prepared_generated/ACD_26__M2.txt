The telegram lay upon the blotter, its angular, black letters stark against the white paper like a scar upon pale skin. I had been watching Mr. Arthur Pemberton for some time. He was a man of considerable wealth, the sole heir to the vast brewing empire of the Pemberton family, yet his face held the pallor of a man who has seen the bottom of a deep well. He sat in my consulting room at 221B Baker Street, his hands trembling so violently that he could not grip the small silver case from which he had drawn the paper.

"It is the third, Mr. Holmes," he said, his voice a thin, reedy thing. "The first two are dead. The third is me."

Holmes, who had been sitting in his armchair with his eyes closed, opened them. They were sharp, cold, and immediately fixed upon the young brewer. He did not rise. He did not move. He simply looked, with that terrible, patient intensity that I had come to know so well.

"Pray, Mr. Pemberton," Holmes said, his voice low and measured. "Do not attempt to narrate the matter in fragments. Tell me, from the beginning, exactly what has occurred. Be precise. I require facts, not emotions."

Pemberton swallowed hard, his Adam’s apple bobbing in his throat. He straightened his tie, a futile gesture of dignity. "It began three weeks ago. I received a telegram from my solicitor, Mr. Halloway. It stated that my uncle, Silas, had been found dead in his carriage. I rushed to Kent. It was true. He had died of a sudden heart attack, or so the doctors said. The telegram had arrived two days before the body was found."

"And this second incident?" Holmes asked.

"Mr. J. T. Vane, a partner in my firm. Another telegram. This one stated that Vane would be found floating in the Thames. He was, indeed, found there. Drowned. The telegram arrived three days prior."

Holmes leaned forward, resting his elbows on his knees, his fingers steepled beneath his chin. "And this third telegram? When did it arrive?"

"Yesterday, sir. It says: 'A. Pemberton. 1894. 14th October. End.'"

"And today is the 14th of October?"

"Yes, sir."

"Have you informed the police?"

"I have not. I came to you first. I know the police would think me mad, or perhaps an accomplice. If I am the recipient, I must be the target. Who is sending these? And why?"

Holmes stood up and walked to the window, looking out upon the grey London street. The fog was thick, curling around the lamp-posts like grey smoke. "You say the first two men died exactly as predicted?"

"Exactly, Mr. Holmes. There is no other explanation. It is a curse, or a madness."

"Curse is a word for those who lack imagination, Mr. Pemberton. Madness is a word for those who lack method. Let us look at the mechanics." Holmes turned back to me. "Watson, did you observe the paper of the telegram?"

"I noticed it was of the standard yellowish stock, Mr. Holmes. But there was something peculiar about the impression. The letters seemed deeper than usual."

"Exactly," Holmes said, turning back to Pemberton. "Who sent these telegrams? Did they come from a specific office?"

"The first and second were sent from the central office in London. This one... I do not know. It was delivered by the post."

Holmes picked up the telegram from Pemberton’s hand. He held it to his nose, then held it up to the light. He turned it over, examining the watermark. "There is no postmark," he said. "No date. No sender’s address. Only the message. This is unusual. The Post Office requires the sender’s details for most telegrams, though it is not always enforced for private matters. However, the style of the message is distinctive. It is concise, factual, and devoid of punctuation."

"Is that significant?" Pemberton asked.

"It suggests a mind that is ordered, perhaps obsessive. It also suggests that the sender wishes to remain anonymous, yet confident enough to issue a proclamation." Holmes paused. "You said your uncle died of a heart attack. And Mr. Vane of drowning. Do you know either of these men?"

"Uncle Silas was a miser. He had enemies. Vane was a gambler. He owed money to dangerous men in Whitechapel."

"Then we have two different motives, or perhaps two different methods of killing. But the common thread is the telegram. It is a premonition. Or is it?" Holmes’s eyes glittered. "Mr. Pemberton, have you considered that the telegram is not a warning, but a confirmation? That the death has already been arranged, and the telegram is merely a notification to the beneficiary?"

Pemberton looked pale. "Beneficiary? Of what?"

"Of an insurance policy. Or a will. Or a marriage settlement. Who benefits from the deaths of your uncle and Mr. Vane?"

"I do... I do not know. I am the heir to the brewery, but Uncle Silas was not dead before this week. And Vane... Vane had no family."

"Then who?"

Pemberton shook his head. "I have no idea, Mr. Holmes. I am a brewer. I deal in hops and malt, not in death."

Holmes smiled, a thin, cold expression. "We shall see. I am going to Kent with you, Mr. Pemberton. We must examine the premises of your brewery. We must speak to the employees, the drivers, the stokers. We must look for the one man who has access to the private telegraph office, or who knows the habits of these three men well enough to predict their ends."

"But if I go to Kent, I am safe?" Pemberton asked, his hope kindling.

"No, Mr. Pemberton. You are no safer in Kent than you are here. The telegram said October 14th. It is the 14th. The clock is running."

We left Baker Street within the hour. The train to the south took us into the flat, green countryside of Kent. The air was cool, carrying the scent of damp earth and brewing yeast. As we approached the town of Ashford, I saw the massive brickworks of the Pemberton Brewery rising above the treeline, its chimneys belching smoke into the grey sky.
