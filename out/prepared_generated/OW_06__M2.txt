The snow in the city of Veldora did not fall so much as it asserted itself, a cold and crystalline judgment upon the soot-stained cobblestones and the jagged silhouettes of its iron towers. It was a city that loved the dark, for in the dark one could not see the rot in the wood or the grime in the face, and the lamps, though few, burned with a deceptive amber warmth that promised a coziness the wind outside ruthlessly denied. In this atmosphere of polite shivering and frozen grandeur, Elara, a seller of matches whose fingers were perpetually numb and her soul perpetually hungry, was engaged in the profound philosophical act of keeping her inventory dry.

She had been standing beneath the eaves of the Palace of Whispers, a structure so tall it seemed to pierce the belly of the low-hanging clouds, when the object appeared. It lay in a drift of pristine white, a streak of emerald and sapphire against the monochrome, so vividly coloured that it looked less like an object and more like a wound in the winter’s uniform. It was a comb, fashioned of tortoiseshell so dark it was nearly black, set with jewels that caught the faintest glint of the streetlamp and threw them back in defiant, defiant sparks.

Elara picked it up. The cold metal of the handle bit into her palm, a sharp, clear pain that was almost pleasant in its simplicity. She had never touched anything so smooth, so hard, so utterly unlike the rough, splintered wood of her own world. To hold it was to hold a piece of the sun, trapped and tamed. She knew, with the cold clarity that comes to those who have known hunger long enough to make it a companion, that this comb could buy bread. Not just a crust, but a loaf, warm and golden, with butter that would melt on her tongue like a secret. It could buy a blanket, a coat, perhaps even a night in a room where the fire did not need to be begged for. It was, in the economy of Veldora, worth more than her dignity, and perhaps more than her life.

But she did not sell it.

Instead, she tucked it into the inner pocket of her threadbare shawl, close to her heart, where the heat of her body began to warm the cold stone of the jewels. She walked home through the swirling snow, feeling a strange, illicit thrill, as if she had stolen a star from the sky. The comb was heavy, a weight that anchored her to the earth, yet it made her feel light, lifted above the grime of the street. For the first time in her life, Elara possessed something beautiful, and the possession of it changed the texture of her reality. The shadows in the alleyways did not look so menacing; the wind did not sound so cruel. Beauty, she realized, was not a luxury, but a shield.

The news of the lost comb reached the palace within the hour, carried by the invisible network of gossip that moves faster than snow. The Princess Isolde, who had worn the comb to a dinner where the wine was thin and the conversation thinner, was in a state of what the courtiers called “distress” and what Elara would have recognized as panic. The comb was not merely an accessory; it was a symbol of her station, a declaration that she was of the blood that rules, even if the rule was becoming increasingly theoretical. Without it, she was merely a girl in a palace, vulnerable to the same cold and hunger that plagued the streets below.

Isolde descended into the city not in a carriage, but on foot, draped in furs that made her look less like a princess and more like a bear who had wandered into a ballroom. She was accompanied by her lady-in-waiting, Lady Muriel, who carried a parasol that served no purpose in the snow, and a guard who looked as though he would rather be anywhere else in the kingdom. They walked the narrow streets, asking questions that were answered with shrugs and silence. The people of Veldora did not like the palace, and they did not like the princess, who was known for her sharp tongue and her indifference to the suffering that lay just beyond the palace gates.

Elara watched them from the window of her hovel, a room no larger than a closet, with a roof that leaked when it rained and froze when it snowed. She watched the princess’s retinue pass by, a flash of purple and gold in the grey street. A sudden, sharp pang of guilt struck her, not for the theft, but for the lie of omission. She had told herself she would return it, but she had not. She had told herself it was for her, but it was for her pride. She was not a thief, she reasoned, because the comb had found her. It had chosen her. But was that not the most dangerous kind of theft, the one where the victim is never aware of the loss until it is too late?

The next day, the snow had stopped, leaving the city under a hard, white crust that reflected the pale winter sun. Elara stood at her usual corner, arranging her matches. She felt the weight of the comb in her pocket, a secret heartbeat. She decided that she would return it, not out of moral rectitude, but because she realized that keeping it would poison the very beauty it represented. To possess what was not yours was to own a mirror that showed only your own lack.

She went to the palace gates, where the guards, bored and shivering, let her pass. She walked through the halls, which were so clean they seemed to hurt the eyes, and so silent that the sound of her own breathing was a disruption. She was led to the princess’s chamber, a room flooded with light from tall windows. Isolde sat by the fire, looking not like a ruler, but like a child who had lost a toy.
