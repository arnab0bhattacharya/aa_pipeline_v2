The summons, when it came, was a thing of no more weight than the slip of paper upon which it was printed—a formal request, couched in the chill civility of officialdom, that Mr. Elias Gann, harbour pilot of some five-and-twenty years’ standing, should present himself before the Board of Trade and Harbour Commissioners to offer explanation concerning a certain delay in the docking of the barque *Aurelia* on the preceding Tuesday. It was a trivial matter, the kind of thing that in the ordinary course of affairs would have been settled by a word, a nod, a shake of the head between men who understood the vagaries of wind and tide as they understood the lines upon their own palms. Yet the paper lay upon Gann’s deal table like a stone upon his conscience, and the name at the foot of it—that of Mr. Percival Thraves, Chairman of the Board—struck at him with the dull, delayed impact of a blow received in darkness and recognised only in the light.

He was a man of middle age, Gann, spare of frame and weathered to the colour of old rope, with eyes that had looked too long upon the grey wastes of the Irish Sea to retain much illusion concerning the ways of men or the mercies of the elements. He lived alone in a narrow house in a street that sloped precipitously down toward the river, a street of mean brick and grudging light, where the sound of the tide came up at night like the breathing of some vast, indifferent creature. His life was a thing of routine, of small certainties—the pipe, the pint, the evening walk along the landing-stage—and he had come to regard his own past as a man might regard a foreign country, visited long ago, whose customs he had forgotten and whose language he no longer spoke.

Yet the name Thraves had the power to summon it all back.

He remembered the night—it was in ‘74, or perhaps ‘75, the precise year had blurred—when the *Thetis*, a brig of some four hundred tons, had lain at the North Landing with her cargo stowed and her crew aboard, and the glass falling, falling, falling with that steady, remorseless persistence that speaks to the sailor in a language older than words. The wind had come up from the nor’-west, and by midnight it was blowing a full gale, and the sea in the river was a churning confusion of black water and white spume, and the *Thetis* was plunging at her moorings like a frightened horse. Gann had been the pilot assigned to her. And Thraves—then a shipowner of some substance, a pushing, aggressive man with a voice like a file upon iron—had come down to the quay in person, his greatcoat flapping about him, and had demanded that the brig be taken out.

“She must sail tonight,” he had shouted above the wind. “I have a contract, do you hear? A contract! There is a penalty, a ruinous penalty, for every day’s delay, and I will not be ruined by the cowardice of a pack of—”

Gann had not let him finish. He had stood there, his sou’wester streaming with rain, and had said, in that flat, level voice which was the only voice he knew how to use: “She will not go out tonight, Mr. Thraves. Not with me at the helm. You may find another pilot, and God help him, for I will not answer for the consequences.”

Thraves had raged, had threatened, had called him a fool and worse, had sworn that he would never again employ a man who valued his own skin above the interests of his betters. But Gann had held his ground, and in the end the *Thetis* had stayed at her moorings, and the gale had risen to such a pitch that by morning two vessels at anchor in the Mersey had foundered, and the bodies of eleven men were recovered from the river. The *Thetis* had sailed two days later, in a fair wind, and had reached her destination without mishap. But Thraves had never forgiven him. He had been dismissed from the service of the line, had lost the little influence he had, and had been compelled to subsist for years upon the precarious earnings of a freelance pilot, waiting at the Pier Head for the chance of a ship, taking whatever came his way, a man marked, in the small, unforgiving world of the port, as one who had crossed a powerful man and had paid the price.

And now Thraves was Chairman of the Board. The irony of it, Gann thought, was of that quality which the Greeks had understood—the kind of irony that is not merely cruel but absurd, that makes of a man’s life a jest for the amusement of indifferent gods.

He presented himself at the Board’s offices on a Tuesday morning, in his best coat, which was old but decent, and with his papers in order. The building was a thing of grey stone and tall windows, standing in a square where the wind from the river cut like a blade. He was shown into a waiting-room, where he sat for the better part of an hour upon a hard wooden bench, listening to the muffled sounds of official business—the scratching of pens, the murmur of voices, the occasional ring of a bell. Then a clerk came for him, a pale young man with the air of one who has never known the salt upon his lips, and conducted him into the boardroom.

It was a long, high room, panelled in dark oak, with a great window that looked out upon the river, where the ships lay at anchor in the grey light of morning, their masts a forest of slender lines against the sky. The members of the Board sat about a long table, and at the head of it, in a chair that seemed to Gann to have something of the throne about it, sat Percival Thraves.
