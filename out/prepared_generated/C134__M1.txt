The steam that rose from Silas Croft’s pies was the same as the steam that had once curled from the copper pots in the galley of the *Seraphina*. He knew it in his bones, in the way the heat settled in the damp chill of the river air, smelling of boiled meat and baked crust instead of salted pork and ship’s biscuit. His pie shop, ‘Croft’s Pies’, was a narrow, timber-framed box wedged between a sail loft and a chandler’s, its one window fogged with the promise of warmth within. For thirty years he had fed sailors on the high seas; now, with every penny of his prize money and pension sunk into a stove and a sack of flour, he fed the dockhands and mudlarks of Rotherhithe.

Business was slow. The river was a greedy, grey beast, and the men who worked its banks had little to spare. They’d pause, sniff the air, and their eyes would linger on the golden-crusted pies cooling on the sill, but their hands would stay in their pockets. Silas would watch them go, his shoulders, broad from a lifetime of hauling dough and wrestling with recalcitrant galleys, slumping a little more each day. He was a man made for the vast, rolling chaos of the sea, not the quiet desperation of a London quayside.

Then the boy appeared. He was a creature of the river mist and the city’s grime, all sharp angles and quick, darting eyes. His name, he said, was Finn. His clothes were a patchwork of rags, but his gaze was as clear and direct as a ship’s compass.

“A fine smell, sir,” Finn said, not looking at Silas but at the pies. “But a smell don’t fill a belly. A pie on a tray, now that does.”

Silas grunted, wiping his floury hands on his apron. “And what would a scrap like you know about selling pies?”

“I know men are hungry and lazy,” the boy said with a shrug that was far too worldly for his years. “They’ll pay a ha’penny more not to walk the twenty yards from the wharf to your door. Let me carry ’em. I’ll take a penny on every shilling I bring you.”

Silas squinted at him. It was a fool’s bargain, yet the boy’s logic was as sound as a ship’s keel. He had nothing to lose but the pies he couldn’t sell anyway. He nodded curtly. “One penny. And if you run off with my goods, I’ll know who to look for.”

Finn’s grin was a flash of white in a grimy face. “A deal, Captain.”

The boy was a marvel. He moved through the dockyards like a phantom, his high, clear voice calling out, “Hot pies! Hot pies from Croft’s! Fill your gut for a penny!” He’d charm the surly foremen, trade a joke with the sailors, and appear back at the shop with an empty tray and a handful of coins, his commission already separated. Business doubled. Then it trebled. Silas was kneading dough from dawn till dusk, his old sea cook’s muscles singing with a familiar rhythm. He started to look forward to the clatter of Finn’s worn boots on the cobblestones, the sight of the boy’s bright, hungry face.

He grew fond of the boy. He’d save him the choicest scraps, a piece of suet pudding or the end of a bacon roll, and watch him eat with a ferocious gratitude. He saw in Finn a kindred spirit, a fellow survivor on the unforgiving tide of the city. He began to teach him things, how to tell if the meat was fresh, how to crimp a pastry edge so the steam wouldn’t escape. Finn listened, his clever eyes missing nothing. Silas felt a warmth in his chest that had nothing to do with the stove. It felt like purpose.

The first crack in the hull of his contentment came on a Tuesday. A constable, a burly man named Mr. Hackett, stopped by for a pork and apple. As he ate, he chatted with Silas, his eyes scanning the river.

“Quiet today,” Hackett said, nodding towards the opposite bank, where the crumbling tenements of Jacob’s Island festered in the gloom. “Not much coming over the bridge. Good. That’s where the trouble is. A fellow by the name of Blackwood runs his… enterprise from that side. A nest of thieves and fences. We had a spate of stolen silverware from the big houses in Bermondsey last week. It’ll all end up in Blackwood’s parlour, melted down and gone before we can say ‘Bob’s your uncle’.”

Silas felt a prickle of unease. He thought of Finn’s errands, how he sometimes took longer than necessary, disappearing over the old wooden bridge that led to the island. He shook the feeling off. The boy was selling pies. That was all.

But the doubt, once planted, grew like weed. He started to watch Finn more closely. The boy’s tray was always empty when he returned, but sometimes he carried other things. A small, tightly-wrapped bundle under his arm. A heavy-looking object in his coat pocket that clinked when he ran. Silas told himself it was nothing, that the boy was entitled to his own life. Yet the image of Hackett’s grim face and the name ‘Blackwood’ echoed in his mind.

One grey, drizzly afternoon, Silas sent Finn out with a dozen pies. The boy was gone for over an hour. The rain thickened, plastering Silas’s thinning hair to his scalp as he stood in the doorway, scanning the quay. Finally, he saw Finn, not returning from the wharves, but walking away from the bridge, from Jacob’s Island. He wasn’t carrying the pie tray. He was walking with a stooped, rat-faced man in a long, dark coat. Silas’s blood ran cold. He knew, with a certainty that felt like a punch to the gut, that he was looking at one of Blackwood’s men.
