The letter which reached me at my lodgings in Falmouth was brief and businesslike, and it was signed by a name I did not then know. Mr. Abel Tregarth, of Wheal Lanoweth, near St. Austell, desired the opinion of a practical engineer upon a tin mine which had lain idle since the great fall of prices in the seventies, and which he was now minded to reopen. The fee named was generous, and the season was fine; I took the morning coach, therefore, and by early afternoon I was set down at the cross-roads above the village, with my bag in my hand and the sea-wind sharp in my face.

Wheal Lanoweth is one of those old workings which are to be found scattered over the Cornish moors, where the grass grows green over the mouths of shafts that have swallowed men and money for three hundred years. The engine-house was roofless, and the great beam stood against the sky like the rib of some dead leviathan. Yet I observed, as I came down the track, that the path was trodden, and that a new padlock hung upon the gate of the count-house. Mr. Tregarth met me there—a heavy, slow-spoken man of fifty, with the hands of a labourer and the coat of a merchant—and he led me at once to the adit's mouth.

"You'll find her dry enough," said he, "for the water has been low this summer. But I'll not deceive you, Captain, for I am no miner myself. My father worked her, and my brother after him, and when the price fell we shut the doors and let the sea-wind blow through her. Twenty years she has stood so. And now the price is up again, and I have money in the bank, and I would know whether there is tin enough in her to pay for the pumping and the timbering."

I lit my candle and went in. The adit ran straight for some two hundred feet, a narrow tunnel driven through killas, and the air was cold and clean and smelt of wet stone. Then it opened into a gallery where the lode had been worked upward in the old fashion, and there I stopped, and held my candle high, and looked about me with a sudden interest that had nothing to do with tin.

For the floor was swept. Not clean, you understand, as a floor is swept by the sea-wind and the drip of years, but swept by a broom, and swept recently, so that the dust lay in little ridges against the walls. And upon a ledge of rock at the foot of the old stope there stood a candle-end, guttered and cold, but with the wick still black and fresh; and beside it, in the soft mud of the floor, were the marks of a boot—a small boot, nailed, and turned inward at the toe in a manner I had not seen before.

I am no Sherlock Holmes, gentlemen, and I make no pretence to his methods. But I have spent thirty years underground, and I know the difference between the droppings of a mine that has been dead for twenty years and the droppings of a mine that is being worked to-day. I said nothing to Mr. Tregarth, but I asked him for a ladder, and he sent his man to fetch one from the count-house. It came—a short ladder of seven rungs, and I set it against the ledge and climbed up into the old stope, and there I found what I had half expected. The stope had been worked, and worked lately. The rock was fresh-broken at the face, and the broken stuff had been sorted, and the rich stuff carried away, and the dead stuff thrown back into the old workings. And in a corner, wrapped in a piece of sacking, I found a small hand-drill and a hammer of a foreign pattern, such as I have seen in the mines of the Hartz.

When I came down, Mr. Tregarth was standing at the adit's mouth with his hands behind his back, and his face was not the face of a man who expects good news.

"Well, Captain?" said he.

"Mr. Tregarth," said I, "I will give you my opinion upon the mine when I have asked you one or two questions. Who has the key of that padlock?"

"I have," said he, "and my man Matthew has the other."

"Has any stranger been about the mine of late?"

He looked at me sharply. "There was a man here in the spring," said he, "a foreign-looking fellow, with a beard, who said he was a mining engineer from the Continent, and wished to see the old workings. I showed him the adit myself, and he went in with a candle, and came out again in half an hour, and said that the mine was worthless. I thought no more of it."

"And your man Matthew—where is he now?"

"Gone to St. Austell for the timber," said he. "He will not be back till dark."

I asked him then to take me to the count-house, and there I sat down at the old desk and wrote out a list of the things I had seen; and while I wrote, Mr. Tregarth stood at the window and looked out over the moor, and his heavy face was troubled.

"Captain," said he at last, "you have found something."

"I have found," said I, "that your mine is being worked, and worked at night, by some person who has a key, or who has no need of a key. The candle was burned within the week. The drill is of a pattern I do not know in Cornwall. The rich stuff has been carried away, and carried away in small quantities, so that no great carting should be seen upon the road. And the man who has done it has been careful to leave the dead stuff behind him, so that if any fool like myself should come in with a candle he should see nothing but the old workings."
