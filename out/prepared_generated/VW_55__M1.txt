The light came in through the high windows of the haberdashery in great slanting columns, and in each column a thousand motes of dust were turning, endlessly, like the little wheels of some celestial clockwork. Miss Eliza Price, leaning against the counter with her hands folded upon the cool marble, watched them turn. It was a slow business, the afternoon, the kind that stretches and thins until one feels one might snap it with a finger. A bell tinkled somewhere near the door, a sound that was at once sharp and distant, a signal from another world. A lady in a coat of grey fur, smelling faintly of cold air and expensive scent, drifted towards the ribbon display. Eliza straightened. Her smile, which she kept in a small, ready box at the corner of her mouth, was produced. “Can I help you, madam?” The words came out smooth and polished, worn smooth by a thousand repetitions. The lady’s fingers, gloved in fine kid, fluttered over the spools of satin and velvet. They were not her own fingers, Eliza thought; they belonged to the coat, to the scent, to the life that existed somewhere beyond the plate-glass window. They selected a length of deep crimson, a colour like a secret. Eliza measured, cut, wrapped. The exchange of coins was cold and quick. The lady departed, and the great bell jangled again, leaving the silence thicker than before.

Her own hands, when she looked at them, were pink and chapped from the water in the basin where they washed the cups at lunch. They were honest hands. They did their work. But last night, last night they had been held. The memory came not as a thought but as a sensation, a warmth that spread from the base of her spine up through her chest. The dance hall, with its paper lanterns swaying like pale fruit, the brassy clamour of the band, the floorboards humming beneath a hundred shuffling feet. And then him. Mr. Leo Fitzwilliam. He had not asked, not in so many words, but had simply appeared before her, a tall shape in the shifting light, and his hand had found hers. His own hand was warm and dry, and when he spoke, his voice was a low murmur beneath the music, a rumour meant only for her. He had said something about her eyes, how they held the light of the lanterns. It was a foolish thing to say, a line from a penny novelette, and yet it had lodged in her, a small, bright stone. Now, in the flat, grey light of the afternoon, it seemed both utterly real and impossibly distant. The bell. Another customer. A man this time, wanting buttons for a waistcoat, his breath smelling of pipe tobacco. Eliza’s mind, which had been swimming in the warm, coloured water of the dance, was pulled back to the dry land of bone and horn buttons, of shillings and pence.

At half-past one, the bell for luncheon rang, a single, weary toll. The staff room was a small, airless space at the back, smelling of boiled cabbage and damp wool coats. The girls were already there, their faces slack with the morning’s work. Miss Gable, who was older and had a way of looking at you as if she knew all your secrets and found them wanting, was stirring her tea with a grim little spoon. Eliza unwrapped her sandwich—cheese and pickle, the bread already going soft—and listened to the talk. It was the usual talk, of sore feet and difficult customers, of a new film at the Picture House. They were all of them, Eliza thought, looking at their tired faces under the harsh electric light, living small lives, contained lives. They did not have the memory of a warm hand in a crowded hall. They did not have a name like Leo Fitzwilliam to turn over and over in their minds, a smooth, grey pebble from the shore of another existence. She felt a sudden, sharp pity for them, and a sharp, secret pride. She was different. Something had happened to her. She took a bite of her sandwich. It tasted of nothing at all.

The afternoon resumed its slow, turning rhythm. The dust motes still danced in their columns of light. The light itself began to change, losing its white clarity and taking on the soft, golden-grey of a London evening. The lamps along Oxford Street were being lit, one by one, like a string of pearls against the darkening sky. Eliza began her closing duties, the familiar, comforting rituals. She tidied the counters, straightened the ribbons, her hands moving with an economy that left her mind free. The thought of him was with her now, a constant companion. Would he be at the dance again on Saturday? Would he remember? The questions were a soft, persistent hum beneath the clatter of the closing-up. The great bell gave its last, final jangle for the day, and Miss Eliza Price, pulling on her thin coat, stepped out into the roar and rush of the street.

The air was cold, sharp with the promise of rain and the smell of roasting chestnuts from a vendor’s cart. The crowd was a river, flowing westwards towards the tube stations and the suburbs, and she was a single drop in it, anonymous and free. She walked, her head full of the dance hall, the music, the pressure of his hand. She saw the reflection of the street in the darkened shop windows—herself, a pale, indistinct figure moving through a world of light and motion. And then she saw him. Not a reflection, but him. He was standing under the awning of a cinema, the coloured lights of the marquee casting a blue and red glow upon his face. He was just as she remembered, tall, with that same way of holding his head. A wave of warmth, of pure, unadulterated joy, broke over her.
