The rain had beaten against the shutters of the pensionnat since vespers, a steady, relentless deluge that seemed to wash the very colour from the grey stones of the Rue Pépin. Inside, the saloon was chill, save for the dying fire which sent long, flickering shadows across the polished floor. I sat at my writing-desk, my fingers stiff with the unseasonable cold, yet my mind was in a tumult that no bodily temperature could govern. I had been a teacher in this establishment for seven years, exiled by circumstance from my native Yorkshire, and I had learned to look upon the follies of youth with a measured, if sorrowful, eye. But the matter that pressed upon me tonight was not one to be surveyed with cold philosophy; it demanded action, and action of a kind that revolted my sense of personal dignity.

There was a girl in my charge, Clara Vane by name, sent abroad by a father whose estates in the West Riding were broad, whose pride was broader still, and whose affection for his motherless daughter was, I feared, less robust than either. She was nineteen, handsome in a way that drew the eye not by softness but by a certain defiant brightness, a glow of spirit that reminded me, painfully, of the moorland heather in its brief, fierce bloom. She had been courted these three months past by a Count de Rousset, a man who attached himself to the fringes of our little English colony with the easy, indolent grace of one long accustomed to live upon the favour of others. He was penniless; I knew it, though Clara did not, or would not. His title was genuine enough, for what little that signifies when the coat of arms must be painted on a borrowed carriage. He had charm—such charm as is the particular and dangerous gift of those who have nothing else to bestow.

I had watched the progress of this intimacy with an anxiety I strove to conceal. I had seen Clara's eye brighten at his approach, had marked the flush that mounted to her cheek when his low, measured voice addressed her in that fluent English which he spoke with just enough of a foreign accent to lend it an air of distinction. And I had seen, too, the look in his face when he supposed no one observed him—a look cold, calculating, and intent, that turned my blood and set every instinct of my nature in arms against him.

Tonight the matter had come to a crisis. I had encountered them in the garden gallery, sheltered from the rain, he bending over her hand with a devotion too pronounced for the occasion, she with her face turned up to his, soft, trusting, fatally unwary. I had said nothing then. I had passed with the austere silence that is, perhaps, the most eloquent rebuke a woman of my years can offer. But Clara had followed me to my room an hour later, her eyes blazing with that resentful fire which springs from a consciousness half-formed and already rebellious.

"You disapprove of me, Miss Heston," she had said, standing in my doorway with the candle held high, her very attitude a challenge. "You think me a fool, I dare say, as my father does."

"I think you a girl entrusted to my care, Clara," I had answered, "and one whose happiness is very near to my heart."

"My happiness!" she had repeated, and laughed, but the laugh was not a merry one. "You would cage me here with grammar and embroidery till I grow as withered and joyless as—you will forgive me—as yourself. The Count loves me. He has said so. Is love, then, a thing to be weighed and measured and found wanting against a ledger of pounds and pence?"

"I do not weigh his love against a ledger, Clara. I weigh it against his character."

"And what do you know of his character?" she demanded, her voice rising. "You see a man who is poor, and you conclude he is base. That is the English way, is it not? To distrust all who have not gold in their pockets and acres in their deeds?"

She had turned and gone before I could answer, her skirts rustling down the corridor with a swiftness that spoke of anger too hot for parley. And I had been left, sitting in my cold room, with the weight of responsibility pressing upon me like a stone upon the chest.

For I knew what I knew. I had heard, from a source beyond question, that the Count de Rousset had, in the previous year, paid his addresses to the daughter of a German merchant settled in this very city; that he had pressed for an early and private marriage; and that the merchant, having made inquiries, had discovered debts so vast and so intricately woven through the gaming-houses of three countries that no fortune less than a principality could have discharged them. The match had been broken off, and the girl had been sent home to Bremen in a state of prostration from which, it was said, she had not yet recovered.

This was the man who now whispered of love into Clara Vane's ear. And I, her teacher, the surrogate of that stern, distant father in Yorkshire, held the knowledge that could save her, and must decide whether to wield it.

It was not an easy question. To write to Mr. Vane was to confess that my authority had been insufficient, that the supervision entrusted to me had failed. It was to expose Clara to a wrath which, I feared, would fall not upon the Count but upon the daughter, visited with that harsh, unreflecting justice which men of his temperament mistake for paternal rigour. He would recall her, perhaps; would drag her home in disgrace, and the stain of the affair would cling to her in that society which forgives a man his debts and his deceits more readily than a woman her trust.
