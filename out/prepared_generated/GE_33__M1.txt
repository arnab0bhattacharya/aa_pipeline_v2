It is a truth generally acknowledged in the small town of Netherfield-on-Stowe that a man with a hobby which does not contribute to the general prosperity of the neighbourhood is a man to be viewed with a certain degree of pity, and, where that hobby is of a particularly abstruse or dusty nature, with a degree of ridicule that is all the more potent for being unspoken. Mr. Elias Thorne, who had inherited the pleasant but not prosperous rectory at the edge of the town and the small competence that went with it, was such a man. His passion, which had begun in his youth as a respectable interest in the natural sciences, had long since congealed into the fixed and all-consuming pursuit of the ancient life preserved in the limestone that formed the very bones of the surrounding country.

The quarries that pockmarked the hillsides were, to the owners and labourers who worked them, a source of hard-won profit and daily toil; to Mr. Thorne, they were a vast, unpaginated library of the earth’s deep history, and he spent his days—and many of his evenings—in the patient, dusty work of deciphering its text. His study was not a room for genteel conversation but a workshop of geology: shelves groaned under the weight of grey stones, each carefully labelled in his precise hand; a great table was perpetually covered with trays of ammonites, crinoid stems, and the enigmatic, segmented bodies of trilobites; and the air was thick with the faint, chalky smell of deep time. This devotion, which gave his life a profound and private meaning, made him an object of gentle mockery to the townspeople. The farmers, who knew the seasons and the soil, thought him a man lost in a fog of his own making; the tradesmen, whose lives were governed by the price of corn and the turn of the market, saw only a gentleman with more money than sense; and the children, catching sight of his tall, stooped figure returning from the quarries with his canvas bag of stones, would sometimes trail behind him, chanting a rhyme about “Old Thorne and his fossil bones.”

Among the quarry owners, Mr. Silas Croft was the most successful and, consequently, the least given to such foolishness. A man whose prosperity was written in the stoutness of his waistcoat and the confident set of his jaw, Croft regarded Thorne’s collection with the pragmatic scorn of a man who sees value only in what can be sold for a good price. “Aye, he can have the broken bits,” Croft would say to his foremen, wiping his hands on his trousers. “It keeps him out of mischief, and it costs us nowt.” Yet the relationship was not without its mercenary foundation; for Mr. Thorne, despite his scholarly air, was not without a certain shrewdness in his dealings. He knew which fossils were mere curiosities and which were of true scientific importance, and he had been known to pay a surprising sum for a well-preserved specimen, a fact that Croft, a man who never let a penny escape his notice, was not slow to learn.

It was on a damp afternoon in late autumn that Silas Croft made his way up the gravel path to the rectory, a brown-paper parcel held in one hand. His knock was heavy and assured. Mr. Thorne, disturbed in the midst of cataloguing a new series of brachiopods, opened the door with a look of mild surprise. Croft did not wait for an invitation but strode into the hall, his eyes taking in the familiar disarray of the study beyond. “Mr. Thorne,” he began, his voice a low rumble, “I’ve summat you’ll want to see. Found it this morning, deep in the new face at the Long Quarry. Near broke the new cutting bar on it.”

He unwrapped the parcel on a clear corner of the table, pushing aside a tray of delicate specimens with a lack of ceremony that made Thorne flinch. The object revealed was a slab of dark grey limestone, and upon it was a fossil of a perfection and scale that made Thorne’s breath catch in his throat. It was a trilobite, but of a species he had only ever seen in fragmentary form in the most advanced European treatises. It was complete, from its complex, multi-lensed head-shield to the final segment of its tail, every spine and tubercle exquisitely rendered in the stone. It was not merely a fossil; it was a revelation, a perfect window into a world hundreds of millions of years gone.

For a long moment, Thorne could only stare, his mind, usually so ordered, a whirlwind of scientific ecstasy. He saw not a stone, but the creature itself, scuttling across a long-vanished seabed. He saw the paper he would write, the sensation it would cause at the Geological Society, the vindication of his life’s work. Then, Croft’s voice, devoid of wonder and full of calculation, cut through his reverie. “It’s a rare one, I reckon. That’s why I brought it to you first. I’ll not be giving it away.”

The question of price, when it came, struck Thorne not as a matter of business but as a kind of sacrilege. “Fifty pounds, Mr. Thorne. Not a penny less. I know what it’s worth to a man like you.”

Fifty pounds. The sum echoed in the quiet room. It was a vast sum, a sum that could outfit a small farm, or furnish a handsome house. It was also, as Mr. Thorne knew with a sudden, sickening clarity, the precise sum that was set aside in the Three Per Cents for his daughter, Clara. It was her marriage portion, the tangible foundation of her future security and happiness. He had watched her grow into a young woman of gentle disposition and quiet charm, and he had nurtured this fund with the same patient care he gave his fossils, seeing in it the one practical legacy he could leave her.
