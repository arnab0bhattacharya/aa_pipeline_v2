The fog rolled in from the Scheldt, a thick, grey shroud that swallowed the spires of Bruges and choked the very air in the small, damp classroom. It was a Tuesday in November, 1843, and the cold had a bite that no amount of peat fire could quell. I sat at my desk, the ink freezing on the nib of my pen, watching the figure of Mademoiselle Van Houten move among the rows of wooden forms. She was a severe woman, her face as angular and unyielding as the flinty cliffs of the coast she had once shown me from a distance. To her, we were not girls with beating hearts and aching souls, but a flock of sheep to be sheared, trimmed, and sold for profit. I was the shepherd, or rather, the lamb who had grown teeth.

I had not seen her approach. One moment I was arranging my slate; the next, her shadow fell across my desk, cutting off the pale light from the window. I looked up, and there, in her gloved hand, was a letter. It was not mine. It was the letter of Elise De Smet, the youngest pupil, who sat in the back row, her head bowed so low that her brown hair curtained her face, hiding a flush that I knew would soon turn to the white of death.

"Mademoiselle," Van Houten said. Her voice was not loud, but it carried the sharp, metallic ring of a blade drawn from its sheath. "You have been observed in possession of this correspondence. It is not from your mother. It is not from a relative. Explain."

The room held its breath. The other girls, who had been writing, stopped. The scratching of quills ceased. A silence, heavy and suffocating, descended upon us, broken only by the distant tolling of a church bell from the belfry.

I looked at the letter. The envelope was soft, worn at the edges, the ink of the address smudged, as if held too tightly in a trembling hand. I knew the signature before I opened it. It was young Jan, the son of the chandler who lived near the gate. He was a boy of sixteen, with eyes that held a wildness and a goodness that I recognized, because they were the same eyes I had seen in the mirror of my own loneliness.

"Mademoiselle," I said, my voice steady, though my heart hammered against my ribs like a trapped bird. "I found it. It fell from the satchel of the girl behind me when she rushed to the board to correct an error. I was merely holding it safe until the lesson ended. I was going to return it to its owner."

Van Houten’s eyes narrowed. They were small, dark, and devoid of mercy. "You found it? You, who have never been known to stray from the path of duty? You, who are so careful to maintain the discipline of this house? And you did not inform me immediately?"

"I was waiting for the appropriate moment," I lied. The words tasted like ash. "To avoid a scene."

"A scene," she repeated, the word dripping with disdain. "You think this a matter of manners? This is a matter of principle. A young man’s letter, hidden in a pupil’s bag, is a symptom of disorder. It is a crack in the wall through which the wolf may enter. I am bound by my contract to report such incidents to the parents. To hide it would be to condone it. To condone it would be to betray my trust."

She turned to the class. "Elise De Smet, come forward."

Elise rose. Her legs seemed to fail her; she swayed, and for a second, I thought she would collapse. But she forced herself to stand, her chin lifted in a defiance that was more pitiful than proud. She walked to the front of the room, her steps soft and uncertain on the cold floorboards.

"Mademoiselle Van Houten," I said, interrupting her next command. "Let me speak. I know Elise. I know her history. She has no family here. Her father is dead, and her mother is ill in Ghent. She is alone. This letter is not a plot of rebellion. It is a lifeline. It is the only thing that keeps her from despair."

Van Houten turned her head slowly, looking at me with an expression of cold curiosity. "And what do you know of despair, Mademoiselle? You are well provided for. You have a position, a salary, a future. You are not like these children. They are clay; you are stone. Do not try to paint yourself in the mud to save the pig."

"I am not saving the pig," I said, my voice rising, the anger that had been building for months finally breaking the surface. "I am saving a soul. You speak of discipline, of order, of the contract. But what of the spirit? What of the heart? If you take this letter, if you send her away in disgrace, you do not punish a rule-breaker. You break a human being. And you will have done so in the name of duty, while your conscience rots in the corner of your closet."

The room was silent. The fog pressed against the window panes, seeming to lean in to listen.

Van Houten stared at me. For a long moment, I thought she would call for the headmaster. I thought she would dismiss me on the spot, stripping me of my position, my pay, my dignity. I saw the flash of triumph in her eyes, the satisfaction of a predator cornering its prey.

But then, something shifted. A faint tremor in her hand. A flicker in her eyes that was not anger, but fear. Not fear of me, but fear of what I had named. I had named the thing she had spent twenty years trying to suppress: the loneliness. The cold. The empty rooms of her own heart.
