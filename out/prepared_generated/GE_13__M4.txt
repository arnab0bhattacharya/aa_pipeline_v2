It was a fact often remarked in the neighbourhood of Fennimore St. Mary that the Reverend Osmund Ashby had married above his sphere; and the remark, though delivered with that air of neutral observation which the English villager assumes when he is about to be censorious, carried in it a weight of implication sufficient to bow the shoulders of a less sensible woman than Mrs. Ashby. For Cecilia Ashby was the daughter of a schoolmaster at once ambitious and penurious, who had lavished upon her an education whose ornament was out of all proportion to its use—French, Italian, a little Latin, a great deal of music, and that dangerous familiarity with the poets which makes the possessor fancy herself capable of a finer discrimination than her neighbours. She had come to Fennimore with a dowry of three hundred pounds and a mind richly furnished, and had discovered within a twelvemonth that a curate's wife may be learned and yet not influential, may be refined and yet not respected.

The parish ladies, indeed, were not unkind; they were merely, as they conceived it, judicious. Mrs. Briscoe of the Hall, a woman whose benevolence was regulated by a nice sense of precedence, had called upon her in the first month with a basket of preserves and a volume of sermons, and had continued to call at intervals, always with the air of one who condescends to a species of merit which is not quite of her own order. The Misses Trewin, daughters of a retired attorney, spoke to her of books with a patronage which suggested that they regarded her acquirements as a sort of polite accomplishment, like embroidery, to be praised but not emulated. And when she attempted to institute a class for the elder girls of the village—a scheme conceived in the purest ardour of usefulness—she found the poor suspicious. They did not wish their daughters to be taught French, they said; they did not see the good of a girl's knowing the names of heathen gods and goddesses; and they had heard tell that Mrs. Ashby's own household was not managed as their mothers had managed theirs, and that the curate's shirts were mended with a neatness which savoured of vanity rather than of thrift. So the class dwindled, and Cecilia, who had meant to be a light in the parish, found herself rather a phenomenon in it—looked at, talked of, and not quite trusted.

Her husband, meanwhile, was a man of that gentle and somewhat passive goodness which is more apt to be loved than to be advanced. He had no arts of popularity; he did not flatter the squire, nor dine at the Hall with that assiduity which the incumbent expected of his curates; he was conscientious in his duties, mild in his reproofs, and so entirely without ambition for himself that his wife's ambition on his behalf had come to seem to her a kind of holy work, a duty she owed to his merits. She had begun by loving him for his goodness; she continued by scheming for his preferment, and the transition had been so gradual that she had never detected in herself the growth of that self-will which is the shadow of a devoted affection.

The living of Fennimore was in the gift of Mrs. Briscoe, and it was understood that when old Mr. Harrow should be gathered to his fathers, the curate who had served the parish so long and so faithfully would be his natural successor. The expectation had become so fixed in Cecilia's mind that she had ceased to regard it as an expectation and had begun to regard it as a certainty; she had furnished the vicarage in imagination a dozen times over, and had planned a garden which was to be at once a recreation and a model to the parish. Then Mr. Harrow died, and the living was given to the Reverend Josiah Pettigrew, a man of no particular parts, who had been at Oxford with Mrs. Briscoe's son, and whose chief recommendation was that he was the cousin of a baronet.

The blow fell upon Cecilia with a severity which surprised her. She had not known how much of her daily life had been sustained by that prospect; she had not known how much of her patience with the parish, with the poor, with the condescension of the Hall, had been fed by the secret consciousness that she was enduring for a purpose. Now the purpose was gone, and she was left with nothing but the endurance. She looked at her husband as he sat at breakfast reading the letter which announced the appointment, and she saw him—perhaps for the first time—as others saw him: a man of five-and-thirty, with a stoop in the shoulders, with grey beginning to show at the temples, with a face whose mildness was not far removed from vacancy. He laid the letter down and said, with a sigh that was not bitter, "Well, my dear, it is a disappointment; but I dare say Mr. Pettigrew will do very well."

"Do very well!" she cried. "Osmund, do you not see that it is an injustice? Do you not feel that you have been passed over for a man who has no claim but his connections? Have you no sense of what is due to you—to your work—to your years of service?"

He looked at her with a mild bewilderment. "I have done my duty," he said, "as well as I could. If Mrs. Briscoe thinks Mr. Pettigrew will serve the parish better, it is not for me to complain. Perhaps he will."

"Perhaps he will!" she repeated, and there was that in her voice which made him wince. She rose from the table and went to the window, and stood looking out upon the garden—the little garden which was not to be hers—with a heart swelling with a sense of wrong.
