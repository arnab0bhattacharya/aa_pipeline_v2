The rain fell on Manchester with the persistence of a debt, a grey, soaking misery that found its way through the cracked putty of the window frames and settled in a permanent chill against the walls of the two rooms Elara Vance called home. Here, in the shadow of the great brick mills, the air was a constant taste of soot and damp wool. Her world had shrunk to this space: a small bedroom where she and her son, Thomas, slept in a narrow bed, and this main room, where she ate, cooked, and worked.

Work was the rhythm of her days. The great black Singer sewing machine, a wedding gift from her husband, Arthur, stood in the corner like a patient iron beast. Its steady, percussive clatter was the only music in the room, a sound that meant another few pennies were earned. Piles of coarse cotton and cheap linen lay in her lap and spilled onto the floor, destined to become shirts for factory hands or shifts for mill girls. Her fingers, once soft, were now calloused and pricked, the skin around her nails permanently stained with dye and grime.

Thomas, at four years old, was a creature of quiet observation. He would sit on the floor with a wooden spool and a length of discarded thread, his brow furrowed in concentration as he tried to wind it, his small face a pale oval in the gloom. He was the reason for the clatter of the machine, the reason she mended her own stockings until they were more darn than original fabric, the reason she had refused the Blackwood family.

The memory of that refusal was a cold stone in her gut. They had come a week after the funeral, Arthur’s father and his formidable wife, their carriage a black behemoth that drew stares in their narrow street. They had sat in this very room, their fine wool coats seeming to drink the little light there was, their expressions a mixture of pity and distaste.

“The boy is a Blackwood, Mrs. Vance,” Mr. Blackwood had said, his voice accustomed to boardrooms and final decisions. “He requires a proper upbringing. An education. The company of his peers. We can offer him all of that. He will want for nothing.”

“What he wants,” Elara had replied, her voice thin but steady, “is his mother.”

The offer was not a request. It was a transaction. She would be provided for—a small annuity, enough to see her through—and in return, she would sign over custody. Thomas would be raised at their country estate, given the Blackwood name and fortune. She could, of course, visit. It was presented as a rescue, a lifting of a burden. They saw her not as a mother, but as an inconvenience, a temporary vessel who had served her purpose.

“He will be cold,” she had said, thinking of the chill in the grand houses she’d heard of. “He will not know my face in the mornings.”

“He will know comfort,” Mrs. Blackwood had countered, her voice like ice cracking. “He will know security. You offer him poverty and this… squalor.”

Elara had looked at Thomas, asleep in his small cot, and then back at them. “He is not a possession to be traded for a better room. He is my son. I will not give him up.”

They had left with a finality that felt like a door slamming shut on their world. Now, the Blackwoods’ lawyer, a Mr. Croft, had begun to call.

He arrived on Tuesdays, always at the same hour. He was a man made of sharp angles and polished leather, his presence an intrusion of the outside world into her small, hard-won sanctuary. He would knock, a precise, authoritative rap, and when she opened the door, he would stand on the threshold, shaking the rain from his umbrella before stepping inside. He never sat.

“Good afternoon, Mrs. Vance,” he would say, his eyes sweeping the room, cataloging the damp patch on the ceiling, the threadbare rug, the single candle burning on the table. His gaze was not unkind, but it was assessing, like a butcher weighing a piece of meat.

“Mr. Croft.”

“I trust you are well?”

“We are managing.”

“That is not the same as being well,” he would observe smoothly. “My clients remain concerned for the boy’s welfare. Their offer, I must remind you, is not withdrawn. It is an act of generosity, Mrs. Vance. A chance for a life you cannot possibly provide.”

“I provide him with love, sir. And his mother. Things your clients seem to value very little.”

Today, his visit was the same as the last. He stood by the window, looking down at the slick, muddy street where a stray dog nosed through a pile of refuse. Thomas, sensing the tension, had retreated to the corner, hiding his face against Elara’s skirts.

“Look at this room, Mrs. Vance,” Croft said, his voice softer now, almost persuasive. “The damp will get into his lungs. The poor food will stunt his growth. You are a proud woman, I see that. But pride will not clothe him or feed him when winter truly bites. You are sewing for pennies. The Blackwoods deal in thousands of pounds. Think of the boy.”

“I do think of him,” she said, her hand stroking Thomas’s hair. “Every stitch I take is for him. Every meal I make. He is fed. He is warm enough. He is loved.”

“Love is a fine sentiment, but it is not a roof over one’s head or a place in a good school. It is not a future.” He paused, letting the silence hang in the air, heavy with the unspoken threat of the workhouse, of sickness, of a life of unending drudgery. “They only wish what is best for him.”

“They wish to own him,” Elara said, her voice gaining a sliver of its old strength. “He is not a thing to be owned, Mr. Croft. He is a person. My person.”
