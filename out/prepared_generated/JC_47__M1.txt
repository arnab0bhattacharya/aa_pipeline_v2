It is a strange thing, and one that the mind, in its slow revolutions, will turn over and over without ever arriving at a full understanding, how a man’s life can be shaped by a silence. I have had many years to consider the matter of Father Emerico and the silver of San Cristóbal de las Cumbres, and I confess that the truth of it remains for me a matter of shadow and interpretation, a story told in the pauses between the words. The village itself, clinging to the spine of the Andes, seemed a place built for silences. The air was thin and sharp, carrying the scent of frost and of damp earth, and the silence of the mountains pressed down upon the terracotta roofs like a great, immovable weight. It was a silence that was not an absence of sound—for the wind was always moving through the ichu grass, and the bell of the little church had its own dry, solemn tongue—but a presence in itself, a moral quality that seeped into the stones and into the souls of the men who lived among them.

In that silence, Father Emerico had tended his flock for thirty years. He was a man from the lowlands, they said, who had come up into the thin air and never gone back down, his faith perhaps hardened and clarified by the altitude, like the water in a mountain stream. He was stooped and spare, his face a collection of fine, dry lines, and his hands, which handled the consecrated wafer with such reverence, were gnarled by the cold and by the work of a life that had known little comfort. The treasure he guarded was not, you understand, a king’s ransom in doubloons and jewels, but the accumulated devotion of generations: chalices of beaten silver, monstrances encrusted with stones from the riverbed that were not gems but had been polished by centuries of faith, censers and candlesticks and a solid silver crucifix that had been the gift of a forgotten Viceroy. It was the soul of San Cristóbal, made visible, and it was Father Emerico’s charge.

Then the revolution came, as it always does, not like a sudden storm but like a slow, rising flood, first as a rumour carried by muleteers, then as a column of smoke on a distant ridge, and at last as the sound of marching feet on the stony road. It was a war of abstract nouns—Liberty, Progress, the Republic—fought by men with very concrete hungers. And leading the column that came for the silver of San Cristóbal was a Colonel Serrano. I saw him once, long after, in a port city on the coast, a man of polished manners and a watchful, weary gaze, who spoke of the past as if it were a foreign country he had visited as a tourist. But in the mountains, he was the revolution made flesh. He was a man who had learned that sentiment was a luxury and that iron will was the only currency that did not devalue.

They say that when the Colonel entered the church, the silence of San Cristóbal became a living thing, a third presence in the room. The soldiers stood awkwardly by the door, their rifles seeming profane in the holy dimness. The Colonel, however, moved with an unhurried certainty. He did not look at the saints in their niches, nor at the fading frescoes on the walls. His eyes went at once to the old priest, who stood before the altar, a frail bastion against the inevitable. The Colonel’s face, which I was told was a handsome one in a hard, ascetic way, betrayed no emotion. It was a mask of command.

“Padre,” he said, and his voice was quiet, formal, stripped of all warmth. “The Republic has need of the silver. It is for the cause of the people.”

Father Emerico did not flinch. He looked at the man before him, and in his eyes there was no fear, but a deep, searching sorrow, the kind one feels for a beloved thing that has been irrevocably changed. He had known this face. He had known it as a small, pale, devout boy kneeling at this very altar, his hands clasped with an intensity that was almost painful to watch. He had known the boy’s fears, his earnest whispers in the confessional, the fierce, untested purity of his belief. That boy had loved God with a passion that was perhaps too great for the world to contain.

“The silver belongs to God, Coronel,” the priest said, his voice thin but steady in the great nave. “And to the poor, who are His representatives on earth.”

The Colonel smiled, a brief, humourless contraction of the lips. “God will provide for His own, Padre. And the Republic will provide for its people. Do not make this difficult. It is a simple matter of accounting.”

An accounting. It was the word he used. As if the chalice from which he had first taken communion were an entry in a ledger, to be balanced against the needs of a new state. The irony of it must have been a bitter taste in the old man’s mouth. He had spent a lifetime building a fortress of faith, and now the enemy stood before him wearing the face of his most cherished victory. Where was the boy who had trembled at the thought of damnation? He had been replaced by this man of accounts, this pragmatist of blood and iron. It was not a betrayal, I think, that the priest saw, but a kind of damnation far more terrible: the complete and utter victory of the world over the spirit.
