The lamp upon my writing-desk burned with a low and steady flame, and the house around me—so lately the scene of a thousand brilliant assemblies, of music, of laughter, and of that light and glittering converse which is the food of vanity—was now as silent as the sepulchre. I had been for some hours alone; the last guest had departed, the last echo of the carriages had died away in the square, and I was left to the company of my own thoughts, a society which, of late, I had found but little to my taste. I had been reading—nay, I had been endeavouring to read—the pages of a new romance, but my mind wandered from the printed line, and my eyes found a more terrible interest in the embers of the fire, which, as they mouldered into ashes, seemed to image forth the decay of all my hopes.

It was then, in that hour of solitude and self-reproach, that a letter was brought to me, and the servant, who knew my humour, placed it silently upon the table. The hand was unknown to me, the paper coarse, the seal unadorned. I broke it open with that listlessness which attends upon a sated spirit, but I had not read three lines before the blood rushed back upon my heart, and I was compelled to grasp the arm of my chair for support. It was from a physician, a man of humanity and skill, who had attended the dying bed of a poor seamstress in a narrow court near the river. The name he mentioned was one I had striven for twenty years to forget; it was the name I had given in the bitterness of my shame, in the secrecy of my flight, to the unconscious babe I had left within the walls of the Foundling Hospital. She was dead—my daughter was dead!—and the writer, who knew not our connexion, but who had found among her few papers a letter, half finished, addressed to a lady of my name and title, implored me, if I had ever known her, to come forward and rescue her memory from the obscurity of a pauper's grave.

I sat for a long time motionless. The letter fell from my hand; a mist gathered before my eyes, and a cold and heavy weight seemed to press upon my heart, such as I had not felt since the day when, a young and trembling girl, I had turned my back upon the gates of that asylum, and hurried away, I scarce knew whither, to hide my disgrace in the oblivion of a foreign land. I thought of the child as I had seen her last—a little helpless being, wrapped in the coarse garments which the charity of the hospital provided, with its small hands stretched out, and its eyes, blue and mild as the morning, fixed upon my face with a look of unconscious appeal. I had kissed it—I had wept over it—I had called it by a name which I had never since pronounced without a shudder; and then I had torn myself away, and left it to the mercy of strangers. And now she was dead—dead in poverty, in want, perhaps in hunger—while I, her mother, rolled in wealth, and was courted and admired, and had never once, in all those years, dared to ask whether the being I had abandoned was alive or in the grave.

The morning dawned before I could compose myself; and when the day was fairly begun, I summoned my resolution, and determined to visit the house where she had lived, and to learn from the lips of those who had known her the sad particulars of her life and death. I disguised myself in a plain and sombre dress, and, with a heart beating with a thousand conflicting emotions, I took my way through the crowded streets, until I reached the narrow and obscure lane which the letter had described. The house was old and ruinous; the staircase dark and narrow; the room to which I was conducted was small and mean, with a broken window, through which the wintry wind whistled mournfully, and a few articles of furniture, which spoke but too plainly of the extremity of want. There was a bed, upon which the poor girl had breathed her last; and beside it, in a chair, sat an aged woman, who had been her companion in poverty, and who now received me with a respect which I felt to be the keenest reproach.

She told me the history of my child. She had been brought up in the hospital, and at the age of fifteen had been apprenticed to a milliner, from whom she had learned her trade. She had been industrious, patient, and pious; she had never known a parent's care, and yet she had never ceased to cherish a secret hope that her mother would one day come to claim her. She had kept a little book, in which she had written down her thoughts—her dreams of a home, of a mother's love, of a happier lot; and in the last page she had traced these words, which the old woman read to me with a voice broken by sobs: "I am very ill, and I fear I shall not live. If my mother should ever seek me, tell her that I forgave her long ago, and that I prayed for her every night. I have no other legacy to leave her but my love."

I could endure no more. I threw myself upon the bed, and clasped the pillow to my breast, and wept as I had not wept since I was a girl. The tears which I had so long suppressed burst forth with a violence which I could not control; and I cursed the pride, the vanity, the dread of shame, which had made me a mother without a heart, and a woman without a soul.
