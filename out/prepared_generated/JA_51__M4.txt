The affair of Miss Harriet Ellington and Mr. Charles Ashby had now been before the neighbourhood of Hartley Green for two full years, and was, by the general consent of all who had the happiness of knowing the parties, as hopeless a business as ever occupied a tea-table. Not that there was any doubt of Mr. Ashby's attachment. His constancy was admitted by everybody; his visits to the parsonage were frequent, his attentions unremitting, and his countenance, whenever Harriet entered the room, betrayed a degree of pleasure which no man of mere politeness could have counterfeited. But he was the younger son of a baronet, with a modest independence and a temper constitutionally cautious, and he had now spent four-and-twenty months in hovering upon the verge of a declaration without ever once alighting.

Harriet, who was two-and-twenty and possessed of that gentle patience which is the ornament of her sex, was content to wait. Her sister Anne, who was nineteen and possessed of no patience at all, was not. Anne had observed the progress of the affair with an impatience bordering upon indignation. She had watched Mr. Ashby hand her sister into the carriage, and take his leave at the gate, and call again the following Tuesday as though nothing had happened; she had listened to his observations upon the weather, the roads, and the price of corn, and had wondered, with a wonder that increased monthly, how a gentleman could spend so many hours in a house without once coming to the point.

"The man is afraid," said Anne, one afternoon in October, when Mr. Ashby had just quitted them after a visit of two hours, during which he had talked of nothing but the new turnpike. "He is afraid of his father, or afraid of you, Harriet, or afraid of his own happiness. But he shall not be afraid any longer, for I have a plan."

Harriet, who knew her sister's plans, begged her to do nothing. She might as well have begged the wind to be still.

It was Anne's design, conceived in that spirit of ingenious mischief which was her particular gift, to throw Mr. Ashby into such a state of alarm for the loss of her sister as should make a declaration the only alternative to despair. She began with the simplest of her devices. When next Mr. Ashby called, she spoke, in his hearing, of a certain Captain Delacourt, lately returned from the Indies, who had been paying very particular attentions to Harriet at the assembly at Winchester. The Captain was a man of fortune, of spirit, and of a boldness in address which Mr. Ashby, she was sorry to observe, did not possess. Mr. Ashby heard her out with great composure, observed that he had not the honour of the Captain's acquaintance, and enquired whether the turnpike was likely to be completed by Michaelmas.

Anne was not discouraged. She proceeded, at the next opportunity, to a bolder stroke. She caused a letter to be brought in during Mr. Ashby's visit, and, having read it with an exclamation of surprise, informed her sister that Captain Delacourt had that morning done her the honour to propose for her hand, and that she had written to refuse him, but that he was not a man to be refused, and had sworn to come down to Hartley Green to press his suit in person. Harriet, who had been instructed in the plot, looked conscious; Mr. Ashby looked grave. He said it was a very fine day, and took his leave earlier than was his custom.

For a week he did not come at all. Anne, who had expected an immediate explosion of jealousy, was disappointed. She had not reckoned upon the stubbornness of a cautious man, who, when he is frightened, retires to consider. But she was not yet at the end of her resources. She wrote, with her sister's unwilling connivance, a letter to Mr. Ashby's elder brother, Sir John, in which she lamented, with every appearance of candour, that Mr. Ashby should have been so long a visitor at the parsonage without any declared intention, and hinted that Miss Ellington's affections, though not engaged, were not insensible to the attentions of other gentlemen, and that it were well if Mr. Ashby should be made acquainted with the state of affairs before it was too late.

This letter she did not send. It was intercepted, in the manner of her sex, by being left upon the table in the drawing-room where Mr. Ashby was sitting, and, being folded and directed to Sir John Ashby, could scarcely fail to attract his eye. He took it up, coloured, and laid it down again. He asked whether Miss Anne was in the habit of corresponding with his brother. Anne replied, with perfect truth, that she had never written to Sir John in her life, but that she had that morning received a letter from him which she had been about to answer. Mr. Ashby said nothing, but his countenance underwent a change which Anne observed with triumph. He left the house within five minutes, and was seen to walk rapidly in the direction of the village.

He did not call again for a fortnight. Harriet wept; Anne, for the first time, began to be uneasy. She had meant to bring him to the point, not to drive him from the house. She had supposed that a man in love, when he found his mistress about to be carried off by a rival, would speak; she had not supposed that a man of Mr. Ashby's temper, when he found himself suspected of neglect and threatened with the interference of his family, would take offence. Yet so it was. Mr. Ashby had been wounded in his pride. He had been two years in making up his mind, and he was not to be hurried into a declaration by the manoeuvres of a younger sister and the impertinence of a Captain Delacourt, who, upon enquiry, appeared to be a person of no fortune whatever, and to have been in the Indies only as a purser's clerk.
