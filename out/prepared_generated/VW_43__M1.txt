The river, he found, had its own way of speaking to the soul, a low, continuous murmur that was not quite sound and not quite silence, but a sort of thinking made audible, the city’s long, grey thought. He stood on the Embankment, the wind pressing his thin coat to his ribs, and watched the brown water fold and unfold the reflections of the bridges, which were like the ribs of some great, dead beast half-sunk in the mud. He had come to London to find a voice, and the first thing it had given him was this immense, impersonal whisper, which promised everything and offered nothing. The gulls wheeled and cried overhead, their sharp, clean cries like splinters of white light against the damp, grey sky, and for a moment he felt he understood them, those scavengers of the air, living on what the great body of the city threw away.

He turned from the river and walked east, into the labyrinth of streets where the morning’s fog had thinned to a milky vapour that clung to the upper storeys of the buildings. The city was not one place but a thousand, each street a new country with its own customs, its own language of light and shadow. Here, a street of booksellers, where the air smelled of paper and leather and decay, a smell so rich and promising that he felt a pang of hunger for a life of quiet, ordered thought. He paused at a window, his reflection a pale, indistinct ghost superimposed upon the spines of a hundred lives, a hundred finished worlds. But the door was locked, and a small card announced that the proprietor was away. A door closing softly in the mind. He walked on.

The day proceeded thus, not as a sequence of events but as a series of impressions, each one laying itself over the last like thin sheets of coloured glass. The clatter of a horse-drawn dray on the cobbles, a sound from another century; the smell of roasting coffee and wet wool from a crowded market; the sudden, shocking red of a woman’s hat passing in a taxi, a flash of life so intense it seemed to leave a scar on the grey air. He saw it all—the weary faces under the streetlamps already beginning to glow in the premature dusk, the children with their hoops, the pigeons in the square, the grand, indifferent façades of the great hotels. It was a spectacle of such variety and density that it seemed to have no centre, no meaning, only a ceaseless, churning vitality. He was a point of consciousness adrift in it, an eye that saw but could not touch. He had looked for a job and had found only windows, his own face staring back at him, asking, Who are you? He had looked for a room and had found only doorways, each opening onto another’s life, another’s ordered world of teacups and mantelpiece photographs, a world to which he could not gain admittance.

By late afternoon the light began to fail, the colours draining from the streets as if a tide were going out, leaving behind the raw, grey bones of the city. A weariness had settled in his bones, a hollow ache that was not of the body but of the spirit. He had seen everything, and therefore he had nothing. The images crowded in on him, a jumble of disconnected scenes: a barrow-boy shouting his wares, the silent, watchful eye of a cat on a wall, the intricate lacework of a gasometer against the bruised purple of the evening sky. What was he to do with it all? It was like being given the whole world to carry in two hands; it was impossible, and so he had nothing at all. He thought of the provincial town he had left, its small certainties, its known faces, and it seemed at once a prison and a paradise of simple form. Here, there was only this overwhelming flood of being.

He found himself in a small square, where a plane tree stood, its branches like a network of black veins against the last of the light. In the centre, a fountain trickled, the sound thin and lonely in the gathering quiet. He sat on a bench, the cold of the iron seeping through his trousers. He was tired of looking. He closed his eyes, and the city pressed in not as an image but as a sound, a feeling, a great, slow pulse. And then, without his willing it, a sentence formed itself in his mind, clear and distinct, as if someone had spoken it beside him. It was not a great sentence, not a profound one, but it was his. It was a small, hard knot of his own making in the vast, formless wool of the day. He did not know what came before it or what would come after, but it was a beginning. He opened his eyes. The streetlamp above him sputtered to life, casting a circle of yellow light on the wet pavement, and in that small, man-made sun, he felt a flicker of something that was not hope, but its humbler, more durable cousin: purpose. The city had shown him everything, and in the end, it had shown him only the small, dark room of his own mind, and the single, flickering candle he had yet to light.
