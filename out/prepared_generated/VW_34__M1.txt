And now that she was gone—the wires of the telegraph had hummed it from the village post office, a thin, metallic sound carrying the news of Mrs. Marsh’s passing—the silence she had left behind was the loudest thing in the house. It was not an absence, but a presence, a great, hollowed-out shape in the air where the rustle of her black bombazine had been, where the faint scent of beeswax and lavender and the iron of the keys she carried had hung like a permanent, invisible weather. In the drawing-room, where the afternoon light fell in long, dusty slants across the Persian rug, Lady Althea sat with her hands lying still in her lap. She did not think of the funeral, or the new woman, or the accounts that would now fall to her. Her mind, like a moth, fluttered against the memory of a sound: the particular, soft thud of the housekeeper’s door closing, a sound that had meant order, that had meant the world was as it should be. Thirty years, Lady Althea thought, and in all that time she had never seen the woman truly angry, only a certain tightening of the lips, a stillness in the hands, which was far more effective than any shout. A great oak, she had been; and now the axe had fallen, and the house was open to the sky.

From the kitchen, the memory was of her hands. Not the gentle hands of a lady, but capable, dry, and firm, with knuckles swollen from the cold of the larder in winter and a small, white scar on the thumb from a long-ago kitchen fire. Young Elsie, who was polishing the silver, saw those hands now, resting on the great scrubbed table, laying out the week’s menus in a neat, sloping script. “She never raised her voice,” Elsie whispered to the cook, who was kneading dough, her own face flour-dusted and weary. “Not once. She’d just look at you. And you knew.” The cook grunted, a sound that was half agreement, half grief for the woman who had managed the pantry accounts so that there was never a want, never a waste. They remembered the jingle of the keys at her belt, a sound that meant the day was beginning, that the complex, interlocking machinery of the great house—its fires, its meals, its linens, its silences—was about to be set in motion. To them, she was not a person so much as a principle, the stern, unbreathing law of the larder and the linen press. And now, with the master and mistress away and the news just arrived, the law was gone, and the butter was left melting in the dish.

Down in the village, where the hedges were thick with cow parsley and the May air was sweet, she was remembered as Edith, a girl who had left and never truly come back. The postman, old Mr. Finch, paused on his rounds, his leather bag heavy on his hip. He remembered her with her hair in a long, dark plait, running barefoot down this very lane, her face turned up to the sun. He had seen her, of course, at the gate of Farringdon House on her half-days, a tall, straight-backed woman in a plain grey dress, her face framed by a severe white cap. She would nod to him, a gesture of recognition but not of intimacy. The girl and the woman had become two different people in his mind, the bright, laughing Edith of the cornfields and the silent, formidable Mrs. Marsh of the great house. They said she had left a child in the village years ago, a girl now married to a baker in Lewes. A sad story, people murmured, but one does not ask about such things. To the village, she was a story that had been told and then stopped, a local girl who had climbed a ladder so high that she had become a part of the sky itself, remote and untouchable.

Upstairs, in the nursery, the light was softer, filtered through the tall windows. The master’s son, now a young man of twenty, stood looking at a small, wooden horse on the windowsill. He remembered Mrs. Marsh’s shadow falling across his bed, a long, dark shape that meant it was time to sleep. He remembered her voice, low and steady, reading to him not from books of nursery rhymes, but from the great histories of England, her finger tracing the lines of battles and kings. She had taught him his letters on a slate, her hand guiding his, her breath smelling faintly of the peppermints she kept in her pocket. To him, she was not a servant, nor a village girl, but a kind of earth-mother, a fixed point in the turning of the seasons, more constant than his own mother, who was always dressing for a dinner party or recovering from one. He remembered once, when he had been ill with a fever, she had sat by his bed all night, her hand cool on his forehead, and in the grey light of dawn he had woken to see her looking at him, her face unguarded, her eyes full of a terrible, aching tenderness he had never seen before and never saw again. It was a glimpse of something vast and secret, a world of feeling locked behind the formidable Mrs. Marsh.

And in the village churchyard, the rector stood over the freshly turned earth, the scent of damp soil and lilies in the air. He thought of the woman who had come to him once, thirty years ago, a young Edith with a pale face and a desperate question in her eyes. He had given her the standard comfort, the talk of Providence and the mysterious ways of the Lord. She had listened, her head bowed, and when she left, he had felt he had failed her entirely, had offered her a stone when she needed bread.
