King Aethelgard of Veridia was a man of such profound and magnificent melancholy that the sun, out of a certain courteous respect for his dignity, often seemed to dim when he passed by. His kingdom was a place of exquisite, if somewhat suffocating, beauty; it was all marble fountains that chimed in the wind, rose gardens that bloomed with an aggressive vitality, and statues of gods who looked as though they were perpetually on the verge of a severe headache. Aethelgard had not laughed in forty years. He had not laughed since the day he discovered that the moon was merely a reflection, and not a lover who had turned her face away from him. This revelation had broken his heart, or at least his heart’s illusion, and he had decided that the only sane response to the absurdity of existence was to sit upon his throne, wearing a crown of cold silver, and to look at everything with the weary skepticism of a man who has seen too many comedies and not enough tragedies.

It was said that the King’s sorrow was a national asset. The poets wrote elegies to his frown; the painters captured the shadow of his brow in oils of deep indigo and charcoal. To laugh, in Veridia, was considered a vulgar act, a cracking of the surface that allowed the chaos beneath to escape. Therefore, when Aethelgard, in a moment of whimsical despair that was more philosophical than emotional, declared that he would give away half his kingdom to anyone who could make him smile, the entire court erupted into a frenzy of intellectual panic.

The first to try was Lord Pemberton, the court poet, a man whose verses were as long as his dinner plates and twice as digestible. He stood before the throne, trembling with the effort of his own artifice, and recited a sonnet on the transience of joy. It was a masterpiece of technical perfection, every rhyme locked tight as a jewel in a setting. Aethelgard listened, his eyes half-closed, and when Pemberton finished with a bow that could have been mistaken for a genuflection, the King merely said, “You have arranged your words very neatly, Pemberton. But you have not said anything. And one cannot smile at silence, no matter how well it is upholstered.”

Next came the philosophers, a group of men who believed that if they could only think hard enough, they could out-think the concept of humor. They presented a treatise on the logical impossibility of joy, arguing that laughter was a glitch in the human operating system. Aethelgard read it, nodded gravely, and asked them to leave. “You are too clever to be funny,” he said. “And I am too tired to be convinced.”

The jesters came last, for they were the most dangerous. They wore motley coats that looked like the skins of exotic, diseased animals. They jingled bells that had no tune. They told jokes that were so old they had fossilized. One of them, a man with a face like a distorted orange, performed a somersault into a basket of lilies, emerging with petals in his hair and a grimace on his face. Aethelgard watched this spectacle with the indifference of a man watching a cat try to catch a shadow. “You are attempting to provoke me,” the King said, his voice as smooth as polished obsidian. “But you have forgotten that I am not a child. I am a king. And I have seen too much of the world to be amused by a man falling down. Besides, you fall down with such a lack of style. It is an aesthetic offense.”

The court fell silent. The fountains continued to chatter their endless, meaningless prattle. The King returned to his thoughts, which were, as always, dark and heavy as wet wool.

It was in this atmosphere of stifled expectation that Elara entered the Great Hall. Elara was a kitchen maid, a girl of eighteen who possessed a beauty that was unassuming, much like a simple white lily that grows in a shadow. She did not know about the offer. She did not know that the King’s smile was worth half the land. She knew only that she had been sent to bring the midday meal to the monarch, a task she performed with a quiet, efficient grace that was her own particular form of art.

She carried a silver tray. Upon it sat a row of tarts, their crusts golden and flaky, their fillings a vibrant mix of raspberry and lemon. They were warm, and the scent of them rose in a sweet, pungent cloud, cutting through the stale, perfumed air of the hall. Elara walked down the long aisle, her eyes fixed on the floor, her heart beating with the nervousness of one who is about to enter the presence of a god, though she suspected this god was merely a man who had forgotten how to breathe.

She reached the foot of the throne. The King looked down at her. He saw her small, brown hands, trembling slightly on the silver rim. He saw the flush of pink on her cheeks, a color that was forbidden in the court. For a moment, time seemed to suspend itself. The fountains stopped their chattering. The air grew still.

Elara, overwhelmed by the weight of the moment and the sudden, inexplicable terror of the King’s gaze, made a movement that was entirely natural and entirely disastrous. Her hand slipped. The tray tilted.

The tarts did not fall with the grace of a dancer. They fell with the chaotic, splattering force of a small, sugary bomb. The tray clattered against the marble floor, a sound that was sharp and final. The tarts bounced, rolled, and smashed. Raspberry juice splattered across the white marble, creating a pattern that looked like a map of a strange, red country. Lemon flesh glistened in the light. The smell of crushed fruit and hot pastry filled the hall, overwhelming the scent of the royal incense.
