The first time I saw the creature, I attributed it to the fog and the fatigue of watching. It was the third week of September in 1894, and a thick, wet fog had settled over the Flannan Isles. I am Budge, the principal keeper of the Eilean Mor light, and I tell you this as an ordinary man of practical habits, not given to drink or fancies. I was trimming the wicks in the lantern room a little before two in the morning, my assistant MacPherson snoring heavily in the quarters below, when my eye caught a strange phosphorescence in the water near the eastern rocks.

I took up my night-glass and looked out. The light swept across the water and revealed, for a moment, an extraordinary mass of dark, gelatinous flesh surging up from the deep. It possessed a peculiar, almost geometric regularity—a vast, dome-like carapace that caught the beam of the light and threw it back with a strange, metallic gleam. Below this dome, a confusion of thick, greenish tentacles churned the water into foam. I rubbed my eyes, feeling a sudden, unaccustomed foolishness, and when I looked again, the thing had slipped entirely beneath the black water.

I mentioned the matter to MacPherson the next morning, a dour, heavy man with little imagination. He grunted, suggested I eat less salt pork before bed, and thought no more of it. But I saw it again two nights later, and twice more the week following. Each time it appeared, it seemed merely to bask in the glare of our lamps, its tentacles trailing idly, displaying no hostility whatsoever. I began to keep a log of it, with the precise, cold details of a mechanic, though my heart beat with a very unscientific curiosity.

My report, transmitted by the supply steamer, eventually reached the Royal Society in Edinburgh. They dispatched a man named Dr. Calloway. He arrived on the next provision boat, laden with a curious assortment of baggage. Calloway was a tall, earnest, and rather argumentative young man, with a shock of pale hair and the impatient, bustling manner of someone accustomed to dissecting small things in a quiet room. He brought a heavy, mahogany-mounted plate camera, two crates of chemical plates, and a formidable .450 black-powder rifle.

“It is unquestionably a cephalopod of unrecorded magnitude, Budge,” he told me, as we hauled his equipment up the iron steps. “A deep-sea titan driven up by volcanic disturbances, or perhaps some aberration of the currents. We must secure a specimen. A photograph will secure my reputation, but a bullet through its mantle will secure the truth of its anatomy.”

I did not like his tone. I had watched the creature for a month, and a queer, protective sentiment had grown in me. It seemed a peaceful, isolated monster, coming up out of the blackness merely to stare at our light.

We kept a vigil for three nights. Calloway established his camera in the external gallery, wrapping it in tarpaulins against the spray. He talked incessantly, irritating MacPherson and exhausting me with his vivisectionist ambitions. He spoke of cutting into the creature, of boiling its flesh to strip the cartilage, of displaying its beak in a glass jar in London. I found myself hoping, with a surprising vehemence, that the beast would not show itself.

But on the fourth night, the fog cleared, and the sea grew calm. The great beam of the lantern swung out over the glassy water, and there, precisely where I had seen it before, the creature rose. It was immense, larger than our supply boat, its carapace gleaming with a wet, silvery iridescence. It floated there, its long tentacles drifting just beneath the surface like the fronds of some monstrous kelp.

Calloway became terribly agitated. He fumbled with the dark slides, his hands shaking as he withdrew the cap from the lens. “Hold the lamp steady, Budge!” he snapped, his voice high and unnatural. “I have it. Fifteen seconds of exposure. Hold!”

He counted the seconds under his breath. Then, with a satisfied exclamation, he dropped the cap back over the lens. “The plate is made,” he said, turning to me with bright, greedy eyes. “Now for the specimen.”

He reached for the heavy rifle propped against the railing. A sudden, violent resentment seized me. I thought of the peaceful, drifting thing out there in the dark, and this wiry, over-educated man with his destructive weapon.

“The light is shifting,” I said, stepping deliberately in front of him. “I must go in and adjust the clockwork.”

“Damn the clockwork!” Calloway cried, shoving past me. He threw the rifle to his shoulder, the brass sight glinting in the lamplight.

I acted on a blind impulse. As he squeezed the trigger, I struck the barrel upward with my open palm. The rifle roared, deafening us both, and the bullet tore into the sky. Calloway staggered back, the smoke engulfing us, and turned on me with a fury I had not anticipated in a man of science.

“You clumsy fool!” he shouted. “You’ve ruined it! The shot will have driven it down!”

I stood my ground, rubbing my stinging hand. “I am sorry, Dr. Calloway,” I said, though I was not sorry at all. “The recoil startled me. I am not accustomed to firearms.”

He glared at me, breathing hard, plainly suspecting my sabotage but unable to prove it. He turned back to the sea. The creature, startled by the noise, was already sinking. I watched its great dome subside into the black water, the tentacles folding gracefully beneath it, and felt a profound, irrational relief.

Calloway developed his plate the next morning in the darkroom, cursing under his breath the entire time. The photograph, when fixed, was a marvel—a blurred, ghostly silhouette of the beast against the white water, its immense proportions vaguely defined. It was enough to make his reputation, but not enough to satisfy his anatomical greed. He left on the return steamer three days later, still regarding me with cold suspicion, clutching his precious glass negative in its padded box.
