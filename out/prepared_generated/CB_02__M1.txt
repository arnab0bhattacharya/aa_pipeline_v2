The rain had not ceased since morning. It ran down the panes of the schoolroom in grey rivulets, blurring the great chimney of Worrell Mill, which stood up black and square against the churning sky. Inside, the fire was built too high and threw a restless, orange glare upon the walls; the heat was oppressive, yet I felt cold to the bone.

"Miss Arden," cried little Clara, holding up her slate, "is the world really round, or is it flat like the map?"

"It is round, Clara," I answered, my voice sounding thin and far away, like a wire vibrating in the wind. "Though it seems flat to those who walk upon it."

"But if it is round," persisted Lucy, the second eldest, knitting her brow in a way that reminded me painfully of her brother, "why do we not fall off when we go to the bottom?"

"Because God holds all things in His hand," I said. The words were automatic, a catechism I had repeated a hundred times, but today they felt like a lie. Did God hold things together? Or did He allow them to shatter?

The door opened. I did not need to look up to know who stood there. The atmosphere in the room changed; the air, already heavy, seemed to thicken and press against my lungs. I knew the step, the rustle of the coat.

"Mr. Worrell," I said, rising and dropping a curtsy. I kept my eyes fixed upon the globe on the table, tracing the continents with my finger, lest he should see the devastation written in mine.

"Miss Arden," said Edmund Worrell. His voice was rich and deep, the voice of a man accustomed to command, yet tempered with a gentleness that had been my undoing. "I trust the children are behaving?"

"They are industrious, sir."

"Good." He walked to the window and stood there, a tall silhouette against the grey rain. He did not look at me. He looked out at the mill, at the smoke that poured relentlessly into the wet air. "The weather is foul. I fear the roads to Ferndean will be impassable by evening."

My hand froze upon the globe. Ferndean. The seat of the Hargreaves family. The home of Miss Adelaide Hargreaves.

"Indeed, sir," I managed to say. "The mud is deep."

He turned then. He looked handsome—too handsome. His dark hair was swept back, his brow noble. There was a light in his eyes I had dared to interpret as kindness, perhaps even as a spark of that other thing I could not name. But now I saw it clearly: it was the light of satisfaction. Of a man who has secured his prize.

"I am to dine there," he said, a slight smile playing about his lips. "Mrs. Hargreaves is eager to discuss the settlement. It is all but concluded, Miss Arden. Adelaide has consented."

There it was. The blow, struck clean and hard. I felt the blood rush to my face, then recede, leaving me icy. I bowed my head.

"Congratulations are due, sir," I said. My voice did not tremble. I praised God for that small mercy. "Miss Hargreaves is a lady of great fortune and accomplishment."

"She is," Edmund said. He came closer to the table, leaning his hand upon it. He was so near I could smell the scent of rain and tobacco on him. "She will make a fine mistress for this house. It is a match of... expediency, of course. The merger of the Worrell and Hargreaves interests will secure the mill for the next generation. But she is also... amiable."

*Amiable.* The word struck me as vulgar. I thought of her—pale, silk-clad, idle, with hands that had never known work or cold. What did she know of the fire in the grate, or the scripture that sustains a lonely heart?

"I am sure she will, sir," I said.

"Miss Arden," he said, and his voice dropped lower, becoming confidential. I hated him then, hated him for the intimacy he offered without right. "You have been with us three years. You have been... excellent. A rock in the household. I want you to know that your position is secure. When Adelaide comes, she will need a companion for the children, perhaps. You shall not be cast out."

*Not cast out.* As if I were a stray dog he had taken in out of pity. The injustice of it rose in my throat like bile. I, who had taught his sisters to read, who had managed his household accounts when the housekeeper was ill, who had sat up night after night nursing Clara through the scarlet fever—I was to be the servant, the fixture, while she, the doll, was to be the queen.

"You are too kind, sir," I said. The restraint was costing me everything. I felt as though I were holding back a torrent with my bare hands. "But I am a governess. I do not seek to be a companion to the mistress of the house. I seek only to teach the children."

He looked at me, a flicker of confusion in his eyes. He was not used to such candor. "You are proud, Miss Arden."

"I am honest, sir. There is a difference."

He straightened up, his momentary warmth cooling. "Very well. See that the girls are ready for their music lesson at four. And tell Mrs. Pike to have the carriage ready at five."

He left the room. The door clicked shut with a finality that echoed in the silence. The fire crackled, a log shifting and sending a shower of sparks up the chimney.

"Miss Arden?" It was Clara again. "Why are you crying?"

I touched my cheek. It was wet. I had not known it. "I am not crying, child. It is the heat of the room. It makes the eyes water."
