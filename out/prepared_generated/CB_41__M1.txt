The coach wheel struck a stone, and the whole vehicle lurched, throwing Miss Vance against the door. She did not cry out, though the bruise upon her shoulder promised to be a dark one. She had learned long ago that to complain of physical discomfort in the presence of Mr. Ashworth was to invite his contempt.

He sat opposite her, a figure of granite and shadow, his greatcoat buttoned to the chin, his hat pulled low over a brow that seemed perpetually furrowed in thought. Between them, wrapped in a cloak of grey wool, slept little Clara—six years old, and the sole reason for this expedition through the driving rain of the Yorkshire moors.

For three hours, silence had reigned, broken only by the clatter of hooves and the hiss of the wind. Mr. Ashworth had been reading a sheaf of papers—legal documents, by the look of them—ignoring the governess as completely as he ignored the landscape. But as the light began to fail, turning the heather outside to a bruised purple, he folded the papers and looked up.

"Miss Vance," he said.

"Sir?"

"We shall reach the inn at Tadcaster within the hour. Are you quite worn out?"

The question startled her. It was not the words themselves, but the tone. There was no command in it, no perfunctory civility. He spoke as a man speaks to an equal, his voice stripped of its usual bark.

"I am tolerably well," she replied, keeping her voice steady, though her heart began to beat with a strange, rapid throb. "The motion of the coach is tiresome, but I am used to endurance."

"Endurance," he repeated, leaning back and crossing his arms. "It is a harsh word. A governess’s lot, I suppose, is to endure—to endure the whims of children, the coldness of employers, the solitude of a life that belongs to no one."

Miss Vance looked at him sharply. Was he mocking her? She searched his face for the cynical curl of the lip she knew so well, but found none. Only a weary, rugged honesty.

"It is a profession," she said, "and like all professions, it has its burdens. But I do not pity myself. Pity is a luxury for those who have nothing else to do."

Mr. Ashworth gave a short, barking laugh—a sound that, for once, held no malice. "You are fierce, Miss Vance. I have seen it in the schoolroom when Clara is naughty. You do not cower; you stand your ground. I admire that. God knows, I have had enough of people who cower."

The wind howled, rattling the glass, but inside the carriage, a strange heat seemed to rise. For the next mile, he spoke to her of his business in London, of the difficulties of the estate, and of his dead wife—not with the dry recitation of facts she expected, but with a raw, unguarded bitterness.

"She was a creature of glass," he said, staring out at the storm. "Beautiful, fragile, and easily shattered. She feared me. She feared the world. She left me this child, and I am terrified I shall break her, too."

"You will not," Miss Vance said, the words rushing out before she could check them. "Clara is not glass. She is of sterner stuff. She has your blood in her, sir. She has your fire."

He turned his eyes upon her then—dark, piercing eyes that seemed to strip away her grey dress and her quiet manners to look at the woman beneath. "And what of you, Miss Vance? Are you of sterner stuff?"

"I am a woman of the world," she said. "I have no choice but to be."

They spoke of scripture, too, for the rain had turned to hail, and the road was becoming a quagmire. He quoted Job, complaining of the darkness that covered him, and she, emboldened by his candor, answered with the words of Ruth. *Whither thou goest, I will go.* She meant it only as a philosophical comfort, a reminder of duty, but as the words left her lips, they hung in the air, charged and heavy.

Mr. Ashworth stared at her. The carriage swayed. For a moment, she thought he might reach out and take her hand. She felt a surge of emotion so violent it frightened her—a longing to comfort him, to touch the hard, cold face of her master and find the man beneath the magistrate.

But the moment passed. The coachman called out that Tadcaster was in sight. Mr. Ashworth withdrew into his coat, his face hardening like cooling iron.

The visit to the school was a blur of grey stone and weeping girls. Miss Vance stood by the gate, holding Clara’s hand, while Mr. Ashworth spoke to the headmistress. When the child was led away, crying out for her father, Miss Vance felt a tear track her own cheek. She brushed it away angrily. She had no right to weep.

The return journey began at dawn. The storm had broken, leaving the world washed and glistening, but the atmosphere within the coach was frozen.

Mr. Ashworth did not read. He sat with his eyes closed, or staring fixedly at the passing hedges. He did not speak of the business, nor of the weather, nor of the child they had left behind.

Miss Vance waited. She waited for the man who had spoken of Job and endurance to return. She waited for the companion who had looked at her as an equal.

"Sir," she ventured, when they were halfway home. "The roads are drying. We shall make good time."

"Yes," he said. He did not open his eyes.

"Clara will settle," she added. "She is brave."

"I trust she will." His voice was flat, devoid of color. It was the voice he used to the servants, to the dogs, to the obstacles in his path.
