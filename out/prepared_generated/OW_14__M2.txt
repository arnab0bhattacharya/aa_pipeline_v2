The invitation had arrived, wrapped in paper of a delicate, improbable pink, smelling faintly of lavender and scandal. It was from Reverend Arthur Pym, a man whose theology was as flimsy as his sermons and whose waistcoat buttons were always one size too small for his ambition. The recipient was Julian Vane, who had not entered a church since his confirmation, nor spoken a word of it since, preferring the company of orchids and opium to that of the pulpit and the psalm. Yet Julian accepted. It was not that he cared for the Almighty, or even for the concept of the afterlife, which seemed to him a rather tedious sequel to the first act; but he cared for the audience. He cared for the texture of their silence and the specific hue of their shock. To preach in a parish church was to introduce a foreign currency into a closed economy, and Julian was always in need of a change of scenery, if not of soul.

The church in Kensington was a structure of grey stone and good taste, smelling of beeswax and damp wool. It was the kind of place where one went to be bored in comfort, to sit in a pew upholstered in velvet the color of dried blood and to watch the sun filter through stained glass that depicted the saints with the weary patience of civil servants. On the day of the sermon, however, the pews were not merely occupied; they were besieged. The air was thick with the scent of expensive perfumes and the electric tension of the newly curious. Lady Mortlake, whose husband had recently discovered that his wife’s correspondence was more entertaining than his own, sat in the front row with a fan that trembled slightly in her hand. The Bishop, who had been invited to observe the progress of the experiment, sat in the vestry with a face the color of a bruised plum, his fingers drumming on the arm of his chair in a rhythm that suggested he was counting the minutes until he could flee the scene of the crime.

Julian entered the church wearing a suit of midnight blue, cut with a severity that was almost aggressive, and a white tie that seemed to glow with an inner, illicit light. He did not look like a preacher; he looked like a young man who had just decided to commit a murder for the sake of the aesthetic. He walked to the pulpit with a slow, deliberate grace, his eyes sweeping the congregation with a look of detached amusement. He did not open the Bible. He did not cross himself. He simply leaned forward, his chin resting on his hand, and began to speak.

His voice was low, a velvet murmur that seemed to vibrate in the very stones of the church. He spoke first of beauty, not as a divine attribute, but as a dangerous, consuming force. He described the garden of Eden not as a place of innocence, but as a nursery for the most terrible of arts: the art of self-deception. He spoke of the apple not as a fruit of knowledge, but as a jewel of forbidden knowledge, red and ripe, dripping with the juice of transgression. The congregation listened, entranced. The usual murmurs of discomfort, the shifting of feet, the rustling of programs, ceased. There was only the sound of Julian’s voice, smooth and cold as a river of mercury, flowing over the jagged rocks of their morality.

He spoke of sin not as a fall, but as a flight. He argued that to be good was to be mediocre, that virtue was the refuge of the unimaginative, a safe harbor for those who had no courage to sail into the storms of the soul. He described the sinner as a connoisseur, one who tasted the bitterness of the cup and found in it a depth of flavor that the pure water of righteousness could never offer. He spoke of the rose, how it must have thorns to be beautiful, how it must bleed to be red. He spoke of the lily, how it must grow in the shadow of the cross to be white. The metaphors were dazzling, each one a gem set in a setting of polished logic. The congregation was not merely listening; they were being seduced.

The Bishop, in the vestry, had stopped drumming on the chair. He was leaning forward, his eyes wide, his lips parted. He could not help but listen. The words were wrong, they were heretical, they were an affront to everything he had spent his life defending, and yet they were so beautifully arranged that they seemed to possess a truth more compelling than the truth he knew. It was the danger of it, the sheer audacity of the inversion, that held him. He realized, with a sudden, chilling clarity, that he was not angry because he was offended, but because he was jealous. He was jealous of the power of the words, of the way they hung in the air like incense, of the way they made the impossible seem inevitable.

In the nave, Lady Mortlake had dropped her fan. It lay on the floor, a small, white object of shattered composure. She was staring at Julian, her eyes wide and dark, seeing in his face not the face of a clergyman, but the face of a mask, a perfect, terrible mask that hid the void. She felt a sudden, sharp pain in her chest, not of guilt, but of recognition. She had spent her life trying to be good, trying to be respectable, trying to fit into the mold that society had provided, and now, in this moment, she realized that she had been merely polishing the surface of a stone, while the stone itself remained cold and inert. She wanted to break the stone. She wanted to see what was inside.
