That a young woman of five-and-twenty, possessing neither a large fortune nor an extensive acquaintance, should be conveyed by a wealthy friend to a fashionable assembly in town, is an event which the world is generally very ready to applaud; but whether the young woman herself is always disposed to join in the applause, is a point upon which the world is apt to be somewhat less inquisitive.

Miss Clara Wynne, the daughter of a respectable though narrow-circumstanced clergyman from the parish of Delamere in Hertfordshire, found her own sentiments on the matter considerably divided as she stood near the doorway of the assembly rooms in Mansfield Street. She was visiting her friend, Mrs. Isabella Fortescue, a lady possessed of every advantage of fortune and consequence that Miss Wynne lacked, and whose kindness in bringing her to London was exceeded only by her determination to display her whenever the opportunity arose. Mrs. Fortescue had no sooner crossed the threshold of the room than she had whisked away to join a circle of acquaintance, leaving Miss Wynne to the quiet contemplation of her own solitude.

Miss Wynne was not naturally of a complaining disposition. She had been bred in the country, and was accustomed to rely upon her own reflections for entertainment when society proved unkind; but the imposing grandeur of a London assembly, with its glittering chandeliers and whispering crowds, might have daunted a firmer mind. She knew not a single face in the room, and though she had been dressed with considerable care by Mrs. Fortescue’s own maid, she felt that her modest muslin, however well it might become her in Hertfordshire, could scarcely compete with the silks and velvets that swept past her with an air of sovereign indifference.

She had stationed herself near a pillar, from whence she could observe the company without appearing to intrude upon it, and was beginning to amuse herself by mentally cataloguing the various absurdities of dress and manner that presented themselves to her view, when she perceived that she was herself the object of scrutiny. An elderly gentleman, of a decidedly comfortable appearance, had paused at a little distance and was regarding her with a benevolent curiosity that could not be mistaken for anything but the most innocent attention.

He was perhaps sixty years of age, with a countenance that spoke of good humour and good living in equal measure. His coat was of a fashion rather antiquated, but worn with an easy assurance that rendered the question of fashion entirely immaterial. Perceiving that she had noticed him, he approached with a civil bow and a frankness of address that surprised her into a smile.

“I perceive, madam,” said he, “that you are suffering under the peculiar affliction of knowing nobody. I am the more confirmed in this suspicion because I observe that your friend has deserted you, and you are left to the support of that pillar, which, though unquestionably substantial, is not celebrated for the brilliancy of its conversation.”

Miss Wynne, though a little startled by so direct an opening, could not but be amused by it. She replied with a smile that she was indeed a stranger in London, and that she had no claim upon the pillar beyond its being a fixed point in a room that appeared to be in constant motion.

“A fixed point!” cried the gentleman, with great appearance of satisfaction. “How admirable! I myself have been a fixed point for the last quarter of an hour, but I had not the ingenuity to phrase it so prettily. You are staying with friends, I collect?”

“With Mrs. Fortescue, sir. She is so good as to convey me about town, though I fear I am but a poor companion for such elegance.”

“Fortescue! Isabella Fortescue? To be sure—I know her husband’s family tolerably well. A very showy set, the Fortescues; very fond of a crowd, and very well suited to it. And you, madam—you are not of London, I think?”

“I am from the country, sir. My father is the rector of Delamere, in Hertfordshire.”

“Delamere!” exclaimed the gentleman, with a sudden animation that startled her. “You amaze me! I know Delamere, or at least I knew it, some five-and-twenty years ago. A pretty village, as I recall, with a very fine avenue of limes leading up to the church. Is old Dr. Wynne still there?”

Miss Wynne coloured with surprise. “My father succeeded Dr. Wynne, sir, some twelve years since. But I fear the avenue of limes has been much diminished. A storm in the winter of 1809 carried away three of the finest.”

“Three of the finest!” repeated the old gentleman, in a tone of lively concern, as though the loss were a personal calamity. “How shocking! I remember them well—noble trees, with a sweep and a droop that was quite beautiful. I dined at the rectory once, and was very kindly entertained. But that was before your time, I suppose. You would have been a child.”

“I was not yet born, sir,” said Miss Wynne, who found herself insensibly drawn into the conversation by the old gentleman’s unaffected warmth. He had the art of making one feel that the fate of a Hertfordshire avenue was a matter of genuine and immediate importance, and Miss Wynne, who had spent the last hour feeling herself of no importance at all, was not ungrateful for the change.

They fell into an easy discussion of country matters, of the changes that time had wrought, and of the peculiar follies of London society, which the old gentleman surveyed with an indulgent contempt that was infinitely entertaining. He was full of quaint observations and mild paradoxes, and Miss Wynne found herself laughing more than once at his dry remarks upon the company. He pointed out to her a lady of imposing grandeur whose feathers were, he assured her, a direct violation of all the laws of proportion; and a young man whose expression of settled melancholy was, he suspected, the result of a too rigid adherence to the dictates of his tailor rather than any genuine affliction of the spirit.
