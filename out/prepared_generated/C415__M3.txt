The frost had taken the last of the marigolds, leaving the garden at Yarrow Cottage a study in brown stalks and black earth. Elara pulled her wool shawl tighter around her shoulders, watching her breath plume in the November air. It was the third year of the Reclamation, the period the provisional government in Reading called the slow crawl back to normalcy, though normalcy felt like a foreign country. The pestilence had swept through the Thames Valley with a terrible, democratic efficiency, taking the old and the young, leaving the middle-aged to bury them. It had taken her twins, Leo and Maisie, during the first bitter winter, turning their bright voices into a silence so profound it had its own weight.

She turned to go back inside, her boots crunching on the gravel, when she saw him. He was standing by the gate, a boy of perhaps six, small for his age, bundled in an oversized, drab military coat that hung past his knees. His hair was the colour of dirty straw, and his eyes, fixed on her, were the flat, unblinking grey of the winter sky. He didn't speak. None of the orphaned children did, not at first. The medics called it systemic withdrawal, a retreat into an inner fortress when the outer world became too unbearable.

Elara gripped the edge of the door, her knuckles whitening. "Go away," she said, her voice hoarse from disuse. "I told Mrs. Thorne yesterday. I told her the day before. I can't take anyone in. Go back to the parish house."

The boy didn't move. He simply stood there, his small hands gripping the iron bars of the gate, which were cold enough to burn. Elara stared at him, feeling a familiar, terrible clenching in her chest, a mixture of grief and a fierce, protective anger. She couldn't afford to let that wound open again. She stepped inside and shut the door, turning the heavy iron key in the lock as though she could lock out the past along with the cold.

The village of Bramfield was a quiet place, nestled in a bend of the river where the water ran slow and dark. Half the cottages stood empty, their windows boarded up, their gardens gone to seed. The survivors were mostly women and older men, moving through their days with a careful, guarded energy, tending their root vegetables and their solar arrays, trading news but rarely opinions. Everyone had lost someone. Grief was the common currency, and like all currency, it was rarely discussed openly.

Mrs. Thorne, the village elder, came by that afternoon, bringing a basket of dried beans and a look of weary persistence. She was a stout woman with a face like a weathered apple, and she had survived the pestilence by virtue of sheer stubbornness. She sat at Elara's scrubbed table, wrapping her hands around a mug of nettle tea, and tried again.

"He has nowhere else, Elara. The parish house is closing. They're sending the children to the work farms up north. He's too little. He'll not last a month."

Elara kept her back turned, scrubbing a pot that was already clean. "There are other women here. Women with children who survived. Women who know how to be mothers still."

"None will take him. He doesn't speak. He doesn't cry. He unsettles people."

"And you think he belongs with a woman who can't bear the sight of a child's coat on a hook?" Elara's voice cracked, and she set the pot down with a clatter. "I buried my children, Hester. I buried them in that garden because the cemetery was full and the ground was frozen. I can't dig another grave. I can't love something and watch it die again."

Mrs. Thorne was quiet for a long moment. "His name is Soren," she said finally. "He was found in a house in Reading, alone. His mother had been dead a week. He hasn't spoken a word since." She paused. "He keeps coming back here, Elara. Not to anyone else. To you."

Elara shook her head. "That doesn't mean anything."

"Perhaps not. But he's at your gate again now."

He was there the next morning, and the morning after that. He came at dawn and left at dusk, sitting on the frozen ground by the gate like a small, patient sentinel. Elara tried to ignore him. She went about her tasks, splitting kindling, mending the solar charger, baking flatbread on the cast-iron stove. But she found herself glancing out the window, counting the hours he sat there, watching the way the wind whipped the oversized coat around his thin legs.

On the fourth day, it rained, a cold, driving rain that turned the lanes to mud and made the river overflow its banks. Elara stood at her kitchen window, watching the rain streak the glass, and saw him still sitting there, water streaming down his face, his hair plastered to his skull. He wasn't shivering. That was what frightened her. He had gone beyond shivering, beyond the body's ordinary protests, into a stillness that looked like surrender.

She stood in the middle of her kitchen, her hands clenched at her sides, and fought a battle that had no words. She thought of Leo's laugh, a bright, spinning sound that had filled this room. She thought of Maisie's careful fingers tying knots in coloured thread. She thought of the way they had looked at the end, small and still and utterly gone, and the sound she had made, a sound that wasn't crying, that wasn't anything human.

She opened the door. The rain hit her face, and she walked down the path, her shoes sinking in the mud, and stood over him. He looked up at her, and his eyes were the same flat grey, but there was something in them, a question, or perhaps just the simple, animal fact of endurance.

"You'll catch your death," she said.
