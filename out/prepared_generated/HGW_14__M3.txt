It is a curious thing, the quiet tragedy of a chemist’s shop in a London suburb. You see the polished bottles, the neat rows of blue and green glass, the shining scales, and you think only of the sober science that presides over human ailments. You do not think of the stifled ambitions behind the counter. I had studied pharmaceutical chemistry with a diligence that surprised even my instructors. I had passed the Minor examination, and my knowledge of materia medica was, I flatter myself, sound. Yet the examining board, with that magnificent blandness which characterizes committees, declined to register me. I was a woman. That fact, it seemed, was an absolute bar to my practising as a pharmacist. The medical logic of it was entirely absent, but the social logic was inexorable.

I was left with a thorough knowledge of alkaloids and a very small income. It was in this state of bafflement that I applied to Mr. Prout, who kept the dispensing chemist’s shop at the corner of Larkfield Lane. Mr. Prout was a man of faltering intellect and a remarkable digestion. He had qualified in the days when pharmacy was a matter of rote learning and Latin abbreviations, and he found the modern advances in organic chemistry vaguely impertinent. He agreed to employ me, not as an assistant, for that would have violated the proprieties, but as a “shop-woman and bookkeeper.” His actual proposition, however, was far more practical. He was tired of the tedious labour of compounding, and I, it turned out, was exceedingly good at it.

So it was that for eighteen months I practically conducted the dispensing business of Larkfield Lane. Mr. Prout would receive the prescriptions, peer at them with a frowning intensity, and then pass them through the flap to the little back-room where I worked. Here, with the precision of a mechanician, I would weigh out the morphine, measure the strychnine, and titrate the tinctures. I would label the bottles, and Mr. Prout would carry them to the counter, receive the shillings, and bow the customer out with an air of profound professional responsibility. It was a situation that would have amused me by its sheer humbug, had it not been so intensely aggravating. I was doing the work of a pharmacist, but because I was a woman, I was denied the name and the pay.

The crisis came on a wet Tuesday in November. Mr. Prout had gone out to lunch, leaving me alone in the shop. A prescription was brought in by a maidservant, an urgent compound for an elderly gentleman, a Mr. Gribble, who lived in the big house on the hill. It was a heart tonic, containing, among other things, a tincture of digitalis and a small, carefully adjusted dose of strychnine. I made it up with my usual exactitude, filtering the liquid and corking the bottle. When Mr. Prout returned, smelling faintly of gin and pickles, he glanced at the label, muttered something about the Gribbles, and handed it over the counter when the maid returned.

By four o’clock that afternoon, the quiet of the shop was shattered by the violent ringing of the doorbell. I went out from my back-room. There stood a young man I recognized as Mr. Gribble’s son, his face white with anger and alarm.

“Where is Mr. Prout?” he demanded. “My father is dying! He has been poisoned by your medicine!”

I felt a sudden coldness in my chest. Mr. Prout, who had been dozing by the gas-fire, stumbled to the counter, his face a picture of foolish terror.

“Poisoned? Nonsense, sir! I made it up myself. A digitalis mixture—”

“Digitalis be damned!” cried the young man. “The doctor says it’s atropine! Or some deadly nightshade preparation! He is raving, his eyes are dilated, and his pulse is racing! What have you given him?”

I did not wait to hear Mr. Prout’s stammering denials. I turned and went quickly into the back-room. My mind was working with a terrible, cold clarity. Atropine? I had not used atropine. I went to the bench where I had compounded the mixture. The bottles were still out, uncleaned. I picked up the glass beaker I had used for the strychnine solution. There was a faint, sticky residue at the bottom. I held it to my nose. The odour was unmistakable. It was not the strychnine bottle I had used. In the dim light of the back-room, I had taken a bottle from the shelf one place to the left. The labels were identical in size and colour, a foolish system of Mr. Prout’s own devising to save money on printed labels. I had used atropine instead of strychnine.

For a moment I stood perfectly still, staring at the little beaker. I had made a mistake. It was a mistake any human being might make, but it was my mistake, and it had nearly killed a man. And then, with a curious swift succession of thoughts, the whole situation swung round before me. If I said nothing, Mr. Prout would be blamed. He would be exposed as an ignoramus, perhaps struck off, certainly ruined. And I? I should be dismissed, my one precarious source of income gone. But if I spoke—if I said, “It was I who made the prescription, and I who made the error”—what then? I should be confessing to an illegal act. I was an unregistered woman practising pharmacy. I should be prosecuted, fined, perhaps even imprisoned. And Mr. Gribble would still be ill.

I came back to the counter. The young man was shaking Mr. Prout by the lapels.

“You shall answer for this!” he was crying. “You and your incompetent—”

“Stop,” said I. My voice sounded singularly clear and steady in that little shop. “It was not Mr. Prout’s error. It was mine.”

They both turned and stared at me. I think the young man was the more astonished of the two. Mr. Prout’s mouth fell open, and I saw a look of dawning hope in his weak eyes.
