I took the lodgings at the Sign of the Blue Anchor in the village of Sandgate-Under-Cliff for the specific purpose of being undisturbed. It was November, the season was long dead, and the town, with its shuttered windows and its bathing-machines standing forlorn and idle on the shingle, was precisely the sort of place where a man of science might pursue his investigations without the constant interruption of well-meaning friends or the idle curiosity of the metropolis. My landlady, a Mrs. Pepperdine, was a widow of considerable girth and a perpetually furrowed brow, who regarded my arrival with a mixture of satisfaction at the extra shilling a week and a deep, unvoiced suspicion of any man who should choose to reside at the seaside in November. My requirements—a room with a good south light, a sturdy table, and absolute privacy—she met with a nod and a series of sighs that seemed to suggest the world was going to the devil in general and that my presence was merely one more proof of it.

My work was of a nature that I flattered myself was on the very verge of a profound significance. I had for some years been experimenting with certain salts of radium and their effect upon the osmotic processes of vegetable tissue. The details need not concern the general reader; it is sufficient to say that I had formulated a compound—a thick, amber-coloured fluid, which I shall call, for want of a better name, the Nutrient—which I believed would so stimulate the natural functions of a plant as to produce a rate of growth hitherto undreamt of. My first trials, conducted upon a few beans in a saucer, had been encouraging, if not altogether conclusive. The beans had sprouted with a vigour that was almost aggressive. I resolved upon a larger experiment, and it was for this purpose that I had come to Sandgate-Under-Cliff.

Behind the Blue Anchor was a small, walled garden, sadly neglected in the winter months, containing a few straggling cabbages, a bare rose-bush or two, and a line of broad-bean plants that had seen better days. Mrs. Pepperdine, when I broached the subject of my using a portion of this plot for my experiments, was visibly taken aback.

"Whatever for, sir?" she asked, her hands folded upon her apron.

"An experiment, madam," I replied, with what I hoped was an air of reassuring authority. "I am a chemist. I wish to test the effect of a certain preparation upon the growth of plants."

She looked at me as though I had proposed to conduct a séance in her parlour. "We've had a gentleman here before," she said darkly, "what painted pictures of the sea. He was very quiet. But a chemist, sir—"

"I assure you, Mrs. Pepperdine, there is nothing in the least improper about it. A few plants. A little watering. That is all."

She consented, reluctantly, and I set to work at once. I selected a single broad-bean plant as my principal subject, and upon its roots I poured a measured quantity of the Nutrient, diluted according to my calculations. I then retired to my room to await developments.

The first day, nothing. The second day, I fancied the plant looked a trifle greener, a shade more erect. By the third day, I was certain. The bean plant had grown at least six inches. By the fourth, it was a foot taller, and its leaves, which had been small and withered, were now broad and of a deep, almost unnatural green. I was elated. I had, I felt sure, made a discovery of the first magnitude. I began to keep a careful record of its progress, measuring it morning and evening, and noting the changes in its appearance with the meticulous care of a man who knows he is on the brink of a great scientific triumph.

By the fifth day, the plant had reached a height of some four feet, and its lower stem was as thick as my wrist. The other plants in the garden, the cabbages and the roses, seemed to be shrinking away from it, as though in a kind of vegetable terror. Mrs. Pepperdine, who had occasion to pass through the garden on her way to the coal-cellar, stopped and stared.

"Lord save us, sir!" she exclaimed. "What have you been doing to that bean?"

"Merely a little fertiliser, madam," I said, with a careless wave of my hand. "A new preparation. It seems to be effective."

"Effective?" She stared at the towering plant, then at me. "It's unnatural, that's what it is. I've never seen a bean grow like that. It's not right."

I assured her that it was perfectly right, that it was, in fact, a triumph of modern science, but she went on her way muttering to herself, and I could see that her suspicions were not allayed.

The sixth day, the plant was eight feet high. The seventh, it was twelve, and its branches, thick and succulent, were spreading across the garden, pushing against the wall, and beginning to overhang the path to the kitchen door. The other plants had been completely overwhelmed; the cabbages were buried under a mass of green, and the roses had been crushed into the earth. The garden was no longer a garden; it was a jungle. The bean plant, with its enormous leaves and its rapid, almost audible growth, seemed to be reaching for the sky itself. I was exultant, but I was also beginning to be a little alarmed. The growth was not merely rapid; it was aggressive. It seemed to be actively seeking to expand, to dominate, to consume.

Mrs. Pepperdine, when she saw it, let out a shriek and dropped the tray of tea she was carrying.

"Mr. Blenkinsop!" she cried. "Mr. Blenkinsop, it's got a pod!"

I looked. It was true. A pod had formed, a pod of monstrous size, as long as my arm and as thick as a marrow. It hung from the plant like some grotesque fruit, and even as I watched, it seemed to swell and grow.
