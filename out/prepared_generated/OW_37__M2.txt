Lord Vane sat in the window seat of the drawing room, his back to the sunlight, which fell in a shaft of dusty gold upon the Persian rug. He was a man who had long ago discovered that the most effective way to maintain one’s authority was to appear bored by the world’s efforts to amuse him. He held a glass of sherry, the amber liquid trembling slightly with the vibration of his own suppressed irritation.

Across the room, Lady Vane—formerly Mrs. Alaric, though the name had been buried under so much velvet and social expectation that it had ceased to have any resonance—was arranging a bouquet of white orchids. She did this with the precise, detached elegance of a woman who has learned that beauty is a form of discipline, and that a crushed petal is a moral failure.

“The letter is from the Duchess of Cray,” Lord Vane said, not turning his head. “It seems she requires your presence at the Matinée for the relief of the underprivileged. I am told the cause is somewhat vague, but the cause of the invitation is entirely clear.”

Lady Vane smiled, a faint, curving line that did not reach her eyes. “The cause is poverty, my dear. Or so they claim. I suspect the cause is boredom. The upper classes are currently suffering from a severe excess of leisure and a total deficit of interest. They wish to see me act. It is the only time they can feel superior to me without having to actually do anything.”

Lord Vane turned then. His face was pale, his features sharp as a blade wrapped in silk. “I forbade it, Elara. I forbade it with a clarity that should have been audible from the park. I told you that your career was over. I told you that you were to be a wife, not a spectacle. I bought you jewels, I bought you a house, I bought you a reputation. I did not buy you a stage.”

“And yet here we are,” she replied, placing the last orchid with a soft click. “You have bought me a reputation, yes. And what is a reputation, my lord? It is merely a debt that society pays to itself for the privilege of gossiping about us. If I stay at home, they will say I am bored. If I go to the theatre, they will say I am foolish. In either case, they are entertained. I might as well be interesting.”

“You are not interesting. You are inconvenient. There is a difference. The interesting woman is the one who stays in the background and lets the man shine. The inconvenient woman steps into the light and blocks the view.”

“Then let me block the view,” she said softly. “I have blocked it for years, and I find it tedious. I shall go, Lord Vane. I shall wear the emerald gown, the one with the train that cost more than a year’s rent in the East End. I shall smile at the Duke, and I shall recite Shakespeare with such precision that the critics will weep with professional jealousy. And you will sit in the box, looking dignified, and you will pretend that you are not watching me. But you will be watching. You are always watching. It is the only thing you are good at.”

Lord Vane set down his glass. The sound was sharp, a small violence in the quiet room. “Do not speak to me of watching. I am a husband, not a voyeur. But be warned. If you go, you do so at the risk of my silence. And in this town, silence is louder than any applause. I shall not invite you to dinner. I shall not speak to you at the clubs. I shall let you stand alone in the eyes of the world, and you will see how cold it is to be admired by people who do not love you.”

Lady Vane picked up her fan, which was made of ivory and painted with a scene from *The Tempest*. She opened it slowly, the sound like the closing of a door. “My dear, you misunderstand the nature of the theatre. In the theatre, we are not loved. We are worshipped. And worship, unlike love, requires no reciprocation. It is a one-way traffic of adoration. I shall go. And I shall be magnificent. I shall be the thing you cannot control. That is the only rebellion left to a woman who has been polished to a mirror finish.”

She turned and walked toward the door. Her heels clicked on the marble floor, a rhythm as steady and indifferent as a clock. She paused at the threshold, her back to him, her silhouette framed by the bright hallway.

“Oh, and Lord Vane,” she said, her voice light, almost playful. “If you do choose to attend, do not sit in the front row. The light is too harsh there, and it shows the wrinkles in the curtain. Sit in the back. It is safer. You can judge me from the shadows. It suits you. You have always preferred the dark. It is where your opinions are most comfortable.”

She left him there, alone with the sherry and the orchids. The air in the room seemed to thicken, heavy with the scent of the flowers and the unspoken threat of his indifference. He looked at the bouquet, the white petals stark against the green leaves. They were perfect, and they were dead. He took a sip of the sherry, and for a moment, he envied the orchids their stillness.

The theatre in the West End was a place of dust and velvet, of shadows that moved like living things. The matinée was crowded with women in hats that looked like explosions of taffeta and men who wore their boredom like a second skin. The air smelled of perfume and old wood.
