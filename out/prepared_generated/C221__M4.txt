The bell above the door of Maison Lacroix had a particular pitch, a bright, silvery tinkle that Monsieur Étienne Lacroix had come to hear as the sound of guineas dropping into his pocket. It was the sound of Lady Ashworth’s arrival for her weekly pomade, of Mr. Fitzwilliam’s discreet consultation regarding his thinning crown, of the Duchess of Ellesmere’s demand for a coiffure that would make her the envy of every woman at the Vauxhall supper. In his sanctuary of gilded mirrors and crystal chandeliers, smelling of bergamot, lavender, and the faint, sharp tang of the hot irons, Étienne was a king. He wielded his scissors with the precision of a surgeon and his compliments with the flourish of a diplomat. He had learned, in his fifteen years in London, that the English aristocracy required not just a servant, but a confidant, a flatterer, an artist who could sculpt their anxieties into an edifice of unassailable style.

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, tucked between a bill from his silk merchant and an invitation to a card party in Bath. The handwriting was achingly familiar, a jagged, impatient scrawl that had not changed since boyhood. It was from his brother, Julien. Étienne’s fingers, so steady with a razor, trembled slightly as he broke the plain wax seal. The paper was coarse, cheap, and smelled faintly of woodsmoke and something else, something metallic and grim.

*Étienne,*

*I hope this finds you well in your palace of powder and lies. The people here in Saint-Aubin do not forget their sons, even those who have grown fat on the vanity of tyrants. The old order is crumbling, brother. The air is thick with change. We have formed a club, the Friends of Liberty, and I have been honoured to lead them. We speak of the Rights of Man, of a world without masters. You may laugh, from your velvet perch, but the tide is turning.*

*We are in need. Not for ourselves, but for the cause. To print our pamphlets, to buy bread for the hungry, to send word to our brothers in Paris. You have prospered. It is only right that you share a portion of your fortune to help liberate the land that made you. Send what you can. Do not think to ignore this. Blood is thicker than the Channel.*

*Your brother,*
*Julien*

Étienne read the letter three times, his face a mask of concentration. He could almost hear Julien’s voice, fervent and sharp, echoing in the small stone house where they had grown up, the son of a village barber. Julien had always been the passionate one, the one who argued with the priest and railed against the local seigneur. Étienne had been the pragmatist, the one who saw that the path to a better life was not through rebellion, but through servile excellence. He had fled the provincial dullness of Saint-Aubin for the glittering opportunity of London, and he had never looked back.

He folded the letter and tucked it into the pocket of his waistcoat, just as the bell tinkled again. It was Lady Ashworth, resplendent in a new gown of Pomona green.

“Monsieur Lacroix,” she cooed, settling into his chair. “I declare, the journey from Grosvenor Square has quite exhausted me. I have the most dreadful headache. You must work a miracle.”

“For you, my lady, only miracles are sufficient,” Étienne murmured, his hands already moving to unpin her hat. His voice was smooth as cream, his smile perfectly calibrated. But his mind was a thousand miles away, in a dusty village square, listening to the roar of a mob.

The change was subtle at first. A new coolness in the air, a shadow in the eyes of his clients. The Duchess of Ellesmere, who once prattled on about the Prince of Wales’s debts, now spoke in hushed, urgent tones about the mob in Paris and the dreadful events of August. She watched him in the mirror, her gaze sharp and assessing. “You are from France, are you not, Lacroix?” she asked one afternoon, her voice deceptively light. “From the provinces?”

“From Normandy, Your Grace,” he lied smoothly, the name of a more respectable, less volatile region on his tongue. “A quiet corner, far from the madness of Paris.”

“Indeed,” she said, and said no more. But the silence that followed was heavier than any words.

Mr. Fitzwilliam, usually so eager for gossip, fell silent when a footman brought news of another château burned. He looked at Étienne’s reflection in the glass, a flicker of something unreadable in his eyes—suspicion, perhaps, or fear. The tinkle of the bell began to sound less like coins and more like a warning. His clients still came, but they no longer lingered. They paid their bills with a briskness that bordered on insult. They saw him not as an artist, but as a Frenchman. A potential Jacobin. A viper in their midst, whose hands were paid to touch their necks.

Then came the second letter. It was delivered by a grubby, sharp-eyed boy who refused to meet Étienne’s gaze and vanished into the warren of Mayfair’s backstreets before a question could be asked. This time, the paper was finer, the handwriting more controlled, but the words were more chilling.

*Étienne,*

*You have not replied. Your silence is an answer in itself. Do you think your fine English lords will protect you? They will turn on you the moment it suits them. You are a foreigner, a hairdresser, a servant. You are nothing to them. But you are my brother. I remember the boy who taught me to read, who patched my knee when I fell. Is he gone? Has the gold of London turned his blood to water?*

*The club has grown. We are many. We have a printing press now, hidden in the old mill. But we need more. We need funds to buy muskets. The seigneur’s château is empty, but his influence lingers. We must be ready. This is not a request, Étienne. It is a duty. Send the money. Or I will be forced to think that you have chosen your side. And you will not like the consequences.*
