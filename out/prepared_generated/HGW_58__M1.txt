Mr. Arthur Pemberton was a man whose chief delight in life was the application of order to chaos. As a chief clerk in a London shipping office, he spent fifty weeks of the year in a world of ledgers, columns, and perfectly balanced accounts. His annual holiday, therefore, was not a descent into idleness but an ascent into a different, more agreeable sort of order: the geometric perfection of the bicycle, and the rational, if unpredictable, system of the English countryside.

His machine, a fine Rover Safety with a chain-drive and pneumatic tyres—a modern marvel he had dubbed ‘The Pelican’—carried him along the dusty lanes of Hampshire with a cheerful whirring that seemed to be the very sound of freedom. The air, thick with the scent of clover and sun-baked earth, was a tonic after the fug of the City. It was on a particularly fine Tuesday afternoon, somewhere between the village of Nether Wallop and the hamlet of Stoke Charity, that he encountered the first and only serious disruption to his week’s itinerary.

The disruption was seated on a stile, a young woman in a travelling dress of sensible grey, which was, nevertheless, hopelessly unsuited for the purpose. She was not weeping, which Mr. Pemberton would have found a manageable emergency, but simply staring at a large tear in her skirt with an expression of profound and practical dismay.

“Hullo!” said Mr. Pemberton, bringing The Pelican to a neat halt. “Trouble?”

She looked up. Her face, though smudged with dust and vexed, was a handsome one. “My dress has caught,” she said, in a voice that was surprisingly calm. “And my guardian has the luggage.”

It was a statement of fact, delivered with the crisp finality of a bill of lading. Mr. Pemberton, a man who appreciated clarity, was immediately on her side.

“A guardian,” he mused, propping his bicycle against the hedge. “An awkward customer, guardians. Generally are. Run away, have you?”

“I have removed myself from his supervision,” she corrected him, with a hint of primness. “My name is Miss Eleanor Vance. I am on my way to my Aunt Beatrice, at Hartley Grange. I was to take the coach from Stockbridge, but my guardian, Mr. Silas Croft, disagreed with my plans.”

“And he took the luggage as a form of argument,” Mr. Pemberton deduced. “A bit low, that. Well, Miss Vance, my name is Pemberton. Arthur Pemberton. I am a clerk, on holiday, and I am at your service. The lanes are not really a place for a lady on foot. Hartley Grange is only five or six miles hence. I can escort you.”

Miss Vance looked from his earnest face to the strange, two-wheeled contraption. “On that?”

“The very latest thing in locomotion,” he assured her. “I shall ride slowly. You can walk, and we can converse. It will be far more agreeable than sitting here and being eaten by gnats.”

Her decision was made with surprising speed. “Very well, Mr. Pemberton. It is most kind.”

They set off, the clerk walking his bicycle and the runaway ward walking beside him. The arrangement was, in its way, another form of order. He learned that her parents were dead, that Mr. Croft was a man of stern and unyielding principles who wished to marry her to a wealthy but ancient cousin, and that her Aunt Beatrice was a novelist of some minor renown who lived a life of bohemian independence.

“And I mean to be independent, too,” Miss Vance declared, her chin set firmly. “I have twenty-three pounds of my own, and a talent for watercolour painting.”

“An excellent beginning,” Mr. Pemberton approved. “A solid foundation.”

They were discussing the relative merits of financial prudence and artistic freedom when a sound broke the pastoral quiet—the clatter of hooves and the rumble of wheels. Around the bend came a dog-cart, driven with furious speed by a man with a face like a clenched fist. This was, without doubt, the guardian, Mr. Silas Croft.

He pulled the horse to a rearing halt, his eyes, small and sharp, taking in the scene with terrible, instantaneous comprehension: the young woman, the young man, the isolated lane, the dust of their shared journey.

“Eleanor!” he boomed, his voice thick with proprietary outrage. “What is the meaning of this?”

Miss Vance stood her ground. “I am going to my Aunt’s, Mr. Croft. This gentleman has been kind enough to offer me his escort.”

“Escort!” Croft spat the word. He leaped from the cart, his gaze fixed on Pemberton. “And you, sir? Who are you, sir, to be escorting a young lady of quality along a public highway? A clerk, by the look of you! With a… a mechanical monstrosity!”

Mr. Pemberton, who was not easily intimidated, drew himself up to his full five feet nine inches. “I am Arthur Pemberton, sir. And this ‘monstrosity’ is a Rover Safety bicycle. I found the lady in some distress and offered my assistance, which is a common courtesy, even in Hampshire.”

“Courtesy!” Croft snorted. “I know your kind! You lure a foolish girl from her duty with talk of ‘assistance’ and ‘kindness’! You have compromised her, sir! Ruined her! Her reputation is in tatters, thanks to you and your… your velocipede!”

The sheer injustice of the accusation left Mr. Pemberton momentarily speechless. His orderly mind could find no column in which to enter such a fantastic charge. “I beg your pardon!” he finally managed. “We have done nothing of the sort! I am a respectable man! I have a bicycle, not a harlot’s reputation to destroy!”

The word ‘harlot’ hung in the air, shocking them all. Miss Vance gasped. Mr. Croft’s face turned a shade of puce.

“You hear him, Eleanor? The fellow’s language! A common adventurer! I shall have the constable on him! I shall have him clapped in irons!”
