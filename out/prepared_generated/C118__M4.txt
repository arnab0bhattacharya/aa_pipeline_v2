The fog had not lifted from the Thames valley by the time Dr. Alistair Finch reached his study, and the gas-lamps in the quadrangle of Ashcombe School burned with a sickly yellow light against the grey. He found the private investigator waiting on the stone step, a thin man in a rain-darkened ulster, his card already in Finch’s hand: Mr. Silas Rook, of Chancery Lane. Finch ushered him in without ceremony, for the matter was delicate and the corridors had ears.

“You have the letters,” Rook said, once the door was shut and the fire had been stirred to something like life. It was not a question.

Finch drew a folded packet from his desk and laid it out. Three cheques, drawn on the Ashcombe endowment account at the London and County Bank, each for a sum that would keep a boy in comfort for a year, each signed with the name of the bursar, Mr. Percival Tidy. “They came back from the bank on Tuesday,” Finch said. “The signature is a fair imitation, but the manager knew Tidy’s hand too well to be gulled. The dates are recent. The money was never drawn.”

“And you suspect a boy.”

“I suspect one boy in particular. Lionel Vane. He is seventeen, in the upper sixth, and his father’s affairs are—” Finch paused, choosing his word with the care of a man who had spent thirty years among the young. “—straitened. Vane has been moody this term. He was seen near the bursar’s office on the afternoon the first cheque was dated.”

Rook turned the papers toward the firelight. “Show me his handwriting.”

Finch produced an exercise book, a Latin prose composition in a neat, sloping hand, the letters upright and disciplined. Rook set it beside the cheques and studied them for a long minute. The forged signature was a cramped, angular thing, the loops of the T and the d pressed flat as if by a heavy hand. The boy’s hand was nothing like it.

“Plainly unlike,” Rook murmured. “You saw this.”

“I saw it. But boys are clever, Mr. Rook. A boy may disguise his hand as readily as he may disguise his voice. And who else? The cheques were taken from Tidy’s office. The boys have access to the corridor. The masters do not.”

“The masters do not,” Rook repeated, and something in his tone made Finch colour.

“You have been engaged to examine the evidence,” Finch said stiffly, “and the school’s rivalries. There are rivalries enough. The headmaster, Dr. Hawley, is a man of rigid principle and no imagination; he has already decided Vane is guilty and means to expel him before the half-term. There is Mr. Tidy himself, who is jealous of his authority and has quarrelled with Hawley over the accounts. There is Mr. Larch, the science master, a radical, who despises the classics and would see the endowment spent on laboratories. And there is the boy’s housemaster, Mr. Pell, who has always defended Vane and now finds his judgment questioned.”

Rook folded the cheques and returned them. “I should like to see the bursar’s office, and the headmaster’s study. I am told the latter was burgled on Thursday night.”

“It was. Hawley’s desk was forced. Nothing was taken but a bundle of old examination papers and a set of keys. The keys were to the endowment cupboard.”

“And the cupboard?”

“Untouched. The cheques were taken from Tidy’s office, not the cupboard.”

Rook asked to be taken to the bursar’s office first. It was a small room off the main corridor, smelling of ink and cold ash, with a single window that gave onto the quadrangle. The window-latch was old and loose. Rook examined it, then the desk, then the floor, and finally stood at the window for a long while, looking out at the fog. “Who else has a key to this room?” he asked.

“Tidy, Hawley, and the porter, Mr. Cribb. And the cleaners, of course.”

“Of course.” Rook turned. “And the boy Vane—he was seen near this door on the afternoon in question. By whom?”

“By Mr. Larch.”

“The science master. The radical.” Rook permitted himself the ghost of a smile. “And what was Mr. Larch doing in this corridor at that hour?”

Finch hesitated. “He had been to see Tidy about the laboratory grant.”

“They quarrelled?”

“They disagreed.”

Rook said nothing more until they had climbed to the headmaster’s study, a lofty room with a view of the fog-bound playing fields and a desk of black oak that had been forced with a chisel. The lock was splintered. Rook knelt and examined the wood, then the carpet, then the window. “The burglar came in by the door,” he said. “The lock was forced from the outside. A boy could do it, but a boy would have made more noise. The chisel was left behind?”

“On the floor. It is Larch’s. From the laboratory.”

“So the burglar wished us to think.” Rook straightened. “Dr. Finch, I should like to speak with Mr. Tidy, and then with Mr. Larch, and then—if you will permit it—with the boy Vane.”

Finch arranged the interviews in the bursar’s office, one after another, and Rook spoke to each man alone. Tidy was plump and aggrieved, and insisted the cheques were a conspiracy against his reputation. Larch was lean and impatient, and admitted the chisel was his but swore he had not missed it until Friday. Vane, brought in last, stood with his hands behind his back and answered in monosyllables. He denied the forgery, denied the burglary, and when Rook asked him whether he had been near the bursar’s office on the afternoon in question, he said, “I was, sir. Mr. Larch sent me to fetch a book.”

“Did you enter?”

“No, sir. The door was locked. I knocked, and no one answered, and I went away.”

Rook dismissed him and sat for a while in the empty office, looking at the loose window-latch. Then he sent for Finch. “The boy is innocent,” he said.
