The churchyard of St. Giles-without-Ludgate was a pocket of quiet in the City’s ceaseless hum. Eleanor Vance sat on a wooden bench, the grit of London dust a fine layer on the worn slats beneath her skirt. Her lunch was a simple affair: two slices of bread and butter, with a thin slice of ham and a smear of mustard that was a little too sharp. She ate slowly, her fingers, nimble and pale from the typewriter keys, careful not to drop any crumbs onto the pressed grey wool of her day dress.

Around her, the lunch hour unfolded in its predictable, comforting rhythm. A trio of clerks in bowler hats and stiff collars stood near the lychgate, their laughter a brief, sharp burst against the low murmur of the city. They passed a flask between them, their faces flushed with a shared joke or perhaps the illicit warmth of the brandy within. An old man, his face a roadmap of wrinkles and his coat a size too large, sat on the opposite bench, feeding pigeons from a paper bag of crusts. The birds, a smudge of iridescent grey and blue, strutted and cooed at his feet, their movements jerky and full of purpose.

Eleanor watched them all, a silent observer in the lunchtime theatre. It was a relief to be out of the office, away from the clatter of the Underwood typewriters and the dry, papery smell of carbon copies. For an hour, she was not Miss Vance, a junior typist for Messrs. Abernathy & Finch, Solicitors. She was simply a girl with a sandwich in a patch of sunlight.

Her mind, however, would not remain in the churchyard. It kept wandering back to the previous evening, to the crisp white envelope that had been propped against her milk jug. The handwriting was unfamiliar at first, a confident, slightly sloping script. *Miss Eleanor Vance.* Inside, a card. *Mr. Arthur Penhaligon requests the pleasure of your company at the Adelphi Theatre to see ‘The Vortex’ on Friday next, at eight o’clock.*

Arthur Penhaligon. She saw his face in her mind’s eye: earnest, with a shock of dark hair that fell across his forehead when he was concentrating, and eyes the colour of a winter sky. He worked in the accounts department, two floors below her. She had seen him in the lobby, on the stairs, a fleeting presence. They had spoken properly only once, last Tuesday, when he had dropped a sheaf of papers on the landing and she had stopped to help him gather them. It had been a clumsy, hurried exchange of names and apologies, but he had looked at her then, really looked at her, and it had made her feel as though the air had changed.

A week had passed since then. A week of her own quiet, secret awareness of him. And now, this.

The Vortex. She had read about it. It was one of those modern plays by Noel Coward, full of scandal and sharp dialogue and people with too much money and not enough to do. It was certainly not the sort of entertainment a girl like her was accustomed to. Her evenings were usually spent with a library book or helping her mother with the darning. The thought of sitting in a plush velvet seat, of the lights dimming and the curtain rising on a world of glamour and heartbreak, was both thrilling and terrifying.

To go would be to step out of her own life, just for an evening. It would mean new stockings, perhaps even a new ribbon for her hat. It would mean sitting beside Arthur Penhaligon in the dark, close enough to smell the faint scent of soap and paper that clung to him. It would mean the journey home on the omnibus afterwards, the city lights streaking past the window, and the uncertainty of what he might say, or do.

A pigeon, bolder than the rest, landed near her feet and cocked its head, its black bead-eye fixing on her sandwich. She broke off a tiny piece of crust and tossed it to him. He snatched it up and fluttered away. Was it so simple? A decision, taken and acted upon. But it was not simple at all. If she said no, she would be sending a message. A message of disinterest, of propriety. She would remain Miss Vance of the third floor, and he would remain Mr. Penhaligon of the second. The strange, fragile connection that had sparked on the landing would be extinguished, and the world would return to its familiar, orderly state.

But if she said yes… what then? She imagined the whispers among the other girls in the typing pool. The raised eyebrows. A young man from accounts taking a typist to the theatre. It was not quite proper. It was the sort of thing that led to expectations. Her mother would worry. *He’s a very nice boy, Ellie, but is he serious? You don’t want to get your hopes up.*

Was it possible she already had? She thought of the way he had looked at her, his slight smile as he had thanked her. He had seemed kind. Not like some of the other clerks, with their swaggering confidence and their eyes that lingered too long. Arthur had seemed almost shy.

The old man on the other bench rose slowly, brushing crumbs from his lap. He nodded to her, a small, courteous gesture, before shuffling out through the stone archway and back into the rush of Fleet Street. The clerks had gone too. The hour was nearly over. The light in the churchyard had shifted, the shadows lengthening across the ancient, moss-eaten gravestones.
