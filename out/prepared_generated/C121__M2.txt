The wind off the moors did not merely blow; it hunted. It whistled through the iron bars of the leaded windows in the east wing of Halloway Hall, a sound that Mrs. Elspeth Vane had come to associate with the presence of Mr. Arthur Halloway. She sat in the small, drafty study, her knitting basket abandoned in her lap, watching the boy.

Thomas was nine, yet he carried himself with the heavy, slumped posture of a man who had already seen too much of the world’s cruelty. He sat on the far side of the desk, his hands wrapped tightly around a wooden toy horse, his knuckles white. He did not look at her. He looked at the fire, where the oak logs were slowly collapsing into ash.

"Thomas," Elspeth said, her voice soft, modulated to the low register she used when the house was silent. "The tea is warm. Shall we have it?"

The boy did not move. A shadow fell across the rug, long and distorted by the dying light of the afternoon. Elspeth did not turn. She knew who it was before the door creaked.

Arthur Halloway entered without knocking. He was a large man, broad in the shoulders, with a face that seemed carved from the same grey stone as the foundations of the house. His eyes were pale, watery things that held no warmth, only a cold, calculating assessment. He looked at his son, then at Elspeth, and finally at the window.

"The storm worsens," he said. His voice was a rumble, like distant thunder.

"Yes, sir," Elspeth replied, keeping her posture perfectly straight. "We have drawn the curtains in the nursery."

Arthur nodded, a stiff, jerky motion. He walked to the fireplace and stared into the flames, his reflection flickering in the dark glass above the mantelpiece. Thomas flinched, a subtle recoil of the shoulders that Elspeth caught like a hawk spotting a field mouse. The boy’s grip on the wooden horse tightened until the wood creaked.

"Go to bed, Thomas," Arthur said, not turning around. "It is early, but the air is bad for your health."

Thomas remained frozen. He was waiting for permission, or perhaps for the order to be revoked, but Arthur’s silence was a wall.

"Thomas," Elspeth said gently. "The bed is made. The blankets are warm."

The boy looked up, his eyes wide and terrified. He looked at his father, then back at Elspeth. It was a split-second exchange, a plea for rescue. Elspeth nodded, a tiny, imperceptible motion.

Thomas stood, his chair scraping loudly against the stone floor. He scrambled out of the room, his footsteps quick and erratic, disappearing down the corridor. Arthur Halloway watched him go, his face unreadable. Then he turned to Elspeth.

"You are a kind woman, Mrs. Vane. Kindness is a virtue, but it can be a weakness. Do not encourage him to be idle."

He left, the door clicking shut behind him with finality. Elspeth waited until his heavy boots faded down the main staircase. She exhaled, a breath she had been holding for an hour.

Later, after Arthur had retired to his library and the house had settled into its creaking, groaning sleep, Elspeth slipped out of her room. She wore her light slippers, making no sound on the cold stone floors. She knew where Thomas slept. Not in his own room, which was large and bright, but in a smaller chamber adjacent to the kitchen, near the servants’ quarters. It was a room of safety, far from the cold geometry of the master’s wing.

She found him awake. He was sitting up in bed, the blanket pulled to his chin, the wooden horse clutched in his hands. He saw her in the doorway and tensed, waiting for the blow.

"Thomas," she whispered, stepping into the room and closing the door behind her. "It is only Elspeth. I am not here to scold you."

He didn’t speak, but his eyes, dark and wet in the moonlight, fixed on her face. It took ten minutes of her sitting silently on the edge of his bed, stroking his hair, before the tension in his small body began to unravel.

"Why did Mama go?" he asked. The question was barely a breath.

Elspeth froze. The household narrative was clear, repeated by the butler, the cook, and even Arthur in his stiff, formal manner. Lady Halloway, Eleanor, had died of a fever in the winter. A sudden, tragic end. A closed casket. No mourners, for the family was too grief-stricken.

"She was ill," Elspeth said, reciting the script. "It was very sad."

"No," Thomas said. His voice was stronger now, edged with a fear that had curdled into anger. "She wasn’t ill. I saw her. She was packing her trunk. She was not sick. She was smiling."

Elspeth felt a chill that had nothing to do with the drafty room. "Thomas, do not say such things. Your father—"

"Father said she died," Thomas interrupted. "But I saw the carriage. It wasn’t the funeral coach. It was a hired gig. And she didn’t look dead. She looked... free."

The boy’s face crumpled. "I tried to follow her. I ran to the gate. But the gate was locked. And then Father came. He held me. He said Mama was gone. He said if I ever mentioned the carriage, if I ever mentioned that she was alive, he would send me to the workhouse. He said he would send me to the dark."

Elspeth looked at the boy, really looked at him. She saw the flinch every time a door slammed, the way he flinched from the shadow of his own hand. She realized then that the fear in this house was not of ghosts, but of a man who had murdered a truth to keep a child in line.

"Why did she leave?" Elspeth asked softly.

Thomas shrugged, a gesture of profound exhaustion. "I don’t know. But I think she was tired. Tired of the moors. Tired of the cold. Tired of him."
