The rain lashed against the high, narrow windows of 42 Harley Street, blurring the gaslight of the street into a smear of amber and grey. Inside the consulting room, the air was thick with the scent of carbolic and old paper, a sterile sharpness that could not quite mask the underlying tension. Dr. Arthur Thorne lay slumped over his mahogany desk, his head resting on a leather-bound ledger, his eyes closed. He was not dead, the pulse in his neck still throbbed with a faint, rhythmic insistence, but he was deeply unconscious, likely the result of a potent dose of laudanum administered by his own hand during a bout of severe migraines.

Inspector Elias Vane stood in the center of the room, his hat in his hand, his knuckles white from the grip. Before him stood Dr. Julian Halloway, Thorne’s partner of twenty years. Halloway was a man of impeccable dress, his waistcoat tailored to hide the slight tremor in his hands. He stood with his back to the door, blocking the exit, a physical barrier between Vane and the rest of the house.

"The window was latched from the inside, Inspector," Halloway said, his voice smooth as oiled silk. "The door was locked. I found him at ten o’clock. I tried the key, but it wouldn’t turn. I had to force the panel. When I did, he was just... there. Sleeping."

Vane did not look at Halloway. He looked at the instrument case on the desk. It was a masterpiece of surgical craftsmanship, silver-handled and open. The scalpels, the forceps, the retractors—all were accounted for, except for one. A fine, curved scalpel, the kind used for delicate tissue work, was gone. The empty slot gaped like a missing tooth.

"Missing property," Vane murmured, more to himself than to the doctor. "Or a weapon?"

"Nothing else was taken," Halloway insisted, stepping closer. His eyes were wide, pleading for belief. "I checked the desk, the safe, the drawers. It is only the scalpel. Arthur has been under considerable stress. He has been working eighteen-hour days. Perhaps he dropped it in the waste bin? Or perhaps he took it with him when he was out of the room earlier, intending to discard it, and forgot?"

"It was in the case when I arrived," Vane said. "The case was open. If he had taken it out, why leave the case open? And why lock the room if he was merely leaving for a moment?"

Before Halloway could respond, a cough came from the shadows near the door. A young woman stepped forward. She was small, wearing a grey dress that was plain but clean, her hair pinned back severely. She held a clipboard to her chest like a shield.

"Dr. Halloway, you said no one was to enter," Vane noted, his gaze shifting to the assistant.

"She is my personal secretary, Miss Elara Vance," Halloway said, his tone cooling slightly. "She was in the next room. She heard me forcing the lock. She has not left the building."

Vane walked over to the desk. He picked up a file folder. It was labeled *Patient: Mrs. Gable, Cardiac Condition*. He opened it. The handwriting inside was elegant, precise, and utterly consistent with Thorne’s. But as he turned the pages, he frowned. The dates were wrong. The progression of the illness had been altered. A diagnosis that should have been noted three weeks ago was now recorded as a mere day old.

"Miss Vance," Vane said, not turning around. "Did you see anyone in this room before Dr. Halloway arrived?"

The silence in the room was heavy, broken only by the ticking of the mantel clock and the distant rumble of thunder. Halloway shifted his weight. "Elara is a faithful employee. She would not lie."

"Faithfulness is not the same as accuracy, Doctor," Vane said softly. He turned to face Elara. She was staring at the floor, her fingers digging into the edge of the clipboard. "Did you see someone enter or exit this room while Dr. Thorne was unconscious?"

Elara lifted her head. Her eyes were red-rimmed, not from crying, but from exhaustion. "No," she said. "I was in the parlor. I heard the rain. I heard the doctor come in. I heard him lock the door. Then I heard nothing but the clock."

"Then who altered the records?" Vane asked, holding up the file.

Halloway’s face tightened. "That is impossible. The records are kept in the locked drawer of this desk. Only Arthur and I have the key. And the key is in Arthur’s pocket."

"Is it?" Vane asked. He reached into his own pocket and produced a small brass key. "I found it on the floor by the door. It had fallen out of the lock when you forced the panel. It was still warm from your hand, Doctor."

Halloway stared at the key in Vane’s palm. The smoothness of his demeanor cracked, revealing a fracture of panic beneath. "I... I did not know it was missing."

"You know exactly what is missing," Vane said. "You know the scalpel is gone. You know the records have been changed to suggest Mrs. Gable’s condition was far worse than it actually is. Why?"

"Because she is dying," Halloway said, his voice rising. "And Arthur is too arrogant to admit it. He believes he can save her with a new procedure. He is gambling with her life. If the insurance companies find out the truth—that she has been deteriorating for weeks, that Arthur was hesitant to operate—they will sue. They will destroy us. I changed the dates to protect the practice. To protect *him*."

"You altered medical records to cover up a colleague’s negligence," Vane said coldly. "And you left the scalpel behind?"

"I did not take the scalpel," Halloway snapped. "I am a doctor, not a thief. I have no need for a weapon."
