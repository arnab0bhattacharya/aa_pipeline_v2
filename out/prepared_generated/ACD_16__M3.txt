It was upon the evening of the fourteenth of November, in the year 1894, that Mr. Silas Vance, editor and proprietor of the *London Clarion*, was ushered into my friend Thorpe’s consulting room in Duke Street. I had called upon Thorpe that afternoon to return a volume on toxicology, and finding Vance in the antechamber, had been invited to remain. Vance was a man of robust build and florid complexion, yet his face was pale, and the hand with which he gripped his walking-stick trembled with agitation.

“You will excuse the intrusion, Mr. Thorpe,” he began, his voice carrying the brisk, authoritative tone of the press, yet strained at the edges. “A most monstrous business has arisen at my office. My chief typesetter, a man named Daker, was found an hour ago in Bouverie Passage, beaten to the very edge of his life. His jaw is broken, and he lies insensible at Charing Cross Hospital.”

Thorpe had been examining a specimen slide upon his desk. He pushed it aside, folded his long hands together, and fixed his keen grey eyes upon the editor. “A robbery, Mr. Vance?” he asked, in the quiet, methodical tone which he habitually employed.

“That is the singular part of it. His watch and purse were untouched. But I cannot think it a common assault, Mr. Thorpe, for it connects itself in a most alarming fashion with certain events at my office. I should tell you that for some months past, the *Clarion* has been receiving personal advertisements of a most curious character. They are composed entirely of numbers, set out in groups of four, and paid for punctually in advance.”

“The substitution of numbers for words is a common enough device among smugglers and political refugees,” Thorpe observed. “But you would scarcely come to me over a cipher.”

“Nor did I, until three days ago. On Monday last I received a letter from a reader in Camberwell, a clerk of some education, who professed to have worked out the key. He declared the messages to be communications between a gang of thieves, detailing the movements of wealthy persons and the disposition of their plate and valuables. He enclosed a translation of one such advertisement, which seemed to prove his point, and demanded a sum of money for the complete key.”

“And you refused to pay?”

“I did. I treated it as an attempt at extortion. But this morning, Daker, who had been with the *Clarion* for fifteen years, came to me privately. He stated that a stranger had called at the publishing office late the previous night, demanding to know the address of the man in Camberwell. Daker, who had chanced to encounter the caller, described him as a short, thick-set man, dressed in a grey ulster and carrying a soft felt hat drawn low over his eyes. Daker refused to give the information, and the man departed with threats. Now the poor fellow lies in the hospital, and I fear the gang has silenced him.”

Thorpe rose from his chair and walked to the window. The rain was beating against the glass, and the gas-lamps in the street below threw long, wavering reflections on the wet pavement. “You have, I presume, one of these numerical advertisements with you?” he said, turning sharply.

Vance drew a cutting from his pocketbook and laid it upon the table. Thorpe examined it for some minutes, his brows drawn together in concentration.

“The key is simple enough,” he remarked at last. “It is a numerical substitution, based upon the position of words in a standard dictionary. The first figure denotes the page, the second the column, the third the word. A child might read it. Let us see what we have.”

He took down a well-worn dictionary from the shelf and began to turn the pages with rapid precision. Presently he read aloud: “‘Tuesday. Train. Four. P. M. Leather case. Left at cloakroom. King’s Cross.’ You see, Mr. Vance, your correspondent in Camberwell was entirely correct. It is a thieves’ directory, and a very clever one.”

“But what has this to do with poor Daker?”

“It has everything to do with him. He was beaten because he knew the man in the grey ulster, and could identify him. The gang, upon learning that your typesetter had been approached, feared exposure. They struck him down to prevent him from giving evidence. The question now is: who is the man in the grey ulster? You say Daker described him as short and thick-set. Was there any other peculiarity?”

“None that Daker mentioned.”

Thorpe’s thin lips curved into a smile. “Then we must find one for ourselves. I propose, Mr. Vance, that we visit Bouverie Passage at once. A man may be beaten in the dark, Mr. Vance, but he leaves his mark upon the pavement, and it is in the nature of a violent encounter to scatter its evidence as freely as a tree scatters its leaves in autumn.”

We set out in a hansom, the rain still falling steadily. Bouverie Passage was a narrow alley connecting Fleet Street with the printing district, and at that hour it was deserted save for a single policeman at the corner. Thorpe walked slowly down the alley, his sharp eyes scanning the ground. Presently he stopped and dropped upon one knee, holding his bull’s-eye lantern close to the cobbles.

“Here is where Daker fell,” he said. “You will observe the discoloration upon the stone. And here—” he pointed to a small dark object in the gutter— “is a button, torn from a coat. Brass, of military pattern, but slightly bent. And here, upon the edge of the wall, is a smear of paint—light grey, fresh, and still damp.”

He stood up, his eyes gleaming with satisfaction. “The man in the grey ulster brushed against this wall as he struck Daker down. The paint is from his coat. The button tells us he is a man who has served, or affects to have served, in the army. And the print of his boot in this soft mud by the gutter shows that he has a peculiar gait, dragging the left foot slightly. We have our man, Mr. Vance, as surely as if he stood before us.”
