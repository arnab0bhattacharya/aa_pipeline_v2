The winter of the year eighteen hundred and eleven had descended upon the manufacturing village of Marshfield not as a season, but as a sentence—a doom pronounced by some unrelenting heaven, and executed through iron frost and ceaseless, driving snow. I, Clara Hartwell, stood at the window of my brother's keeping-room, and watched the white tempest devour the landscape until the distant moor was swallowed and the very rooftops opposite seemed to bend beneath their burden of ice. The mills had stood silent for six weeks. The war and the blockade had choked the trade upon which Marshfield breathed, and now the village was dying by inches, as a man strangled dies—slowly, and with open eyes.

I turned from the casement to where my brother, Joshua Hartwell, sat beside the fire. He was a man of forty years, strong-built, hard-featured, and possessed of a will that had raised him from the counting-house to the ownership of three mills within a decade. That same will now held him rigid in his chair, his ledger open on his knee, his brow contracted into a furrow that no persuasion of mine could smooth. He had dismissed the last of his hands a fortnight ago. The great looms stood idle; the warehouses were full of cloth that no ship could carry to port; and Joshua, staring into the coals, had set his face like flint against the misery that crawled nearer to our very door with every passing day.

"There is nothing to be done, Clara," he said, without lifting his eyes, as though he had felt my gaze upon him. "I cannot weave bread from air, nor pay wages from an empty chest. The blockade has strangled us. Every vessel that puts out from Liverpool is seized or turned back. I have debts in London that cry out for payment, and no means to still them but to wait."

"Wait," I repeated; and the word fell from my tongue with a weight I could not govern. "Yes—wait. But while you wait, Joshua, there are those in this village who cannot. Old Nell Thorne was at the back door this morning, barefoot in the snow, begging for a single handful of meal. Her grandson is sick with the fever, and she has nothing—nothing—to put into his mouth."

A muscle tightened in my brother's jaw. He turned a page of the ledger with a deliberate, heavy hand. "I am sorry for Nell Thorne. I am sorry for them all. But I am not Providence. If I yield—if I open the stores, if I give work where there is none to give—I ruin myself, and in ruining myself, I ruin every soul who depends on me for future bread when the trade revives. Do you think I do not feel it? I feel it as much as any man; but feeling must not govern judgment."

I held my peace. I had learned, in the years since I had come to keep his house, that to press Joshua in this mood was to rouse a stubbornness that grew harder with every assault. He was not cruel by nature—I knew this; I had seen him, in better times, give freely and without ostentation. But adversity had drawn a armour about his heart, and he wore it as a man wears a garment that has grown familiar: he no longer felt its weight, nor perceived how it hardened him.

The morning passed in silence. I went about my duties—ordered the dinner, directed the one remaining servant, attended to the linen—but through every task, the image of Nell Thorne's bare feet in the snow pursued me, and the recollection of certain faces I had glimpsed at the mill-gates on the previous evening—gaunt, hollow, spectre-like, yet wearing a look of patient endurance that cut deeper than any outcry could have done. They had not rioted; they had not threatened. They had only stood, and waited, and looked; and in their looking there was something that smote the conscience as a rod smites a disobedient child—not with violence, but with a quiet, searching shame.

At noon, a sound arose from the lane below. I crossed to the window again, and there, pressing up the frozen track toward the house, came a body of men—perhaps thirty in all—the weavers and spinners of the silenced mills. They came slowly, painfully, some leaning on sticks, some supported by their fellows; and at their head walked a tall, gaunt figure whom I knew at once for Stephen Aycliffe, once the most skilled of Joshua's workmen, now a shadow of that former self, his face sharpened by hunger to a blade's edge. They halted at the gate. Stephen lifted the latch and came alone up the path to the door.

I heard Joshua rise from his chair. He met Stephen in the hall. The two men stood face to face—the master, broad and ruddy with warmth and sustenance; the worker, wasted and pale, his thin garments clinging to a frame that seemed ready to collapse inward upon itself. The contrast was cruel; I felt it strike my heart like a physical blow.

"Mr. Hartwell," said Stephen, and his voice was low and steady, though it carried the effort of one who governs a great exhaustion, "we are come to ask for work. Any work. We do not ask for our former wages, nor for the looms to be opened. We ask only for something to do—something by which we may earn a crust, and keep our children from perishing. We will carry, we will dig, we will break stones on the road. Only let us not sit still and starve."

Joshua's face darkened. I saw the conflict in him—the human impulse warring with the rigid judgment he had imposed upon himself. He spoke at last, and his voice, though not unkind, had the finality of a closing door.
