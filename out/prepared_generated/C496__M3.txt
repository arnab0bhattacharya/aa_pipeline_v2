The rain came off the moors and settled over Brickfield like a second skin, turning the freshly cut cotton dust into a paste that caked the windowsills and choked the gutters. Elias Thorne, surgeon and physician, stood at the basin in his surgery, washing his hands. The water in the ewer was cold and faintly rust-colored, no matter how many times he drew it from the pump. He dried his hands on a stained towel and looked at the boy sitting on the examination table.

The boy’s name was Samuel. He was eleven, though he looked eight. He held his left arm against his chest, the fingers of his right hand clamped around his wrist. The sleeve of his shirt was torn, and below the elbow the flesh was a ruin of crushed muscle and exposed bone, the result of having his arm caught between the heavy rollers of the carding engine at Blackmoor Mill. The injury was three hours old. The overlooker had wrapped it in a strip of sacking and sent the boy home on foot.

Elias unwound the sacking, working gently, though Samuel did not flinch. The boy had passed into a state of dull endurance, his face the color of tallow, his eyes fixed on a point somewhere beyond the wall. Elias examined the arm. The radius was fractured in two places, and the skin had been torn away in a long strip, leaving the tissue beneath open to the air. He would need to set the bone and dress the wound, and even then, infection was likely. He had seen it before. He had seen it dozens of times.

He worked quickly, straightening the limb by feel while Samuel bit down on a leather strap Elias had given him. The boy made no sound. When the splints were in place and the bandages wrapped, Elias gave him a draught of laudanum and told him to sleep on the bench in the surgery. Samuel lay down without a word and closed his eyes.

Elias sat at his desk and opened his ledger. He kept a separate book for the mill children, though he had never been asked to. In it he recorded their names, ages, the nature of their injuries, and the date. Over the past eighteen months, since his arrival in Brickfield, he had filled forty-three pages. Crushed fingers, severed thumbs, burns from the steam pipes, scalds from the dye vats, one case of a girl whose hair had been pulled into the belting and who had been scalped from the crown to the nape of her neck. She had survived, after a fashion. She wore a kerchief now and worked in the card room, because her family could not afford to lose her wages.

The next morning Elias walked to Blackmoor Mill. It was a Saturday, but the machinery was running. The mill owners had begun running the looms on Saturdays to make up for lost time after a strike the previous autumn. The building was a long rectangle of soot-blackened brick, with a single chimney pushing a column of grey smoke into the low sky. The noise of the machinery could be felt in the ground before it could be heard, a vibration that traveled up through the soles of the boots.

He asked for Mr. Blackmoor and was kept waiting in a bare room near the entrance for the better part of an hour. When Josiah Blackmoor appeared, he was wearing a good coat and carried the smell of pipe tobacco and ink. He was a man of about fifty, thick through the shoulders, with a face that had the heavy, immobile quality of a man who had long ago decided that the world was as it should be.

Elias stated his case. He had come to ask that the hours of the children employed in the carding room be reduced. The present shift was thirteen hours, with a half-hour break for dinner. The children were between eight and thirteen years of age. They worked in heat and damp, standing the whole time, and the machinery was unguarded. He had treated Samuel the previous evening. He had treated others before him. He asked that the shifts be shortened to ten hours, and that the youngest children be removed from the carding and spinning rooms altogether.

Blackmoor listened without sitting down. He stood near the window with his hands behind his back, and when Elias had finished, he said that he was not in the habit of taking instruction from a surgeon on matters of business. The mill paid its wages and obeyed the law. If Dr. Thorne wished to concern himself with the law, he might write to his member of Parliament. If he wished to concern himself with surgery, he might attend to his patients. The children of Brickfield were fed and housed because of the mills, and without the mills they would be in the workhouse. He said this without heat, as a man reciting a fact he had recited many times.

Elias said that Samuel’s arm had been crushed because the boy was exhausted and reached into the rollers to clear a jam of cotton waste, a task that should have been performed by an adult. He said that exhaustion was the cause of most of the injuries he treated, and that a child of eleven could not remain alert for thirteen hours.

Blackmoor said that the matter was closed. He nodded to Elias and left the room.

That evening, Elias returned to his surgery. He had been in Brickfield long enough to know that the conversation with Blackmoor would produce nothing. He had had the same conversation with the owners of the other two mills in the town, and the result had been the same. They were polite, they were firm, and they were immovable. The law was on their side, or rather, the law was too thin to press against them. The Factory Act set limits on the hours of children under thirteen, but the limits were routinely exceeded, and no inspector had visited Brickfield in the memory of anyone living there.
