The bell of St. Jude’s had not long ceased its iron tolling, a sound that seemed to shake the very soot from the slates of the houses in Spindle Hollow, when I first heard the news. The day was Sunday, the only day the mills were silent, and the silence of them was a heavy, oppressive thing, like a held breath.

It was little Natty Croft’s mother who told me. She caught my arm as I left the schoolroom, her face a mask of bewildered pain, her hands twisting the corner of her apron until the linen was white with the strain. “Miss Vance,” she whispered, her voice raw, “it’s our Natty. Mr. Hargreaves, the overlooker… he’s beat him. Beat him cruel.”

A cold fury, as sharp and sudden as a winter gust, seized me. Natty Croft! A boy of nine, with eyes as wide and trusting as a fawn’s, and a mind that, though slow to grasp letters, was quick with kindness. He had been in my class for two years. I knew the faint stammer that came upon him when he was frightened, and the way he would draw pictures of soaring birds in the margins of his copybook.

“What for?” I demanded, the words coming out harder than I intended. “What could a child do to deserve such a thing?”

“He dropped a shuttle, ma’am. Just dropped it. And Mr. Hargreaves, he’s a man of temper… he took his strap to him. Natty’s back is all stripes and weals. He cannot rise from his bed.”

I saw the boy then in my mind’s eye, his small, work-worn hands clutching at his mother’s skirts. I saw the great, dark engine-room of the mill, a place of shadows and thunder, where children were but cogs in a relentless machine. The injustice of it burned in my throat like bile. A child, beaten for a moment’s clumsiness, by a man whose duty was to guide, not to tyrannise.

“I shall go,” I said, my voice quiet but firm. “I shall speak to Mr. Blackwood.”

Mrs. Croft looked at me with a terror that was almost comical. “Oh, no, ma’am! You cannot! The Blackwoods… they are like kings in this town. He will not listen to the likes of us.”

“He will listen to me,” I replied. And I turned and walked away, my Sunday bonnet feeling like a fool’s cap, my heart a hard, hot stone in my breast.

The walk to Blackwood Mill was a pilgrimage through desolation. The houses that crowded the road were uniform in their grime, their windows like vacant eyes. As I drew near, the mill itself rose before me, a vast, black cathedral of commerce, its tall chimney a monument to Mammon, staining the grey Yorkshire sky. Even in its Sabbath stillness, it seemed to breathe a latent power, a promise of noise and heat and ceaseless toil.

I was admitted at last through a small, unassuming door, not to the factory floor, but to a quiet office at the side. The contrast was shocking. Here, there was a Turkey carpet, a mahogany desk, and a fire of coals burning cheerfully in a polished grate. The air smelled of beeswax and good paper, not of oil and sweat.

A young man rose from the desk. He was not what I had expected. He was perhaps five-and-twenty, with a fine, thoughtful face and clear, grey eyes. He wore a good coat of dark broadcloth and looked more like a scholar than a mill-owner’s son.

“Good afternoon,” he said, his voice smooth and educated. “I am Edward Blackwood. My father is away on business. How may I be of service to you?”

I stood before him, a plain woman in a plain dress, and felt the great gulf between our stations. But the thought of Natty’s weals steadied me.

“My name is Sarah Vance,” I began, my voice shaking slightly. “I am the Sunday-school teacher at St. Jude’s. I have come to make a complaint.”

He gestured to a chair. “Please, be seated.”

I remained standing. “It concerns one of my pupils, a boy named Natty Croft. He works in your weaving shed. This morning, it was reported to me that your overlooker, Mr. Hargreaves, has beaten the child so severely he is unable to leave his bed.”

Mr. Blackwood’s expression did not change. He listened with a polite, almost languid attentiveness, his head tilted slightly. When I had finished, he gave a small, regretful sigh.

“That is very unfortunate. Hargreaves is a man of strong discipline. Sometimes, I fear, his zeal gets the better of him.”

“Zeal?” I cried, my restraint shattering. “Sir, it is not zeal that takes a strap to a child of nine for dropping a shuttle! It is cruelty! It is tyranny! The boy is not an animal to be broken, but a soul to be nurtured. Where is the justice? Where is the mercy in your house?”

My voice had risen, ringing with the passion of my conviction. I expected anger, a command to leave. Instead, he looked at me with a kind of detached curiosity, as if I were a specimen of unusual emotion he was observing.

“Miss Vance,” he said, his tone still perfectly calm. “I understand your concern. It is admirable. But you must see things from our perspective. We run a great enterprise here. Thousands depend upon its efficiency. Discipline must be maintained. A mistake, however small, can have costly consequences.”

“A boy’s back is not a consequence to be weighed against profit!” I shot back. “He is a human being, made in the image of God. Does your scripture not say, ‘Suffer the little children to come unto me’? It does not say, ‘Suffer the little children to be flogged for the sake of your dividends’!”
