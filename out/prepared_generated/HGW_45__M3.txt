It is a curious thing, that while mankind has for centuries speculated upon the interior life of the lower animals, no one prior to Mr. Bunting thought to apply the rigorous principles of mechanical science to the problem. We are content to write poems about the faithfulness of the dog, or the sagacity of the elephant, yet we shrink from the empirical investigation of their actual thoughts. Mr. Bunting, who occupied a modest semi-detached villa in Ealing, was not so easily satisfied. He was a man of relentless, practical curiosity, and having devoted three years and a considerable portion of his wife’s inheritance to the construction of an apparatus he termed the Lexicon, he at last felt prepared to put it to the test.

The Lexicon was an ungainly contrivance. It consisted primarily of a large copper funnel, a series of oscillating glass valves, and a complex arrangement of selenium cells, not unlike those used in early photophonic experiments, all mounted upon a heavy mahogany trestle. The principle, as Bunting explained it to anyone who would listen, was the translation of the minute, atmospheric vibrations produced by an animal’s laryngeal muscles into corresponding phonetic sounds, via the medium of electrical resistance. It was, he claimed, a direct conversion of emotional impulse into human speech.

It was upon a Tuesday afternoon in April that Bunting resolved to test the machine. His immediate subject was Rover, a large, rather melancholy St. Bernard belonging to his next-door neighbour, a Mr. Grimes. Mr. Grimes was a stockbroker of the more pompous variety, a man who held firmly to the conventions of his class and viewed Bunting’s scientific pursuits with undisguised contempt. Rover, however, was a frequent visitor to Bunting’s garden, being in the habit of pushing through a gap in the garden fence.

Bunting had enticed the dog into his conservatory with a quantity of butcher’s meat, and had adjusted the copper funnel to within a few inches of the animal’s head. Rover sat upon the floor, panting slightly, and regarding the apparatus with an expression of patient resignation.

“I must ask you to remain perfectly still, Rover,” said Bunting, adjusting a rheostat. “We are about to bridge the most profound gulf in nature.”

He threw a switch. There was a hum of the induction coil, a violent fluttering of the glass valves, and then, from a brass trumpet attached to the machine’s base, there issued a voice.

It was a deep, rather sepulchral voice, possessing a curious, grating monotony, but the words were unmistakably English. “I wish,” said the voice, “you would not stare so. It makes my ears itch.”

Bunting leapt back from the machine, his hands trembling. “Good heavens!” he cried. “It speaks! Rover, did you speak?”

“I am not speaking,” replied the voice from the trumpet, while Rover’s mouth remained closed. “I am thinking. You are merely translating. And I was thinking about my ears. They itch abominably. There is a great deal of mange in this neighbourhood.”

Bunting seized a notebook. “This is extraordinary,” he muttered. “This is entirely unprecedented. Tell me, Rover, do you possess a concept of time?”

“I possess a concept of dinner,” said the dog. “It occurs twice a day. At other times, I wait.”

“And your master, Mr. Grimes?” Bunting prompted, scarcely able to contain his scientific excitement. “What are your sentiments regarding Mr. Grimes?”

There was a pause, during which the glass valves quivered violently. Then the machine spoke again. “He is a large, noisy object. He walks heavily upon my tail. He is also a fool.”

“A fool?” Bunting repeated, his pencil racing across the page. “In what sense, precisely?”

“In the sense that he believes I am pleased to see him when he returns from the place where he goes. I am not pleased to see him. I am pleased to see the man who brings the meat. But Grimes brings nothing. He merely makes loud, unpleasant noises with his mouth, and strikes me when I am sleeping. He is, moreover, exceedingly ridiculous in his bathing costume.”

Bunting found this a trifle unscientific, but he wrote it down nonetheless. “And Mrs. Grimes?” he inquired.

“She drops things,” said the dog. “Cake, mostly. I wait beneath the table. She is tolerable. But Grimes is a fool. He conceals his stupidity behind a large red face. I have observed that the volume of a man’s voice is frequently in inverse proportion to the quantity of his intellect.”

The interview continued for some twenty minutes, during which Rover expressed himself with a candour and a fluency that was, to say the least, disconcerting. He had, it appeared, a great deal to say about Mr. Grimes. He spoke of Grimes’s vanity, his greed, his unkindness to the kitchen maid, and a certain irregularity in his financial dealings which Rover had deduced from the agitated tone of Grimes’s conversations with a gentleman who visited the house on Sundays.

It was at this juncture that the conservatory door burst open, and Mr. Grimes himself stood upon the threshold, purple with fury. He had come to retrieve his dog, and had overheard, through the thin glass of the conservatory, the concluding remarks of the machine.

“What is the meaning of this, Bunting?” he roared. “What have you done to my dog?”

Bunting, who was still in a state of considerable scientific exaltation, gestured towards the Lexicon. “I have translated his thoughts, Grimes. I have solved the problem of interspecies communication. It is a triumph of applied physics. Your dog has been telling me about you.”

Grimes advanced upon the machine, his face contorted with rage. “I heard what the brute said! Or rather, I heard what you made him say! Defamation, sir! Gross and malicious defamation! I’ll have the law on you for this!”
