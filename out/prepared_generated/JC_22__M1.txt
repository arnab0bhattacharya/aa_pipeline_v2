The fever broke upon him somewhere between the grey waters of the North Sea and the crushing white silence of the Alps, leaving him, for a time, a creature of pure sensation, devoid of history or purpose. He remembered afterwards only the rocking of the train, a motion that seemed to enter his very bones, and the face of a woman leaning over him, a face as broad and placid as a mountain pasture, framed by a severe black coif. They had put him off the train at a station whose name he did not learn, a place where the air was thin and sharp and smelled of pine and cold stone. He was aware, through the fog of his illness, of being carried, then of a room, a low ceiling of dark timber, and the profound, enveloping silence of a valley shut in by immense, snow-capped peaks.

His name, he told them when he could speak, was Ivan. It was a lie, of course, but a simple one, and simplicity was all he could manage. He did not tell them he was a delegate from a committee in Geneva, nor that the minister of a foreign power was expected to pass through the nearby capital in a fortnight, nor that the small, heavy object wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of his valise was not a family heirloom but an instrument of political necessity. These things belonged to the world he had left below the clouds, a world of pamphlets, of whispered arguments in cramped rooms, of a righteous and cleansing fire. Here, there was only the immense indifference of the mountains and the slow, rhythmic labours of the people who lived in their shadow.

The family consisted of the father, a man named Alois Brenner, whose face was a mask of brown leather carved by wind and sun, and his wife, Elsbeth, the woman who had first looked upon him. There were also two children, a boy and a girl with eyes as clear and blue as the glacial lakes he had seen from the train. They did not question him. They fed him broth and thin gruel, they changed the compress on his forehead, and they moved about their small, clean dwelling with a quiet efficiency that was, to his fevered mind, both comforting and deeply unsettling. Their goodness was not a quality they wore; it was the very substance of their being, as natural and unconsidered as the breath they drew from the mountain air.

He watched them from his cot by the window. He watched Alois go out before dawn with a scythe over his shoulder, and return at dusk, his steps heavy with a weariness that seemed not to defeat him but to confirm him in his place in the world. He watched Elsbeth bake bread, her hands dusted with flour, her movements filled with a kind of patient grace. He watched the children play in the thin grass of the meadow, their laughter a bright, clear sound that did not echo in the vastness but was swallowed by it. They were, he came to understand, living in a state of grace, a condition he had only ever read about in the scornful pamphlets of his comrades—a grace born not of faith, but of labour, of continuity, of an unshakeable connection to the soil beneath their feet.

One evening, as the sun bled its last light across the snow-fields, turning them a colour of impossible rose, Alois sat by the stove, mending a leather strap. Ivan, feeling stronger, spoke of the world outside, of the great cities and the injustices that festered in their dark corners. He spoke carefully, in the oblique terms of a man who has learned to hide his meaning, but the fire of his conviction was unmistakable.

“A man must act,” he said, his voice still thin. “To see a great evil and to stand by is to become a part of it. There is a duty to the future, to a world cleansed of oppression.”

Alois did not look up from his work. His fingers, thick and calloused, worked the leather with a slow, deliberate pressure. “The world is a large thing,” he said at last, his German accent thick and ponderous. “It is a very large thing. My world is this valley. My duty is to the wood for the winter, to the roof that it does not leak, to the bread for my children. Is this not enough for one man?”

“It is a small world,” Ivan countered, a note of impatience, of the old revolutionary zeal, creeping into his voice. “It is a world of submission. You bow to a master, to a system that grinds men down.”

Alois stopped his mending and looked at him then. His eyes, in the lamplight, were not simple. They were deep and ancient, holding the patient wisdom of men who have watched generations of snow melt and return. “We all serve,” he said. “You serve your idea. I serve the ground. The ground does not lie. It asks for sweat, and it gives back bread. It is a hard master, but it is a true one. What does your idea give back, when the work is done?”

The question hung in the warm, still air of the room. It was not an argument; it was a statement of fact, as solid and immovable as the granite peaks outside. Ivan had no answer. His cause, which had seemed so absolute, so luminous with moral certainty in the gaslit rooms of Geneva, suddenly appeared here as a phantom, a piece of abstract machinery designed by clever men to solve a problem they had only ever seen in print. He thought of the oilcloth-wrapped object in his valise, of the cold, metallic purpose it contained. It was a thing of the city, of the dark alleys and the political conspiracy. It had no place here, in this room that smelled of woodsmoke and bread.
