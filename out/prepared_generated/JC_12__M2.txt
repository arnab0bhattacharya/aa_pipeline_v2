The gaslight in the workshop on Turnmill Street had a sickly, jaundiced hue, a faint yellow stain that seemed to seep into the very fiber of the oak bench where Arthur Penhaligon sat. It was a light that did not illuminate so much as it revealed the dust motes dancing in the stagnant air, suspended in a timeless, suffocating quiet. Penhaligon was a man of modest stature and less modest precision, his fingers, though stained with grease and oil, possessing a delicacy that belied the roughness of the trade. He lived in the company of seconds, that most elusive of commodities, and in his twenty years of repairing the mechanisms that governed the lives of Clerkenwell’s respectable merchants, he had come to believe that time was not a river, but a trap. It was a thing to be caught, held, and occasionally, with a gentle touch of the tweezers, released.

The bell above the door chimed with a sharp, intrusive clarity, shattering the silence. It was late, past the hour when the respectable gentlemen had retired to their clubs or their wives, and the street outside was empty save for the occasional splash of a cab wheel on the wet cobblestones. The figure that entered did not look like a customer. He was a man of indeterminate age, dressed in a coat of a dark, lustrous wool that absorbed the light rather than reflecting it. He moved with a hesitation that was not the shyness of a newcomer, but the caution of a man treading upon thin ice.

"Mr. Penhaligon?" The voice was low, stripped of its natural resonance, as if the speaker were trying to keep his own voice contained within the room.

Arthur looked up from the disassembled escapement of a pocket watch, a jewel of a mechanism that lay exposed on his velvet mat. "I am here, sir. The shop is closed, but I suppose the work does not respect the hour."

The man did not answer immediately. He stood by the door, his hands clasped before him, and looked at the shelves lined with brass instruments and glass cases. There was a tension in his posture, a coiled energy that suggested he was waiting for something, or perhaps fleeing from it. He placed a small, wrapped parcel on the counter. It was tied with a string of black silk, a stark contrast to the warm, dusty tones of the workshop.

"I have been told you are a man of discretion," the stranger said. It was not a question. "And that your hands are steady."

"Discretion is the rule of the trade," Arthur replied, wiping his hands on a rag. "As for the steadiness of my hands, that is for the work to judge. What is it you have brought me?"

The man unwrapped the parcel with a slowness that seemed calculated to prolong the moment. Inside lay a device of intricate construction. It was not a watch, nor a clock, but something that resembled both, yet was wholly other. It was a sphere of polished brass, no larger than a cricket ball, studded with tiny, dark lenses that caught the gaslight and threw it back in sharp, cold points. There were no hands, no face, only a series of levers and a central dial that bore no numbers, only a single, jagged line.

Arthur felt a cold prickle run down his spine. He had seen such things in the back rooms of pawnbrokers on the Strand, things that spoke of a different kind of science, a science that did not seek to measure time, but to capture it. To steal it. He recognized the mechanism at once, though he had never seen it in working order. It was a device of the sort that men like Alphonse Lemaire had spoken of in the dim, smoke-filled corners of the cafés, a machine designed not to tell the hour, but to siphon the vitality of the user, to trade years of life for moments of clarity, or perhaps, for something far more sinister.

"I do not know what this is," Arthur said, his voice dry. He did not touch it. "I repair watches. I mend springs and clean jewels. I do not deal in... curiosities."

The man’s eyes, which were pale and watery, fixed on Arthur’s face. "It is a timer," he said, the word heavy with irony. "It counts down. And when it reaches zero, it does not stop. It continues. Into the negative. Into the debt." He pushed the device closer to Arthur. "I need it repaired. The mainspring has slackened. It is losing time. I cannot afford to lose time, Mr. Penhaligon. I have paid for the work in advance. The sum is on the counter."

Arthur looked at the money. It was a roll of banknotes, crisp and new, sitting beside the brass sphere. It was more money than he made in a month. It was a temptation that hung in the air, thick and sweet, like the scent of ozone before a storm. He looked at the device again. The lenses seemed to watch him, cold and unblinking. He thought of his wife, who was ill, her breath coming in shallow rattle in the small room above the shop. He thought of the bills that piled up on the desk, the rent that was due at the end of the week. The money was a lifeline, but it was tied to a noose.

"If I repair it," Arthur said slowly, "what will happen? When the time runs out?"

The man smiled, a thin, cracking expression that did not reach his eyes. "That is not my concern. My concern is that it works. I have a debt to pay. A debt of time. And I need it to be precise. Precision is the only mercy we have, Mr. Penhaligon. Without it, we are merely animals, stumbling in the dark."
