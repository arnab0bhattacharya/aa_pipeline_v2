The letter lay upon the desk, a small, cream-colored rectangle that seemed to mock the gloom of the room. It was from Mr. Thorne, the solicitor in Leeds, whose name I had trusted with the modest accumulation of four years’ toil. I had read the words three times, my eyes fixed, my mind blank. *Insolvent. Bankrupt. The funds are lost.* The air in my chamber grew suddenly thick, as if the very oxygen had been stolen by the weight of that paper. I did not weep; I had no tears to spare for such a trivial, earthly calamity. Instead, a coldness settled in my chest, a rigorous, icy calm that allowed me to examine the catastrophe with the detached precision of a surgeon. I folded the letter, tucked it into the lining of my bodice, and stood up.

I had to teach. In an hour, I was to conduct the recitation of German verbs for the second form. If I faltered, if my voice trembled or my face betrayed the ruin that had befallen me, the pupils would sense it. They are sensitive, these children; they detect the slightest dissonance in the harmony of the classroom. They would whisper, they would stare, and the secret would leak out, not to my advantage, but to my shame. I was a governess, a servant in all but name, and a servant who loses her capital is a servant who is broken.

I walked out into the street. The city was waking. The cobblestones were wet with a recent rain, reflecting the pale, leaden sky. It was November, and the wind carried the scent of damp leaves and coal smoke. I walked briskly, my hands clasped tightly behind my back, feeling the hard edge of the letter pressing against my ribs. It was a painful reminder, a physical anchor to my misery. I passed the bakery, where the smell of warm bread should have been comforting, but it only sharpened the hollow ache in my stomach. I had no money. I had no prospect. I had no home to which I could flee.

At the school, the building stood grim and solid against the gray morning. The gate was open, and I saw several pupils entering, their voices rising in the cheerful, oblivious chatter of youth. I straightened my back, lifted my chin, and entered. The headmistress, Madame Valerian, was in the corridor. She was a tall, severe woman, with eyes like flint and a manner that brooked no weakness.

"Miss Harker," she said, not looking up from her ledger. "Your recitation is due in ten minutes. Are you prepared?"

"I am always prepared, Madame," I replied. My voice was steady, clear, and devoid of any quiver.

She looked up then, her gaze sharp, searching. "You look pale," she observed. "Is there some trouble?"

"No trouble," I said. "Only the cold. It is bitter this morning."

She nodded, satisfied, or perhaps merely uninterested. "See that the girls do not mumble. I have no patience for lazy tongues."

I went to my classroom. The desks were arranged in neat rows, the slate tops black and clean. I opened my book, my hand trembling slightly as I turned the pages. I forced the tremor to stop. I am not a child to be coddled; I am a woman who has stood alone in the world for a long time. I will not crumble now.

When the girls entered, they were loud, their laughter filling the room. I raised my hand, and silence fell. It was a good silence, respectful and attentive.

"Open your books to page forty-two," I said. "We are to review the conjugation of the verb *sein* in the past tense."

I began to speak. My voice rang out, clear and commanding. I watched their faces as they listened, their eyes bright with intelligence. I felt a surge of satisfaction, a professional pride that cut through the despair. Here, in this room, I was competent. Here, I was needed. The failure of the bank was a reality, but it was an external reality, separate from this internal world of order and discipline.

As the lesson progressed, I found myself calculating. I had forty pounds in the savings that were now gone. I had three months’ wages due in the spring. I had no other income. I had to live on those three months’ wages, and I had to live on very little. I did the sums in my head, the numbers spinning like a dizziness. Four pounds a month. That was enough for a room, enough for bread, for meat, for a candle. It was not enough for clothes, not enough for a doctor, not enough for the dignity I had always maintained.

I looked at the girls. They were so young, so carefree. They did not know what it was to be hungry, to be afraid, to be stranded in a foreign land with no friends, no family, no money. They did not know the weight of a secret. I envied them, and yet I pitied them for their ignorance.

The bell rang. The lesson was over. The girls gathered their books and filed out, their voices rising again. I remained seated, my hands resting on the desk. I felt a sudden, fierce anger. Why should I suffer? Why should I, who had worked so hard, who had denied myself every comfort, be punished by the caprice of fate? I had been honest, I had been diligent, I had been good. Was that not enough? I thought of the scripture, *Thou shalt not steal*, and I felt a bitter irony. The thieves had not been caught; I was the one who was robbed.

I stood up and walked to the window. The sky was darkening, the clouds gathering into a heavy, brooding mass. The wind was rising, rattling the glass panes. It was a stormy evening, and it seemed to fit my mood. I would have to speak to Madame Valerian. I could not wait for the spring. I needed an advance. I needed to ask for help.
