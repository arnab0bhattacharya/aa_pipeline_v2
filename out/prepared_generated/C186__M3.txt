The weed had been growing in the cracks of the laboratory steps, its serrated leaves trampled daily by students and porters who never once glanced at it. Elias Thorne glanced at it only because he had spent four years cataloguing the alkaloids of common British flora, a project his uncle considered a pleasant hobby and his aunt considered a waste of a perfectly good Cambridge degree. The plant was purple-flowered, persistent, and entirely unremarkable in every botanical text Elias owned. But when he distilled an extract from its roots and inadvertently licked his thumb afterward, he found himself answering his landlady's question about the rent with a candour so absolute that she retreated from his room as though he had drawn a knife. The rent was late. He had spent it on glassware. He knew the exact hour at which he would be able to pay her, and he told her so, along with the fact that her soup the previous evening had been oversalted and that he had once found a hair in his bread. He had not wished to say any of it. He simply could not shape his tongue around anything except the truth as he perceived it, and the effect lasted very nearly twenty minutes, during which he managed to insult the landlady, confess the rent situation, and weep briefly with the sheer relief of not having to maintain the small fictions that governed his daily existence.

He isolated the compound within a fortnight. It was a pale, bitter oil, heat-stable, soluble in wine, and effective in doses as small as four drops. He tested it on himself three more times, each occasion producing the same involuntary transparency, the same inability to deflect, prevaricate, or omit. He did not write to the Royal Society. He did not tell his uncle. He told his aunt, because his aunt had invited him to dinner and he could not, in the ordinary course of things, decline without lying, and he had grown weary of lying to his aunt.

Lady Adela Marsh occupied a house in Cromwell Crescent, a red-brick terrace with white stucco trim and a front garden maintained with the severity of a military parade ground. Her husband, Sir Godfrey Marsh, had made his fortune in railways and had the decency to die before he could spend it all, leaving Adela in possession of an income that placed her precisely where she wished to be: within reach of the people she wished to reach. She held dinners on Thursdays. She held them with the strategic precision of a general positioning artillery, and the object was always the same. She was building something. A position, a network, a structure of obligation and association that would, she believed, secure the future of the Marsh name in the upper registers of London society. She was close. Elias could see it in the way she held her shoulders when she spoke, the tension of a woman ascending a staircase she was not yet certain would hold her weight.

The dinner on the twelfth of October was to be her finest achievement. Lord Pemberton-Hale was coming. His wife, Lady Constance, was coming. Sir Reginald Bryce, who advised the Colonial Office on matters of trade, was coming, and he was bringing his daughter, Miss Henrietta Bryce, whom Adela had selected as a suitable match for her son, Cedric, a young man of pleasant countenance and no particular distinction. Cedric did not know he had been selected a bride. The bride did not know she had been selected. This was the architecture of the evening, and Elias, who had been invited to make up the numbers and because Adela could not quite bring herself to exclude her only nephew even when he smelled faintly of formaldehyde, was to be a part of it.

He arrived at half past seven with the extract in a small vial in his waistcoat pocket. He had not fully decided to use it. He told himself it was an experiment, that the data would be valuable, that the compound's effects on subjects other than himself had not yet been observed. These were true statements. They were also, he recognised with the irony that his own compound had taught him to appreciate, not the entire truth. The entire truth was that he found his aunt's machinations exhausting and a little cruel, and he wished to see what would happen to them in the presence of unvarnished honesty.

The table was set for twelve. Candles burned in silver holders. The dining room smelled of beeswax and the faintest suggestion of the kitchen, where the cook was wrestling with a partridge. Adela received him at the door in a gown of deep blue silk, her grey hair arranged with architectural confidence.

"Elias, how kind. You look thin. Are you eating?"

"I ate a pork pie on the omnibus," he said, and then caught himself. The compound was not in effect. He had simply answered honestly out of habit. This amused him.

The guests arrived in the approved order: first the less significant, then the more, with Lord and Lady Pemberton-Hale last, as protocol and Adela's design demanded. The wine was poured. The soup was consumed. Elias sat between Miss Henrietta Bryce and a Colonel Ashworth, a retired military man with a moustache like a hedgerow and a fund of stories about the Afghan frontier that he deployed at every opportunity. The soup course passed without incident. The fish arrived. Elias waited.

When the footman came to refill glasses, Elias allowed the man to pour, and as the decanter passed his right hand, he let two drops of the pale oil fall from the vial into the claret of the carafe that would be used for the table at large. He did this beneath the lip of the table, with the practiced slight of hand of a man who had spent years manipulating small instruments in poor light. The oil dissolved without trace. The carafe was set before Lord Pemberton-Hale, who poured for the table with the unconscious authority of a man accustomed to presiding.
