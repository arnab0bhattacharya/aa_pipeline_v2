The wind off the moors did not merely blow; it screamed, a high, thin note that seemed to pierce the very stone of the old mill-house and settle into the marrow of my bones. I stood before the window, watching the sleet lash the pane, my hands raw and red from the cold, yet my face burned with a heat that had nothing to do with the fire in the grate, which was now a bed of gray ash. It had been three months since Arthur died, three months since the world had stripped me of my husband, my home, and my dignity, leaving me with nothing but a debt and a share in a mill that groaned under the weight of its own obsolescence.

My name is Eleanor Thorne, and I am a widow in a town that treats widows as ghosts—visible only to those who choose to see them, and then usually with a shudder. The partner who had cheated Arthur, Silas Vane, had come to me yesterday with a smile that did not reach his cold, calculating eyes. He had offered a sum of money, paltry and insulting, to buy out my interest. He spoke of "practicality" and "the harsh realities of the trade," as if Arthur’s death had been a convenient accident of nature rather than a murder committed by slow poison and financial negligence. I had refused him. I had looked at him, that smooth-tongued vulture, and I had said, "Not a penny less than my share is worth, Silas. And I intend to know its worth."

He had laughed. A dry, hacking sound. "You are a clergyman’s wife, Mrs. Thorne. You do not know the weight of wool or the price of warp. You will sink this mill, and you will sink yourself with it."

I had not answered him then. I could not. But I had felt a surge of something in my chest, fierce and dark, like a coal struck by a hammer. I would not sink. I would not be a ghost. I would not be a curiosity to be poked and prodded by men who saw in my silence a sign of weakness.

So, I sat at the table in the small, drafty room, surrounded by ledgers. They were not books of scripture or poetry, but grim, leather-bound volumes filled with columns of figures. To them I had turned in desperation, for I found that numbers did not lie. They did not care if I was a woman or a man, a widow or a virgin. They were stark and unyielding, and in their rigidity I found a comfort that the fluid, cruel world of men could not offer. I learned the language of the trade. I learned that *wool* was not just a commodity, but a living thing, subject to the whims of the sheep and the weather. I learned that *cloth* was the result of labor, sweat, and skill, and that to sell it cheaply was to insult the hands that had woven it.

The day of the market arrived with a gray, oppressive sky. The air in the town square was thick with the smell of wet stone, horse dung, and the sharp, metallic tang of rain. I had dressed in my best black, the dress Arthur had bought me for our wedding, now faded and worn, but still stiff with dignity. I carried a small book in my hand, my ledger, and a quill tucked behind my ear. I felt exposed, like a bare nerve, but I walked with my head high.

The cloth market was a riot of noise and color. Merchants in broad-brimmed hats and long coats shouted over one another, their voices rising and falling in a cacophony of greed and negotiation. The air was thick with dust and the scent of damp wool. I moved through the crowd, my eyes scanning the faces of the men who had once been my husband’s friends. Now, they looked at me with a mixture of pity and contempt. They could not understand how a woman could stand in this place, among the lions, and not tremble.

I found the stall where I was to present my samples. It was a modest table, covered in a blue cloth, displaying bolts of fine calico and heavy tweed. I arranged them with care, smoothing the folds, adjusting the light. I was not a merchant, but I was a judge. I had studied the quality of the cloth, the tightness of the weave, the depth of the dye. I knew its value.

Silas Vane was there, of course. He stood near the center of the hall, his voice carrying easily over the din. He was speaking to a group of large, red-faced men, his face flushed with the confidence of a man who believed he owned the ground he stood on. He saw me, and his smile returned, but this time it was sharper, more predatory.

"Well, Mrs. Thorne," he called out, his voice laced with mock surprise. "We have a new face in the market. Do you come to buy, or to sell? I trust you are not here to waste our time with your moralizing."

The men turned to look at me. The air grew still. I felt the weight of their eyes, heavy and judgmental. I did not flinch. I walked to the table, placed my ledger on the cloth, and opened it to the page where I had calculated the price.

"I am here to sell," I said, my voice steady, though my heart hammered against my ribs. "And I am here because I know the worth of this cloth. It is the finest in the district. It was woven by the best hands, under Arthur’s supervision. It is not cheap. It is not for the likes of those who seek to underbid and undercut. It is for those who value quality."
