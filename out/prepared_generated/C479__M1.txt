The fever broke on a Tuesday, leaving Captain Thorne a hollowed-out thing, a sack of bones draped in a flannel nightshirt. He had been out of his head for eleven days, a prisoner of the sun and the ship’s slow roll. Now, he lay in the dark of his cabin, his breath a shallow rasp against the creak of the timbers. The air was thick with the smell of camphor and sweat, of stale biscuit and the faint, iron tang of his own sickness.

On deck, the world was sharp and bright. The *Sea Serpent*, a clipper of the old, proud lines, was cutting through the South Atlantic on a fair wind. Her wake was a long, white scar on the sapphire sea. Elias Vance stood at the wheel, his feet planted wide, his hands resting lightly on the spokes. He was not a large man, but he was made of rope and leather, his face a roadmap of sun and salt, his eyes the pale grey of a winter sea. For eleven days, he had been master of this ship and her twenty-two souls. He had taken command not with a shout, but with a quiet efficiency that had quelled the crew’s unease as a hand smooths a troubled sea. He had found the winds Thorne had missed, nursed the sails with a lover’s care, and kept the men on their bellies, mending and splicing, so that the ship ran truer and faster than she had in the six months since leaving London.

The ship’s boy, a spidery lad named Pip, appeared at his elbow. “Mr. Vance,” he said, his voice thin. “The captain… he’s awake. He’s asking for you.”

Vance’s hands did not move from the wheel. He stared ahead at the horizon, a perfect, unwavering line. The wind hummed in the rigging, a song he had learned to trust more than any man’s word. He had felt the *Sea Serpent*’s soul beneath his feet, had understood her moods and her mires. He had felt, for the first time in twenty years at sea, a perfect communion of man and vessel. And now, the ghost was stirring in the machine.

“Secure the wheel, Tom,” he said to the man beside him. “Keep her on this heading. And see the men clear the foretop. That studding sail is luffing.”

He walked aft, his boots silent on the scrubbed planking. The crew watched him go, their gazes following him. They had been a slack, sullen lot under Thorne, a man who ruled by bluster and a keen eye for punishment. Under Vance, they had become a unit. They knew his orders before he gave them, saw the flaw in a splice before he pointed it out. They respected his silence more than they had feared Thorne’s rage.

Below, the air was close and still. Thorne was propped against his pillows, a flickering oil lamp casting long shadows that danced on the bulkhead. His face, once florid and powerful, was pale and sunken, his eyes burning with a feverish intensity.

“Vance,” he rasped, his voice a dry crackle. “Report.”

“Wind is steady from the nor’-nor’east, Captain,” Vance said, his voice level. “Making twelve knots. All’s well.”

Thorne’s eyes narrowed. “The men?”

“They’re well, sir. Kept to their work.”

A silence fell between them, broken only by the ship’s groans. Thorne’s gaze was a physical thing, probing, searching for a sign of insubordination, of usurpation. He saw only the same steady, reliable mate he had hired in London, the man who could tie a bowline in the dark and read the stars like a book. But he could feel the change. It was in the very rhythm of the ship, the quiet competence that now suffused every corner.

“I am on the mend,” Thorne said, the words a declaration, not a statement. “I shall be resuming my duties on the morrow. You will, of course, return to your station.”

Vance looked at the man in the bunk. He saw the pride, the desperate need to reclaim the authority that was the only thing of value he possessed. He also saw the weakness, the tremor in the hand that clutched the blanket. Thorne was a man who believed a ship was governed by the power of the man who held the commission. Vance knew a ship was governed by the wind, the sea, and the strength of the hemp that held her together. He had proved the better seaman. He had saved them time, saved them from at least one gale Thorne would have sailed them into. The men were better for it. The ship was better for it.

To surrender the command would be to hand the *Sea Serpent* back to a lesser man. It would be an act of foolish loyalty to a law written on paper, not in the stars and the waves. He could refuse. The crew would stand with him. He could take the ship to Rio, to Valparaiso, to any port he chose. He was the master in truth, if not in name.

He thought of the long, quiet nights at the wheel, the ship’s timbers speaking to him in a language he had only just begun to understand. He thought of the trust in the crew’s eyes. It was a heady thing, this power. It was not the brutal power of a captain’s fist, but the clean, clean power of competence.

He looked at Thorne, at the flickering hope and fear in his eyes. He saw not a tyrant, but a sick old man clinging to the one thing he had left. And Vance saw, with a sudden, chilling clarity, the nature of his own choice. To hold on would be to become Thorne. He would rule by the right of the stronger, the better man. He would have to watch Thorne, to guard against him, to turn the ship’s company into his own faction. The quiet communion he had found with the *Sea Serpent* would be shattered, replaced by the politics of men. The ship would no longer be his partner; she would be his prize.
