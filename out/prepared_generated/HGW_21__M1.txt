I had been at the M’Fasi station for the better part of two years, and I flatter myself I had learned to keep my nerve. One does not survive the fevers, the heat, and the general inconvenience of the West Coast without a certain practical stoicism. But the arrival of the *Ibis*, a tramp steamer smelling of coal and bilge water, brought me a problem for which my medical training had not prepared me. It brought me Mr. Silas Thorne.

He came ashore in a white linen suit that was already wilting in the humidity, carrying a leather case that clanked with the sound of glass and metal. He was a lean, sharp-featured man with eyes that seemed to possess a restless, hungry energy, the sort of eyes one sees in a stockbroker or a bird of prey. He introduced himself as a Fellow of the Royal Society, sent to investigate the peculiar epidemics of the delta.

"I understand," said he, shaking my hand with a grip like a vice, "that the M’Fasi people possess a remarkable physiological anomaly. A total immunity to the sensation of pain."

"It is true enough," I said, leading him up the veranda to the shade. "Though I call it a curse rather than a blessing. They suffer from leprosy and ulcers without complaint. They break their bones in the mangroves and walk home singing. It is a neurological defect, Mr. Thorne. I have tried to explain it to the elders, but they think it a gift from their river god."

Thorne set his case on the table and clicked it open. Inside lay a terrifying array of scalpels, calipers, and small glass vials. "A gift? My dear doctor, it is a treasure trove of data! Imagine, the separation of sensation from the mechanical reflex! If I could isolate the agent—be it bacterial or a metabolic quirk of the blood—think of the benefits to surgery! The agony of the operating table abolished!"

I poured him a warm whisky and water. "It is not so romantic when you see a man with a gangrenous foot because he did not notice a thorn had pierced his heel. Or a woman who burns her hand on the cooking fire and laughs as the flesh blisters."

"Sentimentalism," Thorne snapped, tapping a glass tube. "I require subjects. Healthy subjects, primarily. I wish to test the thresholds. I shall apply pressure, thermal stimuli, perhaps minor incisions, to map the extent of this immunity."

I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the evening breeze. "I cannot permit you to cut my villagers, sir. I am here to heal them, not to turn them into laboratory rats."

Thorne looked at me with a pitying smile. "Doctor Vance, you are a man of the old school. A good man, no doubt, but limited in vision. I have the authority of the Colonial Office—or rather, I have the funds, which is much the same thing. I shall not harm them. Merely observe."

For the first week, things went smoothly. Thorne was energetic, almost manic. He set up a laboratory in a store hut, filling the air with the acrid stench of carbolic acid and ether. He gathered the village children, bribing them with beads and sugar, and spent hours pricking their fingers with needles, measuring the diameter of their pupils, and timing their reactions with a stopwatch. He was charming in a brusque way, and the villagers, accustomed to the eccentricities of white men, played along.

But then his curiosity turned to the edge of the knife. Literally.

I came into his hut one afternoon to find him holding the arm of a young man named Kobo. Kobo was a fine fellow, a fisherman, and he sat there grinning as Thorne drew a scalpel along the length of his forearm. A thin line of red welled up.

"Thorne!" I shouted. "What in God’s name are you doing?"

"Precision, Vance, precision," he muttered, not looking up. "I am testing the elasticity of the dermis against the stimulus. He feels nothing. Look at him. Not a flicker."

"He feels nothing now," I said, snatching the scalpel away. "But the infection will come later. The pain you cannot feel is a warning system. You are disabling the alarm and then setting the house on fire."

"You are hysterical," Thorne said, wiping the blade. "I have applied antiseptic. I need to know if the nerve endings are simply dead or if the signal is being blocked at the spinal cord. I must go deeper."

I forbade him to touch Kobo again. He acquiesced with a shrug, but that night I saw the light in his hut burning until three in the morning. The next day, Kobo did not come to the river. I went to his hut. His arm was swollen, angry and purple up to the elbow. The small incision had become a raging sepsis. He was shivering with fever, though he still maintained he felt no pain.

I treated him as best I could, but the infection was aggressive. I confronted Thorne in the mess room.

"Your experiment has given the boy blood poisoning," I said.

Thorne was examining a slide under a microscope. "Unfortunate," he said. "But the data is fascinating. The immune response is sluggish. It appears the condition that blocks pain also suppresses the inflammatory reaction. He is a fascinating specimen."

"He is a human being!" I roared. "And he is dying."

"Then he is dying for Science," Thorne said coolly. "Do not be a prig, Vance. If we can cure pain for the civilized world, a few bumps in the road here are a small price to pay."

I realized then that Thorne was not merely a scientist; he was a fanatic. He looked at the M’Fasi not as people, but as a collection of biological anomalies to be exploited. His politeness was a veneer, thin as the skin he liked to slice.
