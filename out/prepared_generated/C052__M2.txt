The sky over Whitechapel had turned the color of a bruised plum, heavy with a silence that was louder than the thunder it threatened. It was the third day of the madness. The horses had not gone wild in the traditional sense, with foaming maws and kicking hooves; they had gone *quiet*. They had simply stopped. They stood in the middle of the busy streets, necks arched like question marks, eyes wide and unblinking, staring at a point in the middle distance that no human could see. When a carriage tried to pass, they did not bolt. They simply stepped sideways, locking their legs, refusing to yield, turning the arteries of London into clogged veins of iron and wood.

Arthur Penhaligon sat in the cab, the leather seat creaking under his weight. His hands gripped the reins so tightly his knuckles were white, but the reins were slack. His horse, a bay mare named Elspeth, stood motionless on the cobblestones of Bermondsey Street, her breath misting in the cold November air. Around them, the city was unraveling. Cabs piled up like discarded toys. Pedestrians, trapped on the pavements, shouted curses and prayers into the grey void. A police constable had fallen from his mount two streets back, not because the horse had kicked, but because the horse had simply ceased to move, and the constable had tried to pull himself over the saddle in a panic.

Arthur loved Elspeth. It was a love that had nothing to do with utility and everything to do with memory. He had bought her when he was a boy, a scrawny thing with a limp, and he had nursed her back to strength with barley porridge and quiet words. Now, she was the finest mare in the district, and she was currently acting as a roadblock to the ambulance trying to reach the infirmary on New Road.

"Move, you beast," Arthur whispered, his voice cracking. He tugged the left rein. Elspeth’s ears pinned back, but her body remained a statue of muscle and bone. A fly landed on her muzzle. She did not blink.

Above him, the sky darkened further. The chaos was spreading. From the direction of the Tower, a sound rose—a low, collective neighing that vibrated in Arthur’s chest, a sound of pure, distilled terror. The horses were not just stopping; they were listening. And whatever they were listening to, it was making the humans mad.

Arthur looked at the crowd gathering around the impasse. They were not angry anymore. They were frightened. A woman in a bonnet was weeping, clutching her child. A man in a top hat was pacing, his face pale as chalk. Arthur knew what would happen next. The military would arrive. They would not negotiate. They would not try to reason with the animals. They would shoot them. They would shoot them where they stood, filling the streets with gore and the air with the smell of cordite and fear.

And they would shoot Elspeth.

The thought hit Arthur with the force of a physical blow. He could not let that happen. He could not watch the woman who had carried him through his worst days, who had shared the warmth of his cab on the coldest nights, be turned into a target.

He looked at the basement door of the chandler’s shop next to the cab. It was locked, but Arthur had the key. He had bought tallow there every week for five years. He looked at Elspeth. She was still staring into the middle distance, her eyes wet and reflective.

"Wait for me," Arthur said. He did not wait for a response. He kicked open the cab door and dropped to the street. The cobblestones were slick with rain. He moved quickly, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. He reached the shop door, fumbling with the key. His fingers were numb. The key stuck. He jiggled it, cursed under his breath, and forced the lock. The door swung open, releasing a smell of wax and damp earth.

The basement was a labyrinth of shelves stacked with blocks of tallow and jars of rendered fat. It was dark, save for the weak light filtering in from the street above. Arthur grabbed a lantern from the shelf. The flame flickered, casting long, dancing shadows.

He ran back to the cab. The crowd was growing. A policeman was shouting into a megaphone, his voice distorted and harsh. "Clear the area! Everyone clear the area!"

Arthur reached the cab. He looked up at Elspeth. She had turned her head, just slightly, to look at him. For a second, the madness seemed to recede from her eyes, replaced by the familiar, gentle intelligence he knew. Then she looked away again, back to the invisible horror.

"Come on, girl," Arthur said. "We have to go."

He unclipped the harness. His hands shook, but he did it with the precision of a man who had done it a thousand times. He pulled the leather straps loose, one by one. The cab groaned, settling onto its springs. Elspeth did not move. She stood there, a pillar of defiance.

Arthur had to lead her. He took the lead rope and gently touched her nose. "Come on. Just a little way. Please."

She moved. One step. Then another. The sound of her hooves on the cobblestones was a sharp, rhythmic click in the chaotic silence of the street. They moved in tandem, Arthur in front, Elspeth behind. The crowd parted for them, not out of courtesy, but out of a superstitious awe. They saw a man and a horse moving with a strange, coordinated grace amidst the paralysis of the city.

They reached the chandler’s shop. Arthur pushed the door open and held it. Elspeth stepped inside, her hooves crunching on the dirt floor. The heavy door slammed shut, cutting off the view of the street, the sky, and the encroaching darkness.
