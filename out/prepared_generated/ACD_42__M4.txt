I had taken chambers in Craven Street that autumn, chiefly because the fogs of the river suited my constitution better than the keen air of the Surrey hills, and it was there that Mr. Alaric Vane was shown up to me one wet Tuesday in October. He was a big, loose-limbed man of sixty, with the yellow skin and the pale, puckered eyes of one who has lived long in the East, and he carried his left shoulder a little higher than the right, as men do who have taken a fall among rocks. He wore a good broadcloth coat, but the collar was frayed at the edge, and his boots had been blacked by a hand less skilful than his own.

“You are the person who finds things out,” said he, when he had settled himself in the chair opposite me. “I have come on a matter that is plaguing me, and I would rather pay a stranger than set the police upon it.”

“You may tell me as much or as little as you please,” I answered. “But I warn you that a story with a hole in the middle of it is the hardest kind of story to act upon.”

He turned his hat over in his hands twice before he spoke again. “My name is Vane. For eleven years I was a tea planter in the Kandyan country, and my partner was a young fellow named Rollo Bastable—a clever lad, too clever by half, with a tongue that could talk a Tamil coolie into thinking himself a rajah. In the year ’eighty-nine he went down to Colombo on business of the firm, and the next thing I heard was that he had been drowned in the harbour by the upsetting of a native boat. There was an inquest, and a body was viewed, and I paid for the stone that stands over him in the churchyard at Kanatte. I was glad to pay it, for I had liked the lad once.”

“And now?”

“And now,” said he, “I am told that he is alive, and in Liverpool. A man who served under me as overseer—a steady fellow named Wickham, who keeps a public-house in Bootle—wrote to me a fortnight back that a gentleman had come into his bar one evening, and had asked for a glass of arrack, which no Englishman asks for unless he has lived where it is drunk, and had fallen into talk of the old estate, and had called the head clerk by a nickname that only three men in the world ever knew. Wickham swears it was Bastable, and Wickham is not a man who sees ghosts. Two days later I came up to Liverpool myself, and I have been here a week.”

“Have you seen him?”

“No. But I have been seen.” He drew a folded paper from his breast-pocket and laid it before me. “On Friday last I found this pushed under my door at the hotel.”

The paper bore a single line in a sprawling, half-formed hand: *Go home, Vane. The dead keep their own counsel.*

“And on Sunday,” he continued, “I was followed from the landing-stage by a lascar sailor—a tall fellow in a blue jersey, with a silver ring in his ear. He walked behind me as far as the corner of Bold Street, and when I turned he stood still and looked at me, and then went off down a side street as easy as you please. I am not a nervous man, Mr. Mordaunt, but I have no fancy for being watched by a heathen in a Christian town.”

“You have enemies in Ceylon, perhaps?”

“Every planter has enemies. It is the nature of the trade.”

“And your partnership with Bastable—did it end in friendship?”

He looked out of the window at the rain blowing against the glass, and for a moment his heavy face was the face of a man remembering something he would rather forget. “It ended,” said he, “as partnerships do. I would rather not go into it.”

“Then I must go into it for you,” said I. “Where did you live in Ceylon?”

“At a place called Heddegalla, four miles from Kandy.”

“And the firm?”

“Vane and Bastable. The bungalow was mine; the money was mostly mine too, though he had a small share. I taught him the trade, and he was quick at it—quicker than I liked, at the last.”

“You quarrelled.”

“I have told you it ended,” said he, with a sudden hardness in his voice. “I will not open the grave to satisfy your curiosity, sir. Find out what the man wants, and tell me, and I will pay you your fee and be grateful. But I will not rake over the past.”

I saw that he was of the unbending Scotch strain that will go to the stake before it goes into a witness-box, so I let him alone and set about my own inquiries.

My first visit was to Bootle, where I found Mr. Wickham, a red-faced, honest fellow, behind his bar. He repeated his story with the simplicity of a man who has no doubt of it. “He had the very trick of the head, sir, when he laughed—a little back in the throat, like a hen clucking. And he says to me, ‘Wickham, you’re getting fat, and the estate is going to the devil without you.’ Them was his very words, and nobody but Mr. Bastable ever spoke to me like that.”

“Did he say where he was staying?”

“No, sir. But he paid for his drink with a sovereign, and he had a card in his hand that he put away when I looked at it. It was a printer’s card, I think, for the corner of it had a picture of a printing-press.”
