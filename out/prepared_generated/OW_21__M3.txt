In the southern kingdom of Vallesia, where the sun is a tyrant and the shade a luxury afforded only to marble, there stood a courtyard paved with porphyry and green jasper. In the centre of this courtyard was a fountain, and in the fountain swam a fish of singular beauty. Its scales were wrought of ruddy gold, and its eyes were carbuncles that burned with a steady, secret fire. The fountain belonged to King Flaminius, a monarch whose wisdom was much praised by his courtiers, principally because he never troubled them with the exercise of it.

It was the custom of the court to gather by the fountain when the heat of the day began to decline. They came in fabrics of silk and samite, bearing fans of dyed ostrich plumes, and their conversation was as polished and as empty as the agates they wore upon their fingers.

It was into this fragrant and idle assembly that the Fairy of the Tamarisk chose to appear. She materialised one evening in a mist of pale heliotrope, her garments dyed in the colours of the dying day, and stood upon the rim of the basin. The courtiers drew back, for the fairies are dread beings, and to speak to one is to invite a sorrow that lasts for seven generations. But the Fairy of the Tamarisk did not look at them. She looked only at the fish.

‘Thou art mute,’ she said, in a voice that sounded like rain falling upon silver lutes, ‘and yet thou observest. To observe without the power of speech is the portion of the mirror and the fool. I shall give thee a tongue, for I delight in truth, which is a malady no physician can cure and a jewel no thief can steal. Speak, therefore, and let the air be tainted with honesty.’

She touched the water with her wand of white holly, and instantly dissolved into a scent of crushed jasmine. The golden fish darted to the surface, and the water churned about its gills. Then it spoke, and its voice was the cold and rhythmic murmur of the sea.

‘How profound is the silence of the court,’ said the fish, ‘save for the rustling of the courtiers’ consciences, which are as dry and brittle as last autumn’s leaves.’

A tremor passed through the assembly. The Duchess of Vallesia, a lady celebrated for her piety and her adultery in equal measure, dropped her fan of blue feathers.

‘The Duchess loses her fan,’ continued the fish, ‘as she has lost her reputation. Yet she retains her mirror, which is a poor substitute, for it flatters her face, whereas the world flatters her fortune. She gives her alms to the poor in public, and takes her pleasures with the poor in private. It is a charming symmetry.’

The Duchess grew grey as a blossom of the Judas tree, and leaning heavily upon her ebony cane, she retreated behind a pillar of yellow marble.

The fish turned its carbuncle eyes upon the Lord Chamberlain. ‘Behold the Lord Chamberlain, a man so upright that his spine has quite forgotten the shape of a bow. He wears a chain of heavy gold about his neck, the emblem of his servitude. He boasts of his noble lineage, forgetting that his grandfather was a dealer in coarse wool, and that his father bought the family escutcheon from a needy herald. The only thing truly ancient in his possession is his hypocrisy, which dates from the foundation of the world.’

The Lord Chamberlain shifted his weight and stared blindly at the painted ceiling, pretending to be absorbed in the mythology of the frescoes, which he had never understood.

‘And here is the poet,’ said the fish, gesturing with a ripple of its golden tail toward a pale young man in a velvet doublet the colour of crushed mulberries. ‘He sings of the stars, but he looks only at the king’s purse. He believes that sorrow is a becoming accessory, and so he wears his melancholy as a lady wears a ribbon, to draw attention to the pallor of her throat. His verses are beautiful, and his soul is a thing of shreds and patches, which is, I suppose, why he is considered the voice of his generation.’

The poet bit his lip, and a sneer settled upon his mouth, for he knew the charge was true, and nothing is so unforgivable as an accurate accusation.

The court was thrown into a wild confusion. Ladies gathered up their silken trains and whispered fiercely behind their fans, their eyes wide with a terrified delight. Men drew together in clusters, their smiles frozen upon their faces like masks of wax. The fountain, which had always been a symbol of the court’s tranquillity, now vomited a stream of intolerable truths. The fish proceeded from guest to guest, stripping them of their velvet and their virtues, laying bare the petty jealousies, the sordid bargains, and the hollow pretensions that lurked beneath their splendid raiment.

King Flaminius, who had been sitting upon a throne of carved ivory, watching the scene with a heavy, indolent amusement, at last descended the steps of the dais. He was a man who appreciated aesthetics above ethics, and he found the anguish of his courtiers rather becoming; it lent a tragic grandeur to their features, which had previously been ruined by complacency.

‘This is a very clever fish,’ said the King, tapping the rim of the basin with his sceptre of enamel and jade. ‘It speaks with the authority of a prophet and the malice of a courtier. We must decide whether to silence it or to listen.’

‘Slay it!’ cried the Duchess from behind her pillar, her voice trembling with a righteous indignation that she had not felt since her last confession. ‘It is a beast of the Evil One, and its words are a poison that will destroy the kingdom.’
