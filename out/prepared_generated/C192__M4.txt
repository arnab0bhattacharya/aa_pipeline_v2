The card in Miss Horatia Vance’s hand was a crisp rectangle of promise, but the name engraved upon it felt like a splinter of ice beneath her glove. Mr. Julian Ashworth. She read it again, hoping the letters might rearrange themselves into something innocuous, a Mr. Ashby or a Mr. Wentworth. They did not. The name was as stark and familiar as the scar on her brother’s temper.

“Horatia, do attend,” her aunt, Lady Bevington, chided gently, her fan a frantic metronome against the stifling air of the assembly rooms. “Mrs. Ashworth is a most respectable woman, and her son is said to be very clever. A barrister. They are newly come to town, and it would be a kindness to be civil.”

Civil. Horatia could be civil. She had been schooled in the art of pleasant nothings for the three weeks since their arrival in London, a debutante paraded like a prize heifer before the scrutinizing eyes of the *ton*. But this was different. This was Ashworth. The man whose very name her brother, Edward, could not utter without his jaw clenching and a dark, bitter light entering his eyes. A quarrel, years ago, when they were both young men at Oxford. The cause was a mystery, a locked room in her brother’s heart into which she had never been permitted to look. All she knew was the aftermath: a shattered friendship, a simmering resentment, and a name that had become a curse in their household.

And now, here he was. Not a monster with horns, but a gentleman who bowed with practised elegance as Mrs. Ashworth, a plump, cheerful woman, drew him forward.

“Miss Vance, may I present my son, Mr. Julian Ashworth.”

He was taller than she had imagined, with a lean, angular face that was more striking than handsome. His hair was a dark, unruly brown, and his eyes, a startling shade of grey, held an intelligence that seemed to miss nothing. They swept over her, and for a fleeting instant, she thought she saw a flicker of something—recognition, perhaps, or a shadow of the same cold splinter she felt in her own heart. Then it was gone, replaced by a flawless, social smile.

“Miss Vance,” he said, his voice a low, pleasant baritone. “I am delighted to make your acquaintance. Your aunt has been most kind in her praise of you.”

The polite words were a shield. “You are too kind, Mr. Ashworth,” she managed, her own voice sounding thin and reedy to her ears. She felt a hot flush creep up her neck. Did he know? Of course he knew. He had been the other party in that mysterious duel of wills that had cost her brother a measure of his peace. He knew, and he was watching her, waiting to see how she would play her part in this grotesque comedy.

The conversation that followed was a masterpiece of evasion. They spoke of the weather, of the recent exhibition at the Royal Academy, of the interminable length of the season. He was articulate and witty, his observations sharp without being cruel. He possessed a dry humour that, under any other circumstances, she might have found charming. But every polished phrase felt like a move in a game she did not understand, and his steady grey eyes seemed to be reading the secrets of her soul.

“And your family, Miss Vance?” he asked, his tone deceptively light. “Are they all in good health?”

The question was a rapier thrust, delivered with a smile. “They are, I thank you,” she replied, her own smile feeling brittle as glass. “My brother, Edward, is at our estate in Northamptonshire. He is a great lover of the country.”

“Ah, Edward,” Mr. Ashworth said, and the name hung in the air between them, heavy with unspoken history. “I recall he was always one for the outdoors. A man of strong convictions, as I remember.”

Strong convictions. It was a generous way to describe the fierce, unyielding pride that was her brother’s greatest strength and his most profound flaw. “He is a man of great principle,” Horatia said, a defensive edge sharpening her voice.

Mr. Ashworth’s smile did not waver, but something in his eyes hardened. “Indeed. Principles are a fine thing. They can, however, sometimes be a luxury one can ill afford.” He paused, allowing the words to settle. “A man must sometimes choose between the letter of his principle and the spirit of… compassion.”

The words were a riddle, a key offered to a lock she could not find. What had happened at Oxford? What principle had Edward clung to so fiercely that it had broken a friendship and left this residue of bitter irony in its wake? She felt a surge of frustration, a desperate need to know.

“I confess I do not take your meaning, sir,” she said, her voice barely a whisper.

He leaned a little closer, and for a moment, the mask of civility slipped. She saw a weariness in his face, a deep, settled sadness that mirrored the anger she knew in her brother. “Do you not? I think you do, Miss Vance. You have your brother’s directness. It is a family trait, I perceive.” He straightened up, the social mask back in place. “But I forget myself. This is hardly the place for such weighty matters. The musicians are striking up. Perhaps you would do me the honour of a dance?”

To dance with him would be a betrayal. To refuse him would cause a scene. Her aunt was watching, her fan stilled, a look of hopeful expectation on her face. Horatia felt trapped, caught between the ghost of her brother’s grievance and the demands of her present reality. She placed her hand in his, her fingers cold and stiff.
