The summons had come upon me as a thunderclap from a serene sky, and even now, as I stood within the great hall of the newly-raised edifice, its echo reverberated through the chambers of my heart with a disquiet that mocked the outward pomp and jubilee. The building itself was a monument to a prosperity so sudden and so vast that it seemed to shame the slow, grey antiquity of the town that cradled it; its walls of fresh-hewn stone rose with a confident and gleaming whiteness, its windows, tall and many-paned, caught the pale, reluctant light of the Lancashire morning, and the great clock in the central tower, a masterpiece of brass and polished steel, ticked with a precision that felt to me like the very pulse of the age—an age of iron, of engines, of unrelenting forward motion, where the past was but a shadow to be trodden underfoot. And at the heart of all this splendour stood the man, Mr. Josiah Hartley, the benefactor, the founder, the idol of the hour, his broad, ruddy countenance wreathed in smiles that seemed to me, in my fevered apprehension, to hold a secret and a mockery.

My name is Thomas Ashworth, and I was, by the strange and cruel alchemy of fate, the grandson of the man whom this same Josiah Hartley had ruined. My grandfather, a weaver of honest, if narrow, means, had taken the young and penniless Hartley into his employ, had taught him the mysteries of the loom, had trusted him with the secrets of his trade; and Hartley, with a cunning that was the more monstrous for its patient dissimulation, had used that trust to steal his designs, to undercut his prices, and to build the first foundation of his colossal fortune upon the broken fortunes of his master. My grandfather had died, not of want, for his pride forbade him to beg, but of a broken heart, a slow and silent decay that ate away his spirit as the canker eats the rose. The story had been dinned into my ears from childhood, a bitter inheritance of shame and sorrow, and I had grown up with the name of Hartley as a synonym for all that was treacherous and base.

And now, by a refinement of cruelty that I could not fathom, this very man had sought me out. He had learned of my position as the schoolmaster of the town, of the small repute I had gained for learning and for the earnestness with which I endeavoured to instil into the minds of my scholars a love of truth and honour; and he had sent for me, with a show of condescension that galled my soul, to request that I would deliver the address at the opening of his new school—a school which he designed, he said, as a lasting monument to his own benevolence, and as a means of educating the children of the poor, of which he had himself once been the humblest. The invitation had been couched in terms so flattering, so full of a seeming respect for my abilities, that I had been for a moment deceived; but the truth had flashed upon me with a sickening certainty. He wished to parade his victim before the world, to show how completely he had triumphed, to make the grandson of the man he had destroyed the mouthpiece of his own praises. It was a subtle and a devilish revenge.

Yet I had accepted. Why, I scarcely knew, save that some dark and irresistible fascination drew me towards the man, some wild hope that in his presence I might read the secret of his guilt, might pierce the armour of his self-complacency, and find, beneath the gilded surface, the worm of remorse that I was certain must gnaw at his heart. I had prepared my address with a feverish diligence, choosing my words with a care that was almost agonizing, seeking to weave into the fabric of my oratory a thread of truth so fine, so delicate, that it should escape the notice of the vulgar crowd, yet sting the conscience of the guilty man. I would speak of the sacredness of early trust, of the meanness of ingratitude, of the eternal laws of justice that, though they may sleep, can never die.

The hour was now at hand. The hall was filled with the magnates of the town, with the gentry of the surrounding country, with the clergy and the magistrates, all arrayed in their bravery, all eager to do homage to the idol of the day. The air was heavy with the scent of hot-house flowers and the murmur of obsequious voices. I stood upon the platform, pale and trembling, my manuscript in my hand, and I felt the gaze of a thousand eyes fixed upon me, and, above all, the gaze of that one man, who sat in the place of honour, his countenance beaming with a paternal benignity that seemed to me the very mask of hell.

He rose to introduce me. His voice was rich, full, and sonorous, the voice of a man who had been accustomed to command, and who had learned to use his tongue as an instrument of power. He spoke of his own humble origin, of the struggles of his youth, of the debt he owed to the kindly patronage he had received—a debt, he said, which he had ever sought to repay by a life devoted to the service of his fellow-men. He spoke of the school as the crowning work of his life, as a thank-offering to the Providence that had prospered him. And then he turned to me, and his eye met mine, and for a moment—a moment that seemed an eternity—I saw something flicker in its depths, something that was not benevolence, something that was cold and hard and watchful. He spoke my name, and the name of my grandfather, and a murmur of sympathy ran through the assembly, for the old story was known, though the truth of it was not.
