The rain coming down on London was the colour of weak tea, stained by the particulate churned up from the Thames Expansion Works. Inside the Thomas Coram Memorial Tower, the rain was a distant memory, buried beneath the relentless, low-frequency hum of the filtration units and the sharp, rhythmic hiss of the auto-syringes. Elara moved through the fourth-floor ward with the practised, economical grace of a ghost, her white dermalfabrics whispering against the sterile polymer floors. She was twenty-six, but the hollows beneath her eyes aged her by a decade. The ward was a gallery of quiet suffocation.

They called it the Lucid Rot, though the official designation on Elara’s datapad was Neuro-Respiratory Schlerosis, Stage IV. It was a disease of the new century, born from a mutated prion that thrived in the city’s overheated, hyper-dense atmosphere. It attacked the lungs first, turning the delicate alveolar sacs into brittle, calcified honeycomb, before creeping up the vagus nerve to the brain. The cruellest aspect of the Rot was its nickname: the lucidity. Unlike the plagues of old, it did not cloud the mind. It left the victim terrifyingly, painfully aware of their own incremental drowning until the brain simply forgot how to tell the lungs to breathe.

In Bed 14, Kael was sitting up, his torso illuminated by the pale blue glow of the biometric monitor suspended above him. He was thirty, an architect who had helped design the solar-canopies over the East End before the prion had breached his blood-brain barrier. His skin had the translucent, greyish pallor of advanced Lucid Rot, the veins beneath his arms mapping out like dark rivers. When Elara approached, he looked up, and his eyes were perfectly clear. They were the colour of wet slate, and they held a quiet, devastating acceptance that made Elara’s chest constrict every time she saw it.

“You’re hovering, Nurse Vance,” Kael said, his voice a raspy, shallow whisper. He spoke in the short, measured bursts the disease demanded, rationing his air. “The auto-drip is fine.”

“The auto-drip is a machine, and machines get complacent,” Elara said, her fingers light as she pressed the cool flat of her diagnostic band to his wrist. “I’m here to make sure it remembers who’s in charge.”

She did not need to check the drip. She knew the dosage was perfect; she had calibrated it herself during the last shift. She just wanted to be near him. She knew what she was doing. She knew that Kael’s lung capacity was down to eighteen percent, that the neural degradation had passed the medulla and was inching toward the motor cortex, and that the survival rate for Stage IV was exactly zero. She knew the hospital was a factory for the dying, overwhelmed by the sheer volume of the afflicted, and that attaching herself to a man who was actively fading was a professional and emotional suicide. She knew all of this. She did it anyway.

“How are the spires?” Elara asked, adjusting the flow valve on his intravenous line.

“Gleaming,” Kael breathed. He closed his eyes, leaning his head back against the sterile pillow. “The solar-canopy over Stratford caught the morning light today. I saw it on the ward screen. My team finished the latticework.”

“It’s beautiful,” Elara murmured, though she was not looking at the ward screen. She was looking at the fragile architecture of his collarbones, the way they sharpened against his skin.

Over the next three weeks, Elara volunteered for every extra shift the ward matron would grant. She became Kael’s primary night nurse. While the rest of the ward slept, or wept, or rattled their last breaths into the recycler units, Elara sat on the edge of Kael’s bed. They spoke in the hushed, intimate tones of people who know their time is severely metered. He told her about the sustainable bamboo-concrete he had pioneered, about the way he wanted to build buildings that breathed for the people inside them. She told him about the coast of Cornwall, where she had grown up, about the smell of salt and the cry of the gulls, things that were entirely absent in the locked-down, smog-choked metropolis of 2084.

On a Tuesday night, when the rain lashed against the reinforced poly-glass windows, Elara bypassed the ward’s ambient sensor grid. She climbed into the bed beside him. It was a violation of every protocol coded into her nursing charter. If the matron caught her, she would be stripped of her credentials and reassigned to the sanitation wards. She did not care.

Kael’s skin was cool, the heat of his body fighting a losing battle against the failing circulation. She rested her head on his chest, listening to the wet, tearing sound of his breath, feeling the shallow, erratic thud of his heart.

“You shouldn’t be here,” Kael whispered into her hair. His hand, trembling with the effort, came to rest on her shoulder.

“I know,” Elara said.

“You’ll get sick. Or you’ll get fired. Or you’ll get your heart broken, Elara.”

“I know,” she repeated, pressing her face into the crook of his neck, breathing in the sterile scent of the antiseptic wipes and the faint, underlying trace of the man he had been. “I know exactly what I’m doing.”

She knew that loving him was like holding a handful of dry sand; the tighter she squeezed, the faster he slipped through her fingers. But she also knew that the alternative—standing at a safe distance, watching him die with clinical detachment—was a kind of death she was not willing to endure. In a world where the Rot stripped away everything else, this deliberate, chosen agony was the only thing that felt real.

By Friday, the lucidity began to fracture. Kael’s mind, starved of oxygen, started to drift. He called her by his sister’s name for a few minutes, then stared at the ceiling, speaking in fragments of architectural blueprints. Elara sat beside him, holding his hand, running her thumb over his knuckles. She did not correct him. She just held on.
