The rain lashed Carndale. It came not in the soft, nurturing showers of a milder district, but in driven, icy sheets that scoured the grey cobbles of the village and beat a relentless, muffled drum against the stone. I stood at the window of my billet, watching the swollen beck churn brown and furious beneath the stone bridge. Behind me, the fire crackled with a harsh, unnatural red, throwing flickering, gigantic shadows against the wainscot, but it cast no warmth into my spirit. The order lay folded on the table, a meagre slip of paper that bore the weight of an empire’s displeasure. I was to arrest Josiah Bryne before dawn.

Bryne was a weaver by trade, a man whose frame had been whittled to iron sinew by years of labour at the loom, and whose tongue, so my captain had warned me, was dipped in the pitch of sedition. In these dark, restless times, when starving men whispered of revolution and the breaking of frames, a loose tongue was deemed as lethal as a Frenchman's musket. I was a soldier of the King; my duty was plain. Yet, as the wind howled down the valley like a legion of wandering spirits, I felt a profound, unquiet reluctance—a heavy, dragging weight upon my conscience that I could neither dismiss nor define.

It was not fear of the weaver, nor of the desperate, ragged men who might rally to his door. I had faced batteries abroad and felt the wind of grapeshot. It was the memory of a still, sunlit afternoon, scarce a week past, that checked my resolve. I had walked up from the mill to the small, austere chapel that stood on the brow of the hill. My sister Clara attends the Sunday school there, placed under my care by our late father’s wishes. I had gone to fetch her home, and through the open door I had witnessed a scene that fixed itself indelibly in my mind.

A young woman stood at the head of the room, teaching. She was not beautiful in the fashion of the London drawing-rooms I had left behind; her face was too pale, her features too sharply drawn by care or poverty. But her eyes—dark, profound, and luminous—held a steady, earnest fire that commanded the ragged urchins before her with an authority no militia officer could hope to wield. She spoke of patience, of the endurance of the oppressed, and her voice, clear and low, struck a chord in my soul that vibrated with a strange, sympathetic pain. When Clara had come out, I asked her the teacher’s name.

"That is Miss Bryne," Clara had replied. "Her father is the weaver whom the masters hate."

I had not sought out the daughter; I had not known. But Providence, it seemed, possessed a harsh, ironic wit. I was to drag the father to the cage, while the daughter taught the Word of God to my sister.

I turned from the window. I must do it. To falter was to betray my oath. I buckled on my sword, the metal clashing with a sound that seemed profane in the quiet room, and stepped out into the storm.

We came to the door of the cottage in a hollow of the lane, where the water dripped heavily from the broken eaves. The mill stood opposite, a dark, brooding mass. I had brought two men, but I motioned them to remain in the shadow of the wall. I would knock alone.

The door was opened by Bryne himself. He was tall, gaunt, and stern. He wore no coat, but his shirt was open at the throat, revealing the harsh, angular bones of his chest. He looked at me, from my martial coat to my belted sword, and a dark, bitter smile crossed his face.

"You are expected, Captain," he said, his voice deep and rough as the grind of a millstone. "I knew the masters would send their wolves before the night was out."

"I am no wolf, Bryne," I returned, feeling a sudden, hot flush of shame that I could not suppress. "I am a soldier doing my duty. I have a warrant for your arrest, on a charge of uttering seditious words against the crown and the peace of the district."

"Seditious words," he repeated, stepping back to let me enter. "Aye, it is sedition to tell a starving man he has a right to bread. It is sedition to say the law that hangs a man for breaking a frame is a law written in hell. Come in, Captain. You shall do your errand."

The cottage was clean, but bare. A single tallow candle flickered on the table. And there, by the hearth, stood his daughter.

She looked at me, and the recognition in her eyes was swift and sharp. I saw the colour drain from her face, leaving it as white as the frosted heath in winter. She did not speak; she did not cry out. She merely drew herself up, her hands clasped tightly before her, and fixed upon me a gaze that demanded an accounting.

"Miss Bryne," I said, and my voice was hoarse. "I am sorry."

"Sorry," she echoed, and now her voice came, low, vibrant, and trembling with a contained fury. "You are sorry, Captain. And what is your sorrow to us? Will it buy bread for the children? Will it keep my father from the dungeon? You speak of sorrow, while you wear the uniform of the men who starve us!"

"Peace, Martha," the old man interposed, laying a heavy hand on her arm. "He is but a tool. The men who send him are the masters. He cannot help it."

"I can help it," I burst forth, stung by her scorn, yet feeling the justice of it deep in my bones. "I can help it, Miss Bryne. I could refuse the order. I could tear off this coat and stand with you against the magistrates. But what would it avail? It would not feed the hungry. It would not stop the machinery that crushes you. It would only add my own ruin to yours."
