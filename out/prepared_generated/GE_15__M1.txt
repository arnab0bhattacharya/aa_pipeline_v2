Mrs. Pargeter, seated in the high-backed armchair that had been her husband’s, regarded the coal fire with the unseeing gaze of one whose eyes are turned inward upon a landscape of memory and grievance. The parlour of her house in High Street, Ashbourne, was a testament to a life of careful, solitary effort. The mahogany gleamed with the polish of her own hand; the Brussels carpet, though faded in the path from the door to the hearth, was scrupulously clean. For twenty years, since the death of Mr. Pargeter, a man of substance if not of imagination, she had maintained this household and, more importantly, the fortunes of her only son, Thomas, with a vigilance that was both her pride and her solitary occupation. She had denied herself a second spring, a companionable evening, the small indulgences of the town, all in the service of an ambition for the boy that had grown into a kind of religious faith. He was to be a solicitor, a man of standing, a bulwark against the financial imbecility that had whittled away at his grandfather’s estate. And Thomas, a dutiful and handsome youth, had seemed to be the very marble from which such a statue might be carved.

Now, the marble was speaking, and its words were causing a profound and painful fissure in the foundations of her world. Thomas stood before her, not with his usual easy confidence, but with the rigid posture of a man awaiting a verdict he dreads. His confession had been delivered in a low, hurried voice, as if he could lessen its impact by hurrying past it: he was married. He had been married these three months. The girl was Clara Sims, the daughter of the ironmonger whose shop stood near the chapel in the lower part of the town. The chapel. That was the fulcrum of the offence. Not merely that the girl was of a station so far below their own, but that she belonged to that sect of dissenters who held their noisy, unsanctified meetings on a Wednesday evening, a constant, grating reminder to Mrs. Pargeter of all that was disorderly and presumptuous in the world. She had brought up her son in the shadow of St. Oswald’s, with its ancient stones and its sober, established faith; she had seen to it that his linen was of the best and his associations unimpeachable. And he had, in one fell, secret stroke, allied himself with cheap calico and a God who seemed to require no proper introduction.

“Her blessing,” Thomas had said, the words catching in his throat. “We ask only for your blessing, Mother.”

For a long time, Mrs. Pargeter said nothing. The silence in the room grew heavy, pressing down on the two figures, broken only by the ticking of the long-case clock and the soft hiss of the gaslight. It was a silence in which a whole history was being re-examined. She saw the years of her widowhood not as a sacrifice, but as an investment, and here was the dividend: a secret betrayal, a dilution of all she had striven to make pure and solid. The bitterness rose in her throat, a physical taste of gall. It was not the girl, not really. A girl could be educated, or at least managed. It was the deception. It was the quiet, smiling conspiracy against her, the knowledge that while she had been counting his shirts and approving his ledgers, he had been walking out with the ironmonger’s daughter, making vows in some dreary meeting-house, living a double life. Her love for him, which had been the very substance of her own life, now felt like a tool that had been used against her. She had given him everything, and he had valued her judgment so little that he had hidden from her the one act that would remake his entire existence.

“Your blessing,” she repeated at last, and the words were as dry as autumn leaves. She looked at him, this young man who was the living image of his father in the set of his jaw, but who possessed a certain softness about the mouth that was all his own. That softness, she now saw, was not a sign of gentle feeling, but of a fatal pliability, a weakness that had allowed him to be led astray. The thought was a fresh wound. To have raised a son only to find him lacking in the one quality she had prized above all others: the strength to stand by one’s own. “You have been very clever, Thomas. A man who can keep a secret from his mother for three months has a talent for concealment that will serve him well in the law, I have no doubt.”

The sarcasm was a thin shield for a profound hurt. She saw the boy flinch, and a part of her, the old maternal part, longed to cross the room and soothe him. But a stronger, colder principle held her in its grip. This was not a childish mistake to be kissed and forgiven. This was a fundamental re-ordering of their world, done without her consent. It was an assertion of his independence that felt like a declaration of war. Her love for him was not a simple, warm current; it was a complex and powerful force, interwoven with her own pride, her anxieties, and the entire structure of the life she had built for him. To bless this marriage would be to bless the demolition of that life. It would be to say that all her careful plans, her economies, her anxieties over his future, had been for nothing. It would be to admit that she had been a fool. And Mrs. Pargeter could not endure to be thought a fool, least of all by herself.
