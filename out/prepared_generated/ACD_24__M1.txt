It was upon a raw and gusty evening in the latter part of November that the matter was brought to my attention. My friend, Mr. Sheridan Blake, had called upon me at my chambers in Half Moon Street, his long, scholarly frame hunched within his overcoat as if to ward off a chill that was not entirely of the weather. He was a man of precise habits and quiet scholarship, the senior classical master at St. Jude’s College, and the agitation in his manner was as out of place as a smudge of ink upon a spotless cuff.

“It is a bad business, Alistair,” he began, having declined a whisky and soda. “A very bad business. I would not have troubled you, but the police are coarse fellows, and my colleague, Dr. Finch, the headmaster, is a man of the old school. He sees things in black and white.”

“Tell me the facts of the case, as you would have me see them,” said I, settling myself in my armchair. “Begin at the beginning.”

“The beginning,” he said, “is a set of forged cheques drawn upon the school’s account at the County and Provincial Bank. Three of them, to be precise, for varying sums, made out to a ‘Mr. J. Carter’ and cashed at a branch in the city. The total comes to nearly two hundred pounds. A tidy sum for a school of our modest means.”

“And the culprit?”

“Dr. Finch is convinced it is one of our senior boys. A boy named Percival Thorne. He is seventeen, a brilliant scholar, but with a proud, secretive disposition. More to the point, he is one of the few boys with access to the headmaster’s study, as he serves as a monitor. The handwriting on the cheques is a bold, flourishing script, full of unnecessary loops and a flamboyant cross on the ‘t’. It is, in fact, a caricature of Percival Thorne’s own hand.”

I steepled my fingers. “A caricature, you say? That is a peculiar turn of phrase.”

“Precisely. It is too like, and yet entirely unlike. The forger has taken the boy’s characteristic flourishes and exaggerated them to the point of absurdity. But look here.” He drew from his waistcoat pocket a folded sheet of paper and passed it to me. “This is a Latin exercise written by Thorne yesterday. The script is small, neat, and upright. There is not a single point of genuine similarity beyond the most superficial attempt at imitation. The police ‘expert’ declared the writing to be ‘unmistakably the same,’ but the man is a fool.”

Before I could examine the paper further, a sharp, impatient rap fell upon the door. My landlady announced a Mr. Finch, and the headmaster of St. Jude’s entered like a gale. He was a stout, florid man with heavy brows and the air of one accustomed to immediate obedience.

“Blake! I thought I should find you here,” he boomed, his eyes sweeping over my modest room with a faint disapproval. “Mr. Alistair Vance, I presume. I am Finch. I have come to fetch my man back. There is a fresh development.”

“Indeed?” I said. “Pray, be seated and tell us.”

He ignored the offer of a chair. “The study has been burgled. Sometime between dinner and now. The window forced, the desk ransacked. Nothing of value seems to be gone, save for the school’s chequebook itself. It is an outrage! A deliberate act of defiance. The boy Thorne was seen near the quadrangle not an hour since. It is as plain as a pikestaff.”

“Plainness,” I observed, “is often a mask for a lack of thought. Mr. Blake has just shown me the forged cheques. May I see the scene of this new crime?”

Finch grumbled, but his desire for a swift resolution overcame his objection. A short carriage ride brought us to the grim, soot-stained edifice of St. Jude’s, and we were ushered into the headmaster’s study. It was a room that spoke of authority and tradition: dark oak shelves lined with calf-bound volumes, a heavy mahogany desk, a globe, and a fire burning low in the grate. The French window to the small garden beyond stood ajar, its catch splintered.

My first attention was for the window. The wood was old, but the break was fresh. “A common jemmy, or perhaps a stout screwdriver,” I murmured, running a finger over the raw wood. I turned to the desk. Papers were scattered, but as Finch had said, the disarray seemed more theatrical than thorough. The inkwell had been overturned, a pool of black spreading across the leather blotter.

“The chequebook,” Finch said, pointing to an empty space. “Gone.”

I did not look at the empty space. My gaze had fallen upon the carpet, just beside the leg of the desk. I knelt. There, caught in the pile of the Axminster, was a single, short thread of wool. It was a brilliant, almost garish, shade of crimson.

“Mr. Finch,” I said, rising slowly. “Which of your masters wears a scarf, or perhaps a waistcoat, of so distinctive a red?”

Finch blinked. “Red? Why, that is the colour of the St. Anselm’s rowing colours. Our rivals. It is a common enough colour, is it not?”

“Common, perhaps. But this thread is not common. It is of a coarse, hard twist. And there is something else.” I moved to the fireplace. The embers were glowing, but a small, charred object lay upon the fender. I coaxed it out with the poker. It was the corner of a cardboard tag, the kind used on luggage or, more specifically, on a boat’s equipment. Upon it, in faded blue ink, I could just make out the letters ‘ANSEL’.

I straightened up and faced the two men. “Mr. Blake’s instinct was correct. The forger is not Percival Thorne. The handwriting was a deliberate plant, designed to point to the boy and, by extension, to implicate Mr. Blake, who championed him. The burglary was a clumsy afterthought, designed to remove the chequebook and suggest a thief who was desperate or bold.”
