The rumour had begun in the taverns, passed from glass to glass like a cold draught, and had settled upon the city of St. Jude’s with the weight of a scientific certainty. It was said that a body of iron, no larger than a house but moving with the velocity of a cannonball, would strike the earth at the stroke of midnight. The newspapers had laughed at it first, then printed the calculations of a minor professor at the university, and then, finding the calculations irrefutable, had ceased to laugh entirely. The city, usually asleep by nine, was now a hive of agitated, sleepless industry. Shops shuttered early; carriages were halted in the streets; and in the houses of the respectable classes, a peculiar, brittle tension had replaced the usual routine of tea and correspondence.

In the large, dimly lit drawing room of the Halloway house, overlooking the cathedral nave, this tension had reached its zenith. The fire in the grate burned low, casting long, trembling shadows against the tapestries, but no one moved to feed it. Arthur Halloway, a man of forty-five with a face that had long since surrendered to a comfortable, bureaucratic roundness, sat in his chair with his hands clasped tightly around a glass of sherry that he had not touched. Opposite him sat his wife, Clara, her posture rigid, her eyes fixed on the grandfather clock in the corner. Their son, Julian, a student of natural philosophy, sat on the floor by the hearth, his back against the wall, looking neither at his father nor his mother, but at the dust motes dancing in the dying light.

The silence was not empty; it was crowded. It was the silence of a room where three people are trying to say things they have never said, while the universe prepares to end.

Arthur broke the stillness first. It was not with a grand confession, but with a petty, cutting remark that seemed grotesquely small against the backdrop of impending annihilation. "I must say, Clara, your management of the servants has always struck me as somewhat... chaotic. The way the housekeeper, Mrs. Gable, handles the accounts. I have always suspected she was skimming the bottom of the till."

Clara did not look up. Her face was pale, illuminated by the faint, blue glow of the gaslight. "You suspect many things, Arthur, but you verify none. You sit in your office and let the world spin out of control while you worry about pence. If we die at midnight, will you be able to reconcile your ledger in the grave?"

"I do not think death will stop my arithmetic," Arthur said, his voice tight. "And I do not think you can hide behind mysticism forever. You speak of the soul, of the hereafter, as if it is a separate room one can step into. But if the world ends, the room goes with it."

Julian shifted on the floor. He had been listening, his mind elsewhere, calculating the trajectory, the mass, the kinetic energy. But the domestic acrimony had a strange, gravitational pull of its own. "Father," he said, his voice young and unaccustomed to such gravity, "you are wrong. The ledger does not matter. Nor does Mrs. Gable. But you are also wrong about the soul. You treat it as a luxury. A decoration. But it is the only thing that is real. The matter is an illusion. We are the thought, not the body."

Arthur turned to look at his son, a flash of genuine anger in his eyes. "You speak like a poet, Julian. And poets are the first to go when the bridge burns. I have spent my life building this house, this family, this life. It is solid. It is real. And if it is to be destroyed, it will be destroyed because of the laws of physics, not because of some metaphysical whim."

Clara finally turned. She looked at her husband with a mixture of pity and contempt. "And what of me, Arthur? What of the years I spent in this house, watching you build your 'solid' life while I withered? I told you, years ago, that I loved another. A man who understood the music, the art. But I was afraid. I was afraid of the scandal, of the loss of position. So I stayed. I married you because you were safe. And now, as we wait to die, I find I cannot bring myself to hate you for your safety. It was the only kindness you knew how to give."

The room fell silent again, but it was a different silence. The air seemed to have thickened. Arthur’s face flushed. He opened his mouth, as if to refute, to correct, to bring the conversation back to the rational plane, but no words came. He looked at his wife, truly looked at her, and saw not the chaotic manager of servants, but a woman who had carried a secret like a stone in her chest for twenty years.

"I do not know what to do with that," Arthur said quietly. "I have built my life on the assumption that we were a unit. A machine. If one part is replaced, the whole thing changes."

"The machine is broken," Clara said. "It has always been broken. But we kept it oiled. We kept it running. And now the fuel is about to run out."

Julian stood up. He walked to the window and looked out at the dark street. The cathedral spire rose above them, a black finger pointing at the stars. The air was still, devoid of wind. There were no birds, no insects. The world held its breath.

"Look," Julian said softly.

Arthur and Clara moved to the window. They stood shoulder to shoulder, the three of them, a family of strangers bound by the walls of the house. They looked up at the sky. It was a clear, cold night, the stars burning with a harsh, white intensity. They waited for the streak of fire, the roar of impact, the end.
