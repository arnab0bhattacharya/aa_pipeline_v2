The hour had arrived; and as I looked upon the assembled faces of those who were to be my judges, I felt that my destiny was suspended by a thread, and that in the space of a few moments, the labour of a lifetime—my hope, my reputation, my very soul’s peace—was to be irrevocably fixed, either to the summit of human knowledge or to the abyss of eternal obscurity. It was the theatre of the Royal Institution; but to me it appeared rather as a Roman amphitheatre, where I was the gladiator cast unarmed into the arena, to be torn by the wild beasts of ridicule and failure.

There I stood, beside the great glass retort and the battery of Leyden jars, which seemed to mock me with their cold, silent insensibility. I had devoted twenty years to the discovery of the *Anima Mundi*, that vital fluid which pervades all organized matter, the subtle ether which separates the living clay from the inert dust. I had seen its phantom, I had felt its breath in the storm and the flower, yet I had never captured it. And now, the moment of proof was at hand.

My antagonist, Mr. Julian Vance, a youth of sharp wit and colder philosophy, stood at the opposite side of the apparatus. He was young, his countenance smooth and unlined by the tempests of thought, his eyes bright with that arrogant confidence which ignorance mistakes for wisdom. He smiled—a smile of pity and triumph—as he adjusted the brass conductors. He did not believe in the vital fluid; to him, life was but a complex mechanism of springs and levers, a clockwork of the nerves, and he had come to wind down my delusions before the eyes of the world.

"Gentlemen," said the President of the Society, his voice booming through the hall, "the experiment is prepared. Dr. Alistair Vance shall attempt to isolate the vital principle from the vegetable specimen. Mr. Vance shall demonstrate the merely chemical reaction."

A hush fell upon the room—a silence so profound that I could hear the beating of my own heart, which seemed to say, *Fail! Fail!* I turned to the audience. There sat the philosophers, the poets, the nobility of England; and among them, I saw the face of my beloved Clara. Her eyes were fixed upon me, swimming in tears, her hands clasped as if in prayer. Oh, the torture of that moment! To love with a passion that consumes the universe, and to stand trembling before a crucible!

"Begin," said the President.

I took the glass tube, within which lay the germinating seed of the *Arum maculatum*, a plant chosen for its rapid growth and sensibility. My hand shook so violently that the liquid within the tube vibrated like a terrified soul. I had theorized that by passing a current of galvanic energy through the sap while submerged in a specific saline solution, the vital principle would be forced to separate, appearing as a phosphorescent glow at the negative pole.

I applied the wire. The acid hissed. A faint mist arose within the glass. The audience leaned forward; the collective breath of the room seemed to be sucked into the vessel. I looked for the light—the divine, ethereal spark that would vindicate me. I looked for the glow of life.

But there was nothing. Only the grey, ugly smoke of burnt carbon.

A murmur rippled through the crowd—a sound like the rustling of dry leaves before a winter storm. It was the sound of doubt, soon to become the roar of derision. My blood ran cold; a film gathered over my eyes. I felt as though I were drowning in the very fluid I sought to prove.

"Is this the spirit of life?" cried out Mr. Vance, his voice clear and cutting as a shard of ice. "It seems, gentlemen, to be nothing more than the spirit of soot. The Doctor has conjured a ghost that refuses to haunt us."

Laughter began to titter in the corners of the hall. It struck me like a physical blow—like daggers piercing my breast. I turned to him, my chest heaving with a mixture of rage and despair. "You mock what you cannot comprehend! The fluid is there, Julian! It is in the air, in the ether! It is only your coarse instruments that blind you to it!"

"Then show us, sir," he sneered, stepping forward and seizing the tube from my unresisting hand. "Show us this phantom. Or admit that the machinery of man is all that exists, and that your *Anima* is but the fever dream of a poet."

He raised the tube high. "Behold! The death of a theory!"

In that instant, a wild, uncontable fury seized me. Was I to be beaten by a stripling? Was my life’s devotion to be laughed away in a London lecture hall? No! I would not endure it. I would force the truth upon them. I would tear the secret from the heart of nature by storm.

With a cry that silenced the laughter, I lunged forward. "You shall see!" I shrieked. "If the fluid will not come gently, I will wrest it from the heavens!"

I abandoned the delicate apparatus. My eyes fell upon the great electrical machine in the corner, the massive cylinder of glass and the prime conductor, charged high for the evening’s entertainment. I seized the discharging rod. The air in the room seemed to thicken, to become heavy with ozone and terror. The gaslights flickered and dimmed, as if the shadows themselves recoiled from my intent.

"Alistair, beware!" cried Clara, rising from her seat, her voice a piercing lament. "In the name of mercy, stop!"

But I heard her not. I was no longer a man of science; I was a being of pure will, driven by a madness that felt like divinity. I thrust the rod toward the ceiling, toward the chandeliers, toward the gathering storm that I felt raging within the very atoms of the room.
