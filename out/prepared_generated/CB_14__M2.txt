The rain in Bruges does not fall so much as it hangs, a cold, grey curtain that blots out the sun and chills the marrow. It was a November afternoon, thick with the scent of damp wool and stagnant water, when I stood in the corridor of the Pensionnat Saint-Amand, feeling the weight of my own isolation settle upon my shoulders like a stone. I was the new English mistress, a position I had accepted out of necessity rather than desire, and already I felt the sharp, invisible teeth of the school’s hierarchy gnawing at my composure.

Enter Madame de Vries, the director, a woman whose face was a map of rigid propriety, and behind her, like a shadow cast by a shorter flame, came Elise. Elise was fifteen, with eyes of a pale, piercing blue that seemed to see through the fabric of my clothes to the trembling spirit beneath. She was clever, painfully so, and possessed of a mind that moved with the speed and deceit of a cat in a house of glass.

"You will take charge of Elise’s studies," Madame de Vries said, her voice dry as parchment. "She is difficult. She does not tolerate error, nor do I."

I looked at the girl. Elise did not look at me. She stood with her arms crossed, her chin tilted up, a posture of such practiced arrogance that it was almost a weapon.

"I am not a governess, Madame," I said, my voice low but steady. "I am a teacher. I will teach her. But I will not be her spy, nor her servant."

Madame de Vries’s eyes narrowed. "Watch your tongue, Miss Harker. In this house, obedience is the first virtue."

"Then I shall teach her the second," I replied, and turned to Elise. "Come. The library is warm."

Elise followed, her footsteps light and silent on the stone floor. I led her to the library, a room dominated by towering shelves of leather-bound volumes and the perpetual smell of dust and decay. A fire burned in the grate, but it did little to dispel the chill that seemed to emanate from Elise herself. I closed the door, the latch clicking with a finality that made her flinch.

"We shall begin with history," I said, taking a chair by the fire. "You know the subject well, I am told."

"Better than you," she said, without a trace of irony, settling into the opposite chair. "My mother teaches me every evening. She is very strict."

"Your mother is a woman of the world," I said, opening my book. "I am a woman of the mind. We shall see if they are not the same thing."

Elise smiled, a thin, cruel curve of the lips. "We shall see."

The days that followed were a war of wits, fought in the silent language of glances and the sharp exchange of words. Elise tested me constantly. She would drop a pen, a handkerchief, a letter, knowing I would pick it up. She would question my grammar, my pronunciation, my very right to stand in that room. And every evening, when we parted, I knew she would report my slightest lapse to Madame de Vries. I felt the net closing in around me, the threads invisible but tight, ready to strangle my independence.

One evening, the rain had turned to sleet, tapping against the windows like fingers demanding entry. We were studying the history of the Netherlands, and the air in the room was thick with tension. Elise had been particularly aggressive that day, probing for weaknesses, trying to provoke me into a display of temper that she could then use against me.

"You pronounce 'Ghent' incorrectly," she said, her voice sharp. "It is 'Gent'. You are a fool, Miss Harker."

I looked up from my book. The fire had died to embers, casting long, dancing shadows on the walls. I felt a surge of anger, hot and fierce, rising in my chest. I wanted to strike her, to throw the book at her head, to shout my defiance at the heavens. But I held it back, a dam breaking slowly, the water rising but not yet spilling.

"I am not a fool, Elise," I said, my voice quiet, but with a steel in it that surprised me. "And you are not a child to be coddled. You are a student, and you will respect your teacher, or you will leave this room."

She stared at me, her blue eyes wide. For a moment, I thought she would run, would scream, would summon her mother. But she did not. She sat back, her face pale, and for the first time, I saw a flicker of something else in her eyes. Fear? Respect? I could not tell.

"Why do you hate me?" she asked, her voice suddenly small.

"I do not hate you," I said, and I was surprised by the truth of it. "I hate the way you are used. I hate that you are taught to see the world as a battlefield, where every interaction is a test of power. I hate that you are clever, but your cleverness is a cage."

Elise looked down at her hands. "My mother says I must be strong. She says the world will break me if I am not hard."

"The world will not break you," I said, leaning forward. "But it will crush you if you do not have a heart to cushion the fall. You have a mind, Elise. It is a gift. But it is also a burden. You must learn to use it not to wound, but to understand. Not to control, but to create."

She looked up at me, and for a moment, the mask of arrogance slipped. I saw the girl beneath, the frightened, lonely girl who had been taught that love was conditional, that safety was found in deception.

"I do not know how," she whispered.
