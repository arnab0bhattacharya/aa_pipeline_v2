I have before me as I write the little volume in its grey paper wrapper, the edges of the pages uncut, the title—*Souvenirs d’un Révolutionnaire*—printed in that severe, unadorned type which the house of Baudry & Cie. affects for its more serious publications. It lies on the table in the lamplight, and beyond it the window gives upon the Rue des Chalets, where the snow has been falling since dusk, softly and without cessation, so that the gas-lamps show but a blurred and trembling glow, and the footsteps of the rare passer-by are muffled into a mere suggestion of sound. It is very quiet. The concierge has long since put out the lights in the vestibule, and the only noise that reaches me is the faint, persistent whisper of the snow against the panes—a sound that seems to me now, in my present humour, like the voice of all the things that are past, murmuring their incomprehensible reproaches.

I am to translate this book. That is the task which Monsieur Verhoeven, of the firm of Baudry & Cie., has confided to me—I, Alexis Feodorovitch Karolsky, aged twenty-six, a Russian of good family, a student of letters, a man of no particular distinction, employed at a modest salary to render into French the memoirs of one Piotr Ilitch Vaskov, sometime member of the Committee of Public Safety, exile, conspirator, and—if his own account is to be believed—a man who has suffered much for the cause of the people. It is a work of no great literary merit, but of a certain historical interest; the house believes in it, and Verhoeven, who is a sentimentalist in his way, spoke to me of it with a kind of unction, as of a sacred trust. “You will do it well, Karolsky,” he said, tapping me on the shoulder with his plump white hand. “You have the temperament—the sympathy. You understand these things.”

I understood. I took the book home with me, and I have read it. And now I sit here, with the snow falling outside and the lamp burning low, and I do not know whether I am to laugh or to weep, or whether, perhaps, the most sensible thing would be to take the manuscript, wrap it in a sheet of brown paper, and return it to-morrow morning to Verhoeven with a polite note of excuse. For on the forty-seventh page of this book—I have the figure by heart—there occurs a passage which concerns me more nearly than any other passage in the whole range of literature with which I am acquainted. It is a passage of some twenty lines, and it denounces, in terms of bitter and contemptuous loathing, a certain agent of the Third Section, a man by the name of Karolsky—Alexei Petrovitch Karolsky—who, in the year ’sixty-three, during the troubles in the western provinces, betrayed to the police a number of young students, and among them, it would appear, the brother of the author himself.

My father.

I never knew him. He died when I was a child of five—died, as I have always been told, of a fever contracted in the marshes of the Vistula, where he had gone to inspect certain estates which he managed for a wealthy Polish family. I have a vague memory of a tall man with a fair beard and kind eyes, who lifted me once to his shoulder and let me look at a bird’s nest in the fork of a birch-tree. That is all. My mother spoke of him always with a hushed and tender reverence; his portrait, a photograph taken in the early ’sixties, hangs in her little sitting-room in Kiev, and shows a young man of a serious and gentle countenance, with a high forehead and a look of somewhat melancholy candour. I have grown up with that face before my eyes; I have shaped my life, in some measure, upon the memory of a man whom I believed to have been honourable, just, and true. And now this Vaskov, this revolutionary of the old school, this man of blood and rhetoric, tells me—tells the world—that my father was a spy.

I have read the passage a hundred times. It is written, I must confess, with a certain power. Vaskov has the trick of the emphatic word, of the phrase that strikes home. He describes the arrest of his brother—the sudden irruption of the gendarmes into the students’ lodging in the dead of night, the confusion, the terror, the blows, the brutal jests of the police; and then, in a colder and more deliberate tone, he names the man who had sold them. “We knew him well,” he writes. “He was one of those whom we had trusted. He had sat at our table; he had drunk our tea and eaten our bread; he had spoken to us of liberty and of the people with the tears in his eyes. His name was Karolsky. He was a man of good family, of some education, and—God forgive him—of a certain charm. He is dead now, I am told. I do not know. I hope that he died in the misery he deserved.”

The misery he deserved. The words have a ring of savage satisfaction, of that peculiar and vindictive righteousness which the revolutionary—I have observed it often—carries into the most private recesses of his soul. And I sit here, in this quiet room in Geneva, with the snow falling and the lamp flickering, and I ask myself, as I have asked myself a thousand times in the last three days, whether it is true.

I do not know.

That is the whole of the matter. I do not know. I have no evidence, no proof, no means of arriving at the truth. My father is dead; his friends are scattered or dead; the archives of the Third Section, if they still exist, are inaccessible to me.
