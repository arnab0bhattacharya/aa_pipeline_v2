The morning had, he thought, a certain polish to it, as though the city itself had been up since dawn with a cloth and a tin of polish, buffing the brass plates of Pall Mall until they answered the sun with a smug, ministerial gleam. He walked with his hands behind his back—a habit, this, of forty years, begun at his father’s table and never broken—and the heels of his black boots struck the pavement with a regularity that pleased him, a measured, parliamentary sort of rhythm, like a gavel that never quite falls. The club had released him into the air with its usual discreet cough of port and leather and yesterday’s *Times*, and now the air itself, cool and faintly sulphurous, came at him in small, investigative gusts, lifting the corner of his coat, fingering the silk of his hat, and passing on about its business. There was a smell of wet stone and horse dung and, from somewhere, a suggestion of oranges, and he thought, without emphasis, that the morning was a good one, and that he would speak well today, and that the speech was, on the whole, a fine piece of work.

It lay in his breast pocket, folded twice, the typewritten pages warm against his ribs, and he could feel them there as one feels a hand upon the shoulder—a small, steady pressure of reassurance. He had read it over at breakfast, and again in the reading-room, and each time it had seemed to him more solid, more inevitable, like a wall that had been built by someone else and which he was merely invited to admire. *The right honourable gentleman opposite*—the phrase rose in him like a phrase of music, and he saw, in anticipation, the benches opposite, the faces, the nodding of the front bench, the slow, reluctant, then generous, then almost eager acquiescence of the House. He would begin quietly, of course. One must always begin quietly. There was a trick in it—he had learned it long ago from old Cardew, who was dead now, poor devil—of beginning so quietly that the House must lean forward to hear, and in leaning forward it committed itself, it gave itself into your hands, and then, when you had it thus, you might do what you liked with it. He smiled at the pavement. It was a good trick. It was, in its way, a kind of love-making.

And at this, as though a door had been opened in a corridor of his mind, there came the image of his wife—Clarissa, not the wife of his youth, but the wife of these later years, who met him at dinner with a small, bright, armour-plated gaiety and who had, he knew, her own life, her own thoughts, her own committee-meetings and her own small treacheries, none of them, he suspected, of any great consequence, but all of them hers. He saw her at the end of the table, her hands—still pretty, still white—arranging the flowers in the way she had, the way that meant she was thinking of something else. She would ask him tonight how it had gone, and he would say it had gone well, and she would say, *I knew it would*, in that voice which was like a door closing softly, and they would both be satisfied, and the evening would fold itself up like a letter and be put away. He did not blame her. He did not blame himself. It was simply that the thing had worn thin, like a carpet in a passage where many feet have passed, and one no longer looked at the pattern.

But the other—the other came to him now unbidden, as she always did when the morning was fine and the blood ran a little faster than the day required. Her name had been—no, he would not say her name, even to himself; he had trained himself not to say it, as one trains oneself not to look at a bright light. He saw only her hands upon the rail of a steamer, and the wind taking her hair, and the way she had laughed, not at him but at the whole absurd business of the world, a laugh that had in it something of the sea. He had been twenty-six. She had been—it did not matter what she had been. They had walked, one summer, in a garden where the roses were going over and the petals lay on the gravel like little discarded programmes, and she had said something—he could not remember what—and he had known, in that instant, that the whole of his life would be a compromise, and that he would make it, and that he would make it well, and that she would go away, and that he would let her. And she had gone. And he had stayed. And here he was, at fifty-four, walking down Pall Mall with a speech in his pocket and the sun on his boots, and it had all been—well, it had been what it had been. One could not, after all, live every life.

A motor-car passed, and the driver touched his cap, and he raised his own hat a fraction of an inch, the precise fraction that a man in his position owed a man in that. The gesture pleased him. It was the sort of thing that held the world together. And indeed, he thought, was not the whole of it—the House, the speech, the careful beginnings, the leaning forward, the acquiescence—was it not all a holding-together? One built, one maintained, one handed on. The wall was not for oneself. The wall was for the ones who came after, who would lean against it and never know whose hands had laid the bricks. And was that enough? He turned the question over as he might have turned over a stone on a beach, looking for the small creatures beneath, and found—nothing very much. A little vanity, perhaps. A little pleasure in the sound of one’s own voice. A little fear of the silence at the end of it.
