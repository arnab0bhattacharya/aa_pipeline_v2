The deck of the *Calyx* bucked beneath Elias Thorne’s boots, the tropical swell of the Pacific rolling heavy and green against the hull. He stood near the mizzenmast, his knuckles white around the ratline, watching the drama unfold at the forecastle head. It was the second dogwatch, the heat of the day finally broken by a wind that smelled of distant rain and crushed guava. They were three weeks out of the Admiralty Islands, running eastward, and the tension that had fermented in the lower decks for a month was finally bubbling over.

Captain Silas Vane stood on the raised quarterdeck, a silhouette against the bruising twilight. He was a tall man, skeletal and severe, his face permanently weathered into a mask of contempt. In his right hand, he held a length of rat-tail line, the ends frayed and dark. Below him, grouped by the foremast, stood six men. They were armed with marlinspikes and belaying pins, their faces drawn into a desperate, animal hostility.

"You think to challenge my authority?" Vane’s voice carried over the slap of the canvas, a cold, cutting tenor that held no fear. "Put down your irons, you wharf-born curs, and I will let only two of you taste the cat."

"We’ll see you in hell first, Vane!" a voice barked from the crowd. It was Rourke, the bo’sun, a man with a scarred jaw and a righteous fury that had been stoked by Vane’s cat-o'-nine-tails. "We’re done being bled for your amusement. Step down from the deck."

Elias, the second mate, watched from his station by the mizzen. His heart hammered a frantic rhythm against his ribs. He wore the officer’s uniform, the brass buttons of the merchant service gleaming dully in the fading light, but his soul was with the men below. For six months, he had endured Vane’s tyranny. He had stood by impassively as the captain ordered a young ordinary seaman keelhauled for a spilled bucket of tar—a sentence that had scraped the boy raw and left him feverish in the hammock nettings for a week. He had listened to Vane’s venomous tirades, swallowed the insults, and executed the brutal orders because the merchant code demanded obedience. But the code was a fraying rope, and it was about to snap.

Vane did not step down. Instead, he turned his head slightly, his gaze sweeping the deck until it locked onto Elias. "Mister Thorne!" Vane barked. "To the quarterdeck. Now."

Elias felt his stomach clench. The men at the foremast turned their eyes toward him, a mixture of pleading and warning in their gazes. Rourke’s face was a mask of grim anticipation. Elias pushed himself away from the mast and walked the length of the pitching deck, climbing the ladder to the quarterdeck. He stopped three paces from the captain, keeping his hand away from the cutlass at his hip.

"Captain," Elias said, keeping his voice flat.

"The men are in a state of mutiny, Thorne," Vane said, his voice dropping to a tone of chilling calm. He did not look at the mutineers; his eyes were fixed on the horizon, as if the rebellion were a minor squall to be weathered. "Go to my cabin. Fetch the flintlock from the rack. Bring it to me. We will shoot Rourke where he stands, and the rest will fall back into line."

Elias hesitated. The command hung in the humid air. "Sir, they are six. One pistol will not deter them. If we fire, they will rush the deck."

"I did not ask for your tactical appraisal, Mister Thorne," Vane said, the rat-tail line twitching in his hand. "I gave you an order. Fetch the weapon."

Below, the men began to move, a slow, creeping advance toward the mainmast. Rourke held his marlinspike low, his eyes darting between the captain and the second mate. The wind shifted, snapping the spanker canvas above their heads.

Elias looked at Vane. He saw the rigid, unyielding posture of a man who believed himself a god upon his own wooden world. He thought of the boy in the sickbay, the weals on his back, the salt rubbed into the wounds on Vane’s explicit orders. He thought of the rancid pork, the brackish water, the constant, grinding cruelty that had turned a capable crew into a pack of cornered wolves. Elias despised Vane with a quiet, corrosive hatred that had eaten away at his sleep and his conscience. He had dreamed of the captain’s downfall, prayed for a squall to sweep the quarterdeck clean of his presence.

But the sea had its own laws, and the Articles of Agreement were the highest among them. A mutiny was a rupture in the fabric of the world. It was the act of casting off the moorings of civilization, an admission that men were no better than the beasts of the deep. If Elias stood by Vane, he was defending a monster. If he joined the men, he was sanctioning anarchy. The merchant code demanded he uphold the captain’s authority, regardless of the captain’s character. But the code also demanded he protect the ship and her crew. Vane’s madness was a threat to the *Calyx*.

"Captain Vane," Elias said, his voice surprisingly steady. "I cannot fetch the pistol."

Vane finally turned, his dark eyes narrowing into slits. "What did you say?"

"I will not arm you, sir." Elias kept his hands clasped behind his back, his posture rigid. "If you fire into that crowd, you will doom this ship. We are weeks from any port. There is no navy vessel to haul them off in irons. We will be left with a dead bo’sun and a crew that will burn the *Calyx* to the waterline just to see you hang."

Vane took a step forward, the smell of stale rum and tobacco rolling off him. "You defy me? You, a half-rate second mate? Do you think because you share a mess with these swine that you are one of them?" He raised the rat-tail line. "I will have you flogged for insubordination, Thorne. I will have your back laid open to the bone."
