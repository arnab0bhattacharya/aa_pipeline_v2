The rain had been falling on Soho since midnight, turning the refuse of Berwick Street into a thin, malodorous soup that crept into the seams of Inspector Alistair Mercer's boots. He stood in the recessed doorway of a closed French laundry, watching the yellow light spilling from the cellar windows of Number 14, Greek Court. The windows vibrated with the guttural cadence of Russian argument, the sharp, defensive clatter of Yiddish, and the occasional, almost operatic despair of a Parisian tenor who called himself Comte d'Artois but was, in reality, an escaped forger from Lyon. They were the usual wreckage of Europe—idealists, bomb-makers, pamphleteers, and the merely desperate—washed up into the murk of London's rookeries. For seven years, Mercer had waded into this murk, cultivating them not with the blunt instruments of the constabulary, but with the patient, delicate art of the angler. He knew their debts, their mistresses, their quarrels, and their terrifying, half-baked plots. He knew them because they told him, whispering across tables in smoky coffee houses, believing him to be a sympathetic ear, a minor official in the Home Office who could arrange clemency or expedite a naturalization paper. It was a fragile, invisible net he had spun, and it had kept the peace far better than any truncheon.

But the peace, Mercer reflected bitterly, was no longer sufficient. The peace did not sell newspapers.

He turned up the collar of his mackintosh and walked away from the light, his steps heavy with the dread of the morning's meeting. The new Assistant Commissioner, Sir Reginald Vance, had arrived at Scotland Yard like a warship dropping anchor, bringing with him the stench of the press barons and the frantic energy of a man obsessed with public image. Vance had no use for invisible nets. He desired a spectacle.

Sir Reginald’s office on the fourth floor of the new Norman Shaw building smelled of beeswax, pipe tobacco, and the metallic tang of ambition. The morning light, pale and watery, filtered through the tall, arched windows, catching the dust motes that danced above the Assistant Commissioner's desk. Vance stood by the window, his silhouette rigid, staring out at the murky sweep of the Thames.

"The press, Mercer," Vance said, not turning around. "They are clamoring for blood. They want the anarchists. They want the foreign menace. They want to see the Crown's authority asserted in the heart of the Empire's capital."

Mercer stood at attention, his hat clutched before him. "We have them contained, Sir Reginald. The network is monitored. Any genuine threat is neutralized before it can mature."

"Contained is not arrested, Inspector." Vance turned. His face was smooth, pink, and utterly devoid of the lines that came from walking Soho alleys at three in the morning. "I do not want containment. I want a cell at Newgate. I want a trial at the Old Bailey. I want a face on the front page of the *Illustrated Police News*. The public is terrified. The *Times* editorialized yesterday about the continental contagion seeping through our ports. We must demonstrate that Scotland Yard is not asleep."

"Asleep, sir? We have deported three bombers this year alone."

"Quietly, Mercer. You deported them quietly. There was no drama. There was no triumph. I need a name, Inspector. Someone prominent enough to satisfy the editors, someone whose arrest will signal to these alien malcontents that their sanctuary is at an end."

Mercer felt a cold stone settle in his stomach. "If I arrest the prominent ones, sir, the network will collapse. The lesser men will go to ground, and we will be blind. The men we truly fear are not the ones who give speeches. They are the quiet ones who mix the chlorate in the back rooms."

Vance waved a dismissive hand. "Metaphysics, Mercer. I deal in realities. Give me a name by Friday, or I will find someone who will."

Mercer walked back out into the rain, the stone in his stomach now a block of ice. He knew the name Vance wanted. There was only one man who fit the criteria—a man prominent enough to please the papers, yet vulnerable enough to arrest without triggering an immediate bombing campaign. That man was Elias Thorne.

Thorne was not his real name. He had been born Ilya Tchernov in Vilnius, but the name had been burned away by the pogroms and the Siberian cold. In London, he was the intellectual center of the Eastern European émigrés, a man with a ragged beard, a voice like a broken cello, and a relentless, burning hatred for the Tsar and, by extension, all authority. He edited a radical broadsheet from a room above a bootmaker's in Frith Street. He was the perfect figurehead for the newspapers: foreign, eloquent, and dangerous in theory.

But Thorne was also Mercer's master informer.

It was Thorne who had tipped Mercer off about the assassination attempt on the visiting Grand Duke the previous spring. It was Thorne who had identified the French cell importing dynamite from Belgium. Thorne did it not for money—he refused all coin—but out of a ruthless, calculating pragmatism. He believed that the random terror of the bombers discredited the true revolution, alienating the British working class whose support he deemed essential. He fed the fanatics to Mercer to protect his own, more subtle, long-term vision. He was, in his way, more of a strategist than a revolutionary. And in doing so, he had kept Mercer alive, and London safe from the sort of carnage that had ripped through Paris and Chicago.

Mercer walked to Frith Street, climbing the dark, narrow stairs to Thorne's room. He entered without knocking, as he always did. Thorne sat at a table littered with typefaces and ink-stained proofs, a solitary candle casting a halo in the gloom. He looked up, his dark eyes unreadable.

"You have the look of a man bringing a bad debt, Inspector," Thorne said, his accent thick but precise.
