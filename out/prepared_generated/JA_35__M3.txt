The carriage which conveyed Miss Mary and Miss Agnes Wilmot from their modest loddings in Blandford Square to the magnificent portals of their cousin’s residence in Berkeley Square, might have been considered by many as a privilege; but to Mary, the younger of the two, it was merely a vehicle of humiliation. She sat erect, her features composed into an expression of rigid resentment, surveying the silken linings with a disdain which the knowledge of their borrowed nature only served to heighten.

Mrs. Bracknell, their cousin, had been a Miss Wilmot, with a fortune of two thousand pounds, and a countenance which, if it did not forcibly command admiration, could at least quiet criticism. She had married Sir Reginald Bracknell, a man of considerable estate and very little intellect, and had immediately assumed the consequence of her station with an exactness that left her poor relations in no doubt of their diminished standing.

"Upon my word, Agnes," said Mary, as the carriage turned into the square, "I almost wish we had walked. The exertion would have been far less painful than this parade of borrowed grandeur. I am sure the very servants know we are not accustomed to such trappings."

Agnes, who was three years her sister’s senior, and possessed of a temper more inclined to observation than to resentment, made no immediate reply. She was reflecting upon the nature of Sir Reginald’s carriage, which, though eminently handsome, was also eminently uncomfortable—a circumstance which led her to suspect that Sir Reginald’s notions of luxury were less refined than his means.

"I dare say the servants know a great many things," said Agnes mildly, "but I am equally sure they do not think of us at all. We must not mistake our own consequence, Mary. If we are to be uncomfortable, let us at least be uncomfortable upon our own account, and not borrow uneasiness from the imaginings of footmen."

Mary was not satisfied, but she was silenced; and the carriage stopping at that moment, they were ushered into the hall, and presently into the drawing-room, where Lady Bracknell—she had insisted upon the title since her husband’s baronetcy—received them with an air of gracious condescension.

"My dear cousins! How very kind of you to come. I told Sir Reginald only this morning that I must have my relations about me, for I am quite desolate without them. But you look fatigued, Mary. The carriage, I suppose? Sir Reginald will have it that his carriage is easy, but I always say that ease is a matter of opinion. Do sit down, both of you, and take some tea. Mrs. Hodges, some tea directly, if you please."

The request for tea, delivered with an emphasis which implied that the beverage was a luxury not usually bestowed upon casual visitors, was characteristic of Lady Bracknell’s hospitality. It was not that she withheld anything which propriety demanded, but that she bestowed it with an air of conferring a favour, which rendered the recipient uncomfortable, and the giver insufferable.

Mary accepted her tea in silence, and Agnes, with a civil expression of thanks, endeavoured to engage her cousin in conversation. But Lady Bracknell was not a woman who conversed; she pronounced. She delivered her opinions upon the fashions, upon the weather, upon the shocking price of muslin, and upon the inferiority of everything she possessed, in a manner which precluded reply, and demanded only assent.

"I am sure I do not know what we shall do with you both," she continued, surveying them with a complacency which sat oddly upon her careworn features. "The house is so full, what with Sir Reginald’s mother, and my sister Grenville’s children, and I really think we shall be packed like sardines. But family, you know, must be attended to. I always say that if one cannot be comfortable, one may at least be respectable."

Agnes looked at her cousin with an attention which Mary, absorbed in her own grievances, did not bestow. She observed that Lady Bracknell’s eyes, though they surveyed her visitors with complacency, had a restlessness in them which was not the restlessness of satisfaction. Her colour was high, but unsteady; her gestures were quick, but not easy; and when she spoke of the house being full, there was a slight compression of the lips which seemed to say that the fullness was not altogether agreeable to herself.

"I am sure," said Agnes, "we would not for the world be troublesome. If our visit is inconvenient—"

"Inconvenient! My dear Agnes, what an idea. I never heard anything so absurd. Sir Reginald would be quite hurt. He is all attention, you know—quite painfully attentive. I sometimes wish he were less so, for I am not used to being so much considered. But there, I must not complain. I am sure I am very fortunate—very fortunate indeed."

She repeated this assurance with a frequency which Agnes found significant, and a vehemence which Mary, had she been less occupied with her own wrongs, might have found surprising.

Sir Reginald entered soon after, a man of large fortune and small understanding, who was distinguished by an extreme solicitude regarding his wife’s health, and an utter indifference to everything else. He greeted the Miss Wilmots with a civility which was meant to be kind, and which, being unaccompanied by any real knowledge of their characters or concerns, was merely cold.

"You find Lady Bracknell pretty well, I hope?" said he, addressing Agnes, but looking at his wife. "She is never so well as I could wish. I am constantly telling her she does too much. The house, the servants, the children—I am sure I do not know how she manages it. But she will not be persuaded to rest. She is all anxiety, you know, all exertion. I tell her it is quite needless, that everything goes on well enough without her, but she will not believe me."
