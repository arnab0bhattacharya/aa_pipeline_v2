The rooms of Mr. Adolphus Vane, in a quiet Chelsea square where the plane-trees shed their leaves with a certain studied elegance upon the pavement, were furnished less for living than for being looked at in. There were tall screens of stamped Spanish leather, and a piano that seemed to dream beneath its draperies of old gold; there were Japanese fans arranged in patterns on the wall like the wings of arrested butterflies, and a bust of Schubert, in whose marble eyes one detected, or fancied one detected, a gentle melancholy at the thought of all the ballads that had been written since his day. It was in these rooms that Mr. Vane had composed, over a period of some twelve years, those songs which had made him the darling of the suburban drawing-room—‘The Rose That Waits,’ ‘When April Weeps,’ ‘My Lady’s Garden,’ and that exquisite thing, ‘The Lute of Love,’ which was sung, as the phrase goes, by every young lady who could reach the high note and every young gentleman who could not. He had been called, by a critic who had a fondness for the phrase, the nightingale of the semi-detached. His royalties had been prodigious. His fame was of that peculiar kind which is measured not by the admiration of the few but by the tears of the many.

It was therefore with a sensation that ran through musical London like the first tremor of an earthquake that the announcement appeared in the columns of a weekly review: Mr. Adolphus Vane had abandoned the ballad. He would write, he declared, only serious music. He was at work upon a symphony.

The publisher, a Mr. Cuthbert Hogg, of the firm of Hogg and Whistlecroft, arrived at the Chelsea rooms within the hour. He was a stout, florid man, with a habit of opening his eyes very wide, as though the world were perpetually proposing to him a proposition of dubious morality.

‘My dear Vane,’ he said, sinking into the deepest of the old gold chairs, ‘this is a catastrophe. This is ruin. Do you know what you have done? You have killed the goose. You have not merely killed it—you have wrung its neck, plucked it, and served it up with a garnish. The public, Vane, the public!’

‘The public,’ said Mr. Vane, who was standing by the window with his hands behind his back, a slender, pale young man with the eyes of a dreamer and the mouth of a spoiled child, ‘the public has had of me twelve years of sugar. I propose now to give it salt. It may find the change salutary. It is a mistake to suppose that people know what they want; they want only what they know. I shall teach them to want something else.’

‘They will not be taught,’ said Mr. Hogg, with feeling. ‘They will simply go away. They will go to Mr. Alfred Pim, who writes “The Cowslip’s Lament,” and they will buy it in thousands. And you will write your symphony, and the critics will say it is very interesting, and nobody will play it, and in five years you will be dead, Vane, dead, and your symphony with you, and “The Lute of Love” will be sung by the daughters of the men who ruined you. That is the way of the world.’

‘It is a very pretty way,’ said Mr. Vane, ‘and I have no doubt it is the way of the world. But I am not of the world. I am of the few. The few are always the future, even when they are in the minority. I shall write my symphony, and it will be beautiful, and if the public does not like it, the public may console itself with Mr. Pim.’

Mr. Hogg departed, and the word went forth. The admirers of Mr. Vane, who had loved him for his melodies, found themselves confronted with a composer who had ceased to be charming, and they deserted him with the promptitude of a fashionable congregation deserting a preacher who has begun to talk of the soul. The young ladies who had wept over ‘When April Weeps’ now wept over the perfidy of Mr. Vane, and transferred their affections to Mr. Pim, whose ‘Cowslip’s Lament’ was, they said, so true. The teacups of Kensington and the pianos of Bayswater knew him no more. His name, which had been on every lip, was now on none. He was, in the phrase of the hour, a thing of the past.

And it was precisely at this moment that the one man in London who had always despised his ballads, and had said so in print with a violence that had made him almost famous, presented himself at the door of the Chelsea rooms. His name was Mr. Sebastian Quill, and he was the musical critic of the *Athenaeum*, a man of a lean, sardonic appearance, with a nose like a hawk’s and the air of one who has discovered, after many years, that the world is even more foolish than he had supposed, and has decided to be amused by it rather than angry.

‘I have come,’ said Mr. Quill, taking the chair which Mr. Hogg had so lately vacated, ‘to offer you my congratulations. I am told that you have renounced the ballad. It is the first sensible thing I have ever heard of you.’

‘You are very kind,’ said Mr. Vane, a little coldly.

‘Not at all,’ said the critic. ‘I have abused you for twelve years, and I have enjoyed it. It is a great pleasure to abuse a man who deserves it. But I have never been able to abuse you for your ballads without a certain sense of guilt, because, you see, I knew that you were doing them too well. That is the tragedy of the popular artist. He is not a bad artist; he is an artist who has been found out by the wrong people.
