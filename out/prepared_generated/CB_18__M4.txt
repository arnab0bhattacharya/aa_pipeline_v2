The pensionnat des Dames Béthune stood in a narrow street of the old town, where the sun came late and left early, and where the sound of the bells from the cathedral of Saint-Vaast fell through the fog with a dull and muffled persistence, like the beating of a heart beneath heavy garments. It was a tall, grey house, narrow of front but deep of courtyard, and within its walls the daughters of English merchants and French lawyers, of Belgian bankers and German pastors, were instructed in French, in music, in the deportment becoming to young ladies of condition. The rooms were high and cold; the windows, long and many-paned, looked upon a paved court where a single lime-tree grew, stunted and patient, and where the rain dripped from the eaves with a monotony that might have driven a less disciplined spirit to madness.

It was here that Miss Agnes Whitmore, a woman of some forty years, plain of dress and plain of speech, had taught English composition and history for the better part of a decade. She was not beloved of her pupils, nor did she desire to be; she was respected, feared a little, and consulted in secret by those whose troubles were too heavy for the frivolous confidences of the dormitory. Her room was at the top of the house, a small chamber with a sloping ceiling and a window that commanded the street; and here she sat of an evening, correcting exercises by the light of a single candle, while the wind rattled the casement and the rain beat against the glass, and the bells of Saint-Vaast tolled the hours with a solemnity that seemed to her not un congenial.

Among her pupils was a girl named Clara Ashford, the daughter of a Yorkshire manufacturer, sent abroad at sixteen to acquire the polish which her father’s wealth could not purchase at home. She was handsome, high-spirited, and generous; her laugh was the gayest in the refectory, her temper the quickest to flame and the quickest to forgive. Miss Whitmore had watched her with an interest that was almost maternal, and had seen, with a sinking of the heart, the gradual change which had come over her during the past six months. The girl had grown abstracted; her exercises, once careless but vigorous, were now scrawled with a feverish haste and blotted with tears; she was for ever at the window, watching the street; and when the bell rang for visitors, she would start and colour and run to the grating of the parlour with a haste that betrayed too plainly the nature of her malady.

The malady had a name, and the name was the Comte de Vaurien. He was a young man of excellent family and no fortune, with a face of the most engaging candour, a manner of the most insinuating deference, and a tongue that could turn the most commonplace observation into a compliment of the most delicate flattery. He had been presented to Madame Béthune by a cousin of the Bishop’s; he had offered to superintend the young ladies’ practice in French conversation; and he had found in Clara Ashford a pupil so apt, so enthusiastic, and so charmingly ignorant of the world, that he had speedily conceived for her an attachment which he was pleased to call the most disinterested passion, and which Miss Whitmore, watching from her high window, recognised for what it was—the calculated siege of a man who had run through his patrimony, and who saw in the Yorkshire heiress a means of repairing his fortunes.

She had spoken to Clara once, and once only, and that was on a Sunday afternoon in the garden, when the lime-tree was in leaf and the bells were ringing for vespers. She had spoken with all the plainness which her nature and her conscience dictated; she had said that the Comte de Vaurien was not a man to be trusted; that his debts were notorious in the town; that his attentions to a girl of Clara’s age and inexperience were an insult rather than an honour; and she had begged her, if she had any regard for her own happiness or her father’s peace of mind, to receive him no more.

Clara had listened with a flushed cheek and a flashing eye; and when Miss Whitmore had finished, she had answered with a vehemence that was half tears and half defiance.

“You are cruel, Miss Whitmore, and you are unjust. You have never spoken to him; you know nothing of him but what the idle gossip of the town has told you. He is poor, I know—he has told me so himself—he has told me everything. He is proud, and he is unfortunate; but he is honourable, and he loves me. And I love him. And if my father were here, he would not speak to me as you have spoken.”

“Your father is not here,” said Miss Whitmore, “and that is the very reason why I must speak. You are in my charge, Clara; and I tell you plainly that this man is a fortune-hunter, and that his affection is a lie.”

“It is you who lie!” cried Clara, with a passion that made the older woman start. “It is you who are cold and hard and envious! You have never loved anyone; you have never been loved; and you cannot bear that I should be happy!”

She had turned and fled into the house, and since that day she had not spoken to Miss Whitmore except in the formal intercourse of the classroom; but her eyes, when they met hers, were full of a wounded and implacable resentment.
