The rain came sideways off the moors, rattling against the diamond-paned glass of the schoolroom like a handful of cast gravel. Clara Nettles stood at the window, her hands clasped behind her back, watching the grey slate roofs of Kettlebridge blur under the downpour. Behind her, eight children of varying ages sat at three rough-hewn oak tables, their slates scratching a ragged rhythm as they worked through their ciphering. The room smelled of damp wool, tallow candles, and the faint, sharp tang of the chalk dust that perpetually lodged beneath Clara’s fingernails. It was a small empire, carved out of nothing but seventy-two pounds of savings and a stubborn refusal to be ordinary.

She had spent four years in a York household, drumming French and basic geography into the heads of two uninterested merchant’s daughters. The wages had been meagre, but Clara had lived like a ascetic, banking every shilling above the cost of her room and board. When she returned to Kettlebridge, the town of her birth, she had leased the upper floor of the old cooper’s shop on Bridge Street, dragged up the discarded slates and benches from a bankrupt charity school, and hung a modest wooden sign outside her window: *Clara Nettles’s Day School. Instruction in Reading, Writing, Arithmetic, and Needlework. Terms: Fourpence per Week.* Within a fortnight, she had eight pupils. Within a month, she had twelve. It was enough to live on. It was, she had begun to believe, a foundation.

Now, the scratching of the slates seemed to falter, as if the children themselves sensed her distraction. Clara turned from the window. Her gaze swept over the rows of heads—some capped, some bare, all bowed over their work. She felt the familiar, fierce protectiveness that surprised her with its intensity. These were the children of weavers and carters, of the butcher and the apothecary. Children whose parents could not afford the guinea a term demanded by the grammar school, but who wanted better for their sons and daughters than a life of illiterate labour. She had given them a place where they could learn to be more.

But the sign across the street had changed everything. Three weeks ago, workmen had appeared in the empty stone building opposite her window. They painted the door a glossy black, hung green velvet curtains, and installed a cast-iron stove that puffed cheerful smoke from its chimney even on mild days. Then the sign went up, rendered in gold leaf: *St. Bartholomew’s Academy for the Instruction of Youth. Under the Patronage of the Reverend Edmund Hale, Rector of Kettlebridge.* The rector’s own carriage had delivered a woman named Mrs. Phyllis Grainger to the door on the first day. She wore a grey silk dress and a bonnet trimmed with lilac ribbons, and she carried herself as though the building had been built expressly for her use.

Clara watched the children trickle across the cobbles to St. Bartholomew’s each morning, their parents clutching them by the hand. Mrs. Grainger charged sixpence a week—twice Clara’s rate—but she offered a superior list of subjects. Her advertisements, printed on fine paper and posted at the market cross and the mill gates, promised instruction in globes, moral philosophy, and the pianoforte. The rector’s endorsement carried weight. He was a man of influence in Kettlebridge, his word shaping decisions from the parish council to the magistrate’s bench. When he stood at the pulpit and spoke of the vital importance of proper Christian education, his congregation listened. And when his wife visited the families of Clara’s pupils over tea and seed cake, gently praising the virtues of the new academy, the families listened to that, too.

Clara’s numbers had thinned. The eight children present today were the remnant. Three more had given notice at the end of last week, their mothers unable to meet her eyes as they explained. The weaver’s wife, Mrs. Thistle, had been the most honest. “It ain’t the money, Miss Nettles,” she had said, standing in the doorway with her shawl pulled tight. “It’s the rector. He spoke to my husband at the Sheep and Shears. Said it would be noticed, like, if we kept our boy with you. Said St. Bartholomew’s had his blessing. My Tom didn’t want trouble with the parish.”

Clara closed her eyes. She could feel the mathematics pressing on her like a physical weight. Her lease had another eight months to run, at three shillings a week. The slates and benches were paid for, but the coals for the fire and the candles for the dark winter afternoons were not. Her savings, that hard-won hoard, had been reduced to a little over eleven pounds. At this rate, she could last until spring. After that, the arithmetic became impossible. She could raise her fees, but the moment she did, she would lose the remaining children to Mrs. Grainger’s silk and pianoforte. She could lower them, but she would still lose them, because the competition was never really about money. It was about power, and she did not have any.

The option of begging was the most humiliating to contemplate, and the most likely to be necessary. She imagined herself standing in the hall at Hale Court, the rector’s residence, waiting while a servant announced her. She could see his face—long, pale, self-satisfied beneath his powdered wig. He would listen to her appeal with that particular expression of benevolent sorrow he wore when delivering a sermon on the fallen state of man. He would explain that St. Bartholomew’s was a charitable venture, designed to bring the highest standard of education to the children of Kettlebridge. He would assure her that there was no personal slight intended. And then he would do nothing, because her little day school was an irrelevance to him, a cottage industry challenging a manufactory.

The fire in the grate was burning low. Clara crossed the room and knelt, feeding a few pieces of coal from the scuttle. The children had gone quiet, watching her. She stood and smoothed her apron. “Thomas,” she said, “recite the rule for finding the product of seven and eight.”
