The vestry of St. Michael’s, perched as it was upon the wild crown of Thornfell, was a room that seemed built to withstand the wrath of the Almighty rather than to minister to His flock. Its walls were of stone, a yard thick, and the wind, coming off the moors with a sound like a rising sea, rattled the leaded panes of the single window. Within, a peat fire sulked in the grate, its heat barely denting the chill that clung to the flagstones and the heavy oak table. Here, the Reverend Mr. Crofton stood, a man whose very form seemed an argument for order and submission. He was tall and rigid, his black coat a stark monument to his calling, his face a landscape of severe planes and righteous creases.

Before him stood Silas Blackwood, the weaver. He was a man made of different stuff—lean and sinewy, with the fine white dust of the mill in his dark hair and the patient, burning intensity of one who has wrestled with God in the night-watches. His hands, large and calloused, were clasped before him, not in deference, but in the posture of a man holding fast to an unshakeable truth.

“The sermon was well-received, I think,” Mr. Crofton began, his voice smooth and grave, a polished stone. “It is a comfort to the afflicted to know their station is appointed by Providence. To bear their cross with meekness is the surest path to heavenly reward.”

Silas’s voice, when it came, was rough as homespun cloth. “It is a comfort to the oppressor, Reverend, to hear the oppressed told to love their chains. You spoke of obedience. You spoke of the servant obeying his master. But you did not speak of the master’s duty to his servant.”

A flush of indignation, hot and sudden, rose to the Reverend’s cheeks. “My duty is to the souls of this parish, and their duty is to their God and their King. The poor you have always with you, saith the Scripture. It is their part to endure.”

“And it is the rich man’s part to sell all he hath, and give to the poor, saith the same Scripture,” Silas retorted, his gaze unwavering. “You quote the text that suits your purpose, Mr. Crofton, and leave the harder part to gather dust. You tell the children in the mill to render unto Caesar what is Caesar’s, but you do not remind the mill-owner that the labourer is worthy of his hire. The children’s backs are bent and their lungs are choked with cotton-dust for a pittance, while the master’s house is full of fatness. Is that the order appointed by Providence?”

The Reverend Crofton drew himself up to his full height. He felt a tremor of something like fear, not of the man, but of the terrible, direct force of his argument, an argument that stripped the varnish from the world and showed the raw wood beneath. “You are a weaver, Silas Blackwood, not a theologian. You twist the sacred word to foment discontent and rebellion. The poor must be taught their place, lest they rise up and bring ruin upon themselves and all of society.”

“And I say the poor are rising up in their spirits already, Reverend,” Silas said, his voice dropping to a fierce whisper. “They are reading. They are thinking. They are finding the words in the Book that speak of justice, not just of patience. And those words are like a fire in their bones.”

In the adjoining room, where the parish vestments were kept, a figure stood motionless behind the thin partition. Eleanor Crofton, the Rector’s daughter, had come for her father’s muffler against the biting wind. Now she stood frozen, her hand on the cold wool, her heart beating with a strange and painful rhythm. She had always seen the world as her father painted it: a great, unassailable hierarchy, with God at its apex and the poor at its base, their suffering a kind of holy merit. But Silas’s words were like stones thrown into the still, clear pond of her mind, and the ripples were spreading, disturbing every reflection.

Silas left the vestry with a curt nod, his argument, like a seed of thistle, left to take root where it might. The Reverend Crofton stood for a long moment, his hands gripping the edge of the table. He felt assaulted, diminished. That a common weaver, a man who worked with his hands, should dare to lecture him on the Gospels was an inversion of all natural law. When Eleanor emerged, pale and quiet, he barely noticed her.

That night, the wind howled around the rectory as if in concert with the tempest in her soul. Sleep would not come. Silas Blackwood’s words echoed in her head. *They are reading. They are thinking.* She rose, and lighting a small tallow candle, she crept down to the study. Her father’s library was a fortress of approved thought: sermons, commentaries, histories of the Church. But she knew, from a half-heard whisper in the village, where the weaver got his books. She knew of a certain Mr. Finch, the bookseller in the mill-valley town of Cragfoot, who kept a back room for such men.

Two days later, under the pretext of buying embroidery silks, Eleanor took the steep, winding path down the fell. Cragfoot was a place of smoke and noise, a stark contrast to the quiet of Thornfell. The bookshop was cramped and smelled of paper and leather. Mr. Finch, a small man with sharp, knowing eyes, did not ask what she wanted; he seemed to understand. He brought her not a novel or a book of poetry, but a slim, unbound volume of political tracts, and beside it, a worn copy of John Milton’s *Areopagitica*.
