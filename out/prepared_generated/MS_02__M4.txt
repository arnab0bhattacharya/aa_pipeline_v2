I had known, from the earliest hour in which my understanding could grasp the import of the words, that I was destined for the healing art; and no childish ambition of rank or fortune mingled with the desire, but a profound and restless pity for the suffering of my kind, which the sight of my sister’s slow and agonizing decay had first awakened in my breast. Agnes was the gentlest of created beings, and the beauty of her spirit seemed to consume the feeble tenement of her body, as the flame devours the taper that feeds it. For seventeen months I watched her, through the long watches of the night and the desolate watches of the day, while the fever that had fastened upon her drew its slow and invisible sustenance from her veins, and mocked, with the subtlety of its approaches, every remedy that the science of that age could furnish. The physicians of our town—grave men, learned in the traditions of their calling—stood about her bed like the priests of a mystery they could not penetrate; they bled her, and blistered her, and administered their bitter draughts, and discoursed learnedly of humours and obstructions, while the fever held its silent and triumphant course; and at length, when the last faint flush had faded from her cheek, and the last whisper of her voice had died away, they departed with many expressions of sympathy, and left me alone with the awful secret of her death.

From that hour I was a changed being. I quitted the home of my fathers, and repaired to the university of Saint Cuthbert’s, in the good town of Edinburgh, where the fame of its medical school had drawn students from every quarter of the civilised world. There I applied myself with an ardour that knew no remission to the study of the living frame; I dissected, I analysed, I questioned nature at her secret springs; and I rose at length to a place of some distinction among my fellows, and was admitted, before the usual term, to the privileges of the hospital. Yet in the midst of all my labours, one thought possessed me wholly—the thought of that wasting fever which had robbed me of my sister, and which I was resolved, if human diligence and human courage could avail, to drag from its hiding-place and to destroy. I had observed, in the course of my attendance upon the sick, that the disorder was not communicable from one patient to another; that it seemed rather to arise within the body itself, as a consequence of certain conditions of the blood and of the nervous power, which I had vainly endeavoured to define. And I had formed a theory—a theory which I will not here set down, lest I should seem to boast of a wisdom I had not yet proved—that the malady was the result of a peculiar and morbid state of the vital fluid, which might be detected, and perhaps corrected, by a method of treatment which no physician had yet dared to employ.

But how was this theory to be brought to the test? I could not experiment upon the dying, for they were beyond the reach of hope; I could not experiment upon the healthy, for I had no right to endanger the life of another. There remained but one course, and I embraced it with a resolution that I mistook for heroism, though I have since learned to call it by a sterner name. I resolved to generate within my own frame the conditions which I believed to be the precursors of the fever, and to watch, with the eye of a discoverer, the progress of the disease I had myself invoked.

I need not recount the particulars of my experiment. It is enough to say that I subjected myself to a regimen which I had devised from long and painful observation, and that, at the end of some weeks, I had the awful satisfaction of perceiving that my theory was justified—that the symptoms of the fever were beginning to manifest themselves in my own person. I concealed my condition from every eye. I had taken a small lodging in a retired street, near the college, where I pursued my studies and my experiment together; and I went forth daily among my fellow-men with a countenance of cheerfulness, while I carried within me the seeds of a mortal distemper.

It was not long before the change in my appearance became too obvious to escape the observation of my friends. But of all who marked it, there was one whose eye was never averted from me, and whose solicitude I could neither elude nor deceive. Eleanor—for so I will name her, though the name is not that which she bore—had been my companion from childhood; our fathers had been friends, and it had been the fond wish of both that we should be united; and I had loved her with a tenderness which I had believed to be the deepest of which my nature was capable, until I found that even love itself could be made subordinate to the passion of discovery. She was now in Edinburgh, residing with her aunt, and she had watched my altered looks with a growing terror which she could not conceal.

One evening—it was in the bleak month of November, and the wind howled dismally without—she came to my lodging, having prevailed upon her aunt to accompany her, and to await her in the carriage at the door. I received her with a forced composure, but the sight of her pale and anxious countenance smote me to the heart. She stood for a moment gazing upon me in silence, and then, with a burst of tears which she could no longer restrain, she threw herself upon my bosom, and implored me, in the most passionate and affecting terms, to tell her what it was that thus consumed me.
