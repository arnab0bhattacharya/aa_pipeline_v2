The heat of San Esteban clings to the harbor like a damp cloth laid over the face of a dying man. I had lived in its grip for twenty years, and in that time the place had worked itself into my very marrow—a sluggish, feverish oppression that seemed to rob the days of their distinctness and the nights of their rest. I was a man of figures, of manifests and duties, a petty official of the customs house, stationed at the end of a rotting wooden quay where the river water lapped with a murky, sepulchral patience against the green-slimed piles. I had been honest there, and I had been poor. The two conditions seemed to me, in those days, to be intrinsically united, a sort of moral necessity of the tropics, where everything else decayed so rapidly that only a man’s incorruptibility remained to mark his grave.

It was in the approach of a dawn that the thing came to me, as if the darkness itself had conspired to put the temptation into my path. The air was thick, oppressive, and a faint, sickly luminescence clung to the oily surface of the water. I had been sitting in my little office, a box of worm-eaten planks that smelled of stale tobacco and damp paper, when I heard the shuffling step of a man approaching along the quay. The sound was furtive, as though the man wished to tread upon the very shadows and leave no echo on the wood.

He appeared in the doorway, a heavy, pale figure of a foreigner, with a face like a sodden mask, upon which the blackness of his eyes rested without expression. He was buttoned up in a dark coat, despite the heat, and he carried a walking stick of some heavy, dark wood. His boots were caked with the yellow mud of the inland roads. He did not enter fully, but stood at the threshold, as if waiting for the very air to grant him permission.

“Senor Vargas,” he murmured, and his voice had the flat, dead quality of a stone dropped into a deep well. It was not a question. He knew my name.

I looked up from my ledger. The flame of the single oil lamp threw a wavering, sooty light across the pages, and I saw that my hand, resting upon the pen, had become very still. “I am Vargas,” I said. My voice sounded strangely hollow in that little box of a room.

He came in then, and shut the door behind him with a careful, deliberate motion, as though the night outside were listening. He did not sit down. He stood opposite me across the narrow table, and the smell of the wet night and some foreign, spicy scent came from him.

“There is a vessel,” he said, leaning forward slightly, his eyes fixed upon mine. “A barque. She lies at the upper anchorage. She will take on a certain cargo tonight, before the light. It is necessary that the eyes of the customs house are turned elsewhere.”

I did not speak. I watched the steady, unwinking gaze, and I felt a strange, slow tightening in my chest, as if the heavy air had suddenly become solid. I knew what he was asking. I had heard of such things, of course. In a port like San Esteban, where the authorities were as corrupt as the wharf-timbers and as easily bought, such propositions were not unheard of. But they had never come to me. I had been left alone in my shabby integrity, like a man standing upon a shrinking island.

“And if my eyes are turned,” I said, very slowly, “what then?”

He smiled then, and it was a mere mechanical contortion of the lips, a thing without warmth or joy. He put his hand into the inner pocket of his coat and drew forth a small, heavy package, wrapped in oilcloth. He laid it on the table between us, beside the lamp. The package made a dull, metallic sound as it touched the wood.

“There is enough there,” he said, “to buy a hundred such lives as yours. Or to save one.”

It was as if he had struck me. My mind, which had been dwelling in a sort of dull, weary acceptance of the scene, suddenly recoiled. One life. He did not know, perhaps, but the words had found their mark with a terrible, accidental precision. My wife, Elena, was lying in our house behind the custom-house, in a room that let in the damp and the mosquitoes, and the doctor from the capital had told me, with a cold, professional civility, that without certain drugs, without a change of air and a cessation of this pestilential heat, she would not see another rainy season. I had looked at him with the stupid, uncomprehending eyes of the poor, knowing that the money for such things was as far from me as the stars.

The man in the dark coat watched me. He saw the hesitation, the flicker in my eyes, and I saw the gleam of comprehension in his own. He had not known, perhaps, but he understood. He saw that he had found the crack in the wall, the point of weakness in the otherwise impenetrable fortress of my honesty.

“It is a great deal of money, Senor Vargas,” he said, almost gently. “More than you would see in twenty years of sitting in this office. And the cargo is harmless. I give you my word. A few crates of rifles, nothing more. They go to men who are fighting for their freedom, for their very lives, in the mountains. You would not be betraying your country. You would be... serving a higher justice.”
