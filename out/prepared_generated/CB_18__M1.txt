Miss Vale had shut the window against the March wind, but she could not shut out the sound of the bells. They clashed and clamoured from the old grey tower of Saint-Aubin, as if every bronze tongue in the city were striving to outshout its neighbour. Below, in the Place des Tilleuls, a carriage had drawn up before the pensionnat of Madame Duval, and from it a gentleman was alighting.

Miss Vale knew him well enough by sight now. It was the Count de la Vire, and he had come, as he had come thrice within the week, for one purpose only. He desired to see Miss Adela Hartwell.

Adela was her pupil—her dear, her gifted, her too-trusting pupil. For three years Miss Vale had had the charge of her, sent out from her father’s rectory in Derbyshire to be polished, as the phrase went, and made fit for the society into which she was born. Miss Vale, herself a woman of some forty years, with a face that had been called handsome in its youth and was now called severe, had taken the duty seriously. She had taught the girl French and Italian, history and a little music; she had watched the growth of a fine understanding and a passionate heart; and she had looked forward to the day when she should deliver her up to her father, a creature as sound in mind as she was lovely in person.

Now, in the space of a single winter, a stranger had appeared, and all her careful work was threatened with destruction.

She heard the rustle of a silk dress in the corridor, and Adela herself came into the room. She was not beautiful in the regular, placid English style; her features were too mobile, her eyes too dark and ardent for that. But there was a fire in her, a vividness, that made other girls seem but wax dolls beside her. She was all in a flush of excitement now, her colour high, her hands clasped.

“He is here!” she cried. “Oh, Miss Vale, did you see the carriage? It is the Count!”

“I saw the Count’s carriage,” replied Miss Vale, keeping her voice steady by an effort of will. “I did not see the Count himself, but I can imagine his errand.”

Adela’s face fell a little at her teacher’s tone. “You are not glad. I thought you would share my happiness.”

“Your happiness? Is it happiness, Adela, to be pursued by a man of whom you know nothing, save that he has a title and a fine coat? Have I not warned you? This man is a pauper. He has no estate, no fortune, no prospects. He lives by his wits and by the good-nature of his friends. He is a butterfly, and you would pin him to your heart.”

“It is not true!” Adela cried, her spirit roused. “You do him injustice. He is noble in soul, if not in wealth. He loves me, and I love him. What is money, when the heart is given? You, who speak so often of the spirit, of the soul’s worth—how can you be so mercenary?”

Miss Vale rose from her chair and faced her. The wind rattled the casement, and the firelight threw their two shadows, one tall and rigid, the other slight and quivering, upon the wall.

“I am not mercenary,” she said, and her voice was low and hard. “I am a realist. I have seen the world, and I know that love without bread is a short and bitter feast. This Count de la Vire is a known adventurer. He flatters you because you are an heiress. He sees in you a means to repair his ruined fortunes. When your father’s money is gone, his love will go with it.”

“Never!” Adela stamped her foot. “You are cruel! You are cold! You judge him by your own English standards, where a man is but a machine for making money. Here, in France, a nobleman may be poor and still be noble. He has shown me letters from his friends, from great ladies in Paris. He is respected.”

“Letters can be forged, and great ladies may be deceived. Adela, listen to me. I beg of you, as one who has cared for you as a daughter, do not see him again. Send him away. Tell him you cannot receive him.”

“I will not!” The girl’s eyes blazed. “I am of age, or nearly so. My heart is my own. I will not be turned from my course by your gloomy fancies. If you cannot rejoice with me, then at least leave me in peace.”

She turned to the door, but Miss Vale caught her arm. Her grip was strong, and the touch sent a shock through the girl’s frame.

“Adela,” she said, and there was a tremor now in the severe voice, a fierce, suppressed passion. “Do not do this thing. It is a precipice. If you fall, you break your father’s heart. He trusts me to guard you. How can I look him in the face when I return you to him a victim of a French adventurer?”

Adela shook her off. “My father would wish my happiness. And I will be happy, in spite of you!” She flung out of the room, her silk skirts hissing like a serpent.

Miss Vale stood alone. The fire had sunk to a red glow, and the room was full of shadows. The bells of Saint-Aubin had ceased, and in the sudden quiet she heard the murmur of voices from the salon below—the Count’s smooth, rapid French, and Adela’s answering laugh.

She went to the window and looked down. The Count was bowing over Adela’s hand. He was a handsome man, with a dark, mobile face and eyes that sparkled with a dangerous charm. He looked up suddenly, as if he felt her gaze, and for a moment his eyes met hers. There was a challenge in them, a cool, insolent triumph.
