Mrs. Arbuthnot had arrived at the resort of Saltley-by-the-Sea with a conviction as settled as the tides: her daughters, Amelia and Charlotte, were to be married, and married well. The object of this felicitous future was Viscount Ashworth, a young nobleman whose expected presence at Saltley had caused a stir in certain circles, and whose arrival Mrs. Arbuthnot had calculated to the day. Her own share in the proceeding was, in her estimation, that of a general directing a brilliant campaign; the girls were her well-drilled infantry, and the Viscount, the prize to be seized.

The first week, however, proved a study in quiet vexation. The Viscount did not appear. Mrs. Arbuthnot was forced to content herself with observing the lesser fauna of the resort—the daughters of a provincial squire, a widowed baronet of declining fortune—and assuring her girls, with a confidence that grew thinner by the hour, that patience was a virtue particularly becoming in those destined for a high station. Amelia, a young woman of good sense and an obliging disposition, bore the disappointment with a calm that might have been mistaken for indifference. Charlotte, whose temperament was livelier and less governed, found the waiting a trial, and expressed it in a series of restless sighs that her mother found quite unbecoming.

The Viscount’s arrival, when it at last occurred, was not the grand event Mrs. Arbuthnot had pictured. There was no flourish of postilions, no gathering of curious onlookers. He was simply *there* one morning on the promenade, a fact which Amelia was the first to discover and report with a mixture of relief and apprehension. “He is here, Mama,” she said, her voice low, as if she feared the news might be premature. “Walking with his governess.”

The word ‘governess’ fell upon Mrs. Arbuthnot’s ears with the force of a physical blow. A governess? A boy of title did not require a governess; he required a valet, perhaps a tutor in the classics, but certainly not the chaperonage of some dreary female. Her spirits, which had soared at the news of his presence, now plummeted. She took up her lorgnette, a formidable instrument of observation, and directed it towards the promenade.

There, indeed, was the Viscount. He was possessed of a title, it was true, and an estate in Derbyshire, but he was also possessed of a painfully thin frame, a complexion of alarming pallor, and an Adam’s apple that seemed to bob with an independent life of its own. He was not more than seventeen. Beside him walked a woman of perhaps forty, with a face as severe as her grey bombazine gown, who occasionally corrected his posture with a sharp tap of her parasol. The Viscount, for his part, seemed engaged in a profound struggle with his own feet, which appeared to be far larger and more disobedient than he was accustomed to.

Charlotte, peering through her own glass, could not suppress a small, unladylike snort of laughter. “Good heavens, Mama. He looks as though he has just been weaned.”

“Hush, Charlotte!” hissed her mother, her face a mask of rigid composure. “You will be observed.” The full weight of her disappointment settled upon her. This was no hero of romance, no dashing figure to inspire sonnets. This was a boy. An awkward, ungainly boy, attended by a dragon. To have pursued him would be the height of folly; to have been seen to pursue him and fail would be social ruin. The entire resort must not learn that the great Mrs. Arbuthnot had mistaken a schoolboy for a suitor.

The problem, therefore, was not one of conquest, but of concealment. They must erase the Viscount from their own narrative as swiftly and silently as the sea erases a footprint on the sand. The afternoon’s assembly, to which they had been looking forward as the theatre of their triumph, must now become the stage for a performance of magnificent indifference.

Amelia, with a natural tact that her mother often undervalued, understood the necessity at once. “We shall speak of the weather,” she proposed gently. “And of the merits of sea-bathing. We shall ask after the health of Mrs. Pomeroy’s children. It will be a very comfortable afternoon, I am sure.”

Charlotte, once her initial mirth had subsided, rose to the challenge with a theatrical flair. “And if anyone should mention him,” she declared, her eyes sparkling with mischief, “I shall say I found him a very *promising* young man. So much youth! So much potential! It will be the very essence of patronising politeness.”

This seemed a dangerous game to Amelia, but her mother, seeing no better alternative, gave a curt nod of approval. The plan was set.

At the assembly, Mrs. Arbuthnot and her daughters were a picture of serene sociability. They moved through the rooms with an air of contented ease, their conversation a masterwork of the commonplace. When Lady Anne, a notorious gossip, remarked, “And so the young Viscount Ashworth has arrived! I trust you have made his acquaintance?” Mrs. Arbuthnot did not so much as blink.

“We have had the pleasure of seeing him on the promenade,” she said, her tone one of mild, uninterested acknowledgement. “He appears to be a very young man.”

“So he is!” agreed Lady Anne, eager for more. “And with a governess! Can you imagine? It seems so very odd.”

“One must suppose his family are careful of his health,” Amelia interjected smoothly, steering the conversation towards the general frailty of the young, a topic upon which every matron present was an expert.

Charlotte, meanwhile, had cornered the squire’s daughter. “And have you seen the Viscount?” the girl asked, all agog. “Is he not perfectly handsome?”

“He is very… young,” Charlotte replied, bestowing upon the word a weight of such profound, unspoken meaning that her companion was left to ponder its significance for the rest of the evening. She had managed, in a single word, to convey pity, condescension, and a world-weary wisdom far beyond her years.
