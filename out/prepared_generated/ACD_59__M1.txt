It was upon the fourteenth of November, in the year 1893, that my friend Mr. Sherwin and I were seated in his rooms in Queen Anne Street, when the matter of the Canton Missionary’s luggage was brought to our attention. The day was one of those damp, sunless affairs peculiar to a London autumn, a fine grey mist clinging to the window-panes and blurring the outlines of the opposite houses. The fire in the grate was a welcome sight, and the air was heavy with the scent of shag tobacco and old leather.

Sherwin, a man whose profession of private inquiry had sharpened every faculty he possessed, was examining a letter through a magnifying glass, when the door-bell rang with a sharp, insistent peal. A moment later, Mrs. Eleanor Vance was shown in. She was a lady of some thirty years, with a pale, composed face and the anxious eyes of one who has travelled far and seen much to trouble her. She wore a travelling dress of grey merino, and her gloves, though clean, were of a serviceable, unornamented kid.

“Mr. Sherwin,” she began, her voice steady despite the tremor of her hands, “I am told you are a man who can find the truth where others see only confusion. My husband, the Reverend Thomas Vance, is home on leave from our mission in Canton. We arrived by the *Orontes* three days ago.”

“Pray be seated, madam,” said Sherwin, gesturing to the armchair opposite his own. “Your husband is well, I trust?”

“In health, yes. But in spirit, he is most unwell. We are being followed, Mr. Sherwin. Not overtly, not with any attempt at menace, but with a quiet, persistent watchfulness that is far more unnerving.”

Sherwin leaned forward, his long, thin fingers steepled before him. “Describe the man.”

“He is a Chinaman, of middling height and slight build. He is always dressed in a manner that might belong to a clerk or a shopkeeper—dark English clothes, but his face is unmistakable. He has never approached us, never spoken, never done anything but watch. If we are in the Park, he is on a distant bench. If we take a cab, his own hansom is never far behind. Thomas, who is a man of profound faith and little suspicion, at first thought it mere coincidence. Now he is convinced of a more sinister purpose.”

“And this purpose, Mrs. Vance? Has your husband any enemies from his time in China?”

“He has made many converts, but he has also opposed the opium trade and the schemes of certain powerful merchants. He is a brave man, but a gentle one. He cannot conceive of such a shadow being cast upon our homecoming.”

I saw Sherwin’s eyes narrow. He was a man who lived upon such shadows. “You mentioned luggage, madam. Has there been any interference with your property?”

A flush rose to Mrs. Vance’s cheeks. “That is the very heart of it. Our trunks were delivered from the docks on Tuesday. Thomas was at his club, and I was here alone. When I came to unpack his effects, I found the work had already been done. Everything was in its place, folded with a care that was almost unnatural. His clerical collars, his sermons, his books on the dialects of the Pearl River Delta—all were in perfect order. But the seals on his private strong-box had been broken and re-fastened. The lock itself shows the faintest scratch of a fine wire. Mr. Sherwin, it is as if a ghost had been at our things, a ghost with the hands of a master locksmith and the patience of a saint.”

Sherwin rose and began to pace the room. “And the papers within? Are any missing?”

“We cannot say. Thomas keeps his most sensitive notes—the names of converts, the locations of out-stations—in a cipher he devised himself. He has checked the volume, and believes nothing is gone. But the violation, the sheer audacity of it, has shaken him to his core. He feels as though his very soul has been rifled through.”

“Tell me, Mrs. Vance,” Sherwin said, pausing by the mantelpiece, “what is the most significant event of your husband’s mission? Not the daily work, but the singular triumph or disaster.”

Mrs. Vance thought for a moment. “It would be the affair of the Dragon’s Tooth,” she said slowly. “Two years ago, Thomas secured the conversion of a man named Feng, a notorious bandit and leader of a powerful triad in the hills above Canton. It was a great victory. But Feng had in his possession a jade seal, an ancient token of authority that commanded the loyalty of his followers. Upon his conversion, he gave it to Thomas for safekeeping, fearing his rivals would murder him for it. Thomas has kept it hidden ever since, believing it a sacred trust.”

“A jade seal,” Sherwin murmured. “And what became of the bandit Feng?”

“He entered a seminary. He is now a catechist, preaching the Gospel in the very territories he once terrorized. But his old life has not forgotten him. There are those who would pay a great price for that seal, and who believe Thomas Vance is the only man who knows its whereabouts.”

Sherwin’s face was alight with a sudden, fierce energy. “The pieces begin to fit. The man who follows you is not a common thief. He is a sentinel, a watcher sent to ensure the missionary does not stray, to learn his habits, to find the moment when the prize might be taken. The search of the luggage was not for money or jewels, but for a small, heavy object, or for a map, a letter, anything that might reveal its location. The care taken in repacking was to allay suspicion, to suggest a common housebreaker rather than a targeted search.”

He turned to me. “Watson, my bag. We shall go to your husband at once, Mrs. Vance. There is not a moment to lose.”
