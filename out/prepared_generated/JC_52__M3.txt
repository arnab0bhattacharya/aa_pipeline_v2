It is a curious thing to consider the vanity of a man who believes his written word can summon the spirits of revolution, as though the mere setting of type and ink might conjure the substantial shadow of a great event from the damp air of a stagnant capital. Decoud had believed it. He had sat in the stifling heat of the *Chronicle’s* narrow office, with the heavy, bruised twilight of the Republic of Sulaco filtering through the begrimed window, and he had composed a paean to an uprising that was, even then, dissolving into the sullen mud of the harbor. He had written it with a fierce, exalted conviction, his pen moving with the rhythmic certainty of a man who perceives the machinery of destiny turning beneath his very hand. The leader of the insurrection, a certain General Montero, was to Decoud a figure of tragic grandeur, a necessary blade to sever the rotting tether of the old oligarchy. And so, in the premature dusk of that Tuesday, while the distant, irregular popping of musketry echoed from the hills like the spiteful crackling of damp thorns, Decoud had launched his editorial into the slumbering heart of the city.

He had called it the Dawn of Justice, if I remember rightly—a grandiose, pathetic phrase, heavy with the borrowed rhetoric of a European liberalism he had never fully understood. He saw the streets rising, the somnolent masses awakening to the pure light of a reformed state, the dark, oppressive weight of the old regime lifted by a sudden, brilliant conflagration. And all the while, the actual dawn of Wednesday was preparing to reveal nothing but a squalid aftermath of failure, a grey, humiliating dispersal of men who had found the courage to revolt but lacked the substance to conquer. The telegraph wire, humming its invisible, treacherous song beneath the plaster of the walls, had carried the fatal news before the ink was scarcely dry on the damp sheets of the newspaper. Montero was dead, shot in the back by his own lieutenant in a quarrel over a woman’s necklace or a stolen mule—the details were obscure, wrapped in the usual sordid mist of these South American tragedies. The Federal troops, loyal to the President, had scattered the remnants of the rebel column with a brutal, effortless cavalry charge, and the dawn had brought not the promised dawn of justice, but the heavy, brooding silence of a restored and vengeful authority.

Decoud sat now in the profound silence of his office, the unopened bundles of the morning edition stacked like so many monuments to his own destruction upon the bare deal table. The heat was immense, a solid, palpable presence that seemed to thicken the air with the scent of rotting fruit and damp plaster. He was a man of forty, perhaps, with a pale, intellectual face and dark eyes that held a nervous, searching intensity. He had come to Sulaco from some European university, bringing with him a vast, formless ambition and a profound ignorance of the actualities of power. He had believed that ideas could move mountains, that the printed word was a force akin to the tides of the sea, irresistible in its march. And now, in the stagnant quiet of the morning, with the distant, rhythmic clatter of hooves beginning to echo from the plaza, he sat and contemplated the sudden, vertiginous collapse of his world.

He was alone in the building, save for the old Spanish compositor, a mute, patient shadow of a man who had set the type for the fatal article and who had now retreated to some hidden corner, perhaps to pray or perhaps to sleep with the equanimity of the indifferent. Decoud had sent away the one errand boy, a mestizo youth with a scarred, sullen face, who had looked at him that morning with a strange, sidelong expression, half pity and half a kind of animal cunning. The boy knew. Everyone knew. The whole city seemed to pulse with a secret, collective awareness of his guilt, a vast, invisible conspiracy of knowledge that pressed against the thin walls of the office. He got up and walked to the window, his footsteps sounding hollow and uncertain on the warped floorboards. The narrow street below was deserted, swept by a windless, oppressive heat. The opposite wall, stained with the greenish damp of decades, seemed to lean toward him, a mute, accusing witness. He saw nothing, heard nothing, and yet he felt the entire weight of the city’s silence bearing down upon him, a silence that was not the absence of sound but the presence of a vast, held breath, a collective waiting for the inevitable.

He returned to his chair and sat staring at the stacked newspapers, his hands lying palm upward on the table in a gesture of profound, abandoned weariness. The options, as he saw them, were three: flight, recantation, or the waiting. Flight was a physical act, a brutal, immediate surrender to the instinct of preservation. The back door of the *Chronicle* opened onto a labyrinth of narrow alleys that led, eventually, to the harbor, where a coastal steamer, indifferent and rust-streaked, was even now preparing to depart for some northern port. He could be on it, lost in the anonymous crowd of the steerage, within the hour. But the very thought of it filled him with a kind of physical nausea. To run was to confess, not merely the error of his judgment, but the inherent hollowness of his convictions. He had praised the revolt as an act of supreme justice, a necessary assertion of the human spirit against tyranny, and to flee now would be to acknowledge that he had been playing with words, that his grand phrases were nothing but the empty, resonant echo of a hollow shell. There was a stubborn, irrational pride in him, a rigid, European intellectual vanity that recoiled from the physical squalor of retreat.
