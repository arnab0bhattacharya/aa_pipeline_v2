The fog of a London November has a quality of penetration quite unlike the sea-mists of the Archipelago; it does not merely obscure, it seeps into the very marrow of the bones, and into the unlit corners of a man’s conscience. I had known it for fifteen years, that particular damp chill, and had learned to read its moods as a sailor reads the sky. It was in that fog, in a little room above a Greek eating-house in Soho, that I first met the man who was to become my most valuable, and most precarious, asset. His name was Stefan Kroll. He sat hunched over a small cup of black coffee, a creature of the shadows, with eyes that seemed to absorb the light without reflecting any. He was a Pole, a man displaced by history and ambition, and he possessed that fatal quality of the émigré: a desperate, all-consuming belief in the power of an idea, which in his case was the overthrow of all established order.

For five years, Kroll was my compass in the bewildering labyrinth of London’s foreign conspirators. Through his whispered information, delivered in the stale air of public houses or the sudden anonymity of a crowded street, I learned the secret names, the hidden meeting places, the schisms and the plots that festered in the city’s foreign colonies. He was my shield. The dynamite plot of ’89, which would have shattered the very foundations of Whitehall, was averted not by the vaunted acumen of Scotland Yard, but by a few scrawled words on a torn piece of paper, passed to me by Kroll in the shadow of the British Museum. He was a traitor to his comrades, a Judas to their faith, and for this service I had secured him a small stipend and, more importantly, a precarious anonymity. There was between us a compact of mutual necessity, an unspoken understanding that was more binding than any oath, and just as morally void. I did not judge him; I merely used him, as a man uses a tool, and felt the cold satisfaction of a job well done.

This equilibrium, so carefully maintained, was disturbed by the arrival of Sir Marcus Thorne. He was a man of the new breed, all polish and political calculation, recently transferred from the Colonial Office, and he regarded the work of the Criminal Investigation Department not as a grimy, necessary craft, but as an instrument of public policy. He spoke of ‘results’, of ‘demonstrable efficacy’, of ‘restoring public confidence’. He had the clear, hard eyes of a man who has never had to look into the murk of human desperation and see his own reflection. One afternoon, in his office that smelled of beeswax and self-importance, he summoned me.

“Murchison,” he began, tapping a sheaf of newspapers on his desk. “These are unacceptable. The press howls for action. They speak of a ‘reign of terror’ fomented by anarchists and nihilists, and we have nothing to show for our efforts but a collection of minor agitators and pamphleteers. The public wishes to see a head on a pike. A significant head.”

I felt a coldness in my stomach, a premonition of the trap. “The work of penetration is slow, Sir Marcus. It requires patience. We are dealing with a secretive and distrustful element.”

“Patience is a luxury we can no longer afford,” he retorted, his voice sharp as shrapnel. “I want an arrest. A major one. A man of standing in their councils. I have been given to understand that you have cultivated certain… sources. This is the moment to use them. Find me a leader, Murchison. Find me the man who directs these plots. I want him in custody by the end of the week.”

He dismissed me with a wave of his hand, leaving me standing in the silent, ordered room, the weight of his command settling upon me like a shroud. He wanted a leader. The only leader whose name and movements I knew with any certainty was Stefan Kroll. To arrest Kroll would be to satisfy the newspapers, to appease Sir Marcus, and to secure my own position. It would also be to destroy the delicate web I had so painstakingly woven, and to betray the one man whose treachery had kept me, and the city, safe. The irony was of a particularly bitter and professional nature. I was ordered to sacrifice my own instrument of safety to demonstrate that the public was safe.

I found him that evening, in a different café, a place thick with the smoke of cheap tobacco and the guttural cadences of a dozen languages. The gaslight threw long, distorted shadows against the grimy windows. Kroll was as I had always known him, a study in nervous energy contained within a frame of profound weariness. He looked up as I sat, and his eyes, those dark, depthless pools, held a flicker of inquiry.

“The air is thick tonight, Stefan,” I said, forgoing any greeting. “It chokes a man.”

He understood at once. He always did. “There is always smoke when men are trying to light a fire, Inspector. You should know this better than anyone.”

“They want a fire put out,” I said, my voice low and even. “They want to see the man who struck the match.”

A long silence fell between us, broken only by the clatter of a cup from the kitchen and the low murmur of a Russian poet reading his verses to a girl who was not listening. Kroll slowly stirred the dregs in his cup. The gesture was one of infinite weariness, of a man contemplating the end of a long and fruitless struggle.

“I see,” he said, at last. “The great machine requires a sacrifice. And I am the most convenient offering.” There was no anger in his voice, only a profound, weary resignation. “It is the logical conclusion of our arrangement, is it not? A tool is used until it is worn out, and then it is discarded.”
