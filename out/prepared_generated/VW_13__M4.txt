The house—"Seaview," though the view was chiefly of a shingled roof and a strip of grey water beyond—stood at the end of a lane that turned its back on the town, and it was here, on the third morning, that Margaret found herself awake before the others, listening to the gulls and to the small, persistent breathing of her sister in the next bed. Ellen slept with her mouth slightly open, one arm thrown back across the pillow, and her face, in repose, had a look of such entire and unguarded peace that Margaret, watching her, felt a curious pang, not of tenderness, but of something nearer to resentment—though why she should resent the sleep of a woman of thirty-two, unmarried, who had come to stay for a week and would go away again, she could not have said.

The children were up before eight, as they always were, and the day arranged itself in its usual pattern: the beach, the bathing, the sandwiches in paper, the small quarrels over spades. Ellen was good with them—better, Margaret thought, than she herself had ever been, though they were her own. She would sit on the sand with her skirts tucked under her and build the most elaborate towers, patient, absorbed, her hat pulled low, while Margaret walked along the water's edge, her hands in her pockets, and thought about nothing at all—or about the house in Norwich, and the dinner on Thursday, and whether Arthur would remember to speak to the man about the gutters. The sea came in and went out again, grey and deliberate, and the gulls hung above the shingle, and it seemed to Margaret that the whole world was composed of these small, repeated movements, and that she had been doing the same things, in the same order, for a very long time.

On the fourth evening Arthur came down from Norwich on the late train, and they sat in the little sitting-room after supper, the windows open, the lamp lit, the children asleep. Arthur talked about the office; he had a way of talking about the office that made it seem a place of enormous consequence, full of feuds and stratagems, and Margaret listened, and Ellen listened, and it seemed to Margaret, watching her sister's face, that Ellen listened in a way that she herself had long since lost—as though the doings of the office were a story, and she wished to know how it ended. When Arthur had gone up to bed, Ellen said, "He seems well," and Margaret said, "Yes, he's well," and there was a silence between them that was not comfortable.

It was on the fifth day, in the afternoon, that Ellen walked into the town alone to buy stamps, and Margaret, left with the children, found herself standing at the window of the sitting-room looking out at the lane and the shingled roof and the strip of water, and thinking—quite suddenly, without preparation—that she was not unhappy, and that this was the strangest thing of all. She had a house, and a husband, and three children, and a life that was full from morning to night, and she was not unhappy; and yet she stood at the window and felt, rising in her, a kind of wonder, as at a landscape she had never properly seen. She thought of Ellen, who had nothing—no house, no husband, no children—and who had built towers on the sand with a patience that Margaret could not help but envy, and she thought, with a clarity that was almost cruel: I do not want her life. I do not want it at all. And having thought this, she felt a great stillness come over her, and she stood there a long time, watching the light change on the water.

Ellen, meanwhile, walked up the hill from the town with the stamps in her bag, and the afternoon was fine, and the gulls were crying, and she felt, in her limbs, the pleasant tiredness of the sea. She had built a very good tower that morning; the children had admired it; Margaret had said, "How clever you are," in her dry way, and Ellen had felt the praise go through her like a warmth. She thought of the week that remained, and of the week after, and of the little flat in Bloomsbury, and of the work she had brought with her and not yet begun—and it seemed to her that she was walking, not up a hill in Norfolk, but through a life that was hers and no one else's, and that the freedom of it was a thing she had never quite dared to look at directly, and that now, in the sun, with the stamps in her bag, she could look at it and see that it was good. And then, with the same suddenness, she thought of Margaret at the window, and of Arthur on the late train, and of the children, and it seemed to her that Margaret's life was a rich, close, tangled thing, full of warmth and noise and consequence, and that her own was thin, thin as paper, and that she would have given—oh, anything, anything at all—to have a child call her mother, to have a man come home on the late train and talk to her about the office.

She stopped on the hill and looked back at the sea. It was very blue, and the town lay below her, small and white, and the gulls were crying, and she felt the tears come into her eyes and she did not know why.

That evening they sat together on the beach, the two of them, after the children were in bed, and the sun went down behind the town and the water turned the colour of pewter, and neither of them spoke for a long time.
