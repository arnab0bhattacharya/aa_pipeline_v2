The house in Camberwell Terrace, Kentish Town—that new and aspiring suburb which lies, as it were, in the lap of the metropolis, with one cheek turned to the smoke of London and the other to the green of Hampstead—was a house of gladness. It was a house of birds, of flowers, of sunshine, and of debt; and if the last-mentioned article of its furniture was at first invisible, it was only because the bright eyes of its inhabitants did not choose to see it.

The inhabitants were Mr. and Mrs. Valentine Merridew. He was a clerk in a wine-merchant’s office in the City—a place of ledgers and dusty sunshine, where the very corks seemed to sigh for the country—and she was a creature of such exuberant spirits, such dancing curls, and such a ready laugh, that she seemed to have been born for the express purpose of making tradesmen forget the calendar. They were young, they were in love, and they were setting up housekeeping on the most delightful system of credit that the world has ever known.

Every tradesman in the street, from the butcher with the blue apron to the baker with the floury whiskers, fell a victim to their charm. When Mrs. Merridew ordered a leg of mutton, she did not merely order it; she made the butcher feel that he was conferring a favour upon her by allowing her to roast it. When Mr. Merridew bought a dozen of sherry, he did it with such a frank, jovial confidence that the wine-merchant felt it would be an insult to ask for the money. They lived in a golden age of bills, where the paper that came in the letter-box was treated not as a demand, but as a polite invitation to continue the acquaintance.

But there is a shadow which follows close upon the heels of every sunbeam, and that shadow is called Quarter Day.

It came upon them one Tuesday morning in November, with a suddenness that was truly theatrical. The fog outside was thick enough to be cut with a knife, and inside, the breakfast table was covered not with buttered toast, but with a collection of blue and white envelopes, each bearing the fatal words: *To be paid on demand.*

Mr. Merridew, who was buttering a roll with his usual serenity, looked up and saw his wife staring at the pile with a face as white as the tablecloth. He rose, walked round the table, and took her hand.

“Come, my dear Fanny,” said he, in a voice that trembled only a little. “What is this? Is the baker angry? Is the milkman sulking? Let us look the enemy in the face.”

He opened the first letter. It was from the grocer. Then the second, from the butcher. Then the third, the fourth, the fifth—until the table was a sea of paper, and the total was a sum that made Mr. Merridew’s hair stand on end.

“We are ruined, Val!” cried Mrs. Merridew, bursting into tears. “We are undone! The gas will be cut off! The furniture will be seized! We shall be turned into the street, and we shall have to live in a workhouse, and eat gruel with a wooden spoon!”

“Nonsense, my love,” said Mr. Merridew, though he was secretly calculating how much he could get for his watch. “We must retrench. We must economize. We must—”

He was interrupted by a sharp, rat-tat-tat at the door—a knock that sounded like a judge’s gavel.

It was not the butcher, nor the baker, nor the candlestick-maker. It was Miss Priscilla Merridew.

Miss Priscilla Merridew was the maiden aunt of Mr. Valentine, and she was a lady of whom the family spoke in whispers, as one speaks of a thunderstorm or a tax-collector. She lived at Peckham Rye, in a house where the blinds were always drawn to preserve the carpet, and where the only living thing allowed to be cheerful was a canary, and he only because he could not help it. She was tall, she was thin, she was dressed in black bombazine that rustled like dry leaves, and she carried a green cotton umbrella which she used less for protection against the rain than as a sceptre of authority.

“Well, Valentine,” said Miss Priscilla, shaking the damp from her umbrella onto the hall floor with a disregard for the oilcloth that proved she considered herself mistress of the house. “I have come. I had a dream. I dreamt that you were drowning in a bowl of punch, and that your wife was trying to pay the baker with a kiss. So I packed my trunk, and here I am.”

“Oh, Aunt Priscilla!” gasped Mrs. Merridew.

“Do not ‘Oh, Aunt Priscilla’ me, girl,” said the old lady, sweeping into the sitting-room and eyeing the breakfast table with the keenness of a hawk. “I see it all. I see the remains of a loaf that is too white for your income. I see the dregs of a tea that is too strong for your digestion. And I see,” she added, picking up a handful of the blue envelopes, “the handwriting of Mr. Sharp, the butcher, and Mr. Plump, the baker. I know them of old. They are the vultures of civilization.”

She sat down in the armchair, the only comfortable chair in the room, and struck the floor with her umbrella.

“From this moment,” she declared, “I take charge. I am the captain of this ship, and you are the crew. Valentine, you will give me your wages on Saturday. Fanny, you will give me the keys of the larder. We will have no more roast beef until the debts are paid. We will have broth—economical, nutritious broth. We will have puddings made of suet and stale bread. And we will have silence. Silence is the mother of thrift.”
