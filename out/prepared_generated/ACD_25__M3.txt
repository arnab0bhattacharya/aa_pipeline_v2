It was upon a raw November morning in '93 that my friend Mr. Bellamy Preston was called from his breakfast by a visitor of such singularly dejected bearing that I at once anticipated a case of some gravity. The man had been shown into the room whilst I was still pouring my coffee, and he stood nervously by the mantelpiece, his heavy uniform overcoat dripping upon the fender.

He was a young man, powerfully built and squarely jawed, but his face was haggard, and his eyes bore the look of one who has spent a sleepless night. His left hand, which rested upon his hip, was encased in a kid glove, a singular affectation for a man in the rough tweeds of a country police constable.

"You are Mr. Preston," said he, in a voice that trembled despite his evident efforts to control it. "I am Vickers, of the Hampshire constabulary, stationed at Wolden. I have come to you because the official machinery has failed, and an innocent person has suffered."

Preston motioned him to a chair and pushed the cigar-box towards him. "Pray sit down, Constable Vickers," said he. "You are off duty, or you would not be here. Let us have the facts from the beginning, and as briefly as you can."

The young man sat down and drew off his gloves. I observed that his right hand was bare, rough, and weather-beaten, whereas the left glove, which he now stripped slowly from his fingers, covered a hand that was remarkably soft and white. Preston's eyes twinkled at the sight, but he said nothing.

"It concerns my sister, sir," Vickers began. "She is lady's maid to Mrs. Carstairs, of Thorpe Manor, near Wolden. Last Tuesday week, Mrs. Carstairs missed her diamond necklace—a heavy piece, worth, I believe, some eight hundred pounds. My sister's boxes were searched by the local inspector, and the necklace was not found. Nevertheless, she was dismissed upon the spot, and the matter has been handed to the county police."

"Upon what evidence was she dismissed?" asked Preston, lighting a cigar with deliberate slowness.

"Upon the evidence of the locked door, sir," said Vickers bitterly. "The necklace was kept in a small iron safe in Mrs. Carstairs's dressing-room. My sister alone had the key, besides the mistress. On the Monday evening Mrs. Carstairs wore the necklace at a dinner party. She returned at eleven, and my sister assisted her to undress. Mrs. Carstairs declares she placed the necklace in the safe herself, locked it, and took the key, which she placed beneath her pillow. In the morning the key was gone, and the safe stood open and empty."

"And where was your sister?"

"She slept in the adjoining room, sir. The door between was unlocked, as is the custom. She swears she never entered the dressing-room after bidding her mistress goodnight. But who will believe a lady's maid against her mistress?"

Preston drew a notebook from his pocket. "You say the local inspector searched your sister's boxes. Did he search elsewhere?"

"He searched the house, sir, and the grounds. The necklace was nowhere to be found."

"And the key?"

"That also has vanished."

Preston sat for some moments in silence, his long white fingers tapping upon the arm of his chair. "Your sister, Vickers," said he presently, "has been very foolishly dealt with, but I doubt she has been wrongly accused. Tell me, do you know whether Mrs. Carstairs had recently increased the insurance upon this necklace?"

Vickers started. "How did you know that, sir? It is a fact only discovered yesterday by the inspector. The insurance was raised from six hundred to nine hundred pounds, and that only a week before the loss."

Preston smiled, and I saw the old sharp light come into his grey eyes. "I did not know it," said he. "I guessed it. A necklace does not vanish into thin air, and a mistress who locks her own safe does not lose her key without cause. When you tell me that the insurance was raised within a week of the disappearance, the whole matter becomes luminous. Watson"—he turned to me—"we shall go down to Wolden this afternoon."

It was late in the afternoon when we alighted at the little station of Wolden, a bleak village upon the edge of the Hampshire downs. The wind was sharp, and a fine rain was driving across the fields. Preston had spoken little during the journey, but he had spent his time studying a folded paper which I presently observed to be a policy of insurance, obtained that morning from the offices of the London and Provincial Assurance Company.

We walked to the village inn, and from there Preston dispatched a note to Mrs. Carstairs at Thorpe Manor. Within the hour a carriage arrived, and we were conveyed to the house, a solid red-brick mansion of the Queen Anne period, standing in formal gardens.

Mrs. Carstairs received us in the drawing-room. She was a tall woman of perhaps forty-five years, handsome in a hard, imperious fashion, and dressed with conspicuous care. Her eyes were restless, and her long fingers, clasped upon her lap, twitched continually.

"You are the gentleman from London," said she, in a voice that she strove to make gracious. "I am told you have been engaged by the brother of that unhappy girl. I must say I think it very foolish. The case is perfectly clear."

Preston bowed. "It may be, madam. Nevertheless, I should be obliged if you would permit me to examine the dressing-room where the loss occurred. I work best upon the spot."

She led us upstairs, her skirts rustling upon the oak staircase. The dressing-room was a spacious apartment, furnished with heavy mahogany. The iron safe stood in a recess near the window, its door ajar. Preston examined it closely, testing the lock, which was an ordinary one, and then turned his attention to the window.

"You keep this fastened at night, madam?" he asked, indicating the heavy iron catch.
