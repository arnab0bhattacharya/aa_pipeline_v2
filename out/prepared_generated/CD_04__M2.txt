The rain fell upon the town of Bumblewick not as a gentle weeping of the skies, but as a relentless, muddy deluge, a sort of atmospheric tantrum that seemed designed specifically to humble the ambitions of its inhabitants. It drummed upon the cobblestones with a deafening, rhythmic thud, turning the thoroughfares into rivers of grey slush that carried away the dignity of the day and left behind only a pungent mixture of wet straw, horse dung, and despair. It was a morning of such profound and unbroken wetness that the very air appeared to have lost its buoyancy, hanging heavy and sodden over the market square like a damp woolen shroud.

In this dismal atmosphere stood the newly constructed edifice of the Bumblewick Municipal Banquet Hall, a structure of such magnificent pretension that it had been the pride of the town for precisely three months, and the terror of its inhabitants for the last forty-eight hours. It was a building of whitewashed stone and ambitious arches, rising from the ground with the confidence of a man who has not yet opened his eyes to the world. Yet, for all its grandeur, it possessed a fatal and conspicuous defect: it had no roof. Or rather, it had a *potential* roof, a skeletal framework of timber beams and half-laid shingles that now stood exposed to the elements, creaking and groaning in the wind like the rigging of a ship in a storm.

Alderman Josiah Fumble, a man of considerable girth and even more considerable self-importance, stood beneath the eaves of the unfinished porch, his cravat soaked through, his wig disintegrating into a ragged halo of white fluff around his ears. He was a man who believed that his voice was a natural force, comparable in power to the wind or the tide, and he now found himself in a situation where the wind was blowing directly into his face, and the tide was rising around his ankles.

"The roof!" he bellowed, his voice cracking in the middle of his sentence as a particularly vicious gust of rain lashed across his cheeks. "Where is the roof? Where are the shingles? Where is the tar? Where is the workman?"

The workman was nowhere to be found. The contractor, a slippery creature named Mr. Silas Grist, who had promised wonders in the name of "industrial progress" and "modern aesthetics," had vanished into the night with the town’s funds, a crate of fine claret, and a distinct lack of conscience. The last thing the townspeople had seen of him was a silhouette hurrying away on a cart, followed by a string of oaths and a handful of gravel thrown in his wake.

But Alderman Fumble could not admit this. To admit it would be to admit defeat, and Alderman Fumble had never in his life admitted anything, least of all that he was a fool. He straightened his spine, a feat of considerable mechanical difficulty, and turned to face the arriving guests.

They came trickling in from the market square, a motley procession of damp vicars, muddy farmers, and shivering merchants, all of whom had been invited to the "Grand Inauguration of the Bumblewick Civic Center." They carried their umbrellas like broken wings, and their faces bore the grim expression of men who had been promised a feast but were being offered a baptism.

"Good morning, good morning!" cried Alderman Fumble, stepping out from under the eaves and into the full fury of the downpour. He raised his arms in a grand gesture of welcome, as if conducting an orchestra of thunder. "Welcome, my dear fellow citizens, to a day of such historic significance! The rain, you see, is merely a prelude! A dramatic prelude! It washes the dust from our souls!"

A collective shudder ran through the crowd. Old Mrs. Puddle, who had lived in Bumblewick for sixty years and had seen many a mayor come and go, looked at the Alderman with a skepticism that was sharp enough to cut glass.

"Mr. Fumble," she called out, her voice thin but piercing. "But the hall is open. The wind is blowing in. We shall be soaked."

"Ah, but that is the spirit of the age, Mrs. Puddle!" Fumble shouted back, his eyes wide with a manic energy. "We are not bound by the walls of the past! We are men of the open sky! We are citizens of the element! Come, come! Let us enter!"

He marched toward the main entrance, a gaping maw of brick and mortar that led directly into the unfinished interior. The floor inside was a swamp of wet plaster and scattered debris. A pile of bricks lay in a corner, looking like the remains of a shattered giant. The air was thick with the smell of wet wood and damp earth.

Fumble cleared his throat, a sound like a clog dragging over gravel. "I propose a toast!" he announced, holding up a glass that he had produced from his coat pocket, miraculously dry. "To Bumblewick! To the future! To the... to the roof!"

The crowd stared. A young clerk, a pale and nervous creature named Timothy Squeak, raised his hand timidly. "Alderman Fumble, sir, I beg to differ. The roof is... it appears to be missing."

Fumble froze. The glass trembled in his hand. A drop of rain, large and cold, landed on his nose. He blinked, as if he had not seen the obvious fact before. "Missing?" he squeaked, his voice dropping an octave. "Missing? Nonsense! It is merely... in a state of transition! A philosophical transition! The roof is not gone; it is *sublimated*! It has risen above the physical plane!"

"Sublimated," repeated a farmer, spitting into his hand. "My hay is sublimated, I suppose? It’s in the gutter."

"Your hay is a different matter, sir!" Fumble snapped, his composure cracking like thin ice. "It lacks the civic virtue of a municipal roof! Now, I shall not be distracted by such pedantic inquiries. We are here to celebrate! We are here to eat! Or rather, we are here to *anticipate* eating! For the meal is a spiritual thing, and the spirit is not dampened by a little water!"
