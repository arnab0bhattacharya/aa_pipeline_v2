In the kingdom of Valeriana, where the fountains wept marble tears into basins of porphyry and the gardens bloomed with a perpetual and tiresome splendour, there reigned a King whose countenance was as unyielding as a winter frost. King Amaryllis had never been known to smile. His face was a perfect mask of melancholy, carved from the palest and most unyielding of despair. Some said it was the result of a childhood spent entirely in the study of moral philosophy; others whispered that he had once read a treatise on political economy and had never quite recovered from the shock. Whatever the cause, the sovereign remained profoundly and magnificently grave.

At last, growing weary of the solemnity of his own court, the King issued a proclamation. He declared that he would give half his kingdom to any man, woman, or child who could make him smile. The decree was written upon parchment of violet vellum and sealed with a great seal of yellow wax. It was sent to the four corners of the earth, and soon the palace was filled with a dreadful and incessant crowd of pretenders.

The first to arrive was a Philosopher from the far North. He was a man of immense learning and no humour whatsoever. He was brought before the King in the great Hall of Mirrors, where the silvered glass reflected the heavy brocades of the courtiers and the sad, tired eyes of the monarch.

‘Your Majesty,’ said the Philosopher, bowing so low that his beard swept the marble floor, ‘I have come to prove that laughter is merely a logical fallacy, a momentary lapse in the cosmic syntax. If I may but demonstrate the inherent absurdity of joy, I am certain your Majesty will find reason to smile.’

The Philosopher then spoke at great length about the nature of the soul, the futility of desire, and the absolute necessity of good drainage in the lower provinces. He employed syllogisms of such crushing tedium that the very candles seemed to droop in their sconces. King Amaryllis listened with a profound and unmoving patience. When the Philosopher had finished, the King merely sighed and said, ‘Your arguments are as sound as a dry well, and quite as empty. I am not amused.’

The Philosopher departed in a huff, muttering about the inherent vulgarity of monarchs.

Next came a Poet from the East. He wore a cloak of peacock feathers and carried a lute strung with silver. The courtiers whispered among themselves, for the Poet was known to weep at the falling of a leaf and to laugh only at the misfortunes of his critics.

‘Your Majesty,’ said the Poet, strumming his lute with a languid hand, ‘I shall sing you the song of the stars, a melody so exquisite that it has been known to make the very stones weep with envy and the hardest of hearts crack with delight.’

He sang of moonlight on dark water, of roses that bloom only in the dreams of virgins, and of a shepherd who loved a cloud. His voice was like honey poured over crushed glass. The courtiers were moved to tears, and several elderly lords had to be carried out in a state of emotional collapse. But the King’s face did not change. It remained as fixed and as pale as the moon he sang of.

‘Your song,’ said the King when the last note had died away, ‘is very beautiful. But it is as sad as a sunset, and I have quite enough sadness of my own.’

Then came the Court Jester from a neighbouring realm. He was a man of infinite jest, dressed in motley of crimson and gold, with a cap topped by the head of an ass. He tumbled and capered, he told tales of foolish husbands and clever wives, he juggled porcelain eggs and balanced a peacock feather on his nose. The courtiers roared with laughter until the hall shook, and tears streamed down their painted faces. But King Amaryllis only looked on with a terrible stillness, and the Jester, seeing that he had failed, crept away in shame, his bells tinkling mournfully in the silence.

And so it went. Conjurers made doves vanish into thin air, and the King did not smile. Dancers from the South performed feats of impossible grace, and the King did not smile. A young man offered to eat a whole goose, and the King was merely ill. It seemed that no earthly thing could pierce the armour of his melancholy.

Now, in the lowest depths of the palace, in the great kitchens where the feasts of Valeriana were prepared, there worked a kitchen maid named Elara. She was a small and rather clumsy girl, with eyes the colour of hazelnuts and a disposition that was entirely her own. She had no interest in philosophy or poetry, and she had never heard of the King’s proclamation, for she spent her days up to her elbows in flour and her evenings asleep by the kitchen fire.

One afternoon, it was Elara’s task to carry a great silver tray of lemon tarts to the King’s private drawing-room, where he was holding an audience with the Ambassador of a distant land. The tarts were things of great beauty, their pastry as delicate as lace, their filling a bright and trembling yellow, dusted with sugar that sparkled like morning frost.

Elara carried the tray with great care, walking slowly through the corridors of polished marble. But as she reached the great doors of the drawing-room, she stumbled. Perhaps it was the polished floor, or perhaps it was the spirit of mischief that resides in all lemon tarts. The tray tilted, the tarts flew, and there was a sound of splintering pastry and scattering sugar that echoed through the hall like the breaking of a thousand tiny hearts.
