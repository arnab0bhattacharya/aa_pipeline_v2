The orator had spoken, and the spell he had woven—vivid, potent, and suffocating—was suddenly broken. The crowd, which had sat imprisoned in the vast, freezing hall as if bound by chains of iron, now rose and surged toward the doors. I found myself borne upon the current of that heavy, foreign tide, constrained to fight for very breath amidst cloaks of rough wool and the stifling odour of lamp-oil and ancient stone.

I was an Englishwoman, alone, a teacher of a tongue not my own by birth, and in that moment, the dreary isolation of my lot pressed upon me with a sudden, sharp familiarity. I wrapped my shawl closer round my shoulders. The night was bitter; a wind from the mountains, sharp as a two-edged sword, swept down the narrow streets of Steinhardt, bearing with it the prophecy of snow.

I gained the threshold at last. The great doors were thrown back, and the winter without rushed in—a winter of black skies and pitiless frost. I stood for a second on the stone steps, gathering my resolution to face the blast, when a voice, quiet, deliberate, and wholly English, struck upon my ear.

"You are abroad, then, Miss March."

I turned. I knew the voice. I had heard it five years ago, in a sunny parsonage garden in Hampshire, modulated to a very different tone—a tone of pleading, of deep, tremulous hope. I looked up, and there, standing a little apart from the jostling throng, was Arthur Vane.

He was changed. The pale, earnest youth, whose slender frame and anxious eye had once spoken so eloquently of a mind labouring under the yoke of poverty and ambition, was gone. In his place stood a man in the prime of his strength, clad in a heavy fur-lined coat, bearing upon his person the unmistakable stamp of prosperity. His face was darker, firmer; the lines of care had settled into lines of power. He looked down at me with an eye that was calm, searching, and profoundly tranquil.

"Mr. Vane," I said. The word came forth with a hard, cold ring, though my heart gave a sudden, violent bound. "I little expected to meet you in Steinhardt."

"Nor I you," he answered. "I am here on business connected with the embassy. I arrived but yesterday. But you—you are a teacher here, I am told. A Miss Helstone mentioned your name to me at Vienna."

"And you thought fit to seek me out?" I asked, perhaps too bitterly. The contrast between his warm, ample garments and my own thin, threadbare array was speaking volumes to my proud spirit.

"Nay, I did not know you were in the city until I saw you entering the hall this evening. I followed. I heard the great De Lacey speak. A strange, fierce genius! But the cold is piercing. You are not warmly clad, Eleanor."

It was the old, familiar tone of authority, assumed as naturally as he breathed. It vexed me. I drew myself up.

"I am accustomed to the climate of Steinhardt, Mr. Vane. My constitution does not require the armor you wear. I am not made of glass."

A faint smile, half-melancholy, half-ironic, crossed his lip. "You were always valiant," he said. "You valued your independence above all things. You have it now."

"I have my bread," I answered, sternly. "I earn it by my own labour. That was the independence I sought."

"And you find it sweet?"

"I find it just. I asked no man's pity, and I accept no man's reproach."

"I do not reproach you, Eleanor." His voice deepened. The crowd had melted away into the dark city; we stood almost alone beneath the portico. The wind howled through the columns like a wandering spirit. "I understood your refusal. I was poor, struggling, heavily burdened. You were right to deny me. I had no right to ask you to share a lot so uncertain. But," he added, his eye kindling with a sudden, suppressed fire, "I thought you might have trusted my future."

"Trusted to what? To a vision?" I retorted. "I was a woman, Mr. Vane, not an angel. I could not live on faith, and I would not drag a good man down into the mire of dependence. I chose my path, and you chose yours. You have prospered. Be satisfied."

"I am satisfied," he said, and his voice fell with a heavy, deliberate weight. "I have a home, Eleanor. I have a wife."

The words smote me. They were spoken not with triumph, but with a quiet, immovable gravity that was worse than triumph. It was the seal set upon the past, the stone rolled over the sepulchre of my youth.

"I rejoice to hear it," I said. I forced my lips into a smile. "She is wealthy, I suppose? She must be, to provide such garments and such ease."

"She is the daughter of Sir Lewis Carstairs," he answered, simply. "She is a good woman. But she is not you."

"God forbid she should be!" I flashed out, the passion surging within me. "I am a solitary woman, fighting my way in a foreign land. She is sheltered, honoured, prosperous. Do not compare the two. It is a mockery."

"I do not compare them. I only say, she is not you." He paused, and his gaze, fixed on my face, seemed to read my very soul. "I have often wondered what would have been my fate had you said yes. I was weak then. You would have made me strong, or you would have made me miserable."

"You would have been miserable," I said, with fierce conviction. "I should have been a chain, not a wing. You needed money, position, a wife who could command a drawing-room. I could command nothing but myself."

"And you have commanded yourself," he replied. "You are iron-souled, Eleanor. You were always so. But iron rusts, and frost bites. I see you are shivering. Let me see you to your door."
