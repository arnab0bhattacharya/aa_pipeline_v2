The wind from the northern mountains swept down upon the city of Vanderveen with a harsh, unrelenting fury, rattling the tall windows of the Maison Delacroix as though the very air bore a grudge against the inhabitants. I sat in the narrow schoolroom, my hands folded over a translation of Virgil, listening to the tempest that raged without, and feeling a colder, more bitter storm brewing within. I was an Englishwoman, exiled by poverty and the sheer necessity of earning my bread, teaching my native tongue to the daughters of Dutch merchants. My position was a lonely one, and in my loneliness, I had become a reluctant witness to a base and petty war.

My two fellow instructresses, Mademoiselle Claire and Fraulein Augusta, desired the headship of the English and Music department, a post lately fallen vacant by the death of old Madame Voss. They were both exceedingly distasteful to me. Mademoiselle Claire was a woman of smooth, calculating civility, whose piety was as thin and brittle as the frost on the windowpane, masking a heart utterly devoid of Christian warmth. Fraulein Augusta was her opposite in manner, yet equal in selfishness; a loud, demanding creature, whose skill upon the pianoforte was eclipsed only by her boundless arrogance. I disliked them both with an equal, though quiet, intensity. Yet, as the Scripture warns us, it is not given to man to always choose his adversaries, and I was forced to live beneath the same roof with these women, eating the same coarse bread, and breathing the same heavy air.

The quarrel had been growing for weeks, a slow, festering wound in the hierarchy of the school. Each sought my support. They came to me separately, in the twilight hours, when the pupils were safely asleep in their dormitories. Mademoiselle Claire had cornered me by the fire only the night before, her pale eyes gleaming with a feverish, unwonted light.

"You will not desert me, Miss Hamilton?" she had said, her voice dropping to a confidential whisper. "You know the sacrifices I have made for this institution. You know my devotion to the moral welfare of the children."

"I know your devotion to your salary, Claire," I had replied, with a hardness that surprised even myself. "I have no interest in the politics of this house."

"But you have influence with the Directors," she pressed, her hand falling heavily upon my arm. "A word from you, and the post is mine. I should be indebted to you forever."

I looked at her hand, and then at her face. The mask had slipped a little. I saw the raw, unseemly ambition that seethed beneath. I felt a sudden, fierce heat rise within me, a disgust at the whole sordid business.

"I cannot speak for the Directors," I said, drawing my arm away. "I have no opinions on the matter."

"You are silent, then?" she asked, her voice trembling with a mixture of hope and suspicion. "You will not oppose me?"

"I will not oppose you, Claire."

It was a coward's answer. I knew it even as the words left my lips. I had no intention of supporting her, but neither did I wish to provoke her ire. I sought only peace, a quiet corner in which to tend my own small garden of grief. But peace, I was to learn, is not purchased by evasion.

Fraulein Augusta approached me the very next morning, as we stood in the long, cold corridor that led to the refectory. She was a tall, angular woman, with a face like a hatchet and a voice that carried the harsh resonance of her native mountains.

"You have heard the news, Hamilton?" she demanded, without preamble. "Claire believes the post is already hers. She has been counting her chickens, as it were, before they are hatched."

"I have heard nothing," I said, my voice carefully stripped of emotion. "I am concerned only with my lessons."

"Come, come," she said, her eyes narrowing. "You are too modest. Your opinion carries weight. You know Claire is a hypocrite. She wears her religion like a Sunday bonnet, only to take it off when the Sabbath is over. She will ruin the school if she is given charge. I, on the other hand, have the talent, the discipline. You must see that."

"I see that you are both eager for a position that will bring you little but added labour and vexation," I replied. "I have no preference."

"No preference?" she echoed, her voice rising. "You are as bad as the rest of them. Weak, timid, English! You have no stomach for a fight."

Her words stung me, because they held a grain of truth. I did lack the stomach for this fight. I was tired of the endless jockeying for position, the whispered confidences, the false smiles. I wanted only to be left alone. But I could not bring myself to tell her so. Instead, I retreated into a cold, formal politeness.

"I am sorry you think so, Augusta," I said. "I wish you well, whatever the outcome."

She departed with a scornful laugh, leaving me with a bitter taste in my mouth. I had lied to them both. I had given them false hope, or at least the absence of true opposition, simply to avoid a confrontation. I had placed my own comfort above the demands of honesty and justice. I knew it, and the knowledge sat heavy upon my conscience, a dark, unacknowledged weight.

The decision came a week later, delivered by the Director himself, a pompous, red-faced man who smelled of tobacco and stale wine. We were assembled in the main hall, the three of us, beneath the gaze of a stern portrait of the founder. The Director cleared his throat, and announced that, after careful deliberation, the post of department head had been awarded to Mademoiselle Claire.
