The water was up to the garden gate by the time Arthur Penhaligon finished his tea. It was a grey, sluggish brown, tasting of mud and something else, something metallic and cold that sat heavily on the tongue. He stood on the step of the semi-detached villa in Millbrook Road, watching the river swell beyond the willows, and felt a peculiar, detached curiosity. It was the same feeling one might have when a watch stops ticking; not panic, exactly, but a sudden, sharp awareness of the mechanism’s failure. The trains had not come. The station, a short walk down the hill, was silent. No shriek of brakes, no clatter of wheels on the viaduct, just the low, continuous moan of the current pressing against the embankment.

Mrs. Penhaligon was already at the window, her face pale and set. "The postman didn't come," she said. Her voice was thin, stripped of its usual domestic warmth. "He said the bridge was out."

Arthur looked at the clock on the mantelpiece. It was half past nine. In his office at the Great Western Assurance Company, there would be a pile of claims waiting, a form to be filled in regarding a flooded basement in Croydon. The thought seemed absurdly small, a speck of dust in a vast, darkening room. "We’ll go over to the Hartleys," he said, pulling on his boots. "They have more room. And they’ve got coal."

The Hartley house was larger, a Victorian square of brick that had once been proud but was now sagging under the weight of the damp. Mr. Hartley, a retired solicitor who spoke of the law with the reverence others reserved for scripture, was sitting in his armchair, staring at the fire. He did not look up when Arthur knocked.

"The railway is a dead line," Hartley said. "I walked to the junction an hour ago. The water is over the tracks. The signalmen are gone. I don’t know where they’ve gone."

"Perhaps they’ve gone to high ground," Arthur suggested, though the suggestion felt flimsy in the cold air.

"High ground," Hartley laughed, a dry, rattling sound. "There is no high ground, Arthur. Not here. The whole valley is a basin. We are sitting in the bottom of a bowl, and the water is filling it."

For the first day, the water rose slowly, with a deceptive patience. It lapped at the bottom of the garden walls, turned the lawn into a spongy, treacherous mire, and began to seep into the cellars. The neighbors, a mixture of shopkeepers and clerks who had always known each other only by sight and the occasional nod over the hedge, began to gather. They stood in their coats and hats, watching the river with the blank, fixed stare of men who do not understand the magnitude of the threat. There was a young man named Silas, who worked in a print shop, who tried to organize a chain to move furniture. He shouted orders, but his voice was swallowed by the wind and the rising roar of the water.

"Stop it, Silas!" Mrs. Hartley cried from the doorway. "You’ll break your back. And what good is it? We can’t save the house."

"Someone has to do something," Silas muttered, his face flushed with exertion and fear. He looked at Arthur, who was standing on the porch, holding a candle that guttered in the damp air. "What do you think we should do, Arthur? Wait for the police? The police are gone. I saw their car sink in the main street this morning."

Arthur felt a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature. "I think," he said slowly, "that we should stop pretending that this is an accident. That it’s just a bad night. It isn’t."

The second day brought the rain. It fell in sheets, grey and cold, turning the air into a mist that obscured the houses on the far side of the street. The water was no longer in the garden; it was in the hall. It was in the kitchen. It was lapping at the legs of the dining table. The orderly world of the suburbs, with its painted fences and its strict schedules, had dissolved. There was no distinction between indoors and out. The air smelled of wet plaster and rotting leaves.

People began to leave. Those who had motorcars, or horses, or even a decent pair of boots, took the high road up to the hills. They went in silence, looking back only once. The ones who stayed were those who had nowhere to go, or who believed, with a stubborn, irrational faith, that the water would stop. Arthur found himself among the latter, not out of faith, but out of inertia. He had no car. He had no horse. He had a ledger book and a pocket watch, and he sat in the Hartley’s living room, watching the water rise against the window glass.

It was a strange, quiet kind of terror. There was no screaming, no running. Just a slow, steady submersion. The furniture floated. The paintings on the walls became blurred, underwater ghosts. Arthur looked at Mr. Hartley, who was still in his armchair, though the chair was now floating. The old man looked calm, almost peaceful.

"Do you think they’ll send a boat?" Hartley asked.

"I don’t know," Arthur said. "I don’t think anyone knows where they are."

"I suppose," Hartley said, "that it is a relief to know that the whole system is broken. I always felt that the machine was too large, too intricate. We thought we were safe because we were part of it. But we are just cogs, Arthur. And when the oil runs dry, the cogs stop."

Arthur wanted to argue, to offer some platitude about resilience, about the strength of the community. But he found he could not. He looked out the window. The street was gone. The shops were gone. The only things visible were the tops of the chimneys and the lower branches of the trees, swaying in the current. It looked like a forest of strange, twisted trees, rising out of a dark sea.
