The wind from the Carpathian passes had teeth in November, and it bit through the wool of Arthur Pendelton’s coat as he hurried across the cobbled expanse of the Hradske Square. The lamps of Valdbourg were already lit, guttering in the frosty air, casting long shadows from the statuary that lined the bridge. He climbed the stairs to his third-floor lodging on Sirkova Street, his boots heavy with the city’s particular grime—a mixture of coal dust and the pulverized leaves of the lindens. Inside, the sitting room was stifling, the porcelain stove pumping out a dry, relentless heat that never quite banished the dampness from the walls. Arthur rubbed his hands together and sat at his desk, where the unmarked translation exercises of his pupils waited in a tidy stack. He had been in Valdbourg for six years, teaching English to the sons and daughters of merchants and minor nobility. It was a respectable exile, but an exile nonetheless.

He was roused from his melancholy by a sharp, authoritative knock. He opened the door to find a woman standing on the landing, her features obscured by a voluminous velvet cloak and a bonnet tied tightly beneath her chin. She carried a leather travelling case, scuffed at the corners.

“Mr. Arthur Pendelton?” she asked, her voice carrying the clipped, unmistakable vowels of the Home Counties. It was a sound so alien in this city of guttural stops and rolling r’s that Arthur felt a sudden, painful tightening in his chest.

“I am he,” Arthur replied, momentarily struck dumb by the sheer Englishness of her.

“How wonderful,” she said, stepping forward as if bidden. “I am Mrs. Georgina Mowbray. Your cousin. I have come a tremendously long way to find you, Arthur. May I come in?”

He stood aside, his manners taking over where his wits had failed him. She swept into the room, immediately dominating the space. She untied her bonnet, shaking out a mane of dark, slightly dishevelled hair, and looked around the apartment with an expression of polite assessment. “How perfectly continental,” she said, gesturing to the heavy oak furniture and the lace curtains. “You have made a true home for yourself here. Now, I am perishing for a cup of tea. Is it too much to ask?”

Arthur busied himself with the small brass kettle on the hob, his mind racing. He knew his father’s family originated in Surrey, but the branches were numerous and tangled. He had left England under a cloud of financial disappointment, and the connection to his relatives had withered into silence over the years. Yet, this woman claimed kinship with an effortless certainty.

“I must confess, Mrs. Mowbray,” he said as he spooned dark Valdbourg tea into the pot, “that I am at a loss. My father rarely spoke of his siblings, and I fear I am ignorant of your particular branch.”

“Oh, the Surrey Pendeltons are a dreadfully secretive lot,” she said, settling into the single armchair by the stove. “Your father, Edward, was my grandmother’s nephew. That makes us... well, I never was very good at the precise arithmetic of consanguinity. Let us simply say cousins. My mother was a Vance, you know, from the Wiltshire Vances. She corresponded with your aunt Agnes for years. Before she passed, my mother told me the most extraordinary stories about your grandfather’s orchard. She said he had a particular variety of pear that he insisted on calling the ‘Vance Russet’, though it was entirely common. She also told me about your elder brother, Thomas. Such a tragedy, his chest. He always coughed so terribly in the spring, didn’t he? Even before the dampness of Oxford claimed him.”

Arthur set the teapot down on the table with a clatter. He had never spoken of Thomas to anyone in Valdbourg. The memory of his brother’s weak chest, the terrible, wet coughing fits that echoed through their childhood home, and his eventual death at Oxford were private griefs, locked away.

“You knew about Thomas,” Arthur said, his voice barely a whisper.

“I know a great deal, Arthur. The Vance women always do. We keep the family ledger, so to speak.” She smiled, and it was a charming, deeply sympathetic smile. She accepted the cup of tea, her gloved fingers brushing his. “You have your mother’s eyes, you know. Dark, very earnest. It is a comfort to see them looking back at me. I have felt so adrift since my husband passed. The continent is a lonely place for an Englishwoman alone.”

They talked for two hours. Georgina was a gifted conversationalist. She did not merely speak; she drew him out, making him feel as though his own mundane existence in Valdbourg was a subject of genuine fascination. She asked about the university, about the Bohemian coffee houses, about the strictures of the Catholic society. In return, she offered tidbits of home. She spoke of the damp, grey skies of London, of the smell of coal smoke and wet wool on the docks, of a political climate that was turning restless. She spoke of the Surrey countryside, of the way the frost looked on the heath. Arthur drank it in, starved for it. For a few hours, the stifling room on Sirkova Street was transformed into a parlour in Dorking.

When the cathedral bell tolled nine, Georgina set down her empty cup and sighed. “I must be candid with you, Arthur. I have not come merely for the pleasure of your company, though it has been immense.”

Arthur felt a cold trickle of apprehension. “How may I help you?”

“I am in a desperately awkward position,” she said, her eyes bright with unshed tears. It was a masterful display, just enough emotion to evoke sympathy without descending into hysterics. “My late husband left me a small estate, but the solicitors are... well, they are continental solicitors. There is a dispute over the deeds, and my funds are entirely tied up in the courts. I have taken lodgings at the Golden Stag on the river, but the landlord is a brute. I need a temporary loan. Just for a fortnight, until my remittances from London arrive. I need two hundred florins.”
