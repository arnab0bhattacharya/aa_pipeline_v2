The rain had not ceased for three days, and the parsonage at Sowerby Bridge wore its November damp like a second skin. Clara Marsh stood at the kitchen window, watching the water trace crooked paths down the glass, and thought about what her brother had told her that morning. The words had come awkwardly, which was unusual for Edmund, a man accustomed to shaping difficult sentences into something a congregation could swallow. He had cleared his throat twice. He had stared at the toe of his boot, worn thin at the seam where the leather met the sole. Then he had said it. He was to marry. Miss Helen Barlow, daughter of the wool merchant over the hill in Shibden. A good woman. A woman of means. The wedding would be before spring.

Clara had known, of course. She had seen the way Miss Barlow’s gloves lingered on the pew rail after services, how she stayed to speak with Edmund in the churchyard while the wind plucked at her bonnet. Clara had poured the tea when Miss Barlow visited, had offered biscuits and retired to the parlour with her mending while their voices murmured through the wall. She had known, but knowing and hearing are different creatures, and the words, once spoken, settled on her like snowfall—quiet, cold, and heavy.

She turned from the window. The kitchen was spotless. It was always spotless. She had made it so for nine years, since their mother died and Edmund returned from Cambridge to take up the curacy at St. James’s. He had been twenty-four then, raw and earnest, and she had been twenty-one, already skilled in the arts of household management that their mother had taught her without ever calling them arts. Cooking, cleaning, mending, preserving, brewing, accounting for every penny of Edmund’s modest stipend so that there was always bread and coal and a shilling for the poor box on Sunday. She had done this without complaint because it was her place, and because she loved her brother, and because there was nothing else for her to do.

Now she was thirty, and there was nothing else for her to do, and the one thing she had been doing was about to be taken from her.

She crossed the hall to the parlour, where the fire had burned low. She knelt and fed it another piece of coal, watching the embers brighten and catch. The room was modestly furnished—a horsehair sofa, a walnut desk that had been their father’s, a shelf of sermons and devotional works. She had polished every surface, beaten every cushion, washed every curtain. This room was hers in the way that a rented field is a farmer’s—not by ownership but by labour. Miss Barlow would preside over it soon. Miss Barlow, who was twenty-three and pretty and had a dowry of four hundred pounds, would sit on the horsehair sofa and arrange flowers on the walnut desk and never once wonder how the laundress managed to remove the soot stains from the net curtains, because Clara would be gone.

Where, she did not know.

She stood and went to the desk. She opened the drawer where Edmund kept the household accounts, the ledger in her own neat hand. She turned the pages slowly. The weekly entries stretched back years—milk, bread, candles, soap, coal, meat, the occasional luxury of oranges when Edmund had a cold. She knew the price of everything in the parish. She knew which butcher gave honest weight and which greengrocer watered his cabbages. She knew the names of the children in the Sunday school and which families were behind on their rent and which widows needed a quiet parcel of soup left at their door. She knew everything about Sowerby Bridge that could be learned by living at its centre and paying attention, and none of it was worth money to anyone.

She closed the ledger and pressed her fingers flat against the desk.

That evening, Edmund came to her in the kitchen while she was preparing supper. He stood in the doorway with the look of a man who has rehearsed what he is about to say and fears he will get it wrong. She turned from the pot and waited.

“Clara, you must know that I would never turn you out. This will always be your home.”

She looked at him. His hair was thinning at the crown. She had noticed it first six months ago and had said nothing, because telling a man his hair is falling out is not a sister’s duty, or at least not one she had been taught. She felt a sudden, unreasonable wave of tenderness, followed by a deeper wave of grief, because tenderness was not enough and never had been.

“That is kind of you, Edmund.”

“Helen is a good-hearted girl. She will welcome you here. There is room enough.”

Room enough. Three bedrooms, one occupied by Edmund, one by Clara, one kept for the occasional visit of the bishop’s inspector. Yes, there was room. But there was a difference between having a room and having a place, and he did not understand this, and she could not explain it without sounding bitter, and she did not want to sound bitter because she was not bitter, not exactly. She was frightened.

“I will think on what is best,” she said, and turned back to the pot.

She did not sleep that night. She lay in her narrow bed and listened to the wind and the rain and the distant clatter of the mill wheel down in the valley, turning and turning. She thought about her options, which were these: she could stay and become the spinster sister, the shadow in the corner, the woman who is introduced at dinner parties with a faint air of apology. She could seek a position as a governess or companion, but she had no references beyond her brother’s household, and the world was full of women younger and better educated competing for the same posts.
