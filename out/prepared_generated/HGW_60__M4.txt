The thing was in every evening paper, and by six o’clock it had driven the customary interests of Mellchester entirely out of the popular mind. A telegram from the Royal Observatory, repeated with varying degrees of accuracy and embellishment, announced that a large meteoric body, first observed some weeks since in the constellation of Auriga, would probably encounter the earth’s atmosphere at or about midnight, and that the point of impact, if impact there were, might lie anywhere within a hundred miles of the Severn estuary. The evening edition of the *Mellchester Guardian* printed the word “probably” in large type, and beneath it, in type still larger, the words “THE END OF THE WORLD?” with a note of interrogation that somehow contrived to be more alarming than any assertion.

By nine o’clock the Close was full of people. They stood in little knots upon the grass beneath the great west front, and looked up at the sky with the solemn, self-conscious faces of persons who have paid for a seat at a conjuring entertainment and are resolved to miss nothing. The lamps were lit, and their yellow glow competed absurdly with the vast and tranquil twilight. Now and then some enthusiast would announce a moving point of light, and a hundred necks would be craned in unison; but the point of light would turn out to be a star, or a bat, or the lamp of a belated cyclist upon the Godshill road.

In the house of Mr. Aubrey Wainwright, solicitor, of Number 9, St. Chad’s Gate, the evening had taken a turn that was singularly unlike the cheerful bustle of the streets. The Wainwrights were a family of the solid professional sort, who dined at seven and thought it vulgar to be demonstrative. Yet here they were at half-past nine, gathered in the drawing-room with the tea-things untouched and the fire burning high, and a curious air of expectancy upon them all that had nothing whatever to do with the comet or the meteor or whatever one called it.

Mr. Wainwright himself had begun it. He had put down his paper, and cleared his throat twice, and said that in view of the possibility—he laid a polite emphasis on the word—of a considerable disturbance tonight, he thought it only right that certain matters, long since settled in his own mind, should be made clear to them all. He was a precise, grey little man, with a habit of holding his pince-nez between finger and thumb as though it were a document requiring careful examination.

“I have,” he said, “for some years past, been the possessor of a small sum, invested in the name of my sister, Miss Wainwright, which yields about forty pounds a year. It was my mother’s money, and it was understood—by me, at least—that it should revert to the family estate upon my death. I have not told you of it, my dear, because I feared you would think me mercenary. I am sorry.”

Mrs. Wainwright, a large, soft, comfortable woman, went very red, and then very pale. “Forty pounds,” she said, in a small voice. “And I have been economising upon the fish.”

“It was wrong of me,” said Mr. Wainwright, with a kind of gloomy satisfaction.

“It was,” said his sister, Miss Wainwright, who was of the angular and uncompromising type. “But I have not been idle either. I have known for eleven years that my brother’s partnership with Mr. Fennick was dissolved, not because of Mr. Fennick’s health, as we were told, but because of a disagreement about the conduct of the Godshill estate business. I have never mentioned it. I have let you suppose me ignorant. I have enjoyed it, I am afraid.”

There was a silence. The clock upon the mantelpiece ticked with an offensive cheerfulness.

“Well,” said Mrs. Wainwright, and her voice had a curious, brittle quality, “since we are all being so very candid, I will tell you that I have known about the forty pounds for six years. I saw the bank-book. And I have known about the partnership for nine. And I have said nothing, because I thought it better that we should go on as we were, and because I did not wish to be the one to make a scene. I have been making scenes in my own mind for years. I hope you are satisfied.”

The son, Mr. Cyril Wainwright, a young man of twenty-four, who had been sitting with his hands in his pockets and his eyes upon the carpet, now looked up with a curious expression of relief.

“I am glad,” he said, “that we are doing this. I have something to say too. I have not been going to the office as I said. For eight months I have been attending the School of Art in the evenings, and I have sold two drawings to a firm in London that makes Christmas cards. I have thirty-one pounds ten in the Post Office Savings Bank, and I have not the slightest intention of becoming a solicitor.”

His father stared at him. “But the practice,” he said. “The practice.”

“The practice,” said Cyril, “may go to the devil.”

It was at this point that the meteor, or whatever it was, became visible. It came out of the north-west, a point of greenish-white fire, moving with a swiftness that was somehow more terrible than any noise. It grew as they watched it—grew from a pin’s head to a pea, from a pea to a cricket-ball, from a cricket-ball to a monstrous, blazing thing that seemed to fill the whole upper heaven. The room, which had been dim, was suddenly flooded with a light that was not the light of the fire or the lamps, a light that cast no shadows and made every object in the room look strange and thin and unreal.
