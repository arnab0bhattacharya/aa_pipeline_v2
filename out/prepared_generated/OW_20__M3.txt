The scandal had begun, as all true scandals must, in a drawing-room. Lady Hemlock was the first to mention it, holding up a volume bound in a shade of yellow so vivid it seemed to constitute a moral offence in itself. She declared the book to be entirely improper, which is the modern method of recommending a work to one’s friends. The volume in question was a novel, bearing the title *The Velvet Sin*, and London, which is ordinarily so exhausted by its own intellect that it requires a whole season to recover from the mere effort of breathing, had found in its pages a fresh and delightful excuse for conversation.

It was a book that no respectable person could be seen reading in the daylight. Consequently, everyone had read it by midnight. The narrative concerned a duchess of dubious virtue, a poet of unquestionable vice, and a quantity of poisoned fruit that served no purpose other than to provide the author with an opportunity to describe the dinner service at great length. The dialogue was entirely epigrammatic. The characters said nothing that was not profoundly shocking, and they said it with the practiced ease of people who have dined too often in the same houses. The morality of the thing was deplorable, which is to say it was perfectly correct.

Mrs. Arthur Pemberton had purchased the book on a Tuesday, concealing it beneath a bolt of muslin, and had read it by the guttering light of a single candle, feeling that she was committing a sin of the most agreeable description. By Wednesday she was entirely familiar with the text, and by Thursday she found herself quoting it in the drawing-room of her house in Kensington, always with the preliminary remark that one really must not repeat such dreadful things.

It was on the Friday that the catastrophe occurred. Mr. Arthur Pemberton, a man whose respectability was so aggressively pronounced that it amounted almost to a social handicap, had been examining the flyleaf of the volume while his wife was engaged in pouring out the tea. He turned pale, which was an unusual occurrence, as his complexion naturally inclined towards a redness that spoke of port wine and an untroubled conscience.

"Good heavens, Eleanor," he said. "This book is dedicated to the silver tea-service. 'To the urn that warmed my earliest sorrows, and the salver that bore my first disillusion.' It is our urn. It is our salver. No one but someone intimately acquainted with our pantry could write such a line."

Mrs. Pemberton set down the teacup with a hand that trembled, though she trembled with a certain pleasurable excitement. "Are you quite sure, Arthur?"

"I am positive. Who else could know of the dent in the salver? Who else could know that the urn requires a particular persuasion before it consents to boil? There is only one person in this house who has engaged in a lifelong struggle with that urn."

They looked at one another. The truth was upon them, heavy and undeniable. The author of *The Velvet Sin*, the book that had made London gasp and shudder and whisper with delight, was their butler, Phipps.

Phipps had been in their service for fifteen years. He was a man of imposing respectability, with a countenance as unreadable as a closed Bible and a manner that suggested he had long since forgiven the universe for existing. He poured wine with the solemnity of a priest performing a sacrament, and he carried the letters to the post with an air of moral instruction. That such a man should have penned a novel containing a chapter on the illicit passions of a duchess was a contradiction so exquisite that it seemed almost an act of justice.

"We must dismiss him," said Mr. Pemberton, though his voice lacked its usual conviction. "A man who writes such things cannot remain beneath my roof. The very foundations of society are threatened."

"But if we dismiss him," Mrs. Pemberton replied, "we must provide a reason. And to provide a reason, we must admit that we have read the book. We cannot tell him we are dismissing him for writing a novel we have not read. That would be unjust, and nothing is more inconvenient than an unjust dismissal, except, perhaps, a just one."

"You are perfectly right, Eleanor. If it were known that we had read *The Velvet Sin*, we should be ruined. We should be asked everywhere, of course, but we should be ruined."

"To be asked everywhere is the definition of social success, Arthur."

"It is also the definition of moral failure. The two are so frequently indistinguishable. But what are we to do? We cannot keep him. Every time he hands me the port, I shall think of the duchess. Every time he closes the curtains, I shall think of the poet. The man’s very presence will be a commentary on my own existence."

"We must keep him," said Mrs. Pemberton. "We cannot dismiss him without confessing our sin, and to confess a sin is always a mistake. It robs the sin of its charm and gives the listener an unmerited advantage. Besides, I have heard a rumour that there is to be a sequel."

Mr. Pemberton rose from his chair and walked to the window. The square garden of Kensington lay before him, green and respectable, a monument to the English talent for making nature appear as though it had been hired for the occasion. He turned back to his wife.

"A sequel, Eleanor?"

"They say it is to be called *The Gilded Shame*. It is said to be even more dreadful than the first. They say there is a chapter devoted entirely to the moral deterioration of a bishop, and another that describes a duel fought with poisoned cuff-links. The women of London are already frantic with anticipation. The men are pretending not to be."
