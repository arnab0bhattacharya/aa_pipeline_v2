The air in the drawing room of Mr. and Mrs. Ferrers’ estate, a stately but somewhat damp residence in Hertfordshire, was thick with the scent of lavender and the heavier scent of ill-concealed anxiety. Eleanor Vance sat near the window, her fan folded neatly in her lap, her eyes fixed on a spot on the carpet where a single dust mote danced in the shaft of afternoon light. It was a safe object of contemplation, far removed from the dangerous vicinity of the conversation happening in the center of the room. There, Mrs. Vance, resplendent in a gown of blue silk that seemed to catch every stray ray of the dying sun, was engaged in a lively discussion with Mrs. Ferrers regarding the price of mutton and the moral character of the local parson.

Eleanor’s peace was of short duration. Her mother, having exhausted the virtues of the mutton, turned her gaze with a sudden, terrifying brightness toward Eleanor. It was the look of a general surveying a field before a battle, or perhaps of a merchant displaying a prize item.

“Mrs. Ferrers,” Mrs. Vance began, her voice rising with an energy that usually preceded either a scandal or a purchase, “I trust you have heard of the pleasant turn of affairs in our family?”

Mrs. Ferrers, a woman of considerable age and proportionally little curiosity, inclined her head. “Indeed, Mrs. Vance. I am all attention. Is it of a healthful nature?”

“It is of a matrimonial nature,” Mrs. Vance declared, sweeping a hand toward Eleanor. “As I have been hoping to announce for some time, my daughter Eleanor has received a declaration of attachment from Mr. Arthur Pym, which we are to consider as an engagement of the most serious kind.”

The silence that followed was not the comfortable quiet of a well-run house, but the sharp, jagged silence of a held breath. Eleanor felt the heat rise instantly to her cheeks, a physical burning that she feared was visible to every soul in the room. She looked up, and there, standing by the fireplace with a book of poetry in his hand, was Mr. Pym. His face was pale, and his eyes, though fixed on the fire, seemed to be looking through it, into a void of profound confusion. He had not proposed. He had not even, to Eleanor’s certain knowledge, spoken to her in private for the last three weeks, save for a polite inquiry about the weather at the last assembly.

Mr. Ferrers, who had been eating a tart with the methodical precision of a man who enjoyed his meals, paused with a forkful of apple in the air. “Pym?” he said, his voice low. “The young solicitor? He is not a man of fortune, is he? I thought he had little more than a small practice and a large library.”

“He is a man of great principle,” Mrs. Vance insisted, though her voice wavered slightly under the weight of Mr. Ferrers’ skepticism. “And principles, in these days, are of more value than mere gold. It is a match of the heart, Mrs. Ferrers. I have long observed the regard he bears for my daughter, and my daughter, being a sensible girl, has returned it.”

Eleanor’s heart sank into the floorboards. To return it was a lie of such magnitude that it threatened to swallow the whole of her integrity. Yet to correct her mother in such a public setting, before the eyes of their neighbors and the potential patronage of the Ferrers family, was a social impossibility. To do so would be to destroy her mother’s vanity, to invite ridicule, and to make herself a target for the very gossip she had spent her life avoiding. She was trapped between the rock of her mother’s fabrication and the hard place of her own reputation.

She looked at Mr. Pym. He had turned now, and his eyes met hers. There was no warmth in them, only a stark, cold surprise that mirrored her own mortification. He gave the smallest, almost imperceptible shake of his head. It was a plea. *Save me,* it seemed to say. *Or at least, let me escape this folly without being made a fool.*

Mrs. Ferrers, who had been listening with the polite inattention of one who knew the price of mutton but not of hearts, now leaned forward. “I am surprised, Mrs. Vance. I had thought Mr. Pym a solitary man. I remember he sat alone at the last harvest supper, reading that dreadful book on metaphysics.”

“He is a man of deep thought,” Mrs. Vance said, her voice gaining a defensive sharpness. “And depth of thought is often mistaken for coldness by those who lack the capacity to feel. I have watched them, Mrs. Ferrers. The way he looks at her. It is unmistakable.”

Eleanor felt a cold sweat break out on her forehead. She could feel the eyes of the other guests—the cousins, the aunt, the visiting clergyman—drilling into her. They were waiting for the confirmation, the smile, the nod. They wanted the story to be true because it was convenient, because it tied up a loose end in the social fabric of the county.

She looked at Mr. Pym again. He had set his book down. His hands were clasped behind his back, his knuckles white. He was not looking at his mother, nor at Mrs. Ferrers, but directly at her. His expression was one of tragic resignation. He knew she was in the same predicament. He was not a hero who would storm forward and declare his innocence, for that would only add to the chaos. He was a man of sense, or at least of pride, and he would not be the center of such a public farce.

Eleanor took a breath. It was a small, quiet breath, but it felt like the intake of a drowning woman. She stood up. The movement was sudden, drawing all eyes to her. She smoothed the skirt of her dress, a futile gesture against the storm of social expectation.
