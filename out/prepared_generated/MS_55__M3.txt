The tempest, which had been gathering its terrors during the whole of that autumnal evening, burst forth in its fullest fury just as the night closed in upon the ancient and secluded town of Knareswood. I sat alone in my study, the heavy curtains of crimson velvet drawn closely against the blast, which howled through the antique casements with a sound like the wail of a lost spirit; yet the storm without was but a faint emblem of the tumultuous discord that reigned within my bosom. I, Edgar Lennard, a man whose name had for twenty years been a synonym for unswerving probity and unblemished honour in that northern county; I, whose wealth had given me an undisputed ascendency in the province, found myself suddenly transformed into the most miserable and agitated of mortals. The proud fabric of my domestic peace, which I had believed founded on a rock, was crumbling into dust beneath my feet, and I was called upon to decide whether I would plunge the partner of my life into an abyss of despair, or whether I would expose my own grey head to the shafts of unforgiving scandal.

It was not three hours since the intelligence had been communicated to me. My wife, the gentle and exalted Eleanor, whose serene disposition and unwearied kindness had shed a perpetual sunshine over our hearth, had been seized, as I entered the house, with a paroxysm of terror so vehement that it seemed to border upon the very confines of distraction. She had thrown herself at my feet, and with tears and burning supplications had confessed a tale, which, though it had slept for more than twenty years in the silence of her own heart, was now awakened to threaten our destruction. Before our marriage, in the early and thoughtless dawn of her youth, she had been united to a man of degraded principles, from whom she had believed herself separated by death; and by him she had borne a child, which, to conceal her shame, she had consigned to the care of strangers in a remote district of the west. That child, now grown to manhood, had come to Knareswood. He had traced his mother to her retired and honoured home, and he was even then waiting in the lower apartments of my house to demand the recognition which his wrongs called upon him to enforce.

When Eleanor had concluded her confession, she had looked upon me with an expression of anguish so deep, so unutterable, that it seemed to tear the very strings of my heart. "My husband," she had exclaimed, "my honoured, my beloved Edgar! Do not cast me from you! I have deceived you, I own it, most grievously, most culpably; but the madness of fear urged me to the deed. I was young, I was friendless, and the recollection of my errors was so terrible, that I hoped, by burying them in eternal silence, to atone for the past. And now he is come—he claims me as his mother—he will publish my shame to the world, and your name, your spotless name, must be involved in my ruin! O, Edgar, if you have loved me, if you have known the peace I have striven to diffuse around you, protect me, save me, or I shall die beneath the weight of my affliction!"

She had then been conveyed to her apartment, where she now lay, the victim of a burning fever, watched by her terrified women, and calling wildly upon my name in the delirium of her sufferings. And I—I was left to encounter the storm alone; to decide whether I should surrender the pride of my life, the respect of my fellow-citizens, and the fair fame which I had toiled through long years to acquire, to the preservation of her tranquillity; or whether, with a stern and selfish virtue, I should abandon her to the consequences of her own concealment, and stand forth before the world as the severe and unoffending judge of her imposture.

Could I hesitate? Could I balance for a moment between the world's opinion and the life's happiness of the gentle being who had confided in my protection? And yet, when I reflected upon the nature of the sacrifice, my soul recoiled with an agony not to be described. I was not young; I had passed the meridian of my days, and the respect of my neighbours, the high consideration in which I had been held, had become necessary to my existence. To be pointed at as the dupe of a woman's artifice; to see the finger of scorn levelled at the stately house which I had tenanted in honour; to feel that the merchants of the county, who had been accustomed to yield to my decision as to an oracle, should now whisper of my domestic disgrace, was a bitter, an almost insupportable trial. But then the voice of Eleanor, as she lay upon her sick-bed, appealed to my heart with a power which silenced every worldly consideration. I had loved her with the deepest, the most confiding affection; I had believed her worthy of all reverence; and though the discovery of her deceit had given a shock to my confidence, the remembrance of her unwearied excellence, and the conviction that she had erred only under the pressure of overwhelming terror, subdued my resentment, and left only compassion and anxiety in my bosom.

I determined at last to seek the stranger. I found him in a small apartment, seated by the fire, which threw a fitful and melancholy glare across his features. He was pale and attenuated, bearing the evident marks of penury and hardship, but there was a dignity in his air, and a stern, though sorrowful expression in his eye, that interested me in spite of myself. I felt, as I looked upon him, that he was no vulgar adventurer, come to extort money by the threat of exposure; but an injured man, claiming his right to a mother's name, and the restitution of a station which had been wrested from him by the errors of others.
