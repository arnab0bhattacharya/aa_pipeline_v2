It is in the nature of a remote moorland parish, where the jagged geography seems to repel the wider world rather than invite it, that the arrival of any stranger must necessarily operate as a profound disturbance upon the local equilibrium. When young Mr. Marlow, a curate of somewhat fragile constitution and fervid intellect, was sent into the upper regions of Blackcarr Vale to assist the aging vicar of Thornhaven, he carried with him not only his clerical duties but a quiet cargo of German philosophy and English poetry, wholly unsuited to the prevailing atmospheric dampness. The mists of Thornhaven, which clung to the hillsides with the tenacity of a lingering grief, proved fatal to his chest. Within a month of his arrival, a feverish catarrh had bound him to his bed, and the economical arrangements of his lodgings being unequal to the demands of a protracted illness, he was carried in a farmer’s cart to the stone-built homestead of Silas Brackenthwaite, whose family had grazed the upper slopes of the Dark Peak for generations.

It was in this low-raftered farmhouse, smelling of woodsmoke and dried herbs, that Mr. Marlow lay through the slow weeks of October, his physical vitality reduced to a mere flickering, while his mind retained that restless, speculative tendency which is often the torment of a body compelled to inaction. He was tended with a blunt, ungrudging efficiency by the Brackenthwaites. Silas himself, a man of few words and deep, agricultural anxieties, regarded the curate as one might regard a delicate piece of mechanism—a thing to be pitied for its lack of sturdy construction, yet to be respected for its inherent purpose. The farmer’s wife, Mrs. Brackenthwaite, administered bitter decoctions and broths with a severe tenderness, her dialect a dense weave of ancient Saxon survivals. But it was the farmer’s daughter, Lettice, who became the curate’s primary nurse, and upon whose quiet, provincial consciousness the presence of the invalid was to leave a mark as indelible as the moorland frost.

Lettice Brackenthwaite was a woman of three-and-twenty, possessing that ripe, unselfconscious physical health which is the natural product of wind-swept labors and a simple diet. She had never traveled further than the market town of Buxton, and her mental horizons were strictly demarcated by the stone walls of the valley. Yet there was in her large, grey eyes a certain gravity of attention—a capacity for silent observation—that suggested a soil deeper than the surface heath. When she sat by Mr. Marlow’s bedside, winding yarn or stitching, she listened to the low mutterings of his fever with an alertness that was not mere animal sympathy, but the stirring of an intellect unused to finding any nutriment in its customary environment.

As the fever abated, leaving the curate as weak and pale as a winter birch, his mind began to return to its habitual pastures. He requested, in a voice still frail, that some of his books be fetched from his lodgings. When the small crate of volumes arrived, Lettice unpacked them with a reverence born of awe. She had been accustomed solely to the Family Bible and an almanac; the mere sight of these slender, tightly printed volumes seemed to her to represent a world of arcane privilege.

Observing her timid curiosity, Mr. Marlow, who possessed a gentle, didactic nature, offered her a volume of essays. "I fear they are poor entertainment for one accustomed to more active pursuits," he said, his voice barely above a whisper. "But perhaps, in these long evenings, they might serve to pass the time."

"I dunna know as I can mak' owt on't," Lettice replied, her Derbyshire vowels broad with hesitation. "It's fine print, and summat heavy i' th' hand for them as is used to a milk-pail."

"Try only to read a little," he urged gently. "Do not be discouraged if the meaning does not present itself immediately. A difficult book is like a moor in the mist; one must walk in it a while before the paths become clear."

It was with this metaphorical encouragement that Lettice embarked upon her solitary education. She read by the light of the tallow candle, her brow furrowed, her lips moving unconsciously over the complex clauses. The immediate effect of this reading was not an illumination, but a profound discomfort. The books spoke of moral struggles, of historical destinies, of a universe of thought that extended infinitely beyond the parish bounds. She felt a sudden, sharp consciousness of her own ignorance—not as a shame, but as a constriction. She looked around the farmhouse kitchen, at the smooth-worn flags and the copper pans, and felt for the first time that these familiar objects were but a small, darkened corner in a vast hall of human experience.

This intellectual awakening was accompanied by a subtle shift in her feelings toward the curate. She no longer saw him merely as a helpless invalid, but as the gateway to this wider realm. She watched him as he sat propped against his pillows, his face still bearing the translucent pallor of convalescence, and she perceived in his features a nobility that heretofore she had associated only with the stone angels in the parish church. He, in turn, observed her slow, earnest struggles with the texts, and recognized in her untrained intelligence a rare, receptive power—a massive capacity for belief, which, lacking any adequate object, had hitherto expended itself in unquestioning adherence to provincial custom.

One evening, as the November rain beat a steady, mournful tattoo against the lattice window, Mr. Marlow asked her what she had gleaned from her reading.

"It's main hard, sir," she said, lifting her eyes from the page, "to think as th' world is so vast, and we here, like a drop o' water in a puddle on th' moor. I read o' men that fought for notions—aye, notions that mun be true, or they'd not ha' suffered for 'em. But here, we just farm, and eat, and sleep. It makes me feel as if I were but a servant in a great house, and had never seen the master's face."
