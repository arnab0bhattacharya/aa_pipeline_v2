It was a truth universally acknowledged, though rarely spoken aloud in the drawing rooms of Shropshire, that a mother-in-law’s affection, when unreciprocated, tends to curdle into a substance both sticky and difficult to wash from the conscience. Mrs. Harcourt, residing in the shadow of her son’s considerable fortune and his equally considerable new wife, had long since learned this particular variety of domestic sorrow. She lived at Oakhaven, a house of respectable proportions and even more respectable silence, where the walls seemed to echo with the memory of better days, and the carpets were thick enough to muffle the sound of a retreating dignity.

Her son, Mr. Arthur Harcourt, was a man of agreeable manners and limited imagination, who had married Miss Lydia Thorne with the same enthusiasm one might display for a new edition of the *Gentleman’s Magazine*—with a polite nod, a brief perusal, and a subsequent assumption that one had fully understood the content. Lydia, however, was not a magazine to be casually perused. She was a woman of few words, a steady gaze, and an abiding preference for solitude, which Mrs. Harcourt interpreted, with the acute sensitivity of the vulnerable, as a deliberate rejection of her maternal warmth.

The morning began, as most mornings did, with Mrs. Harcourt’s anxious survey of the house. She noted with satisfaction that the breakfast things were laid out with precision, the silver polished to a mirror-like shine that reflected her own pale, worried face. She then proceeded to the library, where she found Lydia seated in the deep window-seat, a volume of poetry in her lap, and her eyes fixed on a point somewhere beyond the glass, or perhaps within the text.

“Good morning, my dear Lydia,” said Mrs. Harcourt, her voice pitched to a level of softness that she had perfected over the last six months, a tone designed to suggest both tenderness and a mild reproach for being startled. “I hope you have slept well? The air was rather cool this morning, but I hope it did not disturb your rest. Arthur mentioned that you might prefer the room warmed, so I have spoken to the housekeeper about the fire.”

Lydia did not look up. She turned a page, the movement slow and deliberate. “It is very warm in here, Mrs. Harcourt. I am quite comfortable.”

Mrs. Harcourt blinked, a flicker of confusion crossing her face before she smoothed it away. “Warm? I am sure it is not. I felt a draught as I came in. But perhaps you are of a different constitution. It is a pity, for I had prepared a small breakfast. I had the cook make those little toast points you seemed to like last week, with the honey from the orchard. I thought it might be nice to have something light before the day begins. I do hope I have not overstepped by assuming you would care for it.”

Lydia’s eyes finally moved from the book to her mother-in-law. There was no anger in them, nor any particular warmth. There was only a clear, unyielding patience, like that of a stone enduring a stream. “I am not hungry, thank you. I have been reading.”

“Reading,” repeated Mrs. Harcourt, as if the word were a foreign coin she was trying to assess. “Yes, I see. You are so devoted to your books. It is a fine thing, a literary mind. Arthur has always admired your taste. I only wish I had the same attention for such things. I find my mind wanders, if I am honest. But I am glad to see you engaged. It is so much better than idleness. I do hope you will allow me to bring you the toast, though? You need not say no. I have already sent for it. It would be a waste to have it cold.”

Lydia sighed, a small, almost inaudible exhalation. “I will take it, if it is already prepared. But I would prefer to be left to my reading, Mrs. Harcourt. I find I can concentrate better when there is no one speaking.”

Mrs. Harcourt bowed, a small, stiff movement of the head. “Of course, my dear. I did not mean to intrude. I only wished to ensure you were comfortable. It is a mother’s duty, I suppose, to look after her children, even when they are no longer children. Arthur is so busy with his affairs that he cannot always attend to the small details. I feel I must fill that space, or else it will be left empty. And an empty space is a dangerous thing, don’t you think?”

Lydia returned her gaze to the book. Mrs. Harcourt lingered for a moment, her hand hovering over the back of the chair as if she might offer to brush off a non-existent speck of dust. She looked at the title of the book, *The Lyrical Ballads*, and felt a pang of inadequacy. She did not know if she had ever read it. She suspected she had not. She wanted to ask about it, to show an interest that might bridge the chasm between them, but the words died in her throat. What could she say? *I find the rhythm uneven?* *I do not understand the metaphors?* Such admissions felt like exposures.

Instead, she said, “It is a very fine book, I am told. I have heard it is somewhat... unconventional. But I am sure it is rewarding.”

Lydia did not answer. She turned another page.

Mrs. Harcourt retreated to the doorway, her heart heavy with the sense that she had failed again. She had offered food, warmth, conversation, and companionship, and received in return only the silent, closed door of a book. Was it because she was too much? Too loud? Too old? She thought of the rumors that had been circulating in the village, whispers about the young wife who never smiled, who rarely left her room, who seemed to regard the household with a detached, almost clinical indifference. Mrs. Harcourt had always believed those rumors to be exaggerations, but standing there, watching the back of her daughter-in-law’s head, she began to wonder if they were simply the truth, stripped of its malice.
