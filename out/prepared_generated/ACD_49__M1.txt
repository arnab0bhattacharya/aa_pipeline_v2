The rain was falling in a steady, melancholy stream against the windows of the Royal Naval Hospital at Haslar, washing the grey walls of Portsmouth into a watercolour blur. Within the ward, the air was heavy with the smell of carbolic and wet wool. It was there, in the corner bed by the door, that the man they called the Marquesan lay dying.

He was not a Marquesan by birth, being a Lancashire man named Broderick who had shipped out twenty years prior and returned with skin like tanned leather and a tongue that clacked in a strange pidgin when the fever took him. He had been brought in three days prior with a lung complaint that had turned to pneumonia. Now, his breath came in shallow, bubbling rasps.

Father Lacey, the hospital chaplain, sat by the bedside, his black cassock folded neatly over his knees. He was a man of practical compassion, and he knew that the end was near.

"Is there no one, Broderick?" the priest asked gently, leaning forward as the sailor’s eyes fluttered open. "No one in England you wish to see?"

The sailor’s hand, gnarled and scarred by years of rigging and tar, clutched convulsively at the counterpane. In his fist, he held a small object he had refused to relinquish even for the doctor’s examination. It was a piece of ivory, carved into the shape of a shark’s tooth, etched with the crude, geometric patterns of the Pacific islands.

"The debt," Broderick croaked. His eyes were wide with a terror that seemed to have little to do with the proximity of the grave. "He owes. The tooth... the token of the blood-price."

"Who owes, my son?"

"Hawthorne," the sailor gasped. "Alderman Hawthorne. Shrewsbury. He knows the... the dark water."

"Aldman Hawthorne?" Lacey repeated, his brow furrowing. He knew the name. It was a name of weight in the Midlands, attached to woolen mills and philanthropic subscriptions. "You must be mistaken, Broderick. An Alderman of Shrewsbury?"

"The same," the sailor hissed, his strength surging for a final, terrible moment. "The debt must be paid. Or the sea will take him as it took the others." He pressed the ivory token into the priest’s palm. It was strangely cold. "Give him this. Tell him... tell him the shark is waiting."

Broderick fell back against the pillows, his eyes fixed on the ceiling. He did not speak again.

Lacey was a man of God, but he was also a man of order. He did not believe in the superstitions of the heathen islands, but he believed in the sanctity of a dying man’s word. Two days later, having arranged for the burial, he took the train north to Shrewsbury.

The town of Millbourne, a prosperous suburb of the ancient borough, was a place of solid red brick and well-kept gardens. The house of Alderman Silas Hawthorne was the most imposing of them all, a Victorian monstrosity of gables and bay windows that spoke of old money and newer industry.

Lacey was shown into a drawing-room that was stiflingly hot despite the autumn chill. The Alderman was a heavy-set man with a ruddy complexion, a white waistcoat that strained against his paunch, and small, shrewd eyes that darted about the room like those of a cornered rat.

"I am Father Lacey from the hospital at Portsmouth," the priest said, standing stiffly. "I have come regarding a sailor named Broderick. He died in my care."

Hawthorne did not sit. He stood by the mantelpiece, toying with a heavy silver poker. "Broderick? I have not the pleasure of the name, sir. I have dealings with many merchants, but seamen are not generally among them."

"He knew you," Lacey said quietly. "He spoke of a debt."

"A debt?" The Alderman gave a short, barking laugh, but there was no humour in it. "I pay my bills on the nail, sir. It is the mark of a gentleman. I think you have been taken in by a dying man’s delirium. The fever often plays strange tricks."

"He was most specific," Lacey insisted. He reached into his pocket and withdrew the ivory token. He placed it on the polished mahogany table between them. "He asked me to give you this. He said it was a token."

For a second, the mask of the prosperous civic father slipped. Hawthorne’s face drained of colour, leaving a greasy pallor. His hand, which had been resting on the poker, gripped it so tightly that the knuckles turned white. He stared at the carved tooth as if it were a viper.

"Take it away," Hawthorne whispered, his voice hoarse.

"It is yours, I believe," Lacey said.

"I said take it away!" Hawthorne roared, his composure shattering. He stepped back, his chest heaving. "I do not want your heathen trash. I do not know this man. If you are attempting to extort money from me, Priest, you have chosen the wrong target. I am an Alderman! I have friends in the constabulary!"

Lacey did not flinch. He looked at the Alderman—at the sweat beading on his upper lip, at the way his eyes kept darting to the token and then away, as if looking at it caused him physical pain.

"I seek no money," Lacey said coldly. "I merely sought to discharge a duty. But I see that the man’s ravings were not entirely without foundation. You are frightened, Alderman Hawthorne. You are terrified."

"Get out!" Hawthorne shrieked.

Lacey picked up the token. "I shall leave this with the police, perhaps. They may find it of interest."

"No!" The Alderman lunged forward, then checked himself, realizing he had revealed too much. He forced a ghastly smile. "No, no. Leave it. I... I shall dispose of it. It is merely... a bad memory. A bad investment. Leave it and go."

Lacey placed the ivory tooth on the table and turned on his heel. "Good day, Alderman."
