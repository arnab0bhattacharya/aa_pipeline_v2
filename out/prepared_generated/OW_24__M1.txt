The beggar Elric sat at the western gate of the Cathedral of Saint Aurelian, which the people of Valdria called the House of the Unfinished Rose, for its spire was a filigree of stone that seemed forever on the point of blooming into the sky. Elric sat there as he had sat for thirty years, and his bowl, which was of tin and dented like a crushed moon, sat beside him. He was a master of his profession, which is to say, he had elevated indigence to an art. He did not plead; he presided. He did not whine; he displayed his rags as a sommelier might display a rare vintage, with a quiet pride in their texture and their history of decay.

It was a morning of amber light and cool shadows, when the sun lay upon the city like a golden veil thrown over a sleeping face. It was then that the air grew heavy with the scent of lilies and the sound of invisible wings, and a figure stood before Elric, radiating a light that was not of the sun, a light that made the very cobblestones look like scattered opals.

The figure was an angel, clad in a robe of white silk that seemed woven from the morning mist itself. In his hand, he held a crown of gold, set with a single ruby the size of a pigeon’s heart, which pulsed with a living, crimson fire.

"Elric," said the angel, and his voice was like the chiming of crystal bells. "I have been sent to reward your endurance. Take this crown. It is the symbol of divine favor, of dignity, of the life that awaits you in the heavenly kingdom. But," and here the angel’s expression became one of gentle, celestial sorrow, "you must surrender your bowl. You must give up the badge of your misery to accept the symbol of your glory."

Elric looked at the crown, and then he looked at his bowl. He looked at the ruby, and then he looked at the tin. A smile played upon his lips, a smile of exquisite, philosophical detachment.

"I thank you for the thought," said Elric, his voice dry and rustling like dead leaves. "But I must decline the exchange."

The angel frowned, a gesture that caused the light to flicker. "You refuse the favor of God? You choose the tin over the gold? You choose the shame of the gutter over the splendor of the court?"

"I choose the truth over the ornament," replied Elric. "Your crown is a beautiful lie. It tells the world that I am worthy, that I have been selected, that I am different from the dust. But I am the dust. My bowl is the only honest thing in this city. It is the only object that admits that we are all empty, and that we are all waiting to be filled. The crown is a disguise; the bowl is a mirror. I have no use for a disguise, even a golden one."

At this moment, the heavy oak doors of the cathedral swung open, and Bishop Valerius stepped out. He was a man of immense girth and greater vanity, dressed in vestments of violet brocade that rustled with the sound of falling money. He wore a ring on every finger, and his mitre was so encrusted with pearls that he seemed to carry a small, captive sea upon his head. He had overheard the exchange, and his face, usually a mask of benevolent piety, was flushed with the indignation of a man who has been publicly insulted by the furniture.

"What is this impiety?" thundered Valerius, his voice echoing off the stone saints. "An angel offers you the treasury of heaven, and you, a creature of filth, dare to bargain? You dare to speak of honesty to a messenger of the divine?"

Elric bowed his head slightly, not in submission, but in the manner of a courtier acknowledging a rival. "My Lord Bishop, I speak of honesty because I am a beggar. A bishop has no need of it; he has theology."

Valerius drew himself up, his violet robes straining at the seams. "Insolence! You sit at the gates of the Church, eating the bread of charity, and you reject the very symbol of the salvation we offer. Why? Is it madness? Or is it a perverse pride in your own degradation?"

"It is neither, my Lord," said Elric pleasantly. "It is merely a question of aesthetics. You see, the angel offers me a crown to stop me from asking for bread. But the asking is the only pleasure I have. If I take the crown, I become a prince, and a prince must be dignified. Dignity is a heavy burden, my Lord Bishop. You should know; look how it stoops your shoulders. I prefer the freedom of the bowl. The bowl allows me to beg, and begging is a conversation. The crown is a monologue."

Valerius turned to the angel, his eyes glittering with avarice and confusion. "Holy Messenger, do not waste your breath on this creature. If he will not have the gold, give it to the Church. We shall melt it down for the altar. We know the value of such things."

The angel looked at the Bishop with an expression of infinite, melancholy beauty. "The crown was meant to be worn by a head that knows its own worth. If it is melted down, it becomes mere metal. It loses its soul."

"And what of the bowl?" demanded Valerius, turning back to Elric. "Explain to me, you philosopher of the gutter, why you cling to that wretched vessel of tin as if it were the Holy Grail. Why do you refuse to be saved?"
