The lodgings on St Aldate’s had a particular quality of silence, a dusty hush that seemed to be woven into the very fabric of the threadbare carpets and the dark, heavy curtains. It was a silence that Mr. Alistair Finch found conducive to his work. For three years, he had occupied the small set of rooms above a bakery, a lecturer in physics at the university whose mind was perpetually far from the soot-stained spires of Oxford. His colleagues found him agreeable but remote, a man who spoke of thermodynamics and the nature of light with the same gentle, abstracted air with which he might discuss the weather.

His landlady, Mrs. Gable, was a woman of formidable practicality. She was stout, with a face like a kindly, rumpled potato, and she believed that a clean room was the foundation of a sound mind. Every Tuesday and Thursday, she would ascend the narrow stairs with her bucket and brushes, her tread heavy with purpose, to impose her brand of order upon the chaos of Mr. Finch’s academic life. She would stack his papers into neat piles, dust the strange brass instruments he kept on his mantelpiece, and polish the glass of the window until the grimy view of the street shone with a dull, clean light. Finch would always thank her with a vague, warm smile, and she would depart, satisfied that she had once again wrestled a small corner of the world into submission.

It was on a Thursday that the event occurred. Finch had been out for the morning, attending a lecture by a German colleague on the topic of aetheric resistance. He returned to his rooms to find them immaculately tidy, the air thick with the scent of beeswax and lye. He smiled, and his eyes went immediately to the small mahogany table beside the window. His heart gave a sickening lurch.

On the table, where he had left a sealed glass jar of a most particular nature, there was now a shattered mess. Fragments of thick, hand-blown glass lay scattered across the polished wood. A dark, viscous stain, like spilled ink but with a strange, oily sheen, seeped into a crack in the tabletop. And in the centre of the wreckage, coiled upon itself like a sleeping serpent of shadow, was the anomaly.

It was not large, no bigger than a man’s fist. It was a pocket of pure negation, a knot in the fabric of space that Finch had, through a series of calculations bordering on the alchemical, managed to pluck from the void and contain. Within its boundaries, the laws of nature he spent his life studying were inverted. Entropy ran in reverse. Gravity was a repulsive, not an attractive, force. Time, he suspected, did not flow at all, but existed in a single, shimmering, eternal moment.

The jar had been a masterpiece of engineering. A double-walled vessel of lead-crystal, lined with a fine silver mesh, it had kept the anomaly stable, its impossible energies contained and dormant. Now, exposed to the ordinary air of the room, it was beginning to… react.

A small, loose button from his own waistcoat, which had skittered across the floor during Mrs. Gable’s vigorous sweeping, suddenly stopped its slide. It trembled, then began to move backwards, tracing its path in reverse until it came to rest exactly where it had fallen from. A splinter of wood from the table lifted itself from the floor and slotted perfectly back into the jagged edge from which it had broken.

Finch stood frozen, his mind racing, calculating the vectors of this impossible physics. The anomaly was a drain, a focal point of reversed causality. It was pulling the local reality back towards a state of order, undoing the small chaos of the breakage. But what would happen when it began to undo larger things? What would happen when it reached the floorboards, the walls, the very building itself?

He had to contain it. He looked around for anything he could use. A lead-lined box was out of the question. His eyes fell upon a heavy, cut-glass decanter of sherry he kept for visitors. It was thick, heavy. Perhaps it would do for a moment. He took a hesitant step towards the table, his every instinct screaming at him to run.

Just then, the door creaked open. Mrs. Gable stood in the doorway, her hands twisting in her apron. Her face was pale with distress.

“Oh, Mr. Finch, sir,” she began, her voice trembling. “I am so dreadfully sorry. I was just polishing the table, and my sleeve caught the jar. It went over so fast, there was nothing I could do. I’ll pay for it, sir, every penny. I don’t know what it was you kept in it, but I’ll clean it up right away.”

Before Finch could shout a warning, she bustled into the room, her practical instincts overriding any fear of the strange, dark coil on the table. She reached for the largest piece of glass with a damp cloth.

“Please, Mrs. Gable, do not touch it!” he cried, his voice sharper than he had ever used with her.

She recoiled, startled by his tone. “But sir, the mess…”

“It is not a mess,” he said, his voice dropping to a strained whisper. “It is… a demonstration. A model. Of a theoretical concept. It is extremely delicate. And extremely dangerous.”

Her eyes widened. “Dangerous, sir? It’s just a spill. I’ll have it gone in a jiffy.”

She took another step forward, her cloth outstretched. As her hand neared the edge of the anomaly’s influence, the damp cloth suddenly became dry. The water droplets on it coalesced, lifted from the fabric, and flew in a perfect arc back into the bucket she had left by the door.

Mrs. Gable stared, her mouth agape. She looked from the dry cloth to the bucket, her simple, ordered mind struggling to process the event.

“What in God’s name…?” she whispered.
