It was upon a raw, foggy morning in the late November of 1894 that I found myself walking down Charing Cross Road, the yellow fog thick enough to feel against the cheek, when I happened upon my old acquaintance, Mr. Silas Vance. He was standing beneath the green awning of his antiquarian bookshop, his hands clasped behind his back, staring at the wet paving stones with an intensity that told me at once that something was amiss. Vance was a man of methodical habits, precise in his dress and unshaken in his temper, yet I observed that his usually immaculate greatcoat was buttoned awry and his gloves were clutched rather than worn.

"Halloa, Vance!" said I. "You look like a man who has mislaid a fortune."

He turned his thin, clean-shaven face toward me, and I saw that his eyes were heavy with trouble. "Not mislaid it, doctor," said he, in the low, measured tone of one who chooses his words with care. "But I have been robbed of something, and I cannot for the life of me say precisely what it is. You know my friend Mr. Quayle, the investigator, of whom I have spoken?"

"I know of him by reputation."

"Then I wish you would walk in and let me lay the matter before you both. He is within now, examining the volumes. It is a queer business, and I confess it is beyond me."

I followed him through the narrow shop, where the smell of old leather and damp paper hung thick, past tiers of volumes that climbed to the ceiling, and so into the small back parlour where Mr. Quayle was seated at the table. He was a tall, gaunt man with a sharp, pale face and grey eyes that seemed to take in everything at a glance. His coat was thrown across a chair, and he was bending over six books that lay open before him, examining their interiors through a large magnifying glass. He looked up as we entered and nodded to Vance.

"Pray sit down, doctor," said he, for we had met once before. "Our friend Vance has a problem that may prove more interesting than it appears. Now, Vance, tell the doctor what you have told me, from the beginning, and leave nothing out."

Vance sat down and placed his hands flat upon the table. "I have been in this trade for two-and-twenty years," he began, "and I have never had a dishonest customer until now—or at least, I believe I have one now, though I cannot prove it. The matter concerns a gentleman named Mr. Aldous Carew. He has dealt with me for four years. He is a collector of some means, particular in his tastes, and he has a fancy for volumes of travel and topography. He buys, he reads, and within the fortnight he returns those that do not suit him. I credit him against his next purchase. It is a common arrangement."

"And the returned volumes?" asked Quayle.

"That is the heart of it. Three months ago, he returned a copy of Ogilby's *Britannia* in folio. I shelved it without inspection, for I had no reason to distrust him. A week later, a gentleman from the Bodleian wished to purchase it. When I took it down, I found that plate forty-three—a folding map of Wiltshire—had been cut clean out with a razor. The stub remained, neatly slit. I thought it a mishap, perhaps an accident of binding. But then it happened again, with a speed that told me it was no accident."

Quayle leaned forward. "Which volumes?"

"A second was Moll's *New Description of England and Wales*, returned in October. The map of Hampshire was gone. The third was a Camden, returned only last week. Essex was missing. In each case, the cut was clean, precise, and recent. The surrounding text was untouched. Only the map was taken."

"And you are sure it was Carew?"

"I am sure of this: the pages were intact when I sent the books out, for I examined them upon purchase, as is my custom. I have no assistant. No one else handles the stock. But here is the strangest part. I have compared the three missing maps, and they have nothing in common save that each is a county map of England. Wiltshire, Hampshire, Essex. Different engravers, different scales, different dates. There is no obvious pattern. I cannot conceive what he is assembling."

"When did you last see him?" asked Quayle.

"That is what brought me to you. He has not returned in three weeks. His visits were every Thursday, without fail. I waited, and then I wrote to him at his lodgings in Bloomsbury. The letter came back unopened, marked 'Gone away—no address.'"

Quayle rose and went to the window, where he stood with his back to us, looking out at the fog. "You have the remaining volumes here?"

"I have," said Vance.

Quayle turned. "Then let us see them."

Vance fetched the three books, and Quayle set to work. I watched him examine each volume in turn, running his long fingers over the bindings, holding the pages up to the lamp, measuring the stubs with a small steel rule he produced from his waistcoat. He worked in silence for some minutes. At last he paused before the Camden and held the gutted stub close to his eye.

"You say these maps have nothing in common," said he. "In one sense, you are right. The counties differ, the engravers differ. But you have overlooked one small point. The Ogilby map of Wiltshire, the Moll of Hampshire, and the Camden of Essex—each was the earliest printed map of that county to show the post roads."

Vance stared. "The post roads? What of it?"

"Consider what a man might want with the earliest post-road maps of three counties that lie upon the route from London to the south coast. Wiltshire, Hampshire, Essex. It is not a contiguous line, but it is a pattern. He is not collecting maps, Vance. He is assembling a route. And the route leads to the coast."
