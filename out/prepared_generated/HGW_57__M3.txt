I think it was the singular absurdity of the thing that first struck me, rather than its profound scientific implications. Professor Hedley Crimp was a man of considerable intellect, but he possessed that unfortunate brand of ingenuity which invites disaster. He was a lecturer in natural philosophy at the college, a fellow whose rooms in Logic Lane were perpetually littered with brass voltaic piles, half-dissected frogs, and the peculiar, acrid smell of ozone. I had called upon him that Tuesday afternoon in November to borrow a monograph on terrestrial magnetism, but instead found him standing before his hearth-rug, remarkably subdued, holding a common glass preserving-jar up to the grey light of the window.

He was a slight, nervous man, given to gesturing wildly when agitated, but just then he was entirely still. "You are just in time, Bicknell," he said, without turning his head. "I have made a very monstrous blunder, and I require a witness."

I set my hat upon the chair. "I came for the pamphlet, Crimp. I am late for the provost's tea."

"You must postpone it," he replied, and handed me the jar.

It was sealed with a wad of insulating tape and bladder. Inside, suspended by what appeared to be a few strands of spider-web from the lid, was a small chip of coal. Or rather, it looked like coal. As I tilted the jar, the chip of coal did not fall to the bottom; it swung upwards, pressing with a dull, insistent pressure against the glass roof of the jar. It was attempting to fall upwards.

"The laws of nature," Crimp said, gesturing for me to sit, "are, as we understand them, a localized convention. A statistical average, if you will. I was attempting to reverse the polarity of an aetheric field using a localized galvanic cell. I succeeded, but I succeeded too well. I have punched a hole, as it were, in the fabric of space. A pocket, no larger than a walnut, wherein gravity repels, heat freezes, and entropy runs backwards. I caught it, Bicknell, and I trapped it in this jar."

I confess I laughed. "You are having me on, Crimp. A parlour trick with a piece of pith and a magnet."

"I wish it were," he said, taking the jar back with a heavy sigh. He placed it carefully upon his writing desk, between a stack of unmarked essays and a brass microscope. "It is no trick. Last night, I dropped a matchstick into the jar. The wood did not merely levitate; it began to absorb the ambient heat of the room. Within ten minutes, frost formed on the exterior of the glass. The stick grew colder and colder, until the very moisture in the air condensed and froze it to the bottom of the jar. By morning, the stick had vanished entirely. It did not melt, Bicknell. It un-burned. It devolved into raw cellulose and then into a state of gaseous vapor, drawing the thermal energy of the room into itself to fuel its retrograde existence."

I looked at the jar. The chip of coal was still pressed against the top, quivering faintly. "And what do you intend to do with it?"

"That is the question," he said, rubbing his chin. "It is too dangerous to leave about. If the glass breaks, the pocket will expand. The air around it will freeze; the floorboards will leap upwards; the very heat of the sun will be drawn into this tiny vortex of reversed time. It could, conceivably, un-make the college. I am attempting to devise a leaden casket to contain it, but I am exhausted. I have not slept."

I left him there, still staring at the jar with a mixture of scientific pride and paternal terror. I did not entirely believe his extravagant claims, but I was sensible of his exhaustion. I should have known better than to leave Hedley Crimp to his own devices, or to trust the domestic arrangements of Logic Lane.

The trouble came the following Thursday. Crimp had been called away to London for a symposium, a sudden journey that left him no time to secure his apparatus. He had, however, locked his door, a precaution which he imagined to be entirely sufficient. He had reckoned without the formidable nature of his landlady, Mrs. Gamp.

Mrs. Gamp was a woman of immense practical energy and an unshakeable conviction that she knew what was best for the young gentlemen in her charge. She possessed a large bunch of pass-keys, and a mind that treated locked doors not as barriers, but as personal affronts. Finding Crimp absent on Friday morning, and knowing his rooms to be in a state of perpetual disorder, she let herself in with the noble intention of giving the place a thorough sweeping.

She was a good woman, but she was not a scientist. She saw a dusty room, an unmade bed, and a glass jar sitting incongruously upon the writing desk. The jar was covered in a thick rime of frost, despite the roaring fire she had just built in the grate. To her orderly mind, this was simply another of the Professor's messes. She swept the floor, dusted the microscope, and then reached for the jar.

What happened next is a matter of some speculation, as Mrs. Gamp was not in a state to give a coherent account when she was discovered. It is probable that she lifted the jar, intending to wipe away the frost with her apron. The sudden application of her warm hand to the freezing glass must have stressed the material. The jar shattered.

I was crossing the quad when I heard the scream. It was not the scream of a woman startled by a mouse; it was the shriek of someone confronted by an utter violation of the senses. I rushed up the stairs and found Mrs. Gamp collapsed against the doorframe, her face as white as chalk, pointing a trembling finger into the room.
