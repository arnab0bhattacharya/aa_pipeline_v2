The wind came down from the moors that evening with a scouring edge, whipping the smoke of Netherhough back upon itself and rattling the long windows of the house called Ravenshill. Within, the drawing-room was large and somewhat chill, though a good fire roared on the hearth; for the master of the place—though he was not yet its master—knew the worth of a show of comfort. Mr. Edmund Vane stood with one hand upon the mantel-shelf, his face half in light and half in shadow, and waited for Miss Helena Ashworth to answer him.

She had answered him. She had said “Yes.”

Yet she did not move from her chair by the window, nor did her countenance soften into the joy which such an answer ought to bring. She sat upright, her hands folded in her lap, the firelight striking sharp across the severe line of her cheek. Outside, the bell of Netherhough church clanged eight, and the grinding of the distant mill-wheels seemed to enter the room like a second pulse.

“Well, sir,” said she at last, and her voice was low and hard, “you have my word. You have what you came for.”

“I came for you, Helena,” said Vane, and he said it with a steadiness that cost him something. He was a man of eight-and-thirty, lean and weathered, with a face that had been handsome before poverty had drawn the lines of care across it. “I came to ask you to be my wife.”

“You came to ask me to save you,” said she.

He did not flinch, but his hand tightened on the marble shelf. “I do not deny that I have need of you.”

“Need!” She rose now, and stood before him, her eyes dark and searching. “You have need of my money, sir. Let us speak plainly, for I will not be made a fool of by soft words. You lost all in that mad speculation of yours—the shares in the Blackmoor canal, that bubble of a scheme—and now you stand at the brink of ruin. Your mill is mortgaged, your credit is gone, your workmen are unpaid. And so you come to Ravenshill, to the only woman in the Riding with a fortune at her own disposal, and you ask her to be your wife. Is it not so?”

“It is so,” said he.

His candour seemed to stagger her for a moment; she had looked for protestation, for denial, for the usual arts of a suitor. She drew a sharp breath, and the colour rose in her face—not the soft flush of maidenly confusion, but the hot red of anger.

“And you think,” she cried, “that I will hold you to such a word? That I will give you my hand and my gold, and be a mere counter in your desperate game? No, sir! I will not do it. I release you. Go—go and make your bargain where you can, but do not think to play upon my feelings with a mock marriage. I had rather die an old maid in this house than be the wife of a man who values me only for what I can pay.”

She turned away, her breast heaving, and stood staring into the fire. The flames leapt and snapped, casting wild shadows on the walls.

Vane remained silent for a full minute. Then he spoke, and his voice was very quiet.

“You are right,” said he. “I have used you basely. I came here with a lie in my heart, and I deserve your scorn. But hear me out before you cast me off. When I first knew you, seven years ago, at your uncle’s house in York—I was a proud man then, and a poor one. I had my way to make in the world, and I would not have asked any woman to share my poverty. But I loved you, Helena. I loved you then, and I have loved you ever since, though I had no right to say it.”

“You lie,” said she, still facing the fire. “You never loved me.”

“I did,” he said, with sudden fierceness. “I swear it before God. But I was silent, because I was a fool, and thought it nobler to bear my love in secret than to burden you with it. And then came this cursed speculation—I was mad with the hope of making a fortune, that I might stand before you as an equal, and ask you openly. Instead, I lost all, and I came to you a beggar. And I thought—God forgive me!—that if I could get you by any means, I would spend my life in making you happy, in showing you that my love was real. It was a base thought, but it was not wholly selfish.”

She turned at last, and looked at him with a strange, searching gaze.

“And if I hold you to your word?” said she. “If I say, ‘You shall have my money, and I will be your wife’—what then? Shall I be a partner in your schemes, a tool to retrieve your fortunes? Shall I see you grow rich upon my gold, and thank me only for that?”

“No,” said he. “You shall never be a tool. If you will have me, I will work for you as I have never worked yet. Your fortune shall be yours, to do with as you please; I will not touch a penny of it without your leave. But I will take you, and I will honour you, and I will love you—”

“Love!” she cried, and there was a bitter laugh in her voice. “What is love, sir, but a word to cover a bargain? I have heard it before. My father loved my mother for her estate, and made her life a hell. I will not repeat that history.”

She paced the room, her skirts rustling sharply on the carpet. The fire roared and the wind howled, and the mill-wheels ground on in the dark.
