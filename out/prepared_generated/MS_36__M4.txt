The letter lay upon my escritoire, its seal broken, its paper worn thin by a trembling hand; and the candle, guttering in its silver sconce, cast a wavering light upon the characters that had, in one moment, undone the settled peace of a lifetime. I had come to Ellinwood Hall but three months since, a dependent orphan, grateful for the protection of my guardian, Mr. Francis Ashcombe—a man whose benevolence had been the shelter of my youth, whose generosity had furnished my education and adorned my mind with accomplishments that else had withered in the barren soil of poverty. He had been to me as a second father, a guide, a friend; and when, upon the evening of my twenty-first birthday, he had taken my hand in his and, with a tenderness that moved me to tears, besought me to become his wife, I had felt my heart swell with a gratitude so profound that I had well-nigh mistaken it for the very passion he solicited. I had asked for time—a single day—to compose my spirits and to render him an answer worthy of his goodness; and he, with that noble delicacy which ever marked his conduct, had granted my request, and had left me to the solitude of my chamber and the tumult of my thoughts.

Yet how shall I describe the revolution that a single hour hath wrought within my breast? For in the stillness of that night, while I sat pondering the solemn vows I was about to take, there came to me a packet of letters—old, faded, tied with a ribbon whose colour had once been crimson—which I had discovered but that morning in a cabinet of my mother’s, which had stood locked these many years in the library, and which I had opened with a curiosity that now appeared to me the instrument of a cruel fate. My mother! That gentle being, whose portrait hung above the mantel, whose memory was enshrined in my heart as something sacred and remote, whose death had left me at the age of seven to the care of Providence and of Mr. Ashcombe—she had been young, had been beautiful, had been beloved. And the letters—oh, the agony of that discovery!—were from Francis Ashcombe himself, written in the ardour of a youthful passion, addressed to her as his affianced bride.

I read them with a heart that beat as though it would burst its prison. They spoke of a love so earnest, so confiding, so full of hope and of the future, that I felt my cheeks burn with a shame I could not define, as though I had intruded upon a sanctuary. He had loved her—he had loved my mother! And she—she had broken the engagement; she had forsaken him for my father, whose name I bore, whose memory I revered, but of whom I knew little save that he had won her heart and had died within a few years of their marriage, leaving her to follow him to the grave, and me to the guardianship of the very man she had deserted.

What was I to think? What was I to feel? The man who now sought my hand had once sought hers; the vows he would have me speak had been spoken before, and broken; the affection he professed for me—was it for me, or for the shadow of her he had lost? I rose and paced my chamber in an agony of doubt. The wind without moaned among the ancient oaks of Ellinwood, and the rain beat against the casement with a melancholy importunity, as though nature herself wept for the perplexity of a heart too young to bear the burden of another’s history. I thought of my mother—her gentleness, her beauty, her early death; I thought of my father, whom I had never known, and wondered whether she had found with him the happiness she had denied to Ashcombe. And I thought of Ashcombe himself—his kindness, his patience, his generous care, which had surrounded my orphaned childhood with every comfort, which had cultivated my mind and formed my manners, which had asked no return but that I should be happy. Could such goodness be founded upon a memory? Could such devotion be but the echo of a passion that had once been hers?

I sat me down and wept—not for myself alone, but for him, for her, for the ruin that time makes of all our hopes. The candle burned low, and the shadows gathered in the corners of the room like spectres of the past. I took up the letters once more, and read them through the tears that blinded me. They were the letters of a young man, ardent, impetuous, full of faith in the future; and I marvelled that the grave, composed, benevolent guardian I had known could ever have written with such transport. Yet in one of them—the last—there was a passage that struck me to the heart. “If ever,” he wrote, “thou shouldst find another more worthy of thy love, I pray thee to remember that I shall never cease to wish thee happiness, and that the affection I bear thee shall survive even the loss of thee.” Was it this magnanimity that had enabled him to become my guardian? Was it the memory of her he had loved that had made him love her child? Or had he, in the course of years, transferred to me the affection he had once borne to her, finding in my features and my manners the lineaments of the mother I resembled?

The thought was insupportable. I felt that I could not accept his hand while the shadow of my mother stood between us. I felt, too, that I could not refuse him without appearing ungrateful for all his goodness. And yet—was it not a kind of treachery to my father’s memory to wed the man my mother had rejected? Was it not a betrayal of my own heart to marry where I could not love, and to accept a devotion that might be but the relic of another’s?
