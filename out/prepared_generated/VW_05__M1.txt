The lamps were lit, and the light fell in flat yellow pools upon the wooden desks, and upon the hands of the men who sat behind them; upon thick, ink-stained hands, and upon hands that were pale and nervous, twisting a pen. Miss Ainsworth saw them all—separate, and yet one, a single body of effort and fatigue, and in the second row, third from the left, the hand of Mr. Pargiter, which was broad, and which she knew had the smell of ink and metal and of the streets about it, even now, even here, in the clean, chalk-dusted air of the classroom. The lesson was on the sonnet; the word itself, she said, was a little room, a small chamber for an idea to live in. And as she spoke, her voice, which was clear and pitched for the back of the room, seemed to her to be coming from a great distance, from a person she watched with some curiosity. This person spoke of iambic pentameter, of the turn of the thought, the volta, and the men wrote it down, their pens scratching in the quiet. But what she was thinking was of the way the light caught the side of his face, illuminating the fine hairs on his temple, the solid curve of his jaw. A wave of feeling, not of thought, but of pure, undifferentiated feeling, rose and submerged the lesson, the room, the other men, leaving only that small patch of illuminated skin, a shore in a rising tide. She was drowning, and yet she was perfectly dry, and her voice did not falter as it described the couplet's final, binding rhyme.

Then the bell clanged, a harsh, metallic sound that shattered the quiet, and the men rose, a single organism of creaking chairs and shuffling feet. They gathered their books, their caps, their scarves against the November chill, and the room filled with the low murmur of their farewells. "Good night, miss." "Thank you, miss." And then he was there, before her desk, and the others flowed around him like water around a stone. He stood for a moment, not speaking, and she looked down at the papers on her desk, at the chalk dust on her sleeve, at anything but his face. "Miss Ainsworth," he said. His voice was low, and it seemed to belong to the night outside, to the rumble of the trams and the damp pavement. "I brought you something." He placed a book on the desk. It was a small, slim volume, bound in a deep blue cloth. "It's just… something I made. At the press." She looked at it. It was not a printed book from a shop, but something more particular, more solid. "Thank you, Mr. Pargiter," she said, and the name felt strange and formal in her mouth. "That is very kind." He did not reply, but only nodded, a quick, sharp movement, and then he was gone, swallowed by the dark of the corridor.

She was left alone with the book. The other teachers were putting on their coats, their chatter a distant music. She ran her fingers over the cover. The cloth was rough, and the lettering, stamped in gold, was slightly uneven. *Sonnets and Other Verses*. It was the book she had recommended to them at the start of term. She opened it. The paper was thick, creamy, and the ink was a deep, velvety black. The letters were firm and clear, yet they had a life of their own, a slight tremor, the ghost of the hand that had set them. She turned a page, and the paper made a soft, rustling sound. She did not read the words. She thought of the metal type, the small, cold squares of lead in their cases, and his hands, which she had watched as they wrote, moving them, arranging them, letter by letter, into these lines. What did he mean by it? Was it a simple gift, a student's gesture of thanks to his teacher? Or was it something else, a thing said in the only language he truly commanded, a statement pressed into the very fibre of the page? The thought was a stone in her chest, smooth and cold and impossible to dislodge. She stood up, her knees stiff, and put on her hat, her coat. The book she wrapped in her scarf and held tight against her body, as if it were a living thing.

The walk to the Underground was a passage through a world of shadows and sudden, brilliant light. The gas lamps hissed, casting long, wavering reflections on the wet pavement. A woman laughed, a high, sharp sound. The smell of roasted chestnuts and horse-dung and coal-smoke hung in the damp air. She moved through it all, a ghost in her own life. She was Miss Ainsworth, the teacher, walking home to her small room in Bloomsbury. But she was also this other person, this woman who carried a blue book in her arms and felt its weight as a terrible and wonderful secret. The train rattled and roared, and in the dark of the tunnel, she saw not her own reflection in the window, but his face, the lamplight on his temple. She knew nothing of his life, not really. She knew he was a printer. She knew, from the college register, that he was married. She had seen, once, a woman with tired eyes and two small children waiting for him at the gate, and the image had lodged itself behind her own eyes, a splinter of glass. His life was a room to which she had no key. And yet, here was this book, a small, blue door, perhaps, or perhaps only a picture of a door, painted on a wall.
