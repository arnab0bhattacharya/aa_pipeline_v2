The morning room at No. 17, Chesterfield Row, was a symphony in shades of saffron and old gold, a room so perfectly appointed that it seemed less a place for living than a stage for the exquisite performance of being bored. Lady Anthea Mordaunt regarded her reflection in the silver-backed hairbrush with the serene detachment of a connoisseur contemplating a forgery. Her beauty, after all, was a public institution, and she was its most faithful curator.

It was at this moment of aesthetic communion that her husband, Lord Mordaunt, entered, holding a letter. He was a man whose passions were reserved exclusively for the antique and the inanimate, and he treated the correspondence of his wife with the same critical eye he might turn upon a piece of Sèvres porcelain. He opened her letters, it was understood, not from jealousy, which he considered a vulgar emotion, but from a sense of aesthetic duty. A letter, like a hat or a fan, was an accessory, and it was his business to ensure it was not in poor taste.

“This,” he announced, holding the missive between a thumb and forefinger as if it were a slightly soiled napkin, “is a lamentable composition. The paper is common, the ink is cheap, and the hand is that of a man in a state of moral panic. Positively deplorable.”

Anthea did not turn. “Is it from Lord Harrowby? I do hope he has found me that emerald brooch. Emeralds are so wonderfully sympathetic to despair.”

“It is from a Mr. Cecil St. John,” said her husband, his voice thin with disapproval. “He threatens to make a premature end of his biography, on the grounds that you will not grant him an interview.”

A faint smile, as delicate as frost on a windowpane, touched Anthea’s lips. “How very dramatic. Young men are so devoted to the theatre. They will insist on turning a simple social engagement into a grand tragedy. Do tell him to use a more elegant pistol, should he decide to proceed. The modern ones are so dreadfully unbecoming.”

She turned then, her silk morning gown rustling like dry leaves, and held out a languid hand. “Let me see this masterpiece of melodrama.”

Lord Mordaunt surrendered the letter, which he had already perused twice. “He is quite specific. He speaks of the Serpentine. A watery end is considered very poetic this season, I believe, though it plays havoc with one’s linen.”

Anthea scanned the scrawled, desperate lines. Mr. St. John’s prose was a torrent of adjectives, all of them straining to describe a passion that was, she felt certain, largely imaginary. He spoke of her eyes as twin sapphires in which he would gladly drown, of her laugh as a silver chime that both blessed and damned him, and of the prospect of a life without her as a desert of thorns. The final paragraph contained the threat, couched in terms so overwrought they bordered on the comical. He would, he declared, cast himself into the grey waters of the Serpentine at midnight, unless she would but grant him five minutes of her time, to look upon her face one last time before he journeyed to a world where beauty was but a memory.

“He has a gift for the morbidly ornate,” Anthea conceded, folding the letter with a crisp flick of her wrist. “It is like a bad poem, full of sound and fury, signifying nothing but a desire to be noticed. He does not mean it, of course. It is merely the fashionable way of asking for a dance card.”

“I am not so sure,” said Lord Mordaunt, pacing before the fireplace where a fire of apple logs cast a flickering, roseate glow. He stopped to adjust a Chinese vase on the mantelpiece by a fraction of an inch. “The fellow has the soul of a romantic. And romantics, unlike men of sense, are capable of acting on their own absurdities. The whole affair is in the worst possible taste. Suicide is so declasse. It lacks originality. Everyone is doing it.”

Anthea sighed, a soft, weary exhalation that spoke volumes of the burdens of beauty. “What would you have me do, Alistair? Give an audience to every boy who writes me bad verses and threatens to immolate himself for my amusement? I should have no time for the opera.”

“There is a certain aesthetic obligation,” her husband insisted, his gaze fixed on the pattern of the Aubusson carpet. “A death, however foolish, casts a shadow. It would be a blot upon the season. People would talk. They would say our dinner parties are becoming hazardous.” He paused, a new and dreadful thought dawning upon his features. “Good heavens, what if he were to do it during the Countess of Beaumont’s ball? The disruption would be catastrophic. The blood—think of the blood on the parquet.”

The thought of a stain on the Beaumonts’ famous floor was clearly the most alarming element of the situation. Anthea, however, remained unmoved. She saw the letter for what it was: a crude bid for power, an attempt to make her the unwilling protagonist in his tawdry little drama. To acknowledge it would be to grant it a significance it did not possess.

“He will not do it,” she said with finality, rising and walking to the window that overlooked the manicured square. “He will go home, write another letter, perhaps to a soprano or a baroness, and then he will find a nice, sensible girl with a good dowry and marry her. Men like Mr. St. John do not die for love. They simply find new metaphors.”

Lord Mordaunt was not convinced. The image of the social stain was now firmly fixed in his mind. “It is a question of principle. And of preservation. I will not have my wife’s name associated with some sordid, aquatic suicide. It is an affront to our entire way of life.” He strode to the bell-pull and gave it a sharp tug. “I shall deal with this myself. It is a matter of domestic hygiene.”
