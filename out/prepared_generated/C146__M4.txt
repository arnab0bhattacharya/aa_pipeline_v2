The soap works at Cinder Wharf had a smell that got into everything. It was not merely a smell of rendered fat and lye, though those were the bones of it; there was a sweetness underneath, a greasy floral ghost from the palm oil and the lavender they boiled in for the better sort of bar. Tobias Crane, twelve years old and three months into his employment there, had long since stopped noticing it, which was its own kind of horror. It had settled into his clothes and his hair and the soft skin between his fingers, and when he went home at night to the single rented room in Vellum Row, his mother said he brought the works with him.

His father had been a ship’s chandler, and a good one, until a cargo of hemp he had underwritten went down in the Estuary and took his credit with it. Now his father sat most days by the window, a man whittled down to a silhouette, and Tobias carried tallow to the great iron vats and stirred and skimmed and learned to keep his mouth shut among the men, who were rough and kind in equal measure and who smelled, like him, of the works.

It was in the second month that he first told the lie. It came out of him almost without permission, in the dinner hour, when the men sat on the wharf wall with their bread and cold tea and talked of the sea, for half of them had been at sea and the other half wished they had. They were talking of storms, each claiming a worse one, and young Tobias, who had never seen a wave taller than the wash of a river steamer, found his mouth opening and a story coming out of it.

“My father,” he said, “was in the *Ariel*, out of Limehouse, and she was dismasted off the Cape in the year ’thirty-one.” The men turned to him. He felt the weight of their attention and found he did not dislike it. “He was the master of her. Captain Crane. He had the masts cut away with his own hands to save the ship, and they rode out eleven days under a jury rig, and when the water ran low he gave his own share to the men.”

He had not known he was going to say it. But once said, it could not be unsaid, and the men nodded and asked him questions, and he answered them, drawing on a hundred overheard conversations and the cheap pamphlets he read by candlelight. He told them of the *Ariel*’s voyage to the Indies, of the cargo of indigo and sugar, of the storm, of the jury rig, and of how his father had come home to a hero’s welcome and a purse of money from the underwriters. And then, because a hero must have a fitting end, he told them that the captain had gone out again the next year and had not come back, lost with all hands somewhere south of the Azores, and that his mother wore black still and would not have the sea spoken of in her hearing.

The men were quiet. Old Mercer, who had been a whaler and had a scar across his jaw like a seam, said, “That was a hard thing, lad,” and gave him a piece of his own cheese. Tobias ate it and felt the lie settle into him like a warm meal.

After that it was easy. The story grew. Captain Crane had been a man of temper and generosity. He had once thrashed a bosun for cruelty and been broken for it, and had gone before the Board and won his case. He had a scar on his forehead from a cutlass in the Straits. He could hand, reef, and steer better than any man aboard. Tobias found that he could see him, this tall man with the grey eyes and the quiet voice, standing on the quarterdeck with his coat flapping. He found that he missed him. At night, in the room in Vellum Row, he would lie awake and think of the *Ariel* riding out the storm, and it was more real to him than the damp ceiling or his father’s breathing.

He began to believe it. Not all at once, but by degrees, the way a man might come to believe in a ghost he has conjured himself. He would catch himself thinking, *My father would have known what to do about the vat-man’s temper*, or *My father said a sailor should never be idle*, and then he would remember, with a small cold shock, that his father was sitting by the window at home with a blanket over his knees. But the shock grew smaller each time, and the other thought grew larger.

It was in the fourth month that the old sailor came. He was a thin, brown, leathery man named Daniel Pike, who drank at the corner pub, the Jolly Caulkers, and who had been a master’s mate in the merchant service and had a way of sitting and listening that made men talk. He came to the works one evening to see his nephew, who was a vat-man, and he sat on the wall with the men and heard Tobias tell of the *Ariel* and the storm off the Cape. He said nothing. He watched Tobias with pale, unblinking eyes.

The next evening he was there again. And the next. He never spoke to Tobias directly, but he was always there, listening, and Tobias felt his attention like a cold draught. The men noticed it. “Old Pike’s taken a fancy to your tales, young Crane,” said Mercer, with a grin that Tobias did not like.

On the fourth evening, Pike came and sat beside Tobias on the wall. He took out a clay pipe and lit it, and for a while he said nothing, and Tobias ate his bread and tried to seem at ease.
