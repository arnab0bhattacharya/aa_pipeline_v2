The shop-bell, which had rung with the cheerful iteration of a thing that had no news to tell, had ceased its jangling for the night, and the last customer—a farmer’s wife who could never be persuaded to depart without a full recital of her daughter’s trials with a nursemaid—had been bowed out into the damp, gas-lit dusk of the High Street. Dorcas Pell, left alone in the little parlour behind the shop, with its mingled odours of new calico and stale tea-leaves, drew the letter from her apron-pocket for the third time. It was a thin, foreign-looking letter, with a cramped and uncertain superscription, and the postmark of a place she had never heard of, though it lay, she gathered, somewhere in the fens towards Boston. She had read it so often that the words had come to seem less like a communication from without than a species of internal throbbing, a pulse of dread that beat in time with the ticking of the clock upon the mantel.

Her mother, Mrs. Pell, sat by the fire in the high-backed chair that had been her husband’s—her husband, that is, as the world believed, and as Dorcas herself had believed until the morning of this very day—and her hands were folded over her apron with a stillness that was more alarming than any agitation. She was a small, spare woman, with a face like a winter apple, and eyes that had acquired, through years of silent endurance, a peculiar faculty of looking at nothing. She had not spoken for some minutes, and Dorcas, who knew the symptoms, felt that the silence was only the preface to a declaration of a more than usually irrevocable kind.

“You will not go, Dorcas,” she said at last, and her voice, though low, had the flat finality of a door closed and bolted.

“Mother,” said Dorcas, and then stopped, for the arguments which had been crowding in her mind—arguments drawn from the natural claims of blood, from the duty of forgiveness, from the possibility of a repentance that might be genuine—all seemed, when she was on the point of uttering them, to have a thin and theoretical sound, as if she were quoting from a sermon-book. She had been bred in the shop-parlour, among the tape and the buttons, and she knew that the practical question was not whether a father ought to be forgiven, but whether a woman of thirty-five, who had never in her life gone further than Grantham, could travel alone to a strange town to sit by the bed of a dying man whom her mother had forbidden her to see.

“He asks to see me before he dies,” she said, and the words, spoken aloud, had a strange, theatrical quality, as if they belonged to some other woman’s story.

“He has had twenty years to see you,” said Mrs. Pell, “and he has not seen you. He did not see you when you were a child and had the fever, and he did not see you when you were a young woman and might have been married, if it had not been for the talk there would have been. He did not see you when the shop was burnt, and he did not see you when your uncle died. He has had twenty years, and he has chosen to spend them elsewhere. And now he is dying, and he sends for you, and you think it is a great thing, and that you must go.” She paused, and her folded hands tightened. “I have never spoken of him to you,” she went on, “because I thought it better that you should believe him dead. It was a lie, and I have asked God to forgive me for it, but I have never repented of it, and I do not repent of it now. I told you he was dead, and you have worn mourning for him, and you have prayed for him, and that is all the good you will ever get from him.”

Dorcas looked at the fire, and saw in the glowing coals the little shop as it had been in her childhood, with her mother behind the counter, and the customers who came and went, and the world of Grantham going by outside, and she felt that her whole life had been built upon a foundation of which she had only now discovered the hollowness. She had been a dutiful daughter, and had never questioned the legend of her father’s death; she had pictured him as a man of gentle and melancholy temper, carried off by a decline, and she had sometimes thought that she inherited from him her own love of reading, her own tendency to a certain softness of heart which her mother’s practical sense had always checked. Now she learned that he was a man who had deserted his wife and child, and had lived for twenty years in a remote and unprosperous town, and had written at last a letter in which he said that he was dying, and that he had never ceased to think of his daughter, and that he hoped she would come to him, and that he had something to say to her which he could not say in writing. There was no explanation, no apology, no mention of her mother; it was as if the world had contained only himself and Dorcas, and the twenty years were a blank.

“What does he want to say to me?” Dorcas asked.

“How should I know?” said Mrs. Pell, with a touch of her old sharpness. “He was always one for mysteries. He would have a secret if he could not have anything else. It is my belief that he has nothing to say, and that he only wants to see you, because he is dying and he is afraid, and he thinks that you will be a comfort to him. And so you would be.
