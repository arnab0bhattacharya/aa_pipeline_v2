I stand alone upon the desolate moors that skirt the ancient walls of Ravenscar, where the northern winds, unimpeded by any sheltering wood, sweep down with a melancholy howl that seems the very voice of despair. The autumnal equinox had passed, leaving in its wake a sky of leaden hue, from which the pale, dying light of an obscured sun cast long, spectral shadows across the heath. I had but that morning emerged from the assize courts of this sombre town, acquitted of a crime so foul, so unnatural, that the very accusation had drawn upon my head the execration of all who knew me. Yet, what was this acquittal? It was not a restoration to the embraces of my fellow men, but a severance more complete than the iron bars of a dungeon. I was free, but I bore upon my forehead the invisible, indelible brand of infamy.

The crime was the murder of the venerable Squire Edgar of Thornebrook, a man whose benevolence had endeared him to the surrounding country. The circumstantial evidence against me was appalling. I had been the last to see him alive; I had returned to the vicarage in the dead of night, my garments stained with mire, my countenance pale and convulsed with an terror I dared not name; and, above all, the silver-mounted dagger found buried in the Squire’s breast was known to all as my father’s, stolen, as was believed, by my hand. I had been seized, cast into a noisome cell, and brought forth to a trial where the voices of a hundred witnesses seemed to weave a shroud of guilt around me. I had no defence. I could not say where I had been, nor why my father’s weapon was clutched in the dead man’s heart. I could only look up to Heaven and swear, by all I held sacred, that I was innocent. The jury, moved perhaps by my youth and the agonizing earnestness of my appeals, had brought in a verdict of Not Guilty; but the populace, who thronged the avenues of the court, received the decision with a sullen murmur, and as I passed through their ranks, I felt that I was an outcast, a being accursed.

But why, you may ask, did I not speak? Why did I not reveal the truth that would have cleared my name and consigned the guilty to the scaffold? Alas! The guilty was my father.

It was he who had committed the deed. In a fit of ungovernable rage, the origin of which I have never fully comprehended, he had sought out the Squire, and in the solitude of a woodland path, had sacrificed him to his vengeance. I had witnessed the departure of my father from our house with the dagger in his hand, and I had followed him, with a heart sick with apprehension, to the scene of slaughter. I arrived but to see the old man fall, and my father, his hands reeking with blood, turn upon me with a look so wild, so terrible, that for a moment I believed him possessed by some fiend. He fled, and I was left alone with the dead. I had stooped to withdraw the dagger, to conceal, if possible, the instrument of my father’s guilt, when footsteps approached, and I was seized.

During the long weeks of my imprisonment, and through the agonizing ordeal of my trial, my father had not spoken. He had not come forward to confess his crime, nor had he offered one word in my defence. He had remained secluded in his chamber, a silent, impenetrable spectator of my ruin. I had sent for him, I had implored him, by all the ties of kindred and humanity, to save me; but he had returned no answer. And I, wretched as I was, could not denounce him. The instinct of filial piety, that mysterious law of nature which binds the child to the parent, withheld me, even in the extremity of my anguish. I would rather have perished on the scaffold than have lifted up my voice against him who gave me life.

But now, as I stood upon the moor, the storm of my emotions began to subside, and reason resumed her ascendancy. I reflected upon my situation. I was young, and life stretched before me like a vast, unexplored wilderness, but it was a wilderness without a flower, a path without a hope. My name was blasted; my reputation was gone; I could not look upon the face of man without meeting the glance of suspicion or contempt. I was an outcast, a wanderer on the face of the earth, and all because of a father’s selfishness and a father’s crime. Was I to bear this burden for ever? Was I to drag out a miserable existence, the sport of calumny, the byword of fools, while he, the real culprit, reposed in security?

The conflict within me was terrible. On one side, filial love, that deep-rooted sentiment which survives even the most atrocious wrongs; on the other, the love of justice, of truth, of that fair fame which is dearer to the virtuous soul than life itself. I revolved the matter in my mind a thousand times; I weighed every argument, every motive; I wept, I prayed, I cursed the fate that had placed me in so cruel a dilemma. And as I wandered, thus distracted, over the moor, the wind, which had been increasing in fury, suddenly broke forth in a tempest of rain and hail, that drove me for shelter to the ruins of an old watchtower, whose mouldering walls frowned upon the edge of a precipitous cliff.

Here, in this desolate abode, surrounded by the roaring of the tempest and the darkness of approaching night, I resolved. I would not denounce my father. I would not be his accuser. But I would leave him. I would abandon for ever the home that had been the scene of my misery, and seek, in some distant land, that oblivion which England denied me.
