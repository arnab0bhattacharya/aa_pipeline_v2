The first thing she noticed, as she pushed open the heavy oak door with its brass plate tarnished by the hands of a hundred clerks, was the silence. It was not an empty silence, but a full one, a silence that had weight and texture, like the dust that lay in soft, grey drifts upon the ledges. It was six o’clock in the morning, and the light, thin and watery, was just beginning to spill across the floorboards, illuminating the motes that danced in the air with a slow, purposeless grace. Mrs. Elspeth Vane moved through the room, her broom sweeping the carpet in long, rhythmic strokes, *swish, swish*, a sound that seemed to disturb the very air, to make the dust rise and settle again, as if the room itself were breathing.

She was a small woman, bent slightly by the years, her hands rough and red from the cold water and the soap, but her eyes, bright and sharp behind their spectacles, took in every detail of the office of Harcourt, Leach & Figg. The desks were still bare, their leather surfaces unmarked by the scratch of pens or the stain of ink, waiting for the men who would soon arrive to sit in their chairs and speak their polite, meaningless words. But Elspeth knew them. She knew that Mr. Harcourt would frown at his first letter, that Mr. Leach would hum a tuneless air while he polished his spectacles, and that young Mr. Figg would look out of the window at the river, dreaming of something else, something far away.

She paused by the window, the glass cold against her cheek, and looked out. The Thames was a ribbon of grey lead, moving slowly, almost imperceptibly, beneath the low-hanging clouds. The barges lay moored, their masts swaying gently in the breeze, and the water lapped against the stone embankment with a soft, persistent sound, like a whisper that refused to be heard. It was a beautiful sight, in its way, the river stretching out into the mist, carrying with it the memories of the past, the ships that had gone to war and never returned, the children who had played on the banks and were now men and women, scattered to the four winds.

Elspeth turned back to the room, her mind drifting to her son, Arthur, who was now in Canada. He had written to her last week, his handwriting small and careful, asking how her health was, if the cold had been too severe, if she had found a new spot in the garden for the roses. He did not ask about the work, or the office, or the men who sat in these rooms; he spoke of the snow in Winnipeg, of the cold that bit into the bone, of the quiet that fell over the town when the blizzards came. She imagined him there, standing in the snow, his breath visible in the air, thinking of her, of the kitchen in their old house in Peckham, of the smell of the tea and the toast. It was a strange thought, to feel so close to him and yet so far, separated by the vastness of the ocean, by the years that had passed since he had left.

She moved on to the next room, the office of Mr. Leach, where the air was thick with the scent of pipe tobacco and old paper. On his desk, a letter lay open, its flap torn, the paper yellowed with age. Elspeth picked it up, her fingers trembling slightly, not from the cold, but from a sudden, inexplicable urge to know, to understand, to touch the life of these men who were so different from her own. The letter was from a client, a woman, complaining about a dispute over a property, her words sharp and bitter, full of anger and frustration. Elspeth read it slowly, her eyes moving over the lines, feeling the weight of the words, the pain that lay beneath them. It was not her pain, not directly, but it was pain, and it was real, and it made her think of her husband, Thomas, who lay in the churchyard, his face peaceful, his hands still, no longer working, no longer speaking, no longer present.

She remembered the day he died, the way the light had fallen through the window, the way the air had been still, the way the world had seemed to hold its breath. She had stood by his bedside, holding his hand, feeling the warmth leaving it, feeling the life drain out of him, and she had thought, then, that she would never feel such a thing again, that the world would be empty, that she would be alone forever. But here she was, alive, breathing, moving through the office, sweeping the dust, reading the letters, feeling the weight of the world, the beauty of the river, the distance of her son, the presence of her husband in the churchyard. It was all so strange, so fluid, so hard to grasp.

She put the letter down, carefully, neatly, as she had found it, and moved on to the next room, the office of young Mr. Figg. The light was brighter here, the air clearer, and the window looked out directly onto the river, where a heron stood motionless in the water, its long neck curved, its eye fixed on something unseen. Elspeth stood there, looking at the bird, thinking about how it stood so still, so calm, in the middle of the moving water, and how it seemed to know, to understand, something that she could not. She thought of the churchyard, of the gravestone, of the name carved into the stone, and she thought of the river, of the barges, of the snow in Canada, of the letters on the desks, of the men who would soon arrive, and she felt a sudden, sharp pang of sadness, a sadness that was not for herself, but for the world, for the way things were, for the way they had been, for the way they would be.
