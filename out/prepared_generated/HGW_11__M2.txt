The altitude at Cerrito del Silencio was a physical weight, pressing against the temples with a dull, persistent ache. I had come to the Andes with the specific, unglamorous purpose of inspecting the collapse of Shaft Four for the Barrosa Copper Syndicate. My instructions were brief: assess the timbering, determine the liability, and file a report that would either save the company’s money or bury its reputation. I was a man of calculations, of load-bearing capacities and tensile strengths, not of mysteries. Yet, as I climbed the steep, frozen path toward the solitary lodge of Dr. Elias Vane, the atmosphere felt less like the thin, crisp air of the high mountains and more like the heavy, stagnant breath of a sealed tomb.

Vane was a man of some repute in the scientific circles of Europe before his inexplicable disappearance into the mountains. He had been a geologist, I believed, or perhaps a chemist; his papers had always been dense with terms that suggested a mind more interested in the microscopic than the macroscopic. I had expected a reclusive eccentric, a man of books and cold coffee. I had not expected the smell.

It hit me before I reached the door—a sweet, cloying scent, like overripe fruit left in a warm cellar, mixed with the sharp, metallic tang of ozone. The lodge door was ajar. I pushed it open, my boot heels crunching on a floor that was not wood, but a brittle, crusty substance that crumbled underfoot.

"Dr. Vane?" I called out. My voice echoed strangely, muffled by the thick walls.

"Come in, Mr. Thorne. Close the door. The draft is bad for it."

The voice came from the shadows of the main room. I stepped inside and the light from the small, grimy window caught the interior, and I stood still, my breath catching in my throat. The room was a ruin, but not of disrepair. It was a ruin of consumption. The wooden beams of the ceiling, the stone fireplace, the floorboards—all were covered in a thick, pulsating mat of greyish-white mold. It looked like lichen, but it moved. It rippled in slow, undulating waves, as if the wall itself were breathing.

Vane sat in a chair that was half-dissolved into the floor, his legs hidden beneath a heavy woolen blanket. He was gaunt, his face a map of deep lines, his eyes bright with a feverish intensity that I had not seen since my student days when we had first discussed the theoretical limits of mineral extraction.

"You look surprised, Thorne," Vane said, his voice raspy. "One would think you were a geologist. You know what happens when a force is unleashed without a container. The container fails. The force expands."

"Dr. Vane," I said, my professional detachment cracking under the sheer absurdity of the scene. "What is this? Some kind of fungal bloom? The humidity here must be—"

"Fungal is a generous term," Vane interrupted, leaning forward. The motion caused a shower of grey spores to drift from his shoulder like fine ash. "It is not merely a fungus. It is a solvent. A biological solvent for silicate and oxide structures. I have spent twenty years in the laboratory, Thorne. Twenty years trying to find a way to break the bond of the rock itself, to turn the earth back into its primordial fluid state. I have succeeded. And I have failed. That is the tragedy."

I looked around the room. The grey substance was spreading across the table, eating into the inkwell, dissolving the metal pen into a sludge. It was moving with a purpose, a slow, relentless hunger. "You’ve destroyed your laboratory," I said. "And you’ll destroy this house."

"The house is already gone," Vane said softly. "Look at the walls, Thorne. The stone is soft. It is warm. It breathes. I have bred a life form that views rock as we view bread. It is clean, Thorne. No smoke, no blasting, no noise. Just a gentle, silent dissolution. We can mine the copper from the mountain without breaking it. We can reshape the world with our hands."

I felt a cold horror settle in my stomach, distinct from the chill of the mountain air. This was not science; it was monstrosity. The logic of the man was impeccable, terrifyingly so. He spoke of efficiency, of progress, of the liberation of matter. But I saw only destruction. I saw the end of the solid ground beneath our feet.

"Who is coming?" I asked. "Who is this for?"

"Me," Vane said. "And those who will listen. But first, we must contain it. The roof is compromised. The spores are escaping into the night air. If the wind carries them down the valley, if they find the limestone quarries or the clay pits... God help us."

He stood up, his legs trembling, and reached for a heavy iron key on the table. But the table was gone. The key was a mere smear of rust on the dissolving wood.

"Stay here, Thorne," Vane said, his voice rising in panic. "I must go to the cellar. I have the sealants. The acid. I can stop it at the source."

"You can't," I said. "You’re delirious. The structure is failing. If you go into that cellar, you’ll be buried."

"I am the only one who knows how it works," he snapped, his eyes wild. "You are an engineer, Mr. Thorne. You understand loads and stresses. Do you not see? The load has been removed. The stress is now all on the edges. The mountain is coming apart."

As if to illustrate his point, a low groan came from above us. A chunk of the ceiling, covered in the pulsating grey flesh, detached itself with a wet thud and fell onto the floor. It did not shatter. It splashed, spreading a pool of grey sludge that hissed as it touched the stone.

I grabbed Vane’s arm. He was lighter than I expected, his bones grating under the skin. "We are leaving," I said firmly. "Now."
