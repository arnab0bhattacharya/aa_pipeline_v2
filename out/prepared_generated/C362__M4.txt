The train from the airport had glided through a city of glass and green, its towers threaded with ivy and its streets silent but for the whisper of electric motors. Alistair Finch remembered Vienna from the old vids as a place of imperial stone and horse-drawn carriages, a city of pastry and nostalgia. This was a different creature entirely: clean, cool, and unnervingly quiet. The Congress was to be held in the Palais Epstein, a restored nineteenth-century edifice that now served as a neutral ground for the Continental Assembly. Its marble facade was immaculate, but the windows reflected the same flat, grey sky that seemed to have followed him from London.

He was met in the grand foyer by a junior attaché with a practised smile and a tablet clutched to his chest. "Envoy Finch, welcome. The delegates are assembling in the east salon. The German representative has already arrived, and the French delegation is expected momentarily."

Alistair nodded, his own smile feeling thin and brittle. He had read the briefs on the flight: the harvests from the Ukraine to the Po Valley had failed with a suddenness that baffled the agronomists. The cause was some obscure blight, a mutation that had turned the continent's breadbasket into a withered memory in a single season. The reserves were the only thing standing between Europe and a winter of empty plates. England, with its vast, climate-controlled vertical farms and its carefully hoarded surplus, was in a position of unprecedented power. And his countrymen, he knew, were not inclined to generosity.

The east salon was a cavernous room dominated by a long, polished table. The German delegate, a woman named Katarina Vogel, stood by the window, her back to the room. She was tall and severe, her hair cropped short, her suit a sharp, functional grey. She turned as he entered, and her eyes, a pale, piercing blue, assessed him in a single, efficient glance.

"Envoy Finch," she said, her English clipped and precise. "I trust your journey was comfortable. The irony of meeting in a palace built on the profits of a bygone empire is not lost on me, I hope."

"I'm sure the irony is lost on no one, Frau Vogel," Alistair replied, taking a seat. "Least of all the people queuing for bread in our capitals."

A flicker of something—respect, perhaps, or just recognition—crossed her face. "Indeed. Let us hope the symbolism of this place inspires a more cooperative spirit than the empires it once served."

The French delegate, a man named Jean-Luc Dubois, arrived with a flurry of apologies and a cloud of cologne. He was short, portly, and radiated a nervous energy that made the room feel smaller. The Italian, Signora Rossi, was a study in weary elegance, her face a mask of polite concern. The others—the Dutch, the Spanish, the Polish—filtered in, each taking their place with the solemnity of mourners at a funeral.

The first hour was a masterclass in diplomatic evasion. They spoke of "shared challenges" and "unprecedented circumstances." Dubois proposed a joint scientific task force to study the blight, a suggestion so transparently designed to delay any discussion of actual food that Alistair had to resist the urge to roll his eyes. Vogel was more direct.

"Science is a long-term solution," she said, her voice cutting through the murmurs. "We have a short-term problem. The numbers are clear. The German reserves can sustain our population for five months, perhaps six with strict rationing. The French, I believe, have a similar timeline. The English," she said, her gaze settling on Alistair, "have a surplus that could feed this continent for a year."

The room fell silent. Every eye was on him. He felt the weight of it, the pressure of a thousand years of English insularity pressing down on his shoulders. He thought of the headlines in the London vids that morning: "Britain First," "Our Harvest, Our Right." He thought of the quiet, desperate anger he had seen in his own mother's eyes when he had told her he was being sent to Vienna.

"England is prepared to discuss a framework for the equitable distribution of our reserves," he began, his voice carefully neutral. "But it must be a framework that acknowledges the sacrifices made by the English people to achieve this surplus. We have invested in our agricultural independence for decades. It is not a resource to be simply handed out."

"A framework," Dubois repeated, his tone slick with sarcasm. "And what would this framework entail, Envoy Finch? A price? A set of conditions? We are not talking about trade. We are talking about survival."

"We are talking about responsibility," Alistair countered, the words feeling foreign in his mouth. "Responsibility to our own citizens. The English government has a duty to ensure the security of its people. We cannot simply open our granaries and hope for the best."

"And what of our duty to our people?" Rossi asked, her voice soft but sharp. "Are our citizens less deserving of security than yours? Are we to be your supplicants, begging at the door of the English pantry?"

The accusation hung in the air, thick and ugly. He was the villain of this piece, the representative of the fat, complacent nation that had turned its back on its neighbours. He wanted to explain the complexities, the political realities, the fear that gripped his own countrymen. But how could he? How could he articulate the terror of a nation that had defined itself by its self-sufficiency, now being asked to relinquish that very thing? It would sound like greed. It was greed, he knew, dressed up in the language of prudence.

The sessions dragged on for days. They were a blur of proposals, counter-proposals, and heated arguments in five languages. He met privately with Vogel, who was pragmatic and relentless. "Your government is playing a dangerous game, Alistair," she told him one evening over a glass of mineral water in a quiet café. "They think they can hold the line, but a continent that starves is not a stable neighbour. You will be building a wall against a sea of desperation. It will not hold."
