The boat swam upon the water rather than sailed, for there was no wind to speak of, only a heavy, breathing calm that seemed to rise out of the sea itself, and the sail hung limp and useless from the single mast, a shroud for the dead hope of the day. The sun, having climbed to its meridian, poured down a light that was less a radiance than a weight, a brazen and oppressive fluid in which the men moved slowly, as figures in a dream, their voices sunk to a hoarse and listless murmur. The sea was of a polished, metallic blue, heaving with a slow and regular respiration, like the flank of some great, slumbering animal, and upon its surface the boat left a long, oily wake that seemed to spread out behind them in an ever-widening V of despair.

In the stern of the boat, beneath a scrap of canvas rigged to afford a little shade, lay the master of the unfortunate vessel, the *Santa Lucia*, a barque of Genoa, now somewhere at the bottom of the Java Sea. Captain Marchetti was a Ligurian, a man of a spare, hard frame and a face habitually closed and severe, but now the skin was drawn tight over the cheekbones and the eyes, deep-sunken in their sockets, burned with the dry, febrile light of a fever that was consuming him from within. He had spoken no word of command since the night of the wreck, when, in a black squall that had come upon them with the swiftness of a tiger’s spring, the ship had struck upon a reef and gone to pieces. He had been the last to leave her, and a spar, striking him as he was dragged into the boat, had broken him inwardly. Now he lay still, his hands, grown transparent and blue-veined, plucking at the edge of the canvas, and his gaze was fixed upon the horizon with an expression of profound and unutterable weariness. He was the captain, and the burden of decision rested with him, but he had withdrawn from them into a region of his own, a silent and inaccessible country of pain, and left them to their own devices.

The mate, a young Dane named Kristensen, was a man of a fair, open countenance, now burned to a brick-red by the sun, and of a sanguine and impulsive temper. He crouched in the bow, his eyes for ever searching the empty, glittering expanse to the northward, where, he maintained, lay the island of Bangka and a Dutch settlement, a place of safety and of Christian men. The bosun, on the other hand, an old Genoese named Parodi, a gnarled and leathery veteran of the Eastern trade, squatted amidships, his broad, hairy chest exposed to the sun, and spat with a slow and deliberate contempt into the water.

“It is a dog’s death you are leading us to, Kristensen,” he said, his voice a low and rasping growl, like the sound of a grindstone. “Bangka. Always Bangka. You have the word on your tongue like a prayer. But what do you know of Bangka? You have never smelled it. I have. The Malays there are a bad lot. They have no fear of the Dutch, and less of God. They will fill our bellies with their krises before they fill our mouths with rice. It is a fool’s errand.”

“And what would you have us do, Parodi?” the mate retorted, without turning his head. “Lie here and rot? Drift until the sun sucks the life out of us and the sharks have our bones for a plaything? There is a port at Muntok. There are white men there, and a doctor. The captain . . .” He stopped, and glanced towards the stern, a look of pity and exasperation mingled in his eyes. “The captain will not last another two days in this oven.”

“The captain is already dead,” Parodi said, with a brutal and deliberate calm. “It is only his body that is waiting. And you would kill us all to bury him in a Dutch graveyard. No. I say we make for the Carimon islands. There is water there, and fruit, and the people are simple fisher-folk. They will not trouble us. It is a shorter pull, and the hand of a friend is better than the hand of a stranger, even a Dutch stranger.”

A low, dry sound, like the rustling of dead leaves, came from the stern. The captain had stirred. His eyes, for a moment, seemed to focus upon them, and his lips moved, but no sound came. Then he sank back, and the plucking of his fingers at the canvas began again. It was as if he had wished to speak, and had found the effort beyond his strength, or as if the words he had to say were of a nature that could not be uttered in that company. The silence that followed was more profound than before, filled with the immense and hostile indifference of the sky and the sea.

Kristensen, with a sudden, violent gesture, brought his fist down upon the gunwale. “It is not for us to choose,” he said, his voice trembling with a passion that was half anger and half despair. “He is the master. He must decide. It is his right.”

“His right!” Parodi laughed, a short, ugly sound. “Look at him. He is a man who has forgotten the world. His right is a piece of paper in a dead man’s pocket. The sea has no rights. It has only the strong and the weak. We are the strong, and we must choose.”

“And if I choose Bangka?” the mate said, turning at last to face the old seaman. “Will you obey?”
