The bell above the door of Pennyman’s Bookshop had a thin, apologetic ring, quite unlike the deep, self-assured chime of the brass handle at Foyle’s. It was a sound Clara Morrow knew as intimately as her own heartbeat, a sound that meant a customer, a draft of cold Charing Cross Road air, and the brief, pleasant interruption of her solitude. But on a wet Tuesday in November, when the light was the colour of weak tea and the gas lamps hissed with a damp, sulphurous smell, the bell rang for Mr. Alistair Finch, and Clara’s heart did not so much leap as perform a small, confused stumble.

He was not a bookish man. This was evident in the way he held his hat, a fine grey fedora, and in the way his eyes, instead of scanning the shelves with a lover’s hunger, swept the room as a surveyor might assess a property. He was a man of substance, from the gleam of his patent leather shoes to the heavy gold of his watch chain, which strained across the increasing prominence of his waistcoat. He was in shipping, he had told her once, a detail she had clung to as a means of categorising him, placing him on a shelf far from the dog-eared romantics and the dusty philosophers.

“Miss Morrow,” he said, his voice a low, pleasant rumble. “A dreadful afternoon. I thought a fire and the scent of old paper might be just the thing.”

He smiled, and it was a kind smile, uncomplicated and warm. It was the smile that tempted her. It promised a life of fur-lined coats and hot-house flowers, a life where the damp chill of her lodgings in Crouch End would be a forgotten memory. He was unfailingly polite, and his generosity was staggering. Last week, it had been a box of Fortnum & Mason’s chocolates, so elaborate it had felt almost indecent to open. The week before, he had pressed a five-pound note into her hand, insisting it was for the “trouble” she took in finding him a first edition of Mr. Kipling’s *Kim*, a book he confessed he had not read but had heard was “the sort of thing a man ought to have on his shelf.”

Clara had taken the money, her cheeks burning with a mixture of shame and a thrilling, terrifying sense of her own power.

“It is dreadful, Mr. Finch,” she replied, her voice steadier than she felt. “Can I get you some tea? I’ve just put the kettle on in the back.”

“That would be most kind,” he said, settling his large, well-fed frame into the worn leather armchair by the window. It was the chair where old Mr. Pennyman himself used to sit and read, and seeing Finch there felt like a small, silent act of trespass. He picked up a copy of *The Strand Magazine* from the table beside him, not to read, but to have something to do with his hands.

Clara retreated to the tiny back room, the scent of dust and stewed tea leaves a familiar comfort. Her hands trembled as she poured the boiling water into the chipped china pot. She thought of the letter from her mother that morning, detailing the rising cost of coal and her father’s persistent cough. She thought of the rent book, its columns a stern and unforgiving ledger of her existence. And then she thought of Alistair Finch, of his easy smile and the way he made the world seem a softer, more manageable place.

When she returned, he was not looking at the magazine. He was looking at her. “Miss Morrow,” he began, and his tone had shifted, becoming more formal. “I wonder if you might do me the honour of accompanying me to the theatre on Saturday. There is a new play at the Haymarket. I’m told it is very amusing.”

It was not the first time he had asked. But it was the first time she saw in his eyes not just an invitation, but a proposition. The air in the shop grew thick, charged with the unspoken. Before she could frame an answer, the bell rang again, this time with a frantic, impatient jangle.

It was Leo. Leo Vance, with his rain-soaked hair plastered to his forehead and his threadbare coat collar turned up against the weather. He carried a sheaf of papers under his arm, as he always did, and his eyes, dark and intense, immediately found Clara’s before flicking, with a flicker of disdain, to the man in the leather chair.

“Clara,” he said, his voice a little too loud for the quiet shop. “I’ve finished it. The final canto. I had to read it to you.”

Finch rose from the chair with a leisurely grace, his presence suddenly filling the space between them. “Mr. Vance,” he said, with a polite nod. Leo ignored him.

“It’s the best thing I’ve ever written,” Leo continued, his gaze fixed on Clara. “It’s about truth, Clara. About not being seduced by the gilded cage.” He thrust the papers towards her, and she could see the cheap ink smudged on his fingers.

The contrast was almost theatrical. Here was Leo, all fire and poverty and passionate, impractical ideals. And there was Alistair Finch, solid and warm and offering a seat at the Haymarket. She felt a sudden, sharp resentment towards Leo, for his timing, for his relentless, beautiful poverty, for making her feel that her desire for a little warmth and security was a betrayal of some sacred, artistic cause.

“Leo, not now,” she whispered, her voice tight.

“Why not now?” he challenged, his voice rising. “Because it’s not convenient? Because he’s here, with his fine coat and his theatre tickets?” He turned to Finch, his chin jutting out. “Do you know what she is, Mr. Finch? She is the most brilliant woman in this city. She can talk about Keats and Shelley and Dickens as if they were her neighbours. She has a mind like a diamond. And you… you wouldn’t know a sonnet from a shipping manifest.”
