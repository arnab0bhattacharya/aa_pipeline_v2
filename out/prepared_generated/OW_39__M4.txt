The city was called Valedoro, and it was a city of bridges, though the bridges led for the most part to other bridges, and these in turn to squares where the fountains had long since forgotten how to weep. It was a city of pale stone and pale sunlight, where the bells rang with a kind of languor, as if they too were weary of announcing hours that brought nothing new. Along the river, in the low quarter where the houses leaned together like old women exchanging confidences, there lived a lace-maker named Mirabel, whose fingers were the most delicate and the most unfortunate in all that southern kingdom.

She was poor, as the truly skilled so often are, and she sold her lace at the palace gate, spread upon a cloth the colour of a plum at twilight. The ladies of the court would pass her by with that particular indifference which the well-fed reserve for the clever, and the guards would sometimes buy a collar for a sweetheart, and sometimes take one for nothing, which is the way of guards. But it happened that the Duchess of Montebruno, whose vanity was of that rare and perfect kind which requires no audience to sustain it, since she was herself its perpetual and delighted spectator, paused at the gate one morning and looked down at the lace-maker's wares.

She was a woman who had never in her life been troubled by a second thought, and who therefore possessed the serene countenance of those who are at peace with themselves for no reason whatever. Her name was Violante, and she wore the colours of a wounded sunset, and she carried her chin at an angle that suggested she had once been painted and had never quite recovered from the compliment.

"And is this your work?" she asked, lifting a length of lace between two fingers, as though it might bite her.

"It is, your Grace," said Mirabel, who had learned long ago that the poor must answer questions with a humility they do not feel, or else be thought proud, which is the only crime the poor are permitted to commit.

"It is passable," said the Duchess, which in her vocabulary meant that it was better than anything she could have done and therefore must be disparaged. "But I have a fancy—I have a great fancy—for a veil. A veil such as has never been made. Finer than the breath of a dying saint. Lighter than the regret of a man who has married for money. Do you understand me?"

"I understand your Grace," said Mirabel.

"Then make it. I shall pay you—" and here the Duchess paused, for she was one of those persons who believe that the mention of money is vulgar, though the withholding of it is not—"I shall pay you well enough. Bring it to me in a year, on the morning of my fête. And mind you, it must be a thing to make the angels envious. They have such dull wings, you know."

She laughed at her own wit, which was the only wit she ever fully appreciated, and swept away, and the lace-maker was left with the image of a veil in her mind, and a year before her, and no money at all.

Now, the making of that veil was a thing that no chronicle has properly recorded, for the chronicles of the poor are written in their bodies and not in books. Mirabel rose before the sun and worked by the light of a lamp that smelled of rancid oil, and she worked through the day and into the night, and the pattern grew beneath her fingers like a frost upon a window, like a spider's meditation, like the thought of a god who has decided to be careful. She used thread so fine that it could not be seen against the white of her skin, only against the dark of her sleeve, and she used it sparingly, for it was all she had. She sold her furniture, piece by piece, to buy more. She sold her mother's ring, and her father's knife, and the little painted box in which she had kept the few letters she had ever received, all of them bills. And still the veil was not finished.

In the ninth month her eyes began to fail her. It was not a sudden darkness but a slow gathering of shadows, as though the world were being folded away from her, corner by corner. She would hold the lace up to the window and see only a blur of white against a blur of grey, and she would weep a little, quietly, and then go on working, because the veil was not finished and the Duchess had said a year, and the poor do not break their promises, having so few of them to keep.

In the eleventh month she could no longer see the pattern at all, and she worked by touch alone, by the memory of the pattern in her fingertips, which is the oldest memory of all. Her neighbours brought her bread and sometimes soup, and they told her she was a fool, and she agreed with them, and went on working. On the last night of the year she finished the veil. She knew it was finished because her hands told her so, and her hands had never lied to her. It lay across her lap like a fallen moonbeam, like a sigh that had been persuaded to take solid form, and she could not see it, and she wept, and she was happy.

In the morning she went to the palace, led by a neighbour's child, and she stood at the gate with the veil folded in a cloth the colour of a plum at twilight, and she waited. The Duchess came out at last, in a gown of peacock and gold, and she took the veil and shook it out, and the whole court gasped, for it was indeed a thing to make the angels envious, and the Duchess held it up to the light and smiled.
