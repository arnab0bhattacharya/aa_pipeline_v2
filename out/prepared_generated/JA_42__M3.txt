Mrs. Weston had been a widow for nearly four years, and in that time, she had discovered that a jointure of three hundred pounds a year, while sufficient to keep the wolf from the door, was not calculated to make him feel entirely unwelcome in the parlour. Her house in the High Street of Meryton was modest, her establishment small, and her three daughters—Charlotte, Emily, and Sophia—were fast approaching the ages where a modest establishment and a small jointure become rather less a comfort than a reproach.

It was therefore with no small degree of satisfaction that her daughters received the intelligence that Mr. Gibson, of Gibson & Tradeham of London and Liverpool, had begun to call upon their mama with a regularity that could not be attributed to mere neighbourly feeling. Mr. Gibson had made his fortune in the West India trade, and though the precise nature of his dealings was spoken of in vague terms by those who knew them and vaguer terms by those who did not, the results were undeniable. He had taken a lease on Whitmore Lodge, kept a carriage, and wore a coat which, if not precisely in the first stare of fashion, was at least of a cloth that proclaimed him a man who need not count the cost.

The match, should it proceed, would be entirely sensible. This was the view of Charlotte, the eldest, who at two-and-twenty had already learned to appraise the world in terms of practical outcomes, and who could enumerate the advantages of a stepfather with five thousand a year as readily as she could recite the chapters of the New Testament. Emily and Sophia, younger and less burdened by the necessity of calculation, were nevertheless in cheerful agreement. A merchant was not a baronet, but a baronet had not presented himself, and in the absence of a title, an income would do.

The neighbourhood, however, was not of the same mind. Mrs. Drayton, who lived in the superior house at the end of the street and whose late husband had been in the commission of the peace, was known to have pronounced the matter with considerable finality. She had been heard to say, across a card table, that she could not conceive how a woman of Mrs. Weston's connections—and the Westons had connections of a sort, for her father had been a curate of good family, and her late husband a younger son of the Westons of Bedfordshire—could bring herself to receive the addresses of a man who had, in effect, sold sugar for a living. The phrase "sold sugar" was delivered with such studied disdain that one might have supposed Mr. Gibson had been caught in the very act of weighing it out behind a counter, rather than directing the operations of a considerable fleet from the comfort of a counting-house.

Mrs. Drayton's daughter, Miss Drayton, echoed her mama's sentiments with that perfect fidelity which arises either from filial devotion or an inability to form an independent thought. She was overheard at the milliner's to remark that she thought it very odd, and that she could not imagine what Mrs. Weston meant by it, and that for her part, she should prefer to live on bread and water than demean herself by such a connexion. This last declaration was rendered somewhat less affecting by the fact that Miss Drayton was at that moment purchasing a new bonnet trimmed with French ribbon, which rather diminished the likelihood of her being called upon to test the proposition.

Mrs. Weston, however, was not wholly ignorant of the talk that circulated around her. She knew that her acquaintance was divided between those who thought her prudent and those who thought her mercenary, and she knew, too, that the latter party was the more vocal and the more entertaining in its expressions of disapprobation. But she found, upon examination of her own heart, that her reasons for encouraging Mr. Gibson's attentions were not those which her daughters supposed, nor those which her neighbours feared.

Mr. Gibson was not handsome. He was a man of perhaps fifty, strongly built, with a complexion that spoke of sea air and long voyages, and a manner of speaking that was direct to the point of bluntness. He did not employ the studied civilities which the gentlemen of the neighbourhood had refined into something approaching an art form. He did not bow with grace, nor offer compliments with that air of careless indifference which renders them acceptable. He said what he meant, and meant what he said, and if this habit occasionally produced a remark that startled the more delicate sensibilities of Meryton, it also produced, in Mrs. Weston, an effect which she had not experienced since the early days of her first marriage.

He made her laugh.

This was a circumstance of no small importance to a woman who had spent four years in a state of sober widowhood, and the preceding seven years in a state of sober marriage, for Mr. Weston had been a good man but a serious one, and his conversation had been of the kind that instructs rather than delights. He had been concerned, throughout their married life, with the improvement of his estate and the regulation of his household, and though Mrs. Weston had respected him entirely, she had occasionally felt that a marriage conducted wholly upon principles of improvement and regulation left rather little room for mirth.

Mr. Gibson, by contrast, appeared to regard the world as a place that was fundamentally absurd, and he related its absurdities with a relish and a precision that Mrs. Weston found irresistible. He had a story for every occasion—not the polished, practised anecdotes of a man who has learned to entertain, but the spontaneous observations of a man who has seen a great deal of human folly and has not been corrupted by it into cynicism. He spoke of merchants and sailors and colonial governors with an irreverence that would have shocked Mrs. Drayton into a fit, had she been present to hear it, and he spoke of the neighbourhood of Meryton with a quiet, observant wit that was, if anything, more devastating.
