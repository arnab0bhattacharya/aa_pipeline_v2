The lake, that afternoon, lay as still as a piece of lead set into the grey velvet of the city’s outskirts. A fine, persistent drizzle, which had come down from the unseen mountains at dawn, had washed the world of colour, leaving only the iron-grey of the water, the soaked black of the cathedral’s stone, and the pale, wet gleam of the cobbled square where my own house stood, solid and respectable. It was a silence of water and stone, the kind that presses in upon a man’s thoughts and makes them echo in the hollows of his own conscience. I had been watching the rain trace its patient, meaningless lines upon the windowpanes, a sight that promised nothing but the slow, damp passage of time, when the bell of the side door announced a visitor.

He came in out of the drizzle like a man escaping more than the weather. He was one of the Russians, a frequent attender at my services, though never at the communion table. His name, he had told me, was Volkov; a man of perhaps forty, with the worn, fine-boned face of an aristocrat and the watchful, hunted eyes of the dispossessed. He stood dripping on the worn tiles of my hall, his dark coat steaming faintly in the warmth of the stove, and he refused my offer of tea with a quick, dismissive gesture of his long, pale hand.

“Pastor,” he said, his French precise but carrying the low, resonant music of his native tongue, “I have come to ask a service. A matter of the soul. Of the sacraments.”

I led him into my study, the room where I counselled the troubled and prepared my sermons. It was a place of order, of leather-bound books and the solid, unshakeable presence of a plain wooden cross. Here, the chaos of the world was meant to be held at bay by faith and reason. He remained standing, a dark, angular figure against the shelves of theological texts, and I understood at once that the order of this room was about to be tested.

“It is a marriage,” he said, not looking at me, but at the crucifix on the wall. “For myself. And for a woman. We wish to be married. By you. In the sight of God.”

“This is a happy duty, my friend,” I replied, allowing a warmth to enter my voice. “Of course. We shall need the usual particulars, the banns to be called, a meeting with the bride…”

“No.” The word was flat, absolute. It fell into the quiet of the room like a stone into still water. “There can be no banns. There can be no questions. It must be done at once. Tomorrow, if it is possible. In the early morning, before the city is awake.”

I felt a prickle of unease, the instinct of a shepherd who senses the approach of a wolf not by sight, but by a subtle disturbance in the flock. “But my dear sir… a sacrament cannot be performed in such haste, in such secrecy. The Church requires a public declaration, a truthfulness before God and the community. It is the foundation upon which the marriage is built.”

“The truth,” he said, and for the first time, his eyes met mine. They were of a startling, pale blue, and in them I saw a desperation so profound it was almost serene. “The truth is a luxury we cannot afford. There are… circumstances. Of a political nature. A marriage made public is a marriage made a target. For her. For me. For the child.”

Child. The word altered the entire gravity of the situation. It was no longer a matter of romantic secrecy, but of protection, of a fragile life beginning in the shadow of some great, unnamed danger.

“Then give me a name,” I pressed, my voice gentle but firm. “Not the truth of your past, perhaps, but the truth of your name. I cannot unite two people in the eyes of God when one of them is a phantom.”

He hesitated, and in that pause, the ticking of the great clock in the hall seemed to grow loud and menacing. The rain had intensified, drumming against the leaded glass with a frantic, insistent rhythm. He looked down at his hands, which were clasped before him, the knuckles white.

“My name,” he said at last, the words dragged from him, “is Dimitri Orlov.”

I knew it was a lie. I knew it as surely as I knew the feel of the cold stone of the church beneath my feet. It was a name he had selected from the air, a shield of common sounds. The man before me was not an Orlov. He was a man for whom names had become traps, and identity a burden to be shed. To ask again would be useless; he had given me his lie, and in it was all the truth of his fear.

I walked to the window and looked out at the lake, at the grey, heaving expanse that held no answers. I was a servant of God, a steward of His mysteries. The sacrament of marriage was a sacred covenant, a bond witnessed by heaven. Could such a bond be forged with the base metal of a falsehood? The question was a torment. If I refused, I might be condemning this woman and her unborn child to some dreadful fate, driving them from the only sanctuary they had sought. If I agreed, I would be making a mockery of my office, performing a holy rite upon a foundation of deceit. I would be a party to the lie.

“The sacrament is not a contract of men, but a covenant of souls,” I said, my back still to him, speaking to the grey water and the weeping sky. “It rests on truth. If the name is a lie, what else is not? The vows? The love? The very intention of the bond?”
