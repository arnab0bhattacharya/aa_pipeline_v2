# The Crammer's Classroom

Arthur Penrose had not expected to end up in Tooting. When he had accepted the post at Hargrove House School in Kensington, he had imagined a career spent guiding young minds toward the light of reason, unfolding the architecture of natural selection before boys whose futures might yet bend toward science. That had lasted two terms. The headmaster, Dr. Creswell, had called him into the study on a Tuesday morning in November and explained, with the pained courtesy of a man settling a tradesman's account, that certain families had expressed concern. The boy Ashworth, son of Sir Edmund Ashworth, had gone home speaking of common ancestry with apes. Lady Ashworth had written a letter. Other letters had followed. Penrose was offered a quarter's salary and a reference that described him as "diligent in matters of classical instruction," which was, he supposed, a kind of joke.

The crammer's establishment on Trevelyan Road occupied the ground floor of a semi-detached house belonging to a Mrs. Frobisher, who let the rooms to Penrose and took her tea upstairs behind a green baize door. He had inherited three pupils from the previous occupant, a Scottish classics tutor who had died of drink, and had since acquired two more. The boys sat at a long table beneath a window that looked onto a privet hedge. They were large, pink-faced young men of the sort who had been expelled from more distinguished institutions and sent to London to be prepared for the Sandhurst and Woolwich entrance examinations. Their names were Trefusis, Braithwaite, Hollins, Carmody, and Vane. They were not, in any conventional sense, clever. Hollins could not distinguish between a noun and a verb. Carmody, who was intended for the Royal Artillery, wept when asked to perform long division. Penrose spent his days drilling them through arithmetic, English, Latin, and the geography of the British Empire with its pink expanses and modest expectations.

He had brought his books. They lined a shelf at the back of the room, and during the boys' mealtimes, when they went to the pie shop on the high road, Penrose would sit and read. Huxley, Haeckel, the translations of Darwin that had cost him his post. He read them now not with the old excitement but with something closer to consolation. The world was as it was. Species adapted or perished. He had adapted by becoming a man who taught Carmody that the capital of Queensland was Brisbane.

It was Hollins who mentioned the sister. He did it without meaning to, in the middle of a Wednesday afternoon lesson, while Penrose was attempting to explain the formation of the passive voice in Latin. Hollins had said something about his sister having read a book, and Penrose, grasping at any thread of intellectual engagement, had asked which book. Hollins could not remember. He thought it might have been green.

"Your sister reads natural philosophy?" Penrose had asked, more sharply than he intended.

"She reads everything," Hollins said, with the bewildered tone of a man describing a family affliction. "Father says she'll never find a husband if she keeps it up."

The sister appeared the following Friday. She came to collect Hollins because their mother's carriage had been delayed and she had walked from the railway station. Her name was Eleanor Hollins, and she was twenty-three. She stood in the doorway of the classroom while her brother gathered his things with characteristic slowness, and she looked past him at the bookshelf. Penrose watched her eyes move along the spines. He expected them to stop at the Latin primers or the examination guides. They did not. They stopped at the Huxley, and then at the Darwin, and then at the Haeckel, and her face did something that Penrose had not seen a face do in Tooting. It brightened with recognition.

"You've read Haeckel," she said. It was not a question.

"I have."

"In the original?"

"My German is imperfect, but yes."

Hollins pulled on his coat and said something about dinner. Eleanor Hollins did not move. She was looking at Penrose now with an expression he could not quite read. It was not the polite vacancy he had grown accustomed to in the mothers and sisters of his pupils. It was the look of a person who has been searching for a particular door and believes she may have found it.

"I have read *The Descent of Man* twice," she said. "My father does not know. He thinks I am reading Trollope."

Penrose did not know what to say. He said, "Trollope is very good."

"So is Darwin," she said, and then she smiled, and then she left with her brother.

She began to come on Fridays. At first her presence was incidental, a matter of waiting for Hollins to finish. She sat in a chair by the window and opened a book she had brought with her. Penrose continued the lesson, but he was aware of her as he had not been aware of anyone in months. The boys did not notice. They did not notice much. When the lesson ended and Hollins went to the pie shop, Eleanor remained. She asked questions. She asked about the Galápagos finches, about the gradations of the eye, about the inheritance of acquired characteristics, which Penrose explained Darwin had been wrong to entertain. She had read Spencer and wanted to know why Penrose thought him a philosopher rather than a scientist. Penrose told her, and she argued, and the argument was the best he had had since leaving the university.

The Fridays became a fixed point in his week. He found himself preparing for them, choosing passages he thought she would find interesting, ordering his thoughts more carefully than he ordered them for the boys. She brought her own books now, carried in a canvas bag. She had read Wallace's contributions to the *Journal of the Linnean Society*. She had read Tyndall on heat. She had read John Stuart Mill and found him insufficiently attentive to the natural world. Penrose thought this was a remarkable thing for a young woman to have noticed, and he told her so. She said she did not see what her sex had to do with it.
