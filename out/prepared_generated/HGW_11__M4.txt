I had come up from the coast by mule, a journey of three days that began among the sugar-cane and the flies and ended in a thin, hard air that made my head ache and my breath come short. The mine was called La Esperanza, though what hope remained in it I could not at first imagine, for the road up to the workings was littered with the bones of llamas and the rusted iron of abandoned carts, and the few Indians I passed looked at me with a dull incuriosity that was worse than hostility.

My name is Arthur Prendergast, and I was at that time in the employ of a London firm of mining engineers, who had been engaged by the owners of La Esperanza to report upon the state of the lower galleries, which had been closed for some years owing to a fall of rock and a dispute among the shareholders. The owner of the mine, I had been given to understand, was a Mr. Henry Calderwood, a man of scientific tastes who had purchased the property for a song and had lived there in considerable seclusion for the better part of a decade. My instructions were to inspect the workings, to estimate the cost of reopening them, and to return with all despatch.

The house in which Calderwood lived stood at the very lip of the mine, a long, low building of whitewashed stone that might have been a monastery in the old days. I arrived in the late afternoon, and was received by a silent Indian servant who took my mule and led me into a cool, flagged hall, where I waited some minutes before my host appeared. He was a tall, spare man of perhaps fifty, with a face that had been handsome once and was now chiefly remarkable for the extraordinary brightness and restlessness of his eyes. His manner was courteous, but there was an impatience in it, a nervous haste, as though he begrudged every moment not spent upon some pressing and secret business.

We dined together that evening, and I confess I found him a difficult companion. He asked me a great many questions about London, about the progress of chemical science, about the new methods of assay and the use of the spectroscope, and then, when I had answered him at some length, he seemed scarcely to hear my replies, but sat staring at the candle-flame with a curious, fixed intensity. When I spoke of the mine and of my errand, he waved his hand impatiently.

“The lower galleries,” he said, “are not so important as you suppose. I have other work here, Mr. Prendergast, which interests me far more. You are an engineer, I understand. You have a practical mind. I fancy you may be of use to me.”

I asked him what his work might be, but he would not be drawn. “Tomorrow,” he said, “I will show you. Tonight you must rest. The air here is thin, and you are not accustomed to it.”

I slept badly, as he had predicted, and rose early, restless and curious. The sun was just striking the snows of the great peak that dominated the valley when I came out into the yard, and I saw my host already standing at the mouth of the mine, a lantern in his hand, evidently waiting for me. He greeted me with a kind of suppressed eagerness, and bade me follow him.

We descended by a series of ladders and a long, sloping gallery, the walls of which were propped with timber that looked old and dry. The air was cold and had a curious, earthy smell, not unpleasant, but unlike anything I had encountered in a mine before. At length we came to a place where the gallery had been widened into a sort of chamber, and here Calderwood stopped and held up his lantern.

“Look,” he said.

The walls of the chamber were covered with a greyish-white growth, something between a mould and a lichen, which had spread over the rock in great, fan-shaped patches. It had a faint phosphorescence of its own, so that the place was dimly visible even where the lantern-light did not reach. I put out my hand to touch it, and found it soft and slightly damp, like the underside of a mushroom.

“What is it?” I asked.

“It is a fungus,” he said. “A very remarkable fungus. I have been breeding it for seven years. It feeds upon rock, Mr. Prendergast. It consumes the very substance of the mountain, and it grows—it grows with an extraordinary rapidity. I have calculated that a patch of it no larger than a shilling will, in the course of a month, devour a cubic foot of granite. And it is not merely the surface it attacks. It penetrates, it spreads, it honeycombs the rock until the whole mass is rotten with it.”

I confess I did not at first grasp the significance of what he was telling me. I thought it some curious botanical hobby, and I said something to the effect that it must be a great nuisance in the workings.

“A nuisance!” He turned upon me with a kind of fury. “Man, do you not see what this means? I have in my hands a agent of destruction such as the world has never dreamed of. I can bring down a mountain. I can undermine a city. I can—but no, I am before myself. You do not understand. Come, I will show you more.”

He led me deeper into the mine, and as we went I noticed that the timbering was in many places cracked and bulging, and that the floor was littered with flakes of stone that crumbled to powder under my boot. The white growth was everywhere, thicker now, and in some places hanging from the roof in long, pale tendrils that brushed against my face as I passed. The air grew warmer and closer, and there was a faint, sweetish odour that I did not like.
