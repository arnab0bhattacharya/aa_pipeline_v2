It was a truth universally acknowledged, at least in the drawing room of Ashcombe House, that two sisters who had not a single shilling to their name were a distinct inconvenience to be endured, if not entirely overlooked. The house itself, a sprawling edifice of grey stone that seemed to frown upon the damp streets of London, was the residence of Miss Hester Vane’s late uncle, and now the seat of her husband, the Honourable Charles Vane. It was a place of such vast and echoing proportions that a visitor might easily lose themselves in its corridors, or, more likely, be lost in the silence that followed their departure from the family circle.

Miss Hester Vane had not changed in the six months since her marriage, though her manner had acquired a certain rigidity that her sister, Mrs. Vane, had not previously possessed. She was a woman of fine features and excellent breeding, yet her happiness appeared to have been sacrificed upon the altar of propriety. Her husband, a man of considerable fortune and moderate intellect, was rarely seen, preferring the privacy of his study or the society of his club to the burden of his new domestic responsibilities. Hester, however, remained at home, presiding over the household with the air of a general commanding a besieged city, though her army consisted largely of silent servants and two visiting sisters who were expected to occupy themselves without requiring attention.

The sisters, Eleanor and Julia Thorne, had arrived in London on the morning of a grey Tuesday, their carriage having been received with a deference that was cold but not unpolite. They had been shown to the smallest of the guest rooms, a chamber on the third floor that overlooked the rear garden, where the ivy grew thick and dark against the stone. It was a room that smelled of dust and old velvet, and it lacked the warmth that one might have expected from a cousin’s home. Yet, as the hours passed and the sound of the city faded into a dull murmur, the sisters found themselves seated in the drawing room, waiting for the hostess to appear.

Julia, the younger of the two, was a creature of quick temper and sharper tongue. She sat with her hands clasped tightly in her lap, her eyes fixed on the fireplace, which burned with a feeble and sputtering fire. She had been expecting a different reception; she had expected the warmth of family, the gossip of long-lost friends, and perhaps the hint of a favorable match for herself. Instead, she had been met with a list of rules regarding the use of the library and the strict times at which meals were served.

“I cannot endure this,” Julia said, her voice low but vibrating with resentment. “We are treated as if we were beggars come to the door, not as her cousins. Look at the way she looks at us, Eleanor. It is as if we have brought the plague into the house.”

Eleanor, who was seated in the corner by the window, did not turn from the glass. She was a woman of quiet observation, her mind always working beneath the surface of her calm exterior. She watched the rain streak down the windowpane, blurring the view of the garden into a watercolor of grey and green.

“Be careful, Julia,” Eleanor said softly. “Hester is not unkind; she is merely... confined. Do not mistake her reserve for malice. She has married a man who does not understand the necessity of conversation, and in return, she has lost the ability to engage in it freely. She is not proud of us; she is afraid of us.”

“Afraid of us?” Julia laughed, a short, brittle sound. “Of us, who have no title and no money? She is afraid of the truth. She is afraid that we see how unhappy she is. She wears her finery like armor, but it does not protect her from the cold.”

Eleanor turned then, her gaze meeting Julia’s. There was a sadness in her eyes, a recognition of the pain that lay beneath the surface of their cousin’s composure. “You are not wrong,” she admitted. “I have seen it in the way she touches her wedding ring, as if it were a chain. I have seen it in the way she checks the clock, not for punctuality, but for the hope that her husband might return. He is rarely here, Julia. And when he is, he is distant. She has traded her freedom for a roof over her head, and she is paying for it in silence.”

Julia looked away, her resentment softening into a more complex emotion. “But why must we suffer for her misfortune? We are here to be guests, not to witness her misery. I want to be treated with respect, Eleanor. I want to be invited to supper, to be asked about our health, to be reminded that we are family. Instead, I am a shadow in her hallway, a nuisance in her drawing room.”

Eleanor rose from her chair and moved to stand beside Julia. She placed a hand on her sister’s shoulder, a gesture of comfort that was more spoken in its silence than in any words. “We are here because we had nowhere else to go. Our father’s debts have left us with little but our pride, and even that is wearing thin. Hester knows this. She knows that we are here out of necessity, not choice. And that is why she is so careful. She does not want to see our disappointment. She does not want to add to our burden.”

At that moment, the door opened, and Hester Vane entered the room. She was dressed in a gown of deep blue silk, her hair arranged in the latest fashion, her face composed in an expression of polite neutrality. She carried a book in her hand, a volume of poetry that she did not open.
