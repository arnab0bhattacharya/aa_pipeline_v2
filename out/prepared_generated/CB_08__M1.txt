The snow had not ceased for nine days. It lay upon the moor in a great white sheet, iron-hard where the wind had scoured it, and heaped in fantastic drifts against the grey walls of Thistlewood Farm. Within, the fire in the parlour grate fought a losing war against the cold that crept in at the sills and under the doors. The room was thick with the shadows of illness, the air heavy with the scent of fever and the bitter steam of a cooling draught.

For four months I had known no world but this room, no duty but the care of my aunt, Mrs. Sarah Gresham. My own mother’s sister, she had been my sole refuge since I was ten, and in her declining strength I had found my purpose. I had read to her when her eyes were dim, warmed the sheets that chilled her, and learned the meaning of patience in the long, silent watches of the night. Her will, a document of plain legal phrasing, named me her sole inheritor. It was not a fortune, but it was a roof, a livelihood, a foothold upon the earth. It was mine.

Then came the lawyer, Mr. Croft, his face pinched and blue with cold, shaking the snow from his greatcoat in the passage. He brought with him not only the chill of the outdoors but a chill of a different, more penetrating kind. He had been to see Mrs. Gresham’s solicitor in York. There was a codicil. A late one, signed but a fortnight before the illness had settled deep in her lungs. It named her nephew, my cousin, Mr. Edward Vane of Scarborough.

I did not cry out. I did not faint. I stood by the dying fire and felt the blood drain from my face as water drains from a broken vessel. I merely asked, “Why?”

Mr. Croft would not meet my eye. “He is the male heir, Miss Arden. The name. It is the family name. Mrs. Gresham felt it her duty to the line.” He spoke of duty as if it were a sacred thing, yet he shuffled his papers and mumbled of provisions, of a small sum for my maintenance until I should find a position. A position! As if the world were a marketplace where a woman, alone and friendless, could hawk her qualifications for service.

Edward Vane. I had seen him perhaps twice in my life. A foppish young man with a vacant smile and a taste for fashionable dissipation. He had never written to his aunt, never sent a token of remembrance at Christmas, never made the journey across the county in all the years of her illness. He did not know the sound of her cough, the colour of her fevered eyes, the brave way she fought for every breath. I had held her hand when it grew cold; he would inherit the fire that had warmed it. The injustice of it was a physical blow. It struck me in the breast, a hard, hot stone of fury and grief. Scripture rose unbidden to my lips: *The labourer is worthy of his hire.* I had laboured. I had given my youth to this service. And my hire was to be cast out, penniless, upon the mercy of a world that had shown me none.

Mr. Croft left, and the silence he left behind was more terrible than any noise. The wind howled about the eaves like a hungry beast, and in the next room, my aunt’s breathing was a shallow, ragged thread. I was free. The word tasted like ash in my mouth. I was free of the sickroom, of the long nights, of the scent of death. And I was penniless, homeless, a stray. The two conditions warred within me, a terrible and bitter freedom.

Two days later, a letter was brought by the post-boy, who looked half-frozen himself. It was from Mrs. Beatrice Blackwood of Stonegate Grange. I knew the name, and a colder dread settled upon me than any the winter winds could bring. The Blackwoods were a family of consequence in the district, their Grange a handsome house that stood in stark contrast to our humble farm. But their consequence was of the frostiest order. Mrs. Blackwood’s eyes were like chips of flint, her smile a mere rearrangement of the muscles of her face. Her charity, when she practised it, was a weapon of condescension.

Her letter was brief, formal, and utterly without warmth. She had heard of my sad bereavement and my altered circumstances. She could, as a Christian duty, offer me the post of companion to her invalid daughter, Miss Clara. My duties would be light—reading, needlework, company. My board and lodging would be provided, together with a salary of five pounds per annum. The letter did not ask; it commanded. It was a life-raft, but a raft of splintered, unforgiving oak.

I read the letter twice, my hands trembling not with cold, but with a tempest of conflicting feeling. To accept was to sell myself. To enter Stonegate Grange was to walk into a prison of polished floors and silent judgments. I had seen the way Mrs. Blackwood looked at those she deemed beneath her. I would be less than a servant, for a servant has a trade, while a companion is a dependent, a creature of whim and caprice. The coldness of that house would be a different kind of death, a slow freezing of the spirit.

But the alternative? The workhouse? The streets of some distant town, my hands untrained for any labour but nursing the sick and dying? I looked around the little parlour, at the worn armchair where my aunt used to sit, at the window that framed the bleak, beautiful expanse of the moor. This was my home. To leave it was agony. To have no home to go to was terror.
