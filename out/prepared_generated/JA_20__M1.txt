The advantages of Bath, so universally acknowledged, were to Commander Evelyn a source of considerable vexation. He had consented to accompany his sister, Miss Isabella Thorne, to that city of diversions for the sake of her health and spirits, which had been languid after a long winter in the country; but he found the exercise of his own patience to be the more arduous task. The air, which was pronounced to be of sovereign virtue, seemed to him merely damp; and the society, celebrated for its elegance and animation, appeared a tiresome round of insipidity. It was a world of manners, not of merit, and his naval life had accustomed him to a more direct and consequential way of judging.

His sister, however, prospered. The daily walk in the Pump Room, the evening’s card assembly, and the general obligation to appear cheerful had a most salutary effect. Isabella was naturally of a pleasing disposition, and being of an age to be sensible of society’s approbation, she entered into its pleasures with a will that her brother could not help but admire, even as he lamented its objects. She was handsome, too, in a style that drew the notice of the room, and it was not long before that notice became concentrated in one particular person.

This was Mr. Alistair Finch, a gentleman whose fortune was said to be as ample as his complacency. He was possessed of a fashionable address, a well-filled coat, and a talent for uttering commonplaces with an air of profound discovery. Upon being introduced to Miss Thorne, he was struck by her beauty, and his admiration, which was of the most public kind, soon became the topic of the Pump Room. He attended her in the walks, secured her a place at the card table, and brought her the morning’s newspaper with a devotion that was universally remarked upon.

To Commander Evelyn, this gentleman was an object of immediate and unqualified disgust. He saw in Mr. Finch a perfect specimen of that class of man he most despised: one whose value was entirely external, whose opinions were borrowed, and whose character was a mere varnish of good breeding over a hollow core. The man’s praise of the navy was particularly offensive to him. He would speak of “our gallant tars” and “the glory of Nelson” with a fluency that suggested he had learned the phrases from a playbill, and his ignorance of the service’s real hardships and duties was matched only by his confidence in discoursing upon them.

The Commander’s first impulse was to forbid the acquaintance outright. He was her elder brother, her protector; the man was unworthy of her notice. But a moment’s reflection presented the absurdity of such a command. Isabella was of age, her own mistress in every particular. To forbid her would be to confess a want of authority he did not possess, and to place her in the unenviable position of choosing between a brother’s prejudice and a gentleman’s attentions. He knew his sister well enough to be sure that such a course would only make her resolve more firm. She had a certain spirit of contradiction, which, though usually kept in check by a good temper, was not to be trifled with.

He resolved, therefore, upon a more subtle, though to him a more trying, line of conduct. He would not command, but he would caution; he would not forbid, but he would make his disapprobation so plainly felt as to render the acquaintance disagreeable to her. It required a patience he little possessed. He would sit with them, a silent and disapproving third, his short replies and averted gaze serving as a constant, if unspoken, reprimand.

One afternoon, as the three of them were walking in Sydney Gardens, Mr. Finch was holding forth on the necessity of a strong navy to counter the French threat. “One must have a firm hand at the tiller, as it were,” he concluded, with a simpering glance at Isabella.

The Commander, who had been walking a little apart, could contain himself no longer. “A firm hand is of little use,” he said, his voice quiet but sharp, “if the man at the tiller does not know the soundings from the rocks.”

Mr. Finch looked blank. “I beg your pardon, Commander?”

“I mean, Sir, that a knowledge of the principles of navigation is more valuable than a taste for nautical metaphors.” The rebuke was coolly delivered, but it landed with the force of a broadside. Mr. Finch coloured and fell silent, while Isabella shot her brother a look of profound irritation. She said nothing until that evening, when, as they sat in their lodgings, she finally addressed the matter.

“Edward,” she began, her voice strained with an effort at calm, “I must ask you what you mean by such treatment of Mr. Finch.”

“I mean only to show him for what he is,” the Commander replied, not looking up from his book. “A coxcomb.”

“He is a very respectable gentleman.”

“He is a man who knows the price of everything and the value of nothing. He admires your beauty, Isabella, as he would admire a well-made carriage, and with as little thought for the person within.”

“And you presume to know his thoughts?” she cried, her temper rising. “You, who have known him a week, set yourself up as a judge of his character, while I, who have conversed with him daily, am a fool to be deceived?”

“You are not a fool,” he said, closing his book with a snap. “But you are a young woman, and he is a man who is very practiced in the art of pleasing young women. I see his character clearly. It is my duty to warn you.”

“It is not your duty to insult him! I am of age, Edward. I am capable of judging for myself.”
