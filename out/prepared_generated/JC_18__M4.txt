The affair began, as such affairs will, not with a thunderclap of revelation but with a quiet, insinuating mist of unease. It was on a Tuesday, I believe, in the late autumn of a year that now seems to belong to another life, when the leaves of the great chestnuts along the Promenade des Bastions had fallen into a sodden, yellow-brown carpet, and a fine, penetrating rain washed the grey stones of our city into a uniform and melancholy lustre. I was seated in my study, the smell of damp wool and old books heavy in the air, when my housekeeper announced the visit of Monsieur Ostrovsky.

He was one of that silent, brooding colony of Russian exiles who had washed up on our orderly shores, a man whose face was a map of old sorrows and whose eyes held the smouldering fire of a fanatic’s faith. In my congregation, he was a figure of some pity and not a little mystery. He spoke little, but when he did, it was with a formal, almost archaic politeness that seemed a relic of a vanished world. He stood before me, his hat held in both hands, his thin, scholar’s fingers worrying its brim, and the water from the mist clung in tiny, diamond-like beads to the shoulders of his threadbare greatcoat.

“Pastor Vautier,” he began, his voice low and strained, as though the words were being drawn from him by some painful internal pressure, “I come to you on a matter of great delicacy, a matter that touches upon the immortal soul.” He paused, and his gaze, which had been fixed upon a point just above my head, dropped to meet mine. In that look I saw a profound and weary supplication. “I am to be married,” he said, and the simple statement seemed to cost him a great effort. “And I would ask that you, and no other, perform the ceremony.”

I confess I was surprised, for he had ever seemed a man set apart, a being for whom the consolations of hearth and home were as remote as the steppes of his birth. I offered him my felicitations, but he cut them short with a small, impatient gesture of his hand.

“There is a condition,” he continued, his voice dropping to a near whisper. “A request. It is this: that you ask no questions. Neither of the bride, who is a stranger to this city, nor of me. The name under which I shall be married is not the name of my father.”

The silence that followed his words was profound, broken only by the gentle, persistent drumming of the rain against the window-panes. It was a silence that seemed to fill the room with a cold, grey light, the light of a world where the clear lines of duty were becoming blurred by the fog of human misery. A sacrament, I thought, resting upon a lie. The very idea was a canker at the heart of the thing. To invoke the blessing of God upon a union founded upon a falsehood seemed to me a kind of blasphemy, a mockery of the sacred words I was ordained to speak.

“Monsieur Ostrovsky,” I said, choosing my words with care, “you must know that what you ask is impossible. A marriage is not a mere civil contract. It is a covenant before God. How can I stand at His altar and ask His blessing upon a name that is not a name, upon a man who is not, in this moment, himself?”

He listened, his head bowed, his face a mask of suffering. When he looked up, his eyes were not angry, but filled with a vast and desolate patience. “You speak of truth, Pastor,” he said, and his voice held a strange, melancholy music. “But what is truth? Is it a name? A word spoken in a registry office? Or is it the thing itself, the love that binds two souls together in a world that seeks to tear them asunder?” He took a step closer, and I could smell the dampness of his clothes, the faint, sharp odour of a man who has walked long in the cold. “The name I bear is a burden I would not lay upon her. It is a name proscribed, a name that carries with it the shadow of the gallows or the salt mines of the east. To speak it here, in this sanctuary of peace, would be to invite the darkness in. It would be to put her in peril. The lie, Pastor, is the only shield I have for her. It is not a lie of convenience, but a lie of love.”

He fell silent, and I saw that his hands were trembling. He was not a man making a demand, but one offering a confession, laying bare a fragment of the tragic and violent drama from which he had fled. He was asking me to become a part of it, to lend the sanction of my office to his necessary deception. My mind recoiled from the proposition, yet my heart was moved by the profound and hopeless sincerity of the man. He was a creature of the storm, seeking a moment’s shelter, and he was asking me to be the keeper of the door, even if that door had to be marked with a false sign.

“And the lady?” I asked, my voice sounding strange and hollow in my own ears. “Does she know?”

“She knows everything,” he said, “and she forgives everything. She has a soul that would put many a saint to shame. Her name is Anna. She is waiting for me. We have nothing but each other, Pastor, and the hope of a peace that we have not yet found. Will you refuse us the one blessing that might consecrate that hope?”
