The lens was a thing of my own devising, a queer, unwieldy construction of ground glass and polished brass that I had bought from a German optical firm in Hamburg. I had it in mind for portraiture, to secure a depth of field and a clarity of definition that the standard Petzval lenses could not manage. It was, I told myself, a scientific instrument for an artistic purpose, and I, Arthur Penhaligon, was its master. My studio, ‘Penhaligon’s Photographic Emporium,’ looked out over the grey-green expanse of the sea from a first-floor room in the King’s Road, Brighton. It was a good enough business. The middle classes of London, on their holidays, required souvenirs of their vitality, and I provided them in the form of stiff, sepia-toned card mounts.

The first sittings with the new lens were, by any technical standard, a triumph. The detail was exquisite. One could count the threads in a gentleman’s waistcoat, see the faint stippling of rouge on a lady’s cheek, the very glint of humour—or more often, profound boredom—in a child’s eye. But there was an anomaly. Behind each sitter, in the soft-focus background of the velvet drapes or the painted pastoral scene, there was a… a suggestion. A smudge. A faint, pearlescent stain upon the emulsion, like a breath on a cold windowpane. It was nothing more than that at first. A flaw in my coating, I presumed, or a stray reflection from the gas lamps.

But the flaw was persistent. It appeared on every plate. And it was not a random blemish. With each new commission, the stain seemed more definite. It resolved, slowly, over the course of a dozen sittings, into a shape. A tall, attenuated form, vaguely human in its outline, but utterly without features. It stood directly behind the sitter, its height varying, its posture one of patient waiting. It was as if a shadow had been given substance and then denied the privilege of a face.

The public, of course, was delighted. “Oh, Mr. Penhaligon, how clever!” cried Mrs. Abernathy, a stout woman from Croydon whose portrait showed a distinct, columnar smudge behind her formidable bonnet. “Is it a spirit? A sort of angel watching over me?”

“It is an effect of the light, Madam,” I said, my voice dryer than I intended. “A trick of the new optical arrangement.”

“A lovely trick!” she beamed. “It gives it a certain… mystery. My friends will be green with envy.”

She was not alone. The ‘Phantom Portrait,’ as it came to be known, became my stock-in-trade. People requested it by name. They wanted their spectral guardian captured alongside them. They would crane their necks in the studio, trying to catch a glimpse of the phenomenon in the camera’s dark maw, and laugh when they saw nothing. The shapes, it seemed, existed only for the sensitised plate. I was hailed in the local gazette as a man of “novel artistic sensibilities.” I was, in short, a success.

But I was not deceived. I, a man of science, of emulsions and silver nitrate and the precise mechanics of the shutter, knew this was no mere effect of light. An effect of light does not *advance*. I began to keep a ledger. I made a chart, marking the position of each shape relative to the sitter. It was a simple matter of measurement. In the first portraits, the figures stood a good six feet behind the subject. In the later ones, the distance had halved. They were taller, too, and more defined. The pearlescent quality was hardening into something more solid, more grey. I could almost discern the texture of a long coat, the hollows where eyes ought to be.

My customers saw only novelty. I saw an inexorable, approaching presence. The studio, once my sanctuary of chemistry and commerce, began to feel like a cage. I would develop the plates in the red-lit darkroom with a growing sense of dread, the acid fumes catching in my throat. Each new image was a fresh data point in a terrifying progression. The shapes were no longer content to stand behind their sitters; they seemed to be leaning in, as if to whisper. In one particularly fine portrait of a young naval officer, the form behind him was so close that its shoulder appeared to merge with his own.

“Look at that!” the officer had exclaimed upon collecting his print. “The blighter’s practically breathing down my neck! A fine joke, a fine joke indeed.” He left a generous tip.

The joke, I felt sure, was on me. I alone was the witness to this slow, photographic invasion. I considered smashing the lens, casting the accursed German glass into the sea. But what then? The plates I had already taken were evidence of a phenomenon I did not understand. To destroy the instrument would be to turn my back on the only means of observing it. A scientist does not flee from his data, however monstrous it may be.

Last Tuesday, I made a decision. I would not wait for a customer. I would sit for my own portrait. It was a risk, of course. If the pattern held, the shape would appear behind me. But I needed to know. I set the timer on the shutter, arranged a plain backdrop to ensure the form would be unmistakable, and sat upon the stool. I stared into the lens of my own creation, my heart a frantic drum against my ribs. The flash powder exploded in a blinding, magnesium-white cloud.

I developed the plate myself, my hands shaking so violently I nearly dropped it in the fixing bath. As the image emerged in the developer, I felt a cold dread that was utterly alien to my practical nature. There I was, a gaunt, worried-looking man in a tweed suit. And behind me… it was not behind me. It was closer. The tall, grey, featureless shape was no longer in the background. Its long, indistinct hand rested on my shoulder. The place where its face should have been was a void, a perfect, circular emptiness, and it was bent towards my ear as if in the act of telling me a secret I did not wish to hear.
