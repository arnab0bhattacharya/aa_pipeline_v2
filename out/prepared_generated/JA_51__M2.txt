The village of Hartsham, in the fertile depths of Hampshire, was a place of such unremarkable tranquility that it seemed designed by Nature for the purpose of keeping secrets, rather than for the amusement of its inhabitants. It was here that the Missus Fairley resided in a house of respectable brick and modest pretensions, where the main domestic concern was the prolonged and tedious courtship of their eldest daughter, Miss Eliza Fairley, by Mr. Arthur Penrose. For two years, Mr. Penrose had paid his addresses with a regularity that bordered on the mechanical, visiting on Tuesdays and Fridays, walking with Eliza in the garden, and discussing the weather with a gravity that suggested he found the atmosphere of Hartsham particularly profound. Yet, in all this time, no proposal had been made, nor had any hint of future matrimonial arrangements been dropped so much as to be caught by the most attentive ear of the servants.

This state of affairs was entirely unsatisfactory to Miss Clara Fairley, the younger sister, who possessed a mind of quick perception and a temperament that abhorred stagnation. Clara was not a beauty, nor did she aspire to be; her charm lay in a certain sharp, observant intelligence that often led her to mistake her own impatience for benevolence. She watched her sister’s slow, deliberate progress with the anxiety of a spectator at a race where the horses are moving at a snail’s pace. She had formed an opinion, based on the universal truth that a gentleman who cannot declare his feelings in two years is either a fool or a scoundrel, that Mr. Penrose was likely the former, and perhaps the latter.

It was a Tuesday afternoon, the sky of a pale, undecided grey, when Clara resolved to intervene. Eliza had gone for her customary walk with Mr. Penrose, leaving Clara alone in the drawing room with a volume of poetry she had no intention of reading. Clara knew that Eliza’s silence was not the result of indifference, for her sister’s eyes had grown large and bright whenever the gentleman entered the room. The fault, Clara reasoned, lay in the lack of a catalyst. Mr. Penrose was too careful, too cautious, and entirely too polite to take the first step. He required a shove. And Clara, who considered herself a woman of decisive action, was prepared to provide it.

Her plan was simple in its conception, though its execution required a degree of theatrical dexterity that Clara had not previously known she possessed. She waited until the sound of their footsteps on the gravel path indicated they were returning. She then arranged herself in a chair near the window, holding a letter in her hand, and assumed an expression of such profound distress that it would have moved a statue. When the door opened and Mr. Penrose entered, followed by Eliza, Clara did not rise. She looked up with a gaze of pained reproach, as if the very air of the room had become contaminated by their presence.

“My sister,” Clara said, in a voice of trembling solemnity, “has been most unkind to me.”

Mr. Penrose stopped in the middle of the room, his hat in his hand, looking first at Clara and then at Eliza with an expression of mild bewilderment. Eliza, who had been in high spirits, suddenly turned a shade of crimson that was not entirely due to the walk.

“Unkind, Clara?” Eliza stammered. “I do not know what you mean. I have done nothing.”

“Have you not?” Clara pressed, tearing a corner from the letter she held. “Did you not tell me this morning that Mr. Penrose has spoken of marriage? That he is determined to secure your hand? And did you not promise to tell me? I have been pining for three hours with the knowledge that I am to be made a widow of my own expectations, and yet you sit here, silent as a tombstone.”

The silence that followed was of a quality usually reserved for the moments before a thunderclap. Mr. Penrose looked at Clara as if she had suddenly sprouted horns. Eliza looked at Clara as if she had committed a felony.

“Clara,” Eliza said, her voice very low, “you are mistaken. He has said nothing of the sort.”

“Mistaken!” Clara cried, standing up with a violence that caused the chair to scrape sharply against the floor. “Is that your answer? After what I overheard you whisper in the garden? You are cruel, Eliza. You prefer to keep him in suspense, to play with his affections, while I am left to wonder if I am to be thrown out of the family entirely. But I will not be trifled with. I have a letter from my cousin in London, who offers me a situation as a governess in a family of no particular distinction, provided I am free of all local entanglements. If you will not release me from the tyranny of your silence, I shall accept the offer. I shall leave Hartsham tomorrow, and Mr. Penrose may have his choice of a sister who is not so foolish as to expect a proposal.”

She turned and marched toward the door, her steps heavy with the weight of her own self-drama. Mr. Penrose, who had been standing perfectly still, now moved with a speed that belied his usual sedate nature. He caught Clara’s arm with a firmness that was not ungentle.

“Miss Fairley,” he said, his voice steady but lacking its usual melodic softness. “I believe you are laboring under a very significant misunderstanding.”

Clara stopped, her hand on the latch, but she did not turn to face him. She knew that if she turned, the mask of distress might slip.

“I am laboring under the pain of being lied to,” she said.
