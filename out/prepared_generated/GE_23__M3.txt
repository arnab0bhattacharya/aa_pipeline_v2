In the village of Thoresby-in-the-Marsh, where the willows stood knee-deep in the dykes as if contemplating their own inverted images, the domestic drama of the Linley family was unfolding with a quiet but inexorable pressure. Mr. Linley, a man whose intellectual rigour had been hardened by years of managing a stubborn clay soil, held firmly to the code that an Englishman’s word was his bond and his son’s honour was the prolongation of his own. To say he doted on his younger child would be to misrepresent the grave, somewhat severe texture of his paternal affection; rather, he invested thirteen-year-old Arthur with the concentrated hopes of a practical man who saw in the boy’s quickness and physical courage a triumphant refutation of his own youthful struggles with Latin and arithmetic. Arthur, with his clear blue eyes and a laugh that seemed to banish the damp Fenland mists, was the unchallenged sunlight of the Linley household.

Hetty, the elder by four years, walked in a more shadowy path. She possessed a plain face and a steady mind, and was accustomed to having her virtues praised as a sort of useful secondary crop, while Arthur’s imperfections were excused as the wild oats of a rich and generous nature. Yet it was Hetty, and not the admiring father, who knew the precise ratio of pride to moral laxity in her brother’s composition. She had seen him, on more than one occasion, slide with a charming adroitness from the edge of a falsehood, trusting to his affectionate exterior to coat the bare metal of deceit with a plausible enamel.

The crisis that now demanded her complicity had arrived by way of a letter from Mr. Vetch, the master of the Grammar School in Caistor. Arthur, so the letter stated, had been discovered with a translation of the prescribed Cicero passage concealed beneath his blotting paper—a crib, in the schoolboy vernacular, which rendered the laborious parsing of the previous fortnight entirely nugatory. Mr. Vetch, a man of rigorous conscience but limited insight into the variable chemistry of boyish motive, had stopped short of outright expulsion, pending an inquiry. He had written to Mr. Linley, requiring some assurance of the boy’s general character before deciding whether his continuance at the school was a benefit to himself or a detriment to his peers.

Mr. Linley received this communication with an anger that was the inverse of his love—a cold, concentrated fury that such a stain should be threatened upon the Linley escutcheon. That Arthur should be accused of this specific meanness was, to the father’s mind, a monstrous improbability. He sat in his book-room, the letter spread before him on the leather-topped desk, the scent of wax and damp wool filling the air, and summoned Hetty.

She came in with her usual quiet tread, her hands folded in front of her coarse apron. She was, in her father’s eyes, the reliable witness, the one whose slow, unadorned veracity would furnish the necessary counterweight to Mr. Vetch’s hasty misjudgment.

"Read that, Hetty," Mr. Linley said, tapping the paper with a forefinger that trembled slightly. "A foul calumny, is it not? Vetch writes that my boy, my Arthur, has smuggled a translation into the schoolroom like a common cheat. I have spoken to Arthur, and he denies it with the firmness of an innocent lad. He says it was placed there by Prior, a spiteful, dull-headed fellow who envies Arthur’s facility. I believe the boy. But Vetch demands a character reference from someone who knows Arthur’s habits at home. He asks for your testimony, Hetty, since you help him with his lessons. You will write to Mr. Vetch. You will tell him that Arthur’s nature is open and upright, and that he scorns a lie."

Mr. Linley spoke with the imperious certainty of a man who commands not only his household but the empirical facts of their existence. To him, Hetty’s affirmation was merely the formal seal upon a truth he had already stamped in his own mind.

But to Hetty, the request fell upon her consciousness with the weight of a geological disruption, dislodging the stratified layers of her quiet observance. For she knew, with a certainty that went down to the very marrow of her bones, that Arthur was guilty. Only three nights past, creeping down to the kitchen for a cup of water, she had seen the candlelight escaping from the crack of the schoolroom door, and peering through, had witnessed her brother bent over a tattered, annotated edition of Cicero, copying the English renderings onto a slip of paper. She had said nothing then, paralysed by the very affection she bore him, hoping the temptation had been a fleeting sickness, a temporary failure of the moral digestive system, which would right itself without the need for surgical interference.

Now, however, the disease had presented its symptoms to the world, and she was asked to administer the palliative of a falsehood.

Her heart beat with a heavy, irregular rhythm. She looked at her father’s stern, expectant face, at the deep lines of indignation grooved around his mouth, and then her thoughtswandered to Arthur—Arthur, who had only that morning wept in her room, not with the remorse of a repentant sinner, but with the terrified fury of a trapped animal, begging her not to expose him, clinging to her hands with the very warmth that made him so dear. She saw his blue eyes, swimming in tears, and felt the terrible pull of that blood-tie which weaves itself into the fibres of our judgement long before reason has learned to assert its cold, severing blade.

To tell the truth was to cast her brother out of the school, to blight his prospects, and, perhaps more poignantly, to shatter the beautiful image her father carried in his heart—the image upon which his own later happiness so largely depended. To lie was to poison the very wellspring of her own life, to degrade the one virtue for which she had been consistently valued. It is in such moments of divided impulse that the human soul reveals its most intricate and sorrowful mechanism; for loyalty, when it demands the sacrifice of truth, becomes a species of treason against the self.
