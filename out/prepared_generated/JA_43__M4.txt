The rain, which had begun in the night, showed no inclination to abate, and by the second morning of her visit to Ashcombe, Miss Eleanor Whitfield had exhausted the resources of the library, the pianoforte, and her own patience. Her sister Margaret, she was fond of observing, possessed a temperament so placid as to be almost unfeeling; and it was this very placidity which made a week in her company, in a house belonging to their aunt, and in weather which forbade every species of exercise, a trial rather than a pleasure. Eleanor loved her sister, of course; one could not be a Whitfield and not love one’s own family; but she had long ago decided, and with the comfortable certainty of a mind which had never been contradicted, that Margaret’s heart was a vessel of small capacity, fitted only for the calmest waters.

It was with this conviction firmly in place that Eleanor, seeking a book which her aunt had promised her, and which she had been told might be found in the upper drawer of the writing-desk in Margaret’s room, went one afternoon into that apartment, which her sister had quitted for the garden on a sudden clearing of the sky. The drawer was easily opened; the book was not immediately apparent; and in the course of a search which was perfectly innocent, and which she would have described to anyone as such, her hand encountered, beneath a quantity of ribbon and a folded shawl, a packet of letters tied with a narrow green riband.

She had no intention of reading them. She would have said so, and said it with perfect sincerity, had anyone thought to ask. But the uppermost letter had slipped from its fellows at her touch, and her eye, before she could command it, had caught the superscription and the opening line, and in that instant the world of her sister’s character, which she had believed as fixed and familiar as the furniture of the house, was silently overturned.

The letter was addressed to Margaret, and the hand was that of a gentleman. It spoke of a walk in the shrubbery, of a promise made beneath the old cedar, of a heart which could know no peace until it was assured of hers. Eleanor read no further. She sat down upon the edge of the bed, the letter trembling in her fingers, and endeavoured to compose her thoughts, which had never in her life been so disordered. Margaret, who never spoke of feeling, who received every compliment with the same mild smile, who had declared only last winter that she could not imagine why anyone should make themselves so unhappy about a matter of such small consequence as marriage—Margaret, who had been for three years the calm centre of every family gathering, and whom Eleanor had privately pitied for the narrowness of her sensibilities—Margaret had loved, and been beloved, and had kept the secret from every soul in the world.

A second letter, and a third, confirmed what the first had begun. They were not the effusions of a mere acquaintance. They spoke of months, of constancy, of an attachment which had grown in silence and been nourished in secret. There was one passage, in a hand less steady than the rest, which entreated her to believe that no obstacle could be insurmountable if she would but consent to speak; and another, in reply, though the reply was in Margaret’s own writing, and had never been sent, which spoke of duty, and of a father’s expectations, and of a resolution to bear what must be borne. Eleanor laid the packet down as though it had burned her. She understood, in a moment which she would have given much to unlearn, that her sister had not been incapable of feeling; she had only been capable of concealing it, and the concealment had cost her more than Eleanor had ever thought to spend on anything.

She was still sitting there, the letters in her lap, when the door opened and Margaret came in, her cheeks flushed with the air, and her bonnet in her hand. She stopped upon the threshold. Her eyes went from Eleanor’s face to the packet, and the colour which the walk had brought to her cheeks drained away entirely.

For a moment neither spoke. Then Margaret crossed the room, took the letters from her sister’s hands with a composure which was more terrible than any outcry, and tied the riband afresh.

“You have read them,” she said. It was not a question.

“I did not mean—” Eleanor began, and found that the words would not serve. “I came for a book. The drawer was open. I saw only a little, but I saw enough.”

Margaret stood at the window, her back to the room. The rain had begun again, and the garden beyond the glass was grey and indistinct. “And what is it that you have seen?” she asked, in a voice which was almost her ordinary voice, and which betrayed, to a sister’s ear, a trembling that was new to it.

“That you have loved,” said Eleanor, “and that you have been unhappy, and that you have told no one. That I have lived beside you these three years and known nothing of it. That I have thought you cold, Margaret, and said so, and believed it, when all the while—” She stopped, for her own voice had begun to fail her.

Margaret turned. Her face was pale, but there was in it a dignity which Eleanor had never before remarked, and which she now recognised as the very quality she had mistaken for insensibility. “It was not yours to know,” she said. “It was not anyone’s. There were reasons—there are reasons still—why it could never be spoken of. I do not ask you to understand them. I ask only that you will not speak of what you have found, to my aunt, or to anyone.”
