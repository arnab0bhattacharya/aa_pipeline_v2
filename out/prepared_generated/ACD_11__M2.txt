The fog that morning lay thick upon the Thames, a grey, oppressive shroud that seemed to swallow the cobblestones of Southwark whole. It was not the romantic mist of the poets, but a foul, industrial miasma, reeking of coal smoke and stagnant water, which clung to the clothes and bit into the lungs. I stood on the threshold of the small, whitewashed vicarage in St. Jude’s lane, watching the droplets condense on the glass panes, while my companion, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, paced the length of the narrow hallway with that peculiar, rhythmic stride that betrayed a mind in rapid, turbulent motion.

“The atmosphere here, Watson, is peculiarly charged,” he said, stopping abruptly to examine a smudge on the wall. He sniffed the air, his nostrils flaring. “There is a scent of sulfur and... is that camphor? And beneath it, the damp, earthy smell of a cellar long unused. The Reverend Aldous has been unwell, you say? Not physically, I trust, but mentally?”

I nodded, glancing at the door of the study, which remained tightly shut. “He is a man of great standing, Holmes. His sermons are known for their clarity and moral force. But since his return from the parish visit last Tuesday, he has changed beyond recognition. He sits in the dark. He writes letters, hour after hour, and then, at the break of dawn, he carries them to the kitchen fire and watches them burn. He will not speak of it. He will not preach on Sunday. My sister, Elara, is terrified. She believes he has been threatened, or perhaps corrupted by the poverty he sought to alleviate.”

Holmes raised an eyebrow, a flicker of interest crossing his sharp features. “A clergyman who burns his correspondence at dawn. That is a most unusual habit. Usually, men who destroy letters are hiding secrets from others; men who burn them at a specific hour are often hiding secrets from themselves. Come, Watson. We shall see if the Reverend Aldous is a man of broken nerve or of broken conscience.”

We knocked, and after a long, hesitant pause, the door was opened not by the Reverend, but by a maid of scrubbed but anxious demeanor. She led us into the study, a room that smelled strongly of pipe tobacco and old paper. The Reverend sat hunched in a high-backed chair, his face pale as the wall behind him, his hands trembling as he held a quill. He looked up, and I saw in his eyes a mixture of shame and profound despair.

“Mr. Holmes,” he whispered, his voice cracking. “You are the man they say can see through the fog. I have seen something in the shadow of the river that has unmade me. I cannot speak it. I cannot write it. I can only destroy the words before they take root.”

Holmes moved to the window, pushing aside the heavy curtains. The view was blocked by the roof of a neighboring warehouse, but the sound of the river was a dull, constant roar. “And what did you see, Reverend? In the poor street by the water?”

“The street is called Mudgate’s Alley. It is a dead end, a slum that the city has forgotten. I went there to distribute alms, as is my custom. I saw a woman, Mrs. Gable, who has been on my parish list for years. She was weeping. Not the weeping of grief, but of terror. She pulled me aside, her face black with soot, and showed me a box. It was full of coins. Not charity, Mr. Holmes, but stolen goods. Gold rings, silver spoons, coins from other countries. She confessed that the men in the alley—the dockworkers who should be pulling up the anchors of the ships—are pulling up the bottom of the river instead. They are fishing for lost treasures from wrecks that happened fifty years ago, and they are selling them to a man who never shows his face. I tried to stop them. I spoke of God’s judgment. And they laughed. They laughed, and then they pointed to the water and said, ‘The river keeps its secrets, Father, and so must you.’”

Holmes turned from the window, his eyes narrow and bright. “A classic case of moral confusion, Reverend. You have discovered a crime, but you have made yourself an accomplice by your silence. And this fear of the ‘faceless man’—is it based on fact or superstition?”

“It is fact,” the Reverend said, slamming his hand on the desk. “I saw him. Or I thought I did. As I left the alley, a figure stood in the mist. He wore a long coat and a wide-brimmed hat. I could not see his face, but I saw his hand. It was stained with the same greenish ink that Mrs. Gable uses to mark her ledgers. He held a lantern. The light hit his chest, and I saw a badge. Not a police badge, but a guild mark. The mark of the Watermen’s Union.”

Holmes strode to the fireplace, where a single sheet of paper lay in the ashes. He picked up a poker and pried the charred fragment from the fire. He held it up to the light, examining the edges with intense concentration.

“Interesting,” he murmured. “The paper is cheap, but the ink is of a high grade. Look here, Watson. The burn pattern. It was not thrown into the fire. It was held over the flame, slowly, deliberately. The writer was watching it die. And look at the corner—there is a stain. Not ink, but wax. Sealing wax. He was sealing the letter. He intended to send it. But he changed his mind at the last second. Why? Because the recipient was dangerous.”

“Who is the recipient?” I asked.

“The faceless man,” Holmes said. “But let us not guess. Let us look. You said the alley is a dead end? And the men are dockworkers?”

“Yes.”
