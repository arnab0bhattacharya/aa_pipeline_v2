# The Elderly Gentleman

The Hackney coach had not yet drawn to a halt before Harriet was wishing herself anywhere else. The noise of Bond Street at dusk was a great deal more than she had been prepared for, and the sight of Mrs Fanshawe's townhouse — four white stories tall, with a knockered door the size of a barn — made her feel suddenly and acutely the daughter of a Leicestershire curate.

"I am very sure you will do," said Cecily Fanshawe, already pulling on her gloves as the coachman let down the steps. "You will do very well. Only do not say anything about pigs."

"I have never spoken of pigs in my life."

"I know. Only do not begin."

Harriet had been in London six days. They had been six days of being dressed and displayed and presented to a succession of Cecily's acquaintances, all of whom spoke to her with the particular gentleness reserved for the recently orphaned, the chronically shy, or those whose fathers earned less than three hundred a year. She had borne it because Cecily was kind and because she had nowhere else to go. Her father had died in February, and her uncle's house in Leicestershire, where she had been sent to live, was full of cousins who looked at her as though she were a piece of misplaced furniture. When Cecily's letter had arrived — *Come to me at once, I have a spare room and a great many bonnets and we shall go to assemblies and you shall forget all your troubles* — Harriet had accepted with a gratitude so fierce it had frightened her.

She had not mentioned Mr Edmund Hale.

The assembly was held at the Ellsworth Rooms, a long, bright chamber lined with mirrors and candles, and already so crowded that the heat struck them like a wall. Cecily was immediately seized upon by a young woman in yellow, and Harriet, after a moment's hesitation, moved to a position near one of the pillars and tried to look as though she had chosen it.

She watched the room move around her. She watched officers in red coats and ladies with feathers in their hair. She watched Cecily laugh and gesture, her dark curls bouncing, entirely at home. She watched the older women sitting along the wall, fanning themselves with an air of permanent disapproval. She watched the doorway, because watching the doorway was easier than meeting anyone's eye, and because there was always the possibility — small, persistent, unshakeable — that Edmund Hale might walk through it.

She did not want to see him. That was the whole point of London. She had come to London to be where he was not.

Edmund Hale had been at her uncle's house in April. He was a friend of her cousin Thomas, up from Oxford, and he had been everything that was charming and attentive for the space of a fortnight, and at the end of the fortnight he had said things that Harriet had no business remembering, and then he had gone away without saying anything further, and she had spent May and June in a state of mortification so complete that she had not been able to eat breakfast without crying.

So she had come to London, to forget.

She was standing at her pillar, watching the doorway, when a voice beside her said: "You have the look of a person guarding a fortification. Is the pillar a strategic asset, or merely a friend?"

Harriet turned. The speaker was an elderly gentleman, perhaps seventy, with white hair and a face that seemed to have been designed for the specific purpose of amusement. His eyes were small and bright, and there were lines around his mouth that suggested a lifetime of smiling at inappropriate moments.

"I — I beg your pardon," said Harriet.

"I beg yours. I have a habit of speaking to people who look as though they would rather be elsewhere. It is a compulsion. My late wife called it a disease." He made a small bow. "Cornelius Hale, at your service. Are you one of Mrs Fanshawe's party?"

Harriet felt the floor tilt beneath her. She had a sensation of great heat, then great cold, then a return to ordinary temperature, as though her body could not decide how to receive the name.

"Yes," she said. "I am staying with Mrs Fanshawe."

"Capital woman. I knew her mother. Are you fond of dancing? You do not look fond of dancing. You look as though you would prefer a book and a fire. I entirely sympathise. I have not danced since 1798, and the occasion was a mistake."

Harriet, who had not laughed at anything in three months, found herself laughing. It surprised her so much that she stopped abruptly, and Mr Hale looked pleased.

"There," he said. "That is better. A young woman at an assembly should either be dancing or laughing, and since I cannot offer you the one, I shall do my best to provide the other. May I sit beside you? I should warn you that I am very good company, but only for about twenty minutes, after which I repeat myself and become tedious."

He sat without waiting for an answer, and Harriet found herself seated beside him on a small gilt chair, and the evening, which had been stretching before her like an empty corridor, suddenly became bearable.

Mr Hale talked. He talked about the assembly, about the music, about the quality of the lemonade, about a dog he had owned in 1794, about the relative merits of various types of cake. He asked Harriet where she was from, and when she said Leicestershire, he exclaimed that he had been born in Leicestershire, in a village so small it had no name, only a crossroads and a church. He asked about her family, and she told him, carefully, about her father, and Mr Hale became quiet for a moment and said, with a gentleness that surprised her, that he was very sorry.
