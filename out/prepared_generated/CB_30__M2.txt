The rain lashed against the windowpanes of the Hotel de la Croix Blanche, a rhythmic, ceaseless drumming that seemed to mock the stillness of the room. I sat by the fire, the flames casting long, trembling shadows across the walls, and tried to compose myself. It was no use. My heart beat with a violence that bordered on pain, and my hands, clasped tightly in my lap, trembled with a chill that the heat of the grate could not dispel.

Mr. Arthur Penhaligon stood by the window, his back to me, watching the storm rage over the city of Lyon. He was a man of perhaps thirty, handsome in a cold, aristocratic sort of way, with a face that was always composed, always watchful. He had been in France for a month, traveling light and fast, and had arrived at the hotel only two days before I did. We had spoken little, except in the library, where he had taken a seat near me and watched me read with an intensity that made me feel exposed, as if I were a specimen pinned beneath glass.

“You look as if you are expecting a ghost, Miss Elphinstone,” he said, his voice low and smooth, cutting through the sound of the rain. He turned to face me then, and his eyes, dark and deep, held a look that was neither kind nor cruel, but something far more unsettling: absolute indifference.

“I am expecting nothing,” I replied, striving to keep my voice steady. “I am merely tired. It is a long day’s work to teach twenty children to conjugate verbs, and then to return to a room that smells of damp wool and foreign soap.”

“Damp wool and foreign soap,” he repeated, a faint smile playing on his lips. “A poetic description of your existence. You are a prisoner, Miss Elphinstone. A prisoner of your own duty, and of a life that has no room for passion.”

“I have never claimed to seek passion,” I said, and the words came out sharper than I intended. I looked away, staring at the fire. “I have my work. I have my students. I have my independence. Is that not enough?”

“Independence?” He laughed, a short, dry sound. “It is a cold word for a woman who has never known the warmth of a home that is not a hotel room, or a husband who is not a stranger. You are alone, Eliza. You are so profoundly alone that you have forgotten what it feels like to be seen. I see you. I see the fire in your eyes, the strength in your jaw, and the deep, quiet sorrow that you hide behind your books.”

I felt a flush rise to my cheeks, hot and sudden. “You know nothing of my sorrow, Mr. Penhaligon. You are a tourist, passing through. You see the surface and mistake it for the truth.”

“I see the truth,” he said, stepping closer. The firelight caught the planes of his face, highlighting the sharpness of his cheekbones, the severity of his brow. “I see a woman who is wasting her life in a provincial schoolroom, teaching French to children who will never understand the nuance of their own language. I see a woman who is capable of more. I see a woman who needs a home.”

“And what do you offer?” I asked, my voice barely above a whisper. “A hotel room in London? A carriage ride to a country estate where I shall be expected to smile and obey? You offer me a change of scenery, but not a change of fate.”

“I offer you a life,” he said. “I offer you a home where you will be respected, not merely tolerated. I offer you a husband who will not ask you to forget your books, but will read them with you. I offer you a place in the world, Eliza. I am wealthy, yes, but I am not cruel. I am bored, yes, but I am not heartless. I am lonely, as you are lonely. And I propose that we go to England together. Not as master and servant, not as teacher and pupil, but as husband and wife.”

The silence that followed was heavy, thick with the scent of woodsmoke and the distant sound of thunder. I looked at him, really looked at him, and I saw the hunger in his eyes. It was not the hunger of love, but the hunger of possession. He wanted to own me, to make me part of his collection, to add me to the gallery of his experiences. He was bored, and I was new. I was different. I was a challenge.

“And if I refuse?” I asked.

“Then you will stay here,” he said, his voice dropping to a murmur. “You will continue to teach your verbs. You will continue to be alone. And in ten years, you will look back on this moment and wonder why you did not take your chance. You will wonder why you did not trust a man who saw you clearly. You will wonder why you chose the safety of your solitude over the danger of my company.”

I felt a surge of anger, fierce and hot, rising in my chest. It was not anger at him, but at the situation, at the city, at the life I had led. I was tired of being safe. I was tired of being small. But was I tired enough to throw myself into the arms of a stranger? Was I tired enough to believe that his words were anything more than the idle chatter of a bored man?

“You are a liar, Mr. Penhaligon,” I said, my voice hard and clear. “You are not bored. You are cruel. You see my loneliness and you exploit it. You do not want a wife. You want a conquest. And I will not be your conquest.”

He stared at me, his face unreadable. For a moment, I thought he might be angry. But then he smiled, a sad, weary smile.
