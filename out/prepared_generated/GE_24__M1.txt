It is one of the ironies of a certain temperament, that the very ardour which enables a man to confront great difficulties, renders him a coward before the small, quiet tests of domestic feeling; and Julian Lathom, walking from the gate of the rectory to the great door, was conscious of a timidity in his heart which made the familiar gravel seem treacherous under his feet. He had come from Cambridge with a defeat fresh upon him—the loss of the Bell Scholarship, for which he had laboured with a concentration that had left him pale and thin—and he had come to announce a resolution born of that defeat, a resolution which was in truth a flight. He meant to abandon the life of scholarship, which had been the object of his guardian’s sacrifice and hope, and to go out into the world as a traveller and a seeker after the picturesque and the strange. He was twenty-two, and the world, in his imagination, was a vast theatre of novel sensations, where the mind, freed from the tyranny of Greek metres and metaphysical distinctions, might expand in a more generous atmosphere. But as he stood in the hall, shaking the rain from his coat, he felt that the world he was about to enter was a far less formidable arena than the study where Mr. Cuthbert Renn sat at this hour of the evening.

Mr. Renn was a man of the old school, a clergyman whose learning was of a solid, if somewhat rigid, cast, and who had given up the better part of his own prospects to secure for Julian the education he himself had lacked. He was sitting in his accustomed chair, a volume of Thucydides open on the table beside him, though his eyes were fixed on the fire, and he looked up with a smile of welcome that struck Julian like a blow. There was no reproach in the greeting; only a serene satisfaction, as of a workman seeing the tools he has sharpened put to use. “Well, my boy,” said Mr. Renn, in his slow, measured voice, “and what says the Senate House? I trust you have vindicated our expectations.”

Julian felt the blood rush to his face. He had prepared a speech, an eloquent and somewhat cynical apology for the futility of academic honours, but it died in his throat. He could not meet the old man’s gaze. “I have failed, sir,” he said, and the words, simple as they were, seemed to strip him of all his acquired sophistication.

There was a silence, not of anger, but of a heavy, settling weight. Mr. Renn drew his hand slowly across his forehead, as if to brush away a sudden mist. “Failed?” he repeated. “That is a hard word, Julian. The examination was doubtless severe.”

“It is not the examination,” Julian burst out, compelled to speak the truth which was more cruel than the failure. “It is that I cannot go on. I am weary of it all. I have resolved to go abroad—to see the world. I cannot live any longer among books.”

He looked up then, expecting the storm; expecting the indignation of a man who has been robbed of his investment. But Mr. Renn only looked at him with a profound, searching gaze, and then, rising slowly, went to the window and looked out at the darkening yew-trees. The silence was harder to bear than any anger. It was the silence of a judgment that is already passed, and which leaves the culprit to the torture of his own conscience. Julian felt that he had not merely failed a prize; he had failed a life, and that the old man’s silence was the measure of the ruin he had wrought.

“I have thought it over, sir,” Julian continued, his voice sounding thin and loud in the quiet room. “I am not fitted for the life you designed for me. The pursuit of learning is a treadmill. I want to breathe—to see men and cities.”

Mr. Renn turned from the window. His face was very pale, but his voice was steady. “And how will you live, Julian? Have you considered that?”

“I have a little money,” Julian said evasively. “And I have my wits. I shall write—I shall observe. I shall not be a burden to you.”

“I have never thought of you as a burden,” Mr. Renn said, and there was a tremor in the old voice that made Julian wince. “I thought of you as my successor in the things of the mind. I thought I was laying up a treasure in a field.”

The metaphor, drawn from the familiar parable, pierced Julian’s defence. He saw the years of economy, the careful selection of books, the patient coaching in the rectory study, all swept away by his caprice. He felt a sudden, fierce resentment that the old man should make him feel this guilt, that he should not rage and so give him the excuse of righteous rebellion. But Mr. Renn only sat down again and took up his book, though he did not read.

“You are your own master, Julian,” he said at last. “I have no legal power over you. If you wish to go, you must go. But I confess I had hoped for other things.”

He said no more. He did not ask where Julian was going, or when he would return. He simply accepted the blow with a stoicism that was more terrible than passion. Julian stood there for a few moments, feeling like a thief who has been allowed to keep his plunder because the owner is too noble to wrestle with him. He wanted to be struck, to be cursed, to be told he was an ingrate; but he was met only with this sorrowful acquiescence.

“I am sorry, sir,” Julian whispered, the words inadequate and mean.
