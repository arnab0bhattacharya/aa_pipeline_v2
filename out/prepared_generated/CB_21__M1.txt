The hall of Garth Hall was cold, and the silence within it was colder still. It was the silence of a house where a verdict has been passed, not the silence of peace. Mrs. Vane stood before me, her face a mask of starched indignation, her hands clasped over the silver chatelaine at her waist. The fire in the grate spat a feeble protest against the gloom, but it could not warm the chill that had settled deep in my bones.

“You will understand, Miss Wray,” she said, her voice as thin and sharp as a shard of ice, “that this is a matter of the most delicate family honour. We cannot have a governess who encourages the attentions of a gentleman, particularly when that gentleman is the heir to this estate.”

I felt the blood rise to my face, a hot tide of shame and fury. “Madam,” I began, my voice trembling with the effort to keep it steady, “I have done no such thing. Mr. Edmund has been kind enough to speak to me on matters of literature and music, as any employer might to a governess. There has been nothing in my conduct to warrant such a monstrous accusation.”

“Monstrous?” she echoed, a thin smile touching her lips. “It is monstrous that a person in your position should not know the meaning of propriety. Edmund himself has confirmed the… impropriety of the situation.”

There it was. The final, cowardly blow. Edmund, with his gentle eyes and his borrowed verses, who had spoken of feeling and of injustice, who had looked at me as if I were the only soul in the world who understood him, had thrown me to the wolves to save his own soft skin. The knowledge struck me not like a dagger, but like a stone, blunt and heavy. I looked past Mrs. Vane, through the tall window, at the bleak Lincolnshire landscape. The fields were a uniform grey under a sky like hammered lead. A squall was gathering on the horizon, a smudge of charcoal against the pallor.

“I see,” I said, and my voice was now as hard as the stone in my heart. “And my wages?”

“Your wages will be paid to the end of the month. But you will have no character from this house. We cannot recommend a woman of such… forward habits.”

Forward habits! The injustice of the phrase was a physical thing, a blow that made me gasp. I had spent two years teaching his younger sisters, stitching their dresses, reading them scripture, and bending my own spirit to the narrow confines of this house. For this, I was to be cast out, my name blackened, my livelihood destroyed.

Without another word, I turned and left the room. I did not look back. I walked up the narrow stairs to my small, cold room at the top of the house. My trunk was already packed, a sign of how swiftly and completely I was to be erased. I took my bonnet and cloak from the peg, my hands moving with a strange, mechanical calm. In my purse were a few shillings and sixpence—my entire fortune. Pride was the only other thing I possessed, and I resolved it should be my staff and my shield.

As I passed through the hall for the last time, the great door stood open. And there, standing by the library door, was Edmund. He was pale, his eyes downcast, unable to meet my gaze. He was a coward to the last.

“Miss Wray,” he whispered, his voice cracking. “I am sorry. I could not… she would have…”

I stopped and faced him. All the passion, the rage, the burning sense of wrong that had been held in check by necessity now surged forth. “You could not what, sir?” I demanded, my voice ringing in the cavernous hall. “You could not speak the truth? You could not defend a woman whose only fault was to believe you were a man of honour? You have traded my good name for your own peace. I hope it is a comfort to you. For it is no comfort to me.”

He flinched as if I had struck him. He had no answer. His silence was his confession. I turned my back on him, on Garth Hall, on the life I had known, and walked out into the wind.

The storm broke as I reached the main road. It was not a gentle rain, but a furious, driving deluge that soaked through my cloak in minutes. The wind tore at my bonnet, and the road beneath my feet turned to a sucking morass of mud. Garth Hall dwindled behind me, a dark shape against a darker sky, until it was lost to sight. I was alone. The word echoed in my mind with each step: *Alone. Alone. Alone.* But as I walked, a strange and fierce energy began to fill me. The cold and the wet were real, but they were merely physical discomforts. The pain in my heart was deeper, yet the act of walking, of putting one foot before the other, of moving forward into the storm, was an act of defiance. They had cast me out, but they had not broken me.

After what felt like an age of walking, with the rain beginning to ease and the wind to sigh its way to rest, I saw the lights of the town of Alford twinkling in the dusk. My shoes were ruined, my skirts heavy with mud, and I was chilled to the marrow. I had but a few shillings to my name, and no prospect of a home or a friend. Yet, as I entered the town, a strange sense of hope stirred within me. The world was vast and hard, and I was but a small, solitary creature within it. But I was free. I was my own, to sink or swim, to starve or to strive.
