The burning suns of an Italian August had yielded at length to the melancholy autumnal rains, which fell upon the roofs of San Filippo with a mournful and unceasing cadence, well suited to the gloomy tenor of my thoughts. We had resided in that ancient city nearly six months, drawn thither by my father’s hope that the masterpieces of its galleries and the sublime grandeur of its surrounding mountains would alleviate the decline in my mother's health; yet, for me, the loveliness of the region served only to deepen the shadows of my own impending despair. It was amidst these scenes of imposing, yet to me sorrowful, beauty, that I first encountered Lorenzo Vivaldi. He was possessed of a dark and striking countenance, and a mind fervent with the impassioned love of his country, which groaned beneath the iron sceptre of its foreign rulers. Our acquaintance, begun by chance in the shadowed aisles of a ruined chapel, had quickly ripened into an intimacy of souls, for he saw in me a kindred spirit, capable of sympathizing with his lofty aspirations and his profound grief for the fettered liberties of his native land. Alas! How bitterly do I now lament that my heart, starved of noble pursuit, should have yielded so readily to the dangerous intoxication of his eloquence!

My father, observing our growing attachment, interposed with a severity which, while it commanded my obedience, lacerated my spirit with unutterable anguish. "Clara," he said, his voice trembling with a mixture of paternal anxiety and inflexible resolve, "I have watched the progress of this intimacy, and I forbid it utterly. This youth is surrounded by the deadly atmosphere of conspiracy; his footsteps are dogged by the spies of a vigilant and merciless government. To ally yourself with him is to draw down upon our heads the vengeance of that power which holds this land in subjection, and to plunge the dagger of remorse into the bosom of your dying mother." I dared not reply; I only wept, and, retiring to my solitary chamber, gave loose to the agony that convulsed my frame. I saw, with the vividness of a waking vision, the frightful gulf that yawned between us. Yet, despite my father’s interdictions, the image of Lorenzo haunted me, and when I looked upon the majestic palaces of San Filippo, they seemed but as the granite monuments of a vast and cruel prison, within whose walls the noblest heart I had ever known was pining in captivity.

The tranquillity which my father’s mandate had partially restored was destined to be of short duration. The tempest, which had long been gathering in the political horizon, burst forth with appalling fury. It was on a night of uncommon wildness, when the storm raged through the streets of San Filippo with a violence that seemed to shake the very foundations of the earth, that a heavy and ominous knocking echoed through our dwelling. The tidings it brought were of a nature to freeze the current of my blood. Lorenzo Vivaldi, betrayed by a traitor within the innermost circle of his associates, had been arrested by the Austrian police, and cast into the dismal dungeons of the fortress of San Salvatore. The intelligence was as a thunderbolt to my brain. I sat motionless, bewildered by the multitude of miseries that rushed upon my view; the agonies of remorse, the dread of his fate, and the terrible consciousness that, by my obedience to my father, I had, perhaps, contributed to his ruin, contending within my breast. The imagination tormented me with a thousand hideous spectres; I beheld him, in fancy, loaded with ignominious fetters, torn from the light of day, and consigned to the slow but certain torture of a lingering imprisonment.

When reason returned, I found myself alone in my chamber, the tempest without having subsided into a sullen and fitful moan. The question now arose, with overwhelming force, whether I should venture to plead for him. I knew that my father’s prejudices were invincible; that he regarded the Italian patriots as misguided enthusiasts, whose destruction was inevitable. To appeal to him would be to encounter a denial, clothed perhaps in language of severity. Yet, to remain inactive, to fold my hands in supine despair, while the being I loved was suffering the extremity of misery, was a thought I could not endure. I resolved, therefore, to seek an interview with the Governor of San Salvatore, and, casting myself at his feet, to implore his mercy. I knew the danger to which I exposed myself; I knew that the Austrian authorities were stern, and that to intercede for a state prisoner was to incur the suspicion of being an accomplice in his crimes. But what was danger, what was even death, compared to the hell of remorse that would haunt me through life, if I neglected a single effort to save him? I will go, thought I, and though I should be spurned from his presence as a suppliant for a traitor, the attempt shall at least prove that my affection is as enduring as his misfortunes.

The morning dawned, gloomy and tempestuous, as if in unison with my feelings. I armed myself with all the resolution I could summon, and, eluding the vigilance of my family, pursued my way through the crowded and narrow streets of San Filippo to the fortress of San Salvatore. The massive gateway, frowning with battlements, seemed to forbid approach; but, nerved by despair, I passed the barrier, and, traversing a gloomy courtyard, demanded, with a trembling voice, an audience with the Governor. He was a man of stern and martial aspect, whose eye seemed to read the inmost recesses of the soul; and when I had communicated the object of my visit, a smile of bitter irony crossed his features. "Young maiden," he replied, "you know not what you ask. The prisoner is a traitor to his Imperial Majesty, and his fate is already sealed by the laws which he has violated. Your tears and intercessions are alike unavailing; the sentence of death has been passed upon him, and nothing can avert its execution. Retire, therefore, and weep over the misguided enthusiasm which has brought him to this untimely end."
