They carried her through the village of Pellingshurst on a Tuesday, the rain falling with the particular gentleness reserved for the passing of the old. Eleanor Vane had drawn her first breath in a damp cottage at the far edge of the parish and her last in a room three stories above the gravel drive of Blackmore Hall, and the distance between those two points—scarcely two miles—measured the whole of her sixty-eight years. The vicar spoke of service and devotion. The mourners, huddled beneath black umbrellas, heard what they expected to hear. But each among them carried a different woman to the grave.

To Sir Roland Blackmore, standing at the family plot with his hands clasped behind his back, she was the last unbroken thread. He remembered her as a woman of forty, already formidable, standing in the nursery in 1901 while his mother lay dying downstairs. He had been seven years old, and the house had seemed to him a great ship going down by the bow, the servants sliding across the tilted decks, his father locked in the library with the brandy. Eleanor had come in without knocking, drawn the curtains against the November dark, and set a cup of cocoa on the bedside table with a click that sounded, in his memory, like the turning of a lock. She had not knelt or stroked his hair. She had said, "Drink that, Master Roland, and get into bed. There's nothing to be gained by sitting up." He had obeyed her, then and for decades after, with a completeness he never offered his father. When he returned from the war in 1919, thin and strange to himself, it was Eleanor who met him at the kitchen entrance rather than the front, who fed him broth without asking after the trenches, who said only, "Your room has been aired," as though he had been away at school. She was the one fixed point in a house that had lost its heir in Gallipoli, its younger son to drink, and its mistress to a quiet decline no one could date precisely. Sir Roland stood at her grave and understood that with her death something architectural had failed in the house, some load-bearing wall removed, and that the structure would now begin, slowly, to settle.

The servants remembered a different woman entirely. To them she had been a sovereign, and her kingdom had run on rules as intricate and unyielding as any court protocol. Mrs. Ada Bream, who had come to Blackmore as a scullery maid in 1912 and risen to cook before her own knees gave out, could recite the housekeeper's edicts decades after they had ceased to apply. Silver was polished on Wednesdays. Linen was counted on the first Monday of each month. No servant below the rank of first footman was to enter the library before noon. Eleanor had kept a leather-bound ledger in which she recorded every spoon, every napkin, every cake of soap, and she could detect a discrepancy of a single egg from a delivery of six dozen. She had dismissed a laundry maid in 1908 for using the family's starch on her own petticoats, and the story had passed into the folklore of the servants' hall, told and retold until it acquired the gravity of legend. Yet Mrs. Bream, in the privacy of her own kitchen years later, would confess to a confusion that bordered on tenderness. During the influenza winter of 1919, when three of the staff had fallen ill and the house had taken on the hush of a hospital, Eleanor had appeared in the servants' corridor at midnight carrying a tray of barley water and beef tea, had sat on the edge of a kitchen maid's cot, and had held the girl's hand against her own dry palm while the coughing shook them both. In the morning she had been as exacting as ever, had found a water spot on the breakfast decanter and sent it back twice, but Mrs. Bream had seen what she had seen, and it had unsettled her more than any cruelty would have done, because it suggested that the woman contained contradictions that her system could not account for.

In Pellingshurst itself, among the cottages and the smithy and the church with its lopsided yew, they remembered a girl who had never belonged to them. Eleanor Fossett, she had been then, the daughter of a hedger who drank and a mother who took in washing until her hands broke out in sores that would not heal. She had gone into service at twelve, walking the two miles to Blackmore Hall with a single change of clothes tied in a pillowcase, and she had not come back. Not to live. She attended church each Sunday with the household, sitting in the family pew's shadow, her hymnal held at the exact angle prescribed, and she gave to the harvest collection and the widows' fund with a generosity the village noted and resented, because generosity from a woman who had risen above her birth carried the faint insult of a lesson. Her brother Tom still lived in the cottage, though he had let the roof rot and the garden go to dock and nettle. He stood at the funeral in a coat too large for him, borrowed from the vicar's own wardrobe, and he remembered a sister who had learned to read from the Bible and had taught him his letters by the fire, tracing them on the hearthstone with a stick, and who had once, just once, when they were children, let him hold a bird's nest she had found in the hedge, the eggs still warm, blue as a bruise. She had made him put it back. She had said the mother would grieve. He had not understood then what she meant, and standing at her grave at fifty years old, reeking of the vicar's borrowed dignity, he was not certain he understood now.
