It was a truth universally acknowledged in the more discerning circles of London society that Mr. Cecil Ponsoby had failed at everything. He had failed at the Bar, where his eloquence was considered too ornamental for the grim business of the law; he had failed in the Church, where his sermons on the Beatitudes were thought to be a trifle too witty for the salvation of souls; and he had failed, most spectacularly, in the Arts, for he could neither paint a convincing pear nor write a line of verse that did not dissolve into a perfumed sigh of self-pity. He possessed, in short, no talent whatsoever, a deficiency which he had hitherto regarded as a personal tragedy but which he now saw, in a sudden and brilliant flash of insight, to be his greatest qualification.

“Talent,” he announced to his reflection in the mirror one morning, as he adjusted a cravat of a particularly aggressive shade of heliotrope, “is a liability. It shackles one to the object of one’s creation. The artist is a slave to his own vision, but the critic, ah, the critic is a god who has wisely declined to create a world of his own, that he might more freely and elegantly dismantle the worlds of others. Criticism is the only art that requires no talent, only temperament.”

And what a temperament Mr. Ponsoby possessed. It was a temperament of polished jade, cool to the touch and utterly impenetrable. Armed with this new and liberating philosophy, he secured a commission from *The Palladium of Taste*, a journal so exclusive that its readership consisted chiefly of those who wished to be seen reading it. His first subject was a new play by the celebrated Mr. Alistair Finch, a dramatist whose work was universally adored for its heartwarming sentimentality and its unshakeable belief in the inherent goodness of man.

Mr. Ponsoby attended the premiere, seated in a private box from which he observed the proceedings with the detached air of an entomologist watching a particularly common beetle. The play, *The Lamplighter’s Legacy*, was a four-act ode to virtue, filled with noble paupers, forgiving aristocrats, and dialogue so sweet it threatened to bring on a collective sugar coma in the stalls. The audience wept. Mr. Ponsoby merely raised a languid eyebrow.

His review, published the following Saturday, did not criticise the play; it vivisected it. With the surgical precision of a master jeweller cleaving a flawed diamond, he laid the drama bare. He wrote that Mr. Finch’s prose “possessed the cloying sweetness of an over-ripe peach, a sweetness that soon curdles into a kind of moral indigestion.” He observed that the play’s heroine, a saintly creature of impossible virtue, “was not so much a character as a theological proposition in a crinoline, a walking argument for a benevolent universe that, alas, does not exist outside of Mr. Finch’s rather sentimental imagination.” The final sentence of the review became an instant legend: “To witness *The Lamplighter’s Legacy* is to understand that while hope may spring eternal in the human breast, it is an emotion best cultivated in private, and certainly not in a theatre where one has paid three and six for the privilege of being condescended to by an author’s better nature.”

The effect was instantaneous and seismic. The article was quoted in every drawing-room and club in London. It was read aloud at dinner parties with the same hushed reverence usually reserved for scripture or a particularly scandalous piece of gossip. Mr. Ponsoby, who had hitherto been a familiar but unremarkable figure in the social firmament, a kind of decorative satellite, was suddenly its blazing, controversial sun. He was famous overnight.

And then, the strangest thing happened. The artists, poets, and playwrights of London, far from being outraged, began to court him. To be praised by Mr. Ponsoby was a distinction; to be ignored was a slight; but to be insulted by him was the ultimate mark of cultural significance. His cruelty became a coveted badge of honour, his scorn a coveted prize.

“You simply must be beastly to my new collection of sonnets,” begged the languid poet Lord Carstairs, cornering Ponsoby at a reception. “They are all about autumnal melancholy and dying swans. I feel they are dreadfully important, but no one will take them seriously until you have declared them to be utterly worthless.”

“Being insulted by Ponsoby is the only thing that separates a serious artist from a mere tradesman,” declared the painter Madame Valois, whose canvases were vast, violent splashes of colour that looked, to the untutored eye, like the aftermath of a domestic accident. She had sent Ponsoby a gilt-edged invitation to the opening of her new show, with a personal note: *“Do your worst, you magnificent monster. The blacker the bile, the brighter the fame.”*

The most fervent of them all was Mr. Julian Thorne, a young sculptor whose work consisted of enormous, jagged lumps of granite which he claimed represented “the primal scream of matter against the tyranny of form.” Thorne was a creature of wild hair and wilder opinions, and he pursued Ponsoby with the devotion of a pilgrim seeking a particularly wrathful deity.

“I have laboured for a year on my new piece, *Cosmic Agony*,” Thorne confided to him one evening, his eyes burning with a feverish light. “It is a single block of unpolished basalt, three tons of it. It represents the utter futility of human endeavour. I want you to tell the world that it is a pretentious and meaningless boulder. Please, Mr. Ponsoby. Destroy me.”

One evening, Ponsoby found himself at a soiree given by the Duchess of Abernathy, a woman whose taste was as formidable as her lineage. The room was a galaxy of jewels and wit. Thorne cornered him again, his hands smudged with clay, his tie askew.

“Thorne,” Ponsoby said, with the weary sigh of a man burdened by his own genius, “your desire for my disapproval is becoming tiresomely flattering. You are mistaking notoriety for immortality. They are not the same thing.”
