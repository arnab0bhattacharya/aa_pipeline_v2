When Miss Clementina Van Alstyne of New York first saw London, she declared it looked like a city that had been kept too long. ‘Everything is faded,’ she said to her maid, ‘except the prices.’ It was the Season of 1893, and Miss Van Alstyne had crossed the Atlantic with the calm determination of a general planning a winter campaign. Her objective was singular, her strategy simple, and her ammunition, consisting of several million dollars in Chicago stockyards, was of a kind that made most fortifications in Mayfair tremble.

Her aunt, Mrs. Fish, who had married into English obscurity and found it wonderfully comfortable, did her best to be a good guide. ‘The trouble with dukes, Clementina,’ she said over their morning coffee at the Langham, ‘is that there are so few of them, and they know it. It makes them very difficult. They are like rare postage stamps, and one is always afraid of tearing them.’

‘I do not intend to tear him, Aunt,’ said Miss Van Alstyne, examining a list of titled gentlemen provided by a helpful agent. ‘I intend to mount him. But the list seems to be full of dukes who are already married to women who look like the back of a carriage. Are there no dukes in England who are free to make their own mistakes?’

Mrs. Fish sighed. ‘There is only one unmarried duke in the whole of the peerage, my dear. The Duke of Basingstoke.’

Miss Van Alstyne’s eyes, which were the colour of a winter sky and just as coldly perceptive, brightened. ‘And is he presentable?’

‘He is ninety,’ said Mrs. Fish, ‘and he has not heard a word since the Crimean War. He lives in a sort of magnificent twilight at Basingstoke House, attended by a physician who is nearly as old as he is, and a collection of clockwork birds that sing to him because he cannot hear the real ones. His heir is his nephew, Lord Eustace, who has everything one could possibly want except money.’

Miss Van Alstyne tapped her finger on the table. ‘Deaf, is he? That is not a disadvantage, Aunt. In my experience, the best husbands are the ones who cannot hear what you are saying about them. I shall marry the Duke of Basingstoke.’

It was at the Duchess of Paisley’s ball that the campaign began. The ballroom was a shimmering sea of jewels and silk, and the air was thick with the scent of gardenias and the low hum of gossip. It was here that Miss Van Alstyne first laid eyes on her quarry. The Duke of Basingstoke was a magnificent ruin. He sat in a gilded chair, a monument of black broadcloth and white hair, his face a finely wrinkled parchment, his eyes fixed on a point somewhere in the middle distance of the last century. He looked less like a man than like a family portrait that had been brought down from the wall and given a glass of champagne.

‘He is perfect,’ she whispered to Mrs. Fish. ‘He has the look of permanence. One could put him in a museum.’

Her approach was direct. She requested an introduction and, upon being presented, spoke to the Duke in a clear, deliberate voice, as if addressing a foreign dignitary. ‘Your Grace,’ she said, ‘I understand you have a wonderful collection of automata.’

The Duke did not turn. He continued to stare at the potted palms.

‘He cannot hear you, my dear,’ said the Duchess of Paisley, with a little laugh. ‘You must speak into this.’ She produced a small, silver ear trumpet and held it to the Duke’s ear.

Miss Van Alstyne leaned forward. ‘I SAID, YOUR GRACE, I ADORE AUTOMATA.’

The Duke turned his head slowly, a faint light of comprehension dawning in his milky eyes. He gave a slow, regal nod. It was, she felt, a sign of approval. He was captivated.

It was at this moment that Lord Eustace Beauclerk made his entrance into the affair. He was a young man with the weary elegance of a minor poet and the charmingly hopeless finances of a younger son. He had been watching Miss Van Alstyne’s assault on his great-uncle with the horrified fascination of a man watching a beautiful woman attempt to stuff a peacock into a hatbox. He knew the family history: the mortgages, the leaky roofs, the ancestral silver that had been discreetly sold to a dealer in the Strand. He also knew that if this American dynamo married his uncle, he, Lord Eustace, would remain penniless forever.

He bowed before her. ‘Miss Van Alstyne? Lord Eustace Beauclerk. I believe we are to be, if not related, at least acquainted by proximity.’

‘Lord Eustace,’ she said, her eyes sweeping over his impeccable but slightly worn evening clothes. ‘You are the heir, I presume?’

‘I am the poor relation,’ he said with a disarming smile. ‘A much more interesting social category. We are never expected to pay for anything, but we are expected to be witty. It is a heavy burden.’

‘I find wit a very poor substitute for an income,’ she replied.

‘Ah,’ he said, ‘but it is a wonderful consolation for the lack of one. I see you have been admiring my great-uncle. He is a magnificent specimen, is he not? Like a fossil of a bygone era. One almost expects to find ammonites in his waistcoat pockets.’

Miss Van Alstyne was not accustomed to being amused. She found it irritating. ‘I find him distinguished.’

‘Distinguished is the word we use for old things we are afraid to throw out,’ Lord Eustace murmured. ‘But tell me, Miss Van Alstyne, is it the man you admire, or the title? For the title is a great old coat, but the man inside is a trifle threadbare.’

‘A duke is a duke,’ she said coolly. ‘It is a fact of nature, like a mountain or a river. One does not question its existence, one merely appreciates its grandeur.’
