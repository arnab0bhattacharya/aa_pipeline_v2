My life, since the death of Arthur, had been a thing of small rooms and smaller means. The world had shrunk to the dimensions of our two rooms in a lodging-house off Deansgate, where the gas-lamp from the street threw a sickly, jaundiced light upon the walls, and the air was thick with the smell of soot and boiled potatoes. Outside, Manchester roared and smoked, a beast of iron and steam that had swallowed my husband whole in its mills and now seemed to pant at my very window, waiting to consume the little that was left. My world was the clatter of my sewing machine, the scent of camphor in the winter air, and the weight of my son, Leo, asleep in my lap. He was three years old, with his father’s serious grey eyes and a shock of dark hair that would not lie flat. He was all my wealth, my justification, my reason for breathing the foul air and fighting the battle of each new day.

The battle grew fiercer when the letters began. They came from Halliwell House, that great stone edifice of judgment and tradition which the Barrow family called a home. They were written by Mr. Thorne, their man of law, on paper so thick and cream-coloured it seemed an affront to my thin, ink-stained fingers. The first was a masterpiece of condescending sympathy, lamenting my “genteel poverty” and suggesting, in the most delicate terms, that my maternal feelings, however laudable, were no substitute for the advantages a boy of good blood required. Advantages, I knew, that came at the price of his mother. They wished to take my son, to raise him as a Barrow, to erase the stain of my existence and the memory of his father’s folly in marrying me. To them, I was not a widow; I was a mistake to be tidied away.

I wrote back, my hand shaking not with fear but with a cold, hard rage that settled in my stomach like a stone. I thanked Mr. Thorne for his concern but declined his clients’ proposal. Leo would remain with me. He was my son, not their chattel. The reply was swift and less veiled. It spoke of my duty to the boy’s future, of the selfishness of a mother who would cling to a child at the expense of his inheritance. It was a sermon on the text of worldly prudence, and I read it with the scripture of a mother’s love held fast in my heart. My duty was to the child God had given me, not to the pride of a family that had disowned his father.

Then Mr. Thorne himself began to call.

He was a man made of grey cloth and grey principles. His face was long and narrow, his eyes the colour of a winter sky, and he carried an umbrella and a leather portfolio as if they were the twin pillars of his authority. He would stand in my doorway, a shadow against the grimy hall, and his presence seemed to suck the very warmth from the small room. His voice was smooth and low, a silken thread meant to bind and persuade.

“Mrs. Vance,” he began one Tuesday, his gaze sweeping over the worn rug, the single lamp, the pile of linen on the table. “I trust I find you well.”

“You find me as I am,” I replied, not rising from my chair. “Is there something more to say, Mr. Thorne? I have made my position plain.”

He stepped inside, uninvited, and closed the door softly behind him. The click of the latch was unnervingly final. “Plainness is a virtue, but it is not always wisdom. Have you considered the matter from the boy’s perspective? From the perspective of the boy he will become?”

“I consider him every hour of every day,” I said, my voice harder than I intended. “He is fed, he is warm, he is loved. What more does a child require?”

“He requires a name that is not a whisper, a future that is not a struggle!” The smoothness in his voice cracked, revealing the flint beneath. “He is a Barrow. He has a right to the estate, to the education, to the society that is his birthright. You are robbing him of it.”

“I am giving him the one thing your clients can never offer,” I retorted, rising now, my hands clenched at my sides. “I am giving him his mother. I am giving him a home where he is the light and not an object to be managed. You speak of rights. What of my right to the child I bore in pain and love? You would have me sign a paper and deliver him like a parcel. You speak of justice, but you advocate a theft.”

“A theft?” He gave a short, dry laugh. “Madam, we seek to rescue him. We offer him a life of privilege.”

“And I offer him a life of truth,” I shot back. “A life where he knows who he is, and who he comes from. He will know his father was a good man, a kind man, who worked with his hands and was not ashamed. He will not be taught to despise the world from which he sprang. That is a poison I will not let you administer.”

For a moment, he was silent. His eyes, those cold, grey instruments of calculation, fixed on my face. He was not accustomed to being challenged by a woman in a threadbare gown. He saw only the poverty, the defiance, the irrational obstacle to his clients’ neat arrangement. He did not see the fire that Arthur had lit in me, a fire that no amount of hardship could extinguish.

“You are making a grave error,” he said at last, his voice low and menacing. “You are letting sentiment cloud your judgment. The Barrows are powerful people. They will not be thwarted by a… by a sewing-woman.”
