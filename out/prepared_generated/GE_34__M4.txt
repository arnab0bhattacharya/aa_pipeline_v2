The peace of eighteen hundred and sixteen had scattered the armies of Europe like chaff before the wind, and among the chaff that drifted back to the sober fields of England was one Tobias Renshaw, a younger son of Hollowmeadow Farm, who had gone for a soldier in the heat of a boy’s quarrel with his brother and had come home again with the heat quite gone out of him, and a good deal of the boy too. He had marched through Spain and had seen the Pyrenees in their snows, and had learned to think of a furrowed field and a quiet hearth as the soldier thinks of them who has lain many nights on the bare earth and heard the guns at daybreak. But the home he came to was not the home he had left. His brother Abel, the elder by three years, a man of slow speech and cautious eye, had in the interval taken to himself a wife; and the wife was Ruth Marston, who had been Tobias’s sweetheart since the days when they two had driven the cows together along the lane that skirts the brook, and had promised him, with a girl’s easy faith, to wait for his return. She had waited two years, and the letters had grown rarer, and the war had seemed as if it would never end; and Abel, who was always at hand and always steady, had waited too, in his own fashion, which was that of a man who does not put his fortune to the hazard of a battle, but lets it ripen like a field of corn that he has sown and fenced. So it came about that when Tobias, with his knapsack on his shoulder and his heart beating high with the thought of Ruth, came down the lane on a September evening, the door of Hollowmeadow was opened to him by his brother’s wife, who turned very pale and said, “Tobias!” and then said no more; and Abel came out from the kitchen with his pipe in his hand, and put out his hand, and said, “Thou’rt welcome home, lad,” in a voice that was not quite steady.

There is no need to dwell upon the first days of that return. The reader who has known the world—and who has not, if he has read so far?—will easily conceive how the three persons of that household stood towards each other: the husband, conscious of no wrong, yet uneasily sensible of a certain shadow on his happiness; the wife, divided between shame and a lingering tenderness which she had believed dead and found to be only buried; and the returned soldier, whose first wild anger had spent itself in a night of walking the fields, and who had come back to the house at dawn with a set face and a resolution that cost him more than any battle. For Tobias was not a man to make a noise and a scandal in the parish. He had seen enough of the world’s misery to know that a man may be ruined by his own tongue as surely as by his neighbour’s; and he had a pride in him that would not suffer him to be pitied. So he said to Abel, in the cold grey of the morning, standing under the great pear-tree by the wall: “Brother, what’s done is done. I’ll not be a curse to thy house. I’ll take the labourer’s wage and the labourer’s place, and I’ll ask no more than that; and if I cannot bear it, I’ll go.” Abel would have spoken of money and of shares, for he was a just man in his way; but Tobias would have none of it. “I’ll eat no bread I do not earn,” said he; and so it was settled.

It is one thing to resolve and another to live. The farmhouse at Hollowmeadow was not large; the two brothers sat at the same board, and Ruth, who had once been the lightest-hearted girl in the valley, went about her work with a quietness that was worse than tears. She would have spoken to Tobias alone, had he given her the chance; but he never gave it. He was courteous to her, and cold; he thanked her for his meals, and asked after her health, and never once looked at her as he had used to look when they walked together in the orchard. And Ruth, who had a woman’s quick sense of these things, felt the coldness more than she would have felt a blow. She began to think herself the most miserable of women, and to wonder whether Abel guessed; and the wonder grew into a fear, and the fear into a silence that sat at the table like a third guest. Abel, for his part, said nothing, but he smoked his pipe of an evening with a longer face than common, and once, when Tobias was late from the field, he went out to the gate and looked down the lane with an anxiety that he would not have confessed.

Now it happened that in the October of that year there was a sheep fair at the town of Ashby, and Abel went thither to sell, and was to stay the night, for the roads were bad and the business might keep him late. The house was quiet that evening, and Ruth sat by the fire with her sewing, and Tobias came in from the yard and stood by the door with his hat in his hand, as if he would have gone out again. But the night was cold and the fire was bright, and he sat down at last on the settle on the other side of the hearth, and there was a silence between them that neither knew how to break.
