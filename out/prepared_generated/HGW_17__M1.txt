The apparatus occupied the better part of Arthur Pemberton’s laboratory in Chelsea, a converted stable-yard that smelled perpetually of ozone, hot brass, and the damp Thames air. It was not a beautiful machine; it possessed the raw, industrial aesthetic of a boiler house, all copper piping and heavy glass valves, but it was, Pemberton believed with the fervour of the true inventor, the most significant instrument since the telescope. He called it the Distant Optical Transmitter. Its principle was sound, or at least he argued so to his few visitors: it did not transmit light, but rather the potential for light, the very wave-fronts of existence, focused by his peculiar alloy of selenium and cadmium upon a ground-glass screen.

He was demonstrating it to a sceptical colleague, Dr. Marcus Thorne, a man of rigid opinions and a face that seemed perpetually braced against the possibility of being impressed. They stood before the screen, which currently showed a grey, shimmering haze.

“The trouble with the wireless telegraph,” Pemberton said, pacing before his creation, “is that it is crude. It transmits dots and dashes. A single thread of information. I am transmitting *sight*. Not the mere representation of it, but the event itself, captured in a parallax of time.”

“Parallax of time,” Thorne repeated, his voice dry as old paper. “You are inventing new physics as you go along, Pemberton. I thought you were merely trying to see the French coast without crossing the Channel.”

“That was the first intention,” Pemberton admitted, adjusting a vernier dial. “To catch a passing ship, perhaps. But the instrument has... qualities. It seems to prefer certain distances. Certain epochs.”

He flipped the main switch. The hum of the dynamo in the yard outside deepened, and the glass valves glowed a soft, bruised purple. On the screen, the grey haze swirled and coalesced. Thorne leaned forward, his scepticism momentarily suspended by the sheer novelty of the image.

It was London. But not their London. The skyline was wrong. The familiar jagged silhouette of the City was punctuated by towers of a glassy, impossible height. The sky was not the familiar pearl-grey, but a hazy, sulphurous yellow.

“An artist’s impression?” Thorne asked. “Some sort of projected panorama?”

“Look closer,” Pemberton said, his voice tight. “Look at the river. The bridges.”

The Thames was there, but the bridges were different—sweeping, slender arches of white stone. And then the detail resolved: a great airship, shaped like a teardrop and bearing the Royal Standard, drifted silently over the Houses of Parliament. But the Parliament building was a ruin. The clock tower of Big Ben was shattered, a jagged stump of masonry.

“My God,” Thorne whispered. “Is this... is this some sort of trick?”

“It is the future, Marcus,” Pemberton said. “I have tuned the apparatus to a frequency that vibrates not with the present, but with the coming state of things. Look.”

On the screen, the scene shifted. It was a view from a high window—perhaps the very window they were standing by—looking down into the street. The street was empty of traffic. Instead, it was filled with people. Not a crowd in the usual sense, but a silent, shuffling multitude, moving with a terrible, unified purpose. They wore ragged clothes, and their faces, when the camera—or whatever it was—caught them, were masks of exhaustion and despair. And then the sound came through. Not through the air, but through the machine itself, a low, crackling roar that spoke of distant artillery and the screams of a city under siege.

Pemberton switched it off. The silence in the laboratory was absolute, broken only by the ticking of a cooling valve.

“It’s a war,” Pemberton said, turning to his friend. “A war that will destroy this country. Not a skirmish, not a colonial dust-up. The end of the British Empire. I have seen the smoke rising from Whitehall. I have seen the German airships over the Home Counties.”

Thorne straightened his waistcoat, a gesture of reclaiming his composure. “This is madness, Arthur. A hallucination produced by fatigue and an overactive imagination. The German airships? We have the finest navy in the world. We have treaties, alliances...”

“We have complacency!” Pemberton cried. “We have a government that thinks war is a matter of cricket matches and naval reviews. I have seen the result! I have seen the starvation, the plague, the collapse of the social order. And it is coming, Thorne. Within a generation. Perhaps within a decade.”

He grabbed Thorne by the arm. “You must help me. We must go to the War Office. We must show them what I have seen. We can prevent it. We can change the course of things.”

Thorne pulled his arm away. His face was pale, but his eyes were hard. “Change the course of things? Based on a light-show in a stable? Arthur, you are a brilliant engineer, but you are a terrible politician. This... this *vision* of yours... it is impossible. It violates every known law of causality. If it were true, it would mean we have no free will. It would mean the future is fixed.”

“Perhaps it is!” Pemberton shouted. “Perhaps we are just actors in a play we have already written. But we can change the script! That is the point of seeing!”

“The point of seeing,” Thorne said coldly, “is to confirm what we already know. The future is a continuation of the present. Britain is strong. The Empire is secure. This machine of yours is a toy, or a fraud. I will not be a party to spreading such panic.”

He turned and walked toward the door. “Get some sleep, Arthur. And for God’s sake, turn that thing off. It’s giving me a headache.”

The door closed with a decisive click. Pemberton stood alone in the humming darkness of his laboratory. He looked at the machine, at the dark, silent screen. He had held the future in his hands, the ruin of his world, and the one man he thought might understand had called it a headache.
