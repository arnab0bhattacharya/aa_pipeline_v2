The carriage from London had been announced at least a quarter of an hour before it actually appeared, and in that quarter of an hour the household of Ashby Compton had arranged itself with a precision that spoke of long practice. Mrs. Ellerby, having settled her lace cap to her satisfaction, occupied the great chair by the fire with an air of one who had been sitting there all the morning; her daughters, Harriet and Lucy, were discovered at the window with their work, as though the sight of a carriage were the last thing they expected; and Mr. Ellerby, with his son’s letter in his hand, stood upon the hearthrug in the attitude of a man who had been interrupted in his reading by nothing more extraordinary than the arrival of dinner.

The carriage came, and with it Mrs. Reginald Ellerby, who descended upon Ashby Compton with the air of a woman descending upon a colony. She was handsome in the London fashion, which is to say that she looked expensive and slightly unwell; her pelisse was of a cut that had not yet been seen in Northamptonshire, and her manner of receiving Mrs. Ellerby’s embrace suggested a person submitting to an inspection rather than returning an affection. Reginald, hovering at her elbow, wore the expression of a man who has brought home a prize and is not entirely certain it will be allowed to remain in the house.

“My dear mother,” said he, “I have brought you your new daughter. Augusta, you have heard me speak of Ashby Compton a thousand times.”

“I have indeed,” said Mrs. Reginald, with a smile that was intended to be gracious and succeeded in being instructive. “Reginald has told me so much of the place that I feel I know it already. And this is the famous hall! How delightfully old-fashioned. One feels one has stepped into a novel of Mrs. Radcliffe’s.”

“We are very fond of it,” said Mrs. Ellerby, with a calmness that her daughters recognized as the most dangerous of her moods. “It has been in the family these four hundred years, and has had the goodness to accommodate itself to each generation. I daresay it will accommodate itself to another.”

“Oh, I am sure it will,” cried Mrs. Reginald. “Indeed, I have already a hundred schemes for it. Only a few touches, you know—the hall is so very dark, and those chairs! I declare I could not sit down in one of them without expecting to be lectured on my duty. In London, you know, we have quite given up the Gothic. It is all lightness now, and comfort.”

“We are great believers in comfort here,” said Harriet, without raising her eyes from her work. “We find it in the simplest things. A good fire, a book, a walk to the village—these serve us very well.”

“A walk to the village!” repeated Mrs. Reginald, as though this were a pleasantry. “You are very good. I am sure I should be quite lost without my phaeton. Reginald, you must speak to your father about the road. I was shaken to pieces at the last mile.”

“The road is a very good road,” said Mr. Ellerby, “when it is not wet, and a very bad one when it is. I have never known it otherwise, and I have known it sixty years.”

Mrs. Reginald smiled again. “Ah, that is just the thing, sir. You have known it sixty years. I hope to know it sixty more, but I hope to know it improved. There is nothing so charming as improvement, I think. One must move with the times.”

“I have always found the times move quite fast enough without my assistance,” said Mr. Ellerby. “I am content to let them go on, and to follow at my leisure.”

The dinner was announced, and Mrs. Reginald was conducted to the dining-room, where she found occasion for further reform. The table was long and the dishes plain, and the servants, who had been at Ashby Compton since before Reginald was born, moved with a deliberation that her ladyship’s footmen would have considered a positive vice. She seated herself with a little sigh, and applied herself to the soup as though it were a penance.

“I hope you will not think me impertinent, ma’am,” said she to Mrs. Ellerby, “but I cannot help observing that your cook has a very—how shall I say it?—a very honest way with a joint. In London, we have learned to disguise our meats a little more. There is a sauce, you know, which my Frenchman makes—it is nothing, only a little wine and herbs—but it quite transforms a leg of mutton.”

“I am afraid our cook would consider it a reflection upon the mutton,” said Mrs. Ellerby. “She has been with us thirty years, and is very proud of her roasts. We are simple people, my dear Augusta, and we like to know what we are eating.”

“Simplicity is very charming,” said Mrs. Reginald, “in its place. But one must not carry it too far. There is a point at which simplicity becomes—I hope you will forgive me—a kind of indolence. Reginald, do you not agree?”

Reginald, who had been eating his dinner with the desperate concentration of a man who hopes that if he chews vigorously enough he will not be required to speak, looked up with a start. “I—yes, indeed—that is, I am sure my mother’s cook is an excellent cook, and I have never dined better than at Ashby Compton.”

“You see,” said Mrs. Ellerby, “he is still a Northamptonshire man at heart. We shall make a convert of you yet, Augusta. You will learn to like our plain ways.”

“I am sure I shall learn to like everything that is good,” said Mrs. Reginald, with an emphasis that left her hearers in doubt as to what she considered good. “But you must allow me to try my hand at a little improvement. I have brought down some prints for the drawing-room—quite the latest thing, I assure you—and a new arrangement for the furniture. You will hardly know the room again.”
