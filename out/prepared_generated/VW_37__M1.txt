Light, like a slow tide, came up the hill and found the house, finding first the chimney pots, then the slates, then the single dormer window that looked out over the Heath. And in that room, which smelled of turpentine and dust and the cold, clean scent of the sky, a girl stood before an easel. Her name was Clara. Or was it? It did not matter. Here, in the attic, she was not the daughter of Mr. and Mrs. Ashton of Willow House, she was only a pair of eyes, and a hand that held a brush, and a heart that beat in time with the drip of the rain on the sill. The painting was of the Heath in winter, but it was not the Heath as it was, not the trampled mud and the grey sky, but the Heath as it felt in the memory of a summer afternoon, the light so thick you could taste it, the greens so deep they were almost black. A flicker of gold there, for the sun on the water of the pond, and a smear of violet, for the shadow of the trees.

Downstairs, the house was a body with its own slow pulse. The grandfather clock in the hall ticked off the seconds of a life she was not living. A life of teacups and embroidery, of visiting cards left on the silver salver, of listening to her father’s voice as he read the paper aloud, the words falling like stones into the quiet room. *Marriage*, he had said, not looking at her but at the column of figures he was totting up. *A woman needs a home of her own. A painter needs a patron.* The words had settled on her like dust, a fine grey powder that she could not shake off. And then there was Mr. Finch, his studio smelling of linseed oil and disappointment. *You have a certain diligence, Miss Ashton,* he had said, wiping his brushes on a rag that was stiff with old colour. *But the fire, the divine spark… it is not there. It is a pleasant hobby. Nothing more.* He had smiled, a thin, papery smile, and she had felt the whole world narrow to the space of that small, dusty room.

But here, up here, the world widened again. The light shifted, from the pale pearl of morning to the watery blue of noon. A bird, a sparrow perhaps, cheeped on the ledge, its small, frantic life a counterpoint to the slow, patient work of the brush. What was she painting? Not the Heath, not really. She was painting the feeling of the wind on her cheek as she walked there, the way the light caught the edge of a leaf, the sudden, shocking blue of a piece of crockery in the grass. It was all there, on the canvas, a jumble of sensation, of light and colour, and it was more real to her than the polished mahogany of the dining table, than the starched white of her mother’s collars. It was a secret, this room, a pocket of time stolen from the great, ticking clock of the house below. No one knew. They thought she was reading, or resting her eyes. They thought she was accepting her fate, folding herself neatly into the shape they had made for her.

The afternoon wore on. The light began to thicken, to turn to honey and then to amber. A cart rattled past in the street below, the sound rising up to her like a distant memory. She stepped back from the easel. Her back ached. Her fingers were stiff. She looked at the canvas, at the swirl of greens and the flicker of gold, and she felt a strange, hollow triumph. It was finished. Or it was as finished as it would ever be. It was a piece of her own soul, rendered in oil and pigment, and it was beautiful, and no one would ever see it. The thought was a stone in her chest. She leaned the canvas against the wall, next to the others, the silent witnesses to her secret life. There was the garden in summer, the rain on the windowpane, the bowl of apples on the kitchen table. A whole world, hidden away in the eaves.

She heard her mother’s voice calling her name from the garden, a thin, reedy sound that was absorbed by the thick walls of the house. Clara. Tea was ready. She must go down. She must wash the turpentine from her hands and pin up her hair and become again the daughter of Mr. and Mrs. Ashton. She looked around the room one last time, at the easel, the tubes of paint, the dirty rags, the canvases leaning against the sloping wall. It was all hers. It was a fortress. She blew out the lamp, and the room was plunged into the deep blue of the twilight, the shapes of the canvases just visible against the wall, like ghosts waiting for the dawn. She closed the door softly behind her, the click of the lock a small, final sound in the quiet of the house. And as she went down the narrow stairs, the light from the attic window above her caught the dust motes in the air, turning them for a moment into a swirling galaxy of gold, before they settled again, unseen, into the darkness.
