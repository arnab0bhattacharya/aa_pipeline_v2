The telegram arrived with the morning’s breath, a thin, rattling intrusion of the world upon the stagnant air of the consulate. It was brought by a boy, barefoot and incurious, who accepted the small coin with a shrug that seemed to encompass the whole of his existence. I read the flimsy paper at my desk, beneath the portrait of a Queen whose distant, impassive gaze had presided over twenty years of my life. The message was stark, official, and possessed of an energy that seemed utterly alien to this place. HMS *Vigilant*, a corvette, was proceeding to the port of San Cristobal to protect and uphold British interests. I read it twice, the words meaning less with each repetition. British interests. The phrase was a ghost in the room, a spectre of some forgotten significance.

Outside, the town of San Cristobal slumbered under a sun that had long since baked all urgency from the stones. The heat was not merely a temperature; it was a substance, a heavy, golden liquid that filled the lungs and slowed the thoughts to the pace of a dying insect. From the shuttered window of my office, the port lay below like a feverish dream. The water was a sheet of hammered pewter, incandescent with glare, and the masts of the schooners were a sparse, black forest against a sky bleached of all colour. For twenty years I had watched this scene. I had written about the flow of coffee, the price of cinchona, the petty squabbles of successive juntas, the moral character of the presidents. My reports, dispatched by the unreliable mail steamer, had been a long, monotonous litany of facts that no one in London, I was convinced, had ever truly read. They were a ritual, an act of faith in an empire whose heart was a world away, a heart that had perhaps ceased to beat for this forgotten coast.

The telegram lay on the polished mahogany, a sharp white wound upon the dark wood. *British interests*. What were they? I tried to summon the certainties of my younger self, the man who had first stepped onto this quay with a firm handshake and a profound belief in the righteousness of the enterprise. He was a stranger to me now. The interests, then, had been clear: the trade in hides and tallow, the honour of the flag, the civilising mission. But the trade had dwindled to a trickle, the flag was a faded piece of bunting on the consulate flagpole, and the civilising mission—what of that? I thought of Mr. Harwood, the manager of the Pacific Cable Company’s short-lived station, who had been murdered for his watch by a man he had befriended. I thought of the endless, circular arguments with Minister Valdez over a customs duty that was never paid, a debt that was never acknowledged. These were the facts of my tenure, but they were not interests. They were merely the texture of decay.

I rose and walked to the window, pushing aside the heavy velvet curtain. The glare was immense. Down on the quay, a few figures moved with the languid grace of sleepwalkers. They were of this place; they belonged to the heat and the dust and the shimmering water. I had lived among them for two decades, and yet I felt a profound, unbridgeable separation. I was a curator of a museum no one visited, a guardian of a language that was no longer spoken. The *Vigilant*, with its disciplined crew and its terrible, clean purpose, would arrive and shatter this uneasy truce. It would bring the noise of the modern world, the clash of wills, the demand for a clarity I no longer possessed.

Did I still share these interests? The question was a quiet horror. The idea of the Queen’s uniform, the salutes, the crisp commands, seemed to belong to another man’s life. My loyalty had been worn thin by the years, stretched into a gossamer thread of routine and obligation. I had come to feel a sort of protective pity for this ramshackle republic, for its passionate, fatalistic people, for the very incompetence and corruption that had become the familiar rhythm of its existence. The *Vigilant* would not see it that way. It would see only a challenge to be met, a grievance to be redressed. Its presence would be an accusation.

I thought of Señora Isabella, the widow of a coffee planter, who brought me a basket of mangoes every Christmas and spoke of her dead husband with a serene acceptance that was more eloquent than any sermon. I thought of old Mendoza, the harbourmaster, who could read the weather in the flight of the gulls and whose memory of every ship that had entered the port for forty years was a living history. Were their lives, their world, to be weighed in the scales of London’s interests and found wanting? The notion felt monstrous, an act of profound spiritual violence.

The sun began its slow descent, and the light in the room turned from gold to a bruised, sullen orange. The telegram still lay on the desk. It was an order, a summons to a self I had lost. I was to be the instrument of a purpose I could no longer comprehend. The *Vigilant* was coming. It would steam into the harbour, a grey, implacable fact, and I would have to go down to the quay to meet it. I would have to salute, and present my credentials, and explain, in clear and concise language, just what it was we were here to protect. And in the profound silence that would follow, broken only by the lapping of the alien water against the unfamiliar hull, I knew I would have no answer. I would stand on that quay, a man of the Empire in name only, and feel the twenty years of my life collapse into a single, desolate moment of truth.
