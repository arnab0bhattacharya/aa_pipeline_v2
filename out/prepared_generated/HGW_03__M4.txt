My wife had put her head in at the door at about half-past seven to tell me the water was in the road, and I had answered with the comfortable fatuity of a man who has not yet got his boots on, that it was probably the burst main they had been digging at since Thursday. But when I came down and looked over the little front gate, I saw at once that no main ever burst in that fashion. The road, which ran straight down the hill to the river and the railway bridge, was gone. In its place was a broad, coffee-coloured river, moving with a quiet, deliberate swiftness that was somehow worse than any noise, and carrying on its surface straws and sticks and a hen-coop and the whole of Mr. Pargeter's fence. The water was already at the third step of the Jenkins' house opposite, and while I watched, a little eddy took the corner of it and a board came off and went sailing away towards the bridge.

I am an insurance clerk at the Norwich Union offices in the City, and my name is Albert Vellacott; we live in a semi-detached villa in the road that leads down to the river at Chertsey Meads, which we had chosen because the rent was moderate and the trains were good. I mention the trains because it was the trains that first brought the thing home to me as a public calamity rather than a private inconvenience. I dressed with a haste that was not so much alarm as the instinct of the commuter, and walked up to the station through the drizzle, and there found a crowd of perhaps a hundred men, all in their City clothes, standing about the booking-office with the flat, resentful faces of Englishmen confronted by an Act of God for which no time-table provides. The stationmaster—a stout, worried man named Cribb—told us that the line was under water at Weybridge, that the up-signals were out of order, and that he had no instructions. "No instructions," he kept repeating, as though the phrase were a charm against responsibility. A young fellow in a straw hat asked him whether there would be a train by noon, and Cribb said he couldn't say; and the young fellow said that that was a nice way to run a railway, and Cribb, stung, replied that if the young fellow thought he could run it better he was welcome to try, and there was that in his voice which silenced the crowd. We stood about under the dripping awning, and presently a rumour went round that the water was over the line at Staines as well, and that the great reservoir at Thorpe had been seen to be "giving." Nobody knew who had seen it giving, or what giving meant, but we all believed it, and several men went home again.

I went home too, and spent the day in a state of restless idleness, watching the water climb the Jenkins' steps and enter their front door, and listening to Mrs. Jenkins crying in her kitchen. My wife was wonderful—she always is—and made a list of the neighbours who might need help, and sent me round with a jug of hot tea to old Mrs. Adcock at the end of the terrace, who lived alone and had refused to move. In the afternoon a boat came up the road, rowed by a young man in his shirtsleeves whom I did not know, and he told us that the whole of the Meads was under, that the water was six feet deep at the Ferry Inn, and that they had taken off two families from the bungalows by the towpath. He said the river had risen nine feet since midnight, and that they were saying at the Ferry that the weir at Shepperton had gone. I asked him what the authorities were doing, and he laughed—a short, ugly laugh—and said that the authorities were at Kingston, and that if Kingston was as wet as Chertsey they might be doing a good deal and nobody the wiser. Then he rowed on, and I went in and looked at my wife, and for the first time I felt a small cold hand at the back of my neck.

The second day was worse, because the novelty had gone and the water had not. It rained steadily from a low, leaden sky, and the road was now a single brown flood from the station hill to the bridge, with here and there a chimney-pot or the top of a lamp-post showing above it. The gas had failed, and we cooked on a spirit-lamp in the scullery and ate cold bacon, and my wife said, with the courage that never fails her, that it was like a picnic, and I said yes, and we both lied cheerfully. Towards noon a cart came down from the high road, driven by a farmer named Hooker, who had come to see whether his brother-in-law at the Meads was drowned, and he brought us the news that the whole valley from Windsor to Kingston was under water, that the great reservoirs above Staines had indeed given way in the night, and that the military had been called out at Hampton Court to keep the crowd off the bridges. He said there was talk of a relief committee at the Town Hall, but that the Town Hall itself was standing in two feet of water and the committee had met in the vestry of the parish church instead, and had passed a resolution. I asked him what the resolution was, and he said he understood it was to the effect that the situation was receiving attention. He said it with a sort of grim relish, as a man who has long suspected the universe of being badly managed and now has proof of it.
