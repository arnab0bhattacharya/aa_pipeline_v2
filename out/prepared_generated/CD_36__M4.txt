In the Rue des Trois Pignons, where the houses lean together like gossips and the gutter in the middle runs its own crooked course regardless of any surveyor’s plan, there stood—and, for aught I know, stands yet—the workshop of Monsieur Anselme Bouchardon, Horloger de la Ville. It was a narrow, dusty, tick-ridden little kingdom, crammed to the ceiling with clocks of every description: clocks in tall walnut cases that bowed like aged courtiers; clocks in gilt frames that aped the grandeur of Versailles; clocks without cases at all, mere naked faces and busy hands, lying on the bench like honest men stripped of their coats. And all of them, great and small, French and foreign, ancient and newly made, kept up such a clatter of ticking, such a frenzy of whirring and clicking, that a stranger entering the door might reasonably have clapped his hands to his ears and declared himself in a mill of madness.

Monsieur Bouchardon himself was a small, wiry, bent old man of seventy years, with a face like a walnut shell and a pair of spectacles so frequently mended that they had become a sort of architectural curiosity, being held together by wire, thread, and, it was whispered, a dab of sealing-wax. He had kept the clocks of the Hôtel de Ville for five-and-forty years—through the reign of the late King, through the troubles, through the Terror that was then at its height—and he regarded the various governments that had come and gone in that time with the serene indifference of a man who has observed that, whatever the fashion in politics, the sun continues to rise at its appointed hour and clocks, if properly tended, will tick.

It was on a grey afternoon in the month of Vendémiaire, in the Year Two of the Republic, that there came to his door a young man named Citizen Jacques Fauchelevent, who was, by his own account, a commissioner of the Committee of Public Safety, and who wore his tricolour cockade with the aggressive jauntiness of one who has recently discovered that a ribbon may serve in place of a head.

“Citizen Bouchardon,” said this young man, entering without ceremony and knocking a small pendulum to the floor with his elbow, “I am sent to you by the Committee. The clocks of the Hôtel de Ville are to be set to the new calendar. The old dials are to be removed. The ten-hour day is decreed. You will make the alteration.”

Monsieur Bouchardon, who was at that moment engaged with a pair of tweezers inside the entrails of a bracket clock, did not look up.

“The clocks,” said he, “are already correct.”

“Correct!” cried Citizen Fauchelevent. “Correct, do you say? They show the old hours—the hours of kings and priests and tyrants! They must show the hours of Reason and Liberty. The Convention has decreed it. The decimal day is the day of the future. Ten hours to the day, one hundred minutes to the hour, one hundred seconds to the minute. It is the calendar of the Revolution!”

“It is a very pretty calendar,” said the old man, still not looking up. “I have no doubt it will serve admirably for those who can make it work. But my clocks are set to the sun, and the sun, Citizen Fauchelevent, has not received the decree. He rises and sets by his own old-fashioned notions, and I have found him, in five-and-forty years, to be a very obstinate fellow.”

“You mock the Republic!”

“I mock nothing. I wind clocks.”

The commissioner drew himself up, and his cockade trembled with indignation. “Do you know,” said he, “that it is within my power to denounce you? Do you know that there are those in Paris who have been sent to the guillotine for less than a stubborn clock?”

At this the old man raised his head, and looked at the young commissioner over the rim of his mended spectacles with a gaze that was not without a certain dry amusement.

“Citizen,” said he, “I have seen the Bastille fall, and I have seen the King’s head fall, and I have seen the Goddess of Reason enthroned in Notre-Dame with a very handsome pair of legs, I am told, though I did not go to see it. I have seen the assignats rise and fall, and the price of bread rise and fall, and the names of the months change from January to Nivôse and from Nivôse back again, for aught I know, for I have lost the count. But I have never yet seen a clock that could be made to tick faster or slower by any decree of any government whatsoever. A clock, Citizen Fauchelevent, is a machine for telling the truth about time, and time, if you will pardon an old man’s philosophy, is the one aristocrat that no revolution can guillotine.”

The commissioner stared at him. “You refuse?”

“I refuse to make my clocks lie.”

“Then you are a traitor!”

“I am a horloger,” said Monsieur Bouchardon, with dignity, “and I have been one for five-and-forty years, and I will be one, if it please the Republic, for a few years more, or until my hands shake too much to hold the tweezers. And when I am dead, Citizen, and you are dead, and the Committee is dead, and the Republic—which I do not say with any disrespect—has become something else again, as republics do, those clocks will still be ticking in the Hôtel de Ville, and they will still be telling the truth, because I would not let them lie.”

Citizen Fauchelevent went away in a great fury, threatening denunciation, arrest, the scaffold, and the whole machinery of the Terror; and Monsieur Bouchardon, returning to his tweezers, found that his hand trembled a little, and he set down the tool and sat for a while in silence among his ticking children.
