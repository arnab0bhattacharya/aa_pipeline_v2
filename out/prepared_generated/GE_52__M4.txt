It was a fact often remarked by Mrs. Ellinor Trenchard—remarked with that air of tolerant resignation which she had cultivated as a species of moral corset, keeping her sentiments from any unseemly protrusion—that her husband's benevolence, like the fabled river of the ancients, was apt to lose itself in the sands before it reached the sea of human need. Mr. Josiah Trenchard, of Marchmont Hall, was a man in whom the love of improvement had taken the form of a passion for the concrete. He could not conceive of a virtue that did not express itself in brick and mortar, in drainage and in the width of a roadway. To him the betterment of mankind was a matter of plans and specifications; and he had long cherished the design of erecting upon the southern slope of his park a row of model cottages, which should stand as a visible sermon on the text of sanitary reform, a rebuke to the squalid thatch and crumbling plaster of the village of Little Bedingfield.

The architect he had chosen for this enterprise was a young man named Arthur Penhallow, who had come to him with a portfolio of designs so advanced, so full of the new spirit of the age, that Mr. Trenchard had felt himself in the presence of a kindred soul. Penhallow was a young man of earnest aspect and a certain nervous intensity of manner, who spoke of "the housing question" with the fervour of a missionary, and who believed—as young men of his generation were beginning to believe—that the health of the body politic depended upon the ventilation of the labourer's cottage. He had read Mr. Ruskin with enthusiasm, and had come away from that author with a conviction that beauty was a moral agent, and that the Gothic arch was in some way connected with the Sermon on the Mount.

The cottages were built. They stood upon the slope, four of them, in a neat terrace, with pointed windows and a little tower at the end, which contained a pump of the most approved pattern. They were whitewashed to a dazzling purity; they had a common drying-ground, and a covered shed for the chopping of wood. Mr. Trenchard, when he beheld them, felt that he had done something for the age. He walked through the rooms, tapping the walls with his cane, and speaking of the cubic feet of air. "Here," he said to Penhallow, "is the future of England." Penhallow, who was of a modest disposition, and who had been rendered uneasy by the excessive whiteness of the whitewash, murmured something about the necessity of the picturesque, which Mr. Trenchard did not hear.

The labourers, however, did not share their landlord's enthusiasm. They stood in a little group at the gate of the field, and contemplated the new dwellings with a silence that was more eloquent than speech. Old Matthew Rumbold, the shepherd, who had lived for forty years in a cottage whose floor was below the level of the road, and whose chimney smoked with a persistency that had become a proverb, shook his head slowly. "They be main fine," he said, at last, "but I doubt they'll be main cold." His neighbour, a ploughman named John Nettle, who had a wife and seven children, and who was therefore in a position to speak with authority upon the subject of space, remarked that he could not see where a man was to put his pig. The pig, it appeared, was a consideration of the first importance. The old cottages had each a little lean-to at the back, where a pig could be fattened; the new ones had a neat outhouse for coals, and a patent mangle. The labourers were not ungrateful; they were merely bewildered. They had been accustomed to damp, and they distrusted dryness; they had been accustomed to a certain freedom of arrangement, and they resented the symmetry of the model.

Mr. Trenchard was wounded. He had expected a demonstration of gratitude; he received instead a series of evasions, and, when he pressed the matter, a petition from Matthew Rumbold that he might be allowed to remain where he was. "I'm old," said the shepherd, "and I've gotten my bits of things about me, and I don't know as I could sleep in a new place." Mr. Trenchard, who was not without a certain magnanimity, consented to the petition, but he felt that the world was ungrateful, and he spoke of the ignorance of the rural mind.

It was at this juncture that Mrs. Trenchard made her observation. She was a woman of a fine, delicate nature, in whom the habit of observation had been cultivated at the expense of speech. She had watched her husband's career with a mixture of admiration and irony. She knew that his philanthropy was genuine, but she knew also that it was compounded with a subtle vanity, a desire to see himself reflected in the gratitude of others. "My dear," she said to him one evening, as they sat in the drawing-room, "I wonder whether you are building these cottages for the labourers, or for the pleasure of seeing your own ideas made visible."

Mr. Trenchard was startled. "My love," he said, "you do me an injustice. I have no motive but the good of my fellow-creatures."

"Perhaps," said Mrs. Trenchard, "but the good of your fellow-creatures is a large phrase, and it is apt to take the colour of our own minds. You have built them a house that would suit a curate, and they would rather have a house that suits a labourer. You have given them a pump, and they want a pig."

This conversation left Mr. Trenchard uneasy. He was not a man who could endure a doubt; he resolved to consult Penhallow. Penhallow, who had been walking through the village, and who had been struck by the cheerful squalor of the old cottages, had already begun to entertain misgivings.
