The SS *Mekala* wallowed in the muddy swells of the Gulf of Siam, her rusted hull groaning with every pitch. Captain Elias Vane stood on the bridge wing, his hands wrapped tight around the brass railing, feeling the vibration of the engines through his palms and the salt wind against his face. He could not see the horizon. He had not seen it for three months, not since the cataracts had fully sealed his eyes into a permanent, milky twilight. But he stood as he always stood, legs braced wide, chin tilted upward as though scanning the distant interface of sea and sky. He had commanded the *Mekala* for fourteen years, running teak and rice between Chanthaburi and the colonial ports of the Malay Peninsula, and he knew the Gulf the way a blind man knows his own house—by its sounds, its swells, its moods. He listened to the water breaking against the hull and knew they were running south-southeast. He listened to the rhythm of the engine and knew they were making eight knots. And he waited for his mate to read him the world.

Tobin Kessler was twenty-nine, lean and sun-darkened, with quick eyes that moved constantly between the captain and the binnacle. He had been Vane's mate for two years and had learned to read his captain the way a translator reads a dead language—carefully, selectively, with an ear for what to leave out. He stood now at Vane's left elbow, his voice pitched low beneath the wind.

"Course south-southeast by south, Captain. Steady as she goes."

Vane nodded once. "Wind's freshened," he said. It was not a question.

"Freshening, yes. From the northeast. I'd estimate twenty knots."

"We'll make the Cape by morning, then."

"We will."

Vane turned from the rail, his hand finding the doorframe of the wheelhouse by memory. He moved with a deliberateness that could be mistaken for the measured dignity of an old seaman, and he counted his steps—three to the chart table, two to the captain's chair. He sat down and folded his hands in his lap, his clouded eyes aimed at a spot on the far bulkhead where he remembered a clock to be.

Kessler followed him in and stood by the binnacle, his reflection caught in the glass of the barometer. He glanced at the clock. It was where it had always been.

Below decks, in the cramped saloon that served as dining room and office, Mr. Aldous Penwarden of Barrington & Soysa Trading sat with a ledger open before him and a cup of black tea cooling at his elbow. He was a small man, meticulously dressed despite the heat, with a clerk's instinct for irregularity. He had been sent by the owners—Barrington & Soysa of Singapore—to ride the *Mekala* on her run from Chanthaburi to Malacca and to report back on the condition of the vessel, the efficiency of her operations, and the fitness of her master. The letter of instruction in his breast pocket used the word *concerns*. It was a word Penwarden treasured, because it implied a problem that required a man of his temperament to define.

He had been aboard four days. In that time he had noted the following: the captain did not consult the chart himself but asked the mate to mark their position. The captain did not read the barometer but asked the mate for the reading. The captain did not look through the skylight at the weather but asked the mate what the clouds looked like. The captain stood on the bridge with his eyes open, but his eyes did not move. They were fixed, slightly milky, aimed at nothing.

Penwarden wrote none of this in his ledger. Not yet. He wrote instead: *Cargo stowage satisfactory. Crew discipline adequate. Steamer shows wear on boiler casing, recommend inspection at Singapore yard.* He wrote these things because they were true and because they were safe, and he was not yet ready to commit the other observation to paper. He wanted to be certain. A man who accused a captain of blindness on the evidence of a milky gaze and a few questions to the mate would be a fool if he was wrong and a threat if he was right. Penwarden intended to be neither.

That night the *Mekala* ran into a squall. It came out of the northeast as Kessler had predicted, a violent, hisling curtain of rain that turned the sea to chalk. Vane was on the bridge within minutes of the first gust, his oilskins pulled on over his nightclothes, his hands gripping the bridge rail. He could feel the ship yawing, the rudder struggling. The rain was so heavy it sounded like gravel thrown against the wheelhouse.

"What do you see?" he asked.

Kessler was at the bridge wing, clinging to the stanchion, peering into the dark. "Rain. Heavy. No visibility beyond a cable. The sea is confused—short, steep swells from the northeast."

"Compass?"

"South-southeast, Captain. She's holding."

Vane listened to the water. He could hear the sea breaking differently now—not against the hull but over the foredeck, which meant the swells were beam-on. They were being pushed sideways.

"Hard a-starboard," Vane said. "Bring her head into the swell. Two points."

Kessler relayed the order to the helmsman. The *Mekala* swung slowly, her bow climbing the short steep seas. The motion changed from a sickening roll to a heavy pitch, and Vane felt the deck rise and fall beneath his boots. Better. He nodded.

Kessler watched the captain's face in the glow of the binnacle lamp. The milky eyes were open, aimed forward, seeing nothing. The rain ran down Vane's cheeks like tears. Kessler felt something tighten in his chest—not pity, not exactly. Something closer to the feeling a man gets when he holds a fragile object that does not belong to him and knows that if it breaks, he will be blamed. Or rewarded. He was not sure which, and the uncertainty was the thing that tightened.
