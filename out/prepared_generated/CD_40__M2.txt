The fog that morning did not merely settle upon the town of St. Jude’s; it invaded it, seeping into the very marrow of the cobblestones, wrapping the spires of the ancient cathedral in a shroud of grey wool so thick and tenacious that the sun, when it did at last make a feeble, apologetic appearance, looked less like a celestial body and more like a jaundiced eye peering suspiciously through a dirty window. It was a fit atmosphere for misery, and a poor one for hope, yet there, walking with a stride that belied the weight of his heart, was Dr. Arthur Penhaligon. He had returned from the burning plains of India, with his skin bronzed to the color of old mahogany and his eyes holding the peculiar, distant vacancy of a man who has seen the machinery of death up close and found it indifferent to his presence. He was young, lean, and possessed of a jaw that might have been carved from granite by a sculptor who had been short of patience.

He had come home, or so he told himself, to practice his art. But the truth, that uncomfortable little truth which likes to hide behind the ribs, was that he had come home to see her. Or rather, to see the house where she used to be. For Eliza Thorne, the girl who had once braided daisies into his hair and promised that the world was a garden of roses, was now the intended bride of Mr. Josiah Crumble, the local magistrate, a man whose personality was as dry and brittle as a winter branch, and whose wealth was as substantial as it was unyielding.

Arthur had taken a practice in a narrow, crooked street known as The Nook, which snaked away from the cathedral square like a forgotten ribbon. It was a poor street, inhabited by those whom the polished society of St. Jude’s preferred to overlook. The patients were few, the fees were less, and the air smelled perpetually of damp wool and boiled cabbage. Yet, it was here, in this humble abode of mediocrity, that the first thread of his redemption was spun.

It began with a cough. A small, dry, rattling sound that seemed to vibrate through the walls of the Thorne household, which stood a mere three streets away, imposing and proud, with its white paint and its iron gates. Eliza’s younger brother, a boy of twelve named Thomas, had fallen ill. The fever took him with the suddenness of a snap, leaving him high-strung and delirious, his eyes rolling in his head like marbles in a box. The family physician, a man of more years and less agility, declared it to be a case of consumption in its early, terrifying stages, and advised the family to prepare for the worst. But Arthur, who had spent years in the tropics where such fevers were common and curable, knew better. He knew the trick of the thing, the specific combination of quinine, rest, and a cool sponge that could pull a soul back from the precipice.

He went to the Thorne house not as a suitor, for that door was locked, but as a doctor. He wore his white coat, which was not quite white enough, and his hands, which were steady despite the tremor in his chest. Mr. Crumble, who was standing by the sickbed with an expression of profound discomfort, as if he feared the disease might catch the upholstery, looked at Arthur with a mixture of disdain and reluctant hope.

"Is there anything to be done?" Mr. Crumble asked, his voice thin and reedy.

"There is," Arthur said, his voice low and grave. "But it will require time. And it will require you to step back, sir. The air must be fresh, and the room must be quiet. Can you manage that?"

Mr. Crumble, who had never in his life managed anything that required physical effort or emotional sensitivity, nodded stiffly. "I shall leave you to it, Doctor. I trust you know what you are about."

Arthur did know. For three weeks, he lived a double life. By day, he tended to his few patients in The Nook, enduring the complaints of the pennyless and the ingratitude of the proud. By night, or in the early hours of the morning, he went to the Thorne house. He watched over Thomas as the fever broke, as the boy’s breathing slowed, as the color returned to his cheeks like a tide coming in. He sat in the corner, invisible, a shadow in the room, watching Eliza.

She was beautiful still, though there was a new stiffness in her posture, a new formality in her speech. She was engaged to be married, a fact that hung in the air like the scent of lilies, sweet and cloying. Yet, when Arthur administered the last dose of medicine, and Thomas stirred, opening his eyes to look at him with a sudden, lucid clarity, Eliza’s hand, which had been resting on her brother’s forehead, brushed against Arthur’s. It was an accident, a mere collision of fingers. But in that touch, Arthur felt the electric shock of a memory, a spark of the old fire that he had thought long since extinguished.

Thomas recovered. He sat up, then stood, then walked to the window and looked out at the fog, which was finally lifting, revealing the pale, washed-out world beyond. The family’s gratitude was overwhelming. Mr. Crumble, who had previously regarded Arthur with the same interest one might show a stray dog, now bowed to him with a courtesy that was almost suffocating. He invited Arthur to dinner. He spoke of Eliza’s virtue, of his own desire to provide for her, of the stability that their union would bring.
