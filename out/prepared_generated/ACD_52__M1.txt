My name is Eleanor Vance, and my business in Hatton Garden was that of a student of the Royal Academy, though my lodging was of a far less academic character. It consisted of two rooms directly above the premises of a jeweller and watchmaker, one Mr. Josiah Croft. The address was No. 14, and the window below my own looked out upon the steady, purposeful flow of commerce and carriage that is the lifeblood of that quarter. I had taken the rooms for their north light, which was excellent for my work, and for the modesty of their rent, which was essential to my circumstances.

For the first month, my arrangement with Mr. Croft was one of silent, mutual indifference. I would see him below, a man of some fifty years, with a stoop to his shoulders and the pale, perpetually worried expression of one who deals in delicate and valuable things. His hands, as he handled a tray of unset diamonds or a customer’s gold watch, were long and steady. He spoke little, and when he did, it was in a low, measured tone that seemed as carefully calibrated as the timepieces he mended. The shop itself was a marvel of ordered glitter, every velvet tray and glass case arranged with an almost surgical precision.

It was in the second month that the hammering began. It started as a faint, intermittent tapping, audible only in the profound quiet of the deep night, after the last omnibus had rattled past and the gas-lamps had been turned down to a low hiss. I would be at my easel, attempting to capture the last of the twilight, or reading by the light of a single candle, and the sound would commence. *Tap-tap-tap.* A pause. Then a more insistent *tap-tap-tap-tap-tap*. It was a small, precise sound, like a tiny hammer on a tiny anvil, and it seemed to come from directly beneath my floorboards.

At first, I dismissed it. A building of that age has its own complaints—its settling timbers, its groaning pipes. But this was no random creak. It had a rhythm, a purpose. Night after night, from perhaps ten o’clock until well past two in the morning, the sound would continue. It was not the heavy, clumsy noise of a carpenter, but the meticulous work of a craftsman engaged in some minute and repetitive task. My curiosity, at first a dull ember, began to glow with a steady heat.

One afternoon, I encountered Mr. Croft on the narrow landing as I was returning with my paints and he was ascending with a parcel. I decided upon a direct approach.

“Mr. Croft,” I began, with what I hoped was a neighbourly smile, “I hope I do not disturb you with my comings and goings.”

He paused, his hand on the banister. His eyes, a pale, watery blue, blinked at me from behind his spectacles. “Not at all, Miss Vance. Not at all.”

“I was wondering,” I continued, pressing on, “about the noise at night. The hammering. I fear my own late hours might be the cause of some disturbance, but I hear a sound from below that…”

A flicker of something—annoyance, or perhaps fear—crossed his features before being smoothed away. “Ah. Yes. Forgive me. The clocks, you see. The finer repairs. They can only be done when the world is quiet. A steady hand is required, and the slightest tremor from a passing dray can ruin a hairspring. It is a simple matter of concentration.”

His explanation was logical, yet it did not settle my mind. The sound had seemed too small for a grandfather clock, too persistent for the delicate work on a pocket watch. But I could hardly contradict him. “Of course,” I said. “I understand perfectly.”

For a week after that, the hammering was less frequent. Then, it ceased altogether. The silence it left behind was more unsettling than the noise itself. Two days passed, and I had not seen Mr. Croft below. A small card in the shop window read, ‘Closed for Repairs. Will Re-Open Shortly.’ Yet there was no sign of repair, no workman’s van, no activity. The shop was dark, the velvet trays in the window shrouded in a fine layer of dust. On the third day, the silence had grown so oppressive that I descended to the street and tried the door. It was locked fast. A letter, I noticed, was jammed in the letterbox, its edges curling.

My unease had by now curdled into a genuine alarm. The man had vanished. The meticulous, nightly work had stopped. The logical connection between these events was a matter for a more practised mind than my own. It was then that I remembered a name mentioned in conversation at the Academy, a man of singular talents who dealt with matters that fell outside the ordinary purview of the police. Mr. Alistair Finch.

His chambers were in a quiet court off the Strand, a place of dark panelling and the faint, pleasant smell of pipe tobacco and old paper. Mr. Finch himself was a tall, lean man with an air of restless energy that belied his stillness. He did not rise when I entered, but merely gestured to a chair opposite his own, his sharp, dark eyes taking in every detail of my person and dress with a speed that was quite unnerving.

“Miss Vance,” he said, his voice crisp and clear. “You have come about a disappearance.”

I confess I started. “How could you possibly know that?”

A thin smile touched his lips. “A lady of artistic temperament, with a smudge of ultramarine blue on her right cuff and a trace of linseed oil upon her gloves, arrives at these chambers unannounced and with an expression of considerable anxiety. The cases which reach me are seldom of a cheerful nature. The disappearance of a person or an object is the most common. Now, be so good as to present your facts, omitting no detail, however trivial it may seem.”
