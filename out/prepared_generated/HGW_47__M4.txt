The archive at Vell was a quiet place, and that was the whole point of it. It was a vast, low, white building set among lawns of a particularly restful green, and within its walls the air was kept at a steady, dry temperature, and the light was a soft, even glow that fell upon the reading desks and the long shelves of spools without ever casting a shadow. I had come there, as I had come there every day for a month, to work upon the history of agricultural machinery in the Upper Llanddwyn district, a subject so placid and so utterly without incident that my supervisor, the excellent Dr. Merton, had assured me it was a perfectly safe topic for a young woman of my temperament.

I was, I think, a person of ordinary sensibility. I had been raised, like everyone I knew, in the gentle disciplines of the Concordat. We did not fight. We had not fought for nine generations. The very word “war” was a sort of archaism, a term one met with in the more tedious passages of historical spools, a word that had the same dusty, faintly repellent flavour as “gaol” or “slavery” or “brimstone.” We were a reasonable people. We had our differences, of course, and we settled them by the method of the Referendum and the Panel of Conciliation, and if a dispute grew heated, why, the disputants were separated for a cooling period, and that was an end to it.

I mention this so that you may understand how entirely unprepared I was for what I found.

It was my habit, when the machinery spools grew dull, to wander a little in the unclassified stacks, where the archivists kept the material they had not yet catalogued. It was here, in a dusty cabinet at the very back of the hall, that I came upon a box of spools marked simply “Military—Miscellany.” I took one up, idly enough, and fitted it to the viewer.

The first images were dull: maps with arrows, tables of numbers, the sort of thing one could not make head or tail of. But then the spool changed to a different sort of record altogether, and I found myself looking at a moving picture—a genuine, old-fashioned moving picture, grainy and flickering, of a great crowd of people on a road. They were not walking in any order. They were pushing and stumbling, and their faces were grey with dust, and I could see, quite clearly, that some of them were bleeding. There was a woman carrying a child, and the child’s head was bound up in a rag. There was an old man sitting on the grass, and he had taken off his boot, and his sock was dark with blood. A cart went by, and on it a blind man in a curious uniform was shouting something I could not hear. The whole thing had an air of frantic, disorderly misery that I had never seen before, that I had not even imagined.

I sat there for a long time. The spool ran to its end and clicked, and I started it again. I could not make it out. Why were they running? What were they running from? And why did no one help them?

I went to Dr. Merton. He was a kindly man with a long, pale face and spectacles that he was always polishing.

“Dr. Merton,” I said, “I have found a record in the stacks. It appears to show a great number of people in flight from something. They are injured. There are no Conciliators present. I do not understand it.”

He took the spool from me and examined the label. His face did not change, but he polished his spectacles a little more slowly.

“Ah,” he said. “Yes. That would be from the period of the Last Great Folly. The records are kept for completeness, but they are not for general study. They are distressing, and they have no bearing on the present day. I should put it back, if I were you.”

“But what were they running from?” I insisted.

“From other men, I suppose,” he said. “From the armies of their enemies. It was a very backward time. I believe there was a great deal of running to and fro. Now, your work on the Llanddwyn threshing machines is coming along nicely, I hope?”

I said that it was, and I went back to my desk, but I did not look at the threshing machines. I sat and thought about the woman with the child, and the old man with the bloody sock. The image of the blind man shouting in the cart stayed with me, and I found myself wondering, with a kind of chill, what it was he had been shouting.

I returned to the stacks that afternoon. I found other spools in the same box. There were records of a thing called a “trench,” which appeared to be a long, deep ditch in which men lived for weeks at a time, and which was filled with water and rats and the bodies of the dead. There were records of a thing called a “barrage,” which was a kind of continuous thunder that went on for days and drove men out of their minds. There were records of a “gas,” which was a cloud that drifted over the fields and killed everything it touched, and against which the only defence was to put a wet cloth over one’s face. I read the accounts of men who had been in these trenches, and they wrote of fear as if it were a companion, a thing that sat with them at every meal and lay down with them at night. One man wrote that he had killed another man with a spade, and that he could not remember why, and that he supposed the other man had been trying to kill him.
