The workshop smelled of ozone and boiled linseed oil, a combination that had long since ceased to trouble Mr. Alistair Finch, though it caused his housekeeper to leave his morning tray upon the windowsill rather than risk the interior air. It was here, amid a jungle of brass fittings, copper wire, and the skeletal remains of a dozen failed gramophones, that Finch had at last completed his magnum opus. He called it the Syllogistic Resonator, a name he felt lent it a certain classical dignity, and it consisted of a large, cone-shaped brass horn, not unlike those used for the newfangled phonographs, connected by a series of intricate, coiled wires to a box of polished mahogany bristling with dials and levers. Finch believed, with the unshakeable conviction of the truly eccentric, that the device could capture the subtle vibrations of animal utterance and translate them into the King's English.

His opportunity to test it came on a Tuesday afternoon, in the form of a portly, wheezing bulldog named Buster, the property of his next-door neighbour, Mr. Percival Wemyss. Wemyss was a man of rigid habits and a profound sense of his own importance, a retired solicitor who viewed Finch's chaotic garden and nocturnal tinkering with an eye of profound suspicion. Buster, in contrast, was a creature of simple pleasures: a warm patch of sun, a well-gnawed bone, and the occasional illicit foray into Finch's dustbin.

Finch, peering over the fence that divided their properties, observed Buster engaged in his favourite pastime of slumbering beneath a rhododendron bush. With the stealth of a man half his age, Finch crept into the Wemyss garden, his heart thumping with the thrill of scientific discovery. He placed the brass horn of the Resonator a discreet foot from the dog's twitching nose and retreated to his own side of the fence, where he had run a temporary wire to the main apparatus in his workshop.

He threw the primary switch. The machine hummed, a low, resonant thrum that vibrated in his very teeth. The dials flickered, their needles dancing wildly before settling into a steady, rhythmic pulse. For a moment, there was only a soft, static hiss from the machine's internal speaker. Then, a voice, thin and reedy, yet unmistakably distinct, emerged.

"…and the smell of it," the voice was saying, a note of profound weariness in its tone. "Boiled cabbage again. He had it for supper last night, and now the whole house reeks of it. He thinks I don't notice. He thinks I'm just a dog."

Finch nearly dropped his notebook. He fumbled for a pencil, his hand trembling. "Remarkable," he breathed.

The voice continued, a low, rumbling monologue. "He talks to me, you know. Not to me, at me. 'Good boy, Buster,' he says, in that voice he uses for the vicar. But his eyes are on the clock. Always the clock. Seven-fifteen: walk. Twelve-thirty: lunch. Five o'clock: the paper. He's a prisoner of his own routine, and I'm his warder. A fat, bored warder with a bad hip."

Finch scribbled furiously. This was more than he had ever dreamed. The dog was a philosopher, a critic, a keen observer of the human condition as embodied by Percival Wemyss.

"He's a decent sort, I suppose," Buster's translation continued, a sigh implicit in the words. "Gave me a bit of his chop on Sunday. But he's so… alone. The missus has been gone five years, and he hasn't had a soul in for tea since. He polishes his shoes every night, even though he's not going anywhere. He irons his newspaper, for heaven's sake. Irons it! Thinks it kills the germs. I heard him on the telephone to his sister last week. 'Oh, yes, very busy,' he said. 'Never a dull moment.' He was staring at a fly on the ceiling the whole time. The man is a monument to loneliness, and I'm the only one who sees it."

Finch felt a pang of something akin to pity for his neighbour. He had always dismissed Wemyss as a pompous bore. This was a revelation. He leaned closer to the speaker, eager for more.

"And the way he eats," Buster's voice went on, now with a touch of disdain. "He chews each bite forty times. I counted. Forty. It's a wonder he has any teeth left. And he hums. Always the same tune. Some dreadful music hall thing about a daisy and a bicycle. It's been driving me mad for three years. I've considered biting him, just to change the rhythm, but I'm too old and too comfortable."

Finch let out a bark of laughter, clapping a hand over his mouth. The sound, however, was enough. Buster's ears pricked up. The bulldog stirred, yawned prodigiously, and lumbered to his feet. He looked towards the fence, then at the strange brass horn lying in the grass. He sniffed it, grumbled, and then, with a final, disdainful glance in Finch's direction, trotted back towards the house.

Finch, his heart singing with triumph, dismantled his equipment and retreated to his workshop to transcribe his notes. He was still at it, chuckling over the dog's commentary on the humming, when a furious knocking echoed through his house. He opened the door to find Percival Wemyss, his face a mottled crimson, brandishing a piece of paper.

"Finch!" Wemyss thundered, his voice trembling with rage. "What is the meaning of this? My solicitor has just informed me… this… this slander!"

Finch blinked. "Slander? I don't take your meaning, Wemyss."

"Don't play the innocent with me!" Wemyss thrust the paper at him. It was a letter from a firm of solicitors in the City. Finch scanned it, his bewilderment growing. It spoke of a "gross and actionable invasion of privacy," of "mental anguish," and of "the public dissemination of private conversations." It was a writ for libel, naming Alistair Finch as the defendant and Percival Wemyss as the plaintiff, citing a forthcoming publication in a periodical called *The Naturalist's Gazette*.
