If there was one thing Mr. Septimus Quill, of the firm of Quill, Quill, and Quill—though the two elder Quills had long since departed this vale of tears, leaving the junior to conduct the affairs of the living—if there was one thing, I say, upon which Mr. Septimus Quill prided himself, it was a certain dogged, mole-like persistence in burrowing to the bottom of a matter, however unpromising the soil. It was a quality that had won him no small measure of success in the criminal courts, and it was a quality that was, on a certain bleak November morning in the year 1843, to be tried to its very utmost.

The matter in question was the case of one Tobias Grubb, a coal-heaver of Wapping, a man built upon the model of a barrel, with arms like oakum cables and a face perpetually smudged with the honest grime of his calling. Tobias stood accused of having feloniously abstracted, from the warehouse of his master, Mr. Ambrose Pinchbeck, a quantity of sea-coal to the value of forty shillings; a charge supported by the unimpeachable testimony of Mr. Pinchbeck himself, and by the no less damning evidence of a new pair of boots.

Mr. Quill, in his stuffy little chambers in the Temple, with its atmosphere of dust, parchment, and forgotten dinners, regarded his client with an eye of profoundest gloom. The case, he had been heard to observe to his clerk, was as black as the defendant’s face; and the defendant’s face, at that moment, wore the expression of a man who has been hit on the head with a mallet and is not quite sure whether he ought to be angry or to lie down.

“I never took a lump of it, sir,” rumbled Tobias, his voice like the grinding of a cart on cobbles. “Not so much as would black a pair of shoes. It’s a wicked, wicked world, sir, and a poor man’s word is no better than a puff of smoke.”

“The world is wicked, Mr. Grubb,” returned the lawyer, adjusting his spectacles, “and the law, I regret to say, is frequently its most zealous constable. Mr. Pinchbeck is a man of substance. He swears that his coal was stolen, that you were the only man on the premises with the opportunity, and that you were seen, by his own clerk, a Mr. Nathaniel Pottle, to be lurking near the coal-shed at an hour when all honest men should be abed.”

“Lurking!” cried Tobias, with a sudden access of indignation. “I was a-taking a drop of air, sir, before turning in, for the place is that hot and smothery with the fumes of the coal that a man might as well be a herring in a smoke-house. And as for Mr. Pottle—” here the coal-heaver’s face assumed an expression of the most profound contempt—“Mr. Pottle is a wriggling, white-livered piece of goods, sir, as I wouldn’t trust with a ha’penny.”

Mr. Quill sighed. The defence was not, he felt, calculated to sway a jury of London tradesmen, whose sympathies were ever with the man of property. He was about to deliver himself of some consolatory platitude when the door of his chambers was opened by his office-boy, a sharp-featured, red-haired urchin of some twelve summers, by name Timothy Tupp, who, having been found by Mr. Quill in the act of picking a pocket in Cheapside, had been promptly engaged by that gentleman as a boy of parts and promise.

“Please, sir,” piped Timothy, “the summons for Mr. Pinchbeck, sir. I was to take it round to his warehouse in Wapping, you said, and wait for an answer.”

“Ah, to be sure,” said Mr. Quill. “The summons for the production of the ledger. Be off with you, then, and see that you don’t dawdle by the way.”

The boy, with a wink that was intended to convey a world of professional discretion, vanished; and Mr. Quill, turning back to his dejected client, began to turn over in his mind the various shades and subtleties of the law of larceny, as though he might find some hidden virtue in a legal quibble that would serve to unlock the doors of Newgate.

It was some two hours later when Timothy Tupp returned, but he did not return alone. He came bursting into the chambers with the velocity of a small cannonball, his red hair standing on end, and his eyes starting from his head with the excitement of a discovery.

“Sir! Sir!” he gasped, quite out of breath. “Mr. Quill, sir! I’ve found it! The boots, sir, the boots!”

“What boots, you imp of Satan?” demanded the lawyer, starting from his chair. “Have you been drinking?”

“No, sir, not a drop, sir,” cried the boy, his words tumbling over one another in his eagerness. “It’s Mr. Pottle, sir, the clerk! I saw him, I did, a-sitting on a high stool in the counting-house, and a-writing away for dear life. And there, sir, sticking out from under his desk, was the beautifullest pair of new Hessian boots as ever you clapped eyes on. Patent leather, sir, and shining like a bishop’s hat on a Sunday!”

“And what in the name of the Seven Sleepers have Mr. Pottle’s boots to do with the case?” thundered Mr. Quill, who was not a man to be easily diverted from the scent of a legal problem.

“Why, sir,” said Timothy, with the triumphant air of a boy who has solved a riddle, “they was new, sir, brand new. And when I gave the summons to Mr. Pinchbeck, he was a-sitting there a-grumbling about the price of coals, and he says, says he, ‘I’d have the law on the thieving rascal,’ he says, ‘but it’s a poor look-out for a man of business when his own clerks are a-wearing boots as cost a guinea and a half, and him a-paying of ‘em but fifteen shillings a week.’ And then he stopped, sir, as sudden as if he’d been shot, and looked at Mr. Pottle, and Mr. Pottle looked at him, and I looked at the boots, and the boots looked at me, sir, and—and—that’s all, sir.”
