The air in the Great Hall of the Institut Sainte-Cécile was thick with the scent of beeswax and the damp wool of winter coats. It was a suffocating atmosphere, not from heat, for the November wind howled against the high, narrow windows, but from the weight of expectation that pressed down upon us like a physical burden. I sat in the front row, my hands clasped tightly in my lap, my knuckles white. Around me, the parents of the pupils sat in stiff, silent rows, their faces arranged in masks of polite interest or arrogant boredom. At the head of the room, elevated on a small dais, sat Madame de Vaux, the Director, resplendent in black silk that seemed to absorb the dim light of the gas lamps. Beside her, the local clergy and several dignitaries of the city leaned forward, their breath misting in the cold.

I had waited for this moment with a dread that had grown sharper with each passing week. It was not the fear of failure that haunted me, but the fear of exposure. I, Mrs. Arden, an English governess of no title, no fortune, and no family connections in this alien land, was merely a tool in Madame de Vaux’s hands. I taught the girls their grammar and their literature; I corrected their accents; I prepared their minds. But I was invisible. My existence was tolerated because I was useful, but it was not valued. And yet, in the quiet hours of the night, when the wind rattled the shutters of my small, drafty room above the kitchen, I had written. I had poured my soul, my anger, my suppressed joy, onto the page. I had written a poem, a fierce and dark thing, about a bird caged in iron, beating its wings until they bled, refusing to sing for the hand that held the cage.

When the turn came for the recitation, the headmistress called the name of Elise Vane, a girl of fifteen, pale and trembling. Elise looked at me, her eyes wide with a terror that was not for herself, but for me. She knew what she was to recite. She had been forced to memorize it, forbidden to speak of its origin, forbidden to look at me with the recognition that passed between us in the study when we were alone.

Elise stood. The hall fell silent. She opened her mouth, and her voice, thin at first, gained strength as the words poured out. It was a strange voice, high and clear, cutting through the heavy air. The poem spoke of chains, of fire, of a spirit that would not be tamed. As she recited the lines I had written in my own blood, I felt a surge of pride so violent it brought tears to my eyes. This was my voice, speaking through her. This was my truth, declared in the heart of a house that valued only propriety and silence.

The audience was spellbound. The clergyman leaned forward, his lips moving slightly as he followed the words. The parents exchanged glances, some nodding in approval, others looking bewildered by the intensity of the imagery. When Elise finished, there was a pause, a held breath, before the applause broke out. It was polite, measured, but sincere.

Madame de Vaux rose. She smiled, a thin, practiced smile that did not reach her cold eyes. She walked down the steps, her skirts sweeping the floor, and took Elise’s hand. She kissed the girl’s forehead, a gesture of maternal affection that felt grotesque in its falseness. Then she turned to the audience.

“My dear ladies and gentlemen,” she said, her voice smooth and authoritative, “you have just heard the work of one of our most gifted students, Elise Vane. It is a piece she composed herself, inspired by her studies in English literature. I am proud to say that our school encourages such creativity and independence of thought. It is a testament to the education we provide here, under the guidance of the Institute.”

The lie hung in the air, sharp and jagged. My heart hammered against my ribs like a bird striking against a windowpane. I looked at Elise. The girl was staring at the floor, her face flushed with a mixture of shame and relief. She had done her duty. She had kept her secret.

I looked at Madame de Vaux. She was watching me, her eyes hard and challenging. She knew what I was. She knew who I was. She was daring me. She was testing me. If I spoke, I would be exposed. I would be seen as ungrateful, as a usurper, as a woman who could not contain her ambitions within the boundaries of her station. I would be dismissed. I would be left in this cold city, with no money, no friends, no home. If I remained silent, I would keep my position. I would keep my salary, my room, my small, safe life. But I would be complicit. I would be part of the lie.

The applause continued, a dull, rhythmic thumping that seemed to come from far away. I felt a coldness spread through my body, starting at my feet and rising to my throat. I thought of the night I had written the poem. I thought of the fire in the grate, the flickering shadows on the wall, the feeling of power that came with the act of creation. I thought of the years I had spent in England, in the shadow of my brothers, my sisters, my father, always the outsider, always the observer, always the one who felt more than she was allowed to express. I had come to Belgium to escape that silence, to find a place where my voice might be heard, even if only in the pages of a book.

And now, here I was, in a grand hall, with the whole city watching, and my voice was being stolen.

I stood up.
