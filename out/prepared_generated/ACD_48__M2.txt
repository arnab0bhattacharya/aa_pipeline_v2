The fog lay thick and yellow over the quays of Gravesend, a damp shroud that seemed to swallow the outlines of the steamers and the clanking of the gangplanks alike. It was not the crisp, biting cold of the English winter that I had left behind in the Otago highlands, but a wet, clinging chill that seeped into the bones and sat heavily upon the chest. I stood at the water’s edge, my travel-worn coat buttoned to the chin, my eyes scanning the crowd that had gathered to meet the passengers of the *SS. Aotearoa*. I was looking for no one, for my father’s station was a solitary place in the vast, red-soled emptiness of the South Island, and I had come to London solely to settle the affairs of his estate, a matter of cold ledgers and dry legalities.

It was, therefore, with a distinct sensation of unease that I observed the man who approached me. He did not push through the crowd with the boisterous energy of a relative, nor did he wave a placard. He simply stepped forward, his face a mask of polite, neutral attention, and extended a gloved hand.

"Miss Elara Vane?" he asked. His voice was low, cultured, and utterly devoid of the rough cadence of the provinces. "I am Mr. Thorne. I have been sent to assist you with the transfer of your luggage and to direct you to your lodgings."

I paused, my hand still resting on the handle of my valise. "I am afraid I do not know a Mr. Thorne," I said, my voice steady despite the quickening of my pulse. "I have arranged my own passage."

"Quite so, quite so," he said, with a slight, apologetic smile that did not reach his eyes. "It is a precaution taken by the firm of Blackwood & Associates on behalf of your father’s estate. They deemed it prudent that a young woman traveling alone should not have to navigate the complexities of the city unaided. I am a solicitor of that firm."

I looked at him more closely. He was a tall man, slender and angular, dressed in a suit of dark grey wool that fit him with military precision. His face was pale, with high, aristocratic cheekbones and a mouth set in a firm, unyielding line. There was nothing sinister in his appearance, which was precisely what made it so unsettling. A genuine solicitor, I reasoned, would have been perhaps more ostentatious, or at least more eager to introduce himself with the warmth of a professional. This man possessed a stillness, a watchful intensity that reminded me uncomfortably of a hawk perched on a branch, waiting for the smallest movement of prey.

"Very well, Mr. Thorne," I said, forcing a smile. "I shall accept your assistance, if only to avoid the delay of hailing a cab in this mist."

He nodded once, sharply. "We shall proceed at once, Miss Vane."

The journey to the lodgings in a quiet street off Piccadilly was silent. Mr. Thorne drove in the carriage, his eyes fixed on the receding buildings, while I sat opposite, watching the fog blur the windows. I tried to recall the name of the solicitor my father had mentioned in his last letter. It was a name I could not quite grasp, a faint echo in my memory. But as the days passed, the name "Thorne" began to feel increasingly foreign.

It was on the third day that the first crack appeared in his façade. I had gone to the office of Blackwood & Associates to sign the preliminary documents regarding the inheritance. The office was a room of mahogany and dust, smelling of old paper and beeswax. Mr. Thorne was not there. I was met by a junior clerk, a nervous young man with ink-stained fingers, who handed me a bundle of papers.

"Mr. Thorne was called away on urgent business," the clerk said, glancing at the door as if expecting someone to enter. "He left these for you. He asked that you sign them immediately and return them to him at his private residence, not here. He prefers to conduct the more sensitive negotiations in private."

I took the papers, my heart giving a strange, heavy thump. My father had been a man of simple habits and straightforward dealings. He had no "sensitive negotiations." He had no need for a solicitor to conduct business in a "private residence." I looked at the signature line. It was not for the transfer of the estate, but for a power of attorney, granting full control over my funds to a third party.

That night, I did not go to the lodgings. Instead, I walked to a small, unassuming office in Baker Street that I had found in a directory of private investigators. I had read of such men in the newspapers, those who dealt with the darker corners of society where the law was slow to tread. I hired a detective named Mr. Halloway, a quiet, observant man with eyes that seemed to see through the surface of things.

I told him everything. The fog at Gravesend, the man who claimed to be a solicitor, the papers, the private residence. Mr. Halloway listened without interrupting, his fingers steepled beneath his chin.

"Mr. Thorne," he repeated, the name rolling off his tongue with a skeptical weight. "And the firm, Blackwood & Associates?"

"Yes."

"I will look into it," he said. "But I suspect you are dealing with a man of considerable sophistication. He is not a common thief, Miss Vane. He is a predator who enjoys the hunt."

I wrote to my brother in New Zealand that very evening, sealing the letter with my father’s crest. I told him of my arrival, of the strange man, and of my decision to consult a detective. I told him that I feared the inheritance was a trap, a lure to draw me into a web from which I might not escape. I told him that I did not know who this Mr. Thorne was, but I knew he was not a friend.
