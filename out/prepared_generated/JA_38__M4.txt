The weather being fine, and the morning room of Ashfield Place insupportably warm with the fuss of her aunt’s arrangements for the Michaelmas dinner, Miss Harriet Ellwood had proposed a walk to the village, and had the satisfaction of being refused by everybody. It was what she had hoped. She set out alone, therefore, with a heart fortified by purpose, and took the path through the beech wood at a pace that permitted reflection.

Her reflections were of Mr. Charles Penderton, and they were not comfortable. Mr. Penderton was nine-and-thirty, a widower of ample property and unremarkable understanding, who lived at Ashfield with his two motherless daughters and a governess; and it had lately become plain to Harriet — plain, that is, to a degree that would not suffer contradiction — that the governess in question, a Miss Ward, had formed designs upon him. The evidence was of that delicate kind which convinces a mind already inclined to conviction: a readiness to hand him his tea before he asked; a manner of listening to his opinions on drainage with an appearance of interest; and, most damning of all, a new ribbon in her cap upon the very Sunday that he had happened to observe, in Harriet’s hearing, that he liked a woman to look neat about the head. Harriet had watched her ever since with the vigilance of an affectionate cousin, for Mr. Penderton was her cousin, and the girls were her god-daughters, and it was not to be borne that a person of no connexion and no fortune should insinuate herself into a family which had never done her the least harm.

The path through the wood was long, and her meditations kept pace with it. She rehearsed what she might say to him. She would not speak to Miss Ward directly — that would be to descend to a level beneath her — but she would speak to Charles, gently, as a cousin might, and open his eyes to what was going forward under his own roof. He was not a man of quick perception; he would require leading; but a woman of sense might lead him. She had reached the village, and the shop, and was standing at the counter in the agreeable confusion of choosing between two colours of silk, when the door opened and Miss Ward herself came in.

Nothing could have been more inconvenient. Harriet inclined her head with the coolness of a woman who has just decided against a purchase, and Miss Ward, with a smile of the most provoking ease, came forward to speak to her.

“You are come for the silk, I collect,” said Miss Ward. “I am come for the girls’ music-books. They have destroyed the last, between them, in a manner that would make you laugh.”

“I do not know that I should have laughed,” said Harriet.

“No — perhaps not. You are very good to take them seriously. It is a great thing for them, to have a cousin who takes them seriously.”

There was something in the tone — a warmth, an insinuation of intimacy — which Harriet could only interpret as the most refined impertinence. She paid for her silk and went out, and Miss Ward walked a little way with her, because their road was the same, and there was no polite means of preventing it.

“You are a great walker,” observed Miss Ward.

“I am fond of my own thoughts,” said Harriet, with meaning.

“So am I. But I find that one’s own thoughts are a poor exchange for a little company, when the evenings grow long. Mr. Penderton has been so good as to say that he does not know how the house got on before I came to it.”

Harriet’s heart hardened. “My cousin is very obliging. He says as much of everybody who is of use to him.”

“He is very obliging,” said Miss Ward, in a voice that seemed to Harriet to be laden with complacency, “and very steady. There are not many men of his years who know their own mind so well.”

They parted at the gate, and Harriet went in to dress for dinner with the consciousness of a duty before her and the determination to perform it. The opportunity came that very evening. The girls were gone to bed, Miss Ward was at the instrument in the little parlour, and Mr. Penderton, having drawn his chair to the fire in the library, was pleased to observe that the evening was closing in early.

“It is the season for it,” said Harriet.

“It is. And yet I am glad of it. There is a comfort in a fire and a shut door that a man does not value at your age.”

“You speak as if you were an old man, Charles.”

“I am old enough to know what I want,” said he, “which is more than most young men can say.”

Harriet drew a breath. “And do you know what you want, in — in the matter of the household? Forgive me, but I am your cousin, and the girls’ godmother, and I cannot see what passes without — without a wish to be of use.”

Mr. Penderton looked at her with an attention that was almost alarming. “You are very good,” said he. “I have wished for some time to speak to you on that very head.”

“Then let me speak first,” said Harriet, “for I have thought of little else. Charles, you must not think me unkind. Miss Ward is — she is very assiduous, and the girls are fond of her, and I would not have her turned away without a character. But you must see — you cannot but see — that her assiduities have a purpose. She is a woman without fortune, and she is not without ambition, and a man in your position cannot be too careful. I have watched her. I have seen how she looks at you when you speak of the drainage; how she is always at hand with the tea; how she has put on a ribbon because you once said —”
