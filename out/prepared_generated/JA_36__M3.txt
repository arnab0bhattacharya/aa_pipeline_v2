That a young woman of four-and-twenty, possessing neither fortune nor consequence, should accept the hand of a man of five-and-forty, possessed of both, could hardly excite surprise in any rational society; and yet, in the narrow circle of Bartonleigh, the engagement of Clara Mortimer to Mr. Evelyn had been productive of very nearly as much discourse as the arrival of the post-coach itself, and with considerably more variation of opinion.

Mr. Evelyn of Evelyn Park was a man of settled character, unblemished reputation, and an estate yielding six thousand a year. His person was tolerable, his address agreeable if not animated, and his understanding such as to ensure domestic comfort, even if it could not promise intellectual rapture. He had been a bachelor for the whole of his life, not from any declared aversion to the married state, but rather from a habitual caution which, having once secured his fortune, saw no urgent reason to alter its arrangements. That he should, at length, have fixed upon Clara Mortimer—the daughter of a deceased country clergyman, living in a state of elegant dependency upon his widowed mother at the Grange—was attributed by some to compassion, by others to caprice, and by Mrs. Mortimer herself, with a satisfaction as serene as it was unexamined, to the singular merit of her daughter's character.

Clara had not been blind to the advantages of the match, nor was she so wholly deficient in self-knowledge as to imagine herself violently in love. She had read enough of poetry, and felt enough of the natural enthusiasm of a lively mind, to know that her heart had once pictured something more—some union of equal years and kindred tastes, some attachment founded in mutual discovery rather than settled esteem. But five seasons of diminishing hopes, a mother whose anxieties increased with each twelvemonth, and the quiet erosion of that confidence which youth wears so carelessly and age so soon misses, had done their work upon her resolution. Mr. Evelyn was kind; he looked at her with a regard she could not distrust, and when he spoke of the future, it was with a steadiness that her own life had hitherto lacked. She accepted him with a composure which her mother called fortitude and which Clara herself, in less guarded moments, was tempted to call surrender.

The wedding was fixed for the latter end of October, and the fortnight preceding it was to be passed at Evelyn Park, by the particular invitation of Mr. Evelyn, who desired that his future wife might become acquainted with the domestic arrangements of the house over which she was shortly to preside. Clara went with a willing mind and an unwilling heart—a distinction too fine for any of her acquaintance to draw, and which she herself was at some pains not to examine too closely.

She was received with every mark of attention. The housekeeper, Mrs. Poole, conducted her through rooms which were handsome without being splendid, and through gardens which were trim without being enchanting. Everything spoke of order, propriety, and that settled moderation which, while it invites no criticism, offers little transport. Clara surveyed it all with a feeling she was careful to conceal—a faint, persistent melancholy, not amounting to regret, but resembling it as a sister might resemble a brother whom one acknowledges without enthusiasm.

On the evening of the third day, Mr. Evelyn informed her, with his usual calm, that his nephew, Captain Heywood, was expected at the Park on the morrow. "A very fine young man," said Mr. Evelyn, "and one whose opinion I value. He has been some years with his regiment in Spain, and I have not seen him since he was a boy of seventeen. I hope you will make him welcome, Clara. He is, next to myself, the nearest connexion you will have in the world."

Clara received this intelligence with a proper expression of pleasure, and retired to her room that night with a mind no more disturbed than the occasion warranted. She had heard of Captain Heywood before—Mr. Evelyn spoke of him with a warmth he bestowed upon few subjects—but she had formed no distinct image of his character, and was wholly unprepared for the impression his actual presence was destined to make.

He arrived in the forenoon, and Clara, who was walking in the shrubbery with Mrs. Mortimer, encountered him as he crossed the lawn towards the house. He was tall, with a countenance open, animated, and agreeably marked by that air of self-possession which active service and foreign climes bestow more readily than any English education. He bowed to them with an ease that spoke at once of good breeding and genuine warmth, and when Mrs. Mortimer, after a few moments of civil enquiry, contrived to leave them together, Clara found herself in conversation with a man whose mind appeared to have been formed by observation rather than by books, and whose remarks, though never elaborate, were touched with a perception she had seldom encountered and never expected to meet at Evelyn Park.

They spoke of the country, of his travels, of the improvements Mr. Evelyn contemplated in the lower grounds; and Clara, who had been prepared to exert herself in the cause of civility, found that the exertion consisted not in sustaining the discourse, but in moderating the pleasure she derived from it. Captain Heywood listened to her remarks with an attention that was itself a compliment, and replied with a candour that assumed her understanding without flattering it. She was sensible, as the conversation proceeded, of a growing ease which she knew to be dangerous, and of an intellectual sympathy which she recognised, with something very like alarm, as the very thing she had once hoped for and long since ceased to expect.
