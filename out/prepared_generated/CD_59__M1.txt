If ever there was a young man who was born to be a clerk, that young man was Tobias Mump. Not a partner, mind you, nor a managing clerk with a mahogany desk and a box of cigars, but a junior clerk—the sort who is measured by the yard of his copying, the sharpness of his nib, and the capacity of his coat-pockets for holding bundles tied with red tape. He was a pale young man of nineteen, with hair that stood up in the most inquisitive manner, and a nose that seemed perpetually to be sniffing the air for the smell of stationery.

He was employed at the offices of Messrs. Fogg, Fumble, and Fudge, of Staple Inn, Holborn; a firm so ancient and so dusty that the spiders in the corners of the ceiling were said to have grown grey in the service, and the very ink in the wells seemed to have dried up out of sheer despair. It was a place of shadows, of high stools, and of the eternal scratching of pens, where the sunlight struggled feebly through the window-panes, and the only sound, save for the coughing of the head clerk, was the monotonous rustle of parchment.

One murky November afternoon, when the fog had rolled into London like a thick grey blanket, and the gas-lamps were flaring like angry eyes in the gloom, Mr. Fogg—a man with a face like a dried apple and a voice like the rustling of dry leaves—summoned Tobias to his private den.

‘Mump,’ said Mr. Fogg, fixing him with a look that was half-compassion and half-calculation, ‘I have a commission for you. A commission of delicacy, Mump. Of the utmost secrecy. You are to take this packet to Epping Forest—nay, to the very edge of it, to a house called The Cedars. It is for the hands of Lady Brambleton. No one else, Mump. Do you understand? No one else.’

He placed upon the desk a small, square parcel, wrapped in brown paper and sealed with a wafer of red wax. It was not heavy; it was not large; it was, to all appearance, as innocuous as a sandwich. But Mr. Fogg looked at it as though it contained the crown jewels, or perhaps the secret of the philosopher’s stone.

‘And mind this, Mump,’ added Mr. Fogg, dropping his voice to a whisper that was more terrifying than a shout. ‘You are to wait for an answer. An answer, Mump. Do not return without it.’

Tobias took the packet with trembling fingers. It felt, somehow, alive. He placed it in the breast-pocket of his coat, where it sat like a second heart, beating against his ribs all the way to the Shoreditch station.

The journey to Epping was an event in itself. The railway train, that great iron dragon, puffed and snorted through the suburbs, shedding soot upon the passengers with a liberality that was truly democratic. Tobias sat in a third-class carriage, wedged between a basket of onions and a stout farmer who smelled of hay and spirits. He clutched his pocket, and the onions smelled, and the farmer snored, and the train rattled on, until at last they came to a station where the air was green and damp, and the trees stood tall and silent, like sentinels of the forest.

The Cedars was a house of imposing grandeur, set back from the road behind a high iron railing and a drive that curved like a serpent. It was a house that seemed to frown upon the world, with its stone lions and its dark windows. Tobias, feeling very small and very shabby, pulled the bell-chain, which clanged with a sound of doom.

The door was opened by a footman in livery—a giant of a man with calves like hams and a face that expressed a profound and crushing boredom. He looked at Tobias as though he were a beetle that had crawled in from the rain.

‘Mr. Fogg’s office,’ said Tobias, holding out the packet. ‘For Lady Brambleton.’

The footman did not speak. He merely extended a hand, took the packet, and was about to close the door with a bang that would have ended the interview, when a voice rang out from the hall.

‘Who is it, Jenkins?’

‘A clerk, my lady,’ said the footman. ‘With a parcel.’

There was a rustle of silk, and a lady appeared. She was tall and stately, with a face that might have been beautiful once, but was now set in lines of severe resolution. She wore a dress of black velvet, and a necklace of jet that clicked as she moved. She looked at Tobias with eyes that were like two dark pools, and he felt as though he were being weighed in the balance and found wanting.

‘Give it to me,’ she said.

The footman handed her the packet. Tobias waited. He stood on the cold stone step, shifting from one foot to the other, while the wind whistled around the corner of the house. Lady Brambleton looked at the packet. She turned it over. She looked at the seal. But she did not open it.

‘Tell Mr. Fogg,’ she said, in a voice that was low and clear, ‘that I have received it. And tell him to send you again. Next week. Same time. Good day.’

And with that, she turned, and swept back into the shadows of the house, leaving Tobias standing alone with the footman, who closed the door with a decisive click.

Tobias walked back to the station in a state of bewilderment. He had delivered the packet, it was true. But he had no answer. Only an instruction to return. It was a mystery, a puzzle, a riddle wrapped in an enigma and tied with red tape. He felt like a pawn in a game of chess, moved hither and thither by invisible hands.
