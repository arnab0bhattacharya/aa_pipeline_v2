The water at Stockwith Lock was the colour of weak tea and moved with the slow, deliberate patience of a creature that had nowhere to be. Tom stood on the stern of the *River Queen*, his hand resting on the tiller, feeling the gentle shudder of the engine through the oak. The sun was a pale coin in a hazy sky, its light glinting off the wet brick of the lock walls and the dark, still water of the cut beyond. He was twenty now, with a man’s breadth in his shoulders and a calmness in his eyes that had not been there ten years ago, when he was a starved, soot-choked boy running barefoot through the gaslit streets of Wolverhampton.

He had been Tom, the sweep’s boy then, a creature of darkness and dust who knew the world by the feel of brickwork against his knees and the taste of grime at the back of his throat. His master, a man named Grimes with hands like shovels and a heart like flint, had seen him not as a child but as a tool, small enough to be sent up the narrowest flues. Tom had run away on a night of freezing rain, the soot clinging to him like a second skin, and had collapsed by the towpath of the Birmingham and Fazeley Canal. It was there that the *River Queen* had found him.

The barge-master, Elias Croft, had pulled him from the mud, his face a roadmap of wrinkles and his voice a low rumble. His wife, Martha, had wrapped him in a coarse wool blanket and fed him hot broth. Their daughter, Elara, had simply watched him with wide, curious eyes. She was twelve then, with hair the colour of straw and a mind as quick and clear as the water in a well. She was the one who had washed the last of the chimney soot from his hair, her small hands gentle where others had been rough. She was the one who had given him his first real name, for he had only ever been ‘boy’.

“Every person is a story, Tom,” she had told him once, pointing to the words in a worn copy of *Robinson Crusoe*. “And every story is made of words. It’s a shame not to know your own.”

So she had taught him, tracing letters in the dust on the cabin table, her patient voice shaping the sounds for him. ‘A’ for Anchor, ‘B’ for Barge, ‘W’ for Water. From the letters came words, from the words came stories, and from the stories came a world he never knew existed. He learned to read the names painted on the other boats, the notices posted at the locks, the headlines of the newspapers that sometimes reached them from the towns. He grew strong on the canal, his muscles hardening as he worked the locks and towed the lines. The canals were his home, their slow, steady rhythm the only one he had ever known. He was a waterman now, part of the Croft family, the son Elias never had.

The lock gate groaned, a deep, metallic protest, and the *River Queen* began its slow ascent. Tom watched the water level rise, his hand steady on the tiller. On the towpath, a group of men waited. They were tall, lean figures, dressed in black, their faces pale and grim. Chimney sweeps. Tom felt a familiar, unwelcome shiver trace its way down his spine. It was a sensation he hadn't felt in years, the memory of cold brick and the suffocating dark.

One of them, a boy of perhaps fifteen, was looking at him with an unnerving intensity. The boy was tall and wiry, his face smudged with a permanent film of soot. But it was not the boy who held Tom’s gaze. It was the man beside him, the one holding the long brushes and the coil of rope. The man’s face was older, harder, but the eyes were the same. Small, shrewd, and missing the warmth of humanity.

The man stared as the barge rose level with the towpath. His gaze moved from Tom’s face, to his hands on the tiller, to the Croft family name painted in gold letters on the cabin. A slow, unpleasant smile spread across his lips, cracking the grime on his cheeks.

“Well, now,” the man said, his voice a rasp of gravel and old smoke. “Look what the water’s washed up. If it ain’t little Tom.”

Tom’s blood ran cold. He kept his face impassive, a trick he had learned on the canals where a poker face could win you a better price for a cargo of coal. But his heart was hammering against his ribs. It was Jem. Grimes’s other apprentice, the one who had been a year older and twice as cruel, the one who had held him down while Grimes had beaten him for dropping a brush.

“I don’t know you, friend,” Tom said, his voice even.

Jem’s smile widened. “Don’t you? I know you. I know the feel of your head when I’m holding it in the soot-bag. I know the sound you make when you’re scared. You’re Grimes’s Tom. Ran off, you did. He’ll be right pleased to hear I’ve found you.”

Tom’s knuckles were white on the tiller. He could feel Elara’s eyes on him from the cabin door. He had never told them the full story, only that he had run away from a hard master. He had never wanted to poison their world with the ugliness of his own.

“I’m Tom Croft,” he said, the name a shield. “And you’re mistaken.”

“Am I?” Jem took a step closer to the edge of the lock. “You’ve filled out, I’ll give you that. Living soft on the water. But that scar on your chin… from when you slipped on the roof in Coventry. That’s still there.”
