The wind off the moors cut through the thin walls of the rectory like a blade, whistling through the chinks in the stone with a sound that reminded me of the groans of the dying. I sat by the cold hearth, watching the last embers of the peat fire crumble into ash, and felt the familiar, dull ache of poverty settle into my bones. It was a physical weight, this want; it pressed upon my chest, making every breath a labor, and it sharpened my senses to a point of unbearable acuity. I was twenty years old, and I had already seen more of the world’s harsh indifference than most women see in a lifetime of pampered ease.

It was in this state of frigid contemplation that the carriage arrived. I heard the crunch of gravel on the drive before I saw the black box of a coach, its wheels splashing through the slush of the November night. The door opened, and Lady Elspeth Hartley stepped out. She was wrapped in furs of a brightness that seemed almost obscene against the grey gloom of the district, her face pale and severe, her eyes bright with the fever of anxiety.

I had known Elspeth for three months, a period that had seen our friendship forged in the unlikely crucible of our shared care for Mr. Arthur Thorne. Thorne was a manufacturer, a man of iron will and iron lungs, who had fallen from his carriage and broken his hip, leaving his foundry in the hands of his incompetent nephew. The fever that followed the injury was not of the body, but of the spirit; he raged against his helplessness, cursing the rain that leaked into his workshop, and weeping over the ledgers that showed his ruin.

Elspeth and I were alike in our devotion to him, though our motives were of different natures. Mine was born of pity and the desire for duty; hers, I knew, was born of a deeper, more dangerous curiosity. She had come to the West Riding to escape the suffocating parlors of London, seeking a landscape as rugged as her own temper. She had found in Thorne a man who spoke plainly, who did not flatter her wit, and who, in his moments of lucidity, treated her not as a lady, but as a fellow creature capable of understanding the mechanics of steel and the logic of debt.

She crossed the room and took my hand, her grip tight and cold. "He is worse," she said, her voice low and trembling. "The fever has broken, but the delirium has set in. He spoke of you, Margot. He said you were the only one who had not tried to sell him."

I pulled my hand away, a sudden spike of anger rising in my throat. "I have never sought to profit from his misery, Elspeth. I am his niece’s friend, nothing more."

"Friend?" she repeated, the word sounding bitter in her mouth. "Is that all? You sit by his bedside and read to him from the Psalms, and you do it with a dignity that makes me feel like a beggar. You have no need of his gratitude, Margot. I do. I must. I have staked my reputation, and perhaps my future, on this man."

I looked at her, really looked at her, for the first time in weeks. Beneath the furs and the finery, I saw the tension in her jaw, the dark circles under her eyes. She was exhausted, and she was afraid.

"Your future?" I asked, my voice quiet but steady. "You speak of stakes, Elspeth. Do you know what is at stake for him? His life? No. His soul? Perhaps. But primarily, his livelihood. His freedom. He is a man who built his name from nothing, and he will not endure to see it crumble in his hands. If he loses the foundry, he will not survive the humiliation."

Elspeth sat down, her movements stiff. "I know. I know it. And I have the money to save it. A thousand pounds, Margot. Enough to clear the debts, to pay the workers, to keep the furnaces burning until he recovers."

The silence that followed was heavy, broken only by the hiss of the wind outside. I felt a cold sensation spread through my chest, not of fear, but of a profound, aching clarity. Here was the test. Here was the moment where pride, and poverty, and love would collide.

"Then give it to him," I said. "Why have you not?"

She looked up, her eyes flashing. "Because I cannot do it without him knowing that I am the only one who can. And he will not accept it. He is too proud. He would sooner starve than take a penny from a woman he does not love. And if I give it to you, Margot, so that you may present it to him as a charitable act from a niece, he will accept it. Because you are of his blood, of his class. He will see it as a duty, not a debt. He will not feel his pride wounded."

I stared at her, feeling the injustice of it burn in my throat. It was a clever scheme, a maneuver of the mind. But it was a lie. And I, who had spent my life in the shadow of others' expectations, who had learned to hide my own worth behind the guise of servitude, could not bear to be the instrument of such deception.

"You ask me to betray him," I said, my voice rising. "To hide your hand so that he may save himself. You ask me to be a mask for your charity. Do you think I am so weak? Do you think I do not know what it is to be poor, to be dependent, to be seen as a tool? I will not be a tool, Elspeth. Not for him, and not for you."
