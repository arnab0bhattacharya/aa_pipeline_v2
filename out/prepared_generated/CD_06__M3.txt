If any wandering chronicler of the humours and miseries of this great metropolis should find himself upon the Camden Town thoroughfare of a drizzling November evening, he would scarcely fail to notice Mrs. Bumblepore’s Establishment for the Refined Boarding of Young Ladies and Elderly Gentlemen of Quiet Habits. He would know it by the everlasting green curtains, which had faded into a sickly jaundice, and by the singular brass knocker, which, being somewhat loose, bore a stronger resemblance to a convulsive fit than a polite announcement. Within this establishment, where the carpets were worn threadbare in exact correspondence with the anxieties of its inhabitants, and the plaster fell in gentle, melancholy showers from the ceiling, moved the person of Miss Prudence Pidgion.

Prudence was an orphan, a foundling, a mere human peg upon which Mrs. Bumblepore thought it highly convenient to hang the coarser duties of her establishment. She was a drudge, a scavenger, a universal joint upon which the whole domestic machinery turned and screeched. To say she was employed by Mrs. Bumblepore would be a gross libel on the word employment; she was merely permitted to exist, provided she carried up the hot water, carried down the slops, blacked the boots, and bore, with a submissive eye, the multifarious scoldings of the establishment. Mrs. Bumblepore was a lady of such profound and uninterrupted irritability that she could find a grievance in a sunbeam and a personal insult in a misplaced teaspoon; her voice, a high, thin, strident instrument, was in perpetual motion, like the restless ticking of a clock that has lost its mainspring and wishes to take its revenge upon humanity.

It was into this damp, low-ceilinged kitchen, smelling powerfully of boiled cabbage and mildewed plaster, that Miss Petunia Mucksteridge suddenly descended, like a eccentric comet falling from the sky. Miss Mucksteridge was a spinster of vast and unaccountable wealth, possessed of a towering false front of hair, a reticule of immense proportions, and a will as iron and as easily rusted as a damp garden gate. She had come to board for a fortnight, to be near her doctor, and in that fortnight she had observed Prudence. She observed the child carry a heavy coal-scuttle up three flights of stairs, observed her meek, pale face, and observed the extreme ferocity with which Mrs. Bumblepore berated her for dropping a single, harmless lump of coal upon the staircase.

‘Pah!’ had cried Miss Mucksteridge, bouncing up from her chair with an energy that threatened the very foundations of the house. ‘The girl has genius in her bones, ma’am! She has suffered, and suffering is the very best seasoning for the mind! I will adopt her. I will make her my heir. I will wash the soot from her brow and place a piano beneath her fingers!’

And so, upon a whim as sudden and as violent as a November squall, Miss Mucksteridge carried Prudence away from the green curtains and the cabbage water, bundling her into a hackney-coach with a toothcomb, a French grammar, and a pair of kid boots that pinched her toes abominably.

For four glorious months, Prudence Pidgion lived in a delirium. Miss Mucksteridge’s mansion in Bloomsbury was a palace of ottomans, stuffed birds under glass domes, and bewildering musical instruments. Prudence was provided with a governess who spoke only in the passive voice, a French master who swallowed his consonants, and a dancing master who bowed as if his spine were made of India rubber. She was dressed in silks and muslins; she was taught to glide rather than walk; she was instructed in the mysterious art of holding a teacup with the little finger elevated to a degree that threatened to dislocate the knuckle. Miss Mucksteridge called her ‘my dear, precocious pearl,’ and would gaze at her with an air of immense self-satisfaction, as one might gaze at a particularly clever poodle one has taught to stand upon its hind legs.

But comets, as I have intimated, are erratic bodies; and Miss Mucksteridge’s nature was of that singularly restless sort that finds its highest joy in the act of acquisition, and its swiftest boredom in the fact of possession. The novelty of washing the soot from Prudence’s brow faded. The piano proved a tedious instrument, requiring an application of the fingers that Miss Mucksteridge could not herself endure. The passive voice of the governess began to grate upon her ear.

‘The girl is growing dull, ma’am!’ Miss Mucksteridge declared one morning, glaring at Prudence, who was attempting to read a improving book on moral philosophy. ‘She is positively growing dull! There is no spirit in her! I thought she had genius, but she only has manners. I cannot bear a girl who has nothing but manners!’

And so, upon a whim as sudden and as violent as the first, Miss Mucksteridge sent Prudence back. She did not send her entirely empty-handed—she furnished her with a new wardrobe, a polite letter of recommendation, and a profound sense of bewilderment—but she sent her back, nonetheless, to Mrs. Bumblepore’s Establishment for the Refined Boarding of Young Ladies and Elderly Gentlemen of Quiet Habits.

Prudence descended the hackney-coach in Camden Town, arrayed in a fine merino cloak, her boots no longer pinching but exquisitely suited to her feet, and walked up the steps to the green curtains. She entered the parlour. Mrs. Bumblepore was there, her face wearing an expression of sour astonishment, as if she had just bitten into a lemon that had previously promised to be a sweet orange.

‘So,’ said Mrs. Bumblepore, in her thin, strident voice, ‘the fine lady has condescended to return to us! The heiress! The pearl! I suppose you will not condescend to black the boots now, Miss Pidgion? I suppose you will require a velvet cushion to sit upon while you peel the potatoes?’

‘I am very willing to work, ma’am,’ said Prudence, in a voice that was no longer the meek, frightened voice of the drudge, but a voice trained to modulate, to pause, to breathe. ‘I am very willing to do whatever is required of me, Mrs. Bumblepore.’
