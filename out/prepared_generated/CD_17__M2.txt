The fog in Lincoln’s Inn Fields did not merely obscure the view; it seemed to possess a malevolent agency, a thick, grey, and insubstantial curtain that the wind dragged across the cobblestones with a sound like the shuffling of old papers. It was a damp, penetrating chill, the kind that settled into the bones of a man and refused to leave, much like the memory of a particular injustice. Through this murky veil, Mr. Arthur Pymble, a man of forty-five years whose waistcoat buttons strained against a belly that had grown round in proportion to his caution, walked with the heavy, deliberate tread of one who has nowhere particular to go but must, by the laws of propriety and inertia, continue to move.

Mr. Pymble was a creature of habit, a human mechanism of such precise and unvarying operation that it was a marvel of mechanical engineering. For thirty years, he had sat in the same corner of the same office in Chancery Lane, copying the same dull, monotonous legal documents, his quill scratching away at the parchment with a rhythm as steady as a metronome. He had never missed a day. Not in the heat of a July noon when the air hung heavy with the smell of hot iron and dust, nor in the biting frost of January when the ink in the pots turned to sludge. He had been a pillar of the firm, a silent, ink-stained sentinel, whose only crime was an inability to be anything other than what he was. And yet, it was this very fidelity, this unshakeable reliability, that had rendered him obsolete in the eyes of his superiors.

The dismissal had been delivered not with a shout, but with a whisper, a cruel, bureaucratic softness that Mr. Pymble felt had cut deeper than any blunt instrument. "It is the new economy, Mr. Pymble," the partner had said, adjusting his spectacles with an air of mild regret that was wholly insincere. "Young Mr. Bumble is a faster hand. A lighter touch. We must look to the future." The future, Mr. Pymble reflected as he turned the corner into the darker, more congested streets of Pentonville, was a young man who could not spell *indemnification* and who whistled while he worked. The thought was a small, sharp pain, a splinter in the mind that would not work itself out.

He walked on, the city pressing in around him. The houses here in Pentonville were tall and narrow, leaning over one another like conspirators, their windows dark and eyesless. The streets were narrow, choked with the smell of coal smoke, wet rot, and the sharp, metallic tang of the river Thames nearby. It was a place of shadows and secrets, where the fog seemed to cling to the walls and the gutters ran with a black, oily sludge. Mr. Pymble felt a profound sense of isolation, a loneliness that was not merely the absence of company, but the absence of purpose. He was a man whose entire identity had been constructed out of duty, and now that duty had been revoked, he was left with nothing but the hollow echo of his own footsteps.

His destination was a small, damp lodging in a narrow court off Pentonville Road, where his sister, Mrs. Clara Pymble, kept a modest household. Clara was a woman of sharp features and sharper tongue, a creature of angular bones and pricked ears, who had spent her life managing the household accounts with a severity that bordered on the martial. She was not a woman given to sentiment, nor to comfort. To her, Arthur was a responsibility, a liability that had been taken on in a fit of sisterly duty and had never quite been forgiven.

As he ascended the steep, winding stairs, his heart began to hammer against his ribs with a frantic, irregular beat. He paused on the landing, breathing heavily, the air thick with the scent of boiled cabbage and stale tobacco. He had rehearsed the words a dozen times in his mind, but they had lost their shape, becoming a jumble of syllables that refused to form a coherent sentence. How could he tell Clara? How could he explain that the man who had been the backbone of the firm, the man who had never once been late, had been thrown out like a broken tool? It was not just a loss of income; it was a loss of self. It was the recognition that his thirty years of service had amounted to nothing, that he was, in the eyes of the world, a superfluous thing.

He reached the top of the stairs and stood before the door. It was a plain, wooden door, painted a faded brown, with a brass knocker that had been polished by years of use. He raised his hand to knock, but his arm hung limp by his side. He could hear the sound of a kettle boiling inside, the sharp whistle of steam, and the clatter of plates. Clara was making tea. She would be expecting him. She would ask him how the day had been, and he would lie, as he always did, saying that it had been a quiet day, a good day. But he could not lie anymore. The lie had been broken by the truth, and the truth was a cold, hard stone that sat in his stomach, weighing him down.

He knocked at last, a timid, hesitant tap that sounded like a mouse scratching at the wood. The door opened, and Clara stood there, her face framed by a white cap, her eyes narrow and suspicious. She looked him up and down, her gaze lingering on his rumpled coat and his pale, drawn face. She saw the change in him, the subtle shift in his posture, the way he held himself as if expecting a blow.

"Well?" she said, her voice dry and crisp. "You look as if you’ve seen a ghost. Or perhaps you’ve lost your place."
