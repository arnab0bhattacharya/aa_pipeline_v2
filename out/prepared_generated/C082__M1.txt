The quietude of Oakhaven was a thing of substance, a weight of summer air and buzzing bees that had settled over the village since the last of the May rains. For two years, the courtship of Mr. Alistair Finch and Miss Eleanor Vance had been part of this quietude, as natural and unhurried as the climbing roses on the vicarage wall. Mr. Finch, a gentleman of independent means and a temperament so mild it was almost vaporous, would call three times a week. He would take tea, admire Eleanor’s embroidery, walk with her to the lending library, and depart with a bow so precise it seemed a full stop to their conversation. He never spoke of more.

Her younger sister, Clara, watched this slow-motion ballet of non-commitment with a frustration that was becoming a physical ache. She saw Eleanor’s hope dimming with each passing season, her sister’s quiet acceptance of a half-life, a friendship masquerading as a romance. Eleanor was twenty-two, her bloom at its peak, and Clara, at nineteen, possessed a sharper, more impatient nature. She saw the situation not as a gentle impasse, but as a puzzle to be solved with the right lever. Mr. Finch was the lever; he simply needed to be moved.

Her first gambit was subtle. During one of Mr. Finch’s afternoon calls, as he sat opposite Eleanor in the drawing-room, Clara entered, her brow furrowed in theatrical distress. “Oh, Mr. Finch, you must forgive me,” she began, her voice a little too loud for the tranquil room. “I have just had a letter from my aunt in Bath, and she writes of the most dreadful thing! A young lady of her acquaintance, engaged for nearly three years, has been jilted! Her fiancé, it seems, found a better prospect and simply… vanished.” She let her eyes well with tears, dabbing at them with a handkerchief. “It is a cautionary tale, is it not? To think a gentleman could hold a lady’s affections in such a way, with no promise of his own… it is the most cruel sort of sport.”

Mr. Finch had paled, his teacup rattling faintly in its saucer. He offered a few stammering words of sympathy, his gaze fixed on the floral pattern of the carpet. He did not look at Eleanor. Clara, watching him, felt a surge of triumph. She had planted a seed of fear. But in the days that followed, the seed did not sprout. Mr. Finch’s visits became, if anything, more reserved. He spoke of the weather, of the price of wool, of a new book on Roman aqueducts—anything but the future.

Clara grew bolder. The next week, as the three of them walked along the lane bordering the wheat fields, she fell into step beside him. “I was speaking with Mrs. Gable at the haberdashery,” she said, her tone light and conversational. “She was telling me of the Ashworth girl, you know, the one who was courted by that merchant’s son from Portsmouth. For four years, he dangled after her. And what became of it? Nothing! The merchant went bankrupt and the son was shipped off to the Indies. The poor girl is left on the shelf, a spinster at twenty-five. Mama says it is a tragedy. She says a young woman must have a declaration, a certainty, or she is simply wasting her most precious commodity: her time.” She glanced at him, her eyes wide and innocent. “Do you not agree, sir?”

Alistair Finch had stopped walking. The colour had drained from his face, leaving it the colour of old parchment. He looked from Clara’s earnest expression to Eleanor’s, which was now a mask of pained embarrassment. “I… I believe,” he stammered, “that such matters are of a deeply personal nature and should not be… bandied about as gossip.” He excused himself on the pretence of a sudden headache and walked back towards the village at a pace that was nearly a run.

Eleanor rounded on her sister as soon as he was out of earshot. “Clara, what are you doing? You will frighten him away entirely!”

“He needs frightening!” Clara retorted, her frustration boiling over. “He is a coward, Eleanor! And you allow him to be. He will never speak unless he is forced to.”

But the next week, Mr. Finch did not call on Tuesday. Nor on Thursday. The silence in the house was a suffocating presence. Eleanor moved through the rooms like a ghost, her embroidery lying forgotten in its basket. Clara, seeing the devastation she had wrought, felt a cold knot of guilt tighten in her stomach. Her ingenious methods had not pried open a door; they had slammed it shut.

On the following Sunday, as the family was leaving the church, Clara saw him. He was standing by the lychgate, speaking with the vicar, his expression as bleak as a winter sky. He looked thinner, the gentle lines around his mouth hardened into a grim set. This, she thought with a surge of desperate resolve, was her last chance. She would not be subtle. She would not use veiled stories. She would be a battering ram.

As the congregation filed out, she detached herself from her parents and Eleanor and walked directly towards him. He saw her coming and a flicker of something—fear, perhaps—crossed his face. He nodded to the vicar and turned to leave, but she was already there.

“Mr. Finch,” she said, her voice ringing with a clarity that made several heads turn. “I must speak with you.”

He flinched. “Miss Clara. A fine sermon today, was it not?”

“It was,” she said, planting herself squarely in his path. “But I am more concerned with matters of earth than of heaven. My sister is suffering, sir. And you are the cause.”

Alistair Finch stared at her, his mouth agape. He looked past her to where Eleanor stood, her face a mask of mortified horror.

“I do not understand,” he whispered.
