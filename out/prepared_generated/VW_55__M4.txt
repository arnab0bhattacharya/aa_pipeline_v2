The counter ran its polished length from the glove department to the silks, and behind it Miss Ethel Marsh stood, as she had stood since half-past eight, her hands moving in that small, precise rhythm—fold, smooth, fold again—which had become so much a part of her that she sometimes caught herself performing it in her sleep. The gloves lay in their tissue-paper nests like pale, patient creatures, and the light from the great windows fell upon them in a flood that was not quite daylight and not quite artificial, a thick, suspended brightness that made the dust motes dance and the customers appear, for a moment, as figures moving under water. Ethel watched them come and go. A woman in a sealskin coat, her face pinched with the cold, wanting something in a size six; a girl with a fox fur and a giggle, wanting something in a size five and a half, something that would match the hat, something that would make the young man at the dance look twice. Ethel fetched, and smiled, and folded, and the afternoon wore on, the great clock above the doors ticking out its even measure, and the light thickening, slowly, towards the hour when the lamps would be lit and the windows would turn to mirrors.

She had met him at the dance at the Palais—no, not the Palais, the other one, the one on the corner of Wardour Street with the palms in pots and the band that played too loudly. He had asked her for the foxtrot, and his hand had been warm and a little damp, and he had said—what was it he had said?—something about the floor being slippery, and she had laughed, and he had laughed, and then they had danced again, and again, and he had walked her to the corner of her street and had said, “I shall see you again, then,” not as a question but as a statement, as though it were the most natural thing in the world, and she had said, “Yes,” and had gone up to her room and sat on the edge of the bed with her hat still on, her heart beating in a way that was quite ridiculous, quite out of proportion to anything that had actually occurred. His name was Mr. Gervase Pemberton. She said it over to herself sometimes, in the intervals of folding gloves: Gervase Pemberton, Gervase Pemberton, like a line of poetry that one cannot quite forget.

At lunch she sat in the staff room, which was a small, airless place at the back of the building, with a gas-ring and a table covered in oilcloth and a window that looked out onto a brick wall. The other girls chattered around her—Mabel, who was engaged to a man in the accounts department, and Doris, who was not engaged to anyone but had opinions about everyone who was—and Ethel ate her sandwich and drank her tea and smiled at the appropriate moments, but her mind was elsewhere. It was in the future, that bright, nebulous region where she and Mr. Pemberton walked together along the Embankment, or sat in a picture-house, or—she blushed even to think it—stood before a little house with a garden, a house in one of those quiet streets that she had never actually seen but could imagine perfectly, with lace curtains and a canary in a cage and a brass knocker polished to a high shine. The future was a place she visited often, and it was always the same, and it was always beautiful, and the gas-ring hissed and the tea grew cold and the bell rang for the afternoon’s work, and she rose, and smoothed her skirt, and went back to the counter.

The afternoon customers were the worst. They were tired, and they were cross, and they wanted things that did not exist—a glove that was both black and brown, a glove that would fit a hand that was neither large nor small—and Ethel served them with the same patient smile, the same precise movements, while the light thickened and the clock ticked and the dust motes danced. And then, at last, it was half-past five, and she was putting on her coat in the staff room, and Mabel was saying, “Coming for a cup of tea, then?” and Ethel was saying, “Not tonight, Mabel, I’ve a bit of shopping to do,” which was not true, or not entirely true, for she had nothing to buy but she wanted to walk, she wanted to be out in the air, she wanted to feel the cold on her face and the pavement under her feet and the great, humming life of Oxford Street around her, for the shop had been close and the day had been long and she felt, somehow, that she might meet him—that he might be walking, as she was walking, and that they might come upon each other, as in a story, and he might say, “Miss Marsh! What a piece of luck!” and she might say, “Mr. Pemberton! I was just thinking of you,” and the whole world would arrange itself, obligingly, around this small, miraculous coincidence.

Oxford Street was a river of light and sound. The lamps were lit, and the windows of the shops blazed with a thousand small, bright things—jewels and silks and hats and gloves and all the paraphernalia of desire—and the omnibuses rumbled past, and the taxis hooted, and the people surged and eddied and pressed against each other, a great, anonymous tide of faces and voices and hurrying feet. Ethel walked slowly, her hands in her pockets, her collar turned up against the wind, and she looked at the faces as they passed, not consciously, not with any purpose, but with a vague, hopeful expectancy, as though one of them might, at any moment, resolve itself into the one face she sought. And then she saw him.
