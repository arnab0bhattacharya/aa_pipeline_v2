The gas-jet above the bench flickered with a faint, asthmatic wheeze, throwing a diseased yellow light over the minute entrails of a Swiss lever watch. The dusk of a Clerkenwell November had long since settled upon the district, creeping down the damp, soot-stained bricks of Saffron Street, filtering through the grimy panes of the workshop window. It was a darkness that seemed to exhale the cold of the river and the subtle, penetrating odor of decay—a darkness in which the noise of the city died away to a muffled, purposeless murmur, as if the great heart of the world were beating thickly behind a wall of fog. In this profound obscurity, which the weak artificial glare only served to make more palpable, I sat holding a pair of brass tweezers, the pivot of a broken balance staff resting against my thumb. It was an hour when respectability has retired behind drawn curtains, leaving the streets to the vague, creeping shadows that belong neither to day nor to night, but to a sort of moral twilight where things lost or hidden are apt to stir.

It was then that the door latch clicked with a deliberate caution that carried an immense weight of meaning, and a visitor stepped into the shop. He did not knock, which in itself was a strange thing, for the dwellers of that back street are given to a heavy, resounding summons. He was enveloped in a heavy ulster, the collar turned up to conceal the lower part of his face, and a soft, wide-brimmed hat drawn down over his eyes. He carried with him the dampness of the outer night, and an indefinable suggestion of haste, of a man driven by some secret urgency. His eyes, alone visible in the shadow of the brim, glanced swiftly from the glimmering bench to my face, and rested there with an intensity of scrutiny that was almost painful. He did not greet me. He merely stood for a moment, breathing somewhat heavily, as if he had walked fast and far through the obscurity of hidden ways.

“You are Horrocks, the chronometer maker,” he said. It was not a question, but an assertion, delivered in a low, constrained voice, with a slight foreign accent that I could not at once place—perhaps a trace of the Levant, or of some distant, sun-baked coast where life is held cheaper than in this northern clime.

“I repair watches,” I answered, guardedly, laying down my tweezers. “Timepieces of every description. What can I do for you?”

Without a word, he advanced to the bench, and drawing from the breast of his ulster a small object wrapped in chamois leather, laid it down between us. His fingers, I noticed, were remarkably long, of a sallow pallor, and they trembled slightly as he unfolded the wrapping. The object revealed was not a watch. It was a brass cylinder, perhaps three inches in length, fitted with a tiny, jeweled movement, a mainspring of unusual thickness, and a series of levers ending in a hollow, pointed cap. It was a mechanism of the most delicate and exacting description, and its purpose was so apparent to one conversant with the inner workings of things that I felt a sudden, profound chill settle upon my spirits, as if the dampness of the night had suddenly penetrated to my very bones.

I looked at the device, and then I looked at the man. He had not removed his hat, and his eyes, dark and luminous, watched my face with an unwavering, expectant attention. There was in the whole man an air of forced composure, of a desperate resolution masking an inner terror, which I had seen before in men who have been brought low by the unpredictable strokes of fate. He was a foreigner, without doubt, and he was in trouble—trouble of a dark and dangerous kind, which he did not intend to disclose.

“A delicate piece of work,” I murmured, affecting a professional calm, though my mind was racing. “The escapement is shattered, and the timing lever is bent. It will require new jewels. May I ask where it came from?”

A faint, sardonic smile, which held no mirth, appeared for an instant in the shadow of his collar. “It came from a place where such things are made, my friend,” he replied. “And it must return there. I am told you are the only man in this city who can repair it without asking questions. I require it to be in perfect order by tomorrow evening. The timing must be exact—to the fraction of a second. You understand?”

I understood only too well. The device was a detonator mechanism, of the sort used by certain political factions on the continent—those men who, driven by abstract theories of liberty or vengeance, seek to impose their will upon the world by the sudden, violent dissolution of order. It was a machine designed for murder, for the destruction of human life in the name of some shadowy, implacable ideal. I had heard of such things, of course, in the newspapers, but I had never thought to hold one in my hands, here in my quiet shop in Clerkenwell, surrounded by the honest, ticking progeny of the horological art.

He drew from his pocket a leather purse, and from it counted out five gold sovereigns, which he placed upon the bench beside the mechanism. The gold gleamed with a hard, cold luster in the gaslight, and the sound of the coins striking the wood was singularly clear and distinct in the silence of the shop. It was a large sum, vastly in excess of what the repair would normally cost, and the very excess of it was a kind of menace, a tacit acknowledgment that the transaction was not one of ordinary commerce, but of complicity.
