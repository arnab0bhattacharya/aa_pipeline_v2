It was the custom of my mind, in those first days of desolation, to refuse the cruel evidence of my senses, and to hold fast that which the world declared to be no more. When the bell of our little church tolled for Clara—my Clara, my other self, the being who had been the complement of my existence from the earliest dawn of memory—I stood beside her grave and felt only that a great distance, an impassable gulf, had been opened between us; I could not believe that the light of her spirit had been utterly extinguished. The summer came upon our valley of Ashcombe, and with it a heat that seemed to bake the very air into a shimmering, silent stillness; the river, which we had so often traced to its secret sources among the hills, ran low and sluggish, and the great elms that shaded the manor house drooped their leaves as if in a stupor. It was in this season of oppressive, sun-drenched quiet that I conceived the notion, which to my disordered heart appeared not as a fancy but as a necessity of my being, that I would continue our conversation, which death had so rudely interrupted.

I took up my pen, therefore, and addressed myself to her as if she were still at her own home at Thornwood, merely separated from me by the miles of winding country road, and not by that dreadful barrier which reason, in her cold and unforgiving voice, sometimes whispered to me was eternal. “My dearest Clara,” I wrote, “the silence of the house is become a torment to me. I hear the ticking of the long-case clock in the hall, and each beat falls upon my heart like a drop of lead. Is it so with you? Do you find the hours as tedious in your new abode as I do in this one without you?” Thus I began, and once begun, the torrent of my sorrow and my love could not be stayed. I filled page after page with the minutiae of my days, describing the blooming of the roses in the south garden, the flight of the swallows beneath the eaves, the very clouds that traversed the sky, for I believed that she, in her ethereal state, must have some commerce with these things, and that my words might form a bridge of light across the chasm that separated us.

I would write of our past, too, dwelling with a fond and painful delight upon every shared secret, every childish grief and joy, every walk we had taken by the river’s bank, our hands linked together as if to defy the very notion of separation. “Do you remember,” I would ask her, “how we sat upon the fallen oak in the beech wood, and vowed that no power on earth should ever part us? I hold you to that vow, my Clara; I hold you to it still, and I will not release you from it. Your body, that frail and beautiful tenement which the disease so cruelly ravaged, was but a shell; your spirit, I am convinced, is near me, and these letters are the only language I possess by which to address it.” To write thus was a balm to my wounded soul; for a few hours each day, while the ink flowed and the words took shape upon the paper, she lived again for me, and my grief was transmuted into a sort of rapturous communion. I felt her presence in the scent of the honeysuckle that crept about my window, in the sighing of the wind through the poplars, and I was not mad, but blessed, or so I believed.

This strange and solitary occupation, which I guarded with a jealous secrecy, became the central pillar of my existence. I would rise before the sun had fully dispelled the morning mists from the valley, and write until my fingers were cramped and the light began to fail. My mother, whose own grief was of a more conventional and therefore more manageable kind, watched my increasing abstraction with a growing and fearful anxiety. She saw me wander for hours by the river, my eyes fixed upon the water as if I expected Clara to rise from its depths; she saw me refuse the company of our neighbours, and startle at the sound of a voice when I was deep in my reveries. She could not comprehend that my sorrow was not a passive thing to be endured, but an active passion that required its own strange sustenance.

One evening, when the air was thick with the promise of a thunderstorm and the distant hills were purple with shadow, I left my portfolio of letters upon the little writing-desk in my room, believing myself to be alone in the house. I went out to breathe the heavy air, to feel the first cooling drops of rain upon my face. When I returned, I found my mother standing in my chamber, her face as pale as the moon that now struggled through the clouds. In her hands she held the sheaf of my letters, the sacred transcript of my communion with the dead. She did not speak at first, but looked at me with an expression of such profound pity and terror that it pierced my heart more surely than any words.

“Oh, my child!” she at last exclaimed, her voice trembling and broken. “What is this? What dreadful phantasy has taken possession of your mind? To whom are these letters written? To what abyss of sorrow have you consigned yourself?”

I strode forward to reclaim my property, my own voice rising in a passion of anguish and defence. “They are for Clara!” I cried. “They are for her, for she is not gone! You do not understand. You, and all the world, see only the clay that was left behind, but I feel her spirit. It is here, in this room, in the air we breathe! These letters are not the ravings of a disordered mind; they are the only truth left to me, the only means by which I can speak to the half of my soul that has been torn away!”
