It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a scholar in possession of a good fortune and a large library, is in want of a wife who possesses a good memory and a spare larder. The Reverend Mr. Ashworth, a man of considerable intellect and negligible practicality, had long since surrendered the management of his household to his wife, Mrs. Ashworth, whose judgment was as steady as the clock on the parsonage door, and whose temper was as constant as the weather in Oxfordshire in April. Their only daughter, Eleanor, was a young woman of nineteen, who had inherited her father’s benevolence and her mother’s prudence, though she had not yet acquired the full measure of either.

The trouble began, as such troubles often do, with an act of sudden, unconsulted generosity. Mr. Ashworth, having spent the morning in the depths of a treatise on ecclesiastical history, emerged into the parlor with the flushed, satisfied air of a man who has solved a difficult theorem. He had, during his researches, recalled a former student of his, Mr. Thorne, who was now visiting the county to settle some estate matters.

“Mrs. Ashworth,” he announced, with the gravity of one proclaiming a new doctrine, “I have sent for Mr. Thorne to dine with us this evening. He was a most promising pupil, and I wish to see how he has turned out.”

Mrs. Ashworth, who had been arranging the silver with the precision of a general deploying troops, looked up sharply. “Sent for him? My dear, we have no provisions. The butcher did not come yesterday, and the cook has gone to her sister’s in Abingdon. There is nothing but cold beef and stale bread in the house.”

Mr. Ashworth blinked, as if the concept of hunger were an abstract idea too remote for his consideration. “Nonsense. We are a respectable parsonage. We shall manage. You are always so anxious about trifles. Mr. Thorne is a man of refined taste; he will appreciate a simple, home-dieted repast. It speaks to the sincerity of the host.”

Mrs. Ashworth opened her mouth to object, but the doorbell rang. It was Mr. Thorne, who had arrived with the speed of a man who knows he is expected, or perhaps with the speed of a man who hopes to be fed. He was a tall, thin gentleman of about thirty, with a face that suggested he had been thinking too much and eating too little. He bowed with a stiffness that seemed to derive from his collar, and accepted the invitation to dinner with a smile that did not quite reach his eyes.

Eleanor, who had been listening from the hallway, felt a cold sensation settle in her stomach. The dinner hour was in three hours. The larder was bare. The kitchen was empty. The only food in the house was a loaf of bread, a few hard-boiled eggs from the morning, and a bottle of vinegar.

She looked at her mother, who was staring at the clock with an expression of mild horror. She looked at her father, who was already engaging Mr. Thorne in a discussion on the relative merits of Calvin and Luther, oblivious to the culinary catastrophe unfolding around him.

“Leave it to me,” Eleanor said softly.

Mrs. Ashworth turned to her daughter with a look that mixed gratitude with despair. “Eleanor, my dear, you cannot conjure a goose from a hat.”

“I shall not conjure a goose,” Eleanor replied, her voice steady. “I shall conjure a reputation. But I must be left to my devices. Do not let Father believe I am doing anything unusual. Let him believe it is merely a quiet dinner.”

Eleanor spent the next two hours in a state of high-flying activity that would have impressed a general. She sent the maid, a girl of fourteen named Bess, to the village shop to purchase a large, plain cake and a pot of cream, under the pretense of needing ingredients for a sick child’s diet. She then went to the garden, where she gathered a basket of whatever vegetables could be found, however scrawny.

The key, however, lay in the presentation and the conversation. Eleanor knew that Mr. Thorne, like most men of his station, was more interested in the display of intellectual superiority than in the actual sustenance provided. He was a man who liked to feel that he was being treated with a certain exclusivity.

When the dinner was served, it was a modest affair. A single roast fowl, purchased at the last moment from a neighboring farm for a price that made Eleanor wince, was accompanied by a salad of lettuce and radishes, and the plain cake for dessert. It was not a lavish meal, but it was neat, and the tablecloth was clean.

Mr. Thorne, however, seemed remarkably satisfied. He ate with a deliberation that suggested he was savoring every bite, and he complimented the fowl with a warmth that was slightly excessive.

“Truly, Mrs. Ashworth, this is a delicacy I have not tasted in years,” he said, wiping his mouth with a napkin. “The simplicity of it is most refreshing. One finds that in London, one is often served with such frills and fripperies that one forgets the true flavor of the meat. But here, in the country, one appreciates the honest craft.”

Eleanor, seated at the head of the table opposite her father, watched with a mixture of relief and amusement. Her father, oblivious to the fact that the fowl had been bought twenty minutes before it was carved, was beaming.

“Mr. Thorne,” he said, leaning forward, “you remember how I told you that the best education is that which teaches a man to appreciate the simple things? I see you have not forgotten my lessons.”

“Not for a moment, sir,” Mr. Thorne replied, with a bow that was almost theatrical. “I am but a humble student of your wisdom.”
