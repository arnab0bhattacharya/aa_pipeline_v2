The villa had a name, La Fontanella, which was painted in faded blue upon a gate-post, and the gate itself, warped by many summers, swung open with a cry that was half complaint, half welcome, as though it could not decide whether to admit the world or shut it out. Beyond, the gravel drive curved upward between cypresses that stood like dark sentinels, their shadows lengthening across the path in the late afternoon, and at the end of the drive the house itself—a long, low building the colour of old parchment, with green shutters closed against the heat—seemed to dream upon the hillside, indifferent to the arrival of the English family who had come to occupy it.

Mrs. Ashworth, standing in the hall with her hat still pinned and her gloves not yet removed, regarded the marble floor, the high ceiling, the enormous window at the end of the corridor that framed the lake in a blaze of silver light, and thought—but did not say—that it was too beautiful, too large, too much; that already she felt diminished by it, as though the house had absorbed her and would not give her back. She would write to her sister in Hampshire: *We have arrived safely. The villa is charming. The children are delighted.* And she would not write: *I stood in the hall for a long time this afternoon and could not think why we had come, or what we were to do here, or how many weeks must pass before I might sit again in my own small room with the familiar wallpaper and the view of the garden.*

“Mama,” said Eleanor, appearing in a doorway, her hair loosened by the journey, her face flushed with something that was not merely the heat of the day, “there is a terrace, and beyond the terrace there are steps, and beyond the steps there is a garden, and beyond the garden—” She stopped, breathless, as though the enumeration of so much beauty had exhausted her.

“Yes,” said Mrs. Ashworth. “I dare say.”

“And the lake,” Eleanor went on, “is like—like nothing I have ever seen. It is not blue, Mama. It is silver, and then it is grey, and then, when a cloud passes, it is almost black. And there is a boat, a little boat, with a man in it who sings.”

“You will find,” said Mrs. Ashworth, “that there are many boats with many men who sing. It is Italy.”

But Eleanor had already turned away, drawn back to the terrace, to the light, to the vast shimmering expanse of water that seemed to promise something—she could not have said what—something that England, with its hedgerows and its grey skies and its careful proprieties, had never offered. She was nineteen, and she had read too much poetry, and she believed—though she would not have confessed it—that somewhere in the world there must be a life more vivid, more intense, more *real* than the life she had known; and here, she thought, here it might be.

The Count came on the third day.

He came, as it happened, to inquire whether the English family required anything—a boat, a carriage, a guide to the villages in the hills—for he was, he explained, the nephew of the landlord, and it was his duty, his pleasure, to see that his uncle’s tenants were comfortable. He was tall, and very dark, and his English was fluent but not perfect, so that his sentences had a way of hesitating, of turning aside, of finding unexpected words, which gave to the most ordinary remarks an air of significance, of meaning something more than was said. He stood in the salon, his hat in his hand, and the light from the window fell upon him, and Eleanor, seated at the piano with her fingers on the keys but making no sound, felt her heart contract and then expand, as though it had been struck.

“You play?” he asked.

“A little,” she said. “Not well.”

“I do not believe you,” he said. “I believe you play beautifully, and you are modest, which is an English quality I admire.”

“You do not know me,” she said, and heard, as she spoke, how foolish the words sounded, how prim, how inadequate.

“No,” he agreed. “But I shall.”

And Mrs. Ashworth, writing that evening to her sister, said: *A young Italian called this afternoon—a Count, no less—very civil, very polite, offering to arrange excursions. Eleanor seems quite taken with the place. She has quite lost her London pallor already.* And she did not write: *I watched him look at her, and I saw something in his eyes that frightened me, though I cannot say what. She is so young. She knows nothing. And I—I know too much, and yet I do not know what to do.*

The youngest boy, Thomas, who was twelve and had never in his life been permitted to roam beyond the garden gate, discovered the hills on the fifth day, and thereafter was rarely seen. He would slip away after breakfast, when the servants were busy and his mother was occupied with her letters, and he would climb—up through the terraced gardens, past the little chapel where an old woman kept watch over a faded Madonna, up into the chestnut woods where the light came green and filtered through the leaves, up and up until the villa was a small white speck below him and the lake a sheet of hammered metal, and the mountains beyond rose blue and violet and remote. He had never been so happy. He had never been so free. He returned in the evenings, his knees scratched, his shirt torn, his pockets full of stones and feathers and the small hard fruits of trees he could not name, and his mother would say, “Thomas, where *have* you been?” and he would say, “Nowhere,” and she would say, “You must not go so far alone; it is not safe,” and he would say, “I was not alone; there were goats,” and she would sigh and turn back to her letter, and write: *Tom is very brown. He spends all day out of doors. It is doing him good.*
