It was upon the evening of the fourteenth that I received a hurried note from my old friend, Inspector Tobias Grant, of the South-Eastern Railway Police. The matter, he wrote, was one of some delicacy, and he begged that I would take the first train down to Wicken Junction and see him at the signal box there. My practice being at that time in a state of comparative idleness, I was not slow to comply with his request.

Wicken Junction is a place of some importance, where the main line to the coast divides into two lesser branches. It is a lonely spot, hemmed in by flat, marshy country, and the wind has a dreary way of whistling across the points and through the ironwork of the signal gantries. I found Grant awaiting me upon the platform, a stout, ruddy-faced man whose usual cheerfulness was quite gone.

“It is a bad business, Holmes,” he said, as we shook hands. “A very bad business. We have a theft, a man under suspicion of drunkenness on duty, and a story which no one in their right senses will believe.”

He led me to a small waiting-room, where a fire burned in the grate. Over a cup of strong tea, he laid the facts before me. A goods train, the 11:47 from London Bridge, had passed through the junction two nights since. It carried, amongst other things, a locked iron strong-box containing bearer bonds to the value of some ten thousand pounds, consigned to a bank in Dover. Upon its arrival, the strong-box was found to be intact, its seals unbroken, but the bonds were gone.

The matter was grave enough, but what made it singular was the evidence of the signalman, a steady fellow by the name of Elias Croft. Croft swore that as the train was passing the junction box at midnight precisely, he saw a light in one of the empty goods wagons. It was not a reflection, he insisted, but a steady, hand-held lamp, which flashed twice, then vanished. He was so disturbed that he reported it at once to the night inspector, who found him, an hour later, in a state of some agitation and smelling faintly of brandy. Croft was suspended from his duties, and the railway company was inclined to dismiss the whole thing as a drunken fancy.

“And you, Grant?” I asked.

“I believe the man is telling the truth,” he said firmly. “He has been on the line for twenty years, and there is not a mark against his name. Furthermore, the theft is a reality. A man does not imagine the loss of ten thousand pounds. The two events are surely connected.”

“Then we must take Mr. Croft’s account at its face value,” said I. “A lamp in an empty wagon. The implication is plain. Someone was concealed there.”

“But who? And how did they signal? And how did they escape with the bonds? The train never stopped between London and Dover.”

“Those are the questions we must answer,” I replied. “I will speak with Croft myself, and then I wish to ride the line tonight upon that same train.”

We found Elias Croft in his small cottage near the junction. He was a thin, nervous man, with the deep-set, watchful eyes of one who spends his life in the dark. He was still pale and shaken from his ordeal.

“I am ruined, sir,” he said to me, wringing his hands. “They say I was drunk, but I had but one small drop from a flask to keep out the chill. I saw it as clear as I see you. A lamp, in the third van from the rear. It was a steady light, then two quick flashes. Like a signal, sir.”

“Did you see a face or a figure?” I asked.

“No, sir. It was too far, and the van was dark save for the lamp. But it was a man’s hand holding it. I am sure of it.”

His account was consistent and delivered with a conviction that carried its own weight. I was satisfied that he was no drunkard.

That night, I took my place in the guard’s van of the 11:47. The night was foul, with a driving rain that lashed against the carriage windows and rendered the world outside a mere blur of blackness. The guard, a taciturn man named Petrie, seemed to regard my presence with suspicion, but a word from Inspector Grant had secured his reluctant cooperation. I settled myself upon a bench, my ulster collar turned up against the draught, and prepared for a long and uncomfortable vigil.

As we rattled through the suburbs, the lights of London giving way to the unrelieved dark of the countryside, I considered the problem. The lamp was the key. It was a signal, but to whom? There was no other train upon the line. It was possible that the light was meant for an accomplice waiting by the track, but Croft had been most emphatic that the flashes were directed towards the signal box, towards him. It was a message intended to be seen.

The train slowed as it approached Wicken Junction. I pressed my face to the window, my eyes straining to pick out the landmark Croft had described—a short, stout oak tree that stood beside the line just before the gantries. The rain had eased, but the wind still howled. The junction lights swam into view, and there, for a fleeting second, was the tree. My gaze was fixed upon the wagons as they clattered past. Nothing. The vans were empty, their interiors as black as pitch. The train gathered speed, and the junction was lost to sight.

I confess to a feeling of disappointment. Had Croft imagined it after all? I sat back, my mind turning over the possibilities. The rain began again, a fine, penetrating mist. I was about to suggest to Petrie that we make a hot drink when a sudden, jarring jolt ran through the train. It was not the usual sway of the carriages, but a sharp, violent shock, as if we had struck something upon the line.
