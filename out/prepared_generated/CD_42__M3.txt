If any patronizing philosopher, wrapped in the comfortable conceit of his own untroubled stomach, had wandered into the low-ceilinged, smoke-blackened parlor of the Bountiful Straggler on a certain biting December evening in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and forty-something, he might have supposed he had stumbled upon a convention of sheeted ghosts. But the Bountiful Straggler stood, on this occasion, stripped of its customary bounty; the brass taps were dry, the roaring fire was reduced to a few sullen embers, and the great goose-oven yawned as empty as the pockets of the mournful assembly gathered within.

They were the members of the Mutual Providence Goose Club, an association of humble folk who, for six long months, had denied themselves the very necessaries of existence—foregoing their modest drops of gin, their weekly pinches of tobacco, their occasional indulgence in a hot pie—to scrape together their farthings in the trustful care of Mr. Amos Tuckle. Mr. Tuckle, the publican of the Bountiful Straggler, was a man of such expansive waistcoat and unctuous geniality that he had seemed woven from the very fabric of Christmas itself. He had promised them, with tears of benevolence standing in his small eyes, a goose so plump it would make the Fortune of London itself look lean; a pudding so rich it would darken the intellect; and a barrel of ale so generous it would float a royal barge.

And now, Mr. Amos Tuckle was gone. Vanished! Evaporated into the freezing London fog, taking with him the accumulated coppers of the Mutual Providence Goose Club, the silver spoons from the bar, his mother’s silver snuff-box, and, it was darkly rumored, the affectionate wife of the butcher across the street.

The mournful committee sat upon the empty beer-barrels that lined the grimy walls, their faces drawn and pale in the flickering light of a single tallow candle. Mr. Gabinius Sludge, a cadaverous undertaker’s assistant who naturally viewed all human festive endeavors through a professional veil of melancholy, was the first to break the dismal silence.

“It is a heavy case,” said Mr. Sludge, shaking his head in a slow, professional manner, as if measuring the company for a row of paupers’ pine boxes. “A very heavy case. The deposits are deceased. The fowl is flown. We are left with nothing but the feathers of our own folly.”

“Folly, Gabinius?” cried Mrs. Sowerby, a stout washerwoman whose indignation caused her vast red shawl to heave like a tempestuous ocean. “I calls it swindling! I calls it robbery! I calls it the actions of a wicked, false-hearted, butter-tongued thief! Six months of rubbing and scrubbing the harsh linen of the Countess of Whimple, and for what? For my poor children to look at an empty trencher on Christmas Day, and cry ‘Mother, where is the goose?’ Oh, the wretch! The stony-hearted villain!”

“There, there, Betsy,” murmured Mr. Sowerby, a small, nervous man who worked as a tinman and was entirely accustomed to his wife’s tempests. “Don’t take on so. Anger won’t roast a bird, nor will it boil the dumplings. We are all in the same boat, and the boat is leaky, and the tide is high, and I dare say we shall all be drowned together.”

“Then we shall be drowned with dignity, Sowerby,” said Mrs. Sowerby, wiping her eyes with the corner of her apron. “But oh, to think of the treacle pudding! To think of the raisins and the suet and the spice! Gone, all gone, into the capacious pocket of that fat man!”

“If I might make so bold,” said a small, tremulous voice from the shadows, “it is not the pudding I shall mourn, but the stuffing. A goose without stuffing is like a heart without hope—an empty, flapping cavity.”

This observation came from Mr. Pim, a retired tailor who now existed on the fringes of the parish, sewing buttons for a living. He was a very little man, with a very large pair of spectacles, and he possessed a habit of stating the most devastating truths with an air of cheerful innocence.

“A cavity, Mr. Pim,” assented Mr. Sludge, nodding gloomily, “which the worms of disappointment shall soon inhabit. I have measured the depths of human misery, but this approaches a new low-water mark.”

It was at this precise moment of collective despair that the door of the parlor creaked open, admitting a blast of freezing December air, and a figure that seemed to be composed entirely of soot, rags, and a lantern. This was Old Grimes, the parish lamplighter, a man of uncertain age but immense antiquity, who smelled strongly of hot oil, charred wick, and the frosty night. He carried his long, brass-tipped lighting-pole as if it were a scepter, and he peered at the gloomy assembly through a pair of spectacles that were held together by a piece of wire and sheer determination.

“Eigh! Dreadful cold!” croaked Old Grimes, setting down his lantern and blowing upon his blue, knotted fingers. “Dreadful cold, and dreadful dark! I came to light the lamps in the bar, but I see the bar is dark in more ways than one. The fire’s out, and the master’s gone. Aye, aye! I heard the news at the corner. Tuckle’s flown the coop, and the geese have gone with him.”

“You have heard the truth, Mr. Grimes,” said Mrs. Sowerby, with a tragic sigh. “We are robbed, beggared, and left to starve on the very eve of Christmas.”

“Eigh sure! Dreadful hard!” said the old lamplighter, shaking his hoary head. “Dreadful hard for the likes of you and me. I suppose you are all sitting here, waiting for a goose to drop out of the chimney?”

“We are waiting for justice, Mr. Grimes,” said Mr. Sludge, stiffly. “Or, failing that, a miracle.”
