Lady Stutfield had spent six weeks in the country for the express purpose of composing a single paradox, and had returned to Belgrave Square with the epigram neatly pressed between the leaves of her prayer-book. She was a woman of exquisite fragility, who wore her tragedies as gracefully as she wore her velvet, and who believed that the chief tragedy of life was the inability of the upper classes to dine before half-past eight.

Her dinner that evening in June was universally admitted to be the most crushing triumph of the London season. The orchids were positively immoral in their splendor, drooping with a sort of heavy, scented depravity above the cloth of silver. The candlelight caught the red gleam of the hock glasses and the deep, sullen flash of the rubies at Lady Rellerton’s throat. Society, which is the only audience worth playing to, was gathered in full force.

The guest of honour was Lord Mervyn Quain. He was a man who had achieved an enormous reputation simply by saying nothing in a remarkably loud voice. His silences were considered profound; his pauses were eagerly quoted in the morning papers. To be entertained by Lord Quain was the ultimate ambition of every hostess in London, for a dinner without him was merely a meal.

“He is the only man I know,” Lord Valthrop murmured to his neighbour, “who can make the alphabet sound like a confession of sin. He gives an entirely new meaning to the vowels. He is, in short, a master of the unsaid.”

“And the unutterable,” Lady Stutfield added, sweeping past them to receive her lion.

Lord Quain entered the drawing-room with the heavy, lidded eyes of a man who has been staring at his own genius for too long. He bowed to his hostess. The guests gathered round in a breathless circle, waiting for the first spark.

“We have been waiting for you, Lord Quain,” said Lady Stutfield, extending a hand weighed down by diamonds. “We have been quite starved.”

“Ah,” said Lord Quain.

A little shiver of anticipation ran through the room. It was a masterful opening, they thought.

“The evening is quite warm,” ventured young Lord Goring, who was always desperate to fill a void.

“It is,” said Lord Quain.

The circle exchanged uneasy glances. Surely, they thought, the great man was merely gathering his forces. They moved into the dining-room, the men in their severe black and white, the women trailing clouds of pale silk and whispering chiffon.

The first course was served, a delicate consommé of pale gold. Lord Quain partook of it heavily. He looked at his spoon as if it contained the secret of the universe, but offered no further comment.

“The Prime Minister’s speech last night,” Lady Stutfield ventured, her voice rippling with anxiety, “was considered quite remarkable. What did you think of it, Lord Quain?”

“I did not hear it,” said Lord Quain.

“You did not hear it?” echoed Lady Rellerton, a woman whose intelligence was as artificial as her hair.

“No,” said Lord Quain. “I was asleep.”

He then applied himself to a piece of bread, and a silence fell upon the table. It was not the charming, decorative silence of mutual understanding, but the awful, suffocating silence of a vacuum. Lord Quain had nothing to say. He had left his wit in the country, or perhaps he had merely decided to hoard it. He sat there, a monumental statue of inanity, chewing his bread with the grave, methodical rhythm of a ruminant animal.

A dinner party in Belgravia cannot survive on bread alone. A panic, cold and clammy, began to creep up Lady Stutfield’s spine. She saw her reputation as a salonnière crumbling like a stale biscuit. She looked down the table at Lord Valthrop, who was staring at a salt-cellar with terrifying intensity. She looked at Lady Rellerton, who was nervously tearing her bread into tiny, military squares.

It was a catastrophe.

Then, suddenly, a strange calm descended upon Lady Stutfield. She realised that if the guest of honour would not speak, she must. It was a terrifying thought. She had always been considered a charming woman, but charming in the manner of a beautiful screen—something to put in front of an empty fireplace. She had never been expected to supply the fire itself.

“It is a curious thing,” she said, her voice clear and steady, “that the people who talk the most are invariably the ones with the least to convey. They are like those dreadful new motor-cars, which emit a vast deal of smoke and make a terrible clatter, yet never quite manage to arrive anywhere. The true intellect, I have always felt, is like a well-cut jewel; its value lies in its facets, and its facets are its silences.”

Lord Valthrop looked up, startled. Lady Rellerton dropped her bread. Lord Quain continued to chew, oblivious.

“Silence,” Lady Stutfield continued, adjusting a bracelet of emeralds that gleamed like wet leaves, “is the only true form of conversation. Everything else is merely the noise we make to hide our lack of opinion. When someone tells me they are a profound thinker, I always know they are merely a bad sleeper. Insomnia is the mother of philosophy.”

There was a moment’s pause, and then Lord Valthrop laughed, a genuine, delighted laugh. “By Jove,” he cried, “that is perfectly true! The philosophers are always the men who cannot digest their dinners.”

“Precisely,” said Lady Stutfield, warming to her theme, feeling a strange, intoxicating power bubbling up inside her. “They sit up all night, staring at the moon, and in the morning they call their indigestion a system. It is the same with the modern novelists. They write enormous books, full of tedious descriptions of the weather, and then they claim it is realism. As if nature were not merely a badly painted backdrop for the human comedy! I never look at a sunset without thinking what a pity it is that someone hasn’t put a frame around it and charged admission. It is far too garish to be enjoyed for free.”
