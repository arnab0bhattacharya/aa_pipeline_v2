It was upon a raw, foggy morning in the latter half of November that I found myself in a hansom cab beside my friend, Mr. Novak, rattling out along the clay-coloured road to Heathfield Academy. The damp of the country seemed to creep through my greatcoat, but Novak sat opposite me in his customary thin grey tweed, his hands clasped over the head of his walking-stick, his sharp features entirely composed.

Our client, Mr. Hewitt, a senior master at the Academy, had arrived at Novak’s rooms the previous evening in a state of considerable agitation. The matter, as he had outlined it, was a delicate one. A series of forged cheques, drawn upon the account of Dr. Stourton, the headmaster, had been cashed in the village. The boy suspected was Arthur Pell, a senior scholar of undeniably brilliant parts, yet erratic habits, who had recently been expelled for the very crime. Hewitt, however, held grave doubts. He had taught Pell Latin prose for three years and swore upon his life that the boy’s natural hand was wholly unlike the specimens upon the forged drafts.

To complicate the affair, the headmaster’s private study had been burgled the night before. A window had been forced, drawers rifled, and a small iron cash-box taken. The local police had instantly attributed the break-in to the disgraced Pell, eager for revenge or funds. Yet Hewitt contended that the school was a hotbed of jealousies, and begged Novak to examine the evidence before the authorities closed the net.

We alighted at the gates of Heathfield, a cluster of red-brick buildings surrounded by bare elms, and were shown at once to Dr. Stourton’s study. Hewitt, a tall, clean-shaven man with the stoop of the scholar, was pacing the Turkey carpet. Dr. Stourton, a portly, red-faced gentleman, sat at his desk, eyeing us with evident impatience.

“I am very glad to see you, Mr. Novak,” said Hewitt, extending his hand. “I have persuaded Dr. Stourton to wait upon your judgment before the constable makes an arrest.”

The headmaster snorted, his fingers drumming upon the blotting-pad. “It is a waste of form-time, sir. The case is as plain as a pikestaff. Pell forged the cheques, and Pell broke my window. The boy is a bad lot.”

Novak bowed briefly, drew off his gloves, and turned his attention to the room. “Let us examine the window first,” said he.

The casement in question was at the far end of the study, looking out upon the playground. Novak knelt upon the boards, producing a small magnifying lens and a tape-measure from his pocket. He examined the shattered pane, the brass catch, and the muddy sill with the quiet intensity of a bloodhound upon the scent.

“The glass was cut with a glazier’s diamond,” Novak observed, speaking half to himself. “A clean, professional job. There are two sets of footprints beneath the window, both adult, both heavily nailed boots. You will observe, Watson, the depth of the heel-mark. It is not the tread of a growing boy.”

Dr. Stourton frowned. “Pell might have borrowed a man’s boots.”

“He might,” conceded Novak, rising and brushing the dust from his knees. “But let us look at the desk.”

He approached the heavy mahogany table. The drawers had been pulled out and their contents scattered, but Novak paid little heed to the disorder. Instead, his gaze fell upon the blotting-pad. He lifted it, held it to the window, and studied it closely.

“A fascinating surface,” he murmured. “See here, Watson. The ink has been absorbed in reverse. One can read the impression of the last documents written upon it.”

I looked, but could make out only a few blurred letters. Novak, however, seemed to find what he sought, for a faint, cat-like smile played about his lips. He turned to Hewitt.

“You have the forged cheques, Mr. Hewitt?”

Hewitt produced them from his pocket-book—three slips of paper. Novak spread them upon the desk, and bending over them, subjected them to a scrutiny as minute as that which he had given the window.

“The writing is a fair copy of Dr. Stourton’s signature,” said Novak. “Too fair, if I may say so. It is a careful, deliberate imitation. But the capital ‘S’ in Stourton is formed with a peculiar backward flourish. A flourish, Dr. Stourton, which I observe in your own signature upon the documents on your desk. It is a trick of the pen which one learns only by long practice.”

“Well?” said the headmaster, testily. “The boy copied it.”

“Perhaps,” said Novak. “But there is this. The lettering of the body of the cheque is not the natural hand of Arthur Pell. Mr. Hewitt is quite right. Pell, you say, has a bold, dashing script, with a marked slope to the right. This hand is upright, cramped, and singularly precise. It is the hand of a man accustomed to writing much, yet who is now holding his pen unnaturally, to disguise his fist. And there is more.”

Novak pointed to a small ink-spot upon the corner of one cheque. “You will perceive, Dr. Stourton, that this is a thick, blue-black ink, such as is used in fountain-pens. I observe that upon your desk there are no fountain-pens, only dip-pens and a bottle of Stephens’ ink. The ink of the forgery is of a different make.”

Dr. Stourton sat back in his chair, his face reddening. “This is very fine talking, Mr. Novak. But what do you infer?”

“I infer,” said Novak, “that the forger is an adult, familiar with this study, who has used the blotting-pad to practise the signature. And I infer that the same adult, wearing nailed boots, broke into this room last night, not for revenge, but to remove the cash-box—which, I wager, contained not only money, but certain cancelled cheques which might have served as specimens for the forgery.”

A heavy silence fell upon the room. It was broken by a sharp knock at the door, and a servant entered, announcing Mr. John Varley, the senior classical master.
