It was in the deep twilight of an autumnal evening, when the shadows of the metropolis lengthened and assumed fantastic and mournful shapes, that the heavy tidings of my daughter’s existence were first communicated to my startled soul. I sat in my solitary chamber, a wealthy widow, surrounded by the luxurious appurtenances which the world esteems as the chief good, yet feeling in my inmost heart that I was utterly desolate. The wind swept in fierce gusts around my dwelling, rattling the casements with a sound as of spirits wailing, and the rain beat in torrents against the glass, mirroring, in its wild and uncontrolled desolation, the stormy misery that had suddenly taken possession of my frame. I had believed her lost to me for ever. Twenty years before, in the blind agony of youthful shame, I had consigned my new-born infant to the cold charity of a foundling hospital, stifling the pleadings of maternal love beneath the tyrannical and inexorable demands of worldly reputation. I had wedded, I had lived in affluence and honour, I had seen my husband laid in the grave, and I had gathered around me the wealth which the world so ardently admires; yet, through all this, a phantom had ever pursued me, a voice had whispered in my ear that I was a mother, and that I had forsaken my child.

The messenger of this direful intelligence was a man whose life had been passed in scenes of misery and vice, and whose countenance bore the deep and awful traces of his acquaintance with guilt and woe. He spoke not with the humane consideration of one who feels for the sorrow he imparts, but with the hard and unmoved precision of a mercenary, detailing circumstances which he knew must wring my heart to its very core. He informed me that the infant, cast upon the precarious mercy of that public charity, had been reared in penury and neglect, and was now a seamstress in a low and miserable alley in the eastern quarter of London. Her life had been one continuous tissue of labour and suffering, uncheered by the light of affection, unsoftened by the dews of maternal tenderness. She knew not the name of her parents; she was as a weed growing in the pathless desert, unmarked and unregarded. And I, her mother, who should have been her guardian angel, her protector from the ills of life, had been living in splendour, shutting my eyes to the miserable fate of my own flesh and blood. O, what a tale was this for a mother’s ear! What a revelation of guilt and misery! I felt as if the solid earth were giving way beneath my feet, and I were sinking into a gulf of remediless despair. The storm without raged with redoubled fury, and its wild shrieks seemed to accord with the tumult of my soul.

As soon as the first wild paroxysm of my anguish had somewhat subsided, I resolved to seek her out, to behold the being to whom I had given existence, and to judge, with my own eyes, of the extent of that misery which my selfishness had inflicted. I wrapped my cloak around me, and, disregarding the darkness and the tempest, I issued forth into the streets. The wet pavements gleamed with a dismal light, and the few passengers who crossed my path hurried on with muffled forms and drooping heads, as if they too were flying from the consciousness of guilt. I traversed the wide and busy thoroughfares, where the lamps threw a flickering gleam upon the muddy waters, and entered the narrow and intricate lanes that led to the abode of my daughter. The houses, tall and ruinous, seemed to lean upon each other for support, as if they too were weary of their existence and the scenes of misery they had witnessed. The air was pestilential, and the rain, oozing through the broken roofs, fell in heavy drops upon the slimy and uneven pavement.

I reached the door of the wretched tenement where she lodged. I ascended the crazy staircase, which groaned beneath my feet as if in reproach for my coming. I paused before the door of her room. My heart beat wildly, and every limb trembled with a violent and uncontrollable agitation. I felt as if I were about to confront a judge who would condemn me for my crimes. I tapped gently at the door. A low and melancholy voice bade me enter. I obeyed. The room was small and scantily furnished, but it bore the impress of a struggling and refined taste. A few faded flowers stood in a broken vase upon the table, and the remnants of a piece of needlework lay scattered near the window. The dim light of a tallow candle threw a ghastly glare around the apartment, and revealed the form of a young woman seated by the fire. She rose as I entered, and I beheld her face. It was pale and thin, bearing the traces of premature sorrow and unceasing toil, but it was illuminated by a pair of large dark eyes, full of a sweet and touching melancholy, that seemed to plead for sympathy and love. She looked at me with an air of surprise and gentle deprecation, as if she feared that I had come to add to her burdens. I gazed upon her with an intensity of emotion that froze my utterance. I saw in her the lineaments of my own youth, softened and made beautiful by suffering. I saw the very curl of her hair, the very expression of the mouth that I had so often beheld in my mirror. It was my daughter, my own injured and forsaken child!
