The announcement of Miss Clarissa Vandeleur’s engagement to the young Lord Goring—for it was the thirteenth such announcement, and the season was not yet over—produced in the drawing-rooms of Mayfair a sensation that was part horror and part delight. The horror belonged chiefly to the mothers, who saw in this serial betrothal a menace to the sacred institution of matrimony; the delight belonged to everyone else, who saw in it a subject of inexhaustible conversation. “Thirteen,” murmured Lady Marchmont to Mrs. Delamere, over their tea at the Ritz, “is a number one does not associate with weddings. One associates it with the gallows, or with a dinner-party where the cook has lost his nerve. It is, in a word, unfortunate.”

The lady herself received these congratulations with a composure that was almost insolent. She was a tall, pale girl, with hair the colour of old gold and eyes that seemed to hold within them the memory of a thousand ballrooms. She had broken her engagements with a punctuality that was almost artistic. The first she had broken because the young man had proposed to her in a conservatory, and she had discovered, upon reflection, that she could not endure the smell of orchids. The second she had broken because he had proposed in a ballroom, and she had discovered, upon reflection, that she could not endure the smell of him. The third, fourth, and fifth had been broken for reasons that she had forgotten but which she was certain had been excellent at the time. The twelfth, a young guardsman of impeccable pedigree and no conversation, she had dismissed with a note of three lines, which read: “My dear Arthur, I find that I cannot marry a man who says ‘What?’ after every remark I make. It is not a question of love; it is a question of grammar.”

But Lord Goring was different. He was thirty-two, had a certain reputation for wit, and possessed the one quality that Clarissa found irresistible: he was entirely unimpressed by her. When she had announced, at a supper-party at the Savoy, that she intended to break off their engagement within the fortnight, he had merely raised an eyebrow and said, “I think not. I have made a wager with my club that I shall be the first man to marry you, and I am a man of my word. Besides, I have already ordered the wedding-cake, and it would be a pity to waste it.”

“You are very sure of yourself,” she said.

“I am sure of you,” he replied. “You have broken twelve engagements because you were bored. You will not break the thirteenth because, for the first time in your life, you are not bored. It is a very simple matter of arithmetic.”

Clarissa was, for a moment, silent. Then she laughed—a low, musical laugh that had been known to drive men to poetry and women to the headache. “You are the most insufferable creature I have ever met,” she said. “I believe I shall marry you after all.”

“Of course you will,” said Lord Goring. “I have already spoken to your mother.”

The Duchess of Cumbernauld—for that was Clarissa’s mother—was a woman of immense dignity and no sense of humour. She had watched her daughter’s career of broken promises with a mixture of despair and a curious, secret admiration. She herself had married at nineteen, had produced four daughters and a son, and had never since had a moment’s leisure in which to consider whether she had enjoyed it. But Clarissa had discovered the great secret of the age: that a woman may be engaged indefinitely without ever being married, and that the world will forgive her everything except the actual ceremony. The Duchess had forgiven her twelve times. She was determined not to forgive her a thirteenth.

“Clarissa,” she said, sweeping into her daughter’s boudoir one morning in May, “I have come to speak to you about Lord Goring.”

Clarissa was seated at her dressing-table, engaged in the delicate operation of arranging a single white rose in her hair. She did not turn. “Dear mama,” she said, “how delightful. I was just thinking about him myself. He has the most extraordinary hands. I noticed them at dinner last night. They are the hands of a man who has never done a day’s work in his life, and yet they are not idle. They are—how shall I put it?—they are decorative. I think I shall marry him.”

“You have said that twelve times before,” said the Duchess, “and each time you have changed your mind. I have consulted the Archbishop of Canterbury, who is an old friend of your father’s, and he tells me that thirteen is a number of the most sinister significance. He says that at the Last Supper there were thirteen present, and look what happened.”

“But mama,” said Clarissa, turning at last, “the thirteenth was the one who was not invited. I am sure Lord Goring would be the first to point out that the host is never the unlucky one. Besides, I have already broken twelve engagements. If I break a thirteenth, I shall be obliged to go on to a fourteenth, and a fifteenth, and so on, until I die, and then I shall be remembered only as a woman who never married. That would be very tedious. I have decided to be married instead. It is more original.”

The Duchess sat down heavily on a little gilt chair that creaked beneath her. “You are determined, then?”

“I am determined,” said Clarissa, “to be the first woman in London to break the superstition rather than the engagement. Lord Goring has promised to be at the church an hour before the time, in order to be sure of arriving first. He says that punctuality is the thief of time, but that in this case it is the thief of my reputation, and he intends to steal it before anyone else can. He is a very clever man, mama. I think I shall keep him.”
