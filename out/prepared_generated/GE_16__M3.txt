It is a common error in the arithmetic of human motives to suppose that a strong inclination and a clear perception of its object must inevitably precipitate action, as if the mind were a mere sluice-gate that must fly open when the pressure of desire is high. We forget that the very vividness of an imagination which projects itself into a future occupation may be accompanied by a cautious checking of the present means, and that the soul which is most athirst for a great end may be the most fastidious as to the cup wherein it drinks the preliminary waters. For the will is not a single undivided faculty, but a complex web wherein reason, affection, and the deeply rooted habits of feminine self-restraint are continually operating upon each other, so that a young woman may burn with a vocation and yet hesitate at the gate which opens upon it, if the key offered is one that binds as well as unlocks.

Clementina Farringford, standing in the subdued light of her uncle’s library in Harley Street, was in the very crisis of such a hesitation. She was a young woman whose dark, grave eyes seemed to hold a larger history than her four-and-twenty years could account for, and whose pale face was crossed now by a tremor of indignation which she was doing her utmost to compress into respectful silence. Opposite her, in the leather chair which seemed to have moulded itself to his impatient contours, sat Mr. Alderman Farringford, her guardian, a man whose benevolence was of that robust and self-approving kind which feels itself fully competent to regulate the lives of others, and which is never so much ruffled as by the suggestion that its own purposes, being limited, may not coincide with the purposes of the young and the untried.

"And so I am to understand," he was saying, with the ponderous irony which he mistook for pleasantry, "that you are to abandon the society of rational beings, and the prospects which any young woman of your station might reasonably look forward to, to pore over skeletons and drug-shops. A fine thing, truly, for a Farringford to be mixing with medical students, as if she were a candidate for the dissecting-room at St. Bartholomew’s! I wonder you do not ask me to buy you a pair of top-boots and a bull-whip, so that you might ride a-hunting after cases of the small-pox. I tell you, Tina, it is midsummer madness."

"You are pleased to make it grotesque, Uncle Farringford," said Clementina, her voice low but firm. "I do not ask to abandon society, but to qualify myself for a service which is greatly needed. I ask to study, as my cousin Edgar has studied, and to be permitted to do so without the insult of being told that I am seeking notoriety."

"Nonsense, child. Edgar is a man; his sphere is the world. Yours is the home. And the sooner you rid yourself of these crotchets, the better Mr. Lattimer and Mr. Bellingham will be pleased. They are not going to pay their addresses to a female surgeon. You will find their patience as short as their tempers, I promise you."

Clementina turned away, with a gesture of suppressed protest, and stood by the window. The summer leaves in the square were fluttering in a breeze that seemed to her like the very breath of liberty, so distant and so unattainable. She knew too well that Mr. Lattimer, a florid gentleman who regarded all intellectual women as a species of Eumenides, and Mr. Bellingham, whose wit was of the ponderously jocose variety, would indeed view her ambition as a fitting subject for mockery. They had already made it the theme of drawing-room jests, asking her with smiling condescension whether she preferred the lancet to the fan, and whether she meant to bleed them for the vapours before or after the wedding ceremony. Such jests had the effect upon her of a slow, grinding friction, wearing away the surface of her patience, but leaving the inner resolve harder and more compact.

It was at this point that the door opened and Edgar Lansdale entered. He was a man of nine-and-twenty, tall, with a fair, serious face, and a manner which had the calmness of one who is accustomed to weigh evidence. He had come straight from his own reading in physiology, carrying still the atmosphere of that precise and merciful inquiry into the laws of life which was his chosen pursuit. He glanced from his cousin to his uncle, and read the situation with the quick intelligence of a sympathetic nature. He had heard, indeed, from the lower servants, who were excellent telegraphs of domestic weather, that the Alderman had been greatly exercised by Miss Farringford’s renewed expression of her medical aspirations.

"Uncle," said Edgar, after a brief and courteous inquiry as to the elder gentleman’s health, which was answered with a grunt that implied that health itself was an affront when one’s authority was impugned, "I fear I am an intruder upon a conference of state. But perhaps I may be permitted to say a word on Tina’s behalf."

"You may say what you please, Edgar, so long as you do not encourage her in this tomfoolery," returned the Alderman, gathering up his newspaper as a symbol of his withdrawal from the debate.

Edgar waited until the door had closed behind his uncle’s retreating footsteps, and then turned to Clementina with a look in which admiration and a certain calculating kindness were so blended that it would have required a very delicate perception to separate them.

"Dearest Tina," he said, "you must not be cast down. I have been thinking much of this matter, and I am persuaded that your wishes are not only natural but laudable. It is a shame that the gates of knowledge should be shut against you. And I am come to offer you a key."
