The light was the thing, first of all. Not the weak, watery light of the Bloomsbury square where her lodgings stood, a light that seemed to seep up from the damp flagstones and the soot-stained railings, but a great, celestial wash of it, pouring down from the glass domes of the National Gallery. It fell upon the parquet floors and the polished benches and the hushed faces of the other gazers, and it seemed to wash the dust of the week from one’s very soul. For this was her day, her Thursday, and the school, with its slate-grey walls and the eternal, high-pitched chorus of thirty girls, was a world away, a different country altogether.

She walked from Russell Square, the letter a small, hard rectangle pressing against the lining of her bag. It had been there since Tuesday. She had not looked at the postmark, had only registered the familiar, angular script of her sister, and had felt a sudden, sharp contraction in her chest, like a door closing in a draughty house. So she had put it away, and it had lain there since, a pocket of stillness, a pocket of dread, amidst the jumble of chalk, a spare pair of spectacles, and a half-eaten biscuit. Now, as she crossed the roar and rumble of Trafalgar Square, the pigeons scattering like handfuls of thrown grey ash, the letter seemed to grow heavier, more insistent. It was a weight that had nothing to do with paper and ink.

Inside, the great hall received her with its peculiar, resonant hush. The noise of the city was shut out, replaced by the soft scuff of shoes and the occasional, reverent cough. She paid it no mind. Her feet knew the way, turning left, then right, past the grand Italian madonnas and the dark, burly Dutchmen, until she came to the small, quiet room. And there it was. Not a grand, dramatic canvas, but a simple one, a thing of deep blues and golds and a quiet, profound sorrow. She had been coming to it for months. It was her secret chapel.

She stood before it, as she always did, and let the world fall away. The figures in the painting—three women, their faces obscured by shadow and grief—were more real to her, in that moment, than anyone she had spoken to all week. Their stillness was a balm. The way the light caught the golden thread of a hem, the curve of a shoulder, the deep, impossible blue of the Virgin’s robe, it was a language more eloquent than any she taught. It spoke of things that could not be set as an exercise in composition or parsed for grammatical correctness. It spoke of loss, and of a grace that endured it.

And as she looked, the girls began to drift through her mind, not as a class, but as individual, shimmering presences. There was little Elsie Marsh, with her earnest, upturned face and her hopeless tangles of hair, who had cried today over a misdeclined Latin verb. Her tears had seemed, at the time, a childish nuisance. But now, looking at the sorrow in the painting, Elsie’s small, earnest grief seemed suddenly part of the same great, unending stream. It was all of a piece, this human ache. Then there was Beatrice, clever, sharp-tongued Beatrice, who watched everything with a cynical glint and wrote poetry in secret that was surprisingly, painfully tender. Did Beatrice ever stand before something like this and feel her armour crack? One could not know. The girls were a universe of sealed rooms, and she, the schoolmistress, held only the keys to their Latin and their history, not to the silent, shadowed country of their hearts.

Her hand, of its own volition, strayed to her bag. The leather was worn smooth at the corners. She could feel the letter’s edge through the fabric lining. Her sister’s news. A wedding, perhaps? Or an illness? Or simply the ordinary, grinding news of a life lived elsewhere, a life that went on without her, accumulating joys and sorrows she would only ever learn of second-hand, through these stark, white squares of paper. The thought was a cold stone in the warmth of the gallery. The letter was a fact, hard and unyielding. The painting was a feeling, fluid and deep. One was the world as it was; the other, perhaps, the world as it might be felt.

She thought of the girls again, walking home in their little packs, their futures as blank and terrifyingly open as an unwritten page. They did not know it yet. They chattered of dances and ribbons and the injustice of a particular maths problem, their lives a bright, fleeting surface. She envied them their ignorance, their capacity for a simple, uncomplicated happiness. For her, the surface had been broken long ago. She was left with the depths, and the slow, patient work of learning to see.

A shaft of light from a high window moved across the floor, a slow, golden tide. It touched the toes of her sensible shoes and crept upwards. She watched it, mesmerised. It was like the passage of time itself, silent, inexorable. The painting, for all its stillness, was full of this slow movement, the shift of light and shadow that gave it life. The women would not move, would not speak, but the light around them would change, and in that change, everything was contained. Perhaps that was it. Perhaps one did not need to know the news in the letter, or the secret thoughts of the girls, or even the meaning of the sorrow in the Virgin’s face. Perhaps one only needed to stand, and to watch the light, and to feel the slow, steady beat of one’s own heart in the quiet, and to know that this, too, was enough. The letter could wait. The girls would be there tomorrow. But this moment, this slant of gold on a worn parquet floor, this was now. And for now, it was enough.
