The fog, that grey and persistent visitor to the London of my memory, had settled over the streets of Soho with a particular intimacy that evening. It was not the clean, sharp chill of the river fog, but a thing compounded of coal smoke, the breath of a million souls, and the lingering vapours of a hundred cuisines; a miasma that seemed to seep into the very marrow of the old bricks and to blur the moral outlines of the world. I was making my way along a narrow thoroughfare near the Charing Cross Road, a place where the gaslight struggled and sputtered in its globes, casting a sickly, jaundiced pallor upon the wet pavement and the hurrying figures that flitted past like ghosts in a drab and hurried purgatory. My own pension, a grudging acknowledgment from the Home Office for services rendered—and, more importantly, for services forgotten—was a thing of quiet, calculated misery. It was sufficient to keep me in a state of permanent, respectable decay, a shadow drifting through the city I had once helped to map with the fine, invisible threads of betrayal. I felt myself to be a creature of the twilight, belonging neither to the day of honest labour nor the night of outright crime, but to the grey, uncertain hours in between. It was in this state of mind, this profound and unassailable anonymity, that I was disturbed.

The voice came from behind me, a voice that had no place in the London I had constructed for myself; it was a voice from a buried stratum of my existence, rough-edged and coloured by the harsh vowels of the northern mills. I stopped, my heart giving a single, heavy thud against my ribs, like a prisoner striking a wall. I turned slowly, the fog swirling around my shoulders, and saw him. He stood under the uncertain glow of a shop window displaying bolts of silk, and the light fell upon a face I had not seen in fifteen years, a face that time had carved rather than aged. The smooth, almost boyish features I remembered had been given a hard, geological permanence; a pale scar, thin as a thread of wire, ran from his temple to his jaw, and his eyes, which I recalled as being full of a reckless, fiery impatience, now held the calm, unnerving stillness of a deep pool.

“Silas,” he said, and the name, my name, felt like an old, ill-fitting coat being draped over my shoulders. “Silas Vane. I thought it was you.”

He was Callum Croft. A name I had written in a ledger, a name I had murmured in a stuffy office off Whitehall, a name that had bought me this very life of quiet shame. He had been a firebrand then, a union agitator with a poet’s fury and a conspirator’s guile, and I had been his friend. The word tastes like ash in the mouth even now. I had sold him for the price of my own security. He had gone to Dartmoor, and I had gone to a small flat in Pimlico.

“Croft,” I managed to say, the sound of it thin and reedy in the damp air. My mind was a frantic mill, grinding through possibilities, seeking the angle of attack, the hidden resentment, the cold glint of revenge. But his face betrayed none of these things. He looked at me with a sort of weary, almost gentle curiosity.

“They let you out,” I stated, for want of anything better. A platitude. A stone thrown into a chasm.

“Aye. Time served. Time for everything, they say.” He took a step closer, and I had to resist the urge to retreat into the anonymous stream of passers-by. The scent of him was not of prison, but of clean wool and the faint, sharp aroma of good tobacco. He had made a life, it seemed. “You’ve aged, Silas. The city’s got its teeth in you.”

“We all change,” I murmured, the words catching in my throat.

“Some do,” he agreed, his gaze unwavering. “Some are simply… revealed.” He smiled then, a slow, disconcerting expression that did not reach his eyes. It was a smile that acknowledged the vast, unspoken chasm between us, and yet chose, with a sovereign and terrifying grace, to ignore it. “I find myself at a loose end this evening. All this,” he gestured vaguely at the teeming, fog-shrouded street, “it’s a bit overwhelming after the quiet of the moors. A man needs a proper meal and a decent bottle of wine to remind him he’s still a man, and not a ghost.” He paused, letting the silence stretch. “I know a place. A little French cellar, just off Dean Street. The food is honest, and the wine is better than we deserve. Will you dine with me?”

The invitation hung in the air between us, more menacing than any threat. A dinner. A shared table. A breaking of bread with the man I had broken. It was an act of such profound and calculated civility that it defied all my understanding of the world. To refuse would be an admission of guilt, a coward’s flight. To accept… to accept was to walk willingly into some unknown chamber of judgement, to submit to a trial whose rules I could not possibly fathom. He was not asking for my company; he was demanding my presence. He was reeling me in, just as I had once reeled him in with false promises of solidarity and shared purpose. The irony was a bitter, metallic taste in my mouth.

I looked at his face, at the patient, unreadable calm in his eyes, and I understood that there was no choice. My life of quiet, ignominious peace was over. The past had returned not as a ghost to be placated, but as a solid, breathing man with an invitation to dinner. I was a specimen pinned to a board, and he was the collector, come to examine his prize.
