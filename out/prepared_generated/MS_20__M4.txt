They buried my mother on the twenty-first of October, in the cold rain that had not ceased for a fortnight, and I stood at the graveside with my hands folded upon my breast, neither weeping nor speaking, while the villagers regarded me with that species of pity which is not far removed from reproach. The earth fell upon the coffin with a dull and heavy sound, and I thought—how is it that the world continues thus, indifferent and unaltered, when the being who was its centre has been withdrawn from it for ever? The yew-tree dripped above me; the sexton wiped his brow; the few neighbours murmured their condolences and departed, and I was left alone in that melancholy place, the rain mingling with the tears which I had not the power to shed.

My mother, whose name was Eluned Vaughan, had lain three years upon that bed of suffering from which she never rose. I was but sixteen when the fever first seized her, and from that hour I knew no youth, no joy, no hope, save such as were reflected from her fading countenance. I sat beside her through the long nights, when the wind howled about our cottage on the hill, and the candle guttered, and she spoke in the wild language of delirium; I held her hand when the paroxysms came, and learned to read the varying shades of agony upon her wasted features; I watched the slow approach of that inevitable hour which I dreaded even while I prayed for its arrival, for her sake, that she might be released from torment. And now she was gone—gone utterly—and I, at nineteen, stood alone in the world, as a mariner who, having weathered a tempest, finds himself upon a rock, the vessel wrecked, the companions drowned, and the ocean still roaring about him.

There came to me, in the week that followed, a letter from a distant kinsman of my father's—a certain Mr. Penrhyn, of Bristol, who had never in my remembrance visited our roof, and whose existence I knew only from the mention of his name in former years. He wrote in terms of condescending kindness, offering me a home beneath his roof, the protection of his family, and such instruction as might fit me for the station of a governess—that refuge of the friendless gentlewoman. His words were fair, and I doubt not his intentions were honourable; but as I read, there arose within me a resistance which I could not quell. I thought of the long journey to a strange place; of the faces of strangers; of the necessity of appearing grateful and composed when my heart was breaking; of the thousand petty humiliations which the dependent must endure. I thought, too, of my mother's cottage—that humble dwelling where every stone was hallowed by her presence, where the very air seemed yet to hold the echo of her voice; and I resolved that I would not go.

The villagers, when they learned my determination, shook their heads and whispered among themselves. The good Mrs. Rhys, who had been my mother's friend in youth, came to me with many arguments and tears, representing the folly and danger of a young woman living alone, without protection, in a remote and lonely place. "You will be thought strange," said she, "you will be suspected, avoided; the world will not understand your motives, and will put the worst construction on your conduct." I answered that I cared not for the world's opinion; that I desired only to dwell in peace where my mother had dwelt, and to tend her garden, and to keep her grave green. She departed sorrowing, and I saw her no more.

The cottage stood upon a gentle eminence at the head of a valley, whose green sides sloped down to a stream that brawled among the stones and lost itself in a narrow gorge between the hills. Behind it rose the mountains, wild and barren, their summits often veiled in mist, their sides furrowed with torrents and clothed with heather that in autumn flushed purple to the very edge of the precipices. The nearest village, Penmawr, lay a mile distant down the valley; and between it and my dwelling there was no habitation save a few scattered cottages, whose inhabitants I knew but slightly, and who regarded me with that curiosity which the country people bestow upon those who differ from themselves.

The winter came on with storms of wind and rain. The stream swelled to a torrent; the mountains were hidden in vapour; the trees stood bare and black against the leaden sky. I sat alone in the little parlour where my mother had passed so many hours of pain, and I read the books she had loved—the poems of Milton, the letters of Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, a volume of sermons, and the plays of Shakespeare—and I sought to lose myself in their pages. But often the book would fall from my hand, and I would sit gazing at the empty chair, and listening to the wind as it moaned about the house, and I would feel my heart swell with a grief too deep for utterance. How dreadful is the silence of a house from which the beloved voice has departed! The clock ticks, the fire crackles, the rain beats against the window, and yet there is a stillness more profound than any sound—the stillness of the grave.

Yet I was not wholly without comfort. There was a dog, a shaggy creature of no particular breed, that had been my mother's companion in her walks, and that now attached itself to me with a fidelity that often moved me to tears. He would lie at my feet as I sat reading, and would look up in my face with an expression of dumb sympathy more eloquent than words. And there was the garden, which, though neglected during my mother's illness, still yielded in its season the flowers she had planted—the roses, the lavender, the rosemary that she loved. I tended them with care, and in their fragrance I seemed to breathe again the air of happier days.
