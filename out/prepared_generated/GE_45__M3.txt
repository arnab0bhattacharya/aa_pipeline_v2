It is a common error in the estimation of reformers to suppose that the human mind, like a field of clover, will naturally incline toward the light if left merely to its own devices; whereas it requires a very particular sort of intellectual husbandry to convince a provincial farmer that the sun does not rise exclusively for the purpose of ripening his own wheat. When Mr. Edgar Fenslow arrived in the ancient, half-drained borough of Tilsbury to assume the mastership of the endowed grammar school, he brought with him not only the sound Cambridge degree that satisfied the trustees, but a private enthusiasm for the newer philosophies of natural science—an intellectual contagion that had been fermenting in his solitary studies until it demanded the larger outlet of practical instruction. He was a tall, nervous man, with that subdued eagerness of manner which belongs to minds that have long entertained their thoughts in silence, and are startled to find that the world is not already in possession of them.

Fenslow had conceived that the ancient pedagogy of beaten Latin grammar, which had turned the youth of Tilsbury into mere mechanical reciters of Eton syntax, might be advantageously leavened by a study of the living world: that the structure of a beetle’s wing or the folding of a leaf might serve as a preparatory discipline for the understanding of higher laws. He did not perceive, with that prescience which comes of wider worldly experience, that the average Tilsbury parent cared no more for the anatomy of a weevil than for the theological speculations of a German professor. What Mr. Joel Gobbit, the substantial wool-stapler, demanded was that his son should be able to keep a ledger without reproach, and that his handwriting should be of a flourishing character; while Mr. Vining, the ironmonger, held that a boy who could not construe “Amo, amas, amat” was being defrauded of his birthright as an Englishman.

The murmurs began, as murmurs in such societies invariably do, not in the open daylight of a public meeting, but in the shadowed corners of the market-place and across the counters of the shops where worsted and tape were measured out to hesitating customers.

“Nonsense, I call it,” observed Mr. Gobbit, leaning his broad back against the doorpost of the Golden Sheaf, while he slowly manipulated a silver pencil-case. “Here’s my Tom, come home with his hands full of muck and dead flies, and when I asks him his Latin verb, he tells me as how the river-water’s got little animals in it as swims about. Little animals! If I want little animals, I can look at my own sheep. A pretty thing, indeed, if a boy’s head is to be stuffed with bugs instead of accounts. I’ll have a word with the trustees, or my name’s not Joel Gobbit.”

“Ay, ay,” assented Mrs. Toogood, the draper’s wife, a woman who had achieved a local reputation for acuteness by the habitual practice of contradicting everybody in a tone of injured virtue. “It’s a poor look-out for the boys. My Albert says he must go a-fishing next Saturday, to catch what he calls specimens. I’m sure I don’t want him to be a naturalist. It’s a dirty trade, and only fit for them as has nothing else to do. If Mr. Fenslow can’t learn ’em their Bible and their writing, he’d better make room for them as can. Squire Lupton’s nephew, Mr. Thorne, is looking for a place, and he’s a real gentleman, as knows Latin and Greek, and doesn’t hold with such new-fangled ways.”

The trustees, a body composed largely of those substantial persons whose chief concern was to avoid any disturbance of the comfortable equilibrium in which their own importance was recognized, listened to these murmurs with the gravity of men who felt that the foundations of society were being assailed. Squire Lupton, the chairman, was not wanting in a certain rough good-nature; but his intellect, having been moulded in the previous century, regarded any departure from the discipline of Lily’s Grammar as a step toward revolution. He had been informed that Fenslow had actually brought into the schoolroom a case of stuffed birds and a microscope—an instrument which, to the Squire’s mind, was vaguely associated with conjuring tricks and Popish imposture.

“We must look into this, Mr. Fenslow,” said the Squire, in that large, conclusive tone which is the natural language of men who have been accustomed to decide the price of hay without contradiction. “The parents are dissatisfied. They say the boys learn nothing useful. There’s Gobbit’s boy—why, he can’t write a common receipt. And you’ve been teaching ’em about insects, I’m told. We can’t have that, you know. The school was endowed for the teaching of grammar and good learning, not for turning lads into butterfly-catchers.”

Fenslow’s pale face flushed with the sensitive flush of a man who feels that his highest motive is being misinterpreted, and who lacks the ready rhetoric to vindicate it. He strove to explain that the habit of accurate observation was the foundation of all sound learning; that a boy who could trace the delicate articulation of a beetle’s limb was acquiring a precision of thought that would serve him in the highest offices of life. But the Squire listened with that impatient tolerance which provincial authority accorded to theoretical talk, and the other trustees gazed at the table or softly tapped their boots with their walking-sticks, feeling that Fenslow was proving his own unfitness by the very earnestness of his defence.

“I don’t understand it,” said the Squire, summarily. “The fact is, the parents complain, and we must consider the parents. If you can’t conform to the old plan, why, we must look out for a master as will.”
