The rain fell upon the windows of our London lodging with a heavy, ceaseless drumming, a sound that seemed to mirror the tumultuous and disordered state of my brother’s mind. It was the autumn of 1815, and the air was already tinged with the chill of approaching winter, a season that always struck me as a fitting emblem for the melancholy that had lately taken possession of our household. Edmund, my brother, stood by the grate, his back turned to me, his shoulders hunched as if bearing the weight of an invisible and crushing stone. I had watched him for hours, my heart aching with a silent, desperate hope that the storm within him might pass, yet I knew, with the terrible clarity of long suffering, that this was no passing cloud but the gathering of a tempest that would shatter everything we held dear.

Edmund had always been a creature of the sublime. Where others saw only the mundane mechanics of existence, he perceived the grand, terrifying architecture of the soul. His verses, written in the feverish light of midnight, were not mere words but breaths of a spirit striving to ascend beyond its earthly confines. We had believed, or rather, I had believed, that his genius was of that rare and terrible kind that, once kindled, could not be extinguished. Yet, as the days had drawn toward the appointment with Mr. Sterling, the most formidable and unforgiving critic of the age, a cold dread had begun to settle in my bones. I had seen the way Edmund avoided my gaze; I had noticed the trembling in his hand as he held his quill, the sudden, violent silences that fell over him when he thought himself unobserved. I had tried to speak of hope, to remind him of the beauty he had wrought, but my words seemed to fall upon him like water on stone, sliding away without leaving a mark.

When the news came, it did not arrive as a blow, but as a slow, suffocating darkness. Mr. Sterling had not raged; he had not even raised his voice. He had simply looked at the pages Edmund had offered him with a gaze of such profound and polite indifference that it was more devastating than any scorn. "There is a certain fluency," Sterling had said, his voice dry and devoid of warmth, "but fluency is not poetry. There is no truth here, no struggle, no fire. It is all ornament, and ornament is the refuge of the talentless." The words had hung in the air, sharp and cold, and I had watched Edmund’s face drain of all color, a mask of numb shock settling over his features. He had thanked the critic, bowed his head, and walked out into the rain without a word, leaving me alone with the crushing weight of his humiliation.

For three days, Edmund did not speak. He sat in his chair by the window, staring out at the grey sky, his eyes hollow and vacant. I brought him food, which he did not touch; I spoke to him, which he did not hear. I felt a rising panic, a fear that his spirit had broken, that the core of his being had been shattered by the verdict of a man whose own heart was as dry as the dust of the earth. I knew then that I must act, for if I did not, Edmund would perish in his silence. On the fourth night, the air in the room grew thick with tension. Edmund rose from his chair, his movements stiff and jerky, and walked to the desk where his manuscripts lay stacked in a disorganized pile. I saw his hand reach for the first volume, and a scream rose in my throat, but I was too late to stop him.

He held the book in his hands for a long moment, his fingers white-knuckled, his face contorted in an expression of such profound anguish that I could not bear to look upon it. Then, with a sudden, violent motion, he threw the book into the fire. The flames caught the pages instantly, curling them into black, curling scrolls of ash. I gasped, my hands flying to my mouth, but Edmund did not stop. He took another volume, then another, feeding them one by one into the hungry fire. The heat rose, warming his face, but it was not a warm light; it was the light of destruction, of finality. I wanted to rush to him, to wrestle the books from his hands, to beg him to remember the love he had poured into these lines, but I was paralyzed by a mixture of horror and a strange, terrible resignation. I saw that he was not merely burning paper; he was burning the very essence of his youthful self, the part of him that dared to dream, that believed in the power of the word to transmute suffering into beauty.

"Let it be," he said, his voice rough and broken, as if he had not spoken in years. He looked at me then, and in his eyes I saw not madness, but a cold, hard resolve that was far more frightening than any wild despair. "I have failed," he said. "I am not a poet. I will be a man of science. I will study the laws of nature, for they are fixed, they are true, and they do not lie."

The words struck me with the force of a physical blow. To turn away from the soul, from the mystery of the human heart, to embrace the cold, unfeeling logic of mechanics and matter—it was a betrayal of everything we had been. But I saw the firmness in his jaw, the set of his lips, and I knew that to argue with him now would be to drive him into a deeper, more dangerous isolation. I said nothing. I could not. I simply watched as the last of the manuscripts turned to ash, the smoke rising in thin, grey wisps that curled around the ceiling like the ghosts of his abandoned dreams.
