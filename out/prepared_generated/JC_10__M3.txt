The barque _Adelaide_, bound from Callao to London with a cargo of nitrate, had been lying motionless upon the face of the ocean for eleven days. The calm had fallen upon her suddenly, a breathless oppression dropping out of the stainless sky, and now the sails hung from the yards like dead things, heavy and shapeless, while the sun beat upon the deck with a violence that seemed to melt the very pitch in the seams. The heat was a physical weight, a palpable substance clinging to the masts and rigging, and over the whole vessel there brooded a silence so profound that the occasional creak of a block sounded with startling distinctness, like a groan wrung from the tortured fabric of the ship herself. The ocean presented the appearance of polished metal, stretching away on every side to a horizon that shimmered and danced in the quivering air, and against this immense and glittering emptiness the _Adelaide_ lay suspended, a dark, solitary speck, as though she had been fixed there by some decree of fate and abandoned to the slow consumption of time.

It was in the sixth week of this stagnation, when the water in the scuttle-butt had grown warm and foul to the taste, that the suspicion first crept through the forecastle like a sickness. Certain articles from the stores—tins of preserved meat, a quantity of biscuit, and, most damning of all, a small cask of water drawn from the spirit-room—had vanished. Vanished as though the sea itself had reached up through the planking and drawn them down into its depths. There had been no break in the steward's accounts, no sign of forced entry upon the hatch, and yet the provisions were gone, and the crew, fifty men pent up in that floating prison beneath the relentless sun, turned upon one another with the mute, searching hostility of animals. It was Walden, the ship's carpenter, upon whom the weight of their collective suspicion finally settled. He was a man of perhaps fifty, thick-set and powerful, with a grave, slow manner of speech that some mistook for sullenness. His face, weathered to the colour of old leather, carried an expression of settled melancholy, and his eyes, small and deeply set beneath a heavy brow, looked out upon the world with a sort of weary resignation. He had served in the _Adelaide_ for three voyages, and there was nothing in his record to mark him as a thief; yet he was a solitary man, keeping himself apart from the fellowship of the forecastle, and in the perverse logic of fear and suspicion this very solitude was construed as evidence against him.

Captain Hallowes, standing upon the poop, received the deputation of seamen with a countenance that betrayed nothing of the trouble working within him. He was a tall man, lean and angular, with a face sharpened by years of authority into a mask of habitual severity. He listened to the spokesman, a brawny fellow named Creedy, who stammered out the accusation with a kind of reluctant defiance, his cap twisting in his thick fingers, and when the man had finished, Hallowes spoke with a deliberate coldness that silenced the murmur of the assembled crew.

"I have heard you. You say the carpenter has taken from the stores. I ask you—what proof have you?"

There was a shifting among the men, a casting about of eyes. Creedy shuffled his feet.

"Proof, sir. We have not—there is no proof, as such. But who else? He has the run of the ship, sir. He goes below where we cannot. And the man—begging your pardon, sir—the man is not open with us."

Hallowes looked down at them from the break of the poop, and a singular expression, compounded of pity and a dark ironic comprehension, passed across his features. He knew, as only a commander can know, the dangerous chemistry of a becalmed ship—the way idleness and heat and the slow attrition of discipline ferment into madness. He had observed Walden closely during the preceding days, had noted the carpenter's untroubled movements about the deck, his calm occupation with the minor repairs that gave him excuse to range the vessel from stem to stern, and he had perceived nothing—nothing whatever—to confirm the accusation. Yet he understood, with the profound and melancholy insight of a man long familiar with the obscure depths of human nature, that innocence, in the face of collective suspicion, is a defence almost worthless. The men did not need proof. They needed a victim. The pressure of the calm, the thirst, the intolerable repetition of sunlit days and starlit nights upon that motionless ocean—these things had built up in them a need for action, for retribution, for some violent assertion that they were not mere passive objects upon which fate might exercise its caprice. And Walden, by his very isolation, had offered himself as the necessary object.

"You will return to your duties," Hallowes said. "I will investigate the matter. If the man is guilty, he shall be dealt with. But let no man take the law into his own hands. I will have no violence upon this ship."

They dispersed slowly, with an reluctance that was more menacing than any open defiance, and Hallowes, turning away, stood for a long time at the weather-rail, gazing out upon the glittering waste. His thoughts, returning again and again to the same weary centre, spoke within him with a solemn slowness. He could not prove the man innocent. Neither could he prove him guilty. And between these two negations there opened a gulf into which, he perceived with a gathering sense of helplessness, the whole discipline of his ship might presently slide. He had commanded men for twenty years, and he knew that authority rests not upon force but upon belief—upon the crew's faith that the man upon the poop possesses knowledge superior to their own, a knowledge that penetrates the obscurity of motives and actions and renders them intelligible. Strip away that belief, and the captain becomes merely a man standing higher than other men, conspicuous and alone.
