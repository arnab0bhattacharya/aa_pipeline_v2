The fog in London that evening did not merely obscure the gaslights; it seemed to consume them, turning the streets of Mayfair into a graveyard of grey ghosts. Elara pulled her shawl tighter, the wool scratching against her neck, as she stepped out of the hansom cab and into the foyer of the house of Sir Julian Thorne. She was not there as Elara, the wife of a clerk and the mother of two restless boys. She was there as Mrs. Arthur Pendelton, the sister of the celebrated author, Mr. A. Pendelton.

The invitation had arrived three days prior, sealed with the personal crest of the critic. It was an odd summons for a sister, especially one who was not yet married, yet Thorne had specifically requested "the family connection" to discuss the source of the *Iron Harbor* narrative. Elara had hesitated, her heart hammering a frantic rhythm against her ribs. To appear as herself was suicide. To appear as the sister was a calculated risk, a mask of polite boredom that she had perfected over the last five years of watching men discuss her life’s work as if it were a puzzle they had solved, not a life she had bled to write.

The dining room was a cavern of mahogany and low light. The air smelled of heavy tobacco, roasted game, and the metallic tang of heated metal. Sir Julian Thorne stood by the fireplace, a tall man with a face that had been sharpened by years of cutting remarks. He was flanked by three other men: Lord Halloway, a politician who drank too much sherry; Mr. Vane, a playwright who wrote only for money; and Dr. Aris, a physician who claimed to understand the human mind but rarely listened to it.

"Mrs. Pendelton," Thorne said, his voice smooth as oiled glass. He did not offer a hand. "You honor us. I confess, I am curious. Mr. Pendelton is a recluse. He does not dine, he does not drink, he does not speak. I have read his work, and I know his mind is a place of violent clarity. I suspect his sister shares that same blood. I suspect you know the secret of his silence."

Elara kept her face serene, a mask of pleasant incomprehension. "My brother is a creature of habit, Sir Julian. He prefers the quiet of his study to the noise of the world. I find the world noisy enough for two."

The men laughed. It was a dry, barking sound. Thorne poured the wine, the deep red liquid swirling in the crystal decanter. "Sit, please. The pheasant will be ready soon. But before we eat, I wish to speak to you, not as a host to a guest, but as one man to the woman who knows the artist."

Elara sat. The chair was uncomfortable, the wood hard against her thighs. She watched Thorne’s eyes. They were bright, predatory. He leaned forward, resting his elbows on the table, closing the distance between them.

"You know," Thorne began, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, "that I did not praise *Iron Harbor* because it was good. I praised it because it was dangerous. It was dangerous because it was written by a man who had never seen the sea, yet described it with the accuracy of a sailor. I have read between the lines, Mrs. Pendelton. I have seen the cracks in the armor."

Elara’s fingers tightened on the napkin in her lap. "I do not follow you, Sir Julian."

"You do, I think. You are the sister. You know the habits. You know the hours. You know that Mr. Pendelton writes from dawn until the candle gutters out, a state of trance that is not masculine. It is hysterical. It is the fever dream of a woman."

The silence that followed was thick. Lord Halloway stopped mid-sip. Mr. Vane lowered his fork. Dr. Aris looked up, intrigued.

Thorne continued, undeterred by the tension. "Let us be honest, Mrs. Pendelton. We are all gentlemen. We respect the natural order. A man’s mind is a fortress, built of logic, of structure, of iron will. A woman’s mind is a garden, overgrown, emotional, prone to caprice. When a woman writes, she does not construct; she vents. She pours out her feelings, her frustrations, her desire for attention. It is messy. It is weak."

He took a sip of wine, looking at her with a mixture of pity and contempt. "But this... this *Iron Harbor*. It is cold. It is precise. It is devoid of sentiment. It is the work of a man who has killed the woman in himself. Or, perhaps, it is the work of a woman who has learned to mimic the man she hates. I suspect the latter. I suspect that Mr. Pendelton is a ghost, a persona created by a woman who realized that her voice was too shrill to be heard in the public square, so she lowered her pitch and changed her name."

Elara felt a coldness spread through her chest, different from the London fog. It was the cold of the page, the cold of the ink that had dried on her hands a thousand times. She looked at Thorne, really looked at him, and saw the fear beneath the arrogance. He was afraid. He was afraid that if the voice he admired was not male, his own authority would crumble. He needed the author to be a man so that he could remain a critic. He needed the hierarchy intact.

"Sir Julian," Elara said, her voice steady, though her hands were trembling beneath the table. "If my brother is a ghost, then you are haunting him. You spend more time dissecting his soul than he does living it. You take his words, which are alive, and turn them into corpses to prove a point about biology and gender."

Thorne blinked, surprised by the sharpness of her tone. "I am merely observing the evidence, Mrs. Pendelton. The text is the evidence. And the text says 'man'."
