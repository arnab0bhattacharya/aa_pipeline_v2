Charlotte Vernon had long amused herself with the absurdities of her elder brother, James. Though James Vernon, Esq., of Foxcroft Park, was in every other respect a man of good understanding and unblemished character, he possessed the singular misfortune of an attachment, formed at Oxford, to one Mr. Henry Crawford. This gentleman, possessing a moderate fortune and a countenance that could not, by any stretch of politeness, be called handsome, had been a frequent guest at Foxcroft during the years of his minority, and Charlotte had found in him an inexhaustible source of ridicule. His manners were earnest, his conversation serious beyond his years, and his habit of delivering opinions upon agricultural improvement and the duties of landowners with the gravity of a man twice his age had afforded Charlotte such entertainment as she could scarcely have derived from a more deliberately comic character. She had communicated her observations freely, and with her usual turn for satirical exaggeration, to her particular friend, Miss Lucy Ashworth, who resided with her widowed mother in the neighbouring village of Emberton.

Miss Ashworth was a young woman of more feeling than wit, of a gentle disposition, and a mind which, if not penetrating, was at least sincerely candid. She listened to Charlotte’s lively sketches of Mr. Crawford’s deficiencies with the indulgence one grants to a clever friend, laughed because Charlotte expected it, and formed, perhaps, no very distinct notion of the gentleman at all. This had been the state of things when Mr. Crawford, having inherited a small estate from an uncle and been appointed to the bench of magistrates for the county, ceased to be the object of Charlotte’s active derision and became, instead, a person of acknowledged consequence in the neighbourhood.

It was shortly after this transformation that Charlotte, calling upon the Ashworths one morning in April, found Mr. Crawford already seated in the drawing-room, engaged with Lucy in a conversation which, upon her entrance, was discontinued with a promptness that would have told its own story to a less preoccupied observer. Charlotte, however, was preoccupied. She had observed, during the drive from Foxcroft, that the hedgerows along the Emberton road were beginning to show a greenness which promised an early spring, and her mind was full of plans for the relocation of a favourite rose-bush. She greeted Mr. Crawford with her customary ease, accepted a chair, and launched into an enquiry after Mrs. Ashworth’s health, which occupied the next several minutes.

It was only when Mr. Crawford rose to take his leave, and Charlotte observed the particular softness of Lucy’s farewell, and the lingering glance which accompanied it, that the truth presented itself to her with a suddenness which banished all thought of rose-bushes. She sat in silence for some moments after the door had closed behind him, and then turned to find Lucy regarding her with an expression of timid expectation.

“Charlotte,” said Lucy, with a blush which did honour to her sincerity, “I believe I must tell you that Mr. Crawford has this morning made me an offer.”

Charlotte received this communication with a composure which surprised them both. She congratulated her friend with warmth, enquired into the particulars with a discretion that did not betray the confusion of her thoughts, and, upon taking her leave a quarter of an hour later, walked to her carriage with the settled conviction that she was the most hypocritical creature in Surrey.

For it now appeared that she had, over the course of two years, described her future friend’s suitor as a man of insufferable dullness, of narrow understanding, and of manners so pompous as to render him ridiculous in any company of reasonable persons. She had declared, upon more than one occasion, that she would as soon marry the Foxcroft parish clerk as submit to an evening of Mr. Crawford’s discourse, and had invented, for Lucy’s entertainment, several apocryphal anecdotes of his vanity and absurdity which, upon reflection, she could not entirely reconcile with the truth. These communications had been received by Lucy in the spirit of friendly confidence, and Charlotte had never supposed that they would acquire any practical significance. She had spoken as one speaks of a distant and unimportant figure, and now that figure had stepped forward and proposed marriage to her dearest friend.

The question which now pressed upon her was whether she ought to confess. She turned the matter over during the drive home, and during the remainder of the afternoon, and during the greater part of the evening, with a growing sense that the path of honour and the path of comfort were not, in this instance, the same. To confess would be to diminish herself in Lucy’s eyes, to acknowledge a levity of judgement which might reasonably alarm a woman about to marry the man so described, and to introduce an awkwardness into their friendship at the very moment when it stood upon the threshold of change. Not to confess would be to live with the consciousness of having withheld information which, if known, might have influenced Lucy’s acceptance. Charlotte could not persuade herself that it would have influenced it, for Lucy’s affection appeared to be founded upon observations far more recent and far more favourable than any of Charlotte’s ancient caricatures. Yet the principle remained, and Charlotte, who prided herself upon a strict integrity, found it troublesome.

She was still revolving the subject the following morning when her brother entered the breakfast-room, and, having helped himself to coffee and toast, observed that Crawford had been at Emberton the preceding day.

“I conclude he went to see the Ashworths,” said James, with the indifference of a man who has no suspicion of his sister’s perplexity. “He mentioned something of it last week. I am glad of it. He has been wanting a wife these two years, and Miss Ashworth will do very well.”

“Do you think her suited to him?” Charlotte asked, with a careful neutrality.
