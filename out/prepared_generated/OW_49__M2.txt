Lady Millicent Ashworth was a woman who possessed the rare and enviable gift of being able to ignore the obvious. It was a talent that had kept her marriage to the Honourable Arthur Ashworth intact for twelve years, and had saved her nerves from the particular variety of boredom that afflicts those who live in Mayfair and die in it. The letter, which arrived on a Tuesday, was written on paper of a faintly offensive lavender hue, the ink a shade of purple that suggested either romantic delusion or a mild case of jaundice. It was signed, with a flourish that lacked both fluidity and taste, "Your Devoted Slave, Julian."

Julian was, of course, a nobody. He was one of those young men who believe that if they are sufficiently tragic, the world must stop to listen. He had a beard that looked as if it had been grown in a damp cellar, and eyes that constantly attempted to weep but usually only managed to look rheumy. He had written to Lady Millicent three times previously, each letter more melodramatic than the last, culminating in the current epistle which threatened that if she did not grant him an audience, he would plunge a dagger into his own heart and leave the weapon in her drawing room as a souvenir.

Lady Millicent read it while sipping a glass of champagne that had gone flat, and felt a sensation of mild amusement. It was so terribly like the opera. One liked a plot, even if the plot was carried out by a man who wore his cuffs too high. She did not reply. To reply would be to acknowledge the existence of a ghost, and Lady Millicent had learned long ago that if one ignores a ghost, it eventually fades away, having run out of things to say and no one to say them to.

Her husband, however, was a different matter. The Honourable Arthur Ashworth was a man of immense dignity and very little imagination. He was the sort of person who read the *Times* with the seriousness of a monk reading scripture, and who believed that the stability of the Empire depended entirely on the correct folding of a handkerchief. He was also, by a quirk of domestic arrangement that he considered natural and which Millicent found merely tedious, the custodian of her correspondence.

Arthur found the letter in the waste-paper basket where Millicent had casually discarded it, along with a menu for a dinner she had no intention of attending and a bill for a dressmaker who had overcharged for a hem. He held the lavender paper up to the light, as if expecting to see a watermark of doom.

"My dear," he said, his voice carrying that particular note of wounded pride that men reserve for the discovery of their own irrelevance. "I see you have been receiving rather dangerous correspondence."

Lady Millicent looked up from her fan, which was painted with a scene of the fall of Troy. "Oh, is that so? I do not remember. It must have been from the florist. They are always so dramatic about the delivery of peonies."

"It is not from the florist, Millicent. It is from a young man who intends to commit suicide."

"Suicide is such a vulgar way to make a point," Millicent said, adjusting a pearl earring. "And frankly, if he does it, it will be a nuisance. The police will be here, the newspapers will make a fuss, and I shall have to explain to the *Morning Post* why a respectable wife has attracted the attention of a lunatic. I should think he is better off leaving the knife at home and keeping his dignity."

Arthur’s face turned the color of a ripe tomato. "Dignity! You speak of dignity? He threatens to take his own life, and you speak of the *Morning Post*! My dear, you are being incredibly reckless. If this man acts on his threat, and we are not prepared, it will be a scandal of the first magnitude. We must see him. We must reason with him. We must perhaps offer him a position, or a loan, or a very strong cup of tea."

"A position?" Millicent laughed, a sound like silver bells dropping on marble. "Arthur, you cannot employ a man because he is trying to be poetic. That would set a precedent. Next thing, you will be hiring a gardener because he has written a bad sonnet about the rose garden."

"Millicent, do not be so flippant. This is a matter of honor. A man does not threaten death lightly. It is a sign of deep distress. We must act with kindness."

"Kindness is the last refuge of the incompetent," Millicent said, returning to her champagne. "If he comes, I shall have the door locked. If he knocks, I shall tell the footman I am in my bath. If he breaks down the door, I shall scream until the neighbors call the police. It is a perfectly sound strategy."

Arthur was not a man who appreciated sound strategies, especially when they involved the potential for violence in his own drawing room. He was a man who preferred to smooth things over, to apply a layer of varnish to the rough edges of reality. He decided, with the quiet determination of a man who believes he is saving the world from a minor inconvenience, to invite Julian in himself.

The next morning, Julian arrived. He looked even worse than his letters suggested. His hair was disheveled, his coat was stained with what appeared to be ink and possibly blood, and he carried a small, velvet-lined box under his arm. He stood on the step, trembling, like a leaf in a gale.

Arthur opened the door. "Mr. Julian, I believe. Lady Ashworth is indisposed, but I am at your service. Please, come in. The drawing room is that way."

Julian did not move. He looked at Arthur with eyes that were wide and terrified. "Is she there?" he whispered. "Does she know I am coming?"
