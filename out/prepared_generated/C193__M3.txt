The morning post brought two letters to Blackwood Priory, both scented with lavender, both directed to Mr. Edward Ashby in the cramped, insistent hand of his eldest sister. He broke the seal of the first as he stood by the window of his modest bookroom, watching a thin rain fall upon the Berkshire fields. The letter was, as he had suspected, a treatise upon his future. Mrs. Helen Vance, widow of a Bristol merchant, possessed twenty thousand pounds. She was residing at present with her aunt at nearby Harlow Grange, and she was, in Louisa’s estimation, everything that was amiable. Louisa’s letter did not so much suggest as command. She and their younger sister Clara had arranged an invitation for Edward to dine at the Grange that very evening. The sisters were staying at Harlow as guests of the aunt, and they had engineered the entire scheme with a precision that left Edward no room for refusal.

He folded the letter and set it on the desk. He was a man of nine-and-twenty, possessed of an ancient name and an estate that could barely support its own roof. He did not blame his sisters for their machinations. Louisa and Clara had endured years of pinched economies, of gowns turned twice and gloves mended until the thread gave out. They had married, mercifully, to men of moderate fortune, and now they sought to secure their brother. Yet the mechanism of their interference chafed him. They had selected Helen Vance as a man selects a horse, evaluating her points and her purse, and they expected Edward to step quietly into the traces.

He had met the widow twice. The first occasion had been at the Michaelmas ball, where she had danced with a composed grace that drew his eye. The second had been a chance encounter in the village, when her carriage had stuck in the mud and he had offered his assistance. She had not simpered or fluttered. She had stood beside the wheel, directing her coachman with a quiet authority that he found entirely captivating. Her face was not beautiful in the classical mode, but it possessed a warmth, a directness of expression that made him feel, for a disorienting moment, that she saw him rather than his name.

He did not dislike Helen Vance. That was the crux of his dilemma. If she had been silly or vain or cruel, he could have opposed his sisters with a clear conscience. He could have written to Louisa and declared the match impossible, and the whole affair would have dissolved into a brief family friction that healed over a season. But he liked her, and that liking complicated everything, because to act upon it would be to concede that his sisters had been right.

The second letter, he discovered upon opening it, was from Clara, and it was if anything more pointed than the first. She wrote that Mrs. Vance had enquired after him specifically. Clara urged him to wear his blue coat and to remember that the widow was fond of music. She even suggested the pieces he might offer to play. Edward set this letter beside the first and stood for a long moment in the grey light, feeling the weight of his own indecision pressing upon him like the low sky outside.

He dined at Harlow Grange that evening. The house was a handsome Queen Anne structure of rosy brick, set behind iron gates on a rise of ground that overlooked the river. The drawing room was warmly lit, and he found the party already gathered when he was shown in. Louisa rose to greet him with a kiss that was also a surveillance, her eyes sweeping his coat, his cravat, his general presentation. Clara hovered behind her, smiling with an eagerness that bordered on anxiety.

Helen Vance sat near the fire. She wore a gown of dark silk that caught the firelight, and her hair was dressed simply, without the elaborate ornament that Louisa favoured. She rose when he entered and offered her hand with a smile that was warm but not eager. He observed, as he took it, that there was a trace of irony in her expression, as though she understood precisely what kind of evening this was and had decided to endure it with grace.

Dinner was an exercise in barely concealed engineering. Louisa placed him beside the widow and directed the conversation toward topics calculated to display his virtues. She spoke of his management of the estate, of his skill with horses, of the improvements he had made to the tenants’ cottages. Edward felt himself being presented like a piece of merchandise, his qualities enumerated as though a bill of sale were being drafted. Clara contributed by praising his singing, his reading aloud, his steady temper. Throughout it all, Helen Vance listened with attention, asking occasional questions that were so perceptive they cut through the sisters’ performance and struck at something genuine.

She asked him whether he enjoyed the work of the estate, or whether he felt it a duty. He paused, struck by the distinction. No one had ever asked him that. He answered honestly, that he found satisfaction in the labour but loneliness in the house, that the rooms echoed with the memory of a larger family now scattered. She nodded, and something passed between them, a current of recognition that had nothing to do with Louisa’s calculations.

After dinner, the ladies withdrew, and Edward sat alone with Harlow’s port, staring into the ruby liquid and feeling the familiar pull of obligation. His sisters had invested hope in this match. The family needed it. The roof of the east wing leaked, and the tenants’ rents had fallen again after a poor harvest. He could marry Helen Vance and solve everything, and she would make him a good wife. He was certain of it. She was intelligent, composed, kind. But the thought of giving his sisters the satisfaction, of confirming that their scheming had produced the desired result, made something tighten in his chest.
