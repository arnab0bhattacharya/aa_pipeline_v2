Arthur Pell woke on the Tuesday to the sound of silence. He lay in his bed in the modest semi-detached house on Beadle Lane, listening for the eight-fifteen express that ordinarily rattled the sash windows in their frames, and heard nothing. When he drew back the curtain he saw that the world had altered. The river Braid, which normally ran in a polite, contained channel at the bottom of the gardens, had climbed overnight and swallowed the lower meadows. Where there had been an orderly vista of allotments and washing lines, there was now a slow, brown expanse of water, glinting under a pale morning sun. He dressed in his usual grey suit, ate a boiled egg, and walked to the station, finding the roads strangely empty. At the ticket office, the clerk told him that the line was submerged two miles south and no trains would run until further notice. Arthur went home and sat in his parlour, uncertain what a man was meant to do when the mechanisms of his life simply halted.

By the afternoon, the water had crept up over the lane itself. Arthur stood at his front gate with his neighbour, Edwin Crane, who worked as a senior clerk at the bank in Marlowe and who possessed a neatly trimmed moustache and an unwavering faith in procedure. Crane peered at the water, which was lapping gently at the kerbstones, and declared that the council would surely have the pumps operational by evening. He had sent a telegram to the municipal offices, he said, and expected a swift reply. Arthur nodded, wanting to believe him, though he noticed that the telegram boy had abandoned his bicycle a hundred yards away and was wading through the shallows with his leather satchel held above his head. The postmaster, Mr Hibbert, came striding down the middle of the road in his shirtsleeves, his face flushed. He told them that the sorting office was under three feet of water and that the evening post would not be dispatched. He said it as though he were announcing the collapse of a government, and Arthur supposed that in a small way it was. The three of them stood in a silence punctuated only by the soft, unnatural lapping of the river against the garden walls, and waited for someone in authority to arrive and tell them what to do. Nobody came.

On the second day, Wednesday, the water had risen to the doorsteps. Arthur woke to find his cellar flooded, the coal floating in dark, glistening heaps against the ceiling. He spent the morning carrying his ledgers and insurance manuals up to the first floor, stacking them neatly on the landing, a futile gesture of order against the encroaching disorder. From his bedroom window he could see the broader extent of the disaster. The entire valley had become a lake. The spire of St Agatha’s church rose from the flood like a stranded vessel, and he could see the tops of the hedgerows trailing in the current like the hair of drowned women. A boat appeared, rowed by a young man Arthur did not recognise, and it moved slowly along what had been the high street, its oars dipping into the brown water that covered the shopfronts. Arthur called down to him, asking if the authorities had sent him, and the young man shouted back that he was a farmer’s son from the hills and that he had not seen any officials. He said that the bridge at Lower Braid had been swept away in the night and that the town was cut off entirely. He rowed on, leaving Arthur standing at his window, grasping the curtain.

Edwin Crane’s faith in procedure began to erode visibly. He came to Arthur’s back door that afternoon, having waded through thigh-deep water, his trousers soaked to the waist, his moustache drooping. He said that he had received no reply to his telegram and that the police constable, who had been seen briefly the previous morning, had vanished. There were rumours, Crane said, that the authorities in London were unaware of the severity of the situation, or that if they were aware, they did not consider a few flooded suburbs to warrant intervention. He sat in Arthur’s kitchen, water dripping from his coat onto the linoleum, and stared at the table with the expression of a man who has suddenly discovered that the floor beneath him is not solid. Arthur made tea on the paraffin stove, since the gas had failed, and they drank it in silence, listening to the house creak and settle as though it were a ship adjusting to the tide.

By evening, the water had entered the ground floor. Arthur sat on the stairs and watched it creep across the hall carpet, dark and deliberate, carrying with it a strand of pond weed and a child’s wooden horse that must have floated from some neighbouring garden. The room looked different from this new perspective, the furniture taking on a strange, bloated significance, as though the water were revealing a truth about his possessions that he had never wished to see. He thought of his office in Marlowe, the orderly rows of desks, the brass lamp on his desk, the calendar on the wall that he turned each morning with a small, satisfying motion, and he wondered whether the office too was under water, whether the files and policies and actuarial tables were even now dissolving into the flood. The thought disturbed him less than he would have expected. There was a kind of liberty in it, he realised, a stripping away of the scaffolding that had held his days in place, and though it frightened him, it also left him feeling strangely light, as though he had put down a weight he had not known he was carrying.
