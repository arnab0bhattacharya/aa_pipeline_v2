The light in the drawing-room was already failing, that grey London light which seems to drain the colour from the wallpaper and the faces of the people in it, so that for a moment Evelyn felt she was sitting in a room made of ash. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked with a dull, persistent thud—thud—thud—like a slow heart beating in the silence of the house, and she wondered how it was possible for time to pass so heavily and yet to slip away so entirely before one had grasped it. She had meant to tell him yesterday. She had meant to tell him this morning. But the morning had filled itself with the small, urgent duties of the day—the polishing of the silver, the ordering of the fish, the arranging of the flowers—and now the evening had arrived, wearing its best dress, and she was sitting in the armchair opposite him, and the words were still lodged somewhere behind her teeth, hard and cold as stones.

"Your mother has been asking about the place-cards," said Arthur, looking up from his paper. His voice was kind, always kind, that steady, dependable kindness which she had once thought was the whole of love and which now felt like a weight upon her chest, pressing the breath out of her. "She wants to know if we should put the aunts together or apart."

"Oh—apart, I think," said Evelyn. "They will quarrel over the soup if they are together." She smiled, but the smile felt like a mask that had been moulded to her face and would not come off.

Arthur nodded and made a note in his little book, that little black book in which he recorded everything—the price of coal, the dates of birthdays, the names of the horses his father had backed at Newmarket—and she looked at him, really looked at him, at the broad, honest forehead and the patient eyes and the hands that were so careful with everything they touched, and she thought, I cannot bear it. I cannot bear to sit here and say nothing. But the dinner is at eight, and the guests are coming, and the salmon is already in the ice-box, and how can one break an engagement between the fish and the soup?

"It will be a nice evening," said Arthur, folding his paper. "Your Aunt Gertrude is bringing her new recipe for the sauce. She says it is quite the thing in Paris."

"Yes," said Evelyn. "Quite the thing." She watched the way the light caught the silver frame on the table, the frame that held the photograph of the two of them taken last summer at Bexhill, and she thought how strange it was that a photograph could fix a moment so completely and yet say nothing at all of what was really happening inside the people in it. In the photograph they were smiling, standing on the shingle with the sea behind them, and no one could see that she had been thinking of the way the gulls were crying, crying as if they were trying to tell her something she could not understand.

The bell rang then—a sharp, sudden sound that cut through the quiet of the room—and Arthur stood up, smoothing his waistcoat.

"That will be your mother," he said. "Shall I go down?"

"Yes," said Evelyn. "Yes, do." And she was alone again, alone with the ticking clock and the failing light and the great, unspoken thing that sat between them like a piece of furniture no one had thought to remove.

She could hear them in the hall now, the voices rising and falling like the waves on a shore, her mother’s high, anxious twitter and her father’s deeper, slower rumble, and then the aunts arriving in a rustle of silk and the smell of lavender, and she thought, I shall have to go down. I shall have to go down and smile and say how well you look and how lovely the flowers are and is not this a beautiful evening for a dinner, and all the time the words will be sitting in my throat like a stone.

She stood up and looked at herself in the mirror over the fireplace. The face that looked back was pale and strange, the eyes too bright, the mouth too set, and she thought, Who is this person? Who is this woman in the black dress who is about to sit down to dinner with a man she no longer loves? But the glass said nothing. It only reflected the room behind her, the curtains drawn against the dark, the lamps being lit one by one, and the shadows gathering in the corners like guests who had come too early and did not know where to sit.

Downstairs the table was already laid, the silver shining in the candlelight, the flowers standing stiff and white in their vases, and the family was assembling in the drawing-room with the little glasses of sherry in their hands. Her mother was talking about the weather, her father was talking about the price of petrol, and the aunts were talking about the Queen, and Arthur was standing by the fireplace, so solid and so kind, and he looked at her and smiled and said, "There you are, my dear. We were wondering where you had got to."

"I was just looking at the light," said Evelyn. "It is going so quickly."

"Yes," said Arthur. "It is." And he offered her his arm, and she took it, feeling the warmth of his sleeve, the steady beat of his pulse beneath the cloth, and she thought, I cannot do it. I cannot do it now. Perhaps after the salmon. Perhaps when the aunts have gone home and the house is quiet again. But the clock was ticking, and the guests were hungry, and the evening was already beginning, and she walked into the dining-room on the arm of a man who did not know that the world was about to change.
