If any returning wanderer, laden with the golden spoils of distant Botany Bay, had been rash enough to anticipate that the village of Muddleton-in-the-Marsh would turn out to greet his triumphant arrival with a peal of the parish bells and a chorus of grateful tears, that wanderer would have found himself most cruelly deceived. The world is a sufficiently selfish theatre, and the English village, I fear, is the most selfish box in the whole edifice. I had left Muddleton a ragged, hungry, eager-eyed boy of twelve, with a shilling in my pocket and a half-penny bun in my hand; I returned to it a robust young man of twenty-eight, wearing a good broadcloth coat, with a heavy purse of Australian gold in my waistcoat, and a very profound conviction in my breast that the past is a country which, however eagerly we may seek to revisit it, has invariably altered its borders.

It was a sharp, grey afternoon in November when the heavy stagecoach deposited me at the door of the Drowsy Drover, the village inn. The inn itself appeared to have fallen into a profound slumber during my absence, and to have forgotten to wake up. A thick, yellow fog hung over the duck-pond, giving the geese an appearance of swimming in pea-soup, and the ancient elm-tree by the blacksmith’s forge stretched its bare branches across the murky sky like the crooked fingers of a beggar importuning the clouds. I walked across the cobbled yard, my boots ringing with a confidence I did not entirely feel, and pushed open the low oak door of the taproom.

Within, the air was thick with the fumes of malt and the odour of damp worsted. Mr. Bumper, the landlord—a man who had formerly been as round and red as a Sussex apple, but who was now fallen away to a mere meagre ghost of his former self, with a nose of a pale and melancholy blue—stood wiping a glass behind the bar. Beside him, huddled over a smoking fire of damp peat, sat Mr. Grumple, the parish clerk, a gentleman who had long ago decided that the universe was a mistake, and that it was his duty to look as miserable as possible to reflect the fact.

‘Mr. Bumper,’ said I, advancing with a smile. ‘You do not remember me, I dare say. I am young Pipkin.’

At the mention of that name, Mr. Bumper dropped his glass, which shivered upon the floor, and Mr. Grumple started so violently that he let fall his pipe into the ashes.

‘Pipkin!’ gasped the landlord, gazing at my broadcloth and my glossy boots with a wild, affrighted eye. ‘Not the runaway? Not the transported one?’

‘Transported I was, sir, though not by the law,’ said I, stoutly. ‘I went out to seek my fortune, and I have brought it back with me. Come, Mr. Bumper, surely you will drink a glass with an old neighbour?’

But instead of proffering the flask, Mr. Bumper retreated a pace, and held up both hands as if to ward off a pestilence. He was, I perceived, ashamed of me. He was terrified that my new-found wealth was but a vulgar accident, and that to acknowledge the ragged boy of yesteryear would be to compromise his own respectability. He muttered something about having to see to a brewing in the cellar, and fled precipitately through a side-door, leaving me standing in the middle of the taproom, a prodigal son rejected not for his sins but for his sovereigns.

I left the inn, my heart considerably heavier than my pockets, and bent my steps towards the cottage of Mr. Sallowby, the butcher, who had once been a great crony of my poor father’s. But here, too, I found the door of human fellowship shut in my face. Mr. Sallowby was carving a melancholy-looking joint of mutton in his shop; but no sooner did he behold my countenance through the window than he dropped his cleaver with a clatter, and retreated into the dark recesses of his premises, from whence he did not emerge until he judged I had passed by. The world, I reflected, is very willing to embrace a man when he has nothing; but let him return with a fortune, and he becomes an object of suspicion, a moral monster, a thing to be shunned.

I wandered up the village street, a stranger in the very place where I had first drawn breath. The houses seemed to have shrunk; the people who passed me in the lanes looked at me askance, as if I were a ghost risen from the churchyard to demand a share of their worldly goods. The very dogs barked at me with a peculiar, distrustful intonation, as though they, too, had been warned against the Australian adventurer.

At length, my footsteps, impelled by a melancholy instinct, led me to the low, thatched cottage at the far end of the lane, where the village schoolmistress, Miss Prudence Pinch, had kept her little academy for the children of the poor. I had not seen her since I was a child, but I remembered her as a tall, angular woman, with a sharp nose, a sharper eye, and a heart that, though concealed beneath a formidable exterior of spelling-books and ferules, was as soft and warm as a newly baked muffin.

The garden gate was still on its single hinge, creaking dolefully in the wind. I pushed it open, walked up the moss-grown path, and tapped at the door. It was opened by Miss Pinch herself. She was older, and her hair was white as the snow upon the neighbouring fields; but her eye was as bright as ever, and she stood erect, holding a well-thorned reading-book in her hand.

‘Miss Pinch,’ said I, taking off my hat. ‘You do not remember me, I fear.’

She looked at me long and earnestly, without a trace of that shrinking fear which had marked the demeanour of the landlord and the butcher. She looked into my eyes, not at my coat.
