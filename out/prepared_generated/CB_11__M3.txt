The wind from the moors above Thorncroft came tearing down into the valley like a living thing, dark with soot and heavy with the threat of rain. It rattled the high, narrow windows of the mill-owner’s parlour, making the meagre fire in the grate leap and shudder. I sat in the far corner of the room, a poor rector’s niece, occupying the lowest seat in the household, yet burning with a fierce, indignant fire that the soot and gloom of the West Riding could not quench. I was a dependent, a drudge of all works, set to mend the household linen and tutor the children of the overlooker; but I possessed a spirit that refused to be crushed into the dirt of the manufacturing district.

By the hearth stood Miss Clara Vernon. She was the heiress of the district’s wealthiest landowner, clad in a gown of fine blue merino that fell in faultless folds, her golden hair braided with a precision that bespoke the waiting-maid’s skill. She was beautiful, gentle, and possessed of that serene, untroubled grace which is the natural inheritance of those who have never known a want. Yet there was a goodness in her—a candid, earnest piety—that had drawn my solitary heart to hers in an unlikely, deeply felt friendship. We were sisters in spirit, though the world declared her my superior in every outward station.

Between us, pacing the worn Turkey carpet with the restless, heavy stride of a caged animal, stood Mr. Elias Thorne. He was the master of the mill, a man whose countenance bore the deep, prematurely hewn lines of relentless toil, of anxious days and sleepless nights. His mill stood but a quarter-mile distant, a blackened pile of brick whose tall chimneys breathed a constant, suffocating pall over the valley. I knew—and I burned with the bitter knowledge—that his business tottered on the very precipice of ruin. The failure of a London house had struck a fatal blow to his credit; the machinery was老旧, the hands were unpaid, and the bailiffs circled the estate like ravenous wolves.

I loved him. I, Eleanor Mayhew, loved this stern, care-worn man; not with a soft, smiling, fashionable affection, but with an agony of soul, with a vital, stormy devotion that sprang from the very depths of my being. I knew he was sinking, and I would have given my life’s blood to buoy him up; but I was poor, obscure, and utterly powerless.

"You are very silent, Mr. Thorne," said Miss Vernon, breaking the profound stillness that had fallen upon the room. Her voice was soft, yet it carried a tremor of apprehension. "You wear the look of a man who bears a heavy weight. Will you not share it with us?"

He paused in his stride, and turning his dark, haggard face towards her, a faint, bitter smile crossed his lips. "The weight is mine to bear, Miss Vernon. It is the burden of a man who sees the fabric of his life’s labour crumbling to dust beneath his feet. The mill must close. I am ruined."

"I cannot believe it," she murmured, her delicate hands clasping tightly together. "Surely there is a remedy. My father—"

"Your father, madam, has already advanced me more than prudence dictates," he interrupted, with a sudden, haughty flash of the eye that spoke to a pride as fierce and unbending as my own. "I will not beg. I will not be the pensioner of your family’s wealth."

At that moment, the door opened, and a soot-stained errand boy thrust in a letter. Mr. Thorne seized it, tore it open, and as his eyes scanned the page, the little remaining colour drained from his face. He stood like a man struck by a physical blow. "It is finished," he said, in a low, hollow tone. "The final demand. In a fortnight, the machinery will be seized, and I shall be a beggar."

Miss Vernon rose, her blue eyes swimming with unshed tears. "Mr. Thorne—Elias—do not speak so! You must let me help you. I have means of my own, left to me by my grandmother. It is but a trifle, yet it might stay the bailiffs. Say you will accept it!"

He drew back, his whole frame rigid. "I am a ruined man, Miss Vernon. To accept a loan from you now would be little better than theft. Your father would despise me, and you—you would learn to despise me, too, when you saw your fortune swallowed up in the mire of my debts."

"I should despise you if you refused!" she cried, a sudden, unwonted fire lighting up her gentle features. "Pride is a cruel sin when it stands in the way of salvation!"

I could sit silent no longer. The sight of her pleading, of his stubborn, agonized refusal, roused the tempest within me. I rose from my low seat in the shadow, and stepped into the firelight.

"You speak of pride, Miss Vernon," I said, and my voice rang sharp and clear, vibrating with the intensity of my suppressed emotion. "You speak of it lightly, because you have never felt its iron bite. You do not know what it is to be wholly dependent, to owe your daily bread to the caprice of others. But Mr. Thorne knows. He knows that to take your money would be to forge a chain that would gall him to the end of his days."

They both turned to look at me, startled by the sudden outburst from the obscure corner. Mr. Thorne’s dark eyes fixed upon my face with a searching, coruscating glance. I met his gaze; I felt my own flash back, fierce and unquenched. I knew that he read there the secret of my soul—the love, the anguish, the fierce, unavailing desire to aid him.

"And what would you have me do, Miss Mayhew?" he asked, after a pause, his voice husky. "Submit my neck to the yoke of obligation?"
