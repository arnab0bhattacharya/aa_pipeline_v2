In the heart of the royal city of Aethelgard, where the spires of the palace scraped the clouds and the river of gold flowed through the merchants' quarter, there lived a clockmaker named Silas Vane. His workshop was a cavern of ticking, a place where ten thousand brass hearts beat in perfect synchronization. Gears meshed with gears, pendulums traced their hypnotic arcs, and the air smelled of oil and polished bronze. Silas was a master of his craft, a man who understood the language of escapements and mainsprings as fluently as a poet understands verse. But the clock he had built in the back room, hidden beneath a velvet tarpaulin, was no ordinary timepiece. It was a clock that could stop time itself.

He had not built it for glory or for profit. He had built it because he was old, and his hands were beginning to tremble, and he wanted to know if the moments he had left could be stretched, folded, made to last. He had built it out of fear, which is the engine that drives all great and foolish inventions.

Word of the clock reached King Matthias III through a network of spies and gossips that wound through Aethelgard like bindweed. The king was seventy-two years old, and his flesh hung from his bones like drapery from a ruined frame. He had conquered three neighboring kingdoms, sired nine children, and built monuments to his own glory that would stand for centuries. But death did not care about monuments. Death was coming for him with the same steady, unhurried pace of the pendulums in Silas's shop, and Matthias III, who had never in his life accepted anything he did not want, refused to accept this.

The king's guards came at dawn. They brought Silas before the throne in chains, though the chains were made of silk and the throne room was made of marble and gold. The king sat upon the Obsidian Seat, his crown resting on a head of thin white hair, his eyes still sharp as broken glass.

"You have built a clock that stops time," the king said. It was not a question.

Silas bowed. "Your Majesty, I have built a clock that can pause the forward march of moments. But I must warn—"

"I will have it," the king said. "I will have it brought to the palace. You will operate it for me. When I command, you will stop time so that I may live outside it, untouched by decay, forever young in the frozen world."

Silas felt the weight of the workshop in his bones, the weight of every gear and spring he had ever shaped. "Your Majesty," he said carefully, "the clock does not work as you imagine. When time stops, everything stops. The sun hangs in the sky. The rivers cease to flow. The birds hang in the air like painted figures. You would not age, it is true. But you would not live, either. You would simply persist."

"Persistence is enough," the king said. "I have persisted through wars and plagues and betrayals. I will persist through this."

"There is a price," Silas said. The words came out like the toll of a solemn bell, low and resonant, filling the throne room. "Every moment you steal from time must be paid for. The clock draws its power from the lives of those within its radius. When you stop time, the people of Aethelgard will not simply pause. Their hearts will slow. Their breath will thin. For every hour you spend in the frozen world, a day will be taken from the lives of everyone in the city. Use the clock too long, and the city will die around you while you stand unchanged in the silence."

The king stared at him for a long moment. Then he laughed, a dry, papery sound like autumn leaves skittering across stone. "You ask me to weigh my life against the lives of my subjects? I have done that every day for forty years, clockmaker. A king's life is worth a city. A king's life is worth a kingdom. Bring the clock."

The clock was installed in the king's private chamber, a room at the top of the highest tower of the palace. It was an elegant thing, for all its terrible power: a tall case of dark walnut, a face of silvered brass, and a single hand that moved not around the hours but between two positions marked NOW and NEVER. Silas was commanded to stay by the clock and operate it at the king's pleasure.

For the first week, the king used the clock sparingly. He would stop time for an hour, perhaps two, and walk through the frozen palace. He would stand before the mirror and watch his reflection, unchanging, eternal. He would walk through the gardens where the fountain spray hung in midair like crystal sculpture, and he would feel the thrill of existing outside the natural order.

But soon an hour was not enough. The king began to stop time for days. He would sit in his frozen chamber and read, or simply sit in silence, feeling the stillness settle around him. In the city below, people began to weaken. Old men and women died in their sleep. Children grew pale and listless. The physicians attributed it to a bad season, to damp air, to the alignment of the stars. They did not know that their lives were being siphoned away, drop by drop, hour by hour, to feed an old man's terror of the dark.

Silas watched the city from the tower window and felt the guilt settle into him like rust into iron. He had built the machine. He had given the weapon to the tyrant. He tried to speak to the king, to warn him again, but Matthias III would not hear it.

"I am the king," he said, waving his hand. "The city exists to serve me. If they die sooner so that I may live longer, that is the natural order."
