The sea gave up the soldier at dawn, depositing him on the black sand of Playa Maldita like a slab of refuse from a frigate’s hold. He was face down, his wool coat heavy with brine, one boot lost to the current. Elena pulled him above the tide line by his collar, her bare feet slipping on the wet stones. She was not delicate—ten years of pulling nets alongside Joaquin had given her hands like rope and a back forged from misery. When she turned the man over, the waves had already claimed his face, peeling the skin to a white, featureless pulp. But his chest was buttoned tight, and beneath the oilskin, she felt the hard rectangle of a leather satchel. The buckle was tarnished green. She forced it open with a knife. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were dozens of letters. The paper was swollen and wavy, but the ink had held. She traced a finger over the strange, slanted marks. They meant nothing to her. Elena had never learned more than signing her name at the notary’s office in San Lorieno, pressing her thumbprint beside it like a branded animal. She looked at the dead man. He wore no insignia. His buttons were plain bone. Whoever he served, the sea had stripped him of his colors. She rewrapped the letters and carried the satchel back to her hut, her heart beating a strange, heavy rhythm against her ribs. Information was currency in the republic of Valverde, and currency could buy safety, or buy death.

The village of Playa Maldita had sixty souls, mostly widows and old men, the young having been scraped clean by five years of civil war between the Republic’s federalistas in San Lorieno and the rebel ejército del pueblo hiding in the eastern hills. Elena kept to herself. Joaquin had been conscripted by the federals two years prior; a captain had come through with a ledger and a rifle, pointing at every man between fifteen and fifty. They took Joaquin on a Tuesday. She received no letter, no notice, only a neighbor who had seen the press gang march him toward the interior. She assumed he was dead. The sea, at least, returned what it took. The land swallowed men whole and left no bones. She boiled water and set the letters on the grate above the fire to dry, pressing them flat with a smoothing stone. As the paper uncurled, she could see that some bore heavy official stamps in red, while others were written on cheap, rough pulp, folded into tight squares. She separated them into two piles by the feel of the paper and the look of the marks. Then she sat and considered her problem. She needed a reader. There were three in Playa Maldita who could parse letters. Don Pelayo, the retired harbormaster, was loyal to the Republic. His son wore a federal uniform in San Lorieno, and he flew the national flag from his porch on independence day, the only man in the village to do so. The second was Senora Vidal, who ran the dry-goods store and was rumored to pass messages for the rebels. Her husband had been executed by firing squad in the plaza of San Lorieno three years ago, and she wore black ever since, a walking monument to grievance. The third was Padre Lucio, the village priest, who claimed neutrality but took communion wine from both sides and was seen blessing rebel wounded as readily as federal patrols. Elena did not trust any of them. Trust was a luxury for peacetime, and peacetime was a myth. But the letters were a danger to her as long as they sat unread in her hut. If the federals learned she possessed correspondence meant for the rebels, they would hang her as a courier. If the rebels learned she had letters addressed to the government, they would call her a collaborator and take her house for a field hospital. She had to know what the dead man carried.

She went to Padre Lucio first. The church was a mud-brick building with a roof of palm thatch, cool and dark inside, smelling of tallow and damp adobe. Lucio was a thin man with a tremor in his hands that he attributed to age but that Elena attributed to drink. She set the satchel on the pew beside him and said she had found it on a drowned man. Lucio looked at the bag, then at her, and his fingers shook worse than usual. He asked where the body was. She said she had buried it above the tideline, in the sand, without markings. He opened the satchel and withdrew the first letter, holding it close to his candle. His lips moved silently. Then he stopped. He folded the letter and put it back. He told her the letters were dangerous, that she should burn them, that no good came from the words of dead soldiers. He refused to read further. Elena watched his face. He was afraid, but it was not the fear of a man who had read military secrets. It was the fear of a man who did not want to know. She took the satchel and left.

She went to Don Pelayo. He sat on his porch in a chair made of driftwood, watching the sea as though expecting his son to walk up the beach. He was proud of his literacy and reached for the letters with an eager hand. She watched him read the first, his lips tightening. He read the second, and his face went the color of old wax. He told her the letters were addressed to the rebel command, containing troop positions and the names of federal sympathizers in the coastal villages. He said she must take them immediately to the federal garrison in San Lorieno. He said it was her duty. He said the Republic would reward her. Elena asked if any letters were addressed to the government.
