It was upon a raw, foggy morning in the latter days of November, when the yellow vapours hung thick over the chimneypots of Kensington, that the matter of the Khyber stranger was first laid before me. I had taken up my pen to complete a set of notes upon a case of arterial disease, when my servant announced that a gentleman desired to see me upon a matter of urgency.

I had not long to wait. The door opened to admit a man whose appearance was so strongly marked by the Indian sun that I was instantly transported in mind to the burning plains and rugged passes of the North-West Frontier. He was tall and very lean, with a complexion of a deep, dusty brown. His clothes were of a decent English cut but worn threadbare at the cuffs, and he carried in his right hand a serviceable malacca cane that had been mended with a band of silver. His eyes were restless and bloodshot, and his whole bearing was that of a man under the pressure of some extreme anxiety.

"Dr. Neville?" said he, halting upon the threshold.

"That is my name," I answered, motioning him to a chair.

He did not take the seat, but stood regarding me with a gaze that was at once searching and deferential. "You will not remember me, sir. I was one of the unfortunates who came under your care in the summer of 'eighty, during the affair at Fort Mardan."

I looked at him with some attention. As an army surgeon I had, of course, attended to a great number of men during that arduous campaign, and the wards had been crowded to overflowing. Yet I make it a rule, and a good one, to carry a clear recollection of those who pass through my hands. His face, though deeply lined and haggard, awoke no chord of memory in me.

"I fear you have the advantage of me," said I. "My duties at Mardan were severe, and I saw many wounded. In what capacity did we meet?"

"I was brought in with a gunshot wound to the left shoulder, sir," he replied, his fingers twitching nervously at the brim of his hat. "It was in the dead of night. You extracted the ball, and I was placed in a corner of the tent to recover. We spoke but little, for you were sorely overworked. But I have never forgotten your face, Dr. Neville, nor the kindness of your hands."

"You have my thanks for the compliment," said I, "though I confess I cannot place you. It was a time of great confusion."

He seemed to wince at this, and his restless eyes glanced towards the window. "It is natural, sir. I was not long under your care. But it is upon that very night that I have come to speak to you. A matter of the utmost gravity has brought me to England. I am in search of an object that was buried, with my own hands, in the shadow of the north wall of Fort Mardan. It is a small iron box, containing certain jewels and papers of great value to my family. I have been prevented from returning to the Frontier by ill-health and poverty. But I have lately learned that the fort is to be demolished, and the ground cleared for a new cantonment. I must have that box, Dr. Neville, or I am a ruined man."

I confess that I was somewhat staggered by this narrative. "But why come to me?" I asked. "If you know the location of the box, surely a letter to the commanding officer would be the proper course."

"I have written," he said, with a bitter smile, "and my letters have gone unanswered. The authorities are not inclined to dig up their parade ground upon the word of a discharged private. But you, sir—you were there. You know the ground. If you would be so good as to write a letter to your old chief, Surgeon-Major Halliday, testifying that I was wounded at the fort and that I had a reason to bury my property there, I believe it would carry weight."

There was a plausibility about the man's story, and yet I found myself instinctively on my guard. His manner was too eager, his eyes too furtive. I looked at his left shoulder, which he had named as the seat of his wound. The cloth of his coat hung naturally, and there was no stiffness in his movements.

"I should like to help you," said I, "but I must have some proof of your identity. What is your name?"

"Henry Vickery, sir, late of the Buffs."

"And you say I extracted a ball from your left shoulder?"

"Yes, sir."

I rose from my chair. "Then I am afraid, Mr. Vickery, that you are labouring under a mistake. I never extracted a ball from your left shoulder, for the very good reason that you have never received such a wound. Your coat shows no sign of being adapted to a stiff joint, and your movements are free and unhampered. Furthermore, Surgeon-Major Halliday was not at Fort Mardan in the summer of 'eighty; he was at Peshawar."

The man's face turned a shade paler, and his hand clenched upon his cane. For a moment I thought he was about to be violent, but he mastered himself with an effort.

"You are very observant, sir," he muttered.

"I was a surgeon," I answered. "Observation was my trade."

He turned without another word and left the room, and I heard the front door close behind him with a heavy thud. I sat for some time in my chair, turning the matter over in my mind. It was a curious incident, and the more I thought of it, the more it puzzled me. What could be the object of this man's visit? If he was an impostor, what did he hope to gain by fixing upon me as a witness? I determined to lay the facts before my friend Mr. Novak, whose singular powers of deduction I had more than once had occasion to test.
