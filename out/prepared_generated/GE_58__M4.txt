The question whether a man may be permitted to read what he will is one that has seldom been argued in the hay-field, where, indeed, the very terms of the debate would want a meaning for most of those who laboured there; and yet it was precisely in a hay-field, or rather in the long barn that overlooked it, that the matter came at last to a head in the village of Nettleworth, in the summer of 1817. The barn belonged to Mr. Hosea Blunt, a farmer of the old school, whose word was law over some four hundred acres of heavy clay, and whose temper had been formed by a long series of wet harvests and stubborn cattle into that suspicious hardness which is the natural product of a life spent in wrestling with unmanageable things. He was not a bad man; he was, on the contrary, a man of rough and ready kindness, who would sit up half the night with a sick heifer and give a labourer’s widow a sack of meal without asking for payment; but his kindness was of that practical sort which cannot abide the unseen, and he had a profound distrust of whatever he could not weigh in his hand or measure with a chain.

It was Thomas Ridd, a young labourer of two-and-twenty, who had first brought the ferment into Nettleworth. He was the son of a smallholder who had died of a fever, leaving him nothing but a mother to keep and a stubborn turn of mind that would not let him rest content with the round of ploughing and mowing which was the appointed lot of his kind. He had taught himself to read out of a Bible bought at a sale for fourpence, and from that beginning had gone on to such books as came his way—a torn volume of the “Spectator,” a treatise on farriery, and, at last, by some accident that he could never afterwards account for, a copy of “The Rights of Man,” which he read three times over in a loft above the cow-byre, with a feeling that was half terror and half exultation, as if he had found a key to a door that had been locked against him all his life. He was not a man of many words in the common way of speech, but when he did speak there was a gravity in him that made people listen, and a certain smouldering earnestness in his grey eyes that suggested fires which might be kindled into a blaze.

The reading society had begun, as such things do, in a small way. Six or seven of the younger labourers, with Ridd at their head, had been used to meet of a winter’s evening in the barn, when the work was slack, and there, by the light of a horn lantern, they had read together such books as they could get—a history of England, a book of travels, and once, by way of a great venture, a volume of “The Vicar of Wakefield,” which made them laugh and cry by turns, and was voted better than any sermon. Mr. Blunt had known of these meetings and had said nothing against them; nay, he had once looked in himself and stood listening with his hands behind his back while Ridd read aloud the story of the burning of London, and had gone away muttering that it was “a deal better nor the ale-house,” which was his highest word of praise. But when the society took to reading newspapers—and newspapers, in that year of unrest, were full of things that made the squire’s agent look grave—Mr. Blunt’s countenance changed.

It was on a Tuesday evening in August that he came into the barn where the men were gathered, and stood for a moment in the doorway, a stout figure in a smock-frock, with his heavy brows drawn together and his mouth set in a line that his labourers knew of old. Ridd was reading aloud from a weekly paper—a London paper, which had been sent down by a cousin who worked in a printing-house—and the words he was reading had to do with the meeting of working men in the north, and the demands they were making for a voice in the government of the country. The men were listening with that eager, parted-lips attention which is the sign of a new hunger, and they did not notice the farmer till he spoke.

“So that’s the sort of stuff you’re at now, is it?” he said, and his voice was quiet, which was a worse sign than if he had shouted.

Ridd looked up and closed the paper. “It’s the news, master,” he said, respectfully enough. “We like to know what’s doing in the world.”

“The world!” said Mr. Blunt, with a short laugh. “What’s the world to you, Tom Ridd, but this farm and the parish? You’ll do no good with that paper. I’ve heard tell of these societies—they begin with reading and they end with rick-burning. I’ll have no more of it in my barn.”

There was a silence, and the men looked at one another. Ridd’s face was pale, but he kept his temper, which was more than could be said for some of the younger ones, who muttered and shifted their feet.

“We’ve done no harm, master,” said Ridd. “We’ve paid for the candles, and we’ve broke nothing, and we’ve been quiet.”

“I don’t say as you’ve broke anything,” returned the farmer, “but I say as I’ll not have my barn made a hot-bed for sedition. You can read your Bible and your book of travels, and welcome; but the newspaper goes out of this barn, or you go out of it, and that’s my last word.”
