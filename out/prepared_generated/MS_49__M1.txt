The letters came no longer with the regularity of the tides, but in sudden bursts, like a shipwrecked man’s messages cast upon the shore; and each one that reached the quiet rectory at Ashworth fell upon my spirit with a weight of guilt that was almost insupportable. I, who had urged my brother, my own Edward, to that distant shore—I, who had kindled in his noble heart the flame of a glorious crusade—now read his words with a trembling hand and a soul sickened by the ruin I had wrought. I had spoken of the ancient Greeks, of Marathon and Thermopylae, of a people rising from the tomb of centuries to cast off the shackles of a cruel tyrant. I had painted for him a canvas of sublime sacrifice, of liberty purchased with a hero’s blood, and he, with a heart as open and ardent as the summer sky, had sailed upon that promise, leaving me to the silence of these English woods.

But the Greece he described in his later letters was not the Greece of my imagination. It was a land of dust and dissension, of scorching suns and freezing nights, where the cause of freedom was not a sacred banner borne aloft by a united host, but a tattered rag fought over by squabbling chieftains and rapacious adventurers. “Oh, Clara,” he wrote in one of these desperate missives, the paper itself seeming to sweat with the heat and misery of his prison-camp, “you spoke of a sacred war. I find here only a profane scramble. The leaders quarrel for plunder while their followers starve; the very air is thick with treachery and the cries of the wounded. I have seen men fight with the valour of demi-gods in one moment, and in the next turn upon their comrades for a crust of bread or a stolen horse. Is this the liberty for which we bled in our dreams? It is a hydra, and for every head of tyranny we strike off, two more of anarchy and greed spring forth in its place. I came to offer my life for a cause; I find I am merely a pawn in a game of brigands.”

His words were a physical torment to me. I read them in my little parlour, where the scent of roses from the garden drifted through the open window, and the gentle English sun cast a peaceful pattern of light upon the floor. The contrast was a mockery. My world was one of order, of quiet duty, of the predictable turning of the seasons; his was a maelstrom of dust, blood, and disillusion. I, who had been so sure of the moral grandeur of his mission, now felt the foundations of my own belief crumble to dust. I had sent him, my dearest, my most precious Edward, into a squalid hell, and for what? For a phantom of my own making, a beautiful, foolish dream of antique virtue. The guilt was a fire that consumed my peace, leaving only the ashes of regret. I would walk to the edge of the moor, and the vast, indifferent sweep of the sky and the heath, which once had filled me with a sense of the sublime, now only served to magnify my own insignificance and the terrible, unbridgeable distance between my safety and his suffering.

His final letter arrived on an autumn evening, when a storm was gathering over the hills. The wind howled like a banshee around the gables of the rectory, and the rain lashed against the windowpanes as if demanding entry. The letter was short, the handwriting a jagged scrawl that spoke of fever and despair. “They speak of a final assault,” he wrote, “a glorious charge to break the siege. There is no glory here, Clara, only the certainty of a pointless death in a forgotten skirmish. Do not weep for a hero, for I am not one. I am only a boy, lost and weary, who followed a siren’s song to this broken shore. Forget the cause; it is a phantom. Forget the war; it is a butchery. Remember only your brother, and pray that God, in his infinite mercy, will forgive us both for our folly.”

A great cry was torn from my soul as I read these words. I looked up from the page, and through the rain-streaked glass, I saw the storm in all its terrible majesty. The sky was a churning vortex of bruised purple and furious grey; the trees were bent double, their branches thrashing like the limbs of drowning men; and the lightning, in its sudden, brilliant flashes, illuminated the world in a ghastly, momentary starkness. It was the very image of the chaos in my own heart. The storm without was but a reflection of the tempest within. “Oh, Edward!” I shrieked, my voice lost in the roar of the gale. “What have I done? My love, my pride, my blind and wretched certainty have been your executioners! I sent you to a glorious death, and have delivered you to a squalid one. Forgive me, my brother, forgive the sister whose fondness has destroyed you!” I fell upon my knees, the letter clutched to my breast, and the rain and my tears mingled upon the floor. The storm raged on, but within me there was only a vast, silent ruin, the desolate landscape of a guilt that no tempest could ever wash away.
