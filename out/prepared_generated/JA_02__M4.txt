The breakfast parlour at Delaford Place had never, within the memory of its present inmates, been the scene of any communication more agitating than a change in the weather or a disappointment in the matter of a dinner-guest; and it was therefore with a sense of something almost indecorous that the family of Mr. Henry Ashcombe received, upon a Tuesday morning in March, the intelligence which was to alter the complexion of their lives.

The letter lay open beside his plate, and he regarded it with the air of a man who has been introduced to a stranger of doubtful reputation and does not know in what terms to acknowledge the acquaintance. “It is from Gresham and Gresham,” said he, at length. “The canal has failed. The shares are worthless. I am the poorer by two thousand a year.”

Mrs. Ashcombe, who had been in the act of pouring out the tea, set down the pot with a deliberation more alarming than any outcry. “Two thousand a year!” she repeated. “Henry, you cannot mean it. Two thousand a year! Why, that is a third of everything.”

“It is a third of everything,” said Mr. Ashcombe, with the gloomy satisfaction of a man who has at least the consolation of being exactly understood.

“Then the carriage must go,” said Mrs. Ashcombe, and paused, and looked about her as though she had uttered a proposal to sell one of the children. “No — I cannot think it. The carriage must not go. Consider, my dear, what is due to your position — to mine — to the girls. We have lived in Wiltshire these five-and-twenty years, and never has an Ashcombe been seen to walk into the village. I would rather we gave up the house.”

“That would be a considerable saving,” observed Mr. Ashcombe, “but I doubt the landlord would consent to it.”

“You are pleased to be witty,” returned his wife, “but I am in no humour for it. If we lose the carriage, we lose everything. Mrs. Pulteney will hear of it before Sunday, and she will say — I know exactly what she will say — that the Ashcombes are come to ruin, and that she always thought the canal a very foolish scheme.”

“She would not be far wrong,” said Mr. Ashcombe.

“That is not the point, Henry. The point is what is to be done.”

At this juncture the eldest daughter, who had listened in silence, and who had moreover been engaged, during the latter part of the conversation, in a calculation which her countenance did not betray, ventured to speak. “Papa,” said she, “may I ask whether the two thousand is the whole of the loss, or whether there is any further call upon you?”

“There is no further call,” replied Mr. Ashcombe. “The concern is at an end. But the income is gone, and it will not return.”

“Then,” said Eleanor, “it is a question of what we can do without. I have been reckoning while you talked. If we keep the carriage, we must give up the London journey, the new piano, and the two maid-servants; we must have no company but such as will come without ceremony; and we must live at Delaford the year round.”

“Eleanor, you are very composed,” said her mother, with an accent of reproach.

“I am very sorry,” said Eleanor. “But somebody must be composed, and it had better be the person who can count.”

Mrs. Ashcombe was silenced, but not convinced. She had a great opinion of her own discernment in matters of feeling, and she now regarded her daughter with a look which said plainly that arithmetic was not the business of a young woman, and that a proper sensibility would have shown itself in tears.

The second daughter, Augusta, had by this time finished her toast, and now looked up with an air of amiable bewilderment. “But I do not understand,” said she. “If the canal has failed, why should we be the worse? I thought canals were always a great advantage to everybody. The one at Marlborough is quite the fashion, and Mrs. Pulteney says that all the best people have shares in it.”

“That is precisely the reason,” said Mr. Ashcombe, “why the best people have lost their money in this one.”

“I am sure I never liked the canal,” said Augusta. “I always thought the barges very dirty, and the men very rude. But, mamma, if we give up the London journey, what is to become of my presentation? Lady Clavering promised to take me.”

“My dear,” said Mrs. Ashcombe, “there are sacrifices to be made.”

“But I have had my gown made,” said Augusta, “and it is white satin, and it cannot be worn at Delaford.”

“It may be worn at Delaford,” said Eleanor, “if you will wear it in the morning, which I do not recommend. Or it may be laid by until some happier occasion.”

“Some happier occasion!” cried Augusta. “And when will that be? I shall be an old woman before the canal pays again.”

“The canal will never pay again,” said Mr. Ashcombe.

The youngest daughter, Maria, who was sixteen, and who had not yet learned that a family in distress is expected to be grave, here broke in with an enquiry whether, if the carriage were kept, she might still have her riding-horse. “For,” said she, “I do not care about London, and I hate white satin, but I cannot do without Sultan.”

“You will have to do without Sultan,” said Mrs. Ashcombe, “if we keep the carriage.”

“Then let us sell the carriage,” said Maria, “and keep Sultan, and I will walk to the village myself, and tell Mrs. Pulteney that we are poor, and that she may say what she likes.”

“Maria, you are very foolish,” said Mrs. Ashcombe. “You do not know what you are talking of. Mrs. Pulteney would be delighted. She has never forgiven me for the business of the subscription, and she would go about the county saying that we were reduced to a pony-cart.”
