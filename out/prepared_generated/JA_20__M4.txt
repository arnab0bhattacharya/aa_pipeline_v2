The season at Bath was now at its height, and the Pump Room of a morning exhibited that agreeable confusion of purpose which is nowhere else so perfectly achieved; invalids in pursuit of health, and idlers in pursuit of one another, mingling in a manner that made the cure of the one and the amusement of the other appear, to a superficial observer, the very same occupation. Among those who drank the waters with no more faith in their efficacy than was necessary to justify the glass in their hands, was Miss Horatia Delacour, who had come to Bath under the protection of her brother, Captain Edmund Delacour of the Royal Navy, lately returned from a station in the Mediterranean and at present enjoying the twofold blessing of half-pay and shore leave.

Captain Delacour was a man of five-and-thirty, of a frank and open countenance, and a temper which, having been formed by the discipline of his profession and the independent habit of command, was somewhat apt to mistake its own decisions for the decrees of Providence. He was warmly attached to his sister, who was ten years his junior and for whom he had stood, since the death of their parents, in the combined office of guardian, friend, and—though he would have been the last to own it—tyrant. Horatia was a young woman of sense and spirit, with a countenance rather distinguished than beautiful, and a manner which recommended her the more on acquaintance. She was of age, and possessed of a fortune sufficient to make her independence real; but her brother, accustomed to consider her as a trust committed to his keeping, had never quite accustomed himself to the notion that the trust was now discharged.

Their establishment in Bath was of six weeks' standing when Mr. Peregrine Vane first came within their circle. He was a gentleman of independent means, of elegant person and easy address, who had been fixed in Bath for the winter by no particular necessity, and who appeared to find in Miss Delacour's conversation a pleasure which he was at no pains to conceal. He had a manner of paying attention which was the more flattering for being perfectly unembarrassed; and he possessed, in an eminent degree, that species of wit which consists in saying the agreeable thing without appearing to have studied it. Horatia, who had known few men of leisure, and fewer still who could converse with her upon subjects beyond the immediate concerns of a ball-room, found his society a gratification of a kind she had not looked for; and if she did not at once confess to herself that she looked for it, she at least took care not to be at home when he called, which is a distinction of some importance.

Captain Delacour, on the other hand, had conceived for Mr. Vane a dislike which was the more obstinate for being founded upon no very definite charge. He had made inquiries, as men of his temper do, and had learned that Mr. Vane had once been concerned in a speculation in the West Indies which had failed, and that a certain Sir James Mordaunt, whose acquaintance Delacour had made at the Navy Board, had spoken of him with a coldness which was almost contempt. He had observed, too, that Mr. Vane was a man of many acquaintance and no intimates; that he was all things to all people, and nothing long to any; and that his attentions to Horatia, however graceful, were of a piece with his attentions to every other woman in the room, and might be transferred to-morrow, with no visible diminution of ardour, to the next heiress who should enter it. From these observations the Captain drew his conclusion, as he was wont to do, with the decision of a man accustomed to act upon his own judgment and to see it justified by the event.

The conclusion was, that Mr. Vane was a fortune-hunter, and that Horatia was in danger. It was a conclusion which he could not communicate to his sister without provoking a discussion he had no mind to enter upon; for he was sensible, in the midst of his displeasure, that he had no right to command her, and he was not yet sufficiently master of himself to trust his own temper in a matter where his affections were so nearly concerned. He therefore contented himself with a line of conduct which he believed to be the perfection of policy, but which was, in fact, the mere indulgence of a proud man's resentment: he treated Mr. Vane with a coldness so studied that it could not escape the observation even of the most careless, and with a haughtiness which, in a man of his open temper, sat upon him as ill as a borrowed coat.

The effect was precisely what might have been foretold. Horatia, who had no reason to suspect her brother's real motive, and who was not of a temper to submit to unexplained authority, felt herself called upon to redress the balance of civility; and her manner to Mr. Vane, which had before been merely complaisant, now took a warmth that was partly the spirit of contradiction and partly a genuine persuasion that she was doing justice to an injured man. Mr. Vane, for his part, bore the Captain's rudeness with a patience that was either the perfection of good breeding or the perfection of policy; and the more forbearing he was, the more did the Captain's dislike increase, for there is nothing so provoking to a man of quick temper as to be met with a composure he cannot penetrate.

Matters were in this state when a circumstance occurred which brought the question to a point.
