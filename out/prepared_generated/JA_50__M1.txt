It is a truth not sufficiently acknowledged that a ballroom is no place for the cultivation of true sensibility; being, as it is, a theatre of artifice where the brightest smiles are often the most laboured, and the most agreeable manners the result of no feeling whatsoever. Yet, to the young ladies of Hartfield, newly arrived at the seaside resort of Weymouth for the season, the assembly rooms presented the very pinnacle of human endeavour, a dazzling prospect of life in its most exalted and romantic form. Miss Clara Vane, whose vivacity was the pride of her family and the terror of her mother’s nerves, saw in the sparkling chandeliers and the press of elegant strangers a field for conquest, while her elder sister, Miss Eleanor Vane, viewed the scene with a more tempered, and indeed a more philosophical, eye.

Clara, with the confidence that accompanies a lively disposition and a well-formed figure, was resolved to enjoy the evening to its fullest extent. Her success was immediate and unequivocal. She was solicited for the first set by a gentleman of considerable fortune, the next by an officer of the dragoons, and so it continued, a triumphal procession of dances from the opening minuet to the final reel. Her colour was heightened, her laughter rang out above the music, and she moved through the throng as one born to command it. To be the object of such general admiration was a delight she had long anticipated, and she did not mean to be disappointed.

Eleanor, meanwhile, found herself in a situation of a very different complexion. Possessed of a quiet elegance and a countenance that repaid closer inspection, she was not without her share of admirers; yet, a certain reserve in her manner, or perhaps a want of that forward spirit which the assembly seemed to demand, left her standing by her mother’s side for the better part of the evening. She declined the few offers she received with a politeness so gentle as to be almost apologetic, pleading a slight indisposition, a want of practice, or a simple disinclination. To see her sister thus surrounded while she remained untouched by the general gaiety might have provoked envy in a less amiable heart; but Eleanor found a peculiar contentment in her observation of the scene. She saw the studied elegance of Mrs. Danvers, the local leader of fashion, and the desperate attempts of her daughters to secure the attentions of the very men who danced with Clara. She noted the fatigue behind the smiles of the chaperones and the calculation in the eyes of the gentlemen. She was a spectator to a pageant, and found the study of it more interesting than the performance.

Her repose was interrupted but once, by a gentleman whose introduction had been made earlier in the day by a mutual acquaintance. He was a Mr. Ashton, a man of perhaps five-and-thirty, with a thoughtful air and a deportment that spoke more of country gentility than of town polish. He did not ask her to dance, but stood for some moments by her chair, remarking upon the oppressive heat of the room and the general want of fresh air. They spoke for a short while of the respective merits of Weymouth and their own county, a conversation so brief and general as to be scarcely memorable. Yet, when he took his leave to rejoin a party of gentlemen, he did so with a bow that seemed to carry a shade more of sincere regard than the elaborate flourishes of her sister’s partners. Eleanor thought little of it, attributing his civility to the simple kindness of a man who had perceived her to be neglected.

The following morning brought the usual post-ball reckoning of fatigue and gossip. Clara, though her spirits were undimmed, was content to lie upon the sopha and recount the events of the evening, a chronicle of conquests and near-conquests that left her mother in a state of anxious ecstasy. It was therefore with some surprise that the servant announced a visitor. The name, however, was not that of any of Clara’s admirers, but of Mr. Ashton.

A flush of confusion coloured Eleanor’s cheeks. She could conceive of no reason for his call, save perhaps some forgotten message from their common acquaintance. Clara sat up, her eyes wide with astonishment. “Mr. Ashton? To call upon us? But he did not dance once! I declare, I never saw so grave a man in my life. What can he possibly want?”

Before Eleanor could form a reply, Mr. Ashton entered the room. His address was calm and self-possessed, his enquiries after their health perfectly proper. After a few minutes of general conversation on the weather and the prospects for the day, he turned to Eleanor.

“Miss Vane,” he began, “I trust you will forgive the liberty of this call. I was concerned, if I may say so, to see you looking so fatigued last evening. I hope the morning finds you in better spirits.”

Eleanor, much affected by a civility so unexpected and so personal, assured him of her complete recovery. It was then that he produced, from his pocket, a small volume bound in simple leather.

“I happened to be in the circulating library this morning,” he said, “and upon seeing this collection of Cowper’s poems, I was reminded of our brief conversation yesterday. You mentioned, I believe, that you found the poets of sensibility more to your taste than the great epic writers. I am no great judge, but I thought these verses might afford some quiet amusement for a morning of repose.”

Eleanor was struck dumb for a moment. That he should have recalled so trifling a remark, and acted upon it with such thoughtfulness, was a demonstration of feeling altogether new to her. She accepted the book with a warmth of gratitude that was more than mere politeness.
