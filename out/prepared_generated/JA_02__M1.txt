It was a truth universally acknowledged in the vicinity of Broadwood Park that a gentleman possessing a comfortable fortune must be in want of a carriage to display it. Mr. Ashworth had, for some years, possessed not only the fortune but a very handsome landau, which was considered by his wife, Mrs. Ashworth, to be as essential to their standing in Wiltshire as the roof over their heads or the port in his cellar. It was therefore with a sensation of profound shock, a feeling that the very foundations of their world had been unsettled, that the family received the news of the Grand Wilts and Berks Canal Company’s failure.

Mr. Ashworth, a man whose equanimity was rarely disturbed by anything more vexing than a poorly-kept fox covert, had delivered the intelligence at breakfast with a gravity that immediately silenced his three daughters. “A third of my income,” he had said, setting down his fork with a quiet finality. “Lost to a ditch that is to go nowhere and a company of optimists who have discovered, rather late in the day, that a great deal of chalk lies between here and Reading.”

Silence, a rare commodity in the Ashworth breakfast room, reigned for a full minute. It was broken, as such silences so often are, by the person who felt the inconvenience most personally. “A third!” cried Mrs. Ashworth, her voice a mixture of disbelief and affront. “But, my dear Ashworth, this is impossible. What is to become of us?”

“We shall manage, my love,” her husband replied, with the weary patience of a man who has already considered the matter from every practical angle. “We are not ruined. We are merely… less comfortable. Some retrenchment will be necessary.”

The word ‘retrenchment’ fell upon the room like a stone into a still pond, and its ripples of unease spread through every mind present. To Mrs. Ashworth, it spoke of social ruin; to her eldest daughter, Eleanor, it suggested a ledger full of grim calculations; to her second, Lydia, it promised the loss of invitations to Bath and London; and to the youngest, Kitty, it meant little more than a vague sense that Papa was cross.

“Retrenchment?” Mrs. Ashworth repeated, the word tasting of ashes. “You cannot mean the carriage.”

Mr. Ashworth sighed. “I confess, Mary, the expense of the coachman and the horses is considerable. It was the first thing that occurred to me.”

“Then it must be the last thing we give up!” she declared, her voice gaining the firmness of a general defending a vital redoubt. “What will the Prestons say? Or Lady Finch? They would see our not driving out as a declaration of utter destitution. We should be objects of pity. No, the carriage must remain. It is a matter of appearances, and appearances are all that stand between a family of consequence and a family of misfortune.”

Eleanor, who had been observing her mother’s performance with a quiet, analytical eye, felt a familiar weariness settle upon her. She did not doubt her mother’s sincerity; to Mrs. Ashworth, the perception of wealth was, in a very real sense, wealth itself. Yet Eleanor’s mind, which was of a more mathematical cast, was already at work. She pictured the carriage not as a symbol, but as a set of figures: the coachman’s wages, the ostler’s fee, the price of oats and hay, the smith’s bill for shoeing, the cost of the harness-maker’s wax. Each figure was a servant to be dismissed, a joint of meat to be forgone, a new dress to be postponed indefinitely.

Lydia, whose thoughts were never far from the next assembly, voiced the general concern. “But if we must retrench, Mama, does that mean no new gowns for the Whitmore ball? And what of our trip to Aunt Sophia’s in Bath?”

“We shall see,” said Mrs. Ashworth, waving a dismissive hand. “The carriage is the first consideration. It is the public face of our private economy.”

Eleanor privately thought that the public face was likely to starve the private body. She began a silent, ruthless inventory. The two footmen must become one. The kitchen garden must be made to supply more. The wine at table must be of a more modest vintage. Her father’s subscription to the London papers might be reduced to a single gazette. Her own pin-money, already modest, would have to be stretched to a degree that required genuine ingenuity. She looked at her sisters. Lydia would notice the lack of new finery first, and would complain bitterly. Kitty, who was amiable and easily led, would notice nothing until it was pointed out to her, and would then feel whatever Lydia felt.

The discussion continued in this vein for some time, with Mrs. Ashworth asserting the primacy of the carriage and Mr. Ashworth nodding in a state of resigned abstraction. Eleanor said little. She knew that to argue with her mother on this point was to invite a lecture on her own lack of worldly understanding. Instead, she turned her attention to the practical consequences. The footmen’s livery, for instance, would not need replacing this year. That was a saving of at least fifteen pounds. If they gave up the carriage for half the year, perhaps using it only for church and for essential visits, they might save enough to keep the appearance of it without the full cost. It was a compromise, but she doubted her mother would see it as such. To her, the carriage was either a daily fixture or it was gone.

After breakfast, Eleanor retreated to the morning room to consult the household books. The numbers were as she had suspected: stark and unforgiving. The carriage, all told, cost nearly two hundred pounds a year. A third of her father’s income was closer to eight hundred. The carriage was a quarter of their loss. Her mother’s instinct, however socially misguided, had landed upon the most significant point of expense.
