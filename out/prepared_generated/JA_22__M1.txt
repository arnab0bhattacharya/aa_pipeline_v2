The intelligence of Mrs. St. Aubyn’s will could not, in the nature of things, be kept a secret; and by the time it reached the ears of Mr. Pomeroy, it had already acquired the embellishments common to all such reports, so that he arrived at Delphine Cottage persuaded that his sisters were now in possession of a fortune that ought, by every principle of justice and filial affection, to have been his own.

The bequest was, in truth, of a very moderate description. Mrs. St. Aubyn, who had been the girls’ godmother and had no other claim upon the notice of the world, had left to Miss Harriet Pomeroy and Miss Charlotte Pomeroy the cottage in which they now resided, together with an annuity of two hundred pounds, secured upon a fund in the Four per Cents. To Mr. Pomeroy, who had flattered himself through a long minority with the expectation of succeeding to the whole of the old lady’s property, she had bequeathed a silver teapot and her good wishes; and it may be imagined with what feelings he contemplated the disproportion.

He was a young man of some consequence in his own estimation, and of less in that of any body else; but he had a turn for arranging the affairs of other people which he mistook for good sense, and a facility of speech which he mistook for eloquence. Having been told that his sisters did not agree, he came down to Dorset with the laudable design of mediating between them, though it might have been observed that his mediation, like that of most gentlemen who undertake it, consisted principally in informing both parties how much better the matter might have been settled had it been left entirely to himself.

The cottage stood upon the edge of the common, and had been improved by Mrs. St. Aubyn with that taste which consists in making things comfortable for the person who is to use them, and inconvenient for every body else. It was small, it was damp, and it commanded a view of nothing but gorse and sky; but it had the advantage of being entirely their own, and Harriet, who was the elder and the wiser of the two sisters, looked upon it as a provision against the uncertainties of life, and valued it accordingly.

Charlotte, who was twenty, and had seen nothing of the world but Weymouth, looked upon it as a prison. Two hundred a year was, in her opinion, a fortune which entitled her to at least one season in London; and she had already formed so many plans for its employment—comprising a house in some fashionable quarter, a box at the opera, and an extensive acquaintance among persons of rank—that the loss of the annuity would have been less painful to her than the suppression of the schemes it was to have supported.

Their brother listened to both with an air of impartiality which was the more admirable as he had determined from the first that the annuity ought to have been settled upon himself.

‘It is a very pretty little place,’ said he, looking round the parlour with the complacency of a man who considers every thing he sees as a future possession; ‘very pretty indeed. And the annuity is not to be despised. But I cannot but feel, my dear Harriet, that two hundred a year is a very inadequate provision for two young women, and that some plan ought to be adopted for rendering it more productive.’

‘I have no wish to render it more productive,’ replied Harriet, with that quiet decision which was the most formidable thing about her. ‘The cottage is our own; the annuity is sufficient for our wants; and I see no occasion for any plan at all.’

‘But consider, my dear sister, how much might be done with it in London,’ cried Charlotte. ‘Two hundred a year, with economy, and a little credit—’

‘A little credit,’ said Harriet, ‘is a very dangerous thing to meddle with, and economy in London is a phrase that means nothing at all. I am very sorry to disappoint you, Charlotte, but I cannot consent to the sale of an annuity that is secured upon good funds, for the purpose of spending the money before we have received it.’

Mr. Pomeroy, who had hoped to see the sisters at variance, and to be called in as arbiter, found them so decided in their respective opinions that he changed his tactics, and undertook to prove that both were wrong.

‘You are both of you,’ said he, with the air of a man who is about to deliver an oracle, ‘equally mistaken. Harriet, you are too prudent; Charlotte, you are too sanguine. The truth lies, as it always does, in the middle. Now, what I would propose is this: that the annuity should be suffered to accumulate for a year or two, and that in the mean time the cottage should be let, and the sisters should reside with me until some more eligible scheme can be devised.’

It was a proposal which united every advantage to himself, and none to them; and it was received with a degree of coldness which would have disconcerted any man less persuaded of his own merits.

‘We are extremely obliged to you,’ said Harriet, ‘but we have no intention of letting the cottage, or of residing any where else. We are very well where we are.’

‘And I am extremely obliged to you,’ said Charlotte, ‘but I have no intention of waiting two years for a season in London. If Harriet will not consent to my plan, I shall write to my aunt at Bath, and ask her to take me with her to town next winter.’

The mention of the aunt at Bath, who was a woman of some fashion and of no fortune, gave a new turn to the conversation, and Mr. Pomeroy, who had a great regard for his own consequence, began to perceive that his sisters might be less tractable than he had supposed.
