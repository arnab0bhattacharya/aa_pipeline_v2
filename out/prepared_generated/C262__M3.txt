# The Visitor

Arthur Fenton had always considered himself a man impossible to deceive. At one-and-twenty, he had read enough philosophy and sat through enough dull dinners to conclude that he understood human nature as well as any person in the county of Kent, and considerably better than most. His sister Margaret, who was three years his senior and had watched him arrive at this conclusion with the quiet exasperation of someone who had once seen him eat a pie he had dropped in the garden, did not share his assessment.

The trouble began on a Thursday in May, when Mrs. Fenton invited the Misses Harlow to tea at their house near Wrotham. The Harlows were new to the neighbourhood — a widow and her daughter who had taken the lease on Paddock Cottage — and Mrs. Fenton, who was conscientious about such things, had called upon them within the week and found them pleasant and genteel. The daughter, Miss Charlotte Harlow, was nineteen, fair-haired, and possessed of a smile that seemed to promise she was thinking exactly what you were thinking, only more kindly.

Arthur, who had been reading in the parlour and had not expected to be called upon to entertain, entered the drawing room with his collar undone and a smudge of ink on his left hand. Margaret watched him realise his error, watched the colour rise in his face, and watched Miss Harlow's eyes settle on him with an expression of such unaffected interest that Margaret's own interest was immediately awakened — though in a very different direction.

"How do you do," said Arthur, bowing slightly and tucking the ink-stained hand behind his back.

Miss Harlow rose and curtsied, and said that she was very well, and that she hoped she had not disturbed him. She said it with such apparent sincerity that Arthur, who generally took pleasure in being disturbed by nothing, assured her she had not disturbed him at all, and that he had merely been wasting the afternoon with a volume of Hume which he did not in the least recommend.

"Not recommend Hume?" said Miss Harlow, her eyes widening. "But my mother's cousin was a great admirer of his. I confess I have not read him myself, of course, but I had thought — that is, I had always understood him to be very clever."

"He is clever," said Arthur, warming with the speed of a man who has found someone willing to listen to him discuss philosophy. "He is very clever. But cleverness is not the same thing as being right, and I have always maintained that his arguments on causation are little better than a parlour trick dressed up in sober language."

Miss Harlow tilted her head. "A parlour trick," she repeated, thoughtfully, as though this were the most interesting thing anyone had said to her in months. "I should never have thought of that. You must think me very ignorant, Mr. Fenton."

"I think you remarkably honest," said Arthur, which was not at all what Margaret would have said, and which Arthur himself would not have said had he been thinking clearly. Miss Harlow received the compliment with a modest lowering of her lashes, and Mrs. Harlow, from across the room, smiled into her teacup.

Margaret said nothing. She drank her tea and watched, and after the Harlows had departed, she helped her mother see them to the door, and then she returned to the parlour, where Arthur was standing in the middle of the room with the expression of a man who has just witnessed a minor miracle.

"Well," he said. "She is not at all what I expected."

"What did you expect?"

"I don't know. Someone dull. Someone who would talk about the weather and the roads. But she — did you hear what she said about Hume?"

"She said she had not read him."

"Yes, but she said it with such honesty. Most people would pretend to have read him, or change the subject. She simply admitted it. That takes a particular kind of character."

Margaret considered pointing out that admitting ignorance of a philosopher one has not read is not especially heroic, but she held her tongue. She had known Arthur all his life, and she knew that the surest way to make him cling to an opinion was to contradict it.

The Harlows came to tea again the following week, and again the week after that. On the second visit, Miss Harlow asked Arthur about the book he was writing — a project he had mentioned to no one but Margaret, and which existed, so far, entirely in the form of a title and three pages of notes. He was astonished that she had somehow divined it.

"I must have mentioned it," he said to Margaret afterwards, frowning.

"You did not."

"Then how could she have known?"

Margaret did not answer. She had noticed that Miss Harlow, during their first visit, had glanced at the writing desk by the window, where Arthur's notes lay in plain view. She had not said anything, because she was not yet certain. She wanted to be fair.

But by the third visit, she was certain. Miss Harlow agreed with everything Arthur said, but she agreed with it in a way that seemed to draw more out of him — a question here, a thoughtful repetition there, a wide-eyed "I should never have considered that" which sent Arthur into transports of further explanation. She never offered an opinion of her own that was not a reflection of his, slightly softened and prettily phrased. When Arthur said he thought the local magistrate a fool, Miss Harlow said she was sure she did not understand such matters but that she had often thought there was something in his expression that suggested a certain — and she paused, delicately — narrowness. When Arthur said he preferred the country to the town, Miss Harlow confessed that she had always found London exhausting, though she had been there only twice and could not say why. When Arthur said he thought the modern novel overwrought, Miss Harlow said she was relieved to hear it, because she had begun to think herself very stupid for finding so many of them tedious.
