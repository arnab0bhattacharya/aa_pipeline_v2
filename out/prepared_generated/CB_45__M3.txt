The autumn of that year settled upon Cheshire with a sullen, persistent gloom. At Thornfield Grange—not the Yorkshire house of that name, but a lesser, damp-bricked manor near the mere—the rain fell for three days without ceasing, driving in grey sheets against the narrow lattice of my attic window. I had sought that chamber for a half-hour of solitude, having spent the morning in the schoolroom at a task that required little of my intellect and much of my patience. The air below stairs was close, heated by fires that seemed to smoke rather than warm, and impregnated with an oppression I could not name but felt acutely in the tightening of my breath.

I was governess to the two younger sons of Mr. Marsden, a man of moderate estate and immoderate ambition. But it was not the boys who occupied my thoughts that afternoon. It was Clara, the elder daughter of the house, now eighteen, and possessed of a beauty that seemed almost to demand a tragic context—dark-eyed, pale, with a stillness about her movements that concealed, I suspected, a nature of considerable force.

The sound of the door opening below the staircase reached me even through the wind. I heard Mrs. Marsden’s voice, sharp and imperative, and then Clara’s reply, low and measured. I could not distinguish the words, but the cadence was familiar: the mother pressing, the daughter withholding. This exchange had become, in recent weeks, the dominant music of the household, and I had learned to read its variations with a precision that surprised me, for I had no wish to be a witness to it.

The matter was this. Mr. Marsden’s fortunes, though sufficient to maintain the appearance of gentility, were strained by debts incurred in speculation—debts of which the world knew nothing, but which pressed upon him with the weight of a millstone. Into this breach had stepped Mr. Edgar Vane, a man of sixty-three years, who had made his considerable fortune in the manufacture of wire in Birmingham, and who now sought, as men of his stamp are wont to seek, a young wife of good family to furnish his drawing-room and bear his name into county society. He had fixed his regard upon Clara, and the Marsdens, with a calculation that made my blood stir hotly in my veins when I permitted myself to think of it, had determined that she should accept him.

I had observed Mr. Vane on three occasions. He was tall, spare, and upright, with a face that bore the imprint of a shrewdness pushed to the very edge of cunning. His eyes were pale and small; his mouth, though firm, wanted nothing of cruelty. He was not, in the common sense, a vulgar man; he had read books, could speak of politics with a fluency that impressed Mr. Marsden, and dressed with a correctness that betrayed the hand of a London tailor. But there was upon him that indelible mark which no amount of refinement can efface—the stamp of a soul that has made of acquisition its sole religion, and that views all human beings as instruments toward that end.

That evening, after the family had retired from dinner, Clara came to me in the schoolroom. I was mending a torn atlas—the younger Marsden boys were destructive of maps—and she stood for a moment in the doorway, her candle held aloft, before she entered and closed the door behind her. The flame cast upward shadows upon her face, giving her cheekbones a sharp prominence and deepening the hollows beneath her eyes. She looked, I thought, like one who had not slept in several nights.

"Miss Hartley," she said, and then stopped, as if the mere utterance of my name had exhausted her purpose. She set the candle down upon my desk and drew a chair near, but did not sit. Her hand rested upon the back of it, and I saw that the fingers tightened and loosened alternately, in a rhythm that spoke of agitation held under severe command.

"Sit down, Miss Marsden," I said, for I would not presume to call her Clara, though she had invited me to do so more than once.

She sat. "I came to ask you a question," she said, "and I beg you will answer me with complete candour. You are not of my family; you owe us nothing but your professional duty, and I believe—I have reason to believe—that you possess a judgment I may trust."

"You honour me," I replied. "Ask what you will."

"Is it wicked," she said, and her voice dropped to a register that was almost inaudible, "to refuse to obey one's parents in a matter that concerns one's own life and happiness?"

The question, simply framed, carried in its wake the whole burden of her predicament. I felt it press against my own conscience with a force that was almost physical. I set down my needle.

"You must tell me more before I can answer justly," I said, though I knew well enough what she would say.

She told me. She spoke with a composure that astonished me, considering the storm I could see raging behind her still features. Mr. Vane had made his formal offer three days since. Her father had received it with a satisfaction he did not trouble to conceal, and her mother had spoken to her privately, with a tone of wounded entreaty, of the sacrifices that parents make, and of the duty that children owe. Her brother, a young man of twenty-two, had observed that Vane was "deuced rich" and that Clara "might do a great deal worse." No one, it appeared, had inquired whether she might do better, or whether the word "better" signified anything beyond a settlement of fifty thousand pounds and a house in London.

"And you?" I asked. "What do you feel?"
