The night before my wedding, I sat in the window-seat of my father’s study, watching the rain come down over the moor. It fell in long, slanting spears, each one striking the blackened flagstones of the yard and shattering into a thousand lesser drops, so that the whole world beyond the glass seemed to be weeping without ceasing. I had drawn my shawl close about me, though the fire in the grate burned high and hot, and the room was close with the smell of damp paper and my father’s tobacco. He had gone to bed hours since, having pressed my hand with a fervour that sat strangely upon his habitually abstracted manner, and said, “You are a good girl, Honora. A very good girl.” I had smiled, and said nothing; for what was there to say? Good girls do not fling their fathers’ kindness back into their faces, nor remind them that the kindness was purchased at a price no daughter should be asked to pay.

Mr. Ashworth was to come for me at eleven o’clock on the morrow. He would bring a carriage—a handsome one, I did not doubt, for he did nothing by halves—and we should drive to the church at Millbridge, where the banns had been read these three Sundays past, and the parson would join our hands, and I should be Mrs. Ashworth before noon. I had known him six months. He was a widower of eight-and-forty, with a broad, ruddy face and a manner of such unvarying benevolence that it oppressed me like a heavy blanket on a summer’s day. He had been kind to my father when the mill failed; he had paid the debts that would otherwise have swallowed us whole; he had settled a sum upon my mother that would keep her in comfort for the rest of her days. And in return he asked—what? Only that I should be his wife. Only that I should sit at the head of his table, and pour his tea, and listen to his discourse upon wool and worsted and the state of the markets, and bear him children if it pleased God, and grow old beside him in his great stone house on the hill above the village. It was not so much to ask. It was not so much to give. And yet, as I sat there watching the rain, I felt as though I were being buried alive.

I had tried, in the weeks since the engagement was made, to school myself into gratitude. I had told myself a hundred times that Mr. Ashworth was a good man—and so he was; that he would be a kind husband—and so he would; that many women, far better than I, had been forced to accept far worse. I had prayed for a dutiful heart, and had risen from my knees with the same cold, heavy stone lying in my breast where the prayer should have warmed me. I could not love him. I could not even like him, in the way that one likes a friend. I could only endure him, and be grateful, and count the hours until I might be alone again with my books and my thoughts.

My mother came to me at last, when the clock in the hall had struck one, and the rain had slackened to a soft, persistent drizzle. She wore her nightcap and a wrapper of faded chintz, and her face in the candlelight looked older than I remembered it—shrunken and anxious, as though she too had been lying awake, counting the cost of what we were about to do.

“Honora,” she said, “you are not asleep.”

“No, mother.”

She came and sat beside me on the window-seat, and took my hand in hers. Her fingers were cold and thin, and they trembled a little, though whether from the chill of the night or from some inward agitation, I could not tell. She did not look at me, but out at the dark moor and the weeping sky.

“I have been thinking,” she said, “of when I married your father. I was younger than you are now—barely nineteen—and I had known him only three months. My mother told me I was fortunate. He was a schoolmaster, with a small living and a smaller house, and she said that I should be glad to have him, for I had no beauty to recommend me, and no fortune, and I was too fond of my own way to make a good match. I cried all the night before the wedding. I thought my heart would break. And yet—” she paused, and her hand tightened on mine—“and yet, Honora, I came to love him. Not at once. Not for years, perhaps. But the love grew, as a tree grows, slowly and without my knowing it, until one day I looked at him across the table and thought, ‘There is no man in the world I would rather have beside me.’ I tell you this because I would not have you think that gratitude is a poor foundation. It is not. It is a good, honest soil, and love will grow in it if you tend it.”

I turned to her then, and I felt the tears standing in my eyes—not the soft tears of a girl’s disappointment, but the hard, burning tears of a woman who sees her cage being built around her and knows that the door is already locked.

“And if it does not grow, mother? If the soil is barren?”

She was silent for a long moment. Then she said, very low, “You must pray, Honora. You must pray for a willing heart.”

“I have prayed,” I said. “I have prayed until my knees ached and my words ran dry. And still I feel nothing but a cold, dead weight in my breast. I cannot love him. I cannot even respect him. He is kind, and he is generous, and he is dull—so dull that when he speaks I feel my soul shrivelling within me like a leaf in a frost. And I am to spend the rest of my life listening to him, and pouring his tea, and smiling at his jokes, and pretending that I am content. Mother, I would rather die.”
