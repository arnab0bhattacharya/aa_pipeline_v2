The Reverend Septimus Sublime was a clergyman of such advanced views that he had entirely lost sight of anything resembling a orthodox doctrine. He had long ago abandoned the uncouth notion of tormenting his congregation with threats of a future state, preferring to dwell upon the more pressing and agreeable problems of the present. His church in Kensington, dedicated to St. Hilarion, was decorated with a sumptuousness that would have made a Roman cardinal weep with envy. He had replaced the grimly pious windows of his predecessors with stained glass that depicted the virtues in attire more suited to a Parisian boulevard than a Palestinian desert. Purity carried a lily, but it was a lily of Florentine manufacture, and her draperies were decidedly diaphanous.

It was to this exquisite setting that he invited Lord Mervyn Dorian, a young man of independent means and dependent morals, whose sole profession was the cultivation of the decorative arts and the collecting of rare enamels. Lord Mervyn was reputed to have never performed a good deed in his life, a circumstance which he considered the very highest compliment society could pay him.

"My dear Septimus," Lord Mervyn had remarked, lounging against the velvet of the pew with the languid grace of a tired leopard, "you ask me to preach from your pulpit. But I must warn you, I have nothing to offer but aesthetics. Ethics are the refuge of the unimaginative. The Ten Commandments are merely ten exceedingly poor rules of grammar, restricting the eloquence of the soul."

"On the contrary, Mervyn," the Reverend Sublime had replied, adjusting his ascot with a pious smile, "I want a new sensation. My congregation is dying of spiritual euthanasia. They sleep through the liturgy and wake only for the collection. I wish them to be thoroughly alarmed. A congregation that is merely comfortable is a congregation that is entirely dead."

"If you wish to alarm the English middle classes, you must attack their morality, not their theology. They will forgive a man for doubting the existence of the Almighty, but they will never forgive him for wearing a velvet jacket in the morning. Still, I shall come. I have always maintained that sin is the only enduring subject for art. Virtue is merely a lack of imagination. It is the blank canvas; sin is the colour laid upon it."

The announcement that Lord Mervyn Dorian was to occupy the pulpit of St. Hilarion’s produced a profound sensation. The Bishop of Belgravia, a ponderous prelate who had constructed his ecclesiastical career upon the solid foundation of never having an original thought, wrote a severe letter to the Reverend Sublime. He declared that Lord Mervyn was anathema to the serious mind. Naturally, this ensured that the church was crowded to excess on the appointed Sunday.

The women of Kensington arrived in carriages, bringing with them the odour of heliotrope and the heavy scent of moral indignation. They wore their most magnificent sins upon their persons, in the form of opals and pearls, and carried their prayer-books as if they were fans. The men, who had been dragged thither against their will, looked forward to the spectacle with the coarse appetite for novelty that is the one redeeming feature of the British character.

Lord Mervyn ascended the pulpit. He was dressed in a suit of black velvet, and he held a single white lily, which he regarded with more affection than he had ever bestowed upon a human being. He surveyed the congregation with his tired, beautiful eyes. Then, in a voice of exquisite musicality, he began to speak.

"It is a common error, my friends," he said, "to suppose that virtue is beautiful. Virtue is merely the absence of temptation, and the absence of anything is always ugly. The good are simply those who have never had the courage to be interesting. They are the uncoloured sketch of humanity, waiting for the master's hand to add the pigment of transgression. The wicked, on the other hand, are the finished portraits. They possess the rich shadows, the deep chiaroscuro, the subtle gradations of tone that make a life a masterpiece."

A visible shudder went through the assembly. Lady Pomfret, a woman of spotless reputation and a singularly hideous complexion, nearly dropped her smelling-salts. Lord Mervyn smiled.

"Sin," he continued, toying with the lily, "is the only thing that lends distinction to the modern world. Without it, we should be reduced to the level of the lower animals, who are notoriously moral. The horse is a singularly virtuous creature; the dog is entirely without moral complexity. But man, in his magnificent rebellion, has created beauty out of his own damnation. Every cathedral is a monument to a sense of guilt; every painted window is a confession of a crime. The saints are merely those whose sins were of a sufficiently artistic nature to warrant canonization. The wicked are merely those whose sins were found out."

The Bishop of Belgravia, who had come in person to chaperone the occasion, turned a deep and dangerous shade of purple. He clutched the arm of his stall as if he were on the deck of a sinking ship. The Reverend Sublime, seated in his stall, beamed with an air of detached aesthetic enjoyment.

"Repentance, we are told, is a duty," Lord Mervyn went on, his voice dropping to a sweet, confidential murmur. "But repentance is merely the art of spoiling a good action by regretting it. To commit a sin is to assert one's individuality; to repent of it is to surrender to the commonplace. The only excuse for having done a foolish thing is to do it again with greater style. Society is a theatre, and morality is its most tedious play. I advise you all to sin with exquisite grace. A badly committed sin is a vulgar thing; a beautifully committed sin is a work of art. It is better to be beautifully damned than to be clumsily saved. Salvation is so often a matter of chance; damnation is always a matter of choice."
