The rain, which had been falling upon the lake since the grey dawn, ceased at last, leaving the city of Geneva to steam in a dull, wet silence that pressed against the windows of the lodging house in the Rue de la Croix d’Or. It was a silence not of peace, but of an interval; a holding of breath in the murky twilight of a room that smelled of wet wool, stale tobacco, and the particular dampness of stone walls that have never known the sun.

In this room, where the gaslight hissed and flared with a nervous, yellow intensity, sat Ivan Petrovitch. He was a young man, yet he possessed the heavy, motionless gravity of one whose youth has been prematurely weighed down by the burden of conviction—or perhaps merely by the terror of losing it. He sat on a hard chair, his hands resting on his knees, his gaze fixed upon the floorboards, while the Committee of the Geneva Section of the Russian Social-Democratic Labour Party arranged themselves around the table with the slow, deliberate movements of judges—or of executioners.

There were five of them. Old Makarov, who had the face of a dried apple and the eyes of a fanatic; the Polish woman, Helena, whose bitterness was as sharp as the cold; and three others, faces in the gloom, shadows of men who had sacrificed their lives to a cause that now demanded a fresh sacrifice to prove its purity.

“It is not a question of feeling, Ivan Petrovitch,” Makarov said, his voice dry and rasping, like the sound of a saw on dead wood. “It is a question of safety. The safety of the collective. The safety of the idea.”

Ivan Petrovitch did not look up. He felt the weight of their stares upon his neck. He had been a student of chemistry in Zurich; he had come to Geneva to work, to distribute leaflets, to sit in these smoky rooms and argue about the dialectic of history until the dawn broke over the Jura mountains. Now, he was a spy. The transformation had occurred in the space of three days, wrought by the whisper of a man who was not even present.

“Gerasimov lies,” Ivan said, at last. His voice sounded strange to him, thin and echoing in the damp room.

“Gerasimov is a comrade,” Helena cut in, her tone icy. “He has bled for the cause. You are a student, a boy with soft hands. Gerasimov says he saw you with the police agents in the Café de la Gare. He says you took money. He says you gave the names of the printers.”

“Gerasimov is a liar and a thief,” Ivan repeated, but the words lacked force. They were merely words, falling upon the silence like dead leaves upon a stagnant pond. He knew, with the crushing certainty of the condemned, that the truth was not the currency they dealt in here. They dealt in the currency of suspicion, which is infinitely more abundant.

“We have no proof,” Ivan tried again, looking at Makarov. “You have no proof. Only the word of a man who is not here to face me.”

“Gerasimov is ill,” Makarov said, waving a hand dismissively. “He is at the hospital in Carouge. But his statement is written. It is signed.”

“I ask to see him. I ask to bring him here.”

“That is impossible,” Helena snapped. “Do you think we will expose a sick man to your... your police-agitated theatrics? The decision is made, Ivan Petrovitch. We cannot have a traitor in our midst. It is poison. You must understand this. It is nothing personal.”

*Nothing personal.* The phrase struck Ivan with a physical force. It was the ultimate abstraction, the final erasure of the human being. He was to be expelled, ruined, perhaps delivered to the Swiss police as an undesirable, not because he was Ivan Petrovitch, the student who loved Pushkin and the smell of the sea, but because he represented a theoretical impurity in the revolutionary body.

And yet, he thought, there was a way. Gerasimov was the accuser. Gerasimov was the witness. If Gerasimov could be made to speak the truth—or if he could be made to show his hand—the committee might waver. But Gerasimov was absent, tucked away in the safety of a hospital bed, his denunciation doing the work of a bomb while he lay insulated from the blast.

“Listen to me,” Ivan said, leaning forward, his hands gripping his knees. The gaslight threw his shadow, huge and distorted, against the peeling wallpaper behind him. “You say Gerasimov is in Carouge. I will go there. I will walk there now, in the rain. I will bring him back on my back if I must. Let him say it to my face. Let him look me in the eye and tell you I sold the names of the printers for twenty francs.”

Makarov exchanged a glance with Helena. It was a look of weary patience, the look of adults humoring a child who does not understand the rules of the game.

“You are being dramatic, Ivan,” Makarov sighed. “It is a sign of guilt. The innocent man accepts the will of the committee. The guilty man argues.”

“The innocent man fights for his life!” Ivan cried out, standing up. The chair scraped harshly against the floor. “You are not a court of law. You are a circle of fear. You are afraid of the police, so you see police spies everywhere. You are afraid of the truth, so you hide behind signatures of absent men.”

“Sit down!” Helena commanded.

But Ivan did not sit. He looked around the room, at the faces that had once been comrades, now turned into masks of suspicion. The atmosphere was thick, suffocating. He felt a sudden, terrible clarity. He could not prove his innocence to them. To prove innocence requires a presumption of innocence, and that had vanished. The only witness who could clear him was the very man who had destroyed him. It was a closed circle, a trap of logic and malice.
