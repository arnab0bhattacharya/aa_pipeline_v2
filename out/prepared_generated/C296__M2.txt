The rain in Ashwick did not fall so much as it hung, a gray curtain drawn tight against the world. It was a wet, miserable Tuesday in November, the kind of day that seemed to soak the very bones of the stone houses lining the High Street. In the study of the Assize House, the air was thick with the scent of damp wool and stale tobacco. Justice Elias Thorne sat behind his mahogany desk, the leather of his chair creaking softly as he shifted. He was a man carved from the same cold stone as the town itself, his face a map of deep lines etched by forty years of hearing the worst of human nature. His eyes, behind thick spectacles, were fixed on a single sheet of paper resting on the blotter.

The letter had arrived that morning, slipped under his door by a boy who had vanished before Thorne could question him. It was from the infirmary at St. Jude’s, written in the shaky, jagged hand of Silas Vane. Vane, who had confessed. Vane, who had taken the blame for the murder of the Miller boy three years ago. And Vane, who had explicitly named Thorne’s own conviction as the source of his final, bitter regret.

Thorne had spent the last four hours staring at that paper. He had read it until the words blurred, until the ink seemed to swim like blood in water. The evidence he had presented at the trial had been airtight, or so he had believed then. A misplaced knife, a motive rooted in debt, and a character witness who had turned state’s evidence for a lighter sentence. He had sentenced Thomas Halloway to hang. He had watched the gallows drop. He had buried the boy in the churchyard, his small face pale and peaceful in the coffin, and he had gone home to his wife, Elara, and told her he had done his duty. He had told her the law was blind and just. He had never doubted it. Not until the letter arrived.

The door to the study creaked open. Elara stood there, holding a tray with a cup of tea and a plate of cold mutton. She looked tired, her eyes hollowed by the recent loss of their son, who had died of a fever two months prior. The grief had made them both quieter, more fragile.

"Elias?" she asked, her voice soft, cautious. "You haven’t eaten."

Thorne did not look up. "I am not hungry, Elara."

She set the tray down on the side table, the clink of porcelain sharp in the silence. She moved to the desk, placing a hand on his shoulder. He flinched, a violent, involuntary jerk that startled them both. He pulled away, standing up abruptly. The chair scraped loudly against the floorboards.

"What is it?" Elara asked, her brow furrowing. "You look as if you’ve seen a ghost."

Thorne walked to the window, pressing his forehead against the cold glass. Outside, the rain intensified, drumming against the pane. The town below was a blur of slate and gray. He could see the spire of the St. Mary’s church, the place where he had preached a sermon on justice just last month. The irony was a physical pain, a twisting in his gut.

"Elara," he said, his voice rough, scraped raw. "If you did something wrong. If you had broken a promise. A promise you made to yourself, to your own integrity. And you realized, years later, that you had lied. That the lie had cost a life. What would you do?"

Elara turned to face him fully. She saw the tremor in his hands, the sweat beading on his forehead despite the chill in the room. She knew, even before he spoke, that this was not a metaphor.

"Elias," she whispered.

He turned from the window. His face was ashen. "Silas Vane has confessed. He was the one who killed the Miller boy. Thomas Halloway was innocent."

The silence that followed was absolute. Elara’s hand flew to her mouth, stifling a cry. She looked at him, her eyes wide with a mixture of horror and a terrible, dawning understanding.

"Thomas," she breathed. "My Thomas."

Thorne closed his eyes. The memory of the boy’s trial, the boy’s wide, terrified eyes, the finality of the sentence, crashed back into him with the force of a tidal wave. He had been so certain. He had been so proud of the clarity of his judgment.

"He says he did it in a fit of rage," Thorne continued, his voice flat, mechanical. "He says he was drunk. He says he panicked. He has a letter. He wants it made public. He wants the truth."

"And you?" Elara asked, her voice trembling. "What do you want, Elias?"

Thorne looked at the letter on the desk. To publish it was to destroy everything he had built. He was the most respected judge in the Assize. He was a pillar of the community. If it came out that he had sentenced an innocent man to death, his career would be over. His reputation would be in tatters. He would be a pariah, a monster. People would look at him with disgust. His children, if he ever had more, would be born into shame. The stain would be permanent.

But if he did nothing, he would be a murderer by omission. He would be the man who let an innocent man die, and who kept the secret to save his own skin. The guilt would eat him alive. He would look at Elara, at his own reflection, and see only the cowardice. He would never sleep again. He would hear the sound of the rope snapping in his dreams every night for the rest of his life.

He thought of Thomas Halloway. He remembered the boy’s mother, weeping in the gallery. He remembered the way the boy had thanked him for the "fairness" of the trial. The memory was a knife in his heart.
