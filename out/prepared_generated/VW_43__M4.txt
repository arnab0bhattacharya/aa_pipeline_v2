The morning had begun with such promise—a pale, watery sun struggling through the smoke-grey sky above Paddington, and Ernest Frobisher, his suitcase heavy with manuscripts no one had asked to see, had stepped onto the platform with the conviction that London, at last, would recognise him. He had rehearsed this arrival a hundred times in the shabby sitting-room of his aunt's house in Shrewsbury, where the wallpaper peeled in the damp and the clock ticked with a kind of malicious patience; he had imagined the streets opening before him like the pages of a book he had only to write, the editors leaning from their windows to beckon him in, the rooms—oh, the rooms!—with their tall windows and their views of plane trees, their gas-fires hissing softly in the dusk, where he would sit and write and become, at last, himself.

But London had not beckoned. London had jostled and hurried and looked through him with a thousand indifferent eyes. The landlady in Bloomsbury—Mrs. Pemberton, with her black bombazine and her little pursed mouth that seemed to hold all the disappointments of her own life as if they were lozenges—had shown him a room that smelled of cabbage and old carpets, and had asked for a week's rent in advance, and had looked at his suitcase as though it might contain something contagious. He had walked away without a word, his cheeks burning, and had stood for a long time at the corner of the street, watching the omnibuses lumber past, the clerks and the shop-girls and the men in bowler hats all flowing somewhere with such purpose, such terrible, unthinking purpose, while he stood still, his heart beating against his ribs like a bird against a window.

He had tried the newspapers. The *Chronicle*, the *Review*, the *London Mercury*—he had climbed their stairs, had sat in their ante-rooms among other young men with manuscripts on their knees, had waited while the clock on the wall measured out the minutes of his hope. And then the editors had seen him—or rather, had not seen him, had seen only the manuscript, which they had turned over with practised fingers, saying, "Yes, yes, we'll let you know," in voices that already belonged to the next interruption. One of them, a Mr. Hartley, with a red face and a manner of genial contempt, had actually read a page while Ernest watched, had frowned, had said, "A little too—ah—lyrical for us, I'm afraid," and had handed it back as though returning something soiled. The word "lyrical" had lodged in Ernest's chest like a splinter. He had walked out into the Strand, where the afternoon light was turning the windows to gold, and had felt the whole vast machinery of the city pressing down upon him, indifferent, enormous, and he had understood—with a clarity that was almost relief—that he was nothing here, that his name meant nothing, that the things he had written in the quiet of Shrewsbury, which had seemed to him so true, so necessary, were merely words, and words, in London, were cheap as air.

And yet—and yet—the evening had come, and with it a strange, unreasonable lightness. He had walked for hours, it seemed, past the grey stone of the British Museum, where the lions sat in the fading light with their stone eyes fixed on nothing; past the squares where the plane trees shed their bark in patches, like old men peeling off their clothes; past the little shops in Soho where the windows glowed with strange foods, olives and peppers and cheeses, and the smell of coffee drifted out into the street like a memory of somewhere he had never been. The lamps were being lit now, one by one, by a man with a long pole who moved down the street with the unhurried dignity of a priest performing some ancient rite, and Ernest stopped to watch him, and something in the gesture—the reach, the small flame, the retreating darkness—struck him as unbearably beautiful, though he could not have said why.

He found himself, without quite knowing how, on a bridge—which bridge he could not say, for the city had become a maze of bridges and streets and squares, all flowing into one another—and he stood at the parapet and looked down at the river, which was not blue or silver but a kind of oily brown, streaked with the reflected lights of the embankment, and the water moved beneath him with a slow, patient, secretive sound, like someone breathing in their sleep. A barge went past, low in the water, and a gull screamed somewhere overhead, and Ernest thought: I am here. I am actually here. And the thought was not triumphant, as he had imagined it would be, but quiet, and a little sad, and strangely peaceful, as though he had been carrying something heavy for a very long time and had only just set it down.

The river, he thought, had been here before him, and would be here after. The bridge, the lamps, the city—they did not need him. They had their own life, their own vast, indifferent continuity, and he was merely a passer-by, a shadow on the parapet, a young man with a suitcase and a bundle of manuscripts that no one wanted. And yet—and here his thoughts turned and doubled back on themselves, as they always did, like a dog chasing its own tail—and yet, was that not precisely the thing he had come to write about? Not the editors, not the success, not the rooms with their gas-fires and their views of plane trees, but this—the river at dusk, the lamplighter, the gull's cry, the vast, unheeding life of the city that went on and on, and the small, stubborn, ridiculous human heart that beat against it, and beat, and beat, and would not be quiet.
