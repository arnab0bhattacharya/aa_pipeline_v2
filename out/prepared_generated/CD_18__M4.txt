The fog came off the river at Fennock’s Wharf in the manner of a damp and inquisitive neighbour, insinuating itself through keyholes, curling under doors, and settling upon the cobbles with the quiet persistence of unpaid debt. It was a fog that smelled of malt and mud and the sour breath of the tide, and it wrapped the brewery of Messrs. Tumbler and Croke as though the building itself had drawn a blanket about its shoulders against the chill. Within, however, all was steam and industry; the great copper vessels sang and sighed, the mash-tuns breathed their sweet and heavy breath, and the men moved through the vapour like souls in a tolerable purgatory, their aprons stiff with labour, their voices echoing against the brick.

Among them, and indeed at their head in all matters of craft, was one Abel Quince, a cooper of some five-and-forty years, whose hands were so accustomed to the drawing of staves and the driving of hoops that they seemed to have grown into the shape of the work itself, and whose face, when he removed his cap, was discovered to be of a colour betwixt the oak and the walnut, as though the timber had imparted to him something of its own enduring character. He was a widower, with two children—a boy of twelve named Tobias, and a girl of nine called Lucy—and he lodged in a crooked little house in Cinder Lane, where the chimney smoked in easterly weather and the stairs complained of every footfall. He was a man of few words and straight dealings; and if he had a fault, it was that he believed the world to be, upon the whole, as honest as his own hands, and that men in authority were so because they deserved to be.

It was upon a Tuesday, when the fog had lifted sufficiently to permit a man to see the length of his own arm, that Abel Quince made a discovery that was to trouble the quiet of his soul for many a sleepless night thereafter.

He had gone down to the lower cellar—that cool and vaulted place where the great casks lay in their ranks like sleeping giants—to examine a barrel of porter that had been reported as leaking at the seam. The cellar was dim, lit by a single lamp that hung from a beam and swung gently with the draught, casting shadows that moved and shifted like uneasy thoughts. Abel found the cask, marked it with chalk, and was about to ascend, when his ear caught a sound—a thin, trickling sound, as of liquid falling into liquid—and his eye, following the sound, beheld in the corner a sight that stopped him where he stood.

It was Mr. Josiah Croke himself—the junior partner, a man of florid countenance and expensive whiskers, who wore a diamond pin in his cravat and spoke of the brewery as “our humble endeavour”—standing with his coat off and his sleeves rolled to the elbow, a length of hose in his hand, and before him a row of barrels destined, as Abel well knew, for the workhouse at Marrowgate. From a smaller cask beside him, Mr. Croke was drawing a pale and watery fluid, which he was conducting, by means of the hose, into the bung-hole of each barrel in turn; and as he did so, he hummed a tune of the theatre, and smiled at his own dexterity.

Abel Quince stood as one turned to stone. The lamp swung; the shadows danced; the trickling continued. He would have withdrawn, but his foot, shifting upon the gritty floor, gave out a sound, and Mr. Croke looked up.

For a moment, the two men regarded one another. Then Mr. Croke’s face, which had been open and pleasant, underwent a change so rapid and so complete that it was as though a curtain had been drawn across it. He set down the hose, wiped his hands upon a cloth, and advanced with a smile that had no warmth in it.

“Quince,” said he, “you are early at your duties.”

“I came to see the porter cask, sir,” said Abel, “that leaks at the seam.”

“Ah, the porter cask. Very good. Very proper.” Mr. Croke’s eye flicked to the barrels, then back to Abel’s face. “You have observed me, I perceive, at my—shall we say—my little experiment.”

“I have observed you, sir,” said Abel, “putting water into the workhouse ale.”

Mr. Croke laughed—a short, sharp laugh, like the bark of a fox. “Water, Quince? Water? You are mistaken. It is a—a preparation. A tonic. For the health of the inmates. The guardians have complained of the strength of our ale; they say it inflames the passions of the poor. And so, in my benevolence, I am—tempering it. Yes. Tempering it.”

Abel said nothing. He looked at the hose, at the pale fluid, at the barrels stamped with the workhouse mark, and he felt something within him—some honest and stubborn thing that had been planted there in childhood and had grown with him—rise up and stand firm.

“Sir,” said he, slowly, “I have been a cooper these thirty years. I know water when I see it, and I know ale. That is water.”

Mr. Croke’s smile vanished. He stepped closer, and his voice, when he spoke, was low and rapid.

“And if it is, Quince—what then? What then? The workhouse gets its ale at a price that would ruin us. The guardians are paupers in spirit and in purse. We must economise, or we must close our doors. Would you have us close our doors? Would you have the men turned off—you among them—and the children of Fennock’s Wharf go hungry? Think, man. Think of your boy and girl. Think of your place. You are the only man who knows. The only man. And a word from you—a single word—would set the whole town by the ears, and you would be the one to suffer. Not I. I have friends. I have the magistrates. I have—but no matter. You are a sensible man. You will keep your counsel.”
