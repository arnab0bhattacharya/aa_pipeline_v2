The sun was not merely bright; it was a physical weight, pressing the air into a shimmering, gelatinous stillness that made the very stones of the path seem to breathe. Up the side of Mount Ceres, the party moved in a loose, disjointed ribbon, their shadows short and sharp against the limestone, like black slashes on white silk. There was no sound but the crunch of sandals on gravel and the distant, hollow boom of the sea, which seemed less like water than like a vast, sleeping beast turning over in its dreams.

Eleanor walked slightly behind, her gaze fixed not on the path but on the way the light caught the edge of a thorny bush, turning the green leaves to glass. She thought of the house in London, the damp chill of the November fog, and how strange it was that one could carry such a cold inside one’s chest while the external world blazed with such aggressive warmth. Beside her, Julian was walking quickly, his stride long and impatient, his face flushed with the effort of the climb and the heat. He was looking at Clara, who was ahead of them, her hat tilted back, her face a mask of bright, intelligent amusement.

“You’re lagging,” Julian said, his voice thin in the hot air. “We shall be late for the view. The light is best before it gets too slanting.”

Eleanor did not look up. “The light is always best,” she said softly. “It doesn’t wait for us to be punctual.”

There was a pause, a slight tension in the air, a thread of it that might have snapped. Julian stopped, turning to face her. His eyes were bright, almost feverish. “You are being difficult, Eleanor. You are always being difficult. You sit in the corners and judge, and you say nothing, and then you say something that cuts like a knife, and you don’t even know you’ve done it.”

Clara, hearing this, turned her head. The movement was slow, deliberate. “Perhaps,” she said, her voice light, carrying the scent of the wild thyme that grew in the crevices of the rocks, “perhaps she is simply tired. The heat does strange things to the mind. It makes one see things that are not there, or rather, it makes one see things that are there, but which one prefers to ignore.”

Eleanor looked at Clara. There was a flicker of something in Clara’s eyes, a sharpness, a challenge. Was it pity? Or was it contempt? Eleanor felt a sudden, sharp pang of insecurity, a fear that she was indeed that small, that invisible figure in the corner, judging from the shadows. She wanted to speak, to explain, to say that she was not judging, only observing, that the world was so full of noise and motion that she had to retreat into the silence of her own mind to make sense of it. But the words would not come. They felt too heavy, too clumsy for the lightness of the moment.

Julian resumed his walk, but his step was heavier now. The quarrel hung in the air, a thin film of dust that settled over everything. They walked on in silence, the only sound the rhythm of their breathing and the dry rustle of their clothes. The path narrowed, winding through a grove of cypresses that cast deep, cool shadows on the ground. The air here was different; it was cooler, darker, and smelled of resin and earth.

Clara slowed her pace until she was level with Eleanor. “You are afraid of him,” she said, not a question, but a statement, her voice low, intimate.

Eleanor looked at her. “No,” she said. “I am afraid of myself. Of what I think. It is too loud. It drowns out everything else.”

Clara smiled, a sad, fleeting smile. “We are all drowning, Eleanor. We just do it in different waters.” She reached out and touched Eleanor’s arm, a brief, warm pressure that was both comforting and intrusive. “Come. Let us go on. The view is waiting.”

They climbed higher. The trees thinned, and the open sky expanded above them, a vast dome of blue so intense it hurt the eyes. The heat was still there, but it was tempered by the wind, which blew in soft, warm gusts from the sea, carrying the salt spray and the smell of distance. They reached the summit, a broad, flat plateau of rock and scrub, overlooking the bay below.

The view was immense, terrifying in its scale. The sea stretched out to the horizon, a flat, glittering expanse of blue and green, dotted with white sails that looked like scraps of paper. The coastline was a jagged line of white cliffs and dark forests, and in the distance, the mountains of the mainland rose up, blue and hazy, like ghosts. The sun was beginning to dip, and the light changed, becoming softer, more golden, casting long shadows that stretched out across the rocks.

They stood there, the four of them, silent. The words that had been exchanged on the climb seemed to have evaporated, like morning dew in the sun. Julian leaned on his stick, looking out at the sea, his face serious, his eyes closed. Clara stood beside him, her hand on his shoulder, her face turned towards the light, her expression peaceful, almost serene. Eleanor stood apart, looking at the water, seeing in its ripples the reflection of her own face, distorted, broken, but still there.

There was a moment, a fleeting instant, when the boundaries between them seemed to dissolve. They were not individuals, but parts of a single, breathing whole, united by the view, by the light, by the heat. They were part of the landscape, part of the sky, part of the sea. For a second, there was no quarrel, no fear, no judgment. There was only the present, the eternal, unchanging now.
