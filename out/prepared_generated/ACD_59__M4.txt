The fog had lain over London since Tuesday, a greasy, yellow vapour that turned the gas-lamps into dim smears of light and reduced the passing hansom to a clatter of hooves and a shadow. In such weather a man may be followed a great distance and yet see nothing of his pursuer but a darker patch against the gloom. It was in this belief that Mrs. Henrietta Fanshawe had come to consult me, and she sat now upon the edge of my friend’s armchair, her hands clasped tightly in her lap, her whole attitude one of rigid self-command.

“You must think me foolish, Mr. Pemberton,” said she, “to trouble you with a matter which may be no more than a woman’s fancy. But my husband is not himself. He has been home from the mission at T’ai-yüan for a fortnight, and in all that time he has not slept a single night through.”

Pemberton laid down his pipe and regarded her with that quiet, attentive gravity which had won him the confidence of half the troubled folk in London.

“Pray tell me everything, madam, from the beginning. Omit nothing, however trifling it may appear.”

“Then I must begin with the man in the grey ulster. On the very evening of my husband’s arrival—it was the fourteenth, and he had come up from Tilbury by the afternoon train—we walked together from the station to our house in Bloomsbury. The fog was thick, as it has been all week, and I thought nothing of it when I observed a man standing beneath the lamp at the corner of our street. But when we had gone a hundred yards further, I saw him again, or his very image, at the mouth of the mews behind the square. He was a tall, spare man, and he wore a grey ulster with the collar turned up to his ears. I said nothing to my husband, for he was weary from his journey.”

“And you saw this figure again?”

“Every day, Mr. Pemberton. Every day for a fortnight. He is always at a distance—never nearer than fifty paces, never far enough to be lost. He does not follow me, you understand. It is my husband he follows. When the Reverend Fanshawe walks to the Missionary Society in the Strand, the grey ulster is behind him. When he visits his publisher in Paternoster Row, the grey ulster waits at the corner. And yet the man never approaches, never speaks, never makes himself known. It is like being haunted by a shadow that has taken human form.”

“Has your husband observed him?”

“I believe he has, though he will not speak of it. Once, as we came out of church, I saw him turn sharply and stare down the street, and his face was the colour of paper. I asked him what was wrong, and he said it was the fog that troubled his eyes. But his eyes were fixed upon the figure in the grey coat.”

Pemberton was silent for a moment, his thin fingers pressed together.

“You have told me of the follower. You spoke on the telephone of something else—of the luggage.”

Mrs. Fanshawe’s composure, which had held so bravely, seemed for an instant to falter. She opened her reticule and drew from it a small object wrapped in tissue, which she laid upon the table.

“This is a Chinese jade seal, Mr. Pemberton. My husband brought it from T’ai-yüan as a curiosity. It has no great value. But observe the manner in which it was packed.”

She unfolded the tissue. Within lay a square of white silk, and within that a wooden box of the sort in which Eastern merchants send their wares. But the box had been opened and closed again; the paper seal upon its lid, bearing the stamp of a Peking firm, was broken, and the silk had been refolded in a fashion quite unlike the original.

“The trunk came up from the docks three days after my husband,” she continued. “It had been searched, Mr. Pemberton—searched with the most extraordinary care. Every garment was unfolded and refolded, every paper displaced by the breadth of a hair. And yet nothing was taken. The jade seal was left, though it lay at the very bottom of the trunk, beneath the shirts. Whoever searched it was looking for something in particular, and was not satisfied until he had found it—or failed to find it.”

“And you are persuaded that the searcher was the man in the grey ulster?”

“I am persuaded of nothing. I am only afraid.”

Pemberton rose and went to the window, where he stood for a long time looking out into the fog. Then he turned.

“I shall require to see your husband, madam. Not, I think, at your house. Let him come to me here, at four o’clock tomorrow, and let him come alone.”

The Reverend Edmund Fanshawe was a man of fifty, with the stoop of the scholar and the clear, direct gaze of one who has spent his life in the open air of a foreign land. He came at the hour appointed, and he heard Pemberton’s account of his wife’s fears with a face that was very still.

“I have been a fool, Mr. Pemberton,” said he at last. “I should have told my wife. But I hoped—I hoped that if I said nothing, the thing would pass.”

“Tell me about T’ai-yüan.”

The clergyman drew a long breath. “There was a man in our compound, a native Christian named Liu. He was my servant, my friend, my brother in the faith. He died in the spring, of the fever. And in his dying he gave me a packet—a small, sealed packet of papers—and he begged me to carry it to England and to place it in the hands of a certain person in London. He would tell me nothing of its contents, save that they were the evidence of a great crime, and that the men who had committed it would kill to recover them.”
