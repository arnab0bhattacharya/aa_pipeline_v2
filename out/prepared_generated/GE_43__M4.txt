The shop of Abel Thorne, wheelwright and undertaker, stood at the lower end of the market-place in the town of Sallowby, where the cobbles gave way to a miry lane that led down to the canal wharf; and it was there, in the dim workshop that smelt of oakum, varnish, and the damp sweetness of new-sawn elm, that Reuben Thorne stood on a November evening, planing a coffin-lid with a slow, deliberate stroke, as though the work were a kind of thinking made visible. He was a broad-shouldered man of seven-and-twenty, with a face that had the grave composure of one accustomed to weigh matters in private, and hands that knew the grain of every timber in the yard. Since his mother’s death in the spring—a death which had been to him rather a long, slow sinking of the light than any sudden darkening—he had kept the business and the household together, and had kept with them his brother Samuel, a lad of nineteen whose mind had never come to its full stature, so that he was at once a child to be watched and a man to be pitied.

It is one of the quieter tragedies of provincial life that a promise made at a death-bed should seem, to the one who made it, a thing as solid as a wall of brick, while to others it is but a vapour that the first wind of opportunity may blow away. Reuben had promised his mother—and she had exacted the promise with the tenacity of a woman who had nothing else to leave—that he would never forsake Samuel; and in the first months of his grief he had found a melancholy comfort in the bond, as though the dead hand still rested on his shoulder. But the months had brought counsel of another sort. There came letters from a cousin at Liverpool, one Ezra Pike, who had made the passage to Canada and was now a man of substance in the timber trade at Quebec; and these letters spoke of land to be had for the asking, of a country where a man of Reuben’s craft might rise as a builder and a merchant, and of a partnership open to a kinsman of energy and probity. The letters were read aloud in the little parlour behind the shop, and the reading of them wrought in Reuben a strange ferment, half hope and half compunction.

“It’s a fine thing for thee, lad,” said his aunt, Mrs. Mabbot, who kept a small chandler’s shop over against the church, and who came in of an evening to darn the linen and to deliver her mind upon all subjects. “A fine thing, and thee’t not to let it slip for the sake of—thee know’st what. The Lord helps them as helps theirselves, and there’s no sin in bettering thy lot.”

“But there’s Samuel,” said Reuben, without lifting his eyes from the fire.

“Samuel!” cried Mrs. Mabbot, with a toss of her head. “And what’s to become of Samuel if thee stop here and starve? For starve thee will, Reuben, when the railway comes and the coaches go off the road, and there’s no more call for wheels than there is for a fiddler at a funeral. And Samuel—why, the lad’s happy enough with his hoop and his bits of wood; he’ll not miss thee above a week.”

“He’ll miss me more than that,” said Reuben, quietly. “He’s like a dog for knowing where his master is.”

“And is a dog to keep a man from his fortune?” said Mrs. Mabbot, with the triumphant air of one who has found the unanswerable. “Thee’t too tender-hearted by half, Reuben, and that’s the truth. Thy poor mother—rest her soul—she was a good woman, but she was always one to make a great matter of a small promise. It was her way. She’d have had thee promise to fetch the moon down if she’d thought on’t.”

This was not altogether just to the dead, and Reuben felt the injustice without being able to frame a reply; for there are arguments which wound us less by their falsehood than by the grain of truth they carry, and Mrs. Mabbot’s had the grain of truth that a promise made in the weakness of another’s extremity is sometimes the child of our own pity rather than of a clear-sighted duty. He said nothing, but went out into the yard and stood looking at the dark canal, where the last barge of the day was sliding by with a lantern at its bow, and the water gave back the light in long, wavering streaks.

It was on the Sunday following that the matter came to a head. Reuben had walked with Samuel along the tow-path to the lock, as was their custom, and Samuel had been full of a great project of building a boat out of the lid of a chest, a project which he explained with the eager, inconsequent fluency of one whose mind had never learned the difference between a plan and a dream. Reuben listened, and answered kindly, and all the while there was a cold weight at his heart, for he had that morning received from Ezra Pike a letter more urgent than the rest, fixing a date by which the passage must be taken, and speaking of a vessel then lying at Liverpool and due to sail with the spring tides.

“Samuel,” he said at last, “how wouldst thee like to live with Aunt Mabbot for a while—thee and me to part company for a bit?”

Samuel stopped in his walk and looked at him with the troubled, searching look of a child who suspects a meaning he cannot grasp. “Thee’t going away?” he said.

“I’m thinking on’t—to a far country, where there’s work and money to be got.”

“And me?” said Samuel. “Thee’t not going to leave me, Reuben? Mother said thee’d never leave me.”
