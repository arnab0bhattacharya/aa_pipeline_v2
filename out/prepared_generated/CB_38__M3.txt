The rain had not ceased its assault on the cobbled court of the Rue d'Allure since Matins. It beat now against the leaded casement of the schoolroom with a dreary, unrelenting fury, finding a harsh accord with the tempest that had, for three days past, possessed my own mind. I stood at the desk, overseeing the parsing of a French verb by a row of blinking, lethargic scholars, but my eyes saw neither the slate nor the ink-stained fingers hovering over it. They were drawn, as by a physical compulsion, to the far end of the long, bleak room, where Eleanor Marlow sat at her needlework.

She was pale. There was a starkness in her pallor that seemed to rebuke the heavy, Flemish gloom of the place. She was not beautiful in the fashion of the drawing-room; her features were too sharp, her frame too fragile, to court the shallow praises of a society that values mere flesh and symmetry. But in those dark, deep-set eyes there burned a quiet, unquenchable fire—a spirit that had known hardship, and, knowing it, did not cower. I watched the slight, rapid rise of her chest, the steadfast bend of her head, and a fierce, choking indignation swelled within me. She was poor. She was friendless. She was entirely at the mercy of the world’s cold charity, and I—I was but a step above her, trembling on the precipice of a choice that might make or mar us both.

The heavy oak door swung open. It was Vandeput, the Belgian servant, his face wearing its customary mask of surly indifference. "Monsieur Hewitt," said he, in his guttural French, "the Director requires your presence in his cabinet. Instantly."

I laid down my pen. A sudden, prophetic thrill shot through my veins. I knew the hour of reckoning was come. As I crossed the threshold, my gaze inadvertently met Eleanor’s. It was but a momentary flash, yet in that fleeting glance I read a whole volume of apprehensive sympathy. I passed out, closing the heavy door behind me, shutting myself away from her, and from the peace that had lately been my only solace in this foreign purgatory.

The cabinet of Monsieur Vandereck was a room of dark panels and darker purposes. The Director stood by the fire, his broad, heavy frame casting a long, distorted shadow across the oaken boards. He was a man who wore his morality like a Sunday coat, taking it off when convenience demanded, and putting it on when the eyes of the respectable were upon him. His niece, Mademoiselle Hortense, sat on a low stool near the stove, her hands folded in her lap. She was a girl of ample form and fair, insipid face; her eyes were of a watery blue, wholly destitute of depth or animation. She possessed, however, two thousand pounds a year, and a connection which it would be madness for an obscure English master to despise.

"Sit, Monsieur Hewitt," began Vandereck, his voice assuming a tone of unctuous patronage that set my very teeth on edge. "I have been observing your conduct of late. You are diligent. Your classes in the English grammar are not wanting in discipline. I am not slow to recognise merit, especially when it is found in a young man of—shall we say—humble extraction."

I bowed. I would not give him the advantage of a humble word.

"The position of under-master," he continued, rolling a pinch of snuff between his thick thumb and forefinger, "is one of dependence. It is a position I am willing to elevate you from. I propose to make you my associate. The duties will be light, the emoluments will be considerable. You will have a voice in the councils of this establishment."

He paused. The fire hissed. Mademoiselle Hortense gazed at the carpet, a faint, silly smile hovering on her lips.

"I am bound to thank you, Monsieur," I replied, with a hard-won restraint that cost me no little effort. "Such an offer is unexpected. May I ask the conditions upon which this elevation is to be granted?"

"Conditions!" He waved his hand with an air of benevolent tolerance. "Let us not call them conditions. Let us call them the natural, the harmonious ties of domestic life. I am an old man. I desire to see my niece established under the guardianship of a capable, ambitious husband. She is amiable. She is wealthy. You are solitary in a foreign land. I offer you her hand, and with it, the partnership of which I speak."

The words fell upon my ear like the slow dropping of ice-water upon a heated stone. I looked at Hortense. She was utterly, hopelessly commonplace. To link my life with hers would be to chain my soul to a dead weight. It would be to barter my inner truth for a mess of pottage. And yet, the alternative! To remain a drudge, to labour on for a pittance, to see Eleanor Marlow, the woman whose intellect and silent fortitude had twined themselves about my very heart-strings, exposed to the slights and miseries of a teacher's life, perhaps, in time, torn from me by the cruel necessities of poverty. Was I authorized to sacrifice her welfare to my scruples?

For a full minute the combat raged within me. I felt the strong, subtle temptation of the serpent. It whispered of independence, of a fireside of my own, of the power to shield the very woman I loved, should fate ever throw her defenceless on my hands. But beneath this plausible whisper, there rose the stern, unyielding voice of conscience. Could I purchase the power to protect Eleanor by making a mock of the holiest ordinance of God? Could I vow love to Hortense, and give her only a cold, calculating respect? I should be a liar, a traitor, and a coward.

I raised my head. The flush of indignation had mounted to my cheek.
