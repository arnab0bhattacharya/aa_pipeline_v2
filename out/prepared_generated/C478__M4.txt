The roses at Ferndale House were in their final, blowsy glory, shedding petals onto the croquet lawn like a bride’s discarded confetti. Mrs. Araminta Vance, the hostess, presided over the tea table with a smile that did not quite reach her eyes, for a quiet panic was rippling through her guests, a panic as subtle and pervasive as the scent of the overblown blooms.

It had begun an hour ago. Mrs. Vance’s daughter, Beatrice, a girl of earnest disposition and a notorious diarist, had been seen frantically patting the pockets of her pale blue skirt, her face draining of colour. A small, morocco-bound book with a gilt clasp was missing. It was, she whispered to her closest friend, Miss Letitia Pringle, her book of compliments.

“Every one I have ever received,” she hissed, her voice tight with horror. “Since I was fourteen. Written down. With the name of the person who gave it.”

Miss Pringle had gone quite pale. “Good heavens, Beatrice. The one from the Bishop about your confirmation?”

“The very first entry,” Beatrice confirmed, her eyes wide with despair.

The news, as such news will, travelled with the speed of a spark in a dry hayfield. By the time the guests had gathered for the second pouring of tea, a brittle, feverish gaiety had descended. Conversations were conducted in hushed, urgent tones. Mr. Hubert Fowler, a portly gentleman who had complimented Beatrice’s ‘exquisite taste in music’ after a particularly trying piano recital, was now avoiding her eye with the fervour of a man dodging a creditor. Mrs. Daventry, a widow of formidable reputation, who had once told Beatrice her embroidery was ‘almost professional,’ clutched her teacup so tightly her knuckles were white. Even the vicar, Mr. Peabody, who had praised her ‘pious demeanour,’ seemed to be perspiring more than the warmth of the afternoon warranted.

The book was a veritable arsenal of social embarrassment. A misplaced word of praise, a flirtatious endearment, a well-meant but clumsy observation—all were recorded, catalogued, and now, potentially, to be broadcast. The thought of one’s own carefully constructed compliment being read aloud, perhaps with a raised eyebrow or a mocking tone, was a social death sentence. What if one’s words, meant to be fleeting and gracious, were revealed to be trite, or worse, insincere? The garden party, with its trestle tables groaning under the weight of seed cake and cucumber sandwiches, had become a landscape of dread.

It was into this atmosphere of suppressed hysteria that Mr. Julian Ashworth wandered. A young man of a quiet, observant nature, he was a distant cousin of the Vances, invited out of a sense of familial duty. He was not a man of sparkling wit or easy compliments. In fact, in the course of his acquaintance with Beatrice, which spanned several years of such familial gatherings, he had never paid her a single one. He found her pleasant enough, but his mind was more often occupied with the botanical intricacies of the gardens or the architectural follies of the neighbouring estates than with the social graces expected of a young man in a drawing room. He had spent the better part of the afternoon examining a particularly fine specimen of a copper beech, and was now seeking a quiet corner to enjoy a slice of cake.

He found his corner near the old stone sundial, half-hidden by a cascade of clematis. As he settled onto a wrought-iron bench, his foot nudged something solid. He bent down and retrieved a small, dark red book from the grass, its gilt clasp winking in the dappled sunlight. He turned it over in his hands. It was clearly a lady’s diary. He was about to rise and seek out its owner when a sudden, sharp cry from the direction of the rose arbour made him pause.

“It’s found! It’s found!” cried a voice, and Julian saw a knot of guests, led by a distraught Beatrice, hurrying towards him. Their faces were a mixture of relief and terror.

Beatrice’s eyes fell upon the book in his hand. She let out a small gasp, her hand flying to her throat. The entire party seemed to hold its breath. Mrs. Daventry looked as if she might swoon. Mr. Fowler tugged at his collar as if it had suddenly shrunk.

“Oh, Mr. Ashworth,” Beatrice breathed, her voice trembling. “You have found my… my journal. How can I ever thank you?”

Before Julian could hand it over, a strained silence fell. The guests were frozen, their eyes fixed on the little book. It was a silence pregnant with the unspoken, a tableau of collective guilt and fear. The book was a bomb, and Julian, unwittingly, held the fuse.

He saw the panic in their eyes, the desperate plea in Beatrice’s. He understood, with a sudden, intuitive clarity, the nature of the book and the reason for the party’s strange mood. He felt a pang of something like pity for them all—for the vain, the anxious, the insincere, and for the girl who had collected their words like a magpie hoarding shiny trinkets.

He did not open the book. He did not even glance at its clasp. Instead, he held it out to Beatrice with a calm, steady hand. He looked directly at her, and then his gaze swept over the assembled company, a quiet, unreadable expression on his face. The silence stretched, becoming almost unbearable.

Then, he spoke. His voice was low and even, carrying clearly in the still afternoon air.

“I believe you dropped this, Miss Vance,” he said, his tone utterly devoid of accusation or curiosity. “The clasp is quite secure. I am glad I found it before the damp could get to it.”

He placed the book in her trembling hands. For a moment, Beatrice simply stared at him, her eyes wide with a mixture of shock and gratitude. The tension in the air did not break so much as it dissolved, seeping away like water into sand. The guests began to breathe again. Mrs. Daventry’s colour returned. Mr. Fowler’s collar seemed to loosen of its own accord.
