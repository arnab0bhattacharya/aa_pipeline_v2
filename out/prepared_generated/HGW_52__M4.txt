The trouble came upon Bracebridge in the third week of November, and it came, as such things do, not with a dramatic crash but with a slow, grinding silence. First the great mills along the canal fell still, one by one, like tired men sitting down. Then the railway sidings emptied of their trucks, and the long lines of idle wagons stood in the sidings like cattle in a drought. By the end of the month the city was a vast, cold machine, and the machine had stopped.

The cause was simple enough and quite absurd. The coal was there, in the earth, in the trucks, in the yards—but it could not be got to the furnaces. A combination of a wet autumn, a strike upon the northern lines, and a quite extraordinary piece of mismanagement in the offices of the Coal Distribution Board had produced a shortage that no one had foreseen and no one seemed able to mend. The papers spoke of it as a “crisis,” which is the English way of saying that everyone is cold and no one knows what to do.

Now, in the parish of St. Clement’s, in a narrow yard behind the High Street, there stood the coal business of Mr. Herbert Pargiter. It was not a large business—two carts, a weighing machine, and a shed that had once been a stable—but Mr. Pargiter had been in coal for thirty years, and he had a way with him. He was a stout, red-faced man with a habit of rubbing his hands together as though he were washing them, and he had a great faith in the virtue of foresight. When the first rumours of the strike had reached Bracebridge in October, Mr. Pargiter had done what he had always done in times of uncertainty: he had bought. He had bought quietly, through agents, in small lots, and he had stored the coal in the old stable and in a disused warehouse off the canal. And now, while the rest of Bracebridge shivered, Mr. Pargiter’s yard was full.

The news of this hoard did not remain secret long. It never does. A coal merchant with coal, in a city without it, is a figure of considerable interest. First came the neighbours, timidly at first, then with a kind of desperate cheerfulness, asking for a sack, a half-sack, a few shillings’ worth. Mr. Pargiter refused them all with a bland and regretful smile. He was “saving it for his regular customers,” he said. His regular customers, it soon appeared, were those who could pay the new price, which had risen, in the space of a fortnight, from twenty-two shillings a ton to four pounds.

Then came the government.

It came in the person of a young man from the Board of Trade, a Mr. Filby, who arrived in a motor-car that attracted a crowd in the High Street and who sat in Mr. Pargiter’s little office with his hat on his knees and a look of pained efficiency upon his face.

“The fact is, Mr. Pargiter,” said Mr. Filby, “the situation is very serious. The hospitals are without fuel. The workhouse is without fuel. There is talk of riots in the East End. The Government is prepared to requisition your stock at a fair price—a very fair price, I may say—and to distribute it where it is most needed.”

Mr. Pargiter rubbed his hands.

“And what,” said he, “do you call a fair price?”

Mr. Filby named a figure. It was a good figure—better than the old price, certainly—but it was not the figure that Mr. Pargiter had in his mind. He had in his mind a figure that would enable him to retire, to buy a small house at Southport, to put his daughter into a position that befitted her education. He named that figure.

Mr. Filby’s face fell.

“That is—that is quite out of the question,” he said. “The Government cannot pay such a sum. It would be—it would be profiteering.”

“It is the market price,” said Mr. Pargiter, with dignity. “I am a business man, Mr. Filby. I have my stock, and I have my expenses. I cannot be expected to give it away.”

“No one is asking you to give it away,” said Mr. Filby, with some heat. “We are asking you to sell it at a reasonable profit to a Government that is trying to prevent a catastrophe. Do you realise that there are people in this city who are burning their furniture?”

Mr. Pargiter did realise it. He had seen the queues at the pawnbrokers. He had heard the talk in the public-house. But he had also seen the queues at his own gate, and he had noted, with a kind of grim satisfaction, that the people in those queues were the same people who had jeered at him for years as a “small man” and a “retailer.” He had a long memory, had Mr. Pargiter, and a great many small scores to settle.

“I am sorry,” he said. “I have my duty to my family.”

Mr. Filby went away, and the crowd outside the yard grew larger. There were mutterings now, and once a stone was thrown. Mr. Pargiter sat in his office with the door locked and the blinds drawn, and rubbed his hands and looked at his ledger.

It was at this point that his daughter came in.

Miss Ada Pargiter was a young woman of twenty-four, who had been to a college in London and had come back with a great many ideas that her father found inconvenient. She was a socialist, or something of the sort—her father was never quite sure what the word meant, but he knew it involved a great deal of talking and a refusal to be impressed by her father’s money. She was a thin, pale girl with her hair cut short, and she had a way of looking at her father as though he were a specimen under a glass.
