The scheme of a picnic at Delbury Point had been for some time the object of Miss Eleanor Vane’s most sanguine hopes, and the occasion of her sister’s more fanciful ones. To Mrs. Vane, it was a simple matter of providing the hamper; to Miss Harriet, it was an opportunity for picturesque sensibility; but to Eleanor, who was now two-and-twenty and possessed of a good understanding, it was a critical juncture in the affairs of Mr. Arthur Croft. That gentleman, lately arrived in the neighbourhood to visit his uncle, Sir Giles, had already distinguished himself in Eleanor’s estimation by a pleasing countenance, a moderate fortune, and, most importantly, a discernment which she flattered herself he had shown in preferring her conversation to her sister’s more effusive remarks.

The plan, which had been settled with great satisfaction in the Vane drawing-room, was destined to a sudden and unwelcome alteration. A letter, penned in a character so agitated as to be nearly illegible, arrived from Mrs. Penelope Prattle, their great-aunt, a lady whose eccentricities were a source of perpetual, if private, mortification to the family. She announced, with a dramatic flourish of underlinings, that having heard of their intended excursion, and finding the day’s weather most congenial to her spirits, she would be of their party. “And,” the postscript added, “Polly, who is sadly in need of country air, shall accompany me. He has been uncommonly quiet of late, and a change of scene is what he requires.”

A general consternation prevailed. Mrs. Vane declared she had not enough cold chicken for so large a party, a point of practical distress which she felt more keenly than any social embarrassment. Harriet lamented that the presence of an elderly woman and a bird would ruin the romantic atmosphere of the cliffs. Eleanor, with a sinking heart, foresaw the immediate destruction of her hopes. The quiet, intelligent tête-à-tête she had imagined with Mr. Croft, walking along the headland while the others took their repast, now seemed a fantasy. She saw instead Mrs. Prattle’s voluble complaints and, worse, the unpredictable utterances of Polly, a parrot of some reputation, having been bequeathed to her aunt by a sea-captain of her acquaintance.

“It cannot be helped,” Eleanor said, with a composure she was far from feeling. “We cannot refuse my aunt, and to attempt to alter the plan would be to invite a scene of far greater discomfort.”

Her mother and sister saw the wisdom of this, though they did not embrace it with any degree of cheerfulness. When the day arrived, Mrs. Prattle was seen to approach in a chaise so crowded with shawls, cushions, and a large wicker cage that she herself was barely visible. Polly, a creature of formidable grey and red plumage, surveyed the company with a bright, intelligent eye.

“You’ll be hanged for a spy, you dog!” he announced, in a remarkably clear voice, as his cage was lifted from the carriage.

A profound silence fell. Mr. Croft, who had come to offer his services, merely bowed, a curious smile playing about his lips. Mrs. Prattle, far from being discomfited, beamed with pride. “He is so very observant, is he not? Captain Buntline taught him all the nautical phrases.”

The walk to the Point was conducted in a state of suppressed anxiety. Mrs. Prattle, leaning heavily on Mr. Croft’s arm, detailed the parrot’s diet and disposition at considerable length. “He will not touch a biscuit,” she declared, “but he is partial to a bit of plum cake, and will say ‘splice the mainbrace’ for a sip of wine. Is he not a wonder?” Mr. Croft assured her that he was, his politeness so perfect as to be either deeply sincere or exquisitely ironic; Eleanor could not determine which, and the uncertainty was a torment.

Upon reaching their destination, a fine prospect of sea and sky, the hamper was unpacked. Polly was placed upon a flat rock, where he held court. “Damn your eyes!” he cried, as a gust of wind ruffled his feathers. Harriet blushed to the roots of her hair, and Mrs. Vane fussed with the plates. Eleanor dared not look at Mr. Croft. She felt the whole enterprise to be a failure, a piece of folly for which she would be laughed at for a month.

It was Mr. Croft who, to her astonishment, took command of the situation. He did not attempt to ignore the bird, but rather engaged with it directly. “And what is the state of the tide, Polly?” he asked, with a gravity that belied the absurdity of the question.

“Land ho!” squawked the parrot, bobbing its head.

“Excellent,” said Mr. Croft. “Then we are safe from running aground.” He turned to Eleanor, his eyes alight with amusement. “Your aunt’s companion is a most excellent seaman. His reports are concise and his observations sharp.”

Eleanor felt a rush of gratitude so profound it was almost painful. In that moment, Mr. Croft’s character was revealed to her: not merely as a man of sense, but as one of true good humour and kindness, capable of transforming a humiliation into a shared joke. He was not the least bit discomposed; instead, he seemed to find a genuine delight in the ridiculousness of the situation.

He spent the remainder of the afternoon in conversation with the parrot, much to Mrs. Prattle’s delight. “He has a way with him,” she declared to her niece. “The Captain would have liked him immensely.”

“And what of a mutiny, Polly?” Mr. Croft inquired, offering the bird a crumb of cake.

“Hang the crew!” Polly replied promptly, accepting the morsel.

Mr. Croft laughed, a sound of such unaffected pleasure that Eleanor’s heart felt suddenly light. He caught her eye, and in his look she saw a shared understanding, an acknowledgment of the absurd, and a warmth that had nothing to do with the sun. The afternoon, which had promised so much anxiety, had instead provided an opportunity for a far more intimate acquaintance than any quiet walk could have afforded. She had seen him under pressure, and his conduct had been exemplary.
