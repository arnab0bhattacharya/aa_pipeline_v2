Mr. Alistair Finch, a man whose fortune was built on steel and whose reputation was built on charity, sat opposite me in my small Bloomsbury office. The room smelled of damp wool and old paper, a stark contrast to the cloying scent of lavender that clung to Finch’s tweed suit. He was a man made of soft, pale dough, his eyes wide and perpetually startled, as if the world were a constant surprise to him. He twisted a heavy gold signet ring on his finger.

“His name?” I asked, nibbing my pen. “Any detail at all?”

Finch shook his head, his jowls trembling. “None. It was… chaos. The carriage wheel, a shriek of metal, the horse rearing. I was thrown into the road. I saw the cobblestones rushing up to meet my face, and then… then he was there. A blur of dark cloth. He pulled me back as the axle snapped where my head had been. When I came to my senses, sitting on the kerb with a constable fanning my face, he was gone. Vanished into the crowd like smoke.”

My work usually involved unfaithful husbands or industrial espionage, cases with grubby, tangible truths. This felt different. It felt like chasing a ghost.

“I wish to reward him,” Finch insisted, his voice thin and reedy. “A man of such courage, of such selfless instinct… he must be found. I will not rest until I have shaken his hand and pressed a sum into it that will change his life. You will find him for me, Mr. Croft.”

The first week was a lesson in London’s fickle memory. The accident had occurred at the junction of Fleet Street and Ludgate Hill. I spoke to a flower girl with a basket of wilting violets, a newsboy shouting of naval skirmishes, and the constable who had tended to Finch. They all remembered the event—the splintered wood, the screaming horse, the pale, wealthy man in the road. But the rescuer? He was a phantom. The flower girl remembered a man in a “shabby brown coat,” the newsboy insisted he wore a “black bowler hat,” and the constable was sure he’d been a “navvy in a greasy waistcoat.” Three different men, all performing the same miraculous act. It was impossible.

My frustration grew. I began to believe Finch had imagined the entire hero, a convenient figment to soothe a bruised ego. Then, the second week, a strange coincidence occurred. A clerk I’d paid to search parish records for a man of the right description—average height, strong build—came to me with a peculiar story. He’d found no such man, but he’d heard a rumour from a friend in the Post Office. A letter carrier on his round in Whitechapel had seen a man matching my vague description pull a child from the path of a runaway brewer’s dray. The letter carrier, a man of precise habits, noted the time: three o’clock on Tuesday.

The problem was, at three o’clock on that same Tuesday, I was sitting in a pub in Southwark, interviewing a dockworker who swore he’d seen the same man—the very same man, he claimed, because of a distinctive tear in his left sleeve—dive into the murky waters of the Thames to rescue a bargee who had fallen overboard. The dockworker was certain of the time; the pub clock had just chimed the hour.

A cold knot formed in my stomach. I felt a prickle of unease that had nothing to do with the London fog seeping through my windowpanes. A man cannot be in Whitechapel and Southwark at the same moment. It was a trick of the light, a coincidence of stories. It had to be.

I reported my progress, or lack thereof, to Finch. I told him of the conflicting descriptions, the impossible sightings. I expected him to be disappointed. Instead, he grew agitated. The doughy pallor of his face was replaced by a sallow, clammy sheen. He was no longer twisting his ring but clawing at it.

“Where else?” he whispered, his eyes darting towards my office door as if expecting the man to walk through it. “Where else have you heard of him?”

His gratitude was curdling into something else. It was no longer the warm glow of thankfulness; it was the cold, sharp edge of fear. He was not looking for a saviour anymore. He was looking for a threat.

The final piece of the puzzle fell into place a few days later. A frantic message summoned me to Finch’s Mayfair residence. His butler, a stoic man named Davies, let me in. The house was oppressively silent. I found Finch in his library, a grand room of leather-bound books he’d likely never read. He was staring out the window at the manicured gardens below.

“It was him again,” Finch said, not turning around. His voice was a ghost of its former self.

“Where?”

“Here. Yesterday afternoon. I was walking by the Serpentine in Hyde Park. A child’s toy boat had drifted too far out. The boy was crying. And then he was there. Wading into the water without a second thought, his coat getting soaked, retrieving the little wooden boat and handing it back with a smile.” Finch finally turned to face me. His eyes were hollowed out, the skin beneath them bruised with sleeplessness. “I saw his face, Croft. For the first time, I saw it clearly. And I know him.”

He sank into a wingback chair, the air leaving his lungs in a ragged sigh. “His name is Thomas Blackwood. Or it was. He was an engineer in my employ at the Finch & Sons works in Sheffield. Two years ago, there was an accident. A boiler explosion. My fault. I’d ignored the inspector’s report about faulty rivets to save costs. Blackwood was badly burned. He lost an arm. He lost his livelihood. I paid him a pittance in compensation and had my solicitors ensure he could never sue. I made him disappear.”
