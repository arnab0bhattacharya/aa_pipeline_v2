Mr. Hubert Palsgrave, of Palsgrave & Wimble, Estate Agents and Surveyors, was a man who believed profoundly in the solidity of things. He believed in the freehold, in the quarterly rent, in the neat row of brick villas that marched with such commendable precision along the borders of Wandsworth Common, and, above all, he believed in the settled order of his own household in Ravenscourt Terrace. His wife, Edith, he regarded as the most satisfactory of his acquisitions—a young woman of twenty-four, with a clear complexion, a pleasant laugh, and a mind, he had always assumed, as neatly enclosed and unremarkable as the back garden.

It was therefore with a mild and pleasant sense of amusement that he first observed her new enthusiasm. She had joined, at the invitation of a Mrs. Callowhill, a person of vague means and advanced notions who lived in a larger house on the common, a society that called itself the Hampstead-and-Wandsworth Ethical and Economic Circle. The name alone had made him chuckle. “And what,” he had asked, spearing a piece of haddock at breakfast, “do you good people do at this Circle? Sit about and be ethical at one another?”

Edith had smiled, but a trifle thinly. “We discuss things, Hubert. The new thought. The relations of the sexes. The economic independence of women.”

“Ah,” said Mr. Palsgrave, with the comfortable air of a man who has heard it all before. “The economic independence of women. Very fine, very fine. I daresay Mrs. Callowhill’s cook is a great deal more independent than Mrs. Callowhill. There’s nothing like a good trade, my dear, for independence.” And he had returned to his paper, well pleased with his little sally.

But the amusement began to wear off. The Circle met on Thursday evenings, and Edith, who had formerly spent those evenings with a novel or a bit of needlework, now went out with a certain briskness, returning with a heightened colour and a curious glitter in her eye. She began to talk at dinner in a way that struck him as distinctly unwholesome. She spoke of “self-realization” and “the larger life.” She said that the home, as an institution, was undergoing a profound and necessary transformation. She quoted a man named Lankester, and another named Havelock Ellis, and a woman named Olive Schreiner, whose book, she said, had quite opened her eyes.

“Opened your eyes to what?” Mr. Palsgrave demanded, his fork suspended. “To the fact that you’ve a very good husband and a very comfortable home? I should have thought they were open enough for that.”

“It is not a question of comfort, Hubert,” she said, with a patience that irritated him more than heat would have done. “It is a question of purpose. A woman must have a purpose beyond mere cushions and crockery.”

The cushions and crockery, he reflected, had been good enough for her mother, and for his own mother before her. He began to feel a vague alarm. He noticed that she was reading the evening papers now, not for the fashion notes, but for the leading articles, and that she had taken to underlining passages in a copy of *The Economic Journal* with a pencil. The situation came to a head on a Tuesday evening in November.

“Hubert,” she said, as the maid cleared away the pudding, “I have been thinking very seriously about our position. I wish to earn my own money.”

Mr. Palsgrave laid down his napkin. “Earn your own money?” he repeated, as one who hears a foreign language for the first time. “What on earth for? Have you not everything you want? Do you lack for anything?”

“I lack for the experience of labour,” she said. “I lack for the dignity of a wage. I have been talking to Mrs. Callowhill, and she agrees with me that a woman of my abilities should not be content to be a mere pensioner upon her husband’s bounty.”

“Bounty!” cried Mr. Palsgrave, stung. “Bounty! It is not bounty, Edith; it is a natural and proper provision. I am your husband. It is my duty and my pleasure to provide for you. What would you do? Go out as a charwoman? Take in sewing?”

“I thought I might learn typewriting,” she said, calmly. “Mrs. Callowhill says there is a great future in it. Or perhaps I might take up the new art of display advertising. There is a firm in the City, she tells me, that employs lady copywriters. It is a question of the mind, Hubert, not of the hands.”

Mr. Palsgrave rose from the table in a state of considerable agitation. He walked to the window and stared out at the gas-lamp flickering on the wet pavement. The whole solid edifice of Ravenscourt Terrace seemed to tremble about him. A wife who wished to learn typewriting! It was not to be thought of. It was a reflection upon him, upon his business, upon the very foundations of society. It was, he felt, a thing that no man of standing could permit.

He was not a man of ready resource in the higher matters of the mind, but he was a practical man, and his practical mind went at once to the source of all authority. The next afternoon, leaving his office in the hands of a junior clerk, he took the omnibus to Clapham and presented himself at the door of his father-in-law, Mr. Joseph Gubbins, a retired corn-chandler of some substance and a great stickler for the proprieties.

Mr. Gubbins received him in a parlour that smelt of horsehair and old tobacco, and listened to his recital with a face of growing gravity. He was a large, slow man, with a habit of pursing his lips and nodding his head slowly, as though he were mentally weighing out a quarter of oats.

“Typewriting,” he said at last, when Hubert had finished. “Typewriting. And this Mrs. Callowhill. A widow, you say?”

“A widow, and, I fear, a very designing one,” said Hubert.
