The town of Grimworth, which had been in the habit of making iron and cotton and money for the last hundred years, had recently conceived the idea of making men—not men of flesh and blood, for there were plenty of those to be had at a penny an hour, and they multiplied like rabbits in the damp back-alleys—but men of calculation and obedience. It was for this purpose that the Grimworth Mechanics’ Institute and Night-School had been erected in the shadow of the great ironworks, a building of soot-stained brick that looked as if it had been hatched from the egg of a particularly gloomy crow.

It was here, in a room that smelt perpetually of wet wool, chalk dust, and the latent steam of a thousand exhausted bodies, that I, Arthur Penhaligon, held my nightly court. My kingdom was a blackboard, my sceptre a piece of chalk, and my subjects were the tired, grimy, and magnificent men of the foundries and the mills. They came to me when the sun had long since given up the ghost behind the smoke-stacks, shedding their aprons like heavy skins at the door and slumping onto the hard wooden benches with the sigh of oxen returning to the plough.

Among these worthy creatures, one stood out like a diamond in a coal-scuttle. His name was Silas Thrum, and his vocation was the tending of the boilers in the great factory of Messrs. Hardcastle & Flint. He was a man of forty years, though his face looked like a map of a country that had endured a long and difficult war; he was built like a small warehouse, with hands that could have strangled a bull, yet he moved among the delicate apparatus of the schoolroom with the tentative gentleness of a giant handling a butterfly.

Silas was my star, my prodigy, my triumph. While the others fumbled with the figures as if they were hot coals, Silas seized upon arithmetic with a fierce, devouring joy. He had a thirst for numbers that was quite alarming; he drank in fractions as if they were ale, and devoured decimals with the appetite of a man who had been starved of logic all his life. I had taught him to calculate the expansion of steam, the pressure of gases, and the interest on a shilling, until he could have balanced the books of the Bank of England with one hand tied behind his back. He was, in short, a mathematical engine of the first order.

But it was on a Tuesday evening, when the rain was drumming against the window-panes like a thousand impatient fingers, that the trouble began. The Committee had arrived.

The Committee consisted of Mr. Hardcastle, a man whose face was so red and shiny he looked like a prize apple that had been varnished; Mr. Flint, a gentleman of such angular construction that he seemed to be made entirely of elbows and opinions; and a third gentleman, a lawyer, who was there to see that nobody smiled without permission. They sat at the front of the room, surveying my pupils with the benevolent interest of spiders inspecting a fresh web.

‘Mr. Penhaligon,’ began Mr. Hardcastle, his voice sounding like gravel being poured into a tin bucket. ‘We have looked over your reports. The progress in calculation is satisfactory. Satisfactory. But we perceive a deficiency. A grave deficiency.’

‘Indeed, sir?’ I inquired, with a bow that I hoped was deferential yet dignified. ‘I had thought the boys were doing rather well with the compound interest.’

‘It is not the interest,’ snapped Mr. Flint, leaning forward so that his elbows threatened to puncture the atmosphere. ‘It is the catechism. The spiritual condition of the working man is the foundation of the industrial edifice. We want less poetry of the imagination, and more poetry of the pocket-book. We want the Thirty-Nine Articles, and the Multiplication Table. Nothing else. Do you understand me, sir? Nothing else.’

‘But, gentlemen,’ I ventured, my heart sinking into my boots, ‘the human mind is a garden, and if you plant only turnips—’

‘Turnips are food!’ roared Mr. Hardcastle. ‘Poetry is air! We cannot eat air! We want men who can calculate the coal consumption of a locomotive and say their prayers without mumbling. That is the curriculum. Arithmetic and the Catechism. You will confine yourself to these. If I hear of any more of this… this *fancy* work, we shall be forced to reconsider your salary.’

They swept out of the room in a cloud of self-importance, leaving behind them a silence that was heavier than the smoke outside. I turned to my class. They looked at me with the dull resignation of men who are used to having their spirits regulated by the factory whistle.

But Silas Thrum did not look resigned. He looked troubled. He sat turning his great, coal-blackened hands over and over in his lap, as if he were trying to solve a puzzle that had no numbers.

When the bell rang for the end of the lesson, the others scrambled away into the wet night, glad to be free of the chalk and the Committee. But Silas lingered. He approached my desk with the cautious tread of a man walking on thin ice, his cap clutched in his hands.

‘Mr. Penhaligon,’ he said, his voice a low rumble that seemed to originate from the boilers he tended. ‘Beggin’ your pardon, sir.’

‘What is it, Silas?’ I asked, gathering my books. ‘Do not worry about the Committee. We shall manage with the arithmetic.’

‘It ain’t the ‘rithmetic, sir,’ said Silas, looking at the floor. ‘I loves the ‘rithmetic. It’s honest. Two and two is four, and no steam-gauge can alter it. But… well, sir, I was a-wondering.’

He paused, and a deep, brick-red blush spread over his grimy cheeks, clashing horribly with the soot. ‘I was a-wondering… could you teach me to read the poetry?’

I stared at him. ‘Poetry, Silas? Mr. Hardcastle expressly forbade it.’
