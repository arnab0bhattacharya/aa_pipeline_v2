The rain lashed against the windows of the Albion Hotel, a ceaseless, rhythmic drumming that seemed to isolate the lobby from the rest of the world. I stood by the fire, a cup of cold tea in my hand, watching the porter, a broad-shouldered man named Griggs, place a leather valise upon the mahogany table with a thud that cut through the ambient murmur of the room.

"Mr. Blackwood," Griggs said, wiping his brow with a stained handkerchief. "There is something here that I cannot leave in the office. It does not seem right to lock it away with the general baggage."

I looked up. Detective Blackwood was not a man of many words, but he was a man of keen observation. His eyes, narrow and pale, fixed upon the valise. "A guest’s property?"

"Mr. Vance," Griggs replied. "Checked out at half-past ten this morning. Left in a great hurry. Said he had caught the 11:15 to Brighton. But he forgot this."

"And you bring it to me?"

"Is it not my duty, sir? I am only a porter."

Blackwood stood, his movements economical and precise. He approached the table and lifted the valise. It was a standard piece of luggage, hard-shell, lined with velvet. He ran his fingers over the stitching, noting a slight fraying at the corner, before snapping the brass clasps open. The scent of stale tobacco and expensive cologne wafted out, mingling with the damp chill of the rainy London afternoon.

Inside, the contents were sparse. A change of clothes, folded neatly. A book of poetry, its spine cracked. And, resting atop a pile of shirts, a pair of kid gloves, white and immaculate. Beside them lay a small, heavy object wrapped in a handkerchief.

Blackwood did not touch the gloves. He picked up the wrapped object with a pair of tweezers he kept in his waistcoat pocket, a habit that often drew raised eyebrows from my fellow officers. He unwrapped the cloth to reveal a revolver, a Webley Mk IV, with a barrel that still held the faint residue of recent discharge. He tilted the weapon, inspecting the cylinder. "One chamber empty," he murmured. "The others full. No spent shells in the case."

"And the gloves?" I asked.

"White kid," Blackwood said, turning his attention to the fabric. "Lined with silk. The left glove has a tear near the little finger. A small thing, but noticeable. The right is perfect. Why would a man carry a woman’s gloves, and why leave them behind?"

"Perhaps he had a lady friend," I suggested, though the notion felt thin in the face of the revolver.

Blackwood shook his head slowly. "A man in a hurry does not forget his lover’s gloves. He forgets his wallet, his hat, perhaps his dignity. But he does not forget the token of a recent, intimate encounter, unless that encounter was a crime, or a threat."

He reached into the pocket of the shirts. There, folded in thirds, was a railway timetable. It was a standard London and Brighton guide, but one page was marked. A single train, the 11:15 to Brighton, was underlined three times in black ink. The line was jagged, urgent, drawn by a hand that was trembling or moving with excessive speed.

"The train has gone," I said. "It would be at Brighton by now."

"Or at Lewes," Blackwood corrected. "Let us consult the register."

He walked to the desk, where the clerk, a young man with ink-stained fingers, was balancing the day’s accounts. Blackwood spoke quietly, and the clerk produced the ledger. We scanned the pages until we found the entry for Mr. Vance. The name was written in a firm, educated hand. But the address given was a street in Whitechapel, a district known for its dense population and its less savory reputation.

"Did he provide identification?" Blackwood asked.

"No, sir. Just cash. No questions asked."

"Whitechapel," Blackwood said, his voice dropping to a low register. "And a false name, or at least a false address. Why would a man checking out of a respectable London hotel use a Whitechapel address? To misdirect any inquiries? To appear as a man of the working class when he was clearly dressed in the fashion of the upper echelons?"

I looked back at the table. The revolver. The gloves. The timetable. "You believe the gloves belong to the victim?"

"I believe the gloves belong to the person he was with," Blackwood said. "Or the person he was trying to avoid. The tear in the left glove suggests a struggle, or a hurried exit. He grabbed the gloves, perhaps to wipe his hands, or perhaps to hide the evidence of a contact. But he left them. Why?"

"Because he was in a state of shock," I offered. "Or panic."

"Or because he was not going to Brighton," Blackwood said. He looked at the underlined train again. "The underlining is not for emphasis. It is for memory. He needed to remember that train. But if he was going to Brighton, why would he leave the revolver? A man who has committed a crime does not leave his weapon behind in a hotel lobby, no matter how hurried his exit. It is too conspicuous. Too risky."

"Unless he did not intend to use it," I said.

"Or unless he used it, and intended to dispose of it, but never got the chance." Blackwood picked up the gloves again. He held them up to the light. "Look at the tear. It is not a rip from a struggle. It is a cut. A sharp, clean cut, as if from a knife or a broken glass. And look here." He pointed to the inside of the cuff. "There is a faint stain. Not blood. Ink."

"Indelible ink," I noted.

"Blue-black. The same ink used on the timetable." Blackwood’s eyes narrowed. "He was writing. He was writing something on the glove, or he was holding the glove while writing. Or perhaps he was wiping the ink from his fingers after writing."
