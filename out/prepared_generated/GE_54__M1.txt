There are few sojourns more likely to correct the hasty judgments of youth than a fever, which, by stripping away the furniture of daily occupation, leaves the mind in a bare chamber where its own resources are laid open to inspection. It was in such a chamber—both the fever’s and the spare room at Netherfold Farm—that the Reverend Arthur Penrose first learned the true nature of the moorland parish of Stoneycroft, a place he had been sent to with a head full of curative schemes and a heart, he thought, full of Christian charity, but with a soul as yet unacquainted with the slow, patient chemistry of true sympathy. He had come from Cambridge with the unexamined confidence of a man who has read much but suffered little, and he had viewed the scattered, silent folk of the high country as a kind of human problem for which he possessed the elegant solution. The fever, arriving with a damp east wind in the late autumn, did not consult his plans. It laid him low in the midst of his labours, and delivered him, shivering and helpless, into the care of the Rawlins family, who rented the farm from a distant squire and knew the moor’s moods better than they knew the catechism.

The Rawlins household was a study in the material realities that Mr. Penrose’s sermons had hitherto treated as mere impediments to spiritual grace. There was old Mr. Rawlins, a man whose face was a map of the seasons he had weathered, and whose conversation was limited to the prospects of the turnips and the price of wool; Mrs. Rawlins, a bustling woman whose piety was expressed in the relentless application of soap and steam to every surface, including, it seemed to Mr. Penrose, his own person; and their daughter, Esther. It was to Esther that the primary duty of nursing fell, and it was Esther who became the silent witness to the curate’s slow unmaking and tentative reassembly. She was a young woman of nineteen, whose world had been bounded by the stone walls of the farmyard and the grey expanse of the moor. Her education was practical and complete in its own sphere: she knew how to coax a fire from damp peat, how to read the sky for rain, and how to soothe a fretful lamb. Her own countenance, which Mr. Penrose had first noted as merely plain and unlettered, now, in the dim light of the sick-room, seemed to hold a kind of quiet, monumental patience. Her hands, though roughened by work, moved with a deftness that was its own form of grace as she administered his draughts and smoothed his pillows.

The fever broke, but left him weak as a child, and it was in this state of enforced idleness that the true education began. Having no other occupation, he asked for his books. Esther, who had never held a volume heavier than the family Bible, brought them to him with a reverence that was not for the learning within, but for the man who required it. One day, seeing her gaze rest upon a slim volume of Mr. Cowper’s poems with a look of intense but uncomprehending longing, he felt a sudden, unbidden impulse. “You may borrow it, Esther,” he said, his voice still thin. “The words are for all who care to read them.” She took the book as if it were a fragile relic, and for the first time in their acquaintance, a flicker of something other than dutiful care animated her features.

It was a small act, this lending of a book, yet it set in motion a slow and profound alteration. Esther began to read, at first with great labour, stumbling over words like ‘melancholy’ and ‘contemplation’ as if they were stones in her path. But she persisted, her lips moving silently in the firelight after the day’s work was done. The poetry spoke to her of the very landscape she had always known, but endowed it with a significance she had never imagined. The ‘solemn woods’ were no longer merely timber for fencing, but places of divine communion; the ‘melancholy main’ of the sky above the moor was a mirror for the soul’s own depths. She began to see her world through a double lens, the one of long habit, and this new, startling one of poetic feeling. And with this new sight, the man who had given her the lens was transformed. He was no longer merely the ailing curate, a gentle-voiced stranger from another world; he became the source of this new light, the key to a country of the mind she had not known existed.

Mr. Penrose, in his turn, was a willing student of his own convalescence. He observed Esther’s slow blossoming with a scholar’s fascination and a man’s dawning affection. He saw in her not the simple village girl, but a mind of unguessed capacity, a soul like a dormant seed waiting for the intellectual rain. His pity, which had at first been the dominant note of his feelings, was gradually superseded by a profound respect, and then by a tenderness that alarmed him with its quiet, persistent growth. He lent her more books—histories, a volume of Mr. Locke’s philosophy—and they would talk, he from his bed or his chair by the window, and she as she went about her tasks. Their conversations were not of grand passion, but of the texture of a line of verse, the logic of a historical event, the nature of the human understanding. In these exchanges, the vast social gulf between them seemed to dissolve, replaced by the simple, powerful currency of shared thought. He was falling in love not with a face or a form, but with the very process of her mind awakening to his.
