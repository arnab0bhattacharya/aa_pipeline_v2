The rain descended upon London with a sullen, unintermitting fury, as if the heavens themselves wept over the destiny of a renovated nation. I stood alone in my apartment, gazing through the streaming casement upon the broad Thames, whose dark waters, swollen by the tempest, swept onward with a majestic and sorrowful speed, bearing upon their bosom the reflected lights of a thousand vessels—the humble fisherman's skiff, the merchant's heavily-laden bark, and the swift gliding barge, all mingling in one scene of busy life, while I, removed from their toils, felt only the overwhelming solitude of my own thoughts. The wind howled through the high towers of the Assembly House, which loomed colossal and black against the stormy sky, a monument of that freedom which we had so dearly purchased, and which now, perhaps, was about to be torn from us by the very hand that had most contributed to establish it.

Valerius was returned. The conqueror of the Eastern provinces, the idol of the soldiery, the demigod of the populace, had landed on our shores, and was even now advancing towards the capital, amidst the deafening acclamations of a misguided multitude. For three years I had parted from him. I had known him in our youth, when, both poor and obscure, we had striven together in the cause of liberty; I had shared his frugal meal, and cheered him in the hour of despondency; and now, when his name was sounded from every tongue, and his path strewed with the laurels of victory, I alone remembered the Valerius of former days, the ardent, the disinterested, the noble-minded friend. But power is a foul necromancer; it transforms the fairest soul into a fiend; and I feared, alas! too justly, that the Valerius I had loved was no more.

A knock at my door announced a visitor. It was Decius, one of the tribunes of the people, a man of fierce passions and unbounded ambition, who had long been the secret instigator of the designs which now threatened our country. He entered with a countenance flushed with wine and exultation, his eyes flashing with a wild and fearful fire.

"Adrian," he cried, seizing my hand with a convulsive grasp, "the hour is arrived! Valerius is at hand, and his soldiers are impatient for the decree. Tomorrow, in the Assembly, he will be proclaimed Dictator. The Republic is at an end, and an empire begins!"

I shrunk from his touch as from the contact of a viper. "And is it for this," I exclaimed, "that we have shed our blood, and buried our dearest friends? Is it for this that we have toiled and suffered, that we may exchange one master for another? Oh, Decius, you know not what you do! The power you would confer is a curse, not a blessing; it will blast the giver and the receiver!"

Decius laughed aloud—a harsh, grating, and unnatural laugh. "You dream, Adrian," he said; "you are an enthusiast, a visionary. The people are weary of their own rule; they long for a chief, a hero, a god! Valerius is worthy of the throne; his sword has carved out our empire, and his wisdom should govern it. Join us, and you shall share his glory; oppose us, and you will be crushed beneath his chariot wheels."

"Crush me, then!" I replied, with a voice raised to the pitch of desperation. "I will not join in this foul conspiracy against the liberties of my country. I will oppose Valerius himself, if he lifts his hand against the Republic. I will speak in the Assembly tomorrow; I will denounce this treachery; I will tear the mask from his face, and show the world the tyrant that lurks beneath!"

Decius fixed his eyes upon me with a look of mingled scorn and pity. "Beware, Adrian," he said, in a low and solemn tone; "Valerius loves you, it is true; but he loves power more. If you oppose him, he will cast you off; you will lose his friendship, and perhaps your life. Consider well before you take a step from which there is no retreat."

He withdrew, and I was left alone with my thoughts. The storm raged without, and the rain beat against my window with a melancholy sound; but the tempest within my breast was far more terrible. I threw myself into a chair, and gave way to a burst of grief that was almost madness. Valerius, my friend, my brother, my more than brother! Must I then oppose thee? Must I lift my voice against thee in the hall of the Assembly, and call thee tyrant? Oh, agony of agonies! To love a being with the whole fervor of the soul, and yet to be constrained to combat his dearest wishes! I thought of our former days, of our wanderings by the sea-shore, of our conversations by the winter fire, of our vows of eternal friendship, and my heart was rent with anguish. I felt as if a sword were plunged into my bosom, and turned round and round in the wound.

But reflection came, though slowly, and with it a sense of duty. I thought of my country, of the blood that had been shed, of the hopes that had been excited, of the freedom that had been achieved. Was all this to be sacrificed to the ambition of one man? No; I must do my duty, come what might. I must oppose Valerius, even if he hated me for it; I must save my country, even if I perished in the attempt.

The morning dawned, gloomy and tempestuous. The clouds hung heavy and dark over the city, and the rain still fell, as if mourning over the approaching ruin of our liberties. I dressed myself with a slow and reluctant hand, and proceeded towards the Assembly House. The streets were thronged with people, whose faces were flushed with excitement, and whose voices were raised in shouts of "Long live Valerius! Long live the Dictator!" I passed through them in silence, my heart swelling with grief and indignation.
