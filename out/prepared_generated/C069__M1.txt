The letter, thin and brittle as a dead leaf, lay on the polished surface of her dressing table. Eleanor Vance had read it three times, the same phrases striking her anew with the dull, repetitive force of a hammer on a coffin nail. *Unforeseen and catastrophic collapse. The House of Sterling & Finch. Regret to inform you. All assets rendered valueless.* The words were dressed in the finery of polite commerce, but their meaning was stark and brutal: her one hundred and twelve pounds, eight shillings, and fourpence—every spare penny saved from four years of pinching and skimping in this foreign city—was gone.

She did not weep. The feeling was not of sorrow, but of a sudden, violent vertigo, as if the solid floor of the pension had been ripped away, leaving her suspended over a black and bottomless chasm. Her hand, resting on the letter, was perfectly still. Outside the tall window, the city of Valoria hummed with its afternoon life: the rumble of a diligence over the cobblestones, the distant cry of a flower-seller, the merry chime of the bells from the Kirche Sankt-Agnes. The sounds were of a world that continued to turn, oblivious to the silent implosion of her own.

The following morning, she rose at the usual hour. She dressed in her second-best grey merino, the one with the neat, white collar she had starched herself. She pinned her dark hair back in a severe but elegant knot, her movements precise and economical, betraying nothing. In the mirror, the face that looked back was pale, the lips pressed into a thin line, but the eyes were calm. She would not be a woman undone by a piece of paper. She was Miss Vance, senior English mistress at the Schule für Töchter des Bürgertums, and she had duties to perform.

The school was a grand, imposing building of pale stone, its windows looking out onto a manicured square. Inside, the air smelled of beeswax, old books, and the faint, chalky scent of learning. The girls, daughters of Valoria’s merchants and minor officials, rose in a rustle of starched pinafores as she entered the classroom. "Guten Morgen, Miss Vance," they chorused, their German accent lending a charming softness to the English greeting.

"Good morning, young ladies," she replied, her voice clear and steady, a well-worn instrument. "Please, open your copies of *Pride and Prejudice*. We were at the Netherfield Ball."

As she read aloud, her voice tracing the elegant, ironic sentences of Jane Austen, her mind was elsewhere. It was not in the fictional ballroom, but in the grim, grey ledger of her own life. She saw the numbers laid out not on a page, but in stark, cold rows against the darkness of her thoughts. Rent to Frau Schmidt: twelve thalers a month. The midday meal at the school: a deduction from her salary. Her own modest suppers: bread, a little cheese, a jug of milk. Her salary was forty thalers, paid on the first of the month. The letter had arrived on the twelfth.

She had, in her small tin box, enough coin for perhaps five weeks. After that, there would be nothing. No buffer, no safety net. The future, once a landscape of modest but manageable hills, was now a sheer cliff face. And at the bottom of that cliff lay one terrible, unthinkable action: she would have to go to Herr Direktor Albrect, knock on the heavy oak door of his office, and ask for an advance on her salary. The thought was a physical revulsion, a hot flush of shame that crept up her neck. To confess her poverty, her foolishness in entrusting her savings to a distant bank, to become an object of pity or, worse, of censure—it was a degradation she could not bear. Her pride, the one inheritance from her proud, impractical father, was the last fortress she had, and she would defend it to the last.

Days turned into a week, the week into a fortnight. Her life became a meticulous performance. In the classroom, she was impeccable. She corrected essays with sharp, insightful notes, led discussions on the Romantic poets with a passion that was real, and maintained a gentle but firm authority that the girls respected. She laughed at their small jokes, praised their progress, and seemed to them the very model of English composure and grace.

But alone in her small room, the performance ended. The silence was her confidante. Here, she would sit at her table, a single candle casting long shadows, and she would calculate. She weighed the thalers and groschen in her palm, the cool metal a poor substitute for the warmth of security. She mapped out the weeks, each one a step closer to the precipice. Five weeks of food. Four weeks of rent. Then, the last week. A week of air and water, perhaps, before the end.

She began to walk more, to fill the hours when she would have once read a novel or written a letter home. She walked through the city’s grand squares and along the river, observing the life she was now so perilously connected to. She saw a young seamstress, her eyes tired, mending a fine lace gown in the window of a shop. She saw an old bookseller, his back bent, sorting through dusty volumes for a pittance. They were all, she realized, balanced on their own narrow ledges. Her catastrophe was not unique; it was merely invisible.

One afternoon, while the girls were practising their calligraphy, the youngest, little Greta Schmidt, looked up at her with wide, earnest eyes. "Miss Vance," she asked, "are you tired? You look… quiet today."

Eleanor felt a jolt, a sudden fear of discovery. She forced a smile, reaching out to gently straighten the girl’s collar. "I am perfectly well, my dear. Just concentrating on your beautiful penmanship. Now, mind the slant of your letters."
