The telegram had arrived on Tuesday, a flimsy yellow paper that seemed to carry the weight of the world in its curt, typed announcement: FIRST IN DIVISION. For three days, Beatrice Holloway had walked through the familiar, cluttered rooms of her home in a state of exaltation. The heavy tomes of Virgil and Homer, which she had balanced on her knees in the draughty upstairs room, were no longer merely schoolbooks; they were the instruments of her future. They were the keys to a laboratory, a library, a lecture hall at Somerville or Newnham—places where the world was discussed in terms of logic and evidence rather than the price of butter and the quality of the Sunday joint.

But on Thursday evening, the exaltation evaporated like a morning mist under the grey Croydon sun.

It was in the parlour, amidst the antimacassars and the heavy, lingering scent of boiled cabbage and lavender polish, that her mother, Mrs. Holloway, had delivered the verdict. She did not look up from her mending; the needle flashed with a mechanical regularity that suggested the conversation was merely another domestic chore to be ticked off.

"University is out of the question, Beatrice," she said, her voice flat and practical. "It is not a matter of wanting or not wanting. It is arithmetic. Mr. Pritchard at the haberdashery has an opening for a cashier. You start Monday."

"But the exhibition," Beatrice had stammered, the word sounding thin and pathetic in the stuffy air. "The examiners said I might secure a scholarship."

"Scholarships do not pay for board and lodging in Oxford, nor do they mend the roof," her mother replied, biting off a thread with a sharp snap. "And there is your brother. Cecil’s fees at the Technical Institute are due next month. A man must have a trade. A girl..." She paused, finally looking up, her eyes tired and pragmatic. "A girl must have a position."

Now, on Friday night, the silence at the dinner table was a physical presence, as tangible as the meat pie sitting in its dish. The gaslight hissed in the mantle, casting long, dancing shadows that made the cramped dining room feel like a cage. Beatrice sat opposite her mother, her plate untouched. Across from her, her brother Cecil was eating with the vigorous, unthinking appetite of a young man who believes the world owes him a living. He was twenty-one, thick-necked and heavy-jawed, and he was entirely absorbed in the mechanics of his meal.

Mrs. Holloway served the potatoes. "Eat your dinner, Beatrice. It will get cold."

"I am not hungry," Beatrice said. Her voice was trembling, but she forced it to be steady. This was a scientific matter, she told herself. It was a problem of resource allocation, and she intended to solve it. "Mother, I wish to discuss the arrangement again."

Cecil looked up, a piece of beef halfway to his mouth. "Oh, lord. Not this again."

"Be quiet, Cecil," Mrs. Holloway said, though without real conviction. "There is nothing to discuss, Beatrice. The decision is made."

"But it is illogical," Beatrice pressed, leaning forward. "You say we have no money. Yet we are paying for Cecil to study engineering. I have passed the Matriculation with distinction—the first in the history of the school. The potential return on an investment in my education is demonstrably higher than—"

"Return on investment?" Cecil snorted, dropping his fork. "Listen to her. She thinks she’s a banker."

"I think I am a student who has earned the right to continue!" Beatrice cried, her composure cracking. "Why must my future be sacrificed for his? I have worked harder. I have achieved more. Is it simply because he is male?"

The question hung in the air, dangerous and bright. Mrs. Holloway set down the water jug with a heavy thud.

"Do not speak of your brother in that tone," she said, her voice hardening. "Cecil will be the man of this house when I am gone. He will need a trade to support a family. You... you will have a husband to support you. Or you will have a respectable position behind a counter. It is the natural order."

"The natural order!" Beatrice laughed, a sharp, bitter sound. "Is it nature that dictates I should count ribbons while he learns thermodynamics? Or is it just habit? It is the same old prejudice that says a woman’s brain is a decorative ornament, fit for embroidery and nothing else."

"Don't get hysterical," Cecil said, looking bored. "It’s unbecoming. Besides, who’s going to pay for it? You? You haven't got a penny."

"I would work! I would live on bread and water!"

"And ruin your health," Mrs. Holloway said. "And bring shame on us. A girl living alone in a college town... people would talk. It is not respectable."

"Respectability!" Beatrice stood up, the chair scraping harshly against the linoleum. "You would sacrifice my mind, my whole life, for the sake of what the neighbours might say? And for what? So Cecil can spend his allowance on beer and billiards?"

"I do not!" Cecil shouted, reddening.

"Enough!" Mrs. Holloway struck the table with the palm of her hand. The plates jumped. The sudden violence silenced them all. "I have said the last word on this, Beatrice. You are a child of nineteen. You do not understand the world. You do not understand the cost of things. I have scrimped and saved and worried until my hair has turned grey to keep a roof over your heads. I cannot pay for dreams. I can only pay for bread. You will go to Mr. Pritchard’s on Monday. That is the end of it."

Beatrice looked at her mother, really looked at her, and saw not a tyrant, but a tired woman trapped in the same gears of convention that were now grinding Beatrice down. But the pity did not soften her resolve; it hardened it. She saw the future clearly then—not a vision of a laboratory, but a vision of herself in ten years, standing behind that counter, grey and weary, serving the wives of men like Cecil.
