The clock on the mantelpiece did not tick; it seemed to hold its breath, a heavy, brass-lunged creature waiting for the inevitable strike that would shatter the evening’s delicate tension. Elsie sat at the table, her fingers tracing the grain of the mahogany, feeling the wood’s cold indifference beneath her touch. Outside, the Kensington rain lashed the windowpanes, blurring the streetlamps into smears of amber and grey, but inside, the air was still, thick with the scent of beeswax and the impending service of roast pheasant. It was a perfect room. It was a room that had been polished until it could reflect the soul, and tonight, it was reflecting only her own, fractured and strange.

They had been engaged for a year. A year is a long time in the life of a feeling; it is long enough for the sharp, bright edge of excitement to wear down into a smooth, dull stone. Gerald had been kind. Kindness, Elsie thought, watching him reach for the salt cellar with his careful, deliberate hand, was a sort of quiet violence. It smothered the air. It left no room for the wild, uncharted territories of the mind. He had never once asked what she saw in the clouds, or why the sound of the church bells made her weep. He had only asked if she was hungry, if she was warm, if she had written to her mother. And she had said yes, yes, yes, until the words had lost their meaning, becoming merely sounds, like the rustling of the napkins.

Now, the silence between them was no longer comfortable. It was a living thing, coiling in the center of the table. Her father, Mr. Avenell, was speaking about the price of coal, his voice low and rumbling, a sound like stones turning in a riverbed. Her mother, Lady Avenell, listened with the polite, vacant attention of a woman who has long since stopped hearing the words and is only listening to the tone. The aunts, Mrs. Pym and Mrs. Halloway, sat on either side of Lady Avenell, their bodies rigid, their eyes fixed on some invisible point on the ceiling. They were waiting. They were always waiting, for the next move in the game of propriety, for the next drop of tea, for the next sigh.

Gerald sat opposite her. He looked so solid, so unyielding, like a piece of furniture that had been bought long ago and never moved. He was smiling, a small, tight smile that did not reach his eyes. He was thinking, Elsie knew, of the dinner menu, of the seating plan, of the fact that he had saved for this occasion. He did not know. How could he know? The secret sat in her stomach, a cold stone, heavy and immovable. She had broken the engagement only three hours ago, in the privacy of her bedroom, with a letter that had seemed, when she had written it, to be made of glass. Now, it felt like lead.

The door opened, and the maid entered, carrying the pheasant. The smell of it, rich and gamey, filled the room, overpowering the beeswax. It was a smell that evoked feasts, holidays, endings. Elsie looked at the bird, with its head tucked under its wing, its eyes closed. It looked peaceful. It looked dead. She wondered if Gerald could feel the weight of the moment, or if he was still somewhere in the safe, warm world of the past, where everything was simple and known.

“Elsie,” her mother said, her voice soft, a thread of silk in the heavy air. “You look pale. Are you unwell?”

“No, Mother,” Elsie said. Her voice sounded strange to her, thin and distant. “I am quite well.”

She picked up her fork. The silver was cold against her fingers. She looked at Gerald, who was now carving the pheasant, his knife moving with precise, practiced strokes. He was so careful. He was always so careful. He never spilled his wine, never wrinkled his tie, never said a word that might cause offense. He was perfect. And that was the problem. Perfection was a cage. It was a beautiful, gilded cage, but it was a cage nonetheless. She wanted to run. She wanted to scream. She wanted to throw the plate at his head. Instead, she cut a piece of meat and put it in her mouth. It tasted of ash.

The conversation continued, but it had become a performance. Mr. Avenell talked about the war, about the future, about the importance of duty. The aunts nodded, agreeing, disagreeing, offering small, safe opinions. It was a dance, a complex, intricate dance that they had all learned long ago. They moved in and out of the steps, never missing a beat, never losing their balance. And Elsie was in the center of it, turning, turning, until she felt dizzy.

Gerald looked up from his plate. “Elsie,” he said. “Is something wrong?”

His voice was gentle. It was the voice he had used when he had given her the ring, when he had held her hand on the pier, when he had whispered his promises into her ear. It was a voice that carried no weight, no shadow. It was a voice that assumed the world was simple, that assumed that love was a thing that could be measured and contained.

Elsie looked at him. She wanted to tell him. She wanted to say, *I am not what you think I am. I am not the woman who sits quietly and accepts your kindness. I am the woman who looks at the rain and sees the whole world dissolving. I am the woman who is afraid of silence, and you have given me nothing but silence.*

But she did not say it. She smiled. It was a small, sad smile, but it was enough. It was a smile that said, *Everything is fine. Everything is in its place. We are here. We are together. We are safe.*
