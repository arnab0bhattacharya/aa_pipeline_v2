The rain had been falling for three days, and the river was beginning to swell. Lena stood at the edge of the camp, watching the brown water churn past the roots of the old willow, and she felt the weight of every pair of eyes behind her. They had voted at dawn, a show of hands in the dripping shelter of the grain store, and the count had been almost unanimous. Hollis had nominated her, his voice rasping with the cough that never quite left him, and the others had simply raised their hands as though it were the most natural thing in the world. She had not asked for it. She had asked for nothing except to be left to her work in the infirmary, to the careful business of boiling bandages and setting bones.

Now she was their leader, and the valley was drowning.

They had come to the Severn valley in the second winter after the sickness, when the cities were still burning and the roads were choked with the dead and the desperate. The valley had seemed a haven then, sheltered by the long green ridge to the east and the wooded hills to the west, with a river that ran clean and a cluster of farm buildings that still had roofs. They had planted potatoes and kept goats and buried their dead in a row behind the orchard. For four years they had held on, and Lena had begun to believe that the world might be reduced to this small, manageable circle of fields and faces.

But the river had risen twice that spring, and the potatoes were rotting in the ground. The goats had begun to sicken, and the children were thin in a way that made her chest ache. And beyond the ridge, the reports had grown worse. The bands of raiders were moving north, driven by hunger, and the last messenger to reach them—a boy of fourteen with blood on his boots—had spoken of fires in the towns to the south and a great tide of people with nowhere left to go.

Lena turned from the river and walked back through the mud to the grain store, where the others were waiting. They sat on overturned buckets and sacks of last year's barley, their faces drawn and anxious. Hollis was there, wrapped in a blanket despite the mild air, and beside him was Mara, who had been a teacher before and now kept the children occupied with songs and sums. There was Tomas, the quiet man who had once been a carpenter and now repaired everything from ploughs to boots, and the two sisters, Elin and Cerys, who had come up from the coast and never spoke of what they had seen there.

"We need to decide," Lena said, and her voice sounded strange to her, too loud in the low-ceilinged room. "The river will flood again before the week is out. The potatoes are gone. We can stay and hope the water recedes, or we can go north."

"North to what?" Tomas asked. He was not hostile, only tired. "The coast is a rumour. A story the boy brought with him."

"The boy brought more than a story," Elin said quietly. "He brought word of a settlement at the mouth of the river. Boats. Fishing. People who have learned to live with the water instead of fighting it."

"And the road north is long," Hollis said, and coughed. "We have old people. Children. We have no carts that will manage the mud."

"We have the tractor," Lena said. "And the trailer. We can load what we need and walk beside it."

"Walk into the unknown," Hollis said. "The valley has kept us alive."

"The valley is drowning," Lena said, and she heard the sharpness in her own voice and did not soften it. "The valley has kept us alive for four years, and now it is killing us. The goats are sick. The ground is sour. If we stay, we will be trapped here when the water rises, and the raiders will find us with nowhere to run."

There was a long silence. The rain drummed on the roof, and somewhere outside a child laughed, a thin, bright sound that seemed to belong to a different world.

"I never wanted this," Lena said, more quietly. "You chose me. I am trying to choose well. But I cannot make the river stop rising, and I cannot make the potatoes grow in water. We have to go north."

Mara looked at her for a long moment, and then she nodded. "The children can walk," she said. "I will keep them moving."

Tomas sighed and rubbed his face with his hands. "I can fix the trailer," he said. "It will take a day. Maybe two."

"And the old ones?" Hollis asked. "What of us?"

"You will ride in the trailer," Lena said. "All of you who cannot walk. We will make room."

Hollis looked at her, and there was something in his face that she had not seen before—not gratitude, exactly, but a kind of recognition. "You will need to be hard," he said. "Harder than you have been."

"I know," Lena said.

She went out into the rain and stood for a moment in the yard, letting it soak through her coat. The river was louder now, a constant roar that seemed to fill the valley from ridge to ridge. She thought of the sea, which she had seen only once as a child, a grey immensity that had frightened her. She thought of the settlement at the mouth of the river, which might be a rumour or might be a home. She thought of the faces behind her, waiting for her to lead them into the unknown, and she felt the weight of it settle on her shoulders like a physical thing.
