The storm had scoured the valley of Wending, leaving the air washed clean and smelling of wet stone and bruised clover. Silas, his cloak heavy with rain, moved among the scattered flock, his dog, Mab, a dark, damp shadow at his heels. The clouds were breaking apart, spilling a final, watery gold across the hillsides that rose on either side of the valley floor. It was a light that made everything seem new and fragile. He was counting the sheep, his lips moving in the old, familiar rhythm, when a glint of impossible brightness caught his eye. It lay in a patch of trampled mud, half-hidden by a broken thistle stalk, a thing so out of place it seemed to have fallen from the sky with the rain.

He knelt, his knees sinking into the soft earth. It was a bell, no bigger than a hen’s egg, wrought from a silver so pure it seemed to drink the light and hold it captive. There was no clapper. The surface was smooth, unadorned, save for a single, spiralling line that chased itself around its curve, like a river winding to the sea. It felt cool and solid in his palm, a small, perfect weight. Curiosity, a rare and potent force in Silas’s quiet life, compelled him. He raised the bell to his lips and gave a gentle puff of air.

The sound that emerged was not a clang or a chime, but a single, clear note that hung in the air like a drop of honey. It was a sound of purest silver, and it did not fade, but seemed to expand, to settle over the entire hillside.

And then, the world held its breath. Mab, who had been sniffing at a rabbit hole, froze and then slowly lowered her haunches to the ground. The sheep, which had been bleating and shuffling, all at once stopped. One by one, with a strange, synchronous grace, they sank to their knees in the wet grass. Their woolly heads bowed, their eyes turned towards him, placid and deep. A hawk that had been wheeling on the thermals above the ridge faltered in its flight, landed on a crag of rock, and tucked its head beneath its wing. Even the insects in the air seemed to cease their buzzing.

Silas stood in a ring of silent, kneeling life, the silver bell cool in his hand. The valley was utterly still. He felt a tremor of fear, not of the animals, but of the power he held, a power so vast and absolute it felt like a blasphemy. He rang the bell again, a soft, tentative note. As if released from a spell, the hawk lifted its head, the sheep began to stir and rise, and Mab shook herself with a confused whine. The normal sounds of the hillside trickled back, but they were thin and distant now.

He knew, with a certainty that settled like a stone in his gut, that he could not simply put it in his pocket and go home. Such a thing did not belong to a shepherd. It belonged to a story, and stories in this valley all had one ending. He tucked the bell into the deepest fold of his tunic, its cold metal a constant, secret pressure against his skin.

Word travelled faster than sheep, and by the next day, it had reached the high walls of Blackwood Keep. Lord Valerius was a man carved from the very granite of his fortress, his ambitions as sharp as the mountain winds that howled around his towers. He had heard a rumour of a shepherd who could command the beasts of the field, and he summoned Silas to his hall.

The great hall was a cavern of shadow and firelight. Lord Valerius sat on a throne of dark oak, his fingers steepled. His eyes, the colour of a winter sky, missed nothing.

“Shepherd,” the lord’s voice boomed, echoing off the stone. “They say you have found a trinket in my fields. A curiosity.”

Silas stood his ground, his worn boots sinking into the thick rush matting. “I found a bell, my lord.”

“A bell,” Valerius repeated, the word a dismissal. “And what is so special about this bell that it makes men whisper and fools gawk? Show me.”

Slowly, Silas reached into his tunic and drew out the silver object. The firelight danced across its surface, making the single spiral seem to writhe. A gasp went through the assembled courtiers. Valerius leaned forward, his casual authority sharpening into a predatory focus.

“Bring it to me,” he commanded.

Silas walked forward and placed the bell on the edge of the lord’s table. Valerius picked it up, weighing it in his hand. “It is a fine piece of silver, I grant you. But it is just a bell. Ring it.”

“I would rather not, my lord,” Silas said quietly. “The sound is… strange.”

“Do not test my patience, shepherd,” Valerius snapped. “Ring it.”

Silas looked from the lord’s hard face to the faces of the guards and courtiers. They were all watching him, their expressions a mixture of greed and fear. He saw no way out. He took a deep breath and leaned towards the bell on the table. He did not touch it, but blew a soft stream of air across its lip.

The note, so clear and pure on the hillside, was different here. In the hall of stone and steel, it was a shock, a shard of impossible light in the gloom. It was a sound that did not belong. And the effect was immediate and terrifying. The great warhounds that lay at Valerius’s feet whimpered and curled into balls. The guards, hardened men in iron plate, stumbled to their knees, their helmets clattering on the floor. The courtiers sank into a heap of velvet and silk. Even the flames in the great hearth seemed to bend towards the sound, their crackling silenced.
