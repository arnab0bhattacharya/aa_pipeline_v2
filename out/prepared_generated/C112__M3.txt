The Reverend Edmund Ashworth of Little Dingley, Hertfordshire, descended from the York coach at the Bell Inn in Cheapside with a sermon in one pocket and a petition in the other. The petition, addressed to the Bishop of London, concerned the leaking roof of St. Cuthbert's parish church, and Edmund had been deputed by his rural dean to carry it into the metropolis in person, there to press the matter upon the diocesan authorities with whatever eloquence a conscientious curate of seven years' standing might muster. He was a man of nine-and-thirty, plainly dressed, with a weathered complexion that spoke more of country churchyards than of London drawing rooms, and he carried himself with that particular species of self-possession which arises not from worldly confidence but from an unshaken conviction that one's errand is just.

His cousin, Mr. Theophilus Forsythe, of Half Moon Street, had insisted by letter that Edmund lodge with him during his stay. Theophilus was a man of fashion in the way that lesser stock-jobbers are men of fortune: persistently, and with a great deal of display. He had married well, and his wife, Mrs. Clara Forsythe, moved in that ambiguous tier of society which is too respectable for trade and too new for the true ton. Edmund had accepted the invitation with gratitude, for he was not a man to refuse kindness, even when he suspected it sprang less from familial warmth than from a desire to exhibit a live country clergyman to a circle of acquaintances who might find such a creature diverting.

He was not mistaken in this suspicion. Within an hour of his arrival, Mrs. Forsythe had dispatched notes to no fewer than a dozen friends, announcing a small supper for that very evening, at which her cousin from Hertfordshire would be present. "He is quite the original," she informed her husband as Edmund washed the road dust from his hands above stairs. "He travels with a sermon in his coat and thinks the roof of his church a matter of urgent concern. I daresay he has not been to London above twice in his life."

The supper party assembled at eight. The drawing room in Half Moon Street was hung with French paper of a blue sufficiently pale to suggest expense, and lit by candles in silver branches which threw a soft and becoming light upon the assembled company. There was a Colonel Pendle, retired on half-pay and full of opinions; a Mrs. Marchmont, widowed, sharp, and much given to observing the follies of others; a young Mr. Ayscough, who was studying for the bar and considered everything beneath his notice; and several others whose names Edmund caught and promptly lost in the general murmur of greeting.

"He is quite as I described," Mrs. Forsythe murmured to Mrs. Marchmont as Edmund was presented. "See how he stares at the paper. I believe he is comparing it to his own whitewash at Little Dingley."

Edmund was indeed observing the wallpaper, but his thoughts were of a different character. He was reflecting that such elegance must have cost a great deal, and that the sum would have covered the slating of St. Cuthbert's nave twice over. This reflection did not, however, produce in him any visible distress. He bowed to the company with a quiet grace that Mrs. Marchmont, for one, found unexpectedly competent, and applied himself to the business of making himself agreeable without attempting to be remarkable.

"You find London very changed, I suppose, Mr. Ashworth?" said Colonel Pendle, who had been told of the cousin's rural origin and approached him with the condescending jollity of a man addressing a child.

"I cannot say, Colonel. I have so little experience of London that I should hardly know what changes to look for. I daresay the mud is much as it ever was."

This sally, delivered without the least appearance of wit, produced a surprising effect. Mr. Ayscough, who had been lounging against the mantelpiece with an expression of cultivated boredom, permitted himself a short laugh. Mrs. Marchmont raised her fan to conceal what might have been a smile. Colonel Pendle, uncertain whether he had been mocked, contented himself with a bluff declaration that the mud was indeed very much as it ever was, and that a man might drown in it between here and Westminster.

At supper, Edmund was seated beside Mrs. Marchmont, who had determined to draw him out and exhibit whatever rustic simplicities might lie beneath his sober exterior. She began by asking whether he found the society of Little Dingley sufficient for a man of parts.

"I have never had occasion to test the question," said Edmund, helping himself to a piece of cold fowl with the unaffected ease of a man who is hungry and sees no reason to conceal it. "The society of Little Dingley consists, for the most part, of a retired hop merchant, his sister, a dairyman's widow, and the squire's youngest son, who is deaf in one ear and much addicted to chess. Whether I have parts sufficient for them, I leave to their judgement. They have not yet complained."

"You are very modest."

"I am very fortunate, madam. A parish is a society in miniature, and a clergyman who cannot find interest in it is likely to find interest nowhere."

Mrs. Marchmont, who had spent fifteen years finding interest in very little, and had compensated by occupying herself intensely with the affairs of others, found this answer oddly disconcerting. She shifted her ground. "And what do you read, Mr. Ashworth, in that remote corner? I suppose the post is irregular."

"The post is tolerably regular, though it brings more bills than books. But I have my predecessor's library, which is considerable. He was a man of learning, though somewhat deficient in pastoral zeal. He left behind a fine edition of Jeremy Taylor and a great quantity of Greek testaments, which I have not yet had occasion to open, not having any parishioners who read Greek."
