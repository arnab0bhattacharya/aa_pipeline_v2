The village of Marston-Ferry, in the county of Warwick, lay at that time somewhat aside from the great currents of traffic, so that its inhabitants preserved a certain simplicity of habit and opinion which the neighbourhood of a thriving manufacturing town might otherwise have worn away. It consisted chiefly of one long street, straggling down to a mill and a bridge, with a few cottages of the older sort scattered upon the rising ground behind, and among them, at the very edge of a small orchard, stood the dwelling of Abel Wharton, weaver, a man of seventy years and more, who had lived there since his boyhood, and whose loom had been heard through the open casement of a summer evening by three generations of his neighbours.

Now it had come to pass that the Railway Company, whose line was to pass through the valley, had need of this cottage and the strip of orchard ground belonging to it, and had offered a sum for the purchase which seemed to the village a very fortune. The neighbours, one and all, had sold their little plots at prices which made them look upon the coming of the railway as a special providence, and had already begun to talk of new grates and fresh thatch, and of putting their sons to a better trade than hedging. But Abel Wharton would neither sell nor treat. He had been heard to say, in his slow, dry fashion, that the cottage was his father’s before him, and that he would not have it pulled about his ears by a parcel of London engineers; and this saying, repeated and exaggerated, had gone through Marston-Ferry with the rapidity peculiar to small communities, where a man’s private resolution becomes in a week the common property and the common grievance.

It was upon a November afternoon, when the fog hung low over the flat meadows and the loom was silent, that the Reverend Mr. Ellwood, the vicar, walked up the lane to Abel’s door. He was a man of fifty, of a kindly and somewhat timid disposition, who loved peace better than victory, and who had been much exercised in mind as to how he should acquit himself in the task now laid upon him. For the principal inhabitants of the village, headed by one Mrs. Harkness, the miller’s widow, had waited upon him in a body, representing that Abel’s obstinacy was a public injury; that the Company had threatened, if the cottage were not yielded, to carry the line somewhat further to the west, whereby the village would lose the benefit of the station; and that it was the vicar’s plain duty, as the friend and pastor of the old man, to bring him to a better mind. Mr. Ellwood had listened with that air of patient consideration which he carried into every transaction of life, and had promised to do what he could; but as he walked up the lane he was conscious of a sinking at the heart, for he had known Abel Wharton these twenty years, and knew that a more immovable man, where his affections were concerned, was not to be found in the county.

He found the old man seated on a bench before the fire, his hands resting on his knees, and his eyes fixed upon the embers with that look of deep and quiet abstraction which is the habit of those who have lived much alone. The room was small and low, but clean, and there was a bunch of dried lavender upon the mantel-shelf, and a Bible and a book of arithmetic, much worn, upon a little table. Abel rose as the vicar entered, and offered him a chair with the grave courtesy of the old rural school, but said nothing.

“I am come, Wharton,” said Mr. Ellwood, after a little preliminary talk about the weather and the old man’s health, “upon a matter which I dare say you can guess without my telling it. The Company’s agent has been with me again, and I am asked to speak to you about the cottage.”

“They may speak,” said Abel, slowly, “but I shall not hear. I have told ’em my mind once for all.”

“But consider, my friend,” said the vicar, gently. “You are an old man, and you have no child to come after you. The money they offer would keep you in comfort to your dying day, and there is not a man in the village but would be the better for the line coming through.”

“I want no money,” said Abel. “I have enough for a plain man, and I have lived here seventy year, and I’ll die here. The loom was my father’s, and the loom is mine, and the walls have heard the sound of it since before I was born. I’ll not have it pulled down.”

“But the neighbours, Wharton,” urged Mr. Ellwood, with a mild persistence; “they are your neighbours, and they have been your friends these many years. They have all sold, and they think it hard that one man should stand in the way of the whole village. Mrs. Harkness was saying to me only yesterday that if the station is carried further off, her son’s trade will be ruined.”

Abel was silent for a moment, and his old face worked a little. “I am sorry for the neighbours,” he said at last; “but I am not to be turned out of my house for the neighbours’ convenience. Let ’em have their station where they like; I have nothing against it. But I will not sell.”

The vicar looked at him with a sort of helpless pity. He was not a man of strong will, and he felt that the old weaver’s resolution was like a rock against which his gentle arguments beat in vain. He tried another tone.
