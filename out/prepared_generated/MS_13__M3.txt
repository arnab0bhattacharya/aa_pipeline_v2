The autumnal rains descended upon the pinnacles of Bath with a dreary, unintermitting perseverance, shedding a gloom upon my respectable establishment in Camden Place that seemed but the outward manifestation of the tempest that had so long raged within my own bosom. I sat by the parlour fire, my hands folded in my lap, seeking in the quietude of my present life that peace which had been denied to my earlier and more impassioned years; yet the howling of the wind without awoke chords of memory that vibrated with a pang of acute suffering. I was, to the eyes of the world, a respectable widow, the keeper of a lodging house esteemed for its order and its quietude; but I knew myself to be a creature bound to the past by chains of iron, which no lapse of time could entirely rust. My first husband, whose name I had laid aside as a garment stained with shame, had been torn from my arms, condemned to transportation for the crime of forgery—a crime born not of innate depravity, but of a wild and misguided ambition, and of that fatal facility in imitation which had been the curse and the ruin of his life. I had believed him dead, or at least buried in that living tomb beyond the seas, from whence no echo of his existence could reach my ears; and I had built up, stone by stone, the edifice of a new existence, casting over the ruins of my former self the decent veil of a second marriage and a reputable widowhood. But the past is not so easily entombed; it rises, a spectre, to claim its own, and to assert its dominion over the fleeting and fragile present.

It was upon that evening, as the shadows deepened and the firelight flickered upon the walls, that a stranger was announced. Mrs. Hodge, my maidservant, brought the card with an air of uncertain apprehension; it bore a name I did not recognise—Mr. Silas Vane—but the glance she cast towards me, and the look of suppressed unease that settled upon her features, told me that the visitor was not of the ordinary class of applicants for apartments. I bade her admit him, and composed myself to receive him with that calmness which I had learned to assume in the face of adversity. He entered, a man of middle age, whose aspect bore the unmistakable stamp of the colonies—a countenance tanned and seared by suns unknown to our English clime, and eyes that gleamed with a restless, searching intelligence that seemed to penetrate the very recesses of my soul. His garb was decent, yet bore the marks of long travel; his hat was pulled low upon his brow, and his coat, though of a fashion not unfamiliar, had that indefinable air of the foreign which marks the man who has long sojourned beyond the confines of his native land. He stood before me, and for a moment neither of us spoke; the silence was that of two combatants who measure each other before the fatal blow is struck.

At length he spoke, and his voice, though low and guarded, thrilled me with a terror I had not felt since the day when the officers of justice had entered my former home and torn from me the husband of my youth. "Madam," he said, "I seek not apartments, but an interview with one whom I once knew under another name—a name which I shall not here pronounce, but which is familiar to you as the name of your first husband, he who was transported, years ago, for the crime of forgery." He paused, and I felt the blood recede from my face; my hands gripped the arms of my chair, and I sat motionless, as one turned to stone by the Gorgon's head. He continued, his words falling with a measured deliberation that was more terrible than any passionate outpouring: "I have come from the land of his exile; I have seen him, conversed with him, and I bear a message from him to you. He lives, and he is in want—urgent, pressing want; and he claims of you the succour which, as his wife in the sight of Heaven, you are bound to bestow."

Then rose within me that tempest of the soul which those only can know who have been torn from all they loved, and who have laboured, through years of silent anguish, to reconstruct a life from the fragments of a shattered existence. I thought of my first husband—of Ambrose, with his dark, flashing eyes, his eloquent tongue, his fatal and misguided genius—and I felt the old love and the old pity surge up within me. I thought, too, of my present life—of the decent respectability which I had earned through years of toil and self-denial, of the good name which I bore in the eyes of my neighbours, of the modest competence which I had gathered together, and which was the sole bulwark between me and destitution. If I yielded to this man's demand, I must strip myself of the savings of years; I must plunge again into that gulf of poverty and shame from which I had with such pain escaped; I must acknowledge, before the world, the tie which I had believed severed by death and distance. And yet—could I refuse? Could I turn a deaf ear to the cry of one who had been all the world to me, whose hand had clasped mine at the altar, whose voice had whispered in my ear the vows of an eternal love? The conflict was terrible; it tore my soul asunder.

I rose from my chair and paced the room, my hands clasped, my bosom heaving with the tumult of my emotions. The stranger watched me in silence, his arms folded upon his breast, his eyes fixed upon me.
