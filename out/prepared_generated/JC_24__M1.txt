The sickness, when it laid hold of Captain Marwood, came upon him with the stealthy, inexorable logic of a tropical squall. One evening he was the master of the *Siren’s Call*, a man of sharp edges and certain opinions, his authority as much a part of the ship as the keel beneath us. By the following noon he was a shadow in the chart-room bunk, his face the colour of old putty, his commands reduced to the dry, whispered rasp of his breathing. And so, by a necessity as natural and as impersonal as the shift of the wind, the command fell to me. I was the first mate, Ferris, and I had known this ship, her moods and her timbers, for three years. I took the sextant from its case, the log-line from its locker, and I took the ship.

What followed was not a triumph, nor was it a usurpation. It was merely a matter of duty, of the sea’s own severe and unarguable grammar. The trade winds, which had seemed capricious under Marwood’s impatient hand, settled into a steady, trustworthy breath. The *Siren’s Call*, a vessel of fine lines but a certain temperamental stiffness in the stays, seemed to find her own best course under my eye. We made the run to the Line with a speed that the old man had only ever wrung from her with curses and the flapping of canvas. The men, who had regarded Marwood’s authority with a resentful, cowed obedience, moved about their work with a quiet, unthinking efficiency that was the highest form of praise a crew can give. I did not ask for it. I only asked for the ship to be handled well, and she was. There was, in the long, sun-drenched days and the star-pricked nights, a kind of peace in the work—the clean, unambiguous logic of it all. I was master of the *Siren’s Call*, and the knowledge sat in me not as a prize, but as a weight, a responsibility as solid and as real as the great iron ballast in her hold.

Then, one morning, as we were running south-east across the vast, lonely emptiness of the South Atlantic, the steward brought word that the Captain was sitting up. I went aft to the chart-room, my heart strangely constricted in my chest. The place smelled of stale sickness, of liniment and unwashed linen. Marwood was propped against the pillows, a shrunken, yellowed effigy of the man he had been. His eyes, which had always been the colour of a winter sea, were sunk deep in his skull, but they held a flicker of the old, hard light.

“Ferris,” he said, and his voice was a dry rustle of leaves. “The reports.”

I gave them to him. The position at noon, the run for the last twenty-four hours, the state of the sails and the gear. I spoke in the formal, clipped tones of the deck, the language of fact. He listened without interruption, his gaze fixed on a point somewhere past my shoulder, as if he were consulting a chart in his own mind.

“You have done well,” he said at last. The words were a judgment, not a compliment. They fell into the silence of the cabin and lay there between us. There was no warmth in them, no gratitude. There was only an acknowledgment of a completed task. He had been ill; I had kept the ship. It was a matter of professional competence, nothing more.

I left him to his convalescence and returned to the deck. The sky was a hard, polished blue, and the sea, a deep, almost purple, exhaled a fine, warm mist that glittered in the sun. But the peace I had known was gone. The ship, which had been my whole world, my problem and my solution, was now merely a vessel, a thing of wood and iron that was about to be contested. I saw the men watching me, not with the old quiet confidence, but with a new, speculative curiosity. They were the audience, and a drama was about to unfold in the small, self-contained world of our deck.

Marwood grew stronger. Each day he appeared for a longer time, first on a deckchair, then leaning against the fife-rail, his hand gripping the wood as if to re-establish a physical claim upon it. He watched me. He watched the men. He watched the set of the sails, the trim of the yards, his eyes missing nothing. He did not countermand my orders, nor did he offer advice. His silence was a heavier presence than any shout. It was a pressure, a moral weight that settled upon the whole ship. He was the Captain. The articles, the law of the sea, the unwritten code of the merchant service, were all on his side. My possession of the command was temporary, a loan, and the hour of its repayment was drawing near.

One evening, the wind having died to a glassy calm, the ship lay motionless on a sea like polished metal. The sails hung limp and useless, and the only sound was the slow, gentle creak of the masts and the sigh of the water against the hull. The sun bled out across the horizon in a spectacular, silent conflagration of red and gold, a display of cosmic indifference to the small human drama aboard the *Siren’s Call*. I stood at the break of the poop, looking aft, when I heard his step behind me. It was firmer now, no longer the shuffle of an invalid.

He came and stood beside me, not looking at me, but at the dying light.

“The ship is well-found, Mr. Ferris,” he said. It was not a question.

“She is, sir,” I replied.
