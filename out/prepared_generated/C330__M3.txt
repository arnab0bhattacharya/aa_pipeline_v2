The parlour of Ferndale Villa smelled of drying wool and the peculiar mustiness of a house kept too cold to save on coal. Clara Hopper stood by the window, watching the October fog swallow the gardens of Thornhill Crescent, and felt the weight of the brass ring on her left hand. It was not a wedding ring. It was an engagement ring, given to her seven years ago by Edmund Lyle, who had sworn then that their nuptials were but a year away. The ring had grown loose on her finger over time, not because her hands had thinned—though they had—but because the metal itself seemed to have tired of its purpose, wearing down to a pale, dull ghost of the promise it was meant to embody.

She turned from the window. Edmund sat at the table, turning the pages of a pamphlet describing some new speculative venture in railway shares, his long face illuminated by the single candle they could afford to burn. He was thirty-one now. She was twenty-eight. When they had met, he had been a junior clerk at a shipping concern in Limehouse, full of talk about promotion and partnership. The promotion had come, modestly, and then stalled. The partnership never materialised. The shipping concern had folded. He had taken a position as a bookkeeper for a firm of auctioneers, then left it after a quarrel over a shilling discrepancy that he claimed was a matter of principle. He was now between positions again, living in the upstairs room of his uncle's house in Islington, and Clara lived in the parlour floor below, occupying the space that in a happier household would have been called the morning room, paid for by the small income from her late father's trust and the sewing work she took in from the draper's on Upper Street.

She had watched him wait for prospects to improve the way a farmer watches a sky that will not rain. Seven years of prospects failing to improve. Seven years of Edmund explaining, with his careful, reasonable voice, that it would be foolish to marry until he was established. That he would not see her reduced. That a man of honour could not ask a woman to share a life he had not yet built. The speeches were beautiful. She had believed them the first three years, doubted them the fourth and fifth, and by the sixth had come to understand them as a kind of music he played to avoid the silence where a decision should have been.

"Edmund," she said.

He looked up. He had the sort of face that aged without ever quite maturing—the same smooth brow, the same earnest, slightly startled eyes that had looked up at her from across a crowded pew at St Mary's Church on their first meeting.

"I have something to say, and I would ask you to listen before you answer."

He set down the pamphlet. She could see him preparing his expression, the one he wore when he anticipated a conversation he would need to navigate rather than engage with.

"I will not wait another year," she said. "I have decided. If we are not married by Christmas, I will return this ring to you and take the position Mrs Whitelaw has offered me as companion to her sister in Bristol. The wage is twelve pounds per annum and a room of my own. It is not a marriage, but it is a life, which is more than I have at present."

Edmund's face cycled through surprise, hurt, and then a kind of wounded dignity that Clara knew better than to trust. He stood and took her hand, the one with the ring, and pressed it between both of his.

"Clara, dearest. You cannot think I have been idle. You cannot think I do not wish it above all things. The difficulty is purely practical. I have no income to speak of, and you know full well that Uncle Gilbert has made provision for me in his will. The house, the shares, a not inconsiderable sum of money—all of it comes to me when he passes. He is sixty-three years old, Clara. He has the gout. The apothecary has warned him about his heart. It cannot be long now. I have been patient, and I beg you to be patient a little longer."

"You have been patient," Clara said. "That is precisely the word. Patient. As though marriage were an unpleasant medicine to be delayed as long as possible."

"Not patient in that sense. Patient in the sense of waiting for the right moment."

"The right moment has been six months away for seven years, Edmund. It travels at the same speed as your uncle's demise, apparently, which is to say, not at all."

He flinched. She was sorry for it, but not sorry enough to stop.

"I will marry you when Uncle Gilbert dies," he said, with the gravity of a man making a solemn oath. "I give you my word. It is a matter of weeks, or months at most. Surely you can endure until then."

Clara withdrew her hand. She looked at him for a long moment, at the face she had loved since she was one-and-twenty, and felt the strange, cold clarity that comes when love has not died but has finally stopped making excuses. She said nothing. She picked up her sewing basket and went upstairs to her room.

What neither of them knew was that Uncle Gilbert was in the scullery.

Gilbert Murch had come in through the back door, having returned from the chemist on Theberton Street with a bottle of colchicum for his gout, and had paused in the scullery to remove his boots, which were pinching. The scullery shared a wall with the parlour, and the wall was thin, a fact Gilbert had cause to regret each morning when Clara's sewing machine began its clattering at dawn. He had heard every word.
