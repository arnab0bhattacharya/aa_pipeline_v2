The wind came off the Channel like a living thing, rattling the leaded glass of the study window and throwing handfuls of sea-spray against the stone. Captain Elias Thorne sat in his leather chair, watching the grey water churn, his hands resting on the arms with the stillness of a man accustomed to the deck of a ship. He was seventy-one years old, though he carried himself with the rigid economy of someone decades younger. His face was weathered to the texture of old rope, and his eyes, pale and watery as they were, held the kind of authority that made grown men straighten their posture in his presence.

The young man sitting across from him needed no such encouragement. Mr. Arthur Pemberton, a historian of modest reputation and immodest ambition, sat with his notebook open on his knee, his pen poised but unmoving. He had come by coach from London, a journey of some discomfort, and had presented himself at Thorne's door in Deal with a letter of introduction from the Admiralty that the captain had read once and set aside without comment.

"You must understand, Mr. Pemberton," Thorne said, his voice carrying the low, carrying quality of a man who had spent his life making himself heard above wind and cannon, "that I have no interest in contributing to your book. The affair you mention is long past, and I see no purpose in dragging its bones into the light."

Pemberton leaned forward slightly. He was perhaps twenty-five, with dark hair and the kind of earnest, unblemished face that had not yet learned to conceal its disappointment. "Captain Thorne, the mutiny aboard the Orion is a matter of historical record. The Admiralty logs confirm your role in its suppression. I am not asking you to confess to anything. I am asking you to provide your account, so that the fullest picture might be preserved."

"Preserved for whom?" Thorne said. "For those who were not there and never will be? For readers who will turn the pages between their tea and their biscuits and pronounce judgement on men they never knew?"

"For posterity, sir."

Thorne turned from the window and regarded Pemberton with an expression that might have been amusement or might have been something harder. "Posterity. That great and impartial jury that never heard a shot fired in anger. I think not, Mr. Pemberton. You may write your book. You may consult whatever logs and registers you wish. But you will not have my testimony. I have not spoken of the Orion in forty-three years, and I do not intend to begin now."

Pemberton closed his notebook slowly, and Thorne might have taken this for a concession if he had not noticed the way the young man's hand moved to the leather satchel at his side. There was a deliberateness in the gesture, a rehearsed quality, that put the captain in mind of a man preparing to lay a card he had been holding for some time.

"I understand your reluctance, Captain," Pemberton said. "And I respect it. But before I go, I wonder if you might look at something."

He drew from the satchel a folded letter, yellowed with age, the creases so worn they were almost translucent. He held it out across the space between them. Thorne did not take it immediately. He looked at the letter, then at Pemberton, and something shifted behind his eyes, a shadow of recognition or foreboding, though his expression did not change.

"What is it?" Thorne said.

"A letter written aboard the Orion, on the night before the execution. Written by a seaman named Joseph Calloway."

The name fell into the room like a stone into still water. Thorne did not move. His hands remained on the arms of his chair, but the tendons stood out more sharply now, as though the bones beneath had tightened.

"Where did you get that," Thorne said. It was not a question. It was a demand, stripped of all courtesy.

"It was among the personal effects of Calloway's widow, who died last year in Portsmouth. Her daughter preserved it, and it passed through several hands before it came to mine. I have verified the handwriting against Calloway's service records. It is authentic."

Thorne reached out and took the letter. The paper was brittle and faintly damp from the air. He unfolded it with care, and Pemberton watched the captain's eyes move across the lines of text, watched the colour drain slowly from his face, leaving it the colour of chalk.

The letter was written in a cramped, laboured hand, the letters formed with the awkwardness of a man who had learned to write late in life and with difficulty. It was addressed to Calloway's wife, Margaret, and it began with the ordinary endearments of a husband to a woman he expected never to see again. But it moved quickly to the matter at hand.

*I write to you now, Maggie, not to protest my innocence, for I know what I did and I do not shrink from it. But I want you to know the truth of it, so that when they tell you I was a mutineer and a villain, you will know what manner of men are calling me so. We took the ship because we were dying, Maggie. Eighteen men had succumbed to the fever by the time we reached the tropics, and the surgeon himself was among them. The captain, Mr. Thorne, would not put in at Saint Helena as we begged him to. He said the schedule would not permit it, that the Admiralty expected us in Bombay by the seventh of September, and that he would not have his record blemished by the weakness of common sailors. We begged him, Maggie, on our knees. And he told us that if we did not return to our duties, he would have us flogged until we found our courage.*
