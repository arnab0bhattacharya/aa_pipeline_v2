It was a truth universally acknowledged, at least in the circles of the fashionable, that a clergyman from the country, unaccustomed to the glare of society and the subtleties of wit, was to be endured as one endures a tedious sermon: with polite attention and a secret longing for the bell. Mr. Arthur Pym, a man of forty years, modest means, and unshakeable good humor, had arrived in London for the express purpose of petitioning his Bishop concerning a matter of parochial dispute. His host, Colonel Frederick Hartley, his distant cousin and a man of considerable standing in the West End, had received him with the enthusiastic tolerance reserved for specimens of the natural world.

“You are a curiosity, Pym,” Colonel Hartley declared, as they sat in the drawing-room of a townhouse in Belgravia, the air thick with the scent of expensive perfume and the smoke of a dozen cigars. “A genuine, unpolished rustic. I expect my guests to be highly amused by your simplicity. Do try to be as dull as possible; it is the only service you can render to my reputation for eccentricity.”

Mr. Pym smiled, a gentle expression that did not quite reach his eyes, which held a quiet, steady assessment. “I am sure, Colonel, that I shall be as agreeable as my lack of training permits. I leave the amusement to those who have had the advantage of a metropolitan education.”

The conversation that followed was a contest of wits in which Mr. Pym was, by design, to lose. The company, a mix of titled ladies and men of letters, treated him with the condescension one might show a well-behaved puppy. Mrs. Gower, a lady of some sharpness, asked him if he had seen any good plays.

“I have not had the pleasure, ma’am,” Mr. Pym replied. “My time has been occupied with the more pressing duties of my calling. I find that the dramas of the stage often present a more idealized view of human nature than I am accustomed to find in the parsonage, and I am not yet confident that I can distinguish the fiction from the fact with sufficient skill.”

A titter ran around the room. Colonel Hartley looked pleased. “Precisely the sort of provincial blindness we hope to cure,” he said, turning to his wife. “He does not know that the stage is the mirror of society, Mr. Pym. It is where we learn how to live.”

“I am aware, sir, that it is a mirror,” Mr. Pym said calmly. “I have only observed that mirrors, when held up to very bright light, sometimes show only the face of the one who holds them, rather than the scene behind. I suspect the playgoers are more interested in seeing their own reflections than in studying the characters.”

The titter ceased. There was a momentary pause, a slight shift in the atmosphere, as if a stone had dropped into a still pond. Mrs. Gower raised her eyebrows, her fan pausing halfway to her lips. It was not a brilliant retort, nor was it delivered with the flash of a wit; it was stated with such plain, unassuming truth that it seemed to disarm the mockery.

Colonel Hartley, sensing the shift, attempted to regain control. “You are a skeptic, I see. A dangerous quality in a clergyman. It suggests a lack of faith.”

“I find, sir, that faith and doubt are often better companions than faith and ignorance,” Mr. Pym said. “I do not doubt the truth of my religion, but I do doubt the wisdom of men who believe they can command the truth by shouting louder than their neighbors. In the country, we learn to listen. In London, I am told, one must shout to be heard. I am not yet skilled at shouting.”

This, perhaps, was the beginning of the end of his status as a mere curiosity. For there was a quiet dignity in his simplicity that began to appeal to those who were tired of the performative brilliance of their own company. Mr. Thorne, a young man of no particular rank but of considerable observation, leaned forward.

“Do you find the Bishop difficult to please, Mr. Pym? I hear he is a man of very specific tastes.”

Mr. Pym looked at him with a genuine, unguarded smile. “The Bishop is a good man, Mr. Thorne, but he is like a cat. He must be approached slowly, and one must never show that one has a purpose, or he will strike out. I have found that the best way to petition a man of power is to make him feel that he is doing you a kindness, rather than granting you a right. It is a small trick, but it often works.”

The room was quiet. There was a certain freshness in the way Mr. Pym spoke of power, devoid of the usual cynicism or sycophancy. It was a practical wisdom, born of necessity and experience, that cut through the abstract posturing of the drawing-room.

Mrs. Gower, who had been preparing a witty remark, found herself at a loss. She looked at Colonel Hartley, who was staring at Mr. Pym with an expression of mild alarm, as if a familiar animal had suddenly stood up on its hind legs and begun to speak.

“You speak of the Bishop with a familiarity that is unbecoming,” Colonel Hartley said, his voice tighter than before.

“I speak of him as I would of a neighbor,” Mr. Pym replied. “Which, in the eyes of the Church, is perhaps the most appropriate description. We are all neighbors in the valley of the shadow, Colonel. I have little patience for those who pretend to be above it.”

The sentence hung in the air, simple and profound. It was not a joke, nor a flattery, nor a clever turn of phrase. It was a statement of fact, delivered with such sincerity that it seemed to strip the pretensions from the room. The guests looked at one another. The laughter that had been so easy a moment ago now seemed thin and brittle.
