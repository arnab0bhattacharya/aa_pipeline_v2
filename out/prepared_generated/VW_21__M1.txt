The light, which had been thickening all day over the square, now lay upon the floor of the study in long, dusty bars, and in each bar a thousand motes of dust, of powdered plaster and old paper and the skin of books, turned and tumbled in a silent, frantic dance. It was this light, and not her words, which seemed to fill the room now, to press upon the silence after she had spoken. For she had said it. The words, which had for weeks been a shapeless pressure behind her eyes, a weight in her chest, had at last been given to the air, and hung there, thin and definite and irrevocable. I shall take rooms. In Bloomsbury, perhaps. And find work.

Her father did not move. He sat behind his great mahogany desk, a desk which was less a piece of furniture than a continent, mapped with the open pages of his histories, his letters, his small, precise handwriting that marched across foolscap like a disciplined legion. His hand, which had been holding a pen, was still poised above a half-finished sentence, the nib a tiny black point of suspended action. He was a man made of books and paper, of chronologies and the fall of empires, and she had just announced a revolution of her own, a small, quiet, personal one, for which he had no footnotes, no index, no place in his carefully ordered scheme of things. The silence was not empty; it was filled with the turning of the dust, the ticking of the clock on the mantelpiece, and the slow, bewildered pulse of his thought, which seemed to reach out and touch her, to ask, with a terrible, wounded clarity, what had he done? Was it the porridge? Was it the quality of the coal? He, who could trace the causes of the Punic Wars to a dispute over a grain tariff in Sicily, could not comprehend the sudden, seismic shift in his own small, domestic kingdom. His world, which had seemed as solid and as permanent as the bindings of his books, had developed a crack, a fissure, through which the cold, unknown air of the future was now blowing.

But it was the aunts who would give the event its proper weight, its place in the family annals. They arrived the next afternoon, Aunt Beatrice and Aunt Agnes, two formidable monuments in black silk and jet, their rustling entry a proclamation in itself. They did not so much enter a room as annex it. Aunt Beatrice, with her bosom like the prow of a ship, took the armchair by the fire as if it were a throne, and Aunt Agnes, thinner, sharper, with eyes like chips of flint, settled herself opposite, her reticule a small, hard knot of disapproval on her lap. They had been summoned, of course. A wire from her father, a few frantic words. A crisis. They understood crises; they were the stuff of family life, the knots in the thread that held them all together.

“Lucia,” Aunt Beatrice began, and her voice had the timbre of a verdict being read. “Your father tells us you are leaving him.”

The words were so different from her own. Leaving him. It sounded like a desertion, an act of cruelty. But it was not that. It was a shedding, a casting off of a skin that had grown too tight, a life that had been lived for so long in another’s orbit that she had forgotten she possessed a sun of her own. She tried to explain. It was not that she did not love him. It was… it was the quiet. The same quiet, day after day. The same four walls, the same pattern of light on the same worn carpet. The feeling, which had begun as a whisper and was now a constant, humming presence, that her own life was passing her by, like a train she had failed to board, its carriages full of other people’s destinations, other people’s work and laughter and love. She wanted to feel the wind on her face. She wanted to earn her own money, however little, to have a name of her own, to be something other than a daughter, a companion, a silent, efficient presence in the house of a great man.

“A name of your own?” Aunt Agnes repeated, her voice thin and dry as old leaves. “What name is better than the one you have? What work is better than the sacred duty of caring for your father in his declining years?” They spoke of duty as if it were a physical substance, a kind of holy water to be sprinkled upon all difficulties. They spoke of the world outside the drawing-room as a sordid, dangerous place, a marketplace of commerce and grubby compromises, utterly unfit for a gently bred woman. They saw her desire not as a longing for life, but as a peculiar, modern affliction, a sort of spiritual measles that had to be run its course before common sense could reassert itself.

And as they spoke, she saw them not as they were now, but as they had always been: fixed points in a landscape that was supposed to remain unchanged. She saw the garden of her childhood, the two of them on the lawn, their black skirts a stark and permanent feature of the summer green. She saw the Christmases, the birthdays, the funerals. A procession of sameness. And her father, at the centre of it all, a sun around which they all dutifully revolved. But a sun, she thought, also casts shadows. And she had lived in his for twenty-eight years.

Later, when the aunts had gone, their formidable presence leaving behind a faint scent of lavender and condemnation, she found her father in the study. The bars of light had gone, and the room was sinking into a soft, grey twilight. He was standing by the window, looking out at the square where the gas lamps were beginning to flicker on, one by one, like hesitant stars. He turned as she entered, and his face, in the gloaming, looked old and deeply tired. The wounded bewilderment was still there, but it had softened into a kind of sorrow.
