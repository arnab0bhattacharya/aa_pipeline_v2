It was on a Tuesday, I think, that the thing happened, and it happened so quietly that for a long time nobody in Wexcombe understood that anything had happened at all. The morning had begun well enough—a soft, grey Berkshire morning with a hint of rain in it—and I was in the yard behind my premises in the High Street, wiping down the brass of a new Humberette that I had sold the week before to Dr. Ellwood, when I first noticed the silence. It was not a proper silence, you understand; there were birds, and the distant clatter of a milk-cart, and someone’s dog. But the road was empty, and the empty road had a quality about it that I did not like. I stood with my rag in my hand and listened, and it came to me that I had not heard the sound of a motor all morning.

My name is Albert Crale, and at that time I was forty-one years old, a widower, and the proprietor of the Wexcombe Motor and Cycle Company, which was a grand name for a shed, a workshop, and a row of petrol tins. I had come to Wexcombe six years before with a capital of three hundred pounds and a conviction that the motor-car was the coming thing, and I had staked everything upon that conviction. I had sold bicycles first, and then motor-bicycles, and then, as the century turned and the roads improved and the gentry of the county began to take an interest, I had sold motor-cars. By the spring of that year I had eleven machines in stock, a mortgage on the premises, and a note at the bank which I would rather not describe. I was, in short, a man who had bet his whole fortune upon the internal combustion engine, and I was beginning to believe that the bet would come off.

The first intimation I had of the disaster was when Dr. Ellwood came hurrying into my yard on foot, his hat in his hand and his face the colour of a tallow candle.

“Crale,” he said, “my car has stopped.”

“Stopped?” said I. “What do you mean, stopped?”

“I mean it has stopped. It stopped in the middle of the lane by the mill, and it will not go on. The engine turns, and there is petrol, and there is spark—I have looked at the spark myself, I am not a fool—and it will not fire. It turns and turns and nothing happens.”

I went with him to look at it, and I confess that I was inclined to be amused. A man like Ellwood, who had bought his first car only a month before, was always having trouble of one sort or another, and I had already learned that nine-tenths of the motor-car’s so-called breakdowns are the fault of the man who drives it. But when I reached the machine I found that the engine turned freely and the magneto gave a fat blue spark, and there was petrol in the float-chamber, and yet the thing would not so much as cough. I took out the plugs and cleaned them, and I adjusted the carburettor, and I cranked until my arm ached, and it was all to no purpose. The engine spun round like a sewing-machine and never once fired.

“It is the same with all of them,” said Ellwood. “I have spoken to Pearson at the garage, and he says his two cars are the same, and the carrier’s van is the same, and there is a man at the station who says the London train has come in with a gentleman who hired a motor from Reading and it died at the bottom of the hill and would not be persuaded.”

I confess that I laughed at him. “It is the damp,” I said. “It is the weather. These things happen.”

But by the afternoon I was not laughing. Every motor-car in Wexcombe had stopped, and not only in Wexcombe but, as the telegrams and the rumours came in, in Newbury and Reading and Basingstoke and London itself. The thing was universal and it was instantaneous, and it was accompanied by no explosion, no flash, no sign of any kind. The machines simply would not fire. They turned and turned and turned, and the petrol ran down into the cylinders, and the sparks jumped at the points, and nothing happened. It was as though some hand had reached down and taken the fire out of the world.

For two days I was in a condition of the utmost misery. I walked about my yard and looked at my eleven machines, and I calculated what I owed and what I had, and the figures were not pleasant. I had a mortgage of four hundred pounds on the premises, and a note at the bank for two hundred and fifty, and the hire-purchase payments on three of the machines were overdue, and I had a wife’s grave to keep up in the churchyard, and I had a boy of fourteen at school in Reading whose fees were due in a fortnight. If the motor-car was dead, then I was dead too—or worse than dead, for a bankrupt is a man who has to go on living.

On the Thursday morning I made up my mind to run for it. I had a little money in the house, about thirty pounds, and I had a cousin in Liverpool who would give me a bed, and I thought that if I could get to the station and take the train to London and then to the north, I might lose myself and begin again. It was not a noble plan, but it was a plan, and I was in the mood to clutch at anything. I packed a bag and put on my coat, and I had my hand on the door when there came a knocking at it, and I opened it to find Mrs. Pearson, the wife of the man who kept the garage in the Market Place, with her baby in her arms and her face all streaked with tears.
