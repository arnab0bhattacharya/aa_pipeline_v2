I had been established at Ferndean Hall for the space of three months before Mr. Vivian Marlowe arrived, and in that time I had learned the cardinal rule of my position: to be as necessary as the furniture and as invisible as the air. My charge, Miss Rosalind Vane, was a child of ten years, quick-witted and spoiled, whose temper flashed like summer lightning and vanished just as swiftly. Her mother, the Lady Adelaide, occupied herself with the vapours of her nerves and the management of the village poor, leaving the training of the child’s mind entirely to my hands—a burden I bore with the silent resignation of one who has no other roof to shelter her.

It was in the library, a room smelling of dry leather and coal-smoke, that I first encountered the cousin. The wind was howling about the gables of the house that November evening, rattling the sash-windows with a violence that seemed to threaten their shattering. I sat by the fire, correcting Rosalind’s geography exercises, when the door was thrown open and Mr. Marlowe entered. He was a man of thirty, with a face that seemed all angles and intensity, his dark hair swept back from a brow that looked made for thought rather than fashion.

"Is this the little philosopher?" he asked, looking not at the child but at me.

"I am the governess," I replied, rising and gathering my papers. "And this is Miss Vane."

"Marlowe, my dear cousin," boomed the voice of Mr. Vane, the master of the house, who followed him in. "You are late. The roads are a quagmire."

"I came to paint the light, Vane, not to dine punctually," said the artist, his eyes still fixed upon me. There was a scrutiny in his gaze that made me flush; it was not the careless glance of a gentleman passing a servant, but the piercing look of a man seeking a subject. I felt like a specimen pinned beneath a glass.

"Miss Grey," Mr. Vane said, with the heavy indifference he usually employed toward me. "You may leave the room. Rosalind is to come to the drawing-room."

I obeyed instantly. Yet, as I passed Mr. Marlowe, he stepped aside to let me go, and I heard him murmur, "A face with a history. Remarkable."

I distrusted it at once. In my experience, compliments from gentlemen to governesses were either the prelude to an insult or a clumsy attempt at flirtation, neither of which I could afford. But Mr. Marlowe was not to be deterred. The following morning, he sought me out in the schoolroom while Rosalind was at her piano lesson.

"I wish to paint you," he said, standing by the window where the weak winter sun struck the floorboards.

"You are mistaken," I said, not looking up from my sewing. "I am not a subject for art. I am a machine for instruction."

"Then you are the most beautiful machine I have ever seen," he retorted, his voice hard. "Do not play the modest saint with me, Miss Grey. I see the fire in you. I see the rebellion. Why do you bury it under that starched collar?"

"I bury it, sir," I said, my needle pausing, "because the world has no use for the passions of a poor woman. We are paid to be placid."

He laughed, a sharp, barking sound. "And I am paid to see the truth. Come to the conservatory at three o'clock. I shall set up my easel."

"I shall not come."

"You will," he said, "because you are curious. And because you are tired of being invisible."

He was right, though I hated him for it. At three o'clock, the rain was lashing against the glass roof of the conservatory, a sound like a multitude of small fists beating for admission. I went. I sat upon the wrought-iron chair he indicated, my hands folded in my lap, my back straight.

"Look at me," he commanded. "Not at the floor. Look at me as if I have wronged you."

"Why should I look at you so?"

"Because you believe I have. You think this portrait is a trick, do you not? You think I mean to use it to provoke Vane, or to humiliate you before his guests."

The truth of his words struck me like a physical blow. "Do you deny it?" I asked, my voice trembling despite my resolve. "Gentlemen do not paint governesses for charity."

"Perhaps I paint you because you are the only real thing in this mausoleum of a house," he said, his brush moving rapidly across the canvas. "Vane is a dullard. Lady Adelaide is a ghost. But you... you burn. I want to capture that before they extinguish you."

For an hour, we sat thus. He asked me questions—sharp, probing questions about my past, my father’s debts, my opinion of Mr. Vane’s politics. I answered him with candor, for I felt that to lie to this man would be futile.

"You have a fierce sense of justice," he observed, squinting at me. "It is a dangerous quality in a woman of your station."

"It is the only thing I own," I replied.

When the light failed, he stopped. He did not show me the canvas, but covered it. "It is not finished," he said. "But the spirit is there."

The trouble began three days later at the dinner table. Mr. Marlowe had brought the canvas into the dining-room, setting it upon an easel in the corner, though it remained draped in a grey cloth. The guests—local gentry with red faces and loud laughs—were curious.

"What is the mystery, Marlowe?" asked Mr. Hargreave, a neighboring squire. "Is it the Lady of the Lake?"

"It is a study of character," Marlowe said, his eyes finding mine across the room where I sat at the sideboard, waiting to refill the water glasses. "I call it 'The Governess'."
