It was a circumstance of no small moment in the mind of the town of Wenderby, when it became known that Mr. Everard Hatherley, the magistrate, had required of Herr Albrecht Sturm, the music-master, a written declaration of his political faith, or, more strictly speaking, of his freedom from certain dangerous opinions which were supposed to have been imported from the Continent along with his violin and his foreign manners. The town of Wenderby, in the year 1842, was not without its share of that vague alarm which, like a damp fog, had settled upon many an English county, penetrating into the snug parlours of the gentry and the sanded kitchens of the poor, and making men suspicious of their neighbours’ politics as they might be of a smoky chimney or a leaky roof. It was a time when the words “radical” and “chartist” carried with them a flavour of gunpowder and torchlight; and a man who could not give a satisfactory account of his opinions was liable to be regarded as a sort of moral firebrand, capable of setting the whole parish in a blaze.

Herr Sturm had come to Wenderby some seven years before, a thin, grave man of middle age, with a high forehead and a manner of quiet courtesy that had at first been set down to pride, but had gradually won for him a certain respect among those who were not too much afraid of foreign ways to employ him. He taught the piano-forte and the violin to the daughters of the professional men and the more aspiring tradesfolk; and it was said that he could make the instrument speak, as it were, in a language of its own, so that even the dullest pupil caught something of his enthusiasm. He lived in a small house in a back street, with a garden of three apple-trees and a plot of vegetables, and was known to read books in a language which no one in Wenderby could understand, and to take long walks upon the common in all weathers, which was thought to be a sign of a brooding and possibly a discontented mind.

The immediate cause of the present commotion was a trifle in itself, as is often the case with the sparks that set fire to a whole magazine of latent suspicion. At a soirée given by Mrs. Hatherley, the magistrate’s wife, for the benefit of the Dorcas Society, Herr Sturm had been persuaded to bring his violin and to play a sonata. The performance was much admired; but in the course of the evening, a young gentleman of a literary turn, who had been reading some translated German poetry, had asked the musician his opinion of certain political theories then much canvassed in the newspapers. Herr Sturm, who was not a man of many words in English, had replied, with a smile and a shrug, that he was no politician, but that he had observed in his own country that when men were very poor and very ignorant, it was not wonderful if they listened to those who promised them bread and liberty; and that he thought it the duty of the rich to give them something better than promises. The young gentleman had repeated this speech with embellishments at the next meeting of the Wenderby Reading Society; and by the time it had passed through the alembic of half a dozen tea-tables, it had become a declaration that Herr Sturm had spoken of the rich as tyrants, and had expressed a hope that the poor would rise and take their property.

Mr. Hatherley was a man of rigid principles and a somewhat narrow understanding—a combination which produces in some natures a sort of moral petrifaction, and in others a restless desire to impose their own order upon a world that is perpetually slipping away from them. He was not a cruel man; he was, indeed, a kindly father and a just landlord, so far as his light went; but his light was of that kind which, while it illuminates the path immediately before him, throws a very dubious and distorting radiance upon everything beyond. He had heard the report of Herr Sturm’s speech with a dismay that was almost personal. It seemed to him that the man whom he had admitted to the society of his wife and daughters, who had sat at his own table and played to his own guests, was a serpent that had been warmed in his bosom. He did not stop to consider that a man may have a generous sympathy with the poor without wishing to overturn the constitution; that the foreigner’s broken English might have been the vehicle of a very harmless sentiment. He saw only the danger, and he was resolved to meet it with the promptitude of a magistrate.

Accordingly, on a certain Tuesday morning, when the fog hung heavy over the town and the streets were sloppy with recent rain, Mr. Hatherley walked down to the back street where Herr Sturm lived, and knocked at the door with the handle of his umbrella. He was shown into the little parlour, where a fire was burning and a book lay open on the table, and where the musician received him with a grave inclination of the head and a “Good morning, sir,” that had in it nothing of servility.

“Herr Sturm,” said the magistrate, taking the chair that was offered him, and clearing his throat with the solemnity of a man who is about to perform a public duty, “I have come to speak to you upon a matter which I trust you will understand is dictated by no personal unfriendliness, but by a regard for the peace and security of this town. It has been represented to me that you have used language in public which is calculated to excite discontent among the lower orders, and to bring into disrepute the institutions of this country.
