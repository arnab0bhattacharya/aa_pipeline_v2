Of all the places in the world to learn the proper use of a copper and a needle, the Conciergerie is, I grant you, the least likely. Yet it is a truth, and a truth which I have had occasion to observe with a mixture of pity, wonder, and no small degree of exasperation, that the proud family of the de Valois, who would not have known a spit from a poker had their lives depended upon it—which, in a manner of speaking, they soon did—were compelled to acknowledge the superiority of a humble English governess in the arts of survival.

My name is Clara Finch, and I was employed in the household of the Marquis de Valois for the purpose of teaching his two children, the flighty Mademoiselle Isabelle and the perpetually sulky young Monsieur Henri, the intricacies of the English tongue. It was a post of some distinction, or so I was led to believe, though the distinction was largely a matter of enduring the Marquise’s sighs and the Marquis’s utter, magnificent ignorance of anything that did not pertain to the cut of a waistcoat or the price of a new wig. The family was, in its own estimation, as permanent a fixture of the Parisian firmament as the very spires of Notre Dame; they moved through their gilded rooms with an air of divine right, leaving a trail of untied ribbons, misplaced books, and half-finished chocolate cups in their wake.

Then came the storm. It did not arrive all at once, but gathered like a fever, with a rumour here and a strange proclamation there, until the streets themselves seemed to have caught the ague. The Marquis, who had once dismissed the rabble with a flick of his lace cuff, now stood at his tall windows and watched the tricolour cockades with a face the colour of old parchment. The decision to flee was made, as all decisions in that household were, with a great deal of bluster and a lamentable lack of preparation. We were to go at midnight, in a coach with the crest painted over, to a cousin in Lyon.

We did not, as it happened, get to the corner of the Rue de Rivoli. The coach was stopped by a mob, a roaring, ragged sea of humanity that seemed to have boiled up from the very gutters of the city. There was shouting, and the splintering of wood, and a face, grimed with soot and fury, pressed against the window-glass, its eyes like chips of flint. The Marquis, for all his noble blood, let out a sound like a punctured bellows. In a trice, we were all of us hauled from the carriage—the Marquis, the Marquise, their two terrified children, and poor Clara Finch, still clutching a grammar book to her bosom—and marched through the streets to the great grey fortress that loomed over the river like a stone giant.

And so we came to the Conciergerie.

Our cell was a stone box, damp and cold, with a single barred window that looked upon a courtyard of mud and misery. The Marquis, who had never in his life put on his own stockings, sank onto the single wooden bench and stared at the wall as if it had personally insulted him. The Marquise fainted, which was her customary response to any inconvenience more pressing than a misplaced fan, and the children, having lost their governess’s authority along with the rest of the world’s order, began to weep and shriek in a chorus of pure despair.

It was then that I, Clara Finch, felt a strange sense of clarity descend upon me. While the nobility of France performed its tragic pantomime, I saw, with the practical eye of a woman whose mother had raised six children on a vicar’s stipend, the business that lay before us. There was a leak in the corner; a rag was needed. The Marquise was blue in the lips; a fire was needed. The children were filthy and terrified; soap and a firm hand were needed.

I found a bucket and a piece of sacking, and I mopped the floor. I found a flint and, after much effort, coaxed a miserable flame from a few precious sticks of wood in the hearth. I took the children by the shoulders, sat them down, and with a voice that brooked no argument, ordered them to be silent.

“My dear Mademoiselle Finch,” the Marquise whispered, her voice thin as a thread, “what is to become of us?”

“We are to keep clean, and we are to keep warm, and we are to keep our wits about us, Madame,” I said, wringing out the cloth. “And we are to begin by mending that tear in Monsieur Henri’s sleeve, for a hole is an invitation to despair.”

The family watched me as if I were a magician performing some novel and baffling trick. They had always seen me as a piece of the furniture—a useful, invisible object that produced conjugations and corrected posture. Now, as I set a pot of water to boil over the fire and began to wash the grime from the children’s faces, they saw me as something else entirely: a provider.

The days that followed were a strange inversion of our former life. The proud de Valois, who had never deigned to notice the servants who dressed them, now depended upon me for every comfort. It was I who learned to bargain with the grim-faced gaoler’s wife for a little extra cabbage or a knob of butter. It was I who washed their linen in the icy water from the pump, my hands raw and red, while the Marquise looked on, her fine hands clasped in her lap, unable to comprehend the mechanics of a washboard. It was I who sat with a candle-end and mended their tattered finery, my needle flying in and out, a tiny silver sword against the encroaching ruin.
