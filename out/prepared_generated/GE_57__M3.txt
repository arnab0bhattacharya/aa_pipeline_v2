It is a common error in the arithmetic of human sympathy to suppose that a young man, elevated by the sheer friction of his own ambition from the dusty pavement of a provincial town to the high stool of a bank clerk, is thereby rendered invulnerable to the softer, less calculable shocks of fortune. We imagine that because he has conquered the ledgers, mastered the intricate geometry of debits and credits, and acquired a penmanship as firm and unhesitating as the iron railings outside the bank itself, he must possess a corresponding mastery over the vagaries of his own heart. Yet the human will, however resolute in its public trajectory, is privately a web of exquisite and often pathetic susceptibilities; and the very isolation of his ascent leaves him profoundly unguarded against the sudden, intoxicating warmth of a superior station’s notice.

Such was the case with Arthur Pell, a young man of two-and-twenty, whose father had been a journeyman nail-maker in the sprawling, unsavoury alleys of Milby, and whose mother, upon being left a widow with a slender annuity and a singularly obstinate faith in her son’s capacity, had taken a lease on a small haberdashery in the market square. Arthur’s promotion to the chief clerkship at the Basset Mutual Bank had been a quiet revolution in the Pell household, altering the very atmosphere of the back parlour, which now seemed to breathe a more genteel air, scented less by coarse wool and more by the faint, aspiring aroma of India ink. He was a youth of considerable comeliness, possessing a broad, thoughtful forehead and eyes of a clear, dark grey, which looked out upon the world with a gravity that his female customers mistook for haughtiness, but which was, in truth, merely the natural expression of a mind perpetually totting up the consequences of its own actions.

It was into this sphere of careful, arithmetical progression that Miss Clara Vawney projected herself, with a momentum that was as heedless of his calculations as it was of her own father’s designs. Clara was the only daughter of Mr. Vawney, the bank’s manager, a gentleman of portly bearing and an imperious civility who resided in the pretentious red-brick villa on the Rise, overlooking the smoky chimneys of the town. She was a young woman of quick, restless intelligence, and a beauty that was perhaps more dazzling than profound—hair of a pronounced auburn, a complexion that flushed easily, and a mouth given to a certain impetuous curve. But beneath these lively attributes lay a profound, smouldering resentment of her father’s domestic tyranny. Mr. Vawney’s affection for his daughter was of that exacting, inventory-taking sort which weighs every ribbon and music-book against a ledger of filial duty; and Clara, feeling the house on the Rise to be a gilded but airless cage, had fixed upon the desperate resolve of effecting her escape by means of a matrimonial alliance that would at once sting her father’s pride and sever her dependence upon his purse.

Arthur Pell, she perceived, was not entirely disagreeable. He was poor, which was an excellent foundation for a romantic protest; he was presentable, which saved her aesthetic sensibilities; and he was sufficiently grateful. That he should be grateful was the very postulate upon which her scheme rested. She had begun her campaign with a series of seemingly accidental encounters in the long, echoing vestibule of the bank, where the light fell in dusty parallelograms upon the polished oak, and where the quiet scratching of pens seemed to Arthur the only music the world required. Then came the borrowed volumes of poetry, slipped between the ledgers with a murmured, breathless comment on a particular stanza, which Arthur, in the innocence of his literary devotion, took to be a spontaneous outpouring of a kindred soul. He was, indeed, profoundly flattered. The condescension of Miss Vawney was not like the ordinary condescension of the rich; it was softened by a tremor in her voice, a lingering glance, which suggested that she, too, suffered, and that in the communion of their suffering there was a bond which the world’s vulgar distinctions could not sever.

But if Arthur’s imagination, soaring on the wings of a newly awakened passion, carried him to a height from which the whole landscape of his life seemed transfigured, there was one pair of eyes in Milby that viewed the prospect with a very different optic. Mrs. Pell, from behind the counter of her small shop, amid the reels of cotton, the buttons of bone and pearl, and the faint, comforting smell of unwashed linen, had been a silent and minutely observant witness of her son’s altered demeanour. She was a woman of robust frame, her features somewhat hardened by the chafing of a frugal existence, but possessing a shrewdness that was as much a part of her as the dark stuff gown and the clean apron. She had seen Arthur come in of an evening with a heightened colour and a preoccupied step, and she had noted, with the keen scent of a mother who has guarded her treasure through many a bleak winter, the scent of a foreign perfume that clung to his coat.

“Arthur,” she said one evening, as he sat by the fire, turning the pages of a book he did not read, his eyes fixed on the glowing coals with a rapt, almost painful intensity, “tha’rt gotten summat new in thy head, I can see. But mind what I say to thee: fine feathers make fine birds, but they dunna keep ‘em warm in winter. Miss Vawney’s a pretty lass, and her feyther’s a rich man; but when rich folks come a-courting poor folks, there’s allays a cat i’ th’ adow. Tha’rt clerk to her feyther, Arthur, and that’s all. She’d none o’ thee if she hadn’t summat to gain by it, or summat to spite her feyther for. I know the breed. Her mother was a Fetherstone, and they were allays for taking what they could and giving nowt back.”
