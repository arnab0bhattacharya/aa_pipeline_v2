It was a truth universally acknowledged by the inhabitants of Thornfield Park that Miss Arabella Finch was in want of nothing; for she was the eldest daughter of Sir Richard Finch, a widower of respectable fortune and indifferent spirits, and had the comfort of knowing that the estate, though modest, was secured to her by the entail.

Her younger sister, Miss Harriet, was by no means so fortunate in her situation, though she possessed that superior degree of beauty which frequently serves as a substitute for property in the estimation of the world. It was, therefore, with a mixture of alarm and satisfaction that Sir Richard observed the simultaneous arrival of two suitors at his table.

The first was Mr. Peregrine Thorne, a gentleman of some three thousand pounds a year, whose estate of Oakhurst lay adjacent to their own. Mr. Thorne was a man of extensive acres and limited conversation, whose views on agriculture were as unyielding as the clay of his fields. He visited Thornfield not so much to pay his addresses to Miss Harriet, as to inspect the hedgerows, though he possessed the good sense to direct his compliments toward the lady whenever Sir Richard appeared in the room.

The second visitor was Mr. Edmund Sterne, the new curate of the parish, a gentleman of lively parts and a patrimony consisting chiefly of his own abilities. He was possessed of a wit that could make the most tedious sermon appear a triumph of rhetoric, and a pair of dark eyes that Miss Arabella found entirely disagreeable, for he looked at Harriet whenever he spoke.

It was upon a rainy Tuesday in October that the sisters sat together in the morning-room, the window obscured by a persistent drizzle that seemed designed to keep the gentlemen within doors. Arabella was occupied with a piece of needlework of great complexity and no beauty, while Harriet gazed out at the wet gravel, her mind evidently wandering toward matters more agreeable than silk and linen.

"I wonder," said Arabella, breaking a silence of some minutes, "whether Mr. Thorne has taken the trouble to notice the state of the lane. It is quite impassable for a carriage. A man of his consequence ought to speak to the parish about it."

"Perhaps he finds the lane more interesting than the society," replied Harriet, with a smile she did not attempt to suppress. "He has been here three days, and has said more to the stable boy than to me."

Arabella’s needle paused. "You judge him too harshly. Mr. Thorne is a man of substance. He does not deal in the fripperies of conversation. His silence is the mark of a serious mind, one capable of sustaining the responsibilities of a large family."

"Or perhaps," said Harriet, "it is merely the mark of a large appetite. He spoke quite volubly of the venison yesterday."

"Harriet, you are incorrigible," Arabella sighed, putting down her work. "I speak to you as a sister and a friend. You are young, and it is natural for you to be dazzled by the superficial sparkle of a curate’s wit. But consider your future. Mr. Sterne is a gentleman, I allow, but he has nothing. Nothing at all. His education is his only estate, and that is a commodity which depreciates sadly when the winter fires must be paid for."

Harriet turned from the window. "And Mr. Thorne? Is his silence to be my companion for the rest of my life?"

"Mr. Thorne is a man of character," Arabella pressed on, her voice taking on that didactic tone which she reserved for her sister’s benefit. "He is steady. He is known in the county. He does not have to borrow books from the lending library to pass the evening. Think, Harriet, of the comfort of independence. Think of the carriage you would command. Think of the position."

"I think," said Harriet, observing her sister’s flushed cheeks, "that you speak with a great deal of feeling on the subject."

Arabella drew herself up. "I speak only for your good. I have no interest in Mr. Thorne beyond the general welfare of my family. It would be a sad thing to see my sister throw herself away on a man who, for all his cleverness, must calculate the cost of a riband before he buys it. Mr. Sterne is a dangerous man for a young woman of your disposition. He flatters your vanity."

It was at this moment that the object of their debate entered the room, followed closely by Sir Richard and Mr. Thorne. The gentlemen had been engaged in a debate regarding the price of turnips, a subject upon which Mr. Thorne was capable of speaking for some time without interruption.

Mr. Sterne, however, having bowed to the ladies, immediately sought to engage Harriet in conversation, leaving Mr. Thorne to stand by the mantelpiece, where he warmed his hands and looked at the fire with the air of a man who has found his proper element.

"I hope," said Mr. Sterne, taking a seat near the window, "that the weather has not been too severe upon your spirits, Miss Harriet. The rain has a melancholy aspect, though I find it conducive to thought."

"It is conducive to mud," observed Mr. Thorne, without turning round. "The lower meadow is flooded. I saw it as I rode over. You will lose the second crop of hay, Sir Richard."

Sir Richard looked pained. "I had hoped the drainage would hold."

"Drainage requires capital," said Mr. Thorne. "And labor. The men here are indolent."

Harriet looked at Mr. Sterne. "And what does the curate say of the mud? Does it improve the sermon?"

Mr. Sterne smiled, a smile that Arabella, watching from behind her embroidery frame, found to be an impertinent affectation. "I say that mud is the great equalizer, Miss Harriet. It clings to the boot of the squire as readily as to the shoe of the pauper. But I would not trouble you with philosophy when there is music to be had. Will you not play for us?"
