The last of the sun, a thin, buttery wash, slid over the ridges and left the valley to its sudden, blue-shadowed evening. The air, which all day had been thin and sharp with the scent of pine and cold rock, softened now, taking on the damp smell of the earth cooling. From the inn, a low, stone building huddled against the slope as if for warmth, came the sound of a zither being tuned, a series of plucked, metallic questions followed by silence. Martin stood at the window of their small room, watching the light fail on the face of the opposite mountain, a great grey wall that seemed to hold the last of the day in its cracks and fissures. It was all so still, so immense, and he felt himself a small, temporary thing against it, his own heart beating a quick, foolish rhythm against the vast, slow pulse of the stone.

Behind him, Leo was unpacking, the quiet rustle of his shirts a domestic counterpoint to the grandeur outside. Leo, who had planned this whole expedition with that quiet, dogged enthusiasm of his, who had read the maps and booked the rooms and who now, Martin knew, was simply waiting. Waiting for Martin to turn from the window, to say something, to be the centre of things again. The thought was not unkind, merely an observation, a fact of their friendship, as constant and as unremarkable as the weight of Leo’s pack or the particular way he brushed his hair from his forehead. For years, this had been the shape of their world: Martin speaking, Leo listening; Martin deciding, Leo agreeing. A comfortable arrangement, until now, until this strange, alpine silence had begun to feel like an accusation.

The zither had found its tune, a simple, looping melody that seemed to climb the wooden stairs and slip under their door. Down below, in the Stube, the lamplight would be coming on, glinting off the dark, polished wood of the tables, warming the red-cheeked faces of the farmers who came in for their beer. And she would be there. The thought of her was a sudden, sharp intake of breath. He had seen her first that afternoon as they came in from the path, her arms full of firewood, a stray piece of birch tucked behind her ear. She had not seen them. She had been looking at the wood, her brow furrowed in a small, serious concentration, her lips slightly parted. And in that moment, the entire valley, the mountains, the very purpose of their walk had seemed to contract, to narrow to the small, precise space she occupied. It was an absurdity, a madness. He felt it now, standing here in the gloaming, the feeling so large and so new it had no name, only a physical presence, a warmth that spread from his chest to his fingertips.

He turned from the window. “Shall we go down?” he said, and his voice sounded strange to him, too loud in the quiet room.

Leo was folding a sweater, his movements deliberate. He did not look up at once. “In a moment,” he said. “The floor is cold.” It was a simple statement, but Martin felt in it a resistance, a small, deliberate delay. He watched Leo’s hands, the pale, slender fingers smoothing a crease in the wool. He knew those hands as well as his own. He knew the faint scar on Leo’s left knuckle from a boyhood fall, the way the nails were always kept scrupulously clean. They were familiar, known, and in their familiarity was a kind of safety he had never thought to question. Now, he felt a flicker of impatience. “It’s warm enough downstairs,” he said. “There’s a fire.”

“Is there.” Leo placed the sweater in his bag and finally lifted his gaze. His eyes, in the dim light, were very dark. “You seem eager.”

The words hung between them. There was no accusation in them, not really, but Martin felt his own eagerness laid bare, a clumsy, naked thing. He felt the heat rise in his face. “I’m hungry,” he said, the lie thin and childish. “Aren’t you?”

Leo smiled, a small, tired curve of the lips. “Of course.” He stood up, brushing at his trousers. “Let us go, then.”

They went down the narrow, crooked stairs, the zither music growing louder, the smell of woodsmoke and roasting meat growing stronger with each step. The Stube was warm, golden, alive with the low murmur of German and the clink of glasses. And there she was, moving between the tables with a heavy pewter jug, her dark hair braided and pinned up, revealing the pale nape of her neck. She moved with a kind of unconscious grace, her attention on the task at hand—filling a glass, wiping a spill, nodding to a question from the landlord. She was a part of this place, as solid and essential as the stone walls or the fire in the hearth.

They found a table in the corner. Martin could not stop looking at her. He watched the way a stray curl escaped its pin and lay against her temple; the way she tucked it back with a quick, impatient gesture; the way she laughed at something a farmer said, a sound that was not for him, not for anyone, but simply a bubble of sound that rose and burst in the warm air. Every detail was a treasure, a secret he was hoarding. He felt a wild, possessive joy, a feeling so intense it was almost painful. He was aware of Leo sitting opposite him, a silent presence, sipping his beer and looking into the fire. He did not need to look at Leo to feel his gaze, a steady, patient weight.

“More bread, sir?” Her voice was low, with the soft burr of the local dialect. She stood beside their table, a basket on her hip.
