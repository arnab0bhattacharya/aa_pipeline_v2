It was in the dull, leaden heart of the South Atlantic, under a sky that seemed to press down upon the waters like a vast, unyielding weight, that the master of the *Celestia*, Captain Elias Royle, fell into the grip of a feverish malady. I remember the suddenness of it, the strange, quiet withdrawal of the man from the very deck he had trodden with such heavy, deliberate authority for years. One evening he was there, a brooding, corpulent presence under the stars, his voice grating out the usual courses; the next, he had retreated into the shadows of his cabin, leaving behind a silence that felt to us all like the hush of a physical catastrophe. The heat was immense, oppressive, clinging to the rigging and dripping from the spars in a heavy, unwholesome dew.

It fell to the first mate, a man named Henshaw, to assume the burden. Henshaw was a spare, sharp-featured man, with eyes of a pale, watery blue that seemed to look always at some distant, unyielding horizon of his own. He was not popular with the crew; he lacked Royle’s loud, bluff camaraderie, that rough jocularity which commands the affection of forecastle hands. But he possessed a seaman’s instinct of a finer, colder sort. He felt the weather in his bones, read the shifting of the barometer with a glance, and handled the great ship with a precise, silent efficiency that was almost uncanny. Under his watch, the *Celestia*, which had rolled and pitched sluggishly under Royle, began to slip through the water like a living thing released from a restraint. There was no shouting, no frantic piping; the sails were trimmed with a quiet certainty that left the men in a state of disciplined awe.

For three weeks we sailed thus, driving southward into the cold, grey bosom of the ocean. Royle lay in his cabin, hovering between life and death, his great frame shaking with the ague, his mind wandering in a haze of restless, incoherent fancies. The steward, a half-caste from Mauritius, would come out of the cabin door, shaking his head slowly, his dark face drawn with the anxiety of a man who watches a fire dying in a grate. Then, as we approached the latitude of the variable winds, Royle began to mend. The fever broke, leaving him weak, shrunken, with a strange, irritable perplexity in his sunken eyes. He would sit up in his bunk, listening to the creak of the spars, the wash of the sea alongside, as if he were hearing the sounds of another world.

One morning, Henshaw called me aside. We stood by the weather rail, looking out over the long, heaving swells that came rolling up from the south, their crests breaking in long, white lines against the dark water. The wind was light, uncertain, shifting in puffs that made the sails flap and shiver.

"He means to come on deck tomorrow," Henshaw said, his voice low and guarded. He did not look at me, but kept his eyes fixed upon the horizon, as though he expected something to rise from the edge of the sea. "The steward tells me he is dressing himself now. He has been trying on his coat. It hangs very loose upon him."

I made some commonplace remark about the master’s recovery, the natural pleasure of seeing a man restored to health. Henshaw turned his head slowly and regarded me with a long, inscrutable stare.

"He is not restored," he said, with a quiet emphasis that silenced me. "He is a man who has been very near to the other side, and he has come back with the shadow of it still upon him. He thinks he is the same Elias Royle. He thinks he will take this ship by the throat again, as he did. But he is not the same. The fever has eaten something out of him that he will not miss until he stands upon this deck and tries to give an order. And then—"

He stopped, and a slight, involuntary shudder passed through his lean frame. He turned away and walked aft, his hands clasped behind his back, his head bent. I watched him, feeling a dim, uneasy premonition of the conflict that was gathering, like a weather cloud, over the ship.

Royle appeared on deck the following afternoon. He came up the companion-ladder slowly, painfully, his hands gripping the brass rails with a tense, white-knuckled grip. He was wrapped in a heavy pilot coat, though the day was mild enough, and his face, beneath the shadow of his old weather-beaten cap, was the colour of old parchment, drawn and haggard, with a queer, restless light flickering in his deep-set eyes. He stood for a moment on the quarterdeck, blinking at the light, swaying slightly with the roll of the ship. The men forward paused in their work, looking aft at him with a curious, half-furtive regard.

Henshaw was at the wheel, speaking low to the helmsman. He turned as Royle approached, and for a long moment the two men stood facing each other, their eyes meeting in a silence that seemed to thicken the very air between them. It was a silence heavy with unspoken things—with the weight of command, the pride of a man who feels his authority slipping from him, the cold, clear perception of another who sees the naked truth and does not flinch from it.

"Good to see you up, Captain Royle," Henshaw said at last, his voice flat and colorless.

Royle drew a long, labored breath. "I am up," he said, thickly, as though the words were stones in his mouth. "I am up. And I will take the deck now, Mr. Henshaw. I will take her now."

There was a desperate, almost piteous note in his voice, the note of a man clinging to a spar in a dark sea. Henshaw did not move. He stood perfectly still, his pale eyes fixed upon the master’s face with a gaze that was at once compassionate and relentlessly firm.
