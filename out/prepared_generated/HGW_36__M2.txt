The specimen arrived in a crate of damp, rotting wood, smelling of rain and decay, smelling of that green, suffocating jungle that Mr. Arthur Pym had spent the last three years trying to map and measure. Pym, a man who had traded his youth for a compass and his sanity for a theodolite, set the crate down in the hallway of his old tutor’s house in Hampstead with the casual air of a man depositing a heavy parcel. He was gaunt, his skin the color of old parchment, and he looked at the plant as one looks at a recovered heirloom—possessively, and with a touch of fear.

It was a strange thing, this plant. It had no leaves in the traditional sense, but rather a series of fleshy, translucent lobes that pulsed with a slow, rhythmic motion, like the breathing of a sleeping beast. The roots were thick and white, tangled in a mat of black soil that seemed to shift and writh when not being watched. Pym called it *Sanguinaria insatiabilis*, a name he had coined in the field, for it was said to drink not water, but blood.

Professor Josiah Vane, who had taught Pym Latin and Greek until the boy was sent away to the ends of the earth, accepted the gift with a polite, if bewildered, smile. Vane was a small, precise man, a creature of routine and dry sherry, who lived in a house that was less a home than a museum of his own academic past. He had no family, only books and a housekeeper, Mrs. Gable, who watched over his life with the weary vigilance of a nurse tending a long-term patient.

“Put it in the conservatory,” Pym said, already turning to leave. “Keep it warm. It is delicate. It needs... attention.”

Vane did not argue. He was too tired to argue with anyone, least of all a man who had just returned from a continent where the sun seemed to have a different gravity. He had Pym’s coat buttoned by the door, watched him walk down the slope of the hill into the grey London fog, and then turned back to the crate.

For the first week, the plant was a curiosity. Vane watered it sparingly, misting the fleshy lobes with a spray of rose water, and watched it grow. It grew with a speed that defied botanical logic. The lobes elongated, turning a deeper shade of crimson, and the roots spread out across the floor of the terrarium, seeking purchase. Vane found himself drawn to it, spending hours in the conservatory, watching the subtle changes in its color, the way it seemed to lean toward the window, toward the light, or perhaps toward the sound of his own breathing.

Mrs. Gable noticed the change in the Professor. He was pale, his eyes hollow, his hands trembling. He was not eating his meals. He was talking to the plant.

“It is hungry, Mrs. Gable,” he said one evening, his voice thin and reedy. “Not for water. Not for light. It is hungry for something else.”

Mrs. Gable, who had served in three households and had seen many eccentricities, kept her face neutral. “And what is it, sir?”

Vane looked at her, his eyes wide with a fervor that bordered on madness. “It needs protein. It needs life. I have read of such things in the journals, but I had not believed it. It is a carnivore, Mrs. Gable. A true carnivore.”

He began to feed it. At first, it was only small insects, flies and moths that he caught in the garden. He placed them near the roots, and watched, with a mixture of scientific detachment and dawning horror, as the plant absorbed them. The lobes would expand, enveloping the prey, and then, over the course of an hour, they would contract, the insect gone, dissolved into the plant’s tissue. The plant grew larger, its color deepening to a bruised purple.

But the insects were not enough.

Vane’s health began to deteriorate. He became frail, his body wasting away as if the plant were drawing the life out of him through some invisible tether. He stopped going out. He stopped reading. He spent his days in the conservatory, sitting in a chair next to the terrarium, his hand resting on the glass. He spoke to it in Latin, in Greek, in his own halting, broken English. He called it his son, his teacher, his master.

Mrs. Gable, alarmed by the state of her employer, tried to intervene. She brought him broths and tonics. She suggested he see a doctor. But Vane would not listen. He was obsessed with the plant’s need. He had read that in the Amazon, the natives used the plant to trap and kill small mammals. He decided to try it himself.

One night, Mrs. Gable woke to a strange sound. It was a wet, squelching noise, coming from the direction of the conservatory. She crept down the hallway, her heart pounding in her ears. The door to the conservatory was ajar. She pushed it open, and what she saw stopped her breath.

The plant had grown beyond the bounds of its terrarium. Its roots had broken through the glass, spreading across the floor, wrapping around the legs of Vane’s chair. The lobes were no longer crimson; they were a dark, arterial red, glistening with moisture. And in the center of the plant, where the roots converged, was Professor Vane.

He was not dead. Not yet. His eyes were open, staring at the ceiling, but his body was partially submerged in the mass of roots. The plant had wrapped around his legs, its tendrils sinking into his flesh. He was making a sound, a low, moaning groan, but his lips were moving, forming words. Mrs. Gable leaned in, her face pale with terror.

“...more,” he whispered. “It wants... more.”
