The letter lay upon the table between us, its seal broken, its folds still sharp as though it had been opened but a moment before; and I remember that the flame of the taper wavered in the draught from the casement, casting upon the wall the shadows of my father and myself, huge and distorted, so that we seemed already the monstrous things which that night was to make of us. Without, the rain beat against the panes in gusts, and the wind, coming down from the fells, moaned among the chimneys of the parsonage like a spirit that had lost its way; but within there was no sound save the labouring of my own breath and the low, terrible quiet of my father's voice as he said, "Thou art not mine."

I had come home but an hour since, flushed with the triumph of my first prize at the college in Durham, my heart full of filial gratitude, my tongue framing the tender speeches I had prepared upon the road; and I found my father sitting in his study with a letter in his hand and a face so changed that I stood in the doorway as one transfixed. He was a man of small stature and gentle bearing, whose whole life had been a patient ministry among the poor of the valley; his hair was white, his features worn by study and by watching, and in his eyes there had ever dwelt a mild and steady light which I had loved above all things on earth. But when he raised those eyes to me that night, the light was gone out of them, and I beheld there only a desolation which I could not fathom.

"Not thine?" I cried. "Father, what wild words are these? Art thou not thyself, or am I dreaming? What letter is that which has thus undone thee?"

He rose and came towards me, and taking my hand in both his own—his hands, I mind me, were cold as the hands of the dead—he drew me to the window and looked long and earnestly upon my face, as though he would read there the confirmation of some dreadful truth. "I have deceived thee," he said at last, "not willingly, God knows, but because I was bidden to silence by one whose command I dared not disobey. Thou art the son of Lord Ashburton of Ravenshaugh, who lies now at the point of death, and who has sent for thee that he may acknowledge thee before he dies."

The room swam round me; the rain, the wind, the taper, the familiar books upon the shelves, all were confounded in a mist of horror. I heard my own voice asking, as though it belonged to another, "And my mother?" "She died," he answered, "when thou wert born; she was the daughter of a poor schoolmaster in the south, and she was betrayed. The Earl, for such he then was, would have provided for thee in secret; but I, who had loved thy mother as a brother might love a sister, I took thee from his hands when thou wert but a week old, and I swore that thou shouldst never know the shame of thy begetting. I have reared thee as my own; I have loved thee with a love passing the love of fathers; and now—now he claims thee, and I am to lose thee."

He turned away, and I saw his aged frame shaken with a grief which he strove in vain to master. O, what a tempest raged within me! I was a boy of twenty, proud, ambitious, full of the dreams of youth; and I had believed myself the son of a poor curate, and had loved my poverty and my father's lowly lot because they were his. Now, in a moment, the ground was rent beneath my feet; I was the child of shame, the heir of a great name, the object of a dying nobleman's tardy remorse. I thought of the Earl's pride, of his parks and his halls, of the obloquy which would attach to me, of the gentle father who had been all things to me, and I was torn as by wild horses. I flung myself at his feet; I clasped his knees; I wept, I entreated, I declared that I would never leave him, that no title, no wealth, no power of earth should tempt me from the roof beneath which I had been happy. But he only laid his hand upon my head and said, "Thou must go. It is his right; and it is thy duty. I have kept thee from him too long."

The morning came, and with it the carriage from Ravenshaugh, sent by the Earl's steward, with orders that I should be brought to the great house without delay. The day was wild and stormy, the sky of a leaden hue, the rain driving in sheets across the fells, and the wind so fierce that the horses could scarce make way against it. I sat beside my father in the carriage, for he would go with me, and we spoke little; but I held his hand, and I felt that it trembled. The road wound upward through a country of bleak and desolate grandeur, where the hills rose black against the tempest and the torrents came foaming down the gullies; and I looked upon it all with a strange, new sense of alienation, as though I had never seen it before. At length we came to the gates of Ravenshaugh, and passed up a long avenue of ancient oaks, whose branches, stripped and groaning in the wind, seemed to stretch out their arms towards me in warning or in menace. The house itself was vast and gloomy, built in the old time, with many windows and a great hall; and as we drew near, I saw that the blinds were drawn, and that a servant in mourning stood upon the steps.
