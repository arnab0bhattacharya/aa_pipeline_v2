# The Back Room

The fog had settled over Bloomsbury with a kind of patient malice, as though it intended to remain not for an hour or an evening but for all the years that remained to the century. It pressed against the glass of Trevorrow's bookshop in Bury Street with a thickness that was almost substance, so that the light from the single gas-lamp within seemed not to illuminate the room but merely to exist there, trapped and powerless, like a creature kept in a jar. Mrs. Trevorrow, standing behind the counter with an invoice in her hand, could see through the front window only the vague shapes of the opposite houses, their upper storeys wholly swallowed, and below, the faint yellow smear of the streetlamp on the cobbles, which flickered as though uncertain of its own existence.

She had lived above this shop for nineteen years and had grown into the habit of not knowing. It was not ignorance—she was not a stupid woman—but a kind of disciplined innocence, an innocence she had cultivated as one cultivates a difficult plant in an inhospitable climate. The back room, which lay beyond a narrow passage and a green baize door, was her husband's domain. She had always understood this. When the bell rang in the evening—sometimes once, sometimes twice, sometimes three times in quick succession—she did not go to the door. Her husband would emerge from behind his desk with that grave, attentive face he wore for visitors, and she would continue with whatever she was doing, examining an invoice, or marking a price in pencil on the flyleaf of a second-hand volume, and she would not look up. This was their arrangement, though it had never been spoken of as an arrangement, and she would have denied, with perfect sincerity, that any arrangement existed.

The men who came were foreign. She had gathered this much, though she had never spoken to any of them beyond the necessities of a polite greeting. Their clothes were often damp, their boots marked with the mud of the street, and they carried with them an air of haste and unease that she associated—though she would not have said so even to herself—with people who have no right to be at ease. There was one, a man with a fair beard and spectacles, who came almost every Thursday, and she had once heard his voice through the green baize door, a low, rapid, continuous sound, like water running over stones, and then her husband's voice, slower, with long pauses, as though each word were being weighed on a small and precise instrument. She had moved away from the door then, back to the counter, and had bent over her invoice with a concentration that was almost fierce.

She had sometimes wondered—in the way one wonders about things that do not properly concern one—what they discussed in that back room. Politics, she supposed. The politics of countries she could not locate on a map, the politics of people whose names she could not pronounce. Her husband had explained nothing. He was not a man given to explanations. He had come to London from somewhere in the north, from a family of booksellers, and he carried about him always the quiet self-containment of a man who has decided, early in life, that the world's business is not his business unless he chooses to make it so. He read in several languages. The shelves of the back room held volumes in Russian, in French, in some script she did not recognise—Polish, perhaps, or Czech, she could not say. She had entered the room only to clean it, on Monday mornings, when no visitors were expected, and even then she had moved through it quickly, dusting the table and the chairs and the small stove in the corner with the efficiency of a woman performing a task that has no meaning beyond itself.

It was on a Tuesday evening in November, the fog at its thickest, that the bell rang and two men entered whom she knew at once were not the usual sort. They wore heavy dark coats and their hats were pulled low, and one of them carried a walking-stick which he did not need, for he was young and straight-backed. They stood in the doorway, letting the fog in around them, and looked about the shop with a slow, appraising gaze. The one with the stick looked at her. His eyes were grey and very steady.

"Is Mr. Trevorrow within?" he said.

He spoke English perfectly, but with an accent she could not place. She felt, suddenly, the coldness of the evening, as though the fog had followed these men into the shop and wrapped itself around her ankles.

"He is in the back," she said. "I will tell him."

She moved towards the passage. But before she had reached the green baize door, it opened, and her husband stood there. He looked past her, at the two men, and she saw his face change. It was not fear, exactly—her husband was not a man who showed fear—but a kind of settling, as though something he had long expected had at last arrived, and he was not surprised but merely weary of its delay.

"Gentlemen," he said. He did not introduce his wife. He did not look at her. He stood aside, and the two men passed through the door, and it closed behind them, and she was alone in the shop with the fog pressing at the window and the gas-lamp hissing softly in its bracket.

She stood for a long moment. Then she did something she had never done in nineteen years. She crossed to the green baize door and listened.

The voices were low, indistinct. She could hear the man with the grey eyes speaking, a measured, unhurried tone, and then a silence, and then her husband's voice, and then another silence. She pressed her ear to the door. The baize was rough against her skin. She could make out words now—not all of them, but enough.
