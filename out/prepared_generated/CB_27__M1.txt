The rain of Blackmoor does not fall; it assails. It comes down in straight, grey lances, driving the smoke back down the chimneys and turning the cobbles of Mill Street into a slick, black river. I have known it to rain for a week without ceasing, a relentless, weeping sky over a landscape of soot and brick. In such weather, the town seems to shrink upon itself, its people scurrying from the damp of the streets to the infernal heat of the mills, their faces pale and drawn.

My dispensary is two rooms above a baker’s shop. It is a poor place, with a stove that smokes and a floor that has known the tread of many desperate feet. Yet it is my own. Here, I am not merely Mr. Ashworth, a physician of no great name, but a man who can bind a wound and ease a fever. Here, the truth of the town is laid bare before me, and its truth is written on the bodies of children.

They come to me with hands crushed by the machinery, with lungs that rasp like torn cloth, with eyes wide with a terror that is older than their years. Little Thomas Gable, aged nine, his fingers a mangled ruin. Mary Poole, twelve, her spine bent into a question mark by the long hours of stooping. I look at them, and I feel a fury rise in my breast, a righteous anger that is as hot as the furnace fires. This is not the work of Providence; it is the work of man’s greed. It is an injustice so profound, so utterly damnable, that my tongue cannot find words strong enough to condemn it.

It was this fury that led me to the great house on the hill, to the counting-house of Mr. Silas Croft. He is the master of the largest mill, a man whose wealth is built upon the broken backs of these children. I went not as a supplicant, but as a witness. I laid before him the ledger of my work: the litany of crushed limbs and consumptive coughs. I spoke of the Factory Act, of the law that was meant to protect them, a law his overseers treated as a mere suggestion.

He listened with the patience of a stone wall. He was a large man, with a face like a slab of pink granite and eyes as hard and cold as flint. When I had finished my impassioned plea, he did not raise his voice. He simply steepled his fat fingers and looked at me as one might look at a buzzing fly.

“Doctor,” he said, his voice a low rumble of condescension, “your trade is to mend what is broken. It is a noble trade. My trade is to spin cotton, to provide work for this town, to generate the wealth that allows you to have a trade at all. You must attend to your patients, and I will attend to my business. Do not presume to lecture me on the one while I do not presume to instruct you on the other.”

He spoke of profit and progress as if they were holy writ. He saw the children not as souls, but as cogs in his great, relentless machine. I felt my blood boil. “And when a cog is broken, sir, you simply throw it away and find another? Is that your Christian principle?”

A flicker of something—annoyance, perhaps—crossed his face. “Your sentiment does you credit, Doctor, but it does not fill the order books. The world is a hard place. It is not made for sentiment.” He rose then, a clear dismissal. “Good day to you.”

I left his presence feeling as though I had wrestled with a physical power and been thrown. The injustice of it was a bitter gall in my throat. I walked home through the driving rain, the wind tearing at my coat, and I felt the full weight of my own impotence. What was my anger against his money? What was my voice against the thunder of his looms?

That night, I sat in my dispensary, the single candle casting long, dancing shadows on the walls. The rain still lashed against the windowpane. I had no patient, only the ghosts of the day’s work: Thomas’s ruined hand, Mary’s twisted frame, Croft’s implacable face. I felt a despair so profound it was a physical cold. I was a fool, a sentimentalist tilting at windmills of brick and iron.

A soft knock came at the door. It was past ten o’clock, an hour when no honest person should be abroad. I opened it to find a young woman, cloaked and hooded against the storm. A gust of wind and rain swept in with her, and she pushed back her hood. It was Miss Croft, Eleanor. I had seen her once or twice at a distance, a quiet, grave creature who seemed to move through her father’s world like a shadow.

She was pale, her dark hair beaded with rain, but her eyes—her father’s eyes, yet softened by a light I could not name—were fixed on mine with an urgent intensity.

“Doctor Ashworth,” she said, her voice barely a whisper above the storm. “I could not rest. I know you saw my father today.”

I nodded, too astonished to speak.

“He is a hard man,” she continued, her gaze unwavering. “He sees only the ledgers. He does not see… what you see.” She hesitated, then reached into the folds of her cloak and drew out a small, heavy purse. She pressed it into my hand. The leather was cold and damp, but the weight of it was startling.

“What is this?” I managed to ask.

“It is for your dispensary,” she said. “For the children. For medicine, for food, for whatever you need. I have been saving.” A flush of colour rose in her cheeks, a secret fire in the gloom. “He would not approve. He believes charity weakens the spirit. But I believe it is the only thing that saves it.”
