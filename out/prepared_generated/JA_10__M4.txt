The lodgings in Clarence Parade were unexceptionable. They commanded a view of the Pump Room gardens, and if the prospect was somewhat circumscribed by the neighbouring terraces, it was at least genteel, and the rent, Mrs. Ellerby observed, was a great deal more reasonable than anything in Bath. To her daughter this was a matter of no small satisfaction; for though she had never been permitted to question the necessity of the journey, she had privately formed her own opinion of its causes, and was disposed to see economy where she could.

Mrs. Ellerby was not ill. That is to say, she had for some months past been pleased to describe herself as an invalid, in a tone which invited, without absolutely requiring, the sympathy of her acquaintance; and the symptoms she enumerated—a languor of a morning, a flutter upon the least exertion, and a want of appetite which never extended to the dinner table—were of that convenient order which may be indulged at a watering-place without exciting alarm. Miss Ellerby had attended her mother through two seasons at Weymouth with a patience that did her credit, and had come to Cheltenham with the hope of nothing more particular than that the waters might be as efficacious as they were reputed, and the company as unobjectionable.

They had been but four days in the town when Miss Ellerby began to observe that her mother's health admitted of a daily improvement which was almost too rapid to be flattering. The languor of a morning gave way, upon the appearance of the post, to an activity of a very different complexion; and the flutter upon exertion was never so pronounced as when a certain equipage was seen to draw up before the door of the house opposite. It was a handsome carriage, and the gentleman who descended from it was of an age to be past the vanities of youth without having yet entered upon the infirmities of age. He was tall, of a grave and pleasing countenance, and he walked with the air of a man accustomed to be received with deference.

“Mamma,” said Miss Ellerby, on the fifth morning, “is not that Mr. Delacourt, of whom we used to hear so much at Weymouth?”

Mrs. Ellerby looked up from her chocolate with an indifference that was a little too studied. “Mr. Delacourt? I dare say it may be. He has connexions in this part of the country, I believe. I am sure I have not thought of him these ten years.”

“I think you have thought of him these ten days,” returned her daughter, with a smile which was meant to disarm, and did not entirely fail of its purpose. “You have spoken of him twice at breakfast, and once you called me by the name of his sister.”

Mrs. Ellerby set down her cup. “You are very arch, Harriet. It is a great pity that a girl of your sense should have nothing better to do than to watch her mother. I am sure I have no design in coming to Cheltenham beyond my health, and if Mr. Delacourt should chance to be here, I hope I know what is due to civility.”

The civility in question was not long in being offered. The very next day, as they sat in the Pump Room, Mr. Delacourt approached with the ease of a man who had been waiting for his opportunity. He was a widower of some eight years, with a grown son in the army and a house in Gloucestershire which he was pleased to call his retirement. He addressed Mrs. Ellerby with a respect that was almost tender, and enquired after her health with a particularity that seemed to Miss Ellerby to argue a longer and more anxious acquaintance with its fluctuations than the mere exchange of morning calls could account for.

“You are improved, madam,” said he. “I was told you were an invalid, and I find you looking as you did—” He paused, and had the grace to colour. “I find you looking remarkably well.”

“The waters,” said Mrs. Ellerby, “have been of the greatest service. I am sure I am very much obliged to them. And to you, sir, for your enquiries.”

Miss Ellerby, seated at a little distance, with a glass of the waters in her hand, and the agreeable conviction that she was entirely forgotten, had leisure to make her observations. She saw that her mother's fan moved with a quickness that betrayed a fluttered mind; that her colour rose and fell; that she spoke of the weather, of the rooms, of the master of the ceremonies, with a volubility which was not her habit. She saw, too, that Mr. Delacourt listened to all this with an attention which no man of ordinary politeness would have bestowed upon the weather. And when at length he took his leave, with a hope of being permitted to wait upon them on the morrow, she saw her mother's hand tremble as it closed upon her arm.

“You are silent, Harriet,” said Mrs. Ellerby, when they had walked some paces in the gardens. “I hope you are not displeased.”

“Displeased, mamma? I am only surprised. I had no notion that Mr. Delacourt was known to you so—so particularly.”

“He was known to me very particularly indeed,” replied her mother, with a dignity that was meant to close the subject. Then, after a moment, and with a candour that was more affecting than the dignity: “He was known to me before I knew your father. There was a time when—but it is a great while ago, and I dare say he has forgotten it.”

Miss Ellerby was too well bred to say that she was certain he had not forgotten it. She was, indeed, too much astonished to say anything at all. Her mother—her prudent, managing, excellent mother, who had lectured her upon the folly of romance and the necessity of settling well—had been in love.
