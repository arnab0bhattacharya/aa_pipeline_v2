The wind outside the window of the small, draughty room in the Rue des Écoles did not merely blow; it screamed, a raw, piercing shriek that seemed to challenge the very foundations of the modest house where Mr. Arthur Penhaligon sat. It was November in Bruges, a city of canals and shadows, and the year 1843 was closing in with a ferocity that mirrored the turmoil in Arthur’s breast. He sat by the dying fire, his hands clasped tightly in his lap, the knuckles white, his eyes fixed on the grey, swirling mist that obscured the spires of the distant church. He was a young man of twenty-eight, lean and sharp-featured, with a face that bore the marks of a life spent in severe economy and intellectual labor. He was the headmaster’s assistant at the Collège Saint-André, a position of dignity but meager reward, sustained by the pride of a man who refused to beg and the love of a woman who had no property to offer.

The door opened without a knock, and in strode Monsieur Leclerc, the Director. He was a large, plush man, dressed in a velvet coat that seemed to absorb the little light in the room, his face ruddy with wine and authority. He did not sit. He paced, his heavy boots thudding against the stone floor, a sound that Arthur felt in his own bones.

"Arthur," Leclerc said, his voice thick with a false warmth that Arthur despised. "We have spoken. The board is satisfied with your methods. The students respect you. The parents are pleased. But respect is not enough to build a legacy. I have an offer for you. A promotion. The chair of Senior Latin. And with it, a house in the city, a salary that will allow you to live without the shadow of want hanging over your head."

Arthur looked up. "I am grateful, Monsieur, but I am content with my current duties."

"Content!" Leclerc laughed, a harsh, barking sound. "You are a fool, Arthur. You are a man of talent drowning in mediocrity. But I am a generous man. I do not offer this for your Latin, though it is excellent. I offer it for your character. I offer it because I see in you the makings of a pillar in this community. And I have a condition."

Arthur’s heart gave a single, violent throb. "A condition?"

"My niece, Madame Cecile. She is twenty-two, well-educated, and of a disposition that craves a guide rather than a master. She has been lonely, Arthur. Too lonely. And she has taken a liking to you. If you were to make her happy, to secure her future and your own, the position is yours. The house is yours. The respect of the city is yours. Is it not a fair exchange? Love for duty? Or rather, love for a future that is secure?"

The words hung in the air, thick and cloying. Arthur felt a surge of anger, hot and sudden, like a flame catching dry thatch. He thought of Elspeth, his Elspeth. She was a governess in a house across the river, a woman of quiet strength and fierce intelligence, whose father had been a clergyman of no means and whose mother had died leaving her with nothing but her mind and her pride. She wore dresses that were mended and remended, but her eyes, when they met his, held a fire that no amount of velvet or gold could extinguish. She was poor, yes. Poor in the eyes of men like Leclerc. But was she not rich in the only currency that mattered?

"Cecile is a good young lady," Arthur said, his voice low but steady. "I respect her. But my affection is not a commodity to be bartered for a salary increase."

Leclerc stopped pacing. He turned to face Arthur fully, his eyes narrowing. "You speak of honor, Arthur. But honor does not fill the belly. Honor does not pay the rent. You live in a garret. You eat bread and cheese. You shiver in this room. Is that honor? Or is it mere pride? And if it is pride, it is a dangerous thing. It will destroy you. Take the position. Marry my niece. You will be a man. You will be respected. You will be safe."

"I am already a man," Arthur replied, rising from his chair. He stood straighter, his gaze unwavering. "And I am safe in my conscience. If I were to accept your offer, I would be a beggar, Monsieur. I would be selling my soul for a warm hearth. And I would never be able to look Elspeth in the eye. And I would never be able to look myself in the mirror."

Leclerc’s face darkened. The false warmth vanished, replaced by a cold, calculating stare. "Elspeth. You speak of her as if she were a saint. She is a woman with no connections, no dowry, no future but the one you might give her. And you are a fool to think that your love is enough to sustain you both. The world does not run on love, Arthur. It runs on position, on money, on power. You are throwing away your life for a daydream."

"Let me throw it away," Arthur said, his voice rising, "for the truth. I would rather starve in dignity than feast in shame. I will not marry for a position. I will not sell my heart for a house. I am a teacher, Monsieur. I teach my students to seek truth and virtue. If I were to act contrary to those principles in my own life, what kind of teacher would I be? What kind of man?"

Leclerc stared at him for a long moment. The wind outside howled louder, rattling the windowpanes, a sound like the gnashing of teeth. Finally, the Director straightened his coat, a gesture of final dismissal.
