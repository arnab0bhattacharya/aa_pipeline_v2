The manor house at Beechcroft in Hampshire, though scarcely magnificent, possessed that exact degree of comfortable respectability which invites a wealthy neighbour to bore its inhabitants, and a poor curate to wish he might marry into it. Sir Arthur Wilmot, a widower of five years, was a man of so placid a disposition that he left the entire management of his domestic concerns, and the matrimonial prospects of his daughters, to the dictates of his elder child. Miss Wilmot, at eight-and-twenty, was neither plain nor handsome, but she possessed a calm assurance of her own good sense which served her almost as well as beauty, and considerably better than a large fortune.

Her younger sister, Clara, was just twenty, and possessed of that portion of beauty and liveliness which generally ensures a girl of two thousand pounds some degree of notice. She had lately attracted the admiration of two gentlemen, whose rival claims upon her attention formed the chief occupation of Miss Wilmot’s thoughts, and the chief amusement of Clara’s.

The first of these suitors was Mr. Percy, of Grange Park, a man of four thousand a year and a very tedious turn of mind. He had come into Hampshire but a twelvemonth ago, and had already found fault with the roads, the soil, and the manners of the natives, without discovering any intention of removing to a county more worthy of his approval. His attention to Clara had been steady rather than ardent. He drove over in his phaeton two or three times a week, sat for half an hour without saying anything of the smallest consequence, and went away again, leaving the impression that he considered himself to have performed a very handsome duty. His wealth, however, and the fact that he had no near relatives to be embarrassed by his conversation, gave him a certain claim to attention which Miss Wilmot was not disposed to undervalue.

The second was Mr. Henshawe, the curate of the adjoining parish, a man of very small income and very considerable abilities. He had been introduced to the Wilmot family by a common acquaintance, and had soon established himself in Sir Arthur’s good graces by his readiness to discuss the state of the turnips, and in Clara’s by his willingness to discuss almost anything else. He was lively in conversation, quick in understanding, and poor enough to be an object of decided disapprobation to Miss Wilmot, who considered a curate with less than three hundred a year as a species of gentleman whom no young woman of prudence could look at without alarm.

It was not, however, the mere fact of Mr. Henshawe’s poverty which had fixed Miss Wilmot’s resolve against him. She had a further reason, which she did not avow, but which was perhaps the more powerful for being unspoken. Miss Wilmot had no suitors of her own. She had passed the age when young women are commonly supposed to attract them, and she had begun to feel the want of that importance and amusement which an offer of marriage, even a rejected one, might have conferred. It was therefore not entirely disagreeable to her to find herself the confidante of her sister’s romantic difficulties, and to exercise, in the giving of advice, a species of authority which she could not otherwise have enjoyed. She had every intention of exercising it in favour of Mr. Percy, whose fortune would provide for Clara in a manner becoming their birth, and whose dullness would ensure that Miss Wilmot’s counsel continued to be sought long after the wedding.

One morning, a few days after Mr. Percy had called and sat in his usual manner, expressing nothing but a faint hope that the weather might continue "tolerable," Miss Wilmot took the opportunity of a tete-a-tete to open the subject.

"My dear Clara," said she, folding her work with an air of composed reflection, "I have been thinking very seriously of Mr. Percy. He is a man of very superior fortune, and I believe his attentions to you have been such as to warrant a belief that his intentions are serious. I cannot but think that you would do very wisely to encourage him."

"I do not discourage him," said Clara. "He sits on the sofa, and I sit on the chair, and we are both exceedingly silent. I do not see how I could encourage him more without doing something violent."

"You are too lively, Clara. A man of Mr. Percy’s consequence expects a more composed manner in the woman he may honour with his choice. He has been used, I dare say, to women of greater steadiness."

"I dare say he has been used to women of greater sleepiness. But tell me, Margaret, why do you so particularly wish me to encourage Mr. Percy, when you so particularly dislike Mr. Henshawe?"

"Mr. Henshawe is a curate with a hundred a year, and a great deal too much to say for himself. I need say nothing further."

"You have said nothing further for a month," returned Clara, smiling; "but you have said the same thing so often that I have had leisure to consider it, and I confess I am not convinced. Mr. Henshawe’s conversation is at least entertaining."

"Entertaining conversation is the very last thing a married woman has any occasion for. She wants a sober, steady husband, who will not disturb her domestic arrangements by being clever."

Clara, who had great affection for her sister, and a tolerable share of penetration into her motives, heard this speech with a degree of amusement which she did not betray. She had begun to suspect that Miss Wilmot’s advice was not entirely disinterested. The earnestness with which that lady pressed Mr. Percy’s claims, and the uneasiness she betrayed whenever Mr. Henshawe’s name was mentioned, suggested a stronger motive than mere fraternal concern.
