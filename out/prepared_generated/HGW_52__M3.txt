It was upon the third day of the great Coal Famine of 1912 that the people of Swatheley, a sprawling manufacturing borough in the Midlands, completely lost their patience with the elaborate system of supply and demand. The colossal engines at the textile mills had long since ceased their clattering; the tall chimneys that ordinarily vomited a steady plague of black smoke into the grey November sky were now cold and empty, standing up like the fingers of dead giants pointing at the sky. The electric trams stood motionless in their tracks, locked in a paralysis that had spread to the entire region. The gasworks was barely functioning, and the streets after dusk were dark, save for the uneasy flickering of candles and oil-lamps behind the drawn blinds of the middle-class houses.

The immediate cause of this paralysis was a bureaucratic muddle in the distribution of the national coal stocks, aggravated by a sudden, unseasonal frost. But the focal point of the town’s fury was a stout, red-faced man named Mr. Bostock, who kept the largest coal yard in the district. Mr. Bostock was a man of immense practical foresight, a trait he had hitherto considered synonymous with commercial genius. Anticipating a shortage, he had quietly bought up several barge-loads of household and steam coal, storing them in his great iron sheds behind the railway siding. He possessed, perhaps, two thousand tons.

It was common knowledge that he had this coal. In a town where industrial secrets are non-existent, such a hoard could not be kept quiet. The local branch of the Municipal Council, panic-stricken and shivering, had offered him four pounds a ton, a sum that would have made him independently wealthy for the rest of his days. But Mr. Bostock, with the stubborn stupidity of the narrow-minded accumulator, had refused. He held out for five pounds, arguing that the severity of the winter would inevitably drive the price to six. He sat in his office over the yard, wearing a heavy ulster coat, and marvelled at his own financial acumen.

The crowd gathered in the cinder-strewn lane outside his yard shortly after noon. They were a rough, determined-looking crowd, composed largely of men from the foundries and the nail-mills, thrown out of work by the frost and the famine. Their faces were grimed with the cold grime of unheated houses; their hands were deep in their pockets, not so much from custom as to keep their fingers from stiffening in the biting wind. They did not shout or yell at first; they merely stood in a dense, dark mass, looking at the high corrugated-iron gates of the yard with a silent, calculating intensity. It was the silence of men who had not yet quite decided whether to beg or to break.

Presently a deputation of three pushed open the small side door. They found Mr. Bostock standing by his desk, a formidable figure in his greatcoat, his face flushed with a mixture of defiance and a very natural apprehension.

“Well, what is it?” said Mr. Bostock, trying to speak in his ordinary, bullying commercial voice. “You can’t come in here. I’ve told the Council my price. I want five pounds a ton, and not a penny less.”

The spokesman was a big, gaunt man with a ragged red scarf around his neck. He spoke with a slow, deliberate Midlands accent.

“We ain’t come to buy, Mr. Bostock,” he said. “We’ve come to tell you that our kids are shivering, and there’s no fire in the grates. We want a ton or two, just to keep the chill off, till the Government sends relief.”

“And I suppose you want it for nothing?” sneered Mr. Bostock, his hands trembling slightly as he fumbled with a ruler on the desk. “This is my property. I bought it, fair and square. If you want coal, you must pay the market price. I am not a charitable institution.”

“You are a thief, I’m thinking,” said another of the deputation, a young man with a smudge of engine-oil on his cheek. “A thief and a hoarder.”

The word “hoarder” seemed to sting Mr. Bostock to the quick. He brought his fist down on the desk with a bang that made the ink-glass rattle. “Get out of my office!” he roared, losing his temper completely. “Get out before I set the dogs on you! I know my rights. A man’s property is his own. I’ll let the coal rot where it is before I give a handful to a parcel of lazy, unreasonable—”

He got no further. The young man with the smudged face sprang forward. There was a sudden, violent scuffle. Mr. Bostock was a heavy man, but he was no match for the young foundryman. The ruler flew across the room, and the next moment Mr. Bostock was lying on the oilcloth, with his arms pinned to his sides, while a knot of men from the yard outside, bursting through the door, trampled over him to get at the shed.

“Hold him!” cried the young man. “Hold the silly old fool! Don’t hurt him more than you can help, but hold him!”

The noise was terrific. The crowd outside, hearing the commotion, surged against the gates, and the padlock gave way with a snap. Into the yard they poured, a stream of desperate, angry men, swarming over the great black heaps, filling sacks, barrows, buckets, and even their caps and handkerchiefs with the precious dust and slack. The iron sheds were broken open. In ten minutes, the two thousand tons of Mr. Bostock’s commercial foresight were being trundled away in every conceivable kind of conveyance.

Mr. Bostock, pinned to the floor of his office, could hear the roar of triumph and the scraping of shovels. He raved and swore, and struggled impotently, his face purple with rage and mortification. His commercial acumen had brought him to this.

It was while he was in this unenviable posture that the door opened again, and his daughter walked in.
