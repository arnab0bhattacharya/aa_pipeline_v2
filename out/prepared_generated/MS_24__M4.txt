The candle had burned to its socket, and the first grey light of a Gloucestershire morning, creeping through the tall windows of the library, found me still seated at the great oak table where my father’s papers lay heaped in profusion. I had come to Ravensworth Hall but three days since, a soldier newly returned from the bloody fields of Spain, my left arm still bound in its sling, my heart full of that sweet impatience which attends the near approach of nuptials long delayed by war. Amelia was to arrive within the fortnight; the banns were to be read; and I, Edmund Ashcombe, the last of a name I had believed honourable, was to take to my bosom the woman whose purity of soul I revered above all earthly things. Yet now, as I sat amidst the relics of my father’s industry, a horror had descended upon me which I could neither shake off nor comprehend.

It was in the third iron-bound chest, beneath a mass of bills of lading and letters of credit, that I discovered the truth. The name of the ship was the *Deliverance*—a bitter irony that struck me even then—and her cargo was described in terms so cold, so methodical, that I could scarce believe the hand which penned them was that of the man whose knee had dandled me in infancy, whose voice had taught me prayers, whose blessing had sent me forth to battle. “Negroes, prime and sound, three hundred and forty,” ran the entry; and again, in another hand, “sold at Kingston for eighty pounds per head.” I read, and re-read, and the letters swam before my eyes; and a sickness, deeper than any I had known amidst the carnage of Badajoz, rose in my throat and choked me.

I started from my chair, and paced the long room with frantic steps. The portraits of my ancestors frowned upon me from the walls—my grandfather, stern and purse-proud; my father, mild-eyed and smiling, as I remembered him in boyhood. Was it for this that he had amassed the fortune which had purchased my commission, which had repaired the crumbling battlements of Ravensworth, which was to dower my bride and rear my children in affluence? The very roof above my head seemed suddenly accursed; the air I breathed was tainted with the groans of an oppressed and suffering race; and I, the inheritor of this guilt, stood paralysed, as one who awakes from a pleasant dream to find himself upon the brink of a precipice.

Amelia! The thought of her was as a sword in my bosom. Amelia Wentworth, whose father had sat in Parliament with Wilberforce, whose whole life was a hymn to mercy, whose delicate cheek would flush with holy indignation at the mere recital of a tale of cruelty. She had spoken to me, on the eve of my departure, of the cause she loved; and I, thoughtless and vain, had answered with vague words of general benevolence, never dreaming that the gold which glittered on my uniform was the price of human misery. How could I stand before her, and say, “The hand you clasp in love is stained with the blood of Africa”? How could I lead her to this altar, and swear before God to cherish her, while the very stones of the church were laid upon a foundation of iniquity?

The day passed in an agony which I can scarce describe. I walked in the park, and the ancient oaks, the tranquil deer, the very sunlight upon the grass, seemed to mock my wretchedness. I rode to the village, and the sight of the blacksmith’s forge, the children at play, the parson at his gate, filled me with a loathing of my own existence. I was an outcast, a leper, though no man knew it; and the secret burned within me like a fire unquenchable.

Towards evening, I resolved to write to Amelia. I would tell her all; I would renounce her; I would renounce the fortune; I would restore to the last farthing the wealth that had been wrung from the sweat and tears of the wretched. I would go forth a beggar, but an honest man. I seized my pen; but my hand trembled so that I could not form the letters. “Dearest Amelia,” I began, and the words seemed a mockery. I tore the paper, and cast it from me.

At length, as the sun descended in a flood of crimson behind the distant hills, I heard the sound of carriage-wheels upon the gravel. My heart stood still. It was too early for her coming—and yet—I rushed to the window. A travelling chaise, dusty and travel-stained, drew up before the door; and from it descended Amelia herself, attended by her maid. She had come, she said, when she saw me, in haste, because she could not endure the separation; she had prevailed upon her father to permit her to anticipate the day; she had thought only of my happiness, and of the joy of our reunion.

She stood before me in the twilight, her fair hair dishevelled by the wind, her eyes beaming with tenderness, her whole form breathing that angelic purity which had first won my soul. And I—I who had been prepared to renounce her—I could not speak. I sank upon my knee, and hid my face in my hands, and wept like a child.

“Edmund, my Edmund,” she cried, in alarm, “what ails you? Are you ill? Has the wound broken forth again? Speak to me, I implore you!”

I raised my head, and looked upon her sweet countenance, and I knew that I could not deceive her. “Amelia,” I said, in a voice which I scarce recognised as my own, “I am not worthy to touch the hem of your garment. I have discovered that my father—my honoured father, whose memory I have revered—was a dealer in slaves. The fortune which was to have been your dowry is the price of blood. I am the inheritor of a curse; and I must renounce you, and all that I possess, and go forth into the world to expiate, if I may, the guilt of my race.”
