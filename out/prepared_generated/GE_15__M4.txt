The discovery came upon Mrs. Farrant in the second week of October, and it came, as such discoveries generally do, not by the front door of confession but through the back passages of gossip and inference. She had gone to the shop of Mr. Byatt, the linen-draper, to match a ribbon for the trimming of a winter bonnet—an errand of no particular moment, though she carried it out with that air of settled authority which a woman of her means and her opinions learns to wear as naturally as her shawl. Mr. Byatt, a small, obsequious man with a habit of rubbing his hands as though he were perpetually washing them of some petty complicity, had produced his box of ribbons and was spreading them under her inspection, when he observed, with the air of one who offers a piece of news as a garnish to his wares, that he understood Mr. Farrant was to be congratulated.

“Congratulated?” said Mrs. Farrant, her fingers pausing upon a length of dove-coloured satin. “Upon what, pray?”

Mr. Byatt’s hands went through their ritual. “Why, madam, upon his marriage—so I am told. To Miss Trewin, of the chapel in Cottisloe. A very respectable young person, I am sure, and the daughter of the butcher there—a man of substance, they say, though I have not the honour of his acquaintance. But perhaps I speak before the news has reached you. I am sure I beg your pardon if I have been precipitate.”

Mrs. Farrant laid down the ribbon. Her face, which was of that fine, hard cast that age rather dignifies than softens, did not change expression, but her fingers had grown cold, and she was conscious of a curious sensation in her chest, as though a hand had closed upon her heart and were squeezing it slowly, not with violence but with a dreadful patience. She said, in a voice that sounded to her own ears as though it came from another woman standing at a little distance, “You are misinformed, Mr. Byatt. My son is not married.” And then, because the lie was intolerable even to herself, she added, “That is to say, I have heard nothing of it. You will oblige me by not repeating the report until it is confirmed.”

She left the shop without purchasing the ribbon, and walked home through the streets of Wexford—for so the town was called—with her head erect and her eyes fixed upon some point in the middle distance, seeing nothing of the familiar shopfronts, the loungers at the corner, the carrier’s cart unloading at the inn yard. The town, which she had known for five-and-twenty years, had suddenly become strange to her, peopled with whisperers. She felt, in that moment, the peculiar isolation of the woman who has staked her whole existence upon a single affection and finds that affection has been trafficked behind her back.

Her son, Edward, was her only child. Her husband had died when the boy was seven, leaving her with a modest competence and a large and unappeasable grief, and she had devoted herself to Edward with that concentrated ardour which is sometimes the product of a narrow nature and sometimes, as in her case, of a wide one that has been forced into a narrow channel. She had educated him above his station—Latin, mathematics, the rudiments of philosophy—and had denied herself, not ostentatiously but with a quiet and inflexible persistence, that he might go to the grammar school and afterwards to a solicitor’s office in Birmingham, where he was now in his third year of articles. She had written him weekly letters full of counsel and solicitude; she had kept his room aired and his books dusted; she had, in short, made him the centre of a life that had no other centre, and had believed—how could she not?—that she was the centre of his.

And now he had married a butcher’s daughter from the chapel at Cottisloe. The chapel! Mrs. Farrant was a churchwoman of the old school, not devout after the manner of the evangelicals, but attached to the Establishment with a deep and unreflecting loyalty, as to a great oak whose roots go down into the foundations of the land. The chapel, to her mind, was a place of heat and enthusiasm, where persons of no education presumed to interpret Scripture for themselves and to address the Almighty with a familiarity that savoured of presumption. That her son—her Edward, who had been bred to the Church catechism and had knelt beside her at the altar of St. Botolph’s—should have allied himself with such people was a wound in that tenderest part of her which we call, for want of a better word, her pride; but it was not the deepest wound. The deepest was that he had not told her.

She waited three days. On the fourth, a letter came from Birmingham, and she knew before she broke the seal that it was the confession. Edward wrote with the awkwardness of a man who has nerved himself to a distasteful duty and is determined to perform it thoroughly. He had married, he said, on the twenty-first of September, Miss Louisa Trewin, of Cottisloe, and he trusted that his mother would receive her as a daughter. He had not written before because he had feared—he used the word “feared,” and the word smote her—that she would not approve, and he had wished to be able to present the matter to her as an accomplished fact, in the hope that time and reflection might reconcile her to it. Louisa was everything that was amiable and good; she had no fortune, but she had a heart; and he was sure that his mother, when she knew her, would love her as he did. He would bring her to Wexford on the Saturday following, and he begged his mother to give them her blessing.
