In the village of Kingley Thorpe, where the reddish soil of Leicestershire turned to a stubborn clay under the autumn rains, it was generally held that John Borthwick’s election to the office of overseer of the poor was a just recognition of his substantial character. He was a man whose broad shoulders and square, weather-tanned face seemed to argue a natural fitness for authority, as if Providence had designed him to carry the burdens of the parish as easily as he carried a sack of wheat. But Nature, while she had been generous to his frame and had endowed him with a shrewd, slow-moving judgment in all matters of husbandry, had left a singular gap in his equipment: the letters of the alphabet were to him but a confusing swarm of black insects, and the arithmetic of the ledger was a sealed book. He had managed his own acres by a memory that never faltered, balancing seed-time against harvest in the iron safe of his brain; but the parish accounts, with their double-entry demands and formal summonses, were a very different matter. It is one of the bitterest ironies of our social arrangements that the men most trusted by their neighbours for solid honesty are often those least furnished by education to deal with the treacherous abstractions of pen and ink.

John, however, was not a man to confess a deficiency, and he accepted the office at the Easter vestry with a gravity that concealed a deep, unacknowledged anxiety. He walked home to Stoneleigh Farm with a heavier step than usual, the great ledger of the poor rate, bound in greasy brown leather, tucked under his arm like an unwelcome infant. His daughter Susan, a young woman of three-and-twenty, whose clear grey eyes and quiet mouth bespoke a disciplined intelligence, saw at once the shadow on his brow. She had taken her mother’s place in the household since that mother’s death five years before, and she had learned to read the signs of her father’s mind as accurately as she read the signs of the weather.

“Tha’t looken main heavy, Father,” she said, as she set down a basin of broth before him. “Is it th’ new office, or has th’ red cow gone sickly again?”

“Nay, th’ cow’s well enough,” said John, resting his elbows on the table and gazing at the ledger as if it were some venomous reptile. “It’s this book, Sue. I’m o’erseeer now, and I mun make out th’ rates and pay ’em out, and sign my name to th’ magistrates. And I canna so much as tell if a figure’s a five or a nine. Old Grimshaw, as had it afore me, he allus said it were a simple matter o’ addin’ and subtractin’, but when I look at his writin’ here, it’s like a spider’s web i’ th’ dew. I mun get th’ schoolmaster to do it, I reckon.”

Susan drew the ledger toward her and turned a few pages. The writing was cramped and blotted, the figures straggling up and down the margins like drunken men. She had been taught well by the vicar’s sister, and the page was to her an open confession of the writer’s habits. She saw at a glance that the entries were irregular, the sums often untotalled.

“Let me do it for thee, Father,” she said quietly. “I can write a fair hand, and I know my figures. Thee canst tell me who’s to have what, and I’ll put it down, and then thee canst sign it, and we’ll ha’ done wi’ th’ schoolmaster and his charges.”

John looked at her with a relief that he tried to hide under a gruff manner, for he was a man who thought it unseemly to lean upon a woman, even his own daughter. “Well, Sue, I dunna know as it’s a man’s work for a lass, but if thee’st mind to try, I’ll be main thankful. But thee munna tell nobody as I canna read it mysen. I’d be shamed afore th’ parish.”

So Susan Borthwick took the overseer’s book into her own hands, and in the quiet evenings, by the light of a tallow candle, she began to untangle the web that old Grimshaw had left behind. She was a methodical soul, with a turn for accuracy that amounted to a passion, and she found a stubborn pleasure in reducing the chaos of the old entries to clean columns of black and red. But as she worked, she became aware of certain discrepancies that were not merely the result of carelessness. The poor rate, collected from the farmers and cottagers according to a fixed assessment, should have yielded a predictable sum; yet the totals recorded by Grimshaw fell short, year after year, by amounts that, though small in themselves, accumulated to a startling total. Worse, the payments to the paupers—old Nell Foss, and the widow Lambe, and the crippled soldier from the wars—were entered at figures she knew, from her father’s own lips, to be less than what they had actually received. She said nothing for a week, turning the matter in her mind with the cautious deliberation of her breed, weighing the possibility of error against the darker possibility of fraud. She did not wish to accuse a neighbour unjustly, and she was aware that the moral leap from a muddled ledger to a deliberate theft is one that a generous mind should be slow to make.

At last, one Sunday evening, when the farm was still and the church bells had died away over the fields, she laid the book before her father and pointed to a page. “Father,” she said, “look here. This is th’ entry for th’ Lady-day quarter, three year gone. Old Grimshaw has put down that he paid Nell Foss twelve shillin’, but thee know’st thou gav’st her th’ money thysen, for she came to th’ door, and it was but eight. And here, where he’s charged th’ parish for coal for th’ workhouse, it’s nigh on two pound more than what Molly pays now. I’ve gone through it all, and it’s th’ same i’ every quarter. He’s been takin’ th’ money hissen, little by little, thinkin’ none would be th’ wiser.”
