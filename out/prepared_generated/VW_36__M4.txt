The hotel stood, white and angular, against a sky that was itself a kind of mountain, so that one could not tell, in the afternoon, where the snow ended and the clouds began; and the light came down through that vast pale space with a strange, watery brilliance, laying a thin gilt upon the balconies, upon the wicker chairs, upon the pages of the book that Arthur had let fall upon his knee. He was supposed to be resting. Rest, the doctors had said—rest, and the air, and the slow climbing of paths that led nowhere in particular; and he had come, obedient as a parcel, to this place of invalids and their relations, where the days were measured out in glasses of milk and the ringing of a little bell, and where the only event was the passage of the sun from one shoulder of the mountain to the other. He was better; he knew he was better; and yet the knowledge lay in him like a stone, heavy and without warmth, for what was the use of growing strong again in a world where everything was so exceedingly far away?

It was then that he noticed her—or rather, it was then that he began to notice her, for she had been there all the time, a young woman who sat at the end of the terrace with her back to the hotel and her face to the valley, and who was not, he had been told, an invalid at all. She had come, the porter said, to be quiet; her people were in Lucerne; there had been some difficulty, some unpleasantness, and she had taken a room and a chair and a view, and there she sat, hour after hour, with a rug over her knees though the sun was hot, looking out. She was not pretty, precisely—her nose was too decided, her mouth too large—but she had a way of turning her head slowly, as though the air itself were something to be tasted, and of letting her hand lie palm upward on the arm of the chair, as if she were waiting for something to fall into it. Arthur watched her from behind his book. He was ashamed of watching; he was a young man of excellent principles and poor health, and he had been brought up to believe that to stare at a stranger was a form of theft. But the book would not hold him. The sentences ran together and dissolved; and at last, one afternoon, when the terrace was empty and the light had gone the colour of pale tea, he rose, with the uncertain dignity of the convalescent, and walked to the balustrade a few paces from her chair, and stood there looking at the valley as though that had been his purpose all along.

“You are not an invalid,” she said. It was not a question. Her voice was low and rather dry, like the sound of a match being struck and failing to catch.

“No,” he said. “That is—I am supposed to be getting well. It comes to the same thing, I dare say.”

“It does not come to the same thing at all,” she replied, and turned her head to look at him, and he saw that her eyes were grey and very direct, and that there was in them a kind of impatience, a hunger, which had nothing to do with illness. “To be ill is to be excused. To be getting well is to be expected to do something, and to have forgotten what.”

He laughed—a short, surprised laugh, the first he had laughed, it seemed to him, since the spring—and sat down on the balustrade, his hands in his pockets, his heart beating in that hurried, shallow way which told him he was alive. “You have put your finger on it,” he said. “I have been lying in my chair for a fortnight trying to remember what it was I used to do.”

“And have you remembered?”

“I used to walk,” he said. “I used to walk a great deal, and think about—oh, about all sorts of things. And now I walk a little way up the path and sit on a bench and think about my digestion. It is degrading.”

She smiled, and the smile changed her face altogether, made it young and rather reckless. “I came here,” she said, “to escape my family. That is the whole of my story. They wished me to marry a man with a red face and a great many opinions, and I wished to sit in the sun and read French novels and be quiet; and so I said I was tired, and they sent me here, and here I am, and I am not tired at all. I am furious. I am so furious that some days I could throw the rug over the balustrade and run down the mountain and never come back.” She stopped, and looked at him with a kind of defiance, as though she expected him to be shocked, and then, seeing that he was not shocked but only attentive, she added, more quietly, “But I do not run. I sit. And the mountain goes on being beautiful, which is the worst of it.”

“Why the worst?”

“Because it makes one’s own agitation seem so small and so—so badly arranged,” she said. “If the mountain were ugly I could be angry with it. As it is, I have nothing to be angry with but myself, and I am not at all sure that I am in the right.”

The light was going now; the snow on the highest ridge had turned the colour of a peach, and the valley was filling with a blue that was almost black. Somewhere below them a cowbell sounded, and then another, and then a man’s voice, faint and long-drawn, calling the cattle home. Arthur sat quite still.
