The steam-launch *Sonderbund*, drawing a paltry nine inches of water, crept up the Matarre under a sky of burnished brass. The heat was an oppression; it lay upon the water like a heavy, invisible vapor, thickening the air into a substance that entered the lungs with a labored, conscious effort. The banks, low and clogged with the roots of mangroves, slipped past in a silent, unbroken monotony of dark slime and brilliant, venomous green. There was no stir of life, save for the occasional, violent agitation of the surface when some monstrous river-thing rolled in the depths, sending a shudder through the stillness that was less a sound than a pulse in the thick air.

I sat in the stern-sheets, a chart spread upon my knees, a pencil in my hand, and the weight of an immense, unutterable fraud pressing upon my spirit. The chart was the work of a man named Reitz, a surveyor who had preceded me in the service of the West African Concession Company. Reitz was dead; he had died of fever in some obscure creek to the south, but he had bequeathed to the Company, and to Europe, a map of the Matarre. It was a beautiful piece of cartography, clear, precise, and detailed. It showed the river’s course, its sandbanks, its sudden turnings, and, above all, it marked, with a pleasing regularity, the sites of populous villages.

These villages were the foundation of the Company’s wealth. The directors in Antwerp had issued shares upon the strength of Reitz’s map, shares bought by men who trusted in the report of a bustling trade in rubber and ivory, a trade that was to be wrested from the natives by the mere force of European enterprise. The villages were there, on paper, waiting to be taxed, to be traded with, to be exploited. They were a reality to the directors, to the shareholders, to the financial world. And as I looked up from the chart to the silent, desolate banks, I realized that they were a reality to no one else.

We had been ascending the river for eleven days. I had marked the twisting channel, the mudbanks, the fallen trees with the care of a man who knows that upon his survey depends the safety of the Company’s vessels. But I had not seen a village. I had not seen a hut. I had not seen a native. The only sign of human life had been a few blackened stakes in the mud, the remnants of some fishing-stage, so old and decayed that the water had eaten them half away. I had inquired of my native crew—men from the coast, who knew nothing of this region—but they only shrugged their shoulders, their faces blank, their eyes shifting. They knew nothing, or they would not say.

The silence of the river was a thing of menace. It was a silence that seemed to hold its breath, that watched, that waited. The heat, the damp, the foul smell of the mud, the interminable green wall of the forest, all combined to produce an impression of a world withdrawn, hostile, indifferent. And into this world I had come to map villages that did not exist.

It was on the evening of the eleventh day that we came to the place where Reitz’s chart marked the largest village of all. It was called, on the map, “Kwamma.” It was marked with a double square, indicating a place of importance, a center of trade. The chart showed it on the right bank, on a slight elevation, surrounded by cultivated fields. I looked at the right bank. There was no elevation. There was no village. There was only the same low, malarial swamp, the same tangled roots, the same green wall of silent forest.

I folded the chart and put it in my pocket. The engineer, a man named Schomberg, who was leaning against the engine-room hatch, looked at me with his dull, bloodshot eyes. He was a heavy man, with a pendulous under-lip, and he sweated profusely. He had not spoken a word in three days, except to give orders to his black stoker.

“Well, sir?” he said, his voice thick, as though his tongue were too large for his mouth.

I did not answer at once. I was looking at the spot where Kwamma should have been. The launch’s wake spread out behind us, a widening circle of yellow scum, and then the water closed over it, and there was no trace that we had passed. The forest seemed to lean over us, dark, brooding, impenetrable.

“There is no village,” I said.

Schomberg did not appear surprised. He wiped his face with a filthy rag, and his small eyes, buried in fat, blinked slowly.

“No,” he said. “I didn’t think there would be.”

“You knew?” I asked.

“I’ve been on this river before,” he said. “I was with Reitz.”

The statement, coming so quietly, so unexpectedly, startled me. I had assumed that Schomberg was a new hand, a man hired for this expedition. I had not known that he had been Reitz’s engineer.

“You were with Reitz?” I repeated.

He nodded, and his face, gross and impassive, betrayed nothing.

“Then you know that these villages do not exist.”

He looked at me, and for an instant, I thought I saw a flicker of something in his eyes—irony, comprehension, or perhaps merely the reflection of the sun on the water.

“Reitz was a man of imagination,” he said. “He had a wife in Europe. A woman who needed money. The Company wanted a map that would satisfy the shareholders. Reitz gave them what they wanted. What was the difference? The Company would have invented the villages if Reitz had not. They needed them to exist. So they existed.”
