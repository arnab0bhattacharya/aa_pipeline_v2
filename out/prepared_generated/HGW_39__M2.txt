The tonic was called *Vigorite*, and it was, by any honest chemical standard, a rather expensive shade of brown. It tasted of sarsaparilla, quinine, and a distinct, lingering metallic tang that Arthur Penhallow had learned to associate with the taste of ambition. Arthur stood before the full-length mirror in his newly acquired flat in Kensington, adjusting the lapels of a suit that cost more than his father had earned in a decade of honest, if modest, clerical work. He was not a man of vanity, nor was he a man of vanity’s opposite, which is usually a dull and resentful humility. He was simply a man who had been forced, by the sheer velocity of his uncle’s success, to become a figure in the public eye, and he found the public eye a most uncomfortable instrument.

His uncle, Josiah Penhallow, had made his fortune not on the tonic itself, which was largely a mixture of water, alcohol, and aggressive marketing, but on the sheer audacity with which he sold it. Josiah had a voice like a foghorn and a smile that could charm a coal scuttler or a cabinet minister with equal ease. He was a man who looked at the world and saw not its complexities, but its defects, which he then proposed to cure for a shilling a bottle. Now, with the death of the sitting Member of Parliament for Oakhaven—a constituency in the Midlands that had been held by the Liberals for forty years—Josiah had decided that the tonic needed a representative in Westminster, and that representative, by a quirk of family loyalty and a lack of better candidates, was Arthur.

Arthur turned from the mirror. He felt a profound sense of inadequacy, not because he was uneducated—he had read widely and thought carefully—but because he was ashamed. He had helped build the factory. He had managed the accounts, negotiated with the suppliers, and, in the early days, had even helped mix the batches. He knew exactly what went into *Vigorite*. He knew it did nothing to cure the nerves, restore the liver, or give a man the strength of a young horse. He knew it was a placebo of the highest order, a psychological trick dressed in the language of science. And yet, he had signed the checks. He had watched the profits roll in, turning a modest shop in Clerkenwell into an empire of glass bottles and lithographs that plastered every tramcar and hoarding in London.

The door opened, and Josiah entered, carrying a hatbox and a large, crumpled newspaper.

"Well, Artie?" Josiah said, his voice bright and cutting through the silence. "You look like a man going to the gallows. We’re going to Parliament, not the guillotine. There’s a difference, though I suppose to the enemy they look much the same."

"Uncle," Arthur said, trying to keep his voice level. "I don’t think I should stand. I’m not a politician. I’m a chemist, of sorts. And I’m not sure... I’m not sure I can defend the product in a debate."

Josiah stopped in the center of the room, setting the hatbox down with a thud. He looked at Arthur with that familiar, piercing gaze that had always unsettled him. "Defend the product?" Josiah laughed, a short, barking sound. "My boy, you don’t defend a product. You sell a hope. The public doesn’t want to know the molecular structure of sarsaparilla. They want to feel better. They want to believe that for a shilling, they can buy back their youth, their vigor, their place in the world. That’s all politics is, Arthur. It’s selling hope on a larger scale. We’re just offering it in smaller bottles."

Arthur shook his head. "It’s a lie, Uncle. A good lie, perhaps, and a profitable one, but a lie nonetheless. If I stand for Parliament, I have to represent the interests of the people of Oakhaven. And if I can’t be honest about the tonic, how can I be honest about anything else?"

Josiah walked over to the window, looking out at the London traffic, the hansom cabs, the pedestrians rushing toward their destinations. "Honesty," he said, without turning around. "That’s a luxury for men who don’t have to make a living. I made this fortune because I was honest about one thing: I knew what people wanted. They wanted to feel alive. They wanted to be strong. I gave them that. Did I give them the real thing? No. But I gave them the *idea* of the real thing. And isn’t that what life is, mostly? A collection of ideas we hold onto to keep the dark at bay?"

Arthur felt a surge of anger, hot and sudden. "You’re cynical, Uncle."

"I’m practical," Josiah corrected, turning to face him. "And you’re a fool. You’re a brilliant, well-read, well-educated fool who is going to ruin everything we’ve built because you can’t bring yourself to tell a small lie for a large benefit. Look at me, Arthur. Look at this face. I’m fifty-five years old. I’ve worked eighteen-hour days for thirty years. I’ve buried my wife. I’ve fought off three bankruptcies. And I’m still standing. Why? Because I believe in *Vigorite*. I drink it every morning. I feel stronger for it. Is that a lie? Or is it a fact?"

Arthur had no answer. He knew that Josiah drank the tonic, not because it worked, but because it was a ritual, a anchor to his identity. He knew that the placebo effect was powerful, that the mind could command the body to perform feats of endurance if it truly believed it was being aided. But knowing that was different from accepting it as a basis for public policy.

"We’ll go to Oakhaven on Friday," Josiah said, his tone shifting back to business. "I’ve arranged for the train. We’ll stay at the George. The local papers are already printing our biography. They’ve made me out to be a self-made man from the gutter, which is a lie, but a flattering one. They’ve made you out to be my brilliant nephew, the scientific mind behind the tonic. It’s a good story. It’s a clean story. All you have to do is smile, nod, and say the right things."
