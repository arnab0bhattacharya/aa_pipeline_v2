The wind had been unrelenting for three days, stripping the skin of the expedition's exposed equipment and driving grit into every crevice of Elias Vane's being. He knelt on the cracked pan of the Ghor Desert, a vast and pitiless expanse in the heart of central Asia, where the horizon shimmered with the ghosts of ancient lakes. The year was 1911, a time when the empires of Europe still drew their borders across maps of places they did not understand. Vane, a palaeontologist of marginal reputation, had come to the Ghor looking for the ancestors of mammals. What he had found was an impossibility that was slowly reordering his understanding of the universe.

He brushed the last of the red dust from the specimen. The sun was brutal, a white eye glaring down from a bleached sky, but the chill that ran through Vane was entirely internal. The skeletal remains were perfectly preserved in the fine-grained sediment, articulated and undisturbed. The creature had stood upright. Its femurs were angled inward, the pelvis was broad and basin-shaped, and the spinal column rose vertically into a skull that sat balanced directly atop the vertebral column. The proportions were undeniably bipedal. Yet, the morphology was all wrong for a human. The bone density was massive, far exceeding that of any hominid, and the digits of the manus, perfectly preserved in the volcanic ash, ended in long, recurved claws, not nails. The skull, still half-buried, possessed a heavy brow ridge that jutted out like a shelf, but the cranial capacity, judging by the shape of the braincase, was enormous. It was not a man, nor an ape, nor any primate Vane had ever studied. It was something else entirely. Something that had walked on two legs through this ancient landscape millions of years before the first hairless ape ever stood up in Africa.

Vane sat back on his heels and took a measured breath. He had been digging for six hours. His hands were blistered beneath the leather of his gloves, and his lips were cracked and bleeding. He reached for his canteen, drank a mouthful of warm, metallic water, and looked down at the pit again. It was not the skeleton alone that had shaken him. Beside the articulated remains, arranged with deliberate care in the same stratum of compressed ash, were tools.

There were three of them. The first was a large, heavy core of chert, flaked on both sides to produce a sharp, symmetrical edge. The second was a smaller implement, perhaps a scraper, made from a dark, obsidian-like glass that Vane did not recognize from any local geological source. The third was bone, shaped and polished into a long, pointed instrument that could only have been a weapon or a probe. The chert tool lay beside the creature's right hand. The bone instrument was placed near the pelvic area, as if it had been worn on a belt or carried in a pouch. The obsidian scraper rested against the left femur. The association was unmistakable. These tools were not intrusive elements that had worked their way down from a higher stratum. They were part of the same deposit, sealed in the same catastrophic event, buried at the same moment. The creature had made them, or at the very least, had used them.

Vane's mind raced through the implications. A bipedal creature of unknown lineage, possessing the cognitive capacity for tool manufacture, existing in the Miocene, or perhaps even earlier. The sediment layer, the volcanic ash, the surrounding fauna he had catalogued in the preceding weeks, all pointed to a date of at least twelve million years ago. Twelve million years before the present. Humanity's entire existence, from the first tentative steps of *Ardipithecus* to the modern industrial age, occupied a sliver of that span. This creature had walked upright, had crafted tools, had perhaps hunted and thought and planned, in a time when the ancestors of modern humans were still small, quadrupedal creatures scurrying through the undergrowth of a vanishing forest.

He heard the crunch of boots on gravel before he saw the figure. Vane did not turn. He recognized the tread, the deliberate and unhurried pace of a man who believed the world existed to accommodate him.

"Professor Vane." The voice was smooth and carefully modulated, carrying the faintest trace of a London accent polished by years of continental education. "I see you have been productive."

Aldous Cray stepped to the edge of the pit and looked down. He was a tall man, immaculately dressed in a linen suit that seemed somehow impervious to the dust and heat of the Ghor. His face was narrow and angular, with grey eyes that gave nothing away. Cray was the sole sponsor of the expedition. He had financed the transport, the supplies, the local guides, and Vane's meagre salary. He had come to the desert, he said, because he had an interest in the deep history of the Earth. He had a private collection in England, a museum of curiosities that he had assembled over two decades, and he wanted specimens. He had been very clear about that from the outset. He wanted specimens, not theories.

Vane stood slowly, his knees aching. He removed his hat and wiped his brow with his sleeve. "Mr. Cray. I did not expect you at the site today."

"The wind has died down enough for a motorcar to make the crossing from camp," Cray said. His gaze moved across the pit, over the exposed bones and the tools, with the detached precision of a man examining goods at a market. He said nothing for a long moment. Then he crouched, balancing on the balls of his feet, and studied the arrangement of the skeleton and the associated artefacts.

"You understand what this is," Vane said. It was not a question.

Cray straightened. He took a handkerchief from his breast pocket and pressed it to his nose. "I understand what it appears to be, Professor. Appearances, as you well know, can be deceptive."
