The dinner was fixed for half past five, and by a quarter before that hour Mrs. Ellingham had already surveyed the table three times, altered the disposition of the candlesticks twice, and desired the butler to assure himself that the second remove of game should not be wanting in the sauce which her cook, upon the authority of an aunt who had lived eleven years in Lord Seaton’s family, was pleased to call *cullis*. It was an occasion of more than common expectation. Mr. Ellingham, of Ashbury Park, had invited to his board no less a person than Sir John Ferrier, whose interest in the county was very great, and whose good opinion, it was reasonably supposed, might be of material service to a young man just entering upon the law. That the young man in question, his eldest son, was at that moment in his chamber, composing his countenance to the gravity proper to a future barrister, and rehearsing certain remarks upon the state of the nation which he designed to let fall with an air of easy reflection, was a circumstance highly gratifying to both parents.

The family were assembled in the drawing-room, and the Miss Ellinghams, in their best muslins, were disposed upon the sofa in attitudes of becoming composure, when the door was thrown open with a freedom that belonged to no servant of the house, and there entered a young man in a naval uniform, brown as a nut, with a countenance so open and so glad that it seemed to light the whole apartment.

“Tom!” cried the mother, rising. “Good heavens, Tom!”

“Why, mother, you look as though I were the French fleet,” said he, kissing her heartily. “I am not come to burn the house, I promise you. Only I am paid off at last, and here I am, and devil a bit of dinner have I had since Portsmouth.”

It was impossible to receive such a son with displeasure; yet it must be owned that his arrival was felt, by the elder part of the family, to be a thing of some inconvenience. He was the younger brother; he had gone to sea at twelve; he had never been expected to do anything but fight, and to be agreeable when he was at home; and he had a habit of speaking of the most tremendous occurrences in a tone of the most unguarded merriment, which, however it became a sailor, was not exactly what Mr. Ellingham desired to exhibit before a baronet whose politics were grave and whose digestion was delicate.

“You will dine with us, of course,” said Mr. Ellingham, with a look of some meaning at his wife. “We expect Sir John Ferrier.”

“Sir John Ferrier! Do we, indeed? And who is Sir John Ferrier, when he is at home?”

“A gentleman of very large property in this county,” replied the father, with dignity, “and one whose good word, Thomas, may be of the greatest consequence to your brother.”

“Then I shall be monstrous civil to him,” said the lieutenant. “I shall tell him the story of the *Ariel* — she was a tight little sloop, sir, and we lost her on the rocks off Ushant in a sea that would have frightened you out of your seven senses—and if that does not make a man of property take to me, why, I know nothing of human nature.”

Mr. Ellingham looked as though he would have given a good deal to know nothing of it either.

The dinner was served, and the party was not long in taking their places. Sir John Ferrier, a heavy gentleman of sixty, with a face that seemed to have been cast in a mould of formal civility, was placed at Mrs. Ellingham’s right hand, and made those observations upon the weather and the roads which are the natural preface to all country hospitality. The elder son, whose name was Charles, sat with a decent gravity, and threw in now and then a remark of studied moderation. The lieutenant, at the lower end of the table, ate with an appetite that did honour to the sea air, and looked about him with the frankest satisfaction.

For the first half hour, all went as well as could be desired. Sir John pronounced the soup to be excellent; Mr. Ellingham bowed; Mrs. Ellingham hoped the partridges were not overdone; and the Miss Ellinghams, who were three in number, said nothing at all, which is often the most prudent course for young ladies at a dinner of ceremony.

But the second remove had not been long upon the table before Sir John, with the condescension of a man who has seen the world, was pleased to ask the lieutenant a question touching his profession.

“And you have been upon the salt seas, sir, I understand?”

“Upon them and in them, Sir John,” returned Tom, with great good humour. “I have been in them up to my chin, and that not above three weeks ago. I shall never forget it. We were coming home with dispatches, and a foul wind, and a foul night, and the *Ariel* — for she was the *Ariel* then, poor thing — went upon the rocks off Ushant, and there she lay, beating and grinding, while the sea made a clean breach over her. We got the boats out, and the men behaved like men; but the long boat was stove, and there was nothing for it but to swim for it.”

“Good God!” said Mrs. Ellingham.

“And did you swim?” cried Sir John, with a stare.
