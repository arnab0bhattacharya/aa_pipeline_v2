The town of Blackridge, nestled amidst the sombre and undulating landscape of Lancashire, had long been the theatre of Josiah Bellingham's elevation; from the squalid hovels of his youth to the commanding eminence of his present fortune, his path had been marked by that indomitable, remorseless energy which the world, in its ignorant adulation, is too apt to denominate genius, but which, in its secret and terrible essence, is too often but the unhallowed offspring of an ambition that knows no scruple and acknowledges no law. The magnificent edifice of the Bellingham Institute, newly erected upon the summit of the ridge, now stood as the crowning glory of his beneficence, its broad and shadowing portico a monument, as all believed, to the noble and disinterested love of his fellow creatures; and it was upon the occasion of its inaugural ceremony, beneath a sky of leaden and tempestuous aspect, that the inhabitants were gathered together, their faces beaming with gratitude and their voices swelling in acclamation for the man who had raised himself from poverty to wealth, and from nothingness to the pinnacle of civic honour.

Yet amidst this universal exaltation, there moved one whose heart was burdened with a secret and unutterable anguish, one who looked upon the towering fabric and felt his soul shrink within him as before the visible presence of an avenging deity. Edwin Marlowe, the grandson of him whom Bellingham had ruined, had been invited—nay, pressed—to deliver the address upon this momentous day; and he, a poor and humble schoolmaster, had accepted the task, impelled by motives so tangled and so painful that even his own breast could scarcely disentangle them. He had loved Bellingham's daughter, the gentle and ethereal Clara, with a devotion that had absorbed his whole existence; and it was this fatal attachment, mingled with a deep and solemn yearning to confront the author of his family's desolation, that had bound him as by a spell to accept the invitation which prudence and honour alike would have taught him to decline. As he stood apart from the throng, his pale and melancholy countenance marked by that deep and settled sorrow which is the inheritance of those who have drunk deeply of the cup of human adversity, he felt all the bitterness of his position; he was about to sing the praises of the man who had beggared his grandfather, who had driven that broken-hearted old man to an early and dishonoured grave, and from whose treacherous hands the Marlowe name had descended to him stained with poverty and unredressed wrong.

Bellingham himself, upon whose brow the marks of time and worldly care were visibly engraven, yet who still bore himself with that lofty and commanding air which is the peculiar attribute of those who have conquered every obstacle to their advancement, received the applause of his fellow townsmen with a modesty so graciously assumed that it served but to enhance the general admiration. His eye, dark and penetrating, wandered over the assembly until it rested upon the slender and drooping figure of young Marlowe; and at that moment, if any lingering touch of conscience stirred within the deep and turbid waters of that worldly heart, it was quickly suppressed, buried beneath the accumulated weight of years of prosperity and the fatal belief that success had sanctified the means by which it was attained. He had persuaded himself, or rather he had permitted the world to persuade him, that the fraud committed against old Marlowe in the dim and distant days of his apprenticeship had been but the necessary and inevitable assertion of his own superior destiny; and now, as he stood upon the summit of his greatness, the very memory of that transgression had faded into a shadow, a dream of the night, from which he had awakened into the broad and unclouded daylight of power and renown.

But the hour of retribution, though long delayed, was at hand; and the instrument of that retribution was one whose gentle and confiding nature seemed the least fitted for so stern a task. As Edwin Marlowe ascended the steps of the platform, and stood before the assembled multitude, the wind, which had been moaning among the rafters of the new edifice, suddenly rose into a fierce and gusty blast, that shook the casements and drove the rain in torrents against the walls; and in that wild and tumultuous accompaniment of nature, he seemed to hear the voice of his wronged and persecuted grandfather calling upon him for justice, for the vindication of a name too long trodden under foot by the ruthless heel of worldly prosperity. He began to speak; and at first his voice was low and tremulous, as one who struggles with some overpowering emotion; but as he proceeded, gathering strength and fervour from the very depth of his own suffering, his words assumed an eloquence and a majesty that thrilled the hearts of all who heard him, and caused even Bellingham himself to start and turn pale, as though the ghost of his betrayed benefactor had risen from the grave to confront and accuse him.

He spoke of the noble purposes of education, of the sacred trust committed to those who guide the young, of the solemn obligation to instil into their plastic minds not only the precepts of worldly wisdom, but the far higher lessons of integrity and unsullied honour. And then, as if impelled by some irresistible and overmastering impulse, he turned, with a sudden and impassioned gesture, towards the man whose bounty had erected the building in which they stood. "It is a proud thing," he exclaimed, and his voice swelled and deepened until it seemed to fill the vaulted hall with a strain of mournful and prophetic grandeur, "to raise from the dust a temple dedicated to the enlightenment of our fellow beings; but there is a prouder and a holier task, and that is, to ensure that the foundations upon which we build are laid in truth and righteousness, and not upon the ruins of a plundered and betrayed benefactor.
