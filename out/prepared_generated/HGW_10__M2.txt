The notice of dismissal was brief, printed on heavy cream paper, and smelled faintly of lavender water. It stated that Mr. Arthur Penhaligon had been relieved of his duties at St. Crispin’s College with immediate effect, owing to a "fundamental divergence in educational philosophy." It was, I am told, a polite way of saying that I had spent an hour dissecting the fossilized remains of a *Laelaps* while the Headmaster’s nephew was being taught that the world was made in six days and the seventh was for napping. I had shown them the teeth. I had shown them the eye. I had shown them the cold, mechanical indifference of natural selection, and it seemed this had offended the very concept of private education.

I did not weep. I am a scientist, or at least I was, before the world decided that science was a sort of moral hazard. I moved to a cramped room above a chandler’s shop in Dulwich, a place that smelled of tallow and damp wool, and took a post as a crammer. It was not a dignified occupation, but it paid the rent, and the work was simple: to fill the heads of young men destined for the Indian Army with the precise amount of grammar, arithmetic, and geographical trivia required to pass a board exam.

My classroom was a back room in a boarding house on Peckham Rye. The boys—or rather, the young men, though they looked like boys—were a dull, shuffling lot. They wore uniforms that were slightly too large for their thinning frames, and they moved with the heavy, uncertain gait of men who had never had to run from anything but boredom. I would sit at the desk, a formidable piece of mahogany that belonged to the house, and lecture them on the conjugation of French verbs or the area of a trapezium. I watched their eyes glaze over. I saw the spirit leaving them, leaving only the husk of a man who would go to India to die of fever or drink himself into a stupor. It was a grim trade, but it was honest in its futility.

It was in this grey, tallow-scented purgatory that I first noticed Miss Elara Vane.

She did not come in the beginning. She was the sister of one of my pupils, a boy named Cornelius, who was particularly obtuse regarding the Pythagorean theorem. Elara was perhaps twenty, with the kind of sharp, intelligent face that one sees in medical illustrations, though she was dressed in the heavy, fussy fashion of the time, with a hat that defied gravity and a coat buttoned to the chin. She began to visit during the lunch hour, ostensibly to check on her brother’s progress, but she would linger. She would sit in the corner, by the window where the light was poorest, and she would watch me.

I did not like it. I am a man who values privacy, and I had found that privacy is the only luxury left to a dismissed scientist. But she was quiet. She did not chatter like the other women who came to the house—landladies and chaperones who spoke in a high, shrill register that seemed designed to pierce the eardrums. She simply watched.

One Tuesday, as I was explaining the difference between a noun and a pronoun to a group of boys who looked as if they would prefer to be anywhere else, including the gas chamber, I felt a presence behind me. I turned. Elara was standing there, holding a book.

"You are making a mistake," she said. Her voice was low, clear, and entirely without the affectation I expected. "You are treating the language as a set of rules to be memorized, not a tool for thought. It is like teaching a man to use a lever by making him memorize the weight of the fulcrum, instead of explaining the principle of mechanical advantage."

I looked at her, then at my class. The boys were staring at her, their faces a mixture of confusion and mild amusement. Cornelius, in particular, looked as if he had swallowed a lemon.

"Miss Vane," I said, keeping my voice level. "I am preparing these young men for an examination. The examination has specific requirements."

"The examination is a sieve," she said. "It catches the smart and lets the dull pass, provided they can spell 'conscience' and divide by four. But you, Mr. Penhaligon, you are not a sieve. I have read your articles in the *Nature* journal. I have read Huxley. I have read Darwin. I know you are a man who sees the structure of things. Why are you wasting that on conjugation?"

I felt a strange, cold shock run through me. It was not anger, exactly. It was the feeling of a man who has been working in a dark room for years and suddenly sees the sun. I looked at the boys. They were shuffling their feet, looking at the floor, at each other, at anything but the conversation. They were ghosts, walking through a life they had not chosen.

"I am paid to prepare them for the Army," I said.

"The Army," she said, with a slight shrug. "They will go to India. They will die of cholera, or they will return broken. And they will have learned nothing about the world they are meant to rule. It is a tragedy, Mr. Penhaligon. But it is also a lesson."

She looked at me, her eyes bright with a fierce, intellectual hunger that I had not seen in a student for years.

"Teach them something else," she said. "Just for an hour. Teach them about the earth. Teach them about the stars. Teach them that they are not gods, but part of a vast, indifferent machine. It will not help them pass the exam. But it will make them men."
