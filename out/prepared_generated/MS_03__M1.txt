It was in the late autumn of that year, when the mists of the Thames crept like ghosts through the colonnades of the New Whitehall, that the full weight of my station descended upon me. I had been bred to the service of the Republic from my earliest youth; my father had been a Commissioner of Works under the old Consolidation, and his father before him, and I had ever believed that to dedicate one’s faculties to the public weal was the highest and only noble ambition of a rational creature. Yet, as I sat in the chamber of the Executive Council, listening to the dry, measured tones of the Chief Statistician, Mr. Gravesend, I felt a chill of desolation such as no storm in the open country could ever inspire.

Gravesend, a man whose heart had been bleached by the sun of figures and whose soul was bound up in the ledgers of the world, presented his report with the indifference of a physician pronouncing a death sentence upon a stranger. He spoke of the Southern Protectorates; he spoke of the blight that had seized the hydroponic yields and the failure of the atmospheric regulators in the equatorial zones; he spoke of the shipping lanes and the dwindling stores. And then, with a calm that bordered on the diabolical, he projected the curves of his charts upon the illuminated wall. The lines, jagged and cruel, descended inexorably toward the x-axis of the coming year.

"The conclusion is arithmetically certain," said Gravesend, adjusting his spectacles. "The deficit in the caloric index of the home islands will reach critical magnitude by the midsummer. There will be hunger in London. There will be hunger in the North. The Republic cannot import what does not exist."

A silence followed, heavy and suffocating, broken only by the hum of the ventilation systems that sustained our subterranean deliberations. I looked around the oval table at the faces of my colleagues—men of intellect, of education, of renown. I saw in their eyes not the horror that was consuming my own breast, but a swift, instinctive recoil, the look of animals seeking a burrow. Lord Vane, the First Consul, a man of polished rhetoric and noble lineage, was the first to speak.

"Gravesend," he said, his voice smooth as oil upon troubled water, "your figures are, as ever, impeccable. But figures are not fate. We must not panic the populace. There are always variables. The weather may turn. The southern blight may abate. To alarm the citizens now would be to invite the very chaos we seek to prevent. We must maintain confidence."

"And if the variables do not turn?" I asked, my voice sounding hollow even to my own ears. "If the arithmetic is, as he says, certain?"

Vane turned his gaze upon me with a benevolent, pitying smile, the sort one might offer to a hysterical child. "My dear Alban," he said, "you are young, and your heart is of that ardent texture which feels the world too keenly. We are the guardians of the state. To govern is to choose between evils. If we announce a famine that has not yet arrived, we cause a famine of the spirit today. The markets will crash; the granaries will be looted; brother will turn against brother in the scramble for the last loaf. By preserving the peace now, we buy time. Perhaps Providence will intervene."

"Providence?" I cried, rising from my seat, unable to contain the tempest within me. "Are we children, to wait for a miracle? We are the Republic! We are the masters of our destiny, by reason and by science! And now, when science tells us we shall starve, we are told to play the ostrich and bury our heads in the sands of hope?"

"Sit down, Alban," said Gravesend coldly. "Hysteria is unbecoming a statesman."

But I could not sit. I paced the length of the room, my mind a whirlwind of conflicting duties. I thought of the people of London—the millions who lived in the towering habitation blocks, who trusted the Council as they trusted the rising of the sun. I had sworn an oath to them. Was it not the first duty of a representative to tell the truth, however bitter? Yet, as Vane had said, truth is a fire; it gives warmth, but it also consumes. If I spoke, and the famine did not come, I would be the destroyer of the state. If I spoke, and the famine did come, would the knowledge save them? Or would it merely make the end more wretched, dragging the whole of society into an abyss of violence and despair before the hunger even touched their lips?

I left the Council chamber and sought the solitude of the river bank. The night was dark, and a fierce wind, coming up from the estuary, tore at my cloak and whipped the black water into white crests. The city rose around me, a forest of light and steel, a monument to the triumph of human will over nature. And yet, within that glittering shell, the ancient enemy, Famine, was even now sharpening his scythe. I looked at the lights of the great towers, and I thought of the families within—of the mothers tucking children into beds, of the lovers whispering promises, of the old men reading by the fire. They were ignorant of the shadow falling over them. They were happy in their delusion.

"Is it mercy to leave them in darkness?" I asked the wind. "Or is it mercy to arm them with the truth?"

I thought of the history of the world, of the revolutions and the tyrannies, of the times when silence had bred corruption and when speech had brought salvation. I thought of the great men of the past, the martyrs of the Republic who had died for the principle of self-governance. Could I, who had lived for that principle, now betray it for the sake of a quiet year? The cowardice of my colleagues revolted me; their desire to "preserve the peace" seemed to me nothing but a fear of their own fall from power. They wished to rule over a calm populace, even if that calm was the stillness of the grave.
