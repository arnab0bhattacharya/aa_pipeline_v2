The rain came off the moors in silver sheets, driving against the cramped rows of Ashwick's cottages until the slate roofs wept black tears into the gutters. Inside number four, Jubber Row, the damp had crept up the plaster in a creeping bloom of frost and mould. Mary Sutcliffe sat on the edge of the straw mattress, listening to the rattle of the looms at the top of the street. Even at this hour, the great belt of the Victoria Mill groaned through the earth, vibrating up through the flagged floor and into the soles of her worn boots. She had not eaten since the previous morning, but the hunger had long since passed from a sharp ache in her belly to a hollow, dizzying hum behind her eyes. She looked down at her hands, chapped and reddened by lye, the nails torn to the quick, and thought they no longer looked like her own.

Beside her, Tom breathed in short, shallow rasps. He had been a loom fixer at Victoria Mill, quick and strong, until the shuttle had broken free and shattered his knee. Now, the joint was fused stiff and grating, leaving him bed-bound and reliant on the parish and the occasional half-crown from the union. But it was not just his leg that was broken. The coughing had started in the autumn, a dry, tearing sound that left the handkerchief spotted with a rust-coloured damp. Mary knew the dust had got him. She had seen enough men in Ashwick fade into husks, their lungs packed tight with cotton fly, to recognise the look in his sunken, fever-bright eyes.

The door latch clicked, and their eldest, twelve-year-old Joseph, stepped inside. He carried a small canvas sack. His face, pale and sharp beneath a cap that was too large for him, was wet with rain and sweat. He set the sack on the table, and out tumbled three small, hard potatoes and a heel of bread so stale it rang against the wood.

"It were all I could get," Joseph whispered, looking at his mother. "Mr. Hardacre said the slate is paid up, but he won't give no more tick."

Mary pulled the boy to her, feeling the sharp ridges of his spine beneath his threadbare jacket. "You've done well, our Joe," she said, her voice steady despite the trembling in her chest. "Wake the little ones. We'll boil the spuds."

As Joseph nudged his younger sisters awake, Mary took the bread and broke it into four unequal pieces, setting the largest aside for Tom. She carried a cup of water to the bed. Tom pushed himself up against the damp wall, his dark hair plastered to his forehead.

"I heard the lad," Tom said, his voice cracking. "I'm not an invalid, Mary. I can hear him begging."

"He wasn't begging," Mary lied softly. "Take your bread, Tom. You need your strength."

Tom took the crust, staring at it with a mixture of hunger and shame. He bit into it, chewing slowly, his jaw working with a grim determination. "I'll be back at the mill soon," he said, though they both knew it was a lie. "I'll get back on the floor."

"Of course you will," Mary murmured, turning back to the hearth to hide the tears that threatened to spill. She knelt and struck a match to light the small fire, setting the dented tin pot to boil. The steam rose, carrying the smell of damp peat and poverty.

Later that afternoon, as the children slept huddled together for warmth, Mary pulled her shawl tight around her shoulders and stepped out into the cobbled street. The rain had eased to a clinging mist. She had an errand to run, one she had been putting off for three days. She walked past the Victoria Mill, its brick face towering over the terraces, the windows glowing with the pale light of the gas jets inside. She could hear the relentless thud of the looms, the clatter of the shuttles, the hiss of the steam engines. She had worked there once, before Tom's accident, drawing the threads, her lungs burning with the dust. Now, the mill was a monster that consumed the men and women of Ashwick and ground them into dust.

She walked up the hill, past the Methodist chapel, to the wider streets where the overlookers and clerks lived. At the very top of the hill, set back from the road behind a low wall, stood Laurel Cottage. It was a modest house by the standards of the mill owners, but to Mary, with its clean white door and polished brass knocker, it seemed a palace. She knocked, her heart hammering against her ribs.

The door was opened by a young maidservant, who looked Mary up and down with a mixture of pity and suspicion. "Mrs. Sutcliffe to see Mr. Crane," Mary said, her voice firm despite the tremble in her hands.

She was led into a parlour. The air was warm, thick with the scent of coal smoke and beeswax. A fire roared in the grate, and the walls were lined with books. Standing by the window, looking out over the smog-choked valley, was Robert Crane.

He was the mill owner's elder brother, a man of forty-five with a sad, angular face and hair prematurely silvered at the temples. He had been widowed two years past, his wife taken by a fever, and since then he had lived alone in Laurel Cottage, his only company the maidservant and a cook. He was known in Ashwick as a quiet man, a fair man, who had once managed the mill's counting house before retiring with a comfortable share.

"Mrs. Sutcliffe," he said, turning from the window. "Thank you for coming. Please, sit."

Mary did not sit. She stood by the door, twisting her hands in her apron. "You sent word, Mr. Crane. You had a proposition."

Crane nodded, gesturing to a chair. "I did. Please, Mrs. Sutcliffe. I won't have you standing on ceremony."
