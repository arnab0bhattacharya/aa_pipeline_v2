The mistake was made, as most catastrophes in London are made, over a glass of champagne and a misunderstanding of accent. Lord Arthur Pym, who had spent the better part of three decades being described by his peers as "pleasantly vacant" and by his critics as "a decorative piece of furniture with a pulse," was seated in the corner of the Marlborough Club, holding a tumbler of sherry with the rigid caution of a man who knows that if he spills it, he will be blamed for the dampness of the carpet.

Across the room, in a velvet armchair the color of a bruised plum, sat Monsieur de Vaucresson, a French journalist of considerable reputation and even greater vanity. Monsieur de Vaucresson had arrived in England to interview the celebrated wit, Lord Valois, a man whose reputation for brilliance was so loud that it often drowned out the actual conversation. However, Lord Valois had been detained by a trivial indiscretion involving a misplaced monocle and a scandalous hat, and the journalist, impatient by nature and bored by necessity, had drifted toward the quiet corner where Lord Arthur sat.

Lord Arthur, sensing a human presence but lacking the social instinct to initiate small talk, had merely looked up, sighed with the profound boredom of a man who has seen every trick the world has to offer, and said, "It is terribly difficult to be interesting when one is merely polite."

Monsieur de Vaucresson, who had been standing behind him, mistook the sigh for a prelude to a devastating epigram. He turned, his eyes bright with the anticipation of a literary feast. "Ah, Lord Valois!" he exclaimed, bowing with the grace of a crane. "I had heard that your mind was a labyrinth, but I did not expect its entrance to be so guarded."

Lord Arthur blinked. He looked at the tall, mustached stranger, then at his own sherry, which had gone flat. "I beg your pardon?" he said, in a voice that was usually reserved for ordering the bill.

"The labyrinth, my dear Lord," said the Frenchman, patting him on the shoulder with a familiarity that would have horrified Lord Arthur’s valet. "The labyrinth of the intellect. I am de Vaucresson. I have come to record your thoughts. Do not be shy; the pen is a more honest lover than the heart."

Lord Arthur, who had never been spoken to with such aggressive admiration, felt a strange warmth in his chest. It was not the sherry. It was the sudden, terrifying possibility that he might, after all, be something other than a background character in his own life. He smoothed his cravat, a gesture he usually performed when he was about to make a mistake, and said, "I am afraid my thoughts are somewhat disordered. They tend to wander off into the garden and sit on the lawn."

Monsieur de Vaucresson scribbled furiously in his notebook. "Magnificent," he whispered. "The garden. The lawn. The mind as a domesticated wilderness. Tell me more of this... wandering."

Lord Arthur, emboldened by the journalist’s fervor, decided to be as vague as possible, for he had learned in his youth that specificity was the enemy of safety. "Well," he said, "I find that if one looks too closely at a thing, it often turns out to be a mirror. And one rarely likes what one sees in the mirror, unless one is very vain, or very tired."

The Frenchman looked up, his eyes shining. "A mirror! Yes! The reflection of the self! And vanity as a form of exhaustion! My God, how simple, and how deep! I shall need a glass of water. My mind is parched by such wisdom."

From that moment on, the illusion took hold with the tenacity of a vine. Every dull, trite, or nonsensical remark that Lord Arthur made was interpreted by Monsieur de Vaucresson as a profound philosophical statement. When Arthur complained that the club’s roast beef was "dry," the journalist noted in his diary, *The aridity of existence. The flesh of the world is parched. Only the spirit can quench it.* When Arthur mentioned that he had lost his umbrella, the Frenchman gasped, "The umbrella! The protection against the rain of fate! To lose it is to be exposed to the weeping of the heavens. It is a metaphor for the nakedness of the soul."

Lord Arthur, who had always been considered the dullest man in London, began to believe his own legend. He started to speak more, not because he had anything to say, but because he was intoxicated by the applause of his own perceived intellect. He stood before the mirror in his dressing room and practiced his sighs. He adjusted his monocle to look more skeptical. He composed sentences that were grammatically correct but semantically empty, and he delivered them with the gravity of a prophet.

His friends, however, began to notice a change. Lord Valois, the actual wit, returned to the club the following week, and was greeted with a silence that was thick with judgment. "Arthur," said Lord Valois, who had heard rumors of the Frenchman’s visit, "I see you have found your voice. It is a pity it is so loud. It drowns out the music."

Lord Arthur smiled, a smile that was thin and brittle as a winter leaf. "The music," he said, "is often a distraction. One must learn to listen to the silence. The silence is where the truth lives."

Lord Valois looked at him for a long moment. "Arthur," he said softly, "I didn’t know you had a taste for mysticism. I thought you preferred the concrete."

"The concrete is merely the solidified shadow of the abstract," Lord Arthur replied, turning his back to the mirror to admire his profile.
