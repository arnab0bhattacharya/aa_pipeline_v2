The Cathedral of Saint Cyprian in the city of Valerius was a masterpiece of cold stone and colder piety. It was a building entirely deficient in warmth, but possessed of a magnificent severity. Its stained-glass windows, which depicted the martyrdoms of various saints with a remarkable attention to anatomical detail, filtered the winter sunlight into harsh, jewel-like splinters of crimson and emerald. The canons of the cathedral were men of impeccable boredom, and the Bishop, the Right Reverend Father Anselm, was a man who had dedicated his life to the proposition that religion should never be an aesthetic experience. He was a prelate of immense dignity and absolutely no taste. He wore his vestments as though they were a hair shirt, and he delivered his sermons with the monotonous precision of a man who is entirely convinced that God is deaf.

In the north transept of the cathedral stood an organ of vast antiquity. It was a monstrous instrument, its pipes rising like the trunks of petrified trees, its sounding-boards heavy with the dust of a century. No hand had touched its keys, no breath had passed through its metal throat, for exactly one hundred years. It was said that the last organist had died of a broken heart at the console, and the canons, who despised anything that savored of the romantic, had locked the instrument away, allowing it to moulder into a picturesque decay. Decay, in Valerius, was considered a sign of holiness, provided it was entirely devoid of passion.

It was upon the eve of Christmas that the organ began to play itself.

The night was uncommonly still. A light frost lay upon the cobblestones of the city, making them gleam like heaps of crushed pearls beneath the gas-lamps. Inside the cathedral, the sacristan had extinguished the altar candles and retired to his bed, leaving the great nave to the shadows and the moonlight. The moon, which is entirely indiscriminate in its affections, poured through the high clerestory windows, bathing the empty pews in a silver, spectral glow.

It was then, at the very threshold of midnight, that the first chord sounded. It was a low, murmuring harmony, as if the masonry itself were sighing. Then, with a sudden, staggering majesty, the organ broke into a fugue of such piercing beauty that it seemed the very vault of heaven had been cracked open. The music rolled down the aisles, a tide of gold and crimson sound, flooding the dark corners of the cathedral with a passionate, terrible joy. It was music that had no business in Saint Cyprian’s, for it was not the dry, mathematical piety of a hymn, but the wild, weeping ecstasy of a world that had remembered how to feel.

The Bishop, whose apartments adjoined the cathedral, was roused from his slumbers. He was a man who distrusted sleep, as he distrusted all natural impulses, but he was deeply annoyed at being disturbed. He threw a heavy velvet dressing-gown over his nightclothes, placed a skull-cap upon his brow with an air of profound moral indignation, and proceeded into the nave.

The music was magnificent. It was a symphony of rebellion, a cascade of divine disobedience. It spoke of the flesh and the spirit, of the earth and the stars, and it made the cold stone of the cathedral throb with a sudden, secret life. The Bishop listened for a moment, and a look of absolute fury contorted his ascetic features. He was not moved; he was outraged. To him, music was merely a mathematical formula, an aid to devotion, and entirely subordinate to the text. This was not devotion; this was delirium. It was, he decided, highly improper.

He summoned the Dean and the canons, who arrived in various stages of undress and terror. They stood in a terrified cluster near the baptistery, their faces pale in the moonlight.

“It is the old organ,” whispered the Dean, his teeth chattering as though he were reciting the Nicene Creed in a snowstorm.

“I am aware of that,” said the Bishop, coldly. “It is an organ, and it is old. Both are facts that I have long deplored. But the question is not one of age, but of authority. The instrument is playing without authorization. It is violating the rubric.”

“But your Grace,” stammered a young canon, who had a taste for poetry and was therefore universally despised by his brethren, “it is the most beautiful music I have ever heard. It is almost as if—”

“Beauty,” interrupted the Bishop, “is a mere accident of form. Obedience is an essential of character. I forbid anyone to listen to this music. It is unauthorized, and therefore profane. To listen to it is to encourage a disruption of the ecclesiastical order. I command you all to return to your beds, and to stop your ears with wool if necessary. The organ will soon exhaust its unnatural vigor, or I shall have it dismantled in the morning.”

The canons, who were entirely accustomed to obedience, bowed and retreated, though the young canon cast one longing, lingering look at the organ loft before he departed. The Bishop, having asserted his authority, returned to his bed, but not to sleep. He lay in the dark, listening to the muffled thunder of the music, and he thought of the dismantling.

In the cathedral choir school, which was a draughty, miserable building adjoining the cathedral close, a little choirboy named Felix was lying awake. Felix was a child of ten, with a head of unruly golden curls and a heart that was entirely too large for his cassock. He was a perpetual source of annoyance to his masters, for he had a habit of asking questions that were not in the catechism, and of finding beauty in things that were not expressly permitted.
