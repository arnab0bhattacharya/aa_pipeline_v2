It is a curious thing to consider that for three hundred years the whole fabric of human society has rested upon the complete and absolute abandonment of armed conflict. I had been born in the quiet archives of Port Liss, a settlement of some fifty thousand souls nestled in the temperate hills of what the old records call the Southern Peninsula. We were a people given over to the sorting and preserving of human knowledge, gentle-mannered and extraordinarily healthy, living upon the fruits of our automated fields and concerning ourselves chiefly with the classification of old things. We had no soldiers, no weapons, no police; the very vocabulary of strife had dwindled into a few vague phrases applied solely to the weather or to the more boisterous of our athletic games.

It was in the course of my ordinary duties as a classifier that I stumbled upon the Veridan Records. They had been brought down from the North in a quantity of salvage some twenty years before, and dumped in a sub-basement of the central hall because nobody could determine exactly to which department they belonged. I was checking the lower vaults for dampness when I found them—three hundred steel cylinders of a sort our engineers had long since ceased to make, hermetically sealed and marked with symbols that were quite unfamiliar to me.

I opened one, purely out of that idle curiosity which is the chief vice and principal virtue of our profession, and found within a quantity of spooling wire and a set of translated transcripts upon a material resembling paper. They were, I discovered, the field communications of the last great battle of the Old World—a conflict that had raged across the continent of Europa in the final days before the Great Pacification.

I sat upon the floor of the vault and read them through, and as I read, the whole monstrous and incredible fact of organised killing took hold of my mind with a grip I have not since been able to shake off.

The battle—or rather, the series of battles known collectively as the Assault upon the Margrave Heights—had been fought between two coalitions of nations whose very names are now no more than phonetic curiosities. The records were extraordinarily detailed. They spoke of the movement of divisions, of the concentration of artillery fire, of the deployment of gas and of flame. They spoke of casualty figures—casualties! a word I had to look up in an archaic lexicon—with the same flat precision that one of our agricultural engineers might use in reporting the yield of a grain field.

But it was not the scale of the slaughter, staggering as that was, which so gripped my imagination. It was the evident purpose behind it. These men had not killed in anger or in passion, though such things were mentioned. They had killed because they believed, or at any rate their leaders believed, that the alternative was worse. They had killed to defend certain ideas, certain territories, certain arrangements of human life which they held to be indispensable to their future. They had looked forward, as we look forward, and they had concluded that some things were worth the price of blood.

I brought the records to the attention of Councillor Harren, who presided over the archive. He was a man of perhaps sixty, with a placid, ruddy face and the easy, confident manner of one who has never been contradicted.

"What are these?" he said, turning one of the transcripts over with a certain distaste.

"The records of the last war," I said. "They were never catalogued."

"War," he repeated, as one might repeat an unfamiliar word in a foreign tongue. He smiled, not unkindly. "Yes, I suppose we must have had such things once. Put them with the social aberrations, I suppose. Section Fourteen."

"But don't you see," I said, "these people were not so very different from us. They had the same sciences, the same organisations. They were not savages."

"They fought one another," said Harren, as if that settled the matter. "We do not. They were ill. we are well. Put them in Section Fourteen."

I put them in Section Fourteen, but I did not cease to read them. Over the following weeks I returned again and again to that sub-basement, and I began to piece together not merely the history of the battle but the psychology of those who had fought it. I read the personal communications of officers and men. I read the strategic debates that had preceded the engagement. And I began to perceive something which I believe nobody in our age has perceived before.

These people had not been mad. They had been desperate, and they had been frightened, and they had been placed in circumstances where the cost of inaction appeared to them greater than the cost of action. They had looked at the world and seen, as we see, competing possibilities for the future, and they had concluded that some of those possibilities were so dreadful that they must be prevented by any means, even the means of mutual destruction.

Now, we in our age do not have such fears. We have concluded, with a complacency so deep that it has become invisible, that the future will take care of itself. We have no great competing visions of what human life ought to be. We are all, in a manner of speaking, of one mind. And because we are of one mind, we have no war.

But it occurred to me, sitting in that vault among the steel cylinders, that this absence of conflict was not a thing achieved but a thing forgotten. We did not fight because we no longer cared enough about anything to fight for it. We had narrowed our ambitions to the maintenance of comfort. We had made a world so smooth that nothing in it was worth striking a blow.
