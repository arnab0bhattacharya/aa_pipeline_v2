I had remarked to Thorneby more than once that his most singular cases had a habit of announcing themselves upon the very threshold of our sitting-room, and so it proved upon that bleak November morning in '84. The fog had settled upon London like a grey, wet blanket, and our fire was crackling brightly, when there came a sharp, imperative peal at the bell. Within moments, our landlady had ushered in Inspector Gregson of Scotland Yard, his heavy boots leaving damp impressions upon the Persian carpet.

Gregson was a man of unbending routine, and I could tell from the stiff set of his shoulders and the perplexed furrow of his brow that something had occurred entirely outside the usual run of his experience. He carried his official notebook in one hand, and in the other a folded, discoloured newspaper.

"Good morning, gentlemen," said he, casting a gloomy glance out of the rain-streaked window. "I find myself compelled, against my better judgement, to seek your counsel, Mr. Thorneby. We have a most extraordinary business in Lambeth."

Thorneby pushed his breakfast plate to one side and reached for his pipe. "Pray be seated, Inspector," said he. "You speak as though the matter is beyond the talents of the Yard. I trust no one has been killed?"

"That is the puzzle," answered Gregson, dropping heavily into the basket-chair. "A man has been killed, or has killed himself; we cannot tell which. He was found at dawn by a constable on his beat, lying in the front parlour of a derelict house in Princes Road. Number fourteen has stood empty these six months past. The door was found open, and the man lay on his back in the centre of the bare boards, quite dead. There is no mark of violence upon the body, no weapon to be found, and no clue of any kind—save for this."

He tossed the folded newspaper onto the table. It was a broadsheet, printed upon coarse, yellowing paper. Thorneby picked it up and turned it over in his long, thin fingers with the air of a man who finds a peculiar interest in the most mundane objects.

"You say there is no clue," remarked Thorneby, his eyes fixed upon the print. "Yet I perceive that this American journal has been deliberately brought to my attention. There is a ring drawn about a date in this copy, Inspector."

"Yes," said Gregson, leaning forward. "The date is the fourteenth of October, 1864. It is a copy of the *Cincinnati Commercial Gazette*. The ink is fresh. But what does a dead man in Lambeth want with a newspaper from Ohio dated twenty years ago?"

Thorneby was silent for a few moments, his sharp, grey eyes scanning the columns of the foreign sheet. Presently he gave a little start, and a smile of keen interest played about his ascetic features.

"You are a faithful chronicler of the London press, Gregson," said he, "but I fear your knowledge of foreign crime is somewhat limited. What do you know of the Cincinnati Trust and Deposit robbery?"

Gregson looked utterly blank. "I cannot say I have ever heard of it," he confessed.

"It was a very singular affair," Thorneby continued, leaning back and clasping his hands behind his head. "In the autumn of 1864, the Cincinnati Trust was relieved of some sixty thousand dollars in untraceable banknotes. The crime was executed with remarkable ingenuity, the vaults being opened with duplicate keys. The chief cashier, a man named Silas P. Hargreaves, vanished shortly after the robbery. The American police, with their usual precipitate reasoning, concluded that Hargreaves was the architect of the crime and had fled with the spoils. He left behind him a wife and a considerable reputation for probity, which did not prevent the press from tearing his name to shreds. The money was never recovered, and Hargreaves was never seen again."

"And you think this dead man in Lambeth is Hargreaves?" asked Gregson, his eyes widening.

"Not Hargreaves," said Thorneby, rising briskly and throwing his dressing-gown over the back of his chair. "Hargreaves, if I remember the case aright, was a tall, fair man. The individual lying in Princes Road is, if I am not much mistaken, somewhat shorter, and dark. No, Watson, put on your overcoat. We are going to Lambeth. Bring your medical case, for I may need your professional opinion."

The drive through the dismal streets of south London was a silent one. The fog had thickened, lending a ghostly, unreal aspect to the grim facades of the houses. We alighted at Number 14, a grimy, brick-faced building with shuttered windows and an air of profound desolation. A constable stood at the door, and Gregson led us down the bare, echoing passage to the front parlour.

The room was entirely devoid of furniture, the wallpaper hanging in long, damp strips from the walls. Upon the bare boards lay the body of a man, as Gregson had described. He was dressed in a well-worn but carefully brushed suit of dark tweed. His face was turned towards the ceiling, and it wore an expression of profound, unyielding terror. His lips were drawn back from his teeth, and his wide, staring eyes seemed fixed upon some unseen horror in the empty air.

I knelt beside the body and made a thorough examination. "There is no wound," I reported, closing the man's jaw. "The limbs are relaxed, and there is no discoloration of the skin to suggest poison. The pupils are dilated to the utmost, and the heart is enlarged. I should say, Thorneby, that the man has died of shock—of a sudden, overwhelming fright."

"An invisible assailant, then," murmured Thorneby, who was upon his knees examining the floor about the corpse with a magnifying glass. "A murderer who strikes without a weapon, and leaves no trace. Most singular."

He rose to his feet and looked slowly around the empty room. His eyes travelled from the crumbling cornice to the bare window, and finally rested upon the dead man's hands. They were clasped together upon his breast, the fingers stiff and cold.
