The Professor had a habit, common among men of his peculiar temperament, of regarding the domestic arrangements of his lodgings as a temporary inconvenience, a sort of atmospheric friction to be endured while the mind traveled elsewhere. He was Dr. Arthur H. Pym, a lecturer in theoretical mechanics at the University, a man whose face was as angular and unyielding as the glassware he handled in his laboratory. He lived in a narrow, damp house on St. Aldate’s, occupied by Mrs. Gable, a widow of formidable proportions and an even more formidable sense of propriety, and her daughter, Miss Etta, who possessed a talent for silence that was only occasionally broken by a sharp, critical observation.

For three weeks, Pym had kept a secret. It was not, as one might suppose, a secret of moral depravity, but one of scientific curiosity. On a Tuesday evening, amidst a haze of chalk dust and the smell of ozone, he had inadvertently created a pocket of space roughly the size of a large orange. Inside this sphere, which he kept in a stoppered glass jar on his desk, the laws of nature were, to use a term he had coined, "inverted." Light bent away from sources; heat flowed from cold objects to hot ones; and, most disturbingly, time did not flow forward but seemed to ripple, like the surface of a pond struck by a stone.

He told no one. Not the Dean, who was currently engaged in a futile attempt to reconcile the budget with the heating bill; not his colleagues, who would have either dismissed him as a crank or, worse, as a danger to the public order. He kept it because it was beautiful. To look into the jar was to see a miniature universe where the logic of the world was turned on its head. It was a private delight, a secret toy for a man who had spent his life calculating the predictable motions of planets.

Mrs. Gable, however, did not share his reverence for the arcane. To her, a room was a site of potential disorder. Dust was a moral failing. Clutter was an invitation to chaos. She had long regarded Pym’s desk, which was buried under a avalanche of manuscripts and papers, with a suspicion that bordered on hostility. It was, she decided, a breeding ground for dirt.

It happened on a Friday afternoon, while the rain lashed against the windowpanes and the gaslight in the hallway flickered with an unsteady, yellow anxiety. Pym was in the lecture hall, delivering a talk on the conservation of energy, a subject he found increasingly ironic given his recent experiments. Mrs. Gable, armed with a feather duster and a determination to restore order, had entered his room.

She had been cleaning for twenty minutes. The air smelled of vinegar and lemon polish. She had cleared the papers from the left side of the desk, piling them into a neat, indifferent stack. Then she reached for the jar. It was not on the top; it was tucked behind a stack of journals, slightly obscured. Her elbow, moving with the swift, unconscious efficiency of a woman who had mopped a hundred floors, struck the base of the jar.

It did not shatter. Glass is surprisingly strong, until it isn’t. The jar rolled off the edge of the desk and fell onto the carpet. The stopper, which had been loose for some days, flew off and bounced against the wall.

Pym heard the sound from across the street. It was a sharp, metallic ping, followed by a soft thud. He stopped mid-sentence. The lecture hall fell silent. He looked at the clock. He knew, with a certainty that bypassed reason, that something had gone wrong. He dismissed his class with a wave of his hand that was less authoritative than usual and ran.

The house was quiet. The rain had turned to a sleet, tapping against the windows like a impatient audience. Mrs. Gable was in the kitchen, washing the dishes, humming a tuneless melody. Pym burst into his room.

The jar lay on the carpet, empty. The liquid, or rather the substance, that had filled it was gone. In its place was a patch of carpet that looked... wrong. It was not wet, nor was it stained. It was simply *less*. The wool fibers did not meet; there was a gap, a small, circular absence in the fabric, about the size of the jar.

Pym stared at it. He reached out a hand to touch the edge of the void. The air above the gap shimmered, not with heat, but with a cold so profound it seemed to drain the color from the room. A faint, high-pitched whine, like the sound of a tuning fork struck in a vacuum, filled the air.

Mrs. Gable appeared in the doorway, holding a wet cloth. She looked at the Professor, then at the carpet.

"What have you done?" she asked. Her voice was flat, devoid of anger, which was somehow worse.

"I... I knocked it over," Pym said. His voice sounded thin. "I must have been careless."

"I didn't see anything," Mrs. Gable said. "I only saw a jar. It was empty."

"It wasn't empty," Pym whispered. He looked at the hole in the carpet. It was growing. Not rapidly, but steadily. The whine was getting louder. The light from the window seemed to bend around the gap, refusing to enter it. The shadows in the corner of the room were lengthening, stretching towards the center of the floor.

"It’s leaking," Pym said. He felt a sudden, irrational urge to laugh. It was a laugh that felt like it might break him. "The laws are leaking."

Mrs. Gable looked at him with a mixture of pity and disgust. She stepped into the room, her slippers squeaking on the floorboards. She looked down at the gap. She did not flinch. She was a practical woman.

"Pick it up," she said.
