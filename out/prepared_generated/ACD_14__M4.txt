The fog had lifted somewhat by the afternoon, though the air in the narrow street where I had my lodgings remained thick with the smell of damp wool and coal-smoke. I was seated at my window with a volume of Huxley upon my knee, though I confess my attention had wandered from the page, when I heard upon the stairs a shuffling step, accompanied by a wheeze that spoke of lungs long acquainted with the peculiar atmosphere of the East End.

The door opened to admit a man of some sixty years, stooped and grey, wearing a coat that had once been respectable and a hat that had never been so. His hands, I observed at once, were those of a tradesman—stained at the fingertips with the residue of metal polish, and bearing upon the left thumb a callus such as is produced by the constant handling of small instruments.

“Mr. Holloway?” said he, in the wheezing voice I had heard upon the stair. “They told me at the corner that you was the gentleman as found things out.”

“I have occasionally been of service in that way,” I replied, laying aside my book. “Pray be seated, and tell me what brings you here.”

He sat down heavily, turning his hat between his fingers, and for a moment seemed uncertain how to begin.

“My name is Ebenezer Croft,” said he at last. “I keep a pawnbroker’s shop in Cobb’s Lane, not a hundred yards from here. It’s a poor trade and a poor place, sir, but I’ve kept it honest these thirty years, and I’d not have it said that Croft’s was a fence for thieves.”

“I am sure of it,” said I. “Go on.”

“This morning, just after I’d taken down the shutters, there comes in a lad—a ragged little fellow of twelve or thereabouts, with no more flesh on him than a sparrow in winter. He puts down upon the counter a silver watch, and asks what I’ll give him for it. I took it up to examine it, as is my custom, and I’ll confess to you, sir, that what I saw upon the back of it gave me a start.”

He fumbled in his pocket and produced the watch, which he laid upon the table between us. It was a handsome piece, of good English make, and upon the case was engraved a crest—a falcon with wings displayed, bearing in its beak a sprig of oak.

“That crest,” said the pawnbroker, “is the crest of the Ashcombe family—the Viscounts Ashcombe, of Ashcombe Hall in Devonshire. I know it because I was in service in that part of the country as a young man, and I’ve seen it on the gateposts and the carriage doors a hundred times. And what makes it worse, sir, is that I read in the papers, two years back, that the young lord—the only son—was drowned abroad. In Italy, I think it was. There was a great to-do about it at the time.”

“And the boy? What did he say?”

“He said, sir, that a gentleman had given it to him in the street—a gentleman in a black coat and a tall hat—for carrying a message. He was to take the message to an address written on a paper, and the watch was his payment. But when I asked him where the paper was, he said he had lost it, and when I asked him what was written on it, he said he could not read. He is a poor, half-witted lad, sir, by the look of him, and I do not think he was lying. He seemed frightened more than anything.”

“Have you the boy still?”

“I have, sir. I told him to wait in the back room while I came to find you. He is a timid creature, and I think he would have run if I had let him.”

“You did well,” said I, rising and taking down my hat. “Let us go to him at once.”

The pawnbroker’s shop was a dim, close place, smelling of old leather and stale tobacco, with a counter of polished oak and shelves crowded with the melancholy relics of other men’s necessities. In the back room, seated upon a broken chair, I found the boy.

He was, as Croft had said, a wretched little creature, with a pinched face and eyes that started at every sound. His clothes were a collection of rags held together by hope, and his boots were so large for him that they might have been made for a man. He shrank back as I entered, but I spoke to him quietly and sat down upon a stool at some little distance, so that he might not feel himself trapped.

“What is your name, my lad?” I asked.

“Tim,” said he, in a whisper. “Tim Nolan.”

“And where do you live, Tim?”

He gestured vaguely toward the river. “Anywhere,” said he. “Under the arches, mostly.”

“Now, Tim,” said I, “I want you to tell me about the gentleman who gave you the watch. When was it?”

“This morning, sir. Just after it got light. I was down by the fish market, looking for work, and he comes up to me—a tall gentleman, very upright, with a black coat and a tall hat. He says to me, ‘Boy, would you like to earn a silver watch?’ And I says, ‘Yes, sir.’ And he gives me the watch and a paper, and says I’m to take the paper to the address on it, and not to look at it, and not to lose it. And he gives me a shilling besides, and says there’ll be another when I come back.”

“And you lost the paper?”

The boy’s face twisted. “I didn’t mean to, sir. I was pushed in the crowd by the market, and when I looked, it was gone. I went back to where the gentleman was, but he was gone too. So I thought I’d sell the watch, and maybe he wouldn’t find out.”
