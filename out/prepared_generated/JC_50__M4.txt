I had held the office of harbour master at Tandjong Bèsi for the better part of eleven years, and in that time I had come to regard the river mouth and its shifting bars, its mudflats and the low green shoulder of the farther shore, with the dull proprietary affection a man gives to the furniture of a life he has not chosen. The appointment was not one of consequence. The port was small; the trade was chiefly in rattans and copra and the slow, sour business of the salt fish; and the colonial government, which governed at a distance and chiefly in the matter of forms, remembered my existence twice a year with a packet of regulations and a demand for returns. I was, in a manner of speaking, the law’s last outpost in a place where the law had little to do, and I had grown grey in the conviction that nothing would ever come up that river to trouble me.

It was on a Tuesday, I think, in the season when the west wind brings the rain in sheets and the air lies upon a man like a warm wet cloth, that the telegram came. It was from the Resident at Batavia—a curt, official thing, all abbreviation and haste, requiring me to refuse entry to any vessel flying no flag, and to report immediately the movements of such a vessel if sighted. There had been, it seemed, some unpleasantness on the coast farther north; a schooner had been running guns to the up-river people, and the government, having awakened late to the fact, was now in the temper of a man who slams the stable door. I read the message twice, and then I folded it and put it in my breast pocket and went out upon the verandah to look at the river.

The rain had ceased, and the evening was coming down with that sudden, almost theatrical completeness which is the despair of a European painter. The water was the colour of a sword-blade, and the sky above the far shore had the look of a bruise—a sullen, purplish, luminous thing that seemed to press upon the earth. The tide was making, and a smell of mud and vegetation and distant wood-smoke came up to me, and I stood there in the stillness, a man of fifty-five, with a bad liver and a good conscience, and I told myself that the telegram was a formality and that nothing would come of it.

It was three days later, at the very turn of the tide, that the vessel appeared. She came in from the northward, under a ragged foresail and a jib that flapped like a wounded bird, and she anchored in the roads without a gun, without a hail, without the least observance of the courtesies of the port. She flew no flag. I had my glass upon her from the moment she opened the point, and I saw her plainly—a brigantine of perhaps a hundred and fifty tons, her hull the colour of a dead leaf, her spars scraped and her rigging slack, the whole of her wearing that air of neglect which is worse than decay, for it speaks of a hand that has ceased to care. There was a man at the wheel and a couple of figures forward, but they moved slowly, and the boat that came ashore at dusk was pulled by a single pair of oars.

The letter was brought to me by a Malay boy who had the look of one who has been paid to forget what he has seen. He put it into my hand and was gone before I could question him. The envelope was of a coarse, yellowish paper, the sort one buys in the bazaar at Macassar, and it was sealed with a wafer, and it was addressed to me in a hand that I knew.

I have said that I had not seen my brother in twenty years. That is true. But a man’s hand is a thing that does not change; it is the very signature of his soul, and I had grown up with that hand before my eyes—on the fly-leaves of books, on the margins of newspapers, on the backs of envelopes that came from England in the days when we were boys together. It was a small, neat, upright hand, with a certain character in the formation of the capitals, and it had always seemed to me the hand of a man who knew his own mind. I stood there in the doorway of my office with the letter in my fingers and the lamp smoking at my elbow, and I felt the years go out of me like water out of a sieve.

I did not open it at once. I went out and walked upon the verandah, and I looked at the brigantine lying in the roads, and I thought of my brother. He was the elder by three years, and he had been the clever one, the one who was to go far; and I had been the dull one, the plodder, the one who was sent out to the East because there was nothing else to be done with him. We had not quarrelled—nothing so definite as a quarrel. There had been a matter of money, and a matter of a woman, and a matter of a promise that was not kept; and there had been, above all, that slow accumulation of small resentments which is the true substance of family estrangement. He had gone to sea. I had heard of him at Singapore, at Deli, at Thursday Island; and then I had heard nothing, and I had come to think of him as one thinks of a man who has died in a foreign country—with a certain relief, and a certain shame.
