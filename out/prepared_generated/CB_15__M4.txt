The news of my determination flew through the valley of Millcote on the wings of a rumour that spared neither the truth nor my reputation. I, Marian Halstead, ward of my uncle, Mr. Richard Halstead, master of Highbank Mill, had declared my intention to assume the management of the estate bequeathed to me by my late father—not merely to draw its rents, but to walk its fields, inspect its fences, hear the grievances of its tenants, and hold my own courts of audit in the great oak parlour of Whitestone Hall. I was one-and-twenty, and I had a will that my uncle was pleased to call ungovernable, and my neighbours, with that charity which distinguishes the provincial gentlefolk, were pleased to call unnatural.

The first to call upon me was the Reverend Mr. Ellwood, whose living my father had held in his gift, and who came, I am persuaded, as much from a genuine kindness of heart as from the curiosity that is the parson’s second nature. He found me in the library, with the estate books open before me, and the mud of the home-farm still upon my boots, for I had come in from a long walk of inspection and had not thought it necessary to change my raiment for his benefit.

“Miss Halstead,” said he, seating himself opposite and regarding me with a look in which amusement struggled with something like alarm, “I am told you mean to be your own steward.”

“You are told the truth, sir,” I replied. “I have a steward, it is true, one Mr. Garth, who is honest enough, but who has grown old in the belief that a woman’s understanding cannot compass a lease or a ledger. I mean to show him otherwise.”

“And your uncle?”

“My uncle,” I said, and here I felt the colour rise in my cheek, “my uncle has been good enough to tell me that I am mad, and that he will oppose me at every turn. He has threatened to write to my guardians in London. He has called me a fool and a vixen in the same breath, and has forbidden me his house—a prohibition which I have not obeyed, for I dined there on Tuesday.”

Mr. Ellwood laughed outright, a hearty, clerical laugh that did him credit.

“You are not mad, Miss Halstead,” said he, “but you are inconvenient. The world, you know, has a great dislike to inconvenient persons. It can tolerate a fool, for a fool can be led; it can tolerate a tyrant, for a tyrant can be obeyed; but a woman who thinks for herself and acts upon her thought—that, my dear young lady, is a species of rebellion against which all the powers of the shire will array themselves.”

“Let them array,” I cried, with a heat that I could not restrain. “I am not a child, Mr. Ellwood, to be frightened by a frown or a whisper. My father trusted me; he left me his land, and he left me his name, and I will not sit in a cushioned parlour and let his tenants be ground by an idle steward and his farms fall to ruin, for fear the gentry should say I am unwomanly.”

He looked at me a long moment, and his amusement gave place to a gravity that was not unkind.

“And what,” said he, “will you do when your uncle writes to the magistrates? When the farmers laugh in your face? When your own sex turn their backs upon you at the assembly? Will you still be so valiant?”

“I will,” I said. “And if I fail, I will fail in the doing of my duty, and not in the neglect of it. I had rather be called mad for managing my own affairs than be praised for sitting still while others mismanage them.”

He rose, and took his hat, and paused at the door.

“You will need a friend,” said he. “I am not a rich man, and I am not a powerful one, but I am the parson of this parish, and my voice counts for something. I will not fight your battles, but I will not desert you in them. And now, Miss Halstead, I will give you one piece of advice: your uncle is not your enemy so much as he is a man who fears to be thought a fool by his neighbours. Let him save his face, and you may yet save your estate.”

I thanked him, and he went out; and I sat down again to my books with a heart somewhat lighter, though I knew that the parson’s friendship was but a reed beside the oak of my uncle’s obstinacy.

That obstinacy was not long in showing itself. Within the week my uncle came down upon me in person, attended by his solicitor, a thin, dry man named Sowerby, who carried a bundle of papers as though they were the warrants of my execution. My uncle was a large, florid man, with a voice that had been used to command in the mill and the counting-house, and he had never in his life been contradicted by a woman, save by my aunt, who was dead, and who, it was whispered, had contradicted him once too often.

“Marian,” said he, without preface, “I have come to put an end to this folly. You will sign a power of attorney in favour of Sowerby here, and you will confine yourself to your needle and your books, as becomes a young woman of your station.”

“I will not sign it, uncle,” I said, and I was surprised at the steadiness of my own voice, for my heart beat hard against my ribs.

“You will not?” He grew purple. “You will not? Do you know what you are? You are a girl of one-and-twenty, without a husband, without a protector, without the least knowledge of the world, and you set yourself up against me—against the whole county—against nature itself!”
