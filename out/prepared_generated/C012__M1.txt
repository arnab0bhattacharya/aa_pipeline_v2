The gas lamp hissed its thin, constant complaint, casting long, dancing shadows that made the piles of clothes on the floor seem like sleeping beasts. Eleanor sat in the middle of the room, a small island in a sea of her mother’s past. The air was thick with the scent of lavender, cedar, and the faint, lingering ghost of a violet-scented powder. It was the smell of her mother, of the life that had filled this flat in Bloomsbury until a mere three weeks ago.

She was meant to be sorting. The task was a grim one, a necessary dismantling of a life. Wool skirts were to be folded for the poor box, silk blouses for her cousin in Manchester, and the rest… Eleanor did not yet know what to do with the rest. She held a heavy winter coat, the one her mother had called ‘the old bear’, its fur collar matted with years of London fog and damp. It was a formidable garment, dark and serious, and her mother had worn it as a sort of armour against the world. As Eleanor ran her hand along the thick, dark wool, her fingers brushed against something solid in the deep pocket.

It was not a handkerchief or a stray penny. It was a small rectangle of stiff card. She drew it out. A theatre ticket. The ink was a faded blue, the paper softened at the edges from a long, forgotten hibernation.

*The Lyceum Theatre*
*The Importance of Being Earnest*
*Stalls, Row G, Seat 14*
*Tuesday, 14th November, 1899*

Eleanor stared at it. 1899. The year before the turn of the century. The year she was born. She tried to place it, to slot it into the narrative of her mother’s life as she knew it. But her mother’s life, as she knew it, had begun with her own. There was a vague, sepia-toned ‘before’, of course – a girlhood in York, a train journey to London, a meeting with her father, Arthur. But these were anecdotes, polished with repetition, not memories. This ticket was something else. It was a fact, a solid anchor dropped in a sea of unknowns. November 1899. Eleanor would have been a babe in arms, or not yet born at all. She did the quick, frantic arithmetic in her head. Her birthday was in June. She had arrived in June 1900.

A Tuesday evening. Her mother, whom she had only ever known as ‘Mama’, a woman of quiet routines and sensible shoes, had been at the theatre. Alone? Or with her father? She tried to picture it. Arthur, with his neatly-trimmed moustache and his passion for railway timetables, sitting through a Wilde comedy. It did not fit. He would have found it frivolous, a waste of a good Tuesday.

The room seemed to shift around her. The piles of clothes were no longer just clothes; they were a landscape, and she was lost in it. She sank back against the side of the chaise longue, the ticket held carefully in her lap. She closed her eyes and let the ticket do its work. She imagined the woman who had owned it.

She was not ‘Mama’ then. She was Clara. A younger Clara, her hair perhaps still in the loose, fashionable style of the nineties, not yet pinned back in the severe, practical knot Eleanor knew. She would have worn something lovely for the theatre. Not this heavy coat, but something with a touch of colour, a hint of velvet at the collar. Her eyes, which in later life were clouded with a quiet weariness, would have been bright, alight with anticipation.

Eleanor imagined her standing under the gaslights outside the Lyceum, the grand entrance buzzing with the chatter of London’s theatre-goers. The air would have been cold, sharp with the smell of horses and coal smoke. Was she waiting for someone? Eleanor pictured a man, but not Arthur. This man was tall, with a laugh that was easy and a way of looking at Clara as if she were the only person in the bustling crowd. He would have taken her arm, his touch familiar and warm. They would have shared a joke as they went inside, a secret language that Eleanor could not decipher.

She let herself sink deeper into the fantasy. The plush red velvet of the Stalls, the low hum of the orchestra tuning up. Clara and her companion in Row G. Seat 14, and the one beside it. Perhaps he had bought the tickets on a whim, a surprise. Perhaps she had. Eleanor imagined her mother’s hand, younger and smoother than the one that had held hers through childhood fevers, resting on the arm of the seat. The lights would have dimmed, the curtain risen on Algernon’s drawing-room, and for the next two hours, Clara would have been lost in a world of wit and absurdity.

What would she have thought of it? The mother Eleanor knew had little time for nonsense. ‘Life is serious, Eleanor,’ she would say, her voice firm but kind. ‘It requires attention.’ But this Clara, this girl from 1899, had laughed at the triviality of life made earnest. Had she laughed out loud? A bright, clear sound, completely unlike her mother’s rare, quiet chuckle. Eleanor could almost hear it. The man beside her would have turned to her, his eyes full of admiration for her delight.

Afterwards, they would have spilled out into the Strand, breathless with laughter and talk. The play would have been the whole of their conversation. ‘A handbag!’ he might have said, and she would have laughed again. They would have walked, not wanting the evening to end, their footsteps echoing on the pavement. Maybe they found a small tearoom, still open, and he ordered hot chocolate for her. He would have looked at her over the rim of his cup, his gaze serious for a moment. What did he say to her? Did he tell her he loved her? Did they make plans for a future that Eleanor, sitting on the floor of her mother’s flat two decades later, could never know?
