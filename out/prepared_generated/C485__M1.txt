The telegraph machine in the stationmaster’s office at Modlin Junction spoke in a language of pure, unadorned fact. A click was a click. A pause was a pause. Piotr Volkov had built his life on this language. For twenty years, as the Tsar’s official in this windswept Polish outpost, he had measured his world in timetables, tonnages, and the precise, unyielding mathematics of track and signal. The Empire was a great machine, and he was one of its most reliable cogs. Politics were for men with time to waste on abstractions. His business was with the tangible: the groan of steel on steel, the hiss of steam, the scheduled arrival of the *Baltic Express* from Warsaw, bound for the frontier at Alexandrowo.

The message that arrived at 14:00 on Tuesday was, in its form, as fact-based as any other. It was a private cipher, one he and his brother had devised as boys, long before Nikolai had discovered the intoxicating world of ideas and Piotr had discovered the quiet satisfaction of order. The message read: *BALTIC EXPRESS, 19:15. STOP AT MODLIN. SIGNAL FAULT AT KM 42. HOLD UNTIL 20:00. NO EXCEPTIONS.*

Piotr laid the flimsy paper on his desk, next to the pristine leather-bound ledger of train movements. He read it again. The words were simple, operational. But they were a lie. There was no signal fault at kilometre 42. The line was as healthy as a Cossack’s horse. He knew this because he had personally inspected the relay box that morning. He looked out of his window at the skeletal gantries of the signal posts, standing like iron sentinels against the bruised twilight sky. They were his sentinels. They told the truth.

He knew, of course, what the *Baltic Express* carried today. Beyond the first-class passengers with their furs and French novels, and the third-class families crammed into wooden carriages, there was a special carriage, bolted and barred. It carried prisoner 734, a man named Orlov, a pamphleteer and rabble-rouser being sent to a fortress in Siberia. Piotr had read the security notice. A political. A man who dealt in the very abstractions Piotr despised.

And Nikolai… Nikolai dealt in nothing else. His brother, the university firebrand, the man who wrote essays that got him exiled to this same grey province, saw the world not as a machine to be maintained, but as a flawed engine to be dismantled and rebuilt. They had not spoken in a year, not since their last bitter argument over a samovar, where Nikolai had called Piotr a "functionary," a "soulless gear," and Piotr had called his brother a "romantic fool who would burn down the house to save a sparrow."

Now, the romantic fool was sending him an order.

The telegraph key clicked again, a frantic, insistent sound. It was Nikolai again. *TRUST ME. IT IS FOR THE GREATER GOOD.*

Piotr felt a cold knot form in his stomach. Greater good? His good was the 19:15 arriving at 19:15. His good was the unbroken chain of command, the sanctity of the schedule. To delay a state transport, a train carrying a prisoner of the Tsar, was not a minor infraction. It was treason. It was to take a wrench to the very machinery of the state. He could be stripped of his rank, his pension, his home. He could be sent to join Orlov in Siberia.

He thought of his wife, Anya, her quiet pride in his uniform. He thought of his small, ordered garden, the one place in the world where everything grew exactly as it should. He thought of the heavy, satisfying weight of his brass-cased pocket watch. A man’s life was a series of moments, and his had been a long, straight, predictable line. This order was a switch thrown, a diversion onto a dark, unknown track.

The station began to stir with the evening’s purpose. Porters wheeled luggage carts across the platform. The air grew thick with the smell of coal smoke and damp wool. Piotr walked the platform, his boots crunching on the cinders. He nodded to his staff, his face a mask of customary authority. He checked the water tower’s gauge, inspected a coupling on a waiting freight car, and listened to the distant, rhythmic chugging that announced the *Baltic Express* was nearing the junction. Each action was a small anchor in the storm of his thoughts.

He saw the train’s single, powerful headlamp cut through the gloom, a Cyclops eye in the mist. The great machine of steel and steam roared into the station, a living, breathing beast of iron. Brakes shrieked. Steam billowed in vast, white clouds, momentarily obscuring the faces of the waiting crowd. This was his world, a symphony of controlled power and precision.

As the train shuddered to a halt, Piotr felt a tremor in his own hands. He saw the special carriage, near the rear. It was unremarkable, save for the two armed guards standing on the platform beside it, their rifles held loosely. They were bored, complacent. They expected the twenty-minute stop for water and coal, a routine pause in an uneventful journey.

Piotr retreated to his office. The station clock on the wall ticked, each second a hammer blow against his composure. 19:20. 19:35. The guards would be getting restless. The train’s engineer would be wondering about the signal fault. He picked up the telegraph key. His fingers, usually so steady, felt clumsy, alien. He tapped out a message to the signal box at kilometre 42, a routine query. The response came back instantly. *ALL CLEAR. TRACK SECURE.*

The lie was now his to command.

He walked back out onto the platform, his shadow long and distorted in the gaslight. He approached the engineer, a grizzled man named Stefan with a face like a worn-out map.

“A problem, Comrade Stationmaster?” Stefan asked, wiping grease from his hands with a rag.

“A signal issue down the line,” Piotr said, the words feeling like ash in his mouth. “We have a hold order until 20:00.”
