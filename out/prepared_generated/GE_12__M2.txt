It is a peculiar and often dangerous propensity of the human mind to mistake the warmth of a self-flattering interpretation for the light of truth. Mrs. Eliza Vance, a widow of modest means and considerable intellectual vanity, had long considered herself immune to the common delusions of the sex, particularly those concerning the sincerity of men who paid court to her remaining capital. She possessed, indeed, a certain sharpness of observation, cultivated in the dry climate of provincial respectability, which she believed rendered her capable of seeing through the mask of hypocrisy. Yet, even the clearest lens may be clouded by the breath of the observer’s own desires; and Eliza, desiring to assert her independence of judgement against the chorus of her friends’ caution, found herself willingly blind to the discordant notes in the song of Mr. Arthur Prendgast.

Arthur Prendgast was a man of such polished urbanity that his charm seemed to possess an objective existence, independent of the will of the beholder. He was not merely handsome, for beauty is often a transient and superficial thing; he was agreeable, and agreement is the foundation of social pleasure. In the spa town of Bathwick, where the air was thick with the vapors of hot springs and the gossip of the season, Prendgast moved with the ease of a fish in water. He spoke of philosophy with a casual air that suggested deep learning without the stiffness of pedantry, and of art with a passion that was always tempered by good taste. When Eliza’s friends, Mrs. Gifford and her sister, Miss Harriet, whispered warnings of his nature, Eliza smiled with the superior pity of one who believes she sees the error in others rather than in herself.

“He is a man of the world, Eliza,” said Mrs. Gifford, her voice low and trembling with the anxiety of a woman who has seen too many marriages end in silence and estrangement. “He has no family, no property, and yet he moves in circles where money is the only currency that counts. Why should he care for a woman of fifty and a small estate, unless he has calculated the value of your independence?”

Eliza listened to this with a feeling of cold irritation. To be suspected of vanity was to be accused of a moral defect; and to be told that her choice was dictated by financial necessity was to be denied the agency of her own soul. She had married her first husband, the late Mr. Vance, because he was sensible and steady, and the marriage had been a comfortable one, though not a passionate one. Now, in her widowhood, she felt a sudden hunger for the recognition of her own worth, not as a keeper of accounts or a manager of a household, but as a person of intellect and charm. Prendgast looked at her as if she were a masterpiece he had finally found in a dusty corner; and this gaze, more than any words, sealed her fate.

The marriage took place in London, in a quiet church in Mayfair, where the flowers were white and the crowd was small. It was a union that seemed, to all outward appearances, a triumph of reason over prejudice. Prendgast was chaste in his speech, economical in his habits, and attentive to Eliza’s every preference. He praised her reading, her opinions on the new novels, and her management of their modest affairs. For the first three months, Eliza felt a profound sense of vindication. Her friends’ fears had been baseless; Arthur was not a fortune hunter, but a true companion, a man who valued her mind as much as her purse. They dined at the best tables, attended lectures, and walked in the parks with a dignity that drew the envious glances of lesser couples.

But the human heart is a complex mechanism, and the springs that drive it are not always visible to the eye. It was only when the initial flush of marriage had faded into the routine of daily life that the cracks began to show. It was not through any dramatic act of violence or betrayal, but through a series of small, quiet shifts in Prendgast’s behavior. He began to criticize the management of the household, not with anger, but with a weary sigh that suggested Eliza was incapable of handling even the simplest matters. He suggested, gently but firmly, that their current residence in Kensington was too damp for his health, and that they should move to a smaller, cheaper house in a more rural part of the country, where the air was purer and the expenses lower.

Eliza was surprised by the proposal. They had enough money to live comfortably in London; indeed, Prendgast had never complained of their means. But now, he spoke of economy as a virtue, of simplicity as a necessity. He stopped visiting the clubs where he had once been a frequent guest; he ceased to attend the opera; he even became less attentive to Eliza’s conversations, his eyes often drifting to the window or the fire, as if he were calculating the cost of the coal.

It was in the autumn of their first year that the truth began to dawn, not with a sudden shock, but with the slow, painful clarity of a diagnosis. Eliza found a letter in Prendgast’s writing desk, a letter he had written to a lawyer in London. It was not a love letter, nor a confession of guilt, but a dry, legal document concerning the settlement of a debt. It revealed that Prendgast had not been born to a state of indigent gentility, as he had claimed, but was the son of a bankrupt tradesman, and that he had been living on the proceeds of a series of small, speculative investments that had failed. More significantly, it revealed that he had taken out a policy on his own life, naming Eliza as the beneficiary, and that he had been pressing his family for a small sum to keep up the appearance of respectability.
