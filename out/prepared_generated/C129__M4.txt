The invitation had arrived on paper so thick it felt like a slice of tanned hide, and for three days Elias Vogler had done nothing but stare at the imperial seal and calculate the odds of his own destruction. Now, on the twelfth of November, in the year seventeen hundred and ninety-four, he stood in the great hall of the Residenz at Auerbach and watched a footman in scarlet livery arrange the last of the candles around the cabinet that housed his Turk.

The automaton was a handsome thing, he would grant himself that. Life-sized and seated cross-legged on a lacquered chest, its turbaned head inclined in permanent contemplation, its robe of crimson silk embroidered with silver thread that caught the candlelight and threw it back at the assembled courtiers like a challenge. Its right hand hovered above the board. Its left held a pipe it would never smoke. Vogler had spent nine months building it and four years learning to make it appear to think. The court, he suspected, would give it ten minutes to prove itself a wonder or a fraud.

“Herr Vogler.” The chamberlain materialized at his elbow, a thin man named Kessler whose face suggested he had smelled something unpleasant in every room he had ever entered. “His Serene Highness will receive the exhibition in the west gallery at eight. You will demonstrate three games. You will not speak unless spoken to. You will not approach the dais.”

“Of course,” Vogler said, and bowed, and tried to remember how to breathe.

The west gallery was colder than the hall, its tall windows rattling with a wind that had come down from the Harz. The court assembled in a crescent of powdered wigs and jeweled orders: the Prince-Bishop himself, Leopold Kasimir, a heavy man with the weary eyes of someone who had been told yes too often; his nephew and heir, a young count with a face like a spoiled pear; a scattering of generals and canons and wives. At the center of the crescent, on a table draped in green baize, the chessboard waited.

Vogler wheeled the cabinet into position. He opened the doors at the front, turned the crank that rotated the interior on its axis, and let the court peer into the clockwork: brass gears, iron rods, a complexity of springs that meant nothing at all. The secret was not in the machinery. The secret was in the boy.

His name was Anton, and he was fourteen years old, and he was folded into the cabinet’s rear compartment like a letter into an envelope, his knees against his chest, his eye at the small grated aperture that let him see the board through the Turk’s torso. He had been a chimney sweep’s apprentice when Vogler found him, small enough to fit and clever enough to beat the parish priest at chess three times running. He was also mute, which Vogler had initially considered a gift from God and now understood to be a kind of judgment. Anton could not cry out, could not betray them with a word. But he could look at Vogler through the grate, and in four years Vogler had learned to read that look the way other men read scripture.

The first opponent was the young count, who played with the aggressive stupidity of a man who had never lost a game to anyone but his tutor. Anton dispatched him in twenty-three moves. The court murmured. The Prince-Bishop smiled for the first time that evening.

The second opponent was a general of hussars, a lean, scarred man named von Reidel who played cautiously and well. Anton took forty-one moves to beat him, and Vogler, standing beside the cabinet with his hand on the crank, felt the familiar cold sweat prickling his collar. The general’s queen had nearly trapped them in the middle game. Anton had found a sacrifice Vogler himself would have missed. The boy was better than he had been last year. The boy was better than Vogler had ever been.

“Remarkable,” the Prince-Bishop said, and the word went through Vogler like a knife.

The third opponent was the Prince-Bishop himself. His Serene Highness played with the languid confidence of a man who expected the universe to arrange itself around his convenience, and for the first fifteen moves it did. Then Anton began to squeeze. A pawn here, a knight there, a slow constriction of the board that had the courtiers leaning forward and the young count’s face going pink with secondhand humiliation. Vogler watched the Prince-Bishop’s fingers drum the table. He watched the man’s jaw tighten. He watched the moment, somewhere around move thirty, when the Prince-Bishop realized he was going to lose in front of his entire court, and the realization did not sit well on him.

Anton made the move that sealed it—a quiet rook slide that Vogler knew, from the pattern of the boy’s play, was the beginning of the end—and the Prince-Bishop sat back in his chair.

“Herr Vogler,” he said. “A word.”

The courtiers drew back. Vogler approached the dais and bowed so low his spectacles slid down his nose. The Prince-Bishop’s voice was pleasant. That was the worst part.

“You have built a marvel,” he said. “I have never seen its like. But I find myself curious about the nature of its intelligence. Does it think, Herr Vogler? Or does it merely perform?”

“It calculates, Your Serene Highness,” Vogler said. “It weighs the positions. It selects the best move available to it.”

“And who taught it to weigh? Who taught it to select?”

“I did, Your Serene Highness. Over many years.”

The Prince-Bishop nodded slowly. His eyes were not weary now. They were bright and flat and patient, like a cat’s. “Then you will understand my next request,” he said. “I wish to purchase the automaton. Name your price. I will pay it.”
