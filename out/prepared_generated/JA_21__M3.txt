Among the numerous advantages which the village of Chaddleworth in Hampshire possessed over the neighbouring parishes, was not the least the fact that it contained but one family of consequence, and therefore could be entirely free from those rivalries and jealousies which so frequently disturb the tranquility of a small neighbourhood. The Mississons of Chaddleworth Park had long held this distinction, and the present representative of the family, Mrs. Mississon, was a woman who understood the value of consequence and had no intention of diminishing it by any imprudence of conduct.

Her eldest daughter, Clara, had been for two years engaged to Mr. Edward Wyncote, a man of unexceptionable character, whose estate at nearby Bramley St. John was moderate but well-managed, and whose income, though not large, was such as to promise comfort, if not splendour. The match had been arranged with the full approbation of Mrs. Mississon, who had considered Mr. Wyncote’s steady habits and respectable connections a sufficient compensation for his lack of vivacity. Clara, who had been but nineteen at the time of the engagement, had received the addresses of Mr. Wyncote with a composure which her mother interpreted as prudence, and which her sister Margaret, a girl of sixteen with more observation than experience, privately suspected was indifference.

Mr. Wyncote’s circumstances, however, were such as to require him to wait for the living of Bramley, which was then in the gift of his uncle, a Baronet of some sixty years, whose health was spoken of in terms which encouraged expectation without producing satisfaction. The engagement, therefore, had been long, and during its progress, Clara had found occasion to observe her lover with a degree of attention which had not, perhaps, been bestowed upon him in the first warmth of their acquaintance.

It was on a fine morning in April that Clara found herself walking alone in the shrubbery at Chaddleworth Park, her mind occupied by reflections which had grown too troublesome to be dismissed. Mr. Wyncote had called the preceding evening, as he called every Tuesday and Friday, and had sat for the usual hour in the drawing-room, conversing with Mrs. Mississon upon the price of wheat, the health of his uncle, and the prospect of rain before the week was out. Clara, who had sat opposite him, had found her eye wandering to the window, and had caught herself wishing that he would say something — she did not know what — which might distinguish this visit from the twenty visits which had gone before it.

She was not so unreasonable as to require perpetual entertainment. She knew that Mr. Wyncote was good, and she had been taught, by a mother who valued goodness above all other qualities, that a woman who could not be happy with a good man did not deserve to be happy at all. Yet she could not but feel, as she walked between the damp borders, that there was a deficiency somewhere — a deficiency not in Mr. Wyncote’s conduct, which was everything it ought to be, but in her own heart, which refused to respond with the warmth that his devotion merited.

She had attempted, more than once, to convince herself that this was the natural cooling of affection which followed upon long engagement, and that when they were married, and settled in the parsonage at Bramley, she would find contentment in the performance of her duties. But contentment, she feared, was not happiness; and she was not certain that she wished to exchange the remainder of her youth for a composure which resembled nothing so much as the composure she already possessed.

She was interrupted in these reflections by the appearance of Margaret, who came towards her with a letter in her hand and an expression of some importance.

“Mama wishes you to come in,” said Margaret. “Mr. Wyncote has written, and Mama thinks you ought to see the letter directly.”

Clara took the letter and opened it. It was, as she had expected, a communication of the most regular and unexceptionable kind. Mr. Wyncote wrote to inform her that his uncle had been taken ill on the previous Sunday, that the physician had been called, and that there was now every reason to suppose the living of Bramley would be vacant within the year. He expressed himself, throughout, with a steadiness which did credit to his feelings, and concluded by requesting permission to wait upon Mrs. Mississon the following morning, in order that the arrangements for the marriage might be finally settled.

Clara read the letter twice, and then stood in silence, looking at the spring flowers which had begun to appear along the border, as though she expected them to offer some counsel which her own mind could not supply.

“Well?” said Margaret, who had been watching her with a curiosity which she did not think it necessary to conceal. “Is it not what you wished?”

“I do not know what I wished,” said Clara; and then, recollecting herself, added, with a composure which she was far from feeling, “It is, of course, exactly what was to be desired. Mr. Wyncote writes with his usual — correctness.”

Margaret said nothing, but regarded her sister with an expression which, had Mrs. Mississon observed it, would have been thought impertinent.

Clara returned to the house, and delivered the letter to her mother, who received it with a satisfaction which was, perhaps, the more complete for being unaccompanied by any display of tenderness. Mrs. Mississon was a woman who had long since determined that feeling was a luxury which could not be indulged without some sacrifice of judgment, and she had trained herself to regard all events with an equanimity which her acquaintance mistook for coldness.
