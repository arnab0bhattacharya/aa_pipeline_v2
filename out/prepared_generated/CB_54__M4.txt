The lecture had been long, and the room was over-warm, and I was glad to gain the street again, though the air smote me like a blade after the close vapour within. It was a clear, bitter night in late January, and the city—which I shall call M—lay under a thin, hard crust of snow that grated beneath the foot and glittered where the lamps were set. I had come abroad to teach English to the daughters of a merchant family, and I had come to the lecture because the orator was famous, and because I was lonely, and because a woman must do something with her evenings besides darn stockings and read the same three books over again. The orator had spoken of liberty and the dignity of labour, and had spoken well, though with a fluency that I mistrusted; but I will not pretend that I remember his periods now, for what befell me at the door put them all out of my head.

The crowd was thick in the vestibule, and I was buttoning my cloak with fingers stiffened by the sudden cold, when a voice at my elbow said my name.

I turned, and there stood Henry Ashworth.

He was altered, and yet not altered. He had been a thin, eager, palefaced young man when I knew him in England, with a habit of flushing when he spoke to me, and a certain awkwardness of the hands which had used to provoke my impatience. Now he was filled out, and there was a firmness in his bearing, and his coat was of good cloth, and his watch-chain was of gold. But the eyes were the same—grey, steady, and a little sad—and the mouth was the same, though it had learned to close with resolution. He held out his hand, and I gave him mine, and we stood looking at one another while the people jostled past us into the night.

“You are the last person I expected,” said I, when I had found my voice. “I thought you were in the West Indies.”

“I was,” said he. “I returned in the autumn. I am in business here now—a partner in a house that trades in indigo and coffee. I have been here three months, and I had not the good fortune to meet you till tonight.”

“I have been here since September,” said I. “I teach. I am governess to the daughters of Mr. Voss, the merchant.”

“I know the name,” said he. “A respectable house.”

There was a pause. The vestibule was emptying, and the lamplight fell cold and yellow on the stone floor. I felt that I must say something, and I said the first thing that came into my head, which was the worst thing I could have said.

“You are married,” said I. “I heard it.”

“Yes,” said he, quietly. “I married four years since. My wife is here with me. She is at home tonight—she does not care for lectures. She is a good woman, and we have two children.”

“I am glad,” said I. And I was glad, though the words sounded cold. “I hope you are happy.”

“I am content,” said he. “Content is a better word than happy, and it wears better. But you—you have not married?”

“No,” said I. “I have not married.”

He looked at me with a certain grave inquiry, not impertinent, but searching, as if he would read the answer to a question he had asked himself long ago. I met his look without flinching, for I had nothing to be ashamed of, and I would not have him think that I repented of anything.

“I remember,” said he, “a certain evening in the garden at Wrenfield. You were standing by the sundial, and I asked you a question, and you answered it.”

“I remember,” said I.

“You were very young,” said he.

“I was one-and-twenty,” said I. “I was old enough to know my own mind.”

“And you knew it?”

“I knew it,” said I. “I told you the truth, and I have never had cause to think it was not the truth. I did not love you, Henry. I could not have married you without a lie, and I would not begin a life upon a lie.”

He was silent a moment, and then he said, in a low voice: “I thought my heart would break. I was a fool, and I was proud, and I went away because I could not bear to see you. I have thought of it often since, and I have come to see that you did me a kindness—the only kindness that was possible. If you had said yes out of pity, we should both have been miserable.”

“I hope so,” said I. “I mean—I hope you have found it so.”

“I have found it so,” said he. “My wife is not you, and I do not expect her to be. She is patient, and she is kind, and she has given me a home. I have no complaint to make of Providence.”

The cold was biting through my cloak, and I shivered, and he saw it.

“You are cold,” said he. “You must not stand here. Let me walk with you to your door.”

“No,” said I. “I thank you, but I would rather walk alone. It is not far.”

“It is not fit that you should walk alone at this hour.”

“I am accustomed to it,” said I. “I have walked alone in worse streets than these, and I have learned to carry my own defence. Besides, your wife will be expecting you.”

He looked at me then with a strange expression—half pain, half admiration—and I saw that he would have liked to persist, but that he knew me too well.

“You are as you were,” said he. “You will have your own way.”

“I will have my own way,” said I, “when it is right that I should. I am not a child, Henry, and I am not a fool. Go home to your wife.”
