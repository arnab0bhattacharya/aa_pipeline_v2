The sea was the colour of dull tin this morning, heaving with a sluggish, oily regularity that seemed to Eleanor, looking out from the window of the lodging-house, to be keeping time with the slow, pendulous swing of her own thoughts. She had been sitting there for some minutes, her fingers resting on the rough, darned lace of the curtain, watching the grey water merge into the grey sky, when the children burst in, scattering the quiet like a handful of stones thrown into a still pond. They were Cecilia’s children—Cecilia, who was her sister, and who was downstairs now, negotiating with the landlady about the cost of the children’s dinners. Eleanor watched them tear across the threadbare carpet, a whirlwind of sandy knees and shrieking voices, and she felt, as she always felt in their presence, a sort of dizzying detachment, as if she were watching a play from the very back of a theatre.

Cecilia came up the stairs heavily, her face flushed from the heat of the kitchen fire, carrying the baby on her hip. She looked, Eleanor thought, exactly as she had always looked, only worn thinner, stretched over the frame of her life until the bones of her cheekbones seemed to be pushing through the skin. She sat down on the edge of the bed, and the baby immediately began to pull at the collar of her blouse, tearing it open to reveal a stretch of white, tired throat.

“I’ve told them they must have the fish,” Cecilia said, her voice breathless, as if she had been running. “They won’t eat it, of course, but I’ve told them. The girl says she can do no eggs, which is ridiculous, but I hadn’t the energy to fight her. Eleanor, do pull Tommy away from the window, he’ll fall out.”

Eleanor turned, but without urgency. She watched Tommy, who was leaning dangerously over the sill, his small, grubby fingers prising at the putty, and she thought that if he fell, he would fall through the grey air and into the grey water, and that it would be a sort of completion, a final merging of the child with the element that he had been trying to reach all week. But she pulled him back, gently, by the collar of his sailor suit, and he screamed, and the baby began to cry, and Cecilia, still sitting on the bed, let her head drop into her hands with a gesture of such profound exhaustion that Eleanor felt a sudden, sharp stab of pity, which was immediately followed by something else, something she could not name at first, but which she recognized, after a moment, as a sort of relief.

She did not envy Cecilia. She looked at her sister’s bent head, at the grey streak running through her dark hair, at the baby tugging at her breast, and she felt nothing but a vast, clear, luminous sense of her own freedom. She had nothing. She was forty-two, and she had nothing—no husband, no children, no house with a garden and a laburnum tree, no table to lay for supper, no fire to keep lit. She had only her rooms in London, and her books, and her long, solitary walks, and the feeling, which came to her sometimes in the evening, that she was a sort of vessel, empty and transparent, into which the world poured itself, filling her with light and sound and colour, so that she was never quite alone, never quite empty.

Cecilia, meanwhile, was looking at the baby’s hand, which was clenched around her finger, and she was thinking of Eleanor, who was standing by the window with her hands in her pockets, looking out at the sea with that expression of detached, serene absorption which Cecilia had always found so beautiful, and so enviable. She thought of Eleanor’s life in London, of the rooms she had seen once, with their high ceilings and their tall windows looking out over the square, and the books piled on every chair and table, and the flowers in a blue jar on the mantelpiece. She thought of Eleanor waking up in the morning, alone, in her narrow white bed, and lying there for a moment, in the silence, before she got up and drew the curtains and let the morning in. No one would pull at her. No one would cry. She would get up and dress slowly, and drink her tea, and read her letters, and go out into the street, and the whole day would stretch before her, empty and shining, like a long, straight road with no turning.

Cecilia felt a sudden, violent longing, like a physical pain, to be Eleanor. To be unmarried. To be free. To have her body to herself again, and her mind, which was now so fragmented, so scattered among the children and the house and the endless, round of small, domestic duties, that she could not remember, when she woke in the night, who she was, or what she had been before she became Mrs. Arthur Fenwick. She looked at Eleanor, who was standing very still, with the light falling on her face, and she thought: she has kept herself. She is whole. And I am broken, and scattered, and lost.

The children were playing on the floor now, building a fort out of cushions, and their voices rose and fell, filling the room with a sound like the sea, which was outside, grey and heaving, and which went on, and on, and on, regardless of the small human lives that were being lived out on its shore.

“They’re happy,” Eleanor said, turning from the window, and her voice was gentle, with a sort of wonder in it, as if she were speaking of creatures of another species.
