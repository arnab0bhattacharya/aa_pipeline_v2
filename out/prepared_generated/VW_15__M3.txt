The morning had discharged itself with a peculiar brightness, a sharp, cutting clarity that seemed to slice the very air of Bloomsbury into planes of trembling light, so that Miss Eleanor Vance, stepping from her lodgings in Torrington Avenue, felt herself suddenly exposed, as if the shabby brown coat she had worn for seven winters might turn transparent under such a sky and reveal not merely the thin shoulders beneath, but the entire intricate, closely guarded machinery of her spinsterhood. She held her bag rather tight, for there was the letter, the letter that had arrived on Tuesday—no, it was Wednesday now, and today was Friday—and still lay unopened, a thick, cream-laid envelope bearing the Sussex postmark and the heavy, black, slightly smudged handwriting of her sister, Dorothea. It was not that she feared news of disaster; it was rather that Dorothea’s letters always carried with them the whole suffocating atmosphere of the grey house by the sea, the smell of damp plaster, the relentless ticking of the grandfather clock in the hall, and the perpetual, unspoken question—why Eleanor, who had been so clever, who had taken the scholarship, should now be teaching Latin to girls in a London day-school, and living in a single room upon a pittance.

But it was her day off. She repeated the phrase, letting it fall into the rhythm of her boots upon the pavement, her day off, as though the subtraction of duty might alter the very chemical composition of the air she breathed. She walked south, past the British Museum, whose massive portico seemed to frown down upon the scattered papers and orange peels of the gutter, thinking how odd it was that the dead, embalmed in there behind those columns, should demand such silence, such reverence, when the living, pushing past her in their dark coats, their hats pulled down, seemed to carry their own their faces such a far more profound and terrible silence. She thought of the girls. They would be in the middle school now, bending over their Caesar. Forty girls, forty smooth, unmarked foreheads bent over the desks, the scratching of their nibs in the exercise books, and Miss Sims, the mathematics teacher, rapping on the blackboard with that little wooden mallet. Would they notice she was gone? Perhaps Arabella Thorne might notice—Arabella, with her sudden, startling questions that broke the surface of the grammar lesson like a fish leaping in a still pond, bringing up from the depths something mud-coloured and living, something that made Eleanor flush and hesitate, feeling that she was not teaching them language at all, but merely the dry, hollow shell of an empire, the bones of a dead thing from which the flesh had long since rotted. Arabella, she thought, would be the one to open the letter, to tear it open with a kind of savage, impatient joy, but Eleanor, turning into the great expanse of Trafalgar Square, felt the envelope press against her side like a stone, a flat, oblong stone that she must carry to the top of a mountain before she could set it down.

The fountains were playing. She stood for a moment by the edge of the basin, watching the water rise and fall, rise and fall, the spray catching the light and breaking into a thousand transient, iridescent fragments, each one a tiny, complete world of colour that vanished the instant it was born. The lions upon their pedestals seemed to her, today, less like monuments of empire and more like great, worn, domesticated beasts, their stone manes smoothed by the weather of a hundred winters into something resembling the texture of an old, much-washed blanket. She passed them, climbing the shallow steps, and entered the National Gallery.

The hush of the galleries was different from the hush of the museum; it was drier, thinner, scented with the faintly acrid smell of floor polish and the dust that gathered forever in the ornate frames. She moved past the earlier rooms, past the rigid saints with their gilded, staring eyes, feeling no desire to stop, for the religious fervour of those painted faces seemed to demand a surrender she was unwilling to give; she had surrendered enough, she thought, to the timetable, the syllabus, the end-of-term reports, the little, daily, grinding humiliations of being a woman of thirty-eight with no private means and no protectors. She walked on, her footsteps echoing softly on the polished boards, until she came to the room she was seeking, and there, upon the far wall, was the painting.

It was a small picture, a landscape; a river winding through a flat green country under a wide, luminous sky, and in the distance, a church spire, and a group of trees, their foliage painted with such a tender, trembling precision that one could almost feel the wind that stirred those leaves, the particular, gentle, salt-laden wind of a coastal afternoon. The paint was thin in places, and the brown ground showed through, giving the greens a mellow, autumnal warmth, and the sky, which was not the blue of the south but the pale, watery, luminous grey of the north, seemed to hold a light that was not the light of the sun but rather the light of memory itself, a light that illuminated nothing from without but seemed to glow from within, as if the whole painted surface were a window looking out upon a world that had ceased to exist, except in the mind of the artist, and now, perhaps, in the mind of the woman who stood before it.

Eleanor stood. She stood for a long time. The people came and went behind her; a gentleman in a grey suit paused, adjusted his spectacles, and moved on; a party of schoolgirls—no, they were not schoolgirls, they were shop-girls, with their bright blouses and their chatter—swept past in a gust of scent and laughter. But Eleanor did not move. She was looking at the river.
