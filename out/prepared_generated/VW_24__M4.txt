The trouble with Florence—she had begun to think this on the third day, and by the tenth it had hardened into something like a conviction—was that the light did not permit one to be ordinary. It lay upon the yellow walls and the shutters and the foolish little towers of the villa as if every surface had been waiting all morning to be looked at; and the people who lived in that light, the English who had come for it and stayed for it and now could not imagine themselves anywhere else, seemed to her to have caught the habit of the sun, and to require, always, an audience. They arranged themselves. They arranged each other. Even their silences, she noticed, were arranged.

Her sister Margaret had married into it, or rather had married a man who had brought her into it, and Margaret now wore her days like a gown cut a little too low, laughing at the things that were laughed at, and looking, when the talk went over her head, not bewildered but brilliant, brilliantly attentive, as though she had understood and forgiven. It was this look that Clara could not bear, and it was this look, she supposed, that made her unkind; for she had come out from England with her boxes and her good intentions and her rather too thorough reading of Ruskin, and had expected—what had she expected?—olive trees, and the graves of poets, and conversations in which the soul was taken out and examined in the evening air like a piece of old lace.

Instead there was Mr. Alaric Fenn, who was a poet, and who said at table, with his eyes half closed, that he could not endure the sight of a woman eating a peach; there was Mrs. Cora Fenn, his wife, who painted, and who said that Alaric’s nerves were the most delicate instrument in Europe and must be protected at all costs; there was a young man called Bellingham who wrote for the reviews and had a laugh like a knife drawn quickly out of its sheath; and there was Miss Trelawny, who was very rich and very thin and went about with a little dog and a little grievance, and who had once, it was said, been beautiful, and who now exacted from every new person an opinion of her beauty, so that one learned to say, before one had been five minutes in the room, what a tragedy it was that such a face should be wasted on a woman of—and here one lowered one’s voice—forty.

They were kind to Clara, that was the dreadful part. They were kind to her as one is kind to a child who has been brought in to be shown and will presently be sent to bed. They asked her what she thought of the Campanile, and before she could answer told her what they thought of it; they asked her whether she had read this or that, and before she could answer told her what to think. And they were so quick, so glittering, that she found herself laughing at things she did not find funny, and agreeing to things she did not believe, and once—this was at the Fenns’ on the evening of the peach—she heard her own voice saying that of course truth was only a matter of the arrangement of words, and had been so startled by it that she had put down her glass too sharply on the table, and everyone had looked at her, and Bellingham had said, “Ah, Miss Clara is the only honest one among us; she believes in truth; she is going to be a saint,” and they had all laughed, and she had laughed too, and had gone to bed with a headache and a sense of having been robbed.

It was on the afternoon of the following Sunday that she met the one who was neither. He had come in late, when the tea was already cold and the talk was at its highest, and had taken a chair a little apart, near the window where the light was going, and had sat there with his hands folded, not looking at anyone in particular. His name was Henry Ashworth, and he was a painter, though no one, she noticed, asked him about his painting; and when Mrs. Fenn said, in her bright way, “Henry, you are dreadfully silent; tell us something wicked,” he had answered, “I have been looking at the hill,” and had said nothing more.

The hill, beyond the window, was going from green to violet as the sun sank; and Clara, who had been about to say something clever, found that she had forgotten it, and looked at the hill instead, and then at him. He was not handsome. He was rather heavy, and his coat was not well cut, and there was a little dust on his shoes, as though he had walked a long way. But his face had the quality—she searched for it afterwards, lying awake, and could only call it this—of a room in which no one is arranging anything. He was not performing. He was not, she felt, even thinking about how he appeared to be thinking. And when the talk rose again, and Bellingham said something brilliant and cruel about a woman they all knew, and everyone laughed, and Clara laughed too, because one did, Ashworth did not laugh; he looked out of the window; and she thought—how absurd, how childish—that he had not heard, that he was somewhere else, on the hill perhaps, among the olives, in the violet light.

After that she found herself, at the Fenns’ and at the Bellinghams’ and at the villa where Miss Trelawny kept her dog and her grievance, watching for him; and when he was there she was quiet, and when he was not there she was clever, and it seemed to her that she was two people, and that only one of them was real.
