The Rev. Mr. Cornelius Ashby, Vicar of Steepleford, in the county of Dorset, had never in the course of his life travelled farther than Salisbury, and the journey to London—a hundred and seven miles, as he was careful to inform every acquaintance—was an adventure of no small magnitude. His object was a petition to the Bishop of London concerning the disposal of a small preferment in his neighbourhood, in which he had a modest interest; his means were a single horse and a borrowed valise; and his introduction to the metropolis was his cousin, Mrs. Honoria Fitzhugh, of Wimpole Street, who had, in the kindness of her heart, or possibly in the curiosity of it, invited him to pass a fortnight under her roof.

Mrs. Fitzhugh was a widow of independent fortune and considerable fashion, who kept a neat establishment, a neat opinion of herself, and a small but select circle of acquaintance, whom she was in the habit of entertaining with a fortnightly supper and cards. She had not seen her cousin since they were both children in Devonshire, and she remembered him chiefly as a boy with ink on his fingers and a tiresome habit of asking questions. The prospect of his visit she regarded rather as a species of rural exhibition than as a family duty, and she had prepared her friends for it accordingly.

“He is a clergyman, you must know,” she observed to Lady Mowbray, on the evening of his arrival, “and quite the country clergyman. He will talk to you of turnips, I daresay, and of the poor of his parish, and he has a coat which I am persuaded was made in the reign of the last king but one. One must be charitable. He is my cousin, after all.”

Mr. Ashby entered the drawing-room at the hour appointed, and was found to be a plain, spare gentleman of about forty, with a countenance in which good humour and good sense contended for the mastery, and a manner which, though wholly unacquainted with the modes of the metropolis, had yet nothing in it either servile or assuming. He bowed to the company with a cordiality which made Lady Mowbray smile, and took the chair assigned him with a comfortable air of being pleased to be seated.

“And how do you find London, Mr. Ashby?” said Lady Mowbray, with the condescension of one who had never lived anywhere else.

“Very large, madam,” replied he, “and very loud. I confess I had not conceived that so many carriages could contrive to be going in so many directions at once, and all of them apparently upon the point of running over one another. I have walked out twice, and each time I have returned with a new respect for the inhabitants, who seem to possess a sixth sense for the avoiding of horses.”

“We think nothing of it,” said Lady Mowbray, who did not know what to make of a man who could speak of his own ignorance without apology.

“That is the great secret of London, I believe,” said Mr. Delacourt, a young gentleman of elegant leisure, who stood by the fireplace with an air of refined weariness. “We think nothing of anything. It is the only way to live.”

“Then I am afraid I must be very unfit for London,” said Mr. Ashby, smiling, “for I have spent my life in thinking a great deal of very little.”

“And what,” said Mrs. Fitzhugh, with a glance at Lady Mowbray, “do you think of in the country, cousin? I have often wondered.”

“I think of my sermon, ma’am, when I ought to be thinking of my dinner; and of my dinner, I am afraid, when I ought to be thinking of my sermon. And I think of my parishioners, who are fifty-seven souls, including the infants, and who have a way of requiring my attention at the very moment I had designed for my own. I assure you, there is no want of occupation in a country parish. It is only the occupation which is unfashionable.”

“Fifty-seven souls!” cried Mrs. Fitzhugh. “Good heavens, cousin, you speak as if you had them numbered and labelled.”

“I have them in a book, ma’am, with their births and their marriages and their burials, and I could tell you the name of every one of them, and the greater part of their dispositions. It is the only book I have ever written, and I confess I am rather proud of it.”

There was a general smile at this, which Mr. Ashby bore with perfect composure, and the conversation was turning to other matters, when Mr. Delacourt, who had been watching him with the amused attention which a man of fashion bestows upon a novelty, observed, “And pray, Mr. Ashby, what is your opinion of the Bishop? You are come to see him, I collect.”

“I am come to petition him, sir, upon a matter of small consequence to any but myself and one other person; and I shall account it a great happiness if I am permitted to see him at all.”

“They say he is difficult of access,” said Lady Mowbray.

“I have heard so, madam; but I have likewise heard that he is a good man, and I have found in my own small experience that good men are seldom so difficult of access as their porters.”

“You are a philosopher, cousin,” said Mrs. Fitzhugh, with that air of pleasantry which is designed to keep a person in his place.

“I am a clergyman, ma’am,” said he, “and a clergyman who has visited the sick and buried the dead in a country village for eighteen years has learned at least to distinguish between the things which are worth troubling himself about and those which are not. I assure you, I have no philosophy beyond that.”
