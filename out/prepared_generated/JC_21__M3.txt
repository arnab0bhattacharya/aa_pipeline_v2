The heavy curtains of the drawing room in Mount Street admitted a light that seemed not so much to illuminate as to suffocate. It was a Mayfair light, filtered through a persistent drizzle that had settled over the city, a grey and faintly luminous haze that lay upon the silken upholstery and the tarnished gilding of the furniture with the weight of an established, ancient melancholy. Into this atmosphere, which smelled of dried potpourri, wax flowers, and the indescribable scent of money kept too long in the dark, Mr. Julius Vassili had been ushered by a footman of an impassiveness so profound it might have been mistaken for a mechanical defect.

Vassili stood before the marble mantelpiece, his hands clasped behind his back, his head inclined slightly forward as though listening to a sound in the very stones. He was a man of fifty, broad across the chest, with a dark beard streaked with iron and eyes that held the heavy, lidded quality of a creature accustomed to dimness. For twenty years he had lived in London on the subscriptions of those who believed in something, or who found it convenient to pretend they did—the widows of manufacturers, the disillusioned younger sons of earls, the retired civil servants with a taste for abstract justice. His weekly column in the *International Herald*, that faded broadsheet printed above a fishmonger's in Soho, had become a monument to a certain kind of rhetorical fury, a fury so elaborate, so encrusted with qualification and iron parenthesis, that it had long since ceased to signify anything beyond its own cadence. He was, in the phrase of one of his patrons, a "moral force," and he had accepted the designation with the grave and taciturn contentment of a man who has found his niche and sees no reason to disturb it.

The door opened. The footman announced nothing—he merely stood aside—and there entered a young woman of perhaps five-and-twenty, dressed in a gown of dark material that seemed to absorb the meagre light and give nothing back. Her face was pale and narrow, with that peculiar transparency of skin which in certain women suggests not delicacy but a kind of inward combustion, as though some private conviction were consuming her slowly from within. She held in her gloved hand a leather portfolio, and she pressed it against her bosom with the unconscious tenderness of a mother shielding an infant.

"You are Mr. Vassili," she said. It was not a question. Her voice was low and held the faintest trace of a foreign accent—German, perhaps, or Austrian—which she had not troubled to eliminate and which Vassili, who had himself long ago shed the inflections of Riga, found neither charming nor disagreeable but merely significant, a mark of the earnest, the committed, the un-English.

"I have the honour," said Vassili, with a small, precise bow.

She did not sit. She remained standing near the door, as though the interview might be concluded in a moment and she wished to spare herself the formality of departure. "My name is Alma Lehrs," she said. "My father was Hermann Lehrs, of the Bremen shipping line. He died in March. I am his sole heir." She paused, and in the pause the ticking of the ormolu clock on the mantelpiece became suddenly, absurdly prominent, like a heartbeat in an empty cathedral. "I have read everything you have written, Mr. Vassili. I have read it many times. And I have come to give you my fortune."

Vassili did not move. He had heard such declarations before—or variations of them, at lesser pitch and with smaller sums attached—but never delivered with this particular quality of stillness, this air of a woman who has already crossed a threshold and cannot return. He regarded her with his heavy eyes, and in the dim and suffocating light of the room he was aware, first, of an impression—something in the set of her jaw, in the way the portfolio was gripped—and then, following slowly upon that impression, of its meaning, which was that she was in earnest. She was in terrifying, absolute earnest. And it was at this precise moment, standing before the marble mantelpiece in a Mayfair drawing room with the drizzle murmuring against the tall windows, that there occurred within Julius Vassili a kind of subsidence, a quiet inward collapse, as though the foundations upon which he had rested his considerable weight for two decades had been quietly removed, not by force but by the mere proximity of someone who actually believed.

"I am grateful, Miss Lehrs," he said. His voice was steady. He had a gift for steadiness, for the measured cadence that his readers mistook for depth. "But I must ask—what is it you imagine I will do with it?"

She looked at him as though the question were superfluous, or perhaps insulting. "What you have always intended to do. What you have written about. The work."

The word hung in the air between them. *The work.* He had used it so often, in so many columns and private letters and after-dinner speeches in the back rooms of public houses, that it had worn smooth, a pebble in his pocket, touched and retouched until all inscription had been lost. He had spoken of the destruction of the old order, of the edifice of property and law that encaged the human spirit, with an eloquence that had cost him nothing—less than nothing, for it had brought him his bread, his lodging, his reputation, and that agreeable sensation of moral superiority which is perhaps the most exquisite luxury available to a man without convictions. He had spoken of these things, and in speaking he had felt nothing—nothing, that is, beyond the pleasure of the speaking, the craftsman's pleasure in the well-turned period, the irony folded within the parenthesis like a blade within a cane. And he had believed, in a hazy and intermittent fashion, that this emptiness was itself a kind of strength, the hard clarity of a man who sees through all illusions and is left with nothing but the necessity of action.
