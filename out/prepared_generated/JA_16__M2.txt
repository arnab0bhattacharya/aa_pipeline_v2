It was a truth universally acknowledged that a man in debt must be immediately removed from the vicinity of those who might, through the mere accident of affection, prolong his stay. Such was the predicament of Mr. Arthur Hale, whose sister, Miss Emily Hale, had spent the preceding four hours in a state of such profound agitation that she had neglected both her tea and her conversation, leaving her friend, Miss Clara Vance, to sit in a silence that was, if not quite comfortable, at least replete with the heavy weight of unspoken consequence.

Clara, who possessed a mind of considerable acuteness and a heart that was, by her own modest estimation, not entirely devoid of sentiment, had recently discovered that her regard for Mr. Hale was of a nature that transcended the common bounds of polite acquaintance. It was a discovery made in the quiet recesses of her own thoughts, while watching him sketch the outline of the garden from the library window, and it had since become the governing principle of her days. To learn, therefore, that this young man of pleasing manners and modest fortune was to depart for the West Indies within the week, driven there not by ambition but by the relentless demands of creditors, was a blow that struck at the very foundation of her composure.

"He must not go," Clara whispered, as they walked along the gravel path towards the house, the evening air crisp with the approach of autumn. "If he remained, if he could be persuaded to stay but a few weeks, the situation might alter. There are fortunes made in a season of speculation."

Emily stopped, turning to her with a look that was half reproach, half despair. "You do not understand, Clara. His honour is at stake. He has lost more than he can replace, and to linger in England, to be seen in the streets of London or even the markets of our own county, would be to invite the very shame he dreads. He is leaving to save his name, not his life."

Clara felt a pang of resistance. It was an impertinent feeling, born of self-interest and the vanity of believing that her presence, or even her silence, might hold sway over the movements of a man whose future was determined by the cold arithmetic of ledgers. Yet, as she looked at Emily’s pale face and the tremor in her hands, the vanity abated. She saw not a sister in distress, but a young woman facing the ruin of her brother’s reputation, a fate that no amount of personal affection could rightly outweigh.

"I will say nothing," Clara said, her voice low and steady. "I will say nothing that might encourage him to stay, nor anything that might alarm him. I will be as silent as the shadows in the library."

Emily squeezed her hand, her eyes glistening with a gratitude that was both sincere and tinged with sorrow. "You are a good friend, Clara. More than he knows. He thinks of you only as a pleasant acquaintance, one of those who smile at his jokes and praise his sketches. He does not know that you are the only one who sees the struggle beneath the surface."

Clara smiled, a faint, melancholy curve of the lips. "He has been too busy to notice, I suppose. Men are rarely attentive to the feelings of those who do not demand attention."

The farewell dinner was to be held that evening in the dining room of Ashworth Park, a house of moderate size and decent taste, located in a quiet corner of Sussex. The table was set for six, the candles burning with a steady, cheerful light that seemed to mock the gloom of the occasion. Mr. Hale sat at the head of the table, his posture upright, his face composed, though Clara could detect a slight tension in his jaw, a sign of the inner turmoil he so carefully concealed.

As the meal progressed, the conversation turned, as is often the case in such gatherings, to the weather, the health of distant relations, and the quality of the wine. Mr. Hale spoke with his usual ease, making light remarks about the cold and praising the roast pheasant, which was, indeed, of excellent preparation. Clara sat opposite him, her eyes fixed on the pattern of the tablecloth, listening to the cadence of his voice with an intensity that would have been alarming had he known of it.

It was a test of endurance. To hear him speak of his plans for the future, to hear him mention the ship that would carry him to Jamaica, and to feel the pull of her own desire to interrupt, to offer a word of caution, a hint of hope, was a trial of her self-command. She thought of the words she might have said, the gentle suggestion that perhaps a change of scene was not necessary, that perhaps his friends could intercede. But she remembered Emily’s plea, and the weight of that promise held her tongue.

Mr. Hale’s brother, a man of less sensitivity and greater bluntness, made a remark about the prospect of the Indies being "a fine place to forget one’s troubles, though a poor place to remember one’s debts." The room fell silent for a moment, the air thick with the sudden intrusion of the truth. Mr. Hale’s hand, which had been resting on the table, tightened slightly, but his voice remained calm.

"Debt is a matter of calculation, not memory," he replied, his tone dry. "One pays it, and then one forgets it. The Indies offer the opportunity to do the former with some speed."

Clara looked up, meeting his gaze. It was a look of such quiet dignity, such resigned acceptance of his fate, that any thought of interference died in her throat. She saw in his eyes not the fear of a man fleeing his creditors, but the determination of a man preserving his integrity. To suggest that he stay, to imply that his honour could be compromised by delay, would have been an insult to the very quality she admired.
