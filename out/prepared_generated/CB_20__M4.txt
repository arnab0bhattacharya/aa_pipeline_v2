The bank’s failure had come upon us like a hard frost in May, blighting every green promise of our lives. My father, who had been a man of substance and of a cheerful, trusting nature, was taken from us within the year—not by the hand of God, but by the slower, more corrosive action of a broken spirit. He left my mother and me with a house in Leeds too large for our means and too dear to our hearts to quit, and with a competence so reduced that we must either let its rooms or starve. Thus it was that the sign went up in our window, a small, genteel card offering “Apartments to Let, with Board, to a Single Gentleman of Quiet Habits.” My mother wept when she wrote it; I confess I felt a species of fierce exultation, as though we had drawn a line and would henceforth do battle with the world upon our own terms.

Our first lodger was a Mr. Ashworth, a schoolmaster from the West Riding, a man of perhaps forty years, spare of frame and severe of countenance, with hair already grey at the temples and eyes of so dark and penetrating a grey that they seemed to look through one rather than at one. He spoke little, ate with a monkish abstemiousness, and spent his evenings in his chamber with his books. My mother, who was of a sanguine and affectionate disposition, would have made a friend of him; she plied him with tea and questions, and was met with a courtesy so cold and so unvarying that she was at last compelled to pronounce him “a very iceberg of a man.” For my part, I was indifferent to him. I had my own designs, and they did not include the amusement of a lodger.

My designs were these: to become a teacher. I had received a fair education from my father, who had treated me as a son in the matter of learning, and I had since added to it by every means within my reach. But the path to a schoolroom is not opened by knowledge alone; it requires certificates, testimonials, the recommendation of persons of standing. I had none. I had, however, a will of iron and a desperation that would not be denied. I studied at night, when the house was still, by the light of a single candle, poring over treatises on arithmetic and geography, on the English grammar and the rudiments of French, until my eyes ached and my head swam. My mother would come to my door and entreat me to sleep, and I would answer her with a promise I did not mean to keep, and return to my books the moment her step had retreated down the stairs.

It was in the second month of Mr. Ashworth’s tenancy that he discovered my occupation. I had left a slate upon the parlour table, covered with a problem in Euclid that had defeated me, and when I came down in the morning I found it corrected in a neat, scholarly hand, with a brief note appended: “The fifth proposition is better demonstrated by the method of superposition. I shall be glad to explain it, if you will permit me.” I was at first incensed at the impertinence—for so I chose to consider it—of a stranger meddling in my affairs. But the explanation was sound, and I was too honest a student to reject a good lesson because it came unasked. I sought him out that evening and thanked him, somewhat stiffly, and he bowed and said that he had perceived my industry and admired it, and that if I wished for assistance in any branch of study, he would be pleased to afford it.

From that time forth, a curious and constrained intimacy grew between us. He was a man of profound learning, and of a patience that I had not expected from his aspect. He would sit with me for an hour at a time, explaining the mysteries of decimal fractions or the government of the French verb, and his manner, though dry, was never contemptuous. I learned more from him in three months than I had learned in three years of solitary toil. And yet I never liked him. I felt, in his presence, a species of watchfulness, a scrutiny that I could not fathom, and I was always upon my guard. He would sometimes look at me with an expression that I could only interpret as a kind of pity, and I resented it bitterly. I wanted no man’s pity; I wanted only the means to escape.

My mother, meanwhile, had formed her own conclusions. She was a woman who saw life through the medium of her affections, and she could not conceive of a man’s spending so many hours in a young woman’s company without a tender motive. She began to speak of Mr. Ashworth in a tone of arch significance, to invite him to take his tea with us in the parlour, to drop hints concerning the loneliness of a widowed life and the comforts of a well-ordered home. He received these advances with his usual impassivity, but I could see that he was not insensible to them, and I could see also that he was not indifferent to me. There were moments when his cold eye would soften, when his voice would take on a gentler cadence, and I would feel a strange, unwelcome fluttering at my heart—a sensation I crushed with all the force of my reason. I had no room in my life for such follies. I had a goal, and I would not be turned aside from it by any man, however learned, however grave, however secretly dear.
