The rain lashed against the narrow panes of the workshop window, blurring the grey outline of Clerkenwell into a smear of indistinct smog and wet stone. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of ozone, stale tobacco, and the peculiar, metallic tang of heated brass. I stood by the hearth, my hands clasped behind my back, watching my companion move with that peculiar, restless energy which he reserved for moments of intense deduction. He did not sit; he paced. He touched things. He sniffed the air.

"Explain yourself, Mr. Vance," Holmes said, stopping abruptly before the workbench. "You state that your rival, Mr. Halloway, has stolen the design of your luminescent filament. Yet, you also tell me that the original drawings have never left your safe. How, then, does a man steal what is physically inaccessible?"

Arthur Vance, a man of sharp features and a tremor that betrayed his agitation, shifted his weight from one foot to the other. He was a tall, lean figure, dressed in a suit that had once been fashionable but now bore the dust of the trade. "It is not the paper, Mr. Holmes, that I fear. It is the knowledge. Halloway was my partner in research for three years. He knew the alloy. He knew the vacuum technique. If he has the method, the paper is merely a legal formality. He can recreate it."

"And the safe?" Holmes asked, his eyes narrowing as he pointed to the heavy iron strongbox in the corner of the room. "The locks are unbroken. The hinges are pristine. There is no sign of forced entry."

"Exactly," Vance cried, his voice rising. "He could not have taken them. But look at his workshop across the street! It is empty. His tools are gone. His assistant has vanished. He has fled the country, or so the police believe. But why? If he had not stolen my work, he would have no reason to run. He knew I was preparing to file my patent tomorrow. He struck first."

Holmes turned away from the safe and walked toward the door, which stood ajar. Outside, the rain had eased to a drizzle, and the gas lamp across the street cast a long, wavering shadow into the room.

"Mr. Vance," Holmes said quietly, "you must forgive me, but your narrative contains a slight discrepancy. You say Halloway fled. Yet, if he had stolen your secret, he would not need to flee. He would need to stay. He would need to test his invention, to perfect it, to ensure it was viable before he attempted to market it against you. A thief who steals a blueprint does not run away with it; he stays to build the machine. A man who is innocent, however, or a man who is guilty of something far more heinous than theft, might indeed run."

Vance looked confused. "Guilty of something else? What do you mean?"

Holmes picked up a small, brass-rimmed lamp from the table. It was an intricate device, the glass globe cracked, the filament inside a blackened tangle of wire. "This is your prototype, I presume? The one that failed last Tuesday?"

"Yes," Vance said, his face paling. "It exploded. Shattered the glass. That is why I am here. I thought Halloway had sabotaged it."

"Sabotage implies malice," Holmes mused, turning the lamp over in his fingers. "But what if it was not sabotage? What if it was accident? And what if the accident was the result of haste?"

He walked to the window and looked out at the neighboring building. It was a low, brick structure, its windows dark. "Come with me, Mr. Vance. We shall visit the place across the street. I suspect that Mr. Halloway has not fled the country. I suspect he is merely hiding."

We took a hansom cab to the address opposite. The driver, a sooty-faced man, refused to go any closer than the curb, citing the wet and the late hour. We alighted and crossed the street. The door of Halloway’s workshop was locked, but the window on the ground floor was slightly ajar. Holmes raised his eyebrows and pushed it open with the tip of his cane.

We entered. The room was in disarray. Tools were scattered on the floor, and a thick layer of dust covered the benches, disturbed only by the footprints of a single pair of boots. The air here was cold and still. In the center of the room stood a large, wooden crate, its lid pried open. Inside, there was no lamp. There were no drawings. There was only a pile of rags and a single, small notebook.

Holmes picked up the notebook. He opened it and scanned the pages. His eyes widened slightly.

"What is it?" Vance whispered.

"Observations," Holmes said. "Date: three days ago. 'The alloy is too brittle. The vacuum fails at high heat. The current surges. I cannot stabilize it.' And here, dated yesterday: 'Vance has begun to suspect I have copied him. I must be careful. I must appear indifferent. If I show fear, he will know I have failed. I must wait for him to file the patent, then I shall reveal my own superior design. I shall not run. I shall wait.'

Holmes closed the book and looked at Vance. "Mr. Vance, your rival did not steal your design. He failed to perfect his own. And his disappearance is not due to guilt, but to fear. He is afraid of you. Not because you are the victim, but because you are the superior inventor. He knows that if he stays, he will be exposed as a fraud. He has hidden in the cellar of the warehouse next door, where he has rented a room for the week. He is waiting for you to file, so that he can counter-attack with a design that, while inferior, is close enough to confuse the courts."

Vance stared at Holmes, his face a mask of shock. "He is in the warehouse? Next door?"
