Lady Marchmont had conceived the notion—as she expressed it to her husband, who was a man of excellent digestion and no opinions—of giving a dinner at which no one should know anyone else. “It is the last refinement of hospitality,” she said, “for one gives one’s guests not only the pleasure of meeting, but the infinitely rarer pleasure of discovery. Everyone is so weary of the same faces, and the same epigrams, and the same soup. To dine with strangers is to dine for the first time.”

“And to dine with one’s wife,” murmured Lord Marchmont, “is to dine for the thousandth. But I shall be at my club.”

Thus it was that on a certain evening in June, when the lilacs in Grosvenor Square were heavy with a fragrance that seemed to reproach the coal-smoke of London, there assembled in Lady Marchmont’s drawing-room four persons who had never before been in the same room, and who were destined, before the evening was done, to wish they never had been.

The first to arrive was the Bishop of St. Ethelred’s, a large, silken man with a voice like a cathedral organ played softly, and a manner that suggested he had just forgiven someone and was rather pleased with himself for it. He was examining a small Italian picture with the air of a man who examines everything, including his own motives, and finds them all equally creditable.

The second was Miss Celia Vane, an actress whose name was spoken with a certain hesitation in the best drawing-rooms, and with none at all in the best dressing-rooms. She wore a gown of pale green, the colour of a wave just before it breaks, and she entered with that peculiar grace which is the result of having been looked at for twenty years and having learned to look back.

The third was Mr. Rupert Ffolliott, the explorer, a lean, brown man with eyes like pebbles and a habit of silence that people mistook for profundity, chiefly because he never contradicted them. He had been in places where the maps ended, and had returned with a great reputation and a small fortune, which he was now engaged in spending on the theory that a man who has dined off roots in the interior of Africa has earned the right to dine off ortolans in London.

The fourth was a young poet named Aubrey Lestrange, who had published a volume called *The White Sin*, which had been praised by the wicked, condemned by the good, and read by everybody. He was very pale, very thin, and very young, and he wore his hair in a manner that suggested he had been thinking about something else when he dressed.

Lady Marchmont received them with that perfect composure which is the last gift of the gods to the perfectly frivolous. “Bishop,” she said, “I am sure you know Miss Vane.”

“I have not that pleasure,” said the Bishop, looking at Miss Vane with an expression that was intended to be paternal and was, in fact, merely appreciative.

“And Mr. Ffolliott, of whom you have all read, though he has written nothing. And Mr. Lestrange, whose poems you have all read, though you will not admit it. Now, come to dinner, and remember—you have never met before, and you must be as charming as if you were old friends, and as discreet as if you were enemies.”

The table was a triumph of that art which conceals art. The candles were the colour of champagne, the flowers were the colour of dawn, and the china was the colour of a secret. Lady Marchmont had arranged the seating with the care of a general and the malice of a novelist. The Bishop sat at her right, Miss Vane at her left. Mr. Ffolliott was between Miss Vane and Mr. Lestrange. And Lady Marchmont, who knew everything about everybody, and had invited them precisely because she wished to see what would happen, sat at the head of the table with the air of a woman who has set a match to a firework and is retiring to a safe distance.

The first course passed in that agreeable murmur which is the prelude to all good dinners and most bad ones. The Bishop discoursed on the decay of reverence among the young, and Miss Vane agreed with him so charmingly that he began to think the young were not so bad after all. Mr. Ffolliott said nothing, which was his way of saying everything. And Mr. Lestrange looked at Miss Vane with the eyes of a young man who has written a scandalous book and is wondering whether life is going to be as scandalous as literature.

It was at the second course—a delicate consommé that tasted of nothing and cost everything—that the first secret fell into the soup.

“I was in Africa,” said Mr. Ffolliott, in the voice of a man who has not spoken for an hour and is determined to make up for it, “when I heard of the death of poor Lavington. A curious business. He was found in his tent with a bottle of chloral and a letter from a woman. The letter was unsigned, but the handwriting was unmistakable.”

“How dreadful,” said Miss Vane, with a smile that was entirely for the Bishop. “And who was she?”

“That,” said Mr. Ffolliott, “is what the world has been asking ever since. I am told she was an actress.”

The Bishop, who had been raising his spoon, set it down again. Miss Vane, who had been raising her glass, set it down also. There was a silence of the kind that is more eloquent than speech.

“An actress,” repeated Miss Vane, with perfect composure. “How very romantic. And how very dangerous. I have always thought that an actress should never write letters. It is the one form of self-expression she cannot control.”
