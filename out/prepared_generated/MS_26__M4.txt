I had laboured three years in a garret in Vienna, in a solitude so complete that the bells of the city came to my ear like the tolling of a world from which I was forever severed, upon an engine of wood and brass and coiled spring whose purpose I dared not confess even to myself in the first delirium of its conception. When at length it stood before me complete—a figure seated at a cabinet of ebony, its waxen countenance composed into an eternal and terrible serenity, its arm poised above the chequered field—I felt none of the triumph of the artificer who beholds his work accomplished, but rather the sinking of the heart which attends the birth of something monstrous and unlooked for. For I had given it, by a cunning of levers and a hidden compartment beneath the board, the semblance of thought; and the world, I knew, would never forgive me the deception, nor would it ever guess the truth.

It was the Baron von Herrenhausen, master of the household to his Serene Highness the Prince of Kaltenbrunn, who brought me forth from my obscurity. He had heard, through the murmurs of the learned societies, of a machine that played at chess and was never vanquished; and he came to my garret with the eagerness of a man who would make of my invention a bauble for the amusement of a court. I might have refused—I see now that I might have refused, and returned to my solitude and my guilt together—but the promise of patronage glittered before me, and the long years of penury had sapped the courage of my conscience. I consented. I permitted the Baron to bear me and my silent companion to the palace of Kaltenbrunn, where the chandeliers blazed upon gilded cornices and the laughter of courtiers echoed down the marble galleries, and where I first understood the true weight of the burden I had taken upon my soul.

The evening of the exhibition arrived. The great salon was thronged with the nobility of the principality; the Prince himself, a man of languid curiosity, reclined upon a divan near the machine, and beside him sat the Countess Adelaide von Morungen, whose dark and penetrating eyes seemed to pierce through the varnish of the automaton to the miserable secret that crouched within. I was commanded to set the engine in motion. With hands that trembled, I wound the mainspring, and drew forth the little key that opens the cabinet of the board. The figure raised its arm; the court gasped; and the game began.

The Prince's opponent was the court physician, a man of acknowledged skill, who smiled with the condescension of the living for the dead. Yet within twenty moves his smile had faded; within thirty his brow was damp with a cold perspiration; and at the forty-second, he rose from his seat with an oath he could not stifle, and declared himself defeated. A murmur of astonishment ran through the assembly. The Prince laughed aloud, and clapped his hands; the Countess alone remained silent, and I saw her eyes fixed upon the machine with a scrutiny that chilled me to the marrow. Again and again the challenge was given and accepted; again and again the automaton triumphed, until the courtiers began to whisper of sorcery, and the Prince himself grew grave, and bade the thing be put away.

That night I could not sleep. The waxen face of the automaton haunted my pillow; I seemed to see the hidden dwarf—for such was the wretched agent of my fraud, a mute and misshapen creature whom I had purchased from a beggar in the streets of Pressburg—crouched in his narrow prison, his fingers upon the levers, his eye at the peephole, condemned to win victories he could never claim, to think thoughts that were not his own. I had made of a living soul a cog in my machine; I had buried a man alive within a cabinet of wood, that a prince might be amused. The horror of it rose up in the darkness and would not be reasoned away.

On the second evening, the Countess Adelaide requested a private audience with me in the library of the palace. I went, trembling, and found her standing by the window, her figure outlined against the moonlit garden, her countenance pale and resolute. She bade me close the door, and then, turning upon me those eyes which seemed to read the very secrets of my breast, she said, in a voice low and steady: "Signor Aldini, I have watched your machine, and I have watched you. There is a living thing within that cabinet. Deny it if you dare, but I know the sound of breathing, and I heard it when the courtiers were loudest in their applause."

I stood before her as a criminal before his judge. The words of denial rose to my lips and died there; the long habit of concealment could not sustain itself beneath the beams of that penetrating gaze. I sank upon a chair, and covered my face with my hands, and confessed the whole—the dwarf, the levers, the peephole, the three years of solitary toil, the despair that had driven me to the fraud, and the anguish that now consumed me. She heard me to the end without a word; and when I had finished, she said, "You have done a wicked thing, but you are not yet wholly lost. Release the creature you have imprisoned. Restore him to the light and the air, and let the world laugh at your automaton as it will. Better to be mocked for an honest failure than to reign as the master of a living tomb."

Her words fell upon me like the rain upon a parched field. I returned to my chamber, and all that night I wrestled with the demon of my pride. To destroy my fame, to confess my fraud before the assembled court, to abandon the patronage that had lifted me from penury—these were the sacrifices she demanded, and my spirit recoiled from them. Yet the image of the dwarf, crouched in his darkness, would not leave me; and towards morning I arose, resolved.
