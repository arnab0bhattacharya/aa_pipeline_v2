The neighbourhood of Portsdown Lodge was agreed, by all who professed any knowledge of the matter, to possess a society as select as any to be found within five miles of Portsmouth. Mrs. Penhaligon, the widow of a former Admiral, and the present guardian of her niece, Miss Arundell, was a woman of very settled habits, and still more settled opinions; and as she had survived three campaigns in the drawing-rooms of Bath, she considered herself a person consummately skilled in the art of discovering mercenary motives in the young men of the navy.

Miss Arundell, at five-and-twenty, was an heiress to twelve thousand pounds. She had refused two offers in the course of two years, a circumstance which her aunt was pleased to attribute to a laudable coldness of constitution, and which the young ladies of the neighbourhood attributed to an unamiable pride. Miss Arundell, in truth, attributed it to nothing but the extreme difficulty of finding a man whose conversation did not weary her. She was not cold; she was merely reserved, and perhaps a little fastidious.

It was therefore with no small degree of surprise that Mrs. Penhaligon, looking from her drawing-room window on a Tuesday morning in June, beheld a gentleman in a plain blue coat alighting from a hired gig, whose bearing, though unexceptionable, was not the bearing of a man who had spent his youth in the possession of ten thousand a year.

"That," said she, turning to Miss Arundell with a countenance of marked displeasure, "is, I make no doubt, Mr. Holloway. I see he has taken the liberty of calling at eleven o'clock, a time which, in the country, can only be excused by extreme ignorance or extreme presumption."

Miss Arundell, who was hemming a handkerchief, did not immediately raise her eyes, but the colour mounted to her cheeks, a circumstance which her aunt, whose back was turned to the window, did not observe. "Mr. Holloway, ma'am?" she said, with a composure that cost her some effort. "I was not aware that you knew him."

"Know him! I know nothing of him, child, but that he was your poor brother's particular friend, which is no recommendation. A surgeon's mate, I believe, and lately made a surgeon, which is no great matter. Your brother, in the midst of his gallantry, had a fatal talent for collecting acquaintances whom it was impossible to acknowledge. But I suppose we must see him."

Mr. Holloway was shown in, and Mrs. Penhaligon received him with that sort of elaborate civility which is designed to make a man feel that he is not at home. He was a person of plain countenance, and perhaps eight-and-twenty years of age, with a decided air of having been much in the sun and wind, and an address which, though not polished, was perfectly easy. He inquired after Mrs. Penhaligon's health with a sincerity that somewhat discomposed her, for she had prepared herself to be insincere in return, and found no convenient handle for it.

Miss Arundell, who had not spoken, was conscious of a pleasure which she could not but think unreasonable. She had known Mr. Holloway during her brother's lifetime, and had been struck, even then, by the quiet good sense with which he endured the raillery of his messmates, and the gentleness with which he bore her brother's frequent imprudences. She had heard of him since, at long intervals, with an interest which she had carefully suppressed, as a sentiment unworthy of a woman of fortune and understanding.

"Miss Arundell," said Mr. Holloway, after some general conversation, "I am lately returned from the Mediterranean, and I have brought with me a few trifles which belonged to your brother, and which I thought might be acceptable to you. They were found among his effects at the time of his death, and I have kept them by me, in the hope of having the honour of delivering them in person."

He produced a small parcel, which Miss Arundell received with an emotion she did not attempt to conceal. Mrs. Penhaligon, who observed it, thought it expedient to interpose.

"You are very obliging, Mr. Holloway. Miss Arundell is too much affected by every relic of poor Captain Arundell to speak for herself, I dare say. But pray, how long do you purpose to remain in this part of the country?"

"I am uncertain, madam. I have some business with the Commissioner at the yard, and I am, besides, not wholly without hopes of inducing Miss Arundell to believe that I am a man of some steadiness of character."

This was said with a simplicity which left Mrs. Penhaligon in doubt whether he was attempting a jest, or making a declaration of the most serious nature. She coloured, looked at her niece, and finding that Miss Arundell was very intent upon the parcel, determined to take no notice.

"I am sure," said she, "I have no doubt of your steadiness. Surgeons, I believe, are generally steady. But Miss Arundell is very much engaged at present. She has a great many calls upon her time, and I am persuaded she cannot be expected to enter into any new engagements of any kind."

Mr. Holloway bowed, and after sitting a few minutes longer, took his leave. Mrs. Penhaligon, the moment the door closed, turned to her niece with a countenance of real concern.

"My dear Clara, I must speak plainly. This man's meaning is not to be mistaken. He comes here, a person of no family, with no fortune but his pay, and presumes to address you. I must desire that you will not encourage him. I am not one of those who think that a woman of fortune is bound to marry only a man of equal fortune; but there are limits, and a surgeon, however steady, is quite beyond them."

Miss Arundell was silent for some moments. She then said, with a gentleness which her aunt knew to be a sign of determination, "I believe, ma'am, Mr. Holloway's father was a clergyman in Devonshire."
