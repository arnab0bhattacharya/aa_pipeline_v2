Mr. Cornelius Gammidge had spent the better part of forty years in the service of the natural sciences, and in that time he had received many peculiar gifts from former pupils. There had been the fossil ammonite from young Fortescue, who had since gone into the City and grown stout and forgetful; there had been the pressed Alpine flowers from the Hendersons’ boy, now a missionary in some uncomfortably hot part of Africa; and once, memorably, a jar of preserved tapeworms from a medical student with a taste for what he called “the grimly instructive.” But nothing in all that time had quite prepared him for the arrival, on a grey Tuesday afternoon in October, of the Wardour crate.

Algernon Wardour had been his most promising pupil, a lean, sun-browned young man with a gift for languages and a positive genius for getting himself into remote and unhealthy places. He had gone to the Upper Amazon some eighteen months before, ostensibly to study the rubber trade, actually, Gammidge suspected, because the map of that region still held blank spaces and Wardour could not abide a blank space. The letter that accompanied the crate was brief and characteristic.

“My dear Gammidge,” it ran, “I am sending you a specimen which I believe will interest you more than any dried orchid or bottled beetle. It is a plant—if plant it be—which I found growing in a swamp near the Rio Negro, and which the Indians of that district regard with a mixture of awe and loathing which I have never seen equalled. They call it by a name which translates roughly as ‘the hungry one.’ I have kept it alive on the voyage home with considerable difficulty, feeding it on flies and, when flies failed, on scraps of raw meat. It seems to require a surprising amount of nourishment. I am convinced it is a carnivorous plant of a type quite undescribed, and I can think of no one better fitted to study it than yourself. I shall be going up-country again in the spring, so I make you a present of it. Feed it regularly. It has, I fancy, a decided preference for live food. Yours ever, A. Wardour.”

Gammidge read this letter twice, removed his spectacles, polished them, and read it again. Then he went out into the little glass conservatory at the back of his house in Hollymount Gardens, Hampstead, where the crate had been deposited by a carman of melancholy aspect, and set about opening it.

The plant that emerged was a curious thing. It stood perhaps two feet high, with a thick, fleshy stem of a dull olive-green, and from this stem there radiated a number of long, narrow leaves, each terminating in a curious bladder-like structure, something between a pitcher and a clam’s shell, which hung downward on a slender stalk. The whole organism had a faintly repellent appearance, like something that had been designed for a purpose not quite nice. Gammidge, who had seen pitcher-plants before, was not at first greatly excited. But as he watched, the largest of the bladders slowly opened, revealing a pink and palpitating interior, and then, with a motion so deliberate that it seemed almost mechanical, closed again.

“Bless my soul,” said Gammidge.

He set the plant in a large pot of loam and leaf-mould, watered it, and placed it on a bench in the conservatory where it would get the morning sun. Then he went in to his tea.

His housekeeper, Mrs. Pidgeon, was a widow of robust build and decided opinions, who had kept house for him for eleven years and regarded him with a mixture of maternal affection and professional exasperation. She had never quite forgiven him for the tapeworms.

“There’s a queer smell in the conservatory, sir,” she observed, as she poured his tea. “Like something gone off.”

“It is the new plant, Mrs. Pidgeon. A carnivorous specimen from South America. It feeds on insects.”

“Insects,” said Mrs. Pidgeon, with the air of one who has heard it all before. “And who’s to catch the insects, I’d like to know? I’ve enough to do without hunting flies for a foreign plant.”

“I shall attend to it myself,” said Gammidge.

And he did. In the days that followed, the plant—which he christened, with a certain grim humour, *Nepenthes vorax*—became the centre of his existence. He caught flies for it, and then, finding that flies were insufficient, he purchased bluebottles from a boy in the village, and then, when the bluebottles proved inadequate, he began to buy raw meat from the butcher. The plant consumed everything with the same slow, deliberate, obscenely eager motion. Its bladders grew larger. Its stem grew thicker. Its colour deepened from olive to a rich, dark, venous purple.

“It’s got a wonderful appetite, sir,” said Mrs. Pidgeon, watching it one morning with folded arms. “That’s three pennyworth of liver since Monday.”

“It is a remarkable organism,” said Gammidge. “I fancy it is still growing.”

“Growing,” said Mrs. Pidgeon. “It’s growing that fast I shouldn’t be surprised if it wanted a leg of mutton next.”

Gammidge laughed, but the laugh was a little hollow. He had begun to notice certain things about the plant that troubled him. It had developed a habit of turning—slowly, almost imperceptibly—towards whoever entered the conservatory. Its bladders, which had once hung downward, now tended to gape upward, like so many hungry mouths. And the smell Mrs. Pidgeon had remarked upon had grown stronger, a heavy, sweetish, faintly sickening odour, like the smell of a hothouse in which something has died.

One evening, about three weeks after the plant’s arrival, Gammidge was sitting in his study, reading, when he heard a sound from the conservatory. It was a soft, wet, sucking sound, followed by a little sigh of satisfaction. He put down his book and went to the door.
