The morning had begun with a thin, persistent rain that streaked the long windows of the drawing-room in Kensington, rendering the street outside a mere wash of grey and yellow, a smudged watercolour imperfectly blotted; and Eleanor, standing before the glass with a list of names in her hand—names that seemed to her now to possess a life entirely their own, independent of the flesh they designated—felt that the house itself was holding its breath, as if the very chairs and tables knew of the evening that was to come. She smoothed the paper, though it was already quite smooth, and read the third name down, Mr. Cecil Vyvyan, tenor, and thought how odd it was that a man who could command an audience of hundreds with the sheer force of his respiratory apparatus should have telephoned only an hour ago in a voice quivering with the most abject terror, begging her, positively begging her, to tell him whether the hall in Bayswater possessed a sufficient number of gas-heaters, as his throat, that fragile instrument, was already commencing to close. She had soothed him; she had spoken of radiators; she had held the receiver away from her ear and looked at the rain on the glass and thought, quite involuntarily, of Gerald.

Gerald, who had left the house at eight that morning with the expression of a man bearing some secret burden too heavy for communication—was it only the market, then, that troubled him, or was it the deeper, more insidious weight of having said nothing to her, of having preserved over breakfast a silence so polished and impenetrable that it might have been a table newly varnished? She could not say. She had watched him spread marmalade with an attention so concentrated as to be almost ludicrous, and had thought: he is playing a part; he is pretending to be a man eating marmalade, and the pretence is the thing that should alarm me, far more than whatever disaster lies beneath it. But she had said nothing. One does not say things at breakfast. The toast is too brittle, the hour too early, the whole machinery of domestic life too insistent upon its smooth running for one to insert, like a spanner in the works, the question: *What have you lost?* And so she had let him go, and turned instead to her list, to Cecil Vyvyan and his throat, to the question of whether the second violins had received their music, to the hundred small anxieties that clustered about the concert like flies about a jam-pot, each one demanding the flat of her hand.

By three o'clock she was in the hall itself, that building in Bayswater which possessed, in Eleanor's estimation, a character at once lofty and vaguely disreputable, like a governess who has fallen upon uncertain times. The ceiling soared into a dimness where plaster cherubs disported themselves with an enthusiasm that seemed, under the electric lights, merely melancholy; and the chairs—those chairs which had been the cause of so much correspondence, so many telegrams, such an interchange of civilities and near-impoliteness with the caretaker, Mr. Bollard—stood in their long rows with an air of patient expectation, as though they, too, were waiting to hear what the evening would bring. She walked down the central aisle, touching the back of one here and another there, testing its stability, and was surprised by a sudden wave of feeling, a tenderness almost, for these empty seats that would so soon be filled by people she had known for twenty years, people whose faces would emerge from the darkness like memories surfacing in a dream—Mrs. Thorne with her tiara, old Sir Henry with his deaf ear turned perpetually towards the soprano as a sunflower turns towards the sun, the Misses Latimer in their identical lilac, and all the rest, the whole intricate web of acquaintance and obligation and genuine, fading affection that constituted her life in London.

She had organised this concert for the orphans of Bessborough; she had written letters, and telephoned, and cajoled, and pleaded; she had endured the insults of Madame Levitska, who had declared the piano unplayable and the lighting an insult to her art; she had sat through rehearsals where the string quartet had quarrelled amongst themselves with a ferocity that would have done credit to a divorce court; and now, standing in the empty hall with the smell of floor-polish and damp programmes rising about her, she felt a peculiar hollowness, as though the effort of holding all these disparate elements in some kind of equilibrium had left a void at the very centre of herself where she ought to have been.

It was at this moment that Mr. Bollard appeared, carrying a hammer.

"Mrs. Allerton," he said, with the grave deliberation of a man who considers every word before venturing to spend it, "this leg here. On number fourteen. It's not what it should be."

She looked at the leg. It was, indeed, slightly shorter than its companions, so that the chair rocked with a gentle, reproachful motion when pressed.

"Can you wedge it?" she asked.

"I can," said Mr. Bollard, with the air of one conceding a major point. "But it'll take time. And the platform cloth wants pinning."

"Then pin it," she said, and heard her own voice, firm and capable, with something of a stranger's in it, and thought: how effortlessly I become the person who arranges chairs and pins cloths; how readily I slip into this skin; and what would happen if I were to step out of it, if I were to say to Mr. Bollard, *My husband has lost money and has not told me, and I am afraid*? He would, she imagined, continue to hold his hammer, and to look at her with that expression of bovine patience, and would say, after a considerable interval, "Ah," or perhaps, "Well now," and the world would go on precisely as before, the chair still rocking, the cloth still unpinned, the orphans of Bessborough still in need of whatever shillings the evening might yield.
