Lady Aveline Devereux had always maintained that a dinner party was a work of art, and that the guests were merely the pigments with which one composed one’s masterpiece. The dining-room of her house in Belgravia, hung with tapestries of faded heliotrope and gold, was the frame; the Sèvres porcelain, the silver, and the lilies in their alabaster vases were the decorative flourishes. But the centre of the composition, the principal figure around which all lesser lights were arranged, was to have been Mr. Vivian Lyle. Mr. Lyle was the most celebrated talker in London. His conversation was said to be a spangled cloak of paradox and wit, and to listen to him was to be admitted into a salon of the intellect where the air shimmered with epigrams. Indeed, to be invited to one of Lady Aveline’s dinners at which Mr. Lyle was present was the highest social distinction, for it meant one was considered worthy of the master’s discourse. On this particular evening, however, the masterpiece was marred before the first course had been served. Mr. Lyle arrived, it is true, looking every inch the oracle in his perfectly cut evening clothes, his monocle gleaming like a cold and curious eye. But he brought with him not a spangled cloak of paradox, but a profound and unnerving silence. “My dear Mr. Lyle,” Lady Aveline cooed, offering him her hand, which was as white and fragile as a gardenia, “we have been positively pining for you. The very air in the room has been holding its breath, waiting for you to give it something to say.” Mr. Lyle bowed over her hand. His lips, usually curved in some devastatingly ironic smile, were pressed together in a thin, pale line. He said nothing. A cold dread, the kind one feels at a funeral for a joke, descended upon the assembled company. There was Lord Ashworth, who collected snuff-boxes and dull anecdotes; Mrs. Penelope Croft, whose wit was as sharp and as brittle as a spun-sugar sculpture; and a young artist, Mr. Julian Vance, who was hoping to be discovered and painted a single, perfect sea-shell on his napkin in his anxiety. They all looked at Mr. Lyle, their faces arranged in attitudes of expectant delight, like a row of flowers turning towards a sun that had inexplicably failed to rise. The silence lengthened. It was no longer an absence of sound, but a positive presence, a heavy, velvet curtain that had fallen upon the stage. Lady Aveline felt a desperate panic fluttering in her breast like a trapped bird. Her masterpiece was dissolving into chaos. The soup, a delicate consommé, was served. The only sounds were the faint clinking of spoons against porcelain and the agonising cough of Lord Ashworth, who was trying to signal his appreciation of the soup to Mr. Lyle, and receiving nothing in return but a vacant stare. “The consommé is quite remarkable, is it not?” Lady Aveline said, her voice a little too high. “It has a certain… a certain melancholy charm, I think. As if the chicken it was made from had known it was destined for a higher, though perhaps sadder, purpose.” A few polite smiles appeared, but they were strained. Mrs. Croft attempted a diversion. “I saw Lady Althorp today,” she announced, “wearing a bonnet of such an aggressive shade of mauve that I felt quite bruised just by looking at it.” “Indeed?” said Lord Ashworth, seizing the lifeline. “I myself have always found mauve to be the colour of social ambition and poor taste.” But the conversation could not gain any traction. It sputtered and died like a damp firework. All eyes kept drifting back to Mr. Lyle, the silent god at their feast, who sat pushing a piece of bread around his plate as if it were a fascinating philosophical problem. He was a statue of disappointment. Lady Aveline knew that a crisis required a dramatic solution. If the guest of honour would not provide the evening’s entertainment, then the hostess must. She felt a strange sensation, like a tight corset being loosened. For years she had played the part of the appreciative audience, the gracious dispenser of invitations, the silent partner in Mr. Lyle’s dazzling performance. Now, with the stage empty, she felt an unfamiliar and intoxicating urge to step into the spotlight herself. She tapped her knife against her glass. The sound was sharp and clear, cutting through the gloom. “My dear friends,” she began, her voice regaining its usual musical cadence, but with a new, mischievous light in it. “It appears that Mr. Lyle, in his infinite wisdom, has decided this evening to contemplate the profound mystery of the bread-roll. We must not disturb him. It is a subject, I am sure, that requires the deepest concentration. He is, as it were, communing with the very essence of flour.” A ripple of genuine laughter went around the table. Mr. Lyle did not look up. “Indeed,” Lady Aveline continued, warming to her theme, “we all have our moments of profound silence. I myself once spent an entire afternoon contemplating a single tangerine. I had peeled it with the utmost care, and there, on my plate, lay the naked fruit, glistening like a cluster of tiny, golden jewels. And I thought to myself, what a terrible burden it must be to be so perfect, so exquisitely arranged, and yet to be destined for so vulgar a fate as to be eaten by a person who would inevitably stain her gloves.” The laughter was louder now. Julian Vance looked up from his shell, his eyes wide with surprise. “And what of the peel?” asked Mrs. Croft, leaning forward, her brittle wit now aimed and ready. “Ah, the peel!” cried Lady Aveline, her hands sketching a graceful arc in the air. “The peel is the true tragedy! It has done all the work, endured the sun and the rain, protected the delicate fruit within, and then, at the moment of its greatest achievement, it is cast aside.
