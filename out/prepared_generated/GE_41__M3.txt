To walk through the market-square of Whitbridge on a Tuesday morning was, for Mr. Josiah Plucknett, the auctioneer, much like taking a slow inventory of human nature. He was a man whose cheerful countenance and rotund, well-fleshed figure seemed expressly designed to carry the good news of commerce into every corner of the Midlands. He knew the precise degree of financial embarrassment that had preceded the sale of a farmer’s best heifer, and the exact sum required to release a tradesman’s widow from her late husband’s debts; and he carried this encyclopaedic knowledge of human frailty with a benign, impartial jollity, as if the world were a vast, benevolent pawnshop where he was merely the chief clerk. But on this particular Tuesday, the gray October fog hanging over the town seemed to match a certain heaviness in his gait, a departure from his usual elastic tread. He was making his way to Birtle House, the seat of the ancient but now impoverished family of Mardale, to catalogue the effects of the late Squire prior to the next day’s public auction.

The Mardales had been lords of the soil in those parts for some four centuries, but the slow, relentless mathematics of debt had at last concluded their tenure. The present heir, young Mr. Edgar Mardale, was removing himself to lodgings in a distant county, and the house was to be stripped to its bare stones. Josiah, having a master-key provided by the creditors, let himself into the echoing hall. It was a melancholy spectacle, this sudden exposure of a family’s integuments to the cold eye of the public. The auctioneer moved among the shrouded furniture with the professional calm of a surgeon, parting the dust-sheets to note the condition of a walnut cabinet or the worn velvet of a settee, weighing the causes of their decay against the probable prices they would fetch.

It was while he was examining the contents of a heavy, carved oak press in the late Squire’s private closet that his hand fell upon a packet of letters, tied with a faded ribbon. They were not the dusty, innocuous epistles of agricultural business that he had expected to find. The handwriting on the outermost missive was at once familiar to him—the sharp, precise, upward-slanting characters of Mr. Theophilus Gravel, the highly respected magistrate and principal mill-owner of Whitbridge. Theophilus Gravel was a man who had built his imposing, unimpeachable character upon the foundation of an inflexible moral rectitude; he was a deacon of the Independent chapel, a patron of the almshouse, and a stern denouncer of any laxity in the affairs of men. To encounter his handwriting in the secret drawer of a ruined Catholic family was, therefore, to stumble upon a geological fault in the seemingly solid strata of Whitbridge society.

Josiah untied the ribbon, impelled by that same disinterested curiosity which makes the natural philosopher dissect a flower. He read the first page, and then another, with a rising sense of the heavy moral freight he had inadvertently taken on board. The letters, bearing dates some five-and-twenty years back, were passionate, abject, and self-accusing. They revealed not a clandestine romance, but a far more structurally damaging fact: that Gravel, then a struggling young factor, had borrowed a considerable sum from the then Squire Mardale, secured by a mortgage on the very mill he now owned outright; and that, taking advantage of the Squire’s careless, unbusinesslike habits, he had subsequently suppressed the repayment, swearing in a court of law that the debt had never been discharged, thereby acquiring the mill for a song and ruining his benefactor’s credit. The letters were an agonized confession of this perjury, written in a moment of religious terror, extracting from the Squire a promise to keep them as a silent witness, but never to use them.

Josiah Plucknett sat down on the edge of the press, the packet resting on his broad knee. Here was a dilemma of the sort that does not readily present itself in the tidy ledgers of commerce. If he placed the letters among the lots, the revelation would be swift and total. Theophilus Gravel would be ruined in reputation, stripped of his magistracy, and cast out from his chapel. Such a cataclysm might seem, to the severe moralist, the just inevitable consequence of hidden wrong-doing. But Josiah, who had looked a great deal into the mixed motives of men, was not accustomed to view the world through a lens of abstract justice. He knew that the exposure of a hidden fault does not merely punish the offender; it sends out secondary and tertiary waves of destruction, shattering the innocent lives of Gravel’s dependents, his mill-workers, and his delicate, devoted sister.

To burn them was the easiest path—the fire would consume the paper and the past in one indiscriminate gulp. But Josiah had a certain reverence for the facts of human existence, however ugly; to destroy the letters seemed to him a presumptuous tampering with the moral order of things, a taking of the balances into his own hands as if he were a providence entitled to wipe out the record of human travail.

And then there was the third course: to return them to Gravel. The auctioneer sat in the gathering dusk, weighing the causes against the consequences. He saw clearly enough that to return them would be to place a weapon in the hands of a conscience-stricken man, perhaps driving him to a public confession that would achieve the same ruin as an auction, only with the added agony of a self-inflicted wound. But Josiah reflected that conscience, however belated, is a living principle, and that the voice of a man’s own inward shame is a surer, deeper corrective than the hissing incredulity of the crowd. If we want to avoid giving the dose of hemlock in the wrong case, there is a need for fellowship with human travail—a recognition that the same seeds of weakness and error lie in all soils, and that the exposure which gratifies the public appetite for scandal is often but a poor substitute for the slow, silent reparations a man might make in the twilight of his life.
