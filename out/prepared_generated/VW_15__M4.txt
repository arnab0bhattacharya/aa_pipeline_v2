The morning had broken clear and cold, with that thin, rinsed light which seems to fall upon London as though through a sheet of water, and Miss Ada Penhallow, stepping out of her lodgings in Marchmont Street with her gloves still unbuttoned, felt the day stretch before her like a room she had not yet entered. Thursday. The word went through her with a small, guilty pleasure, for Thursday was hers—the whole of it, from the first clatter of the milk-carts to the last lamp—and she had planned it as a miser plans, hoarding the hours. The girls at St. Bridget's would be conjugating French verbs under Miss Wray by now, their heads bent, their plaits falling forward, and the thought of it, of the classroom breathing without her, gave her the peculiar lightness of the ghost who watches the living at their ordinary tasks and finds them beautiful.

She walked south, down through the squares, and the plane trees stood bare and intricate against the pale façades, their branches black as though drawn in ink, and here and there a window caught the sun and flung it back at her, a flash, a signal. She had a letter in her bag. It lay against the lining, a small square envelope, and she was aware of it with every step—aware of it as one is aware of a tooth, of a word one has promised not to say. It had come on Tuesday. She had put it in her bag on Tuesday and had not opened it, and now the not-opening had become a thing in itself, a substance, almost a companion; she carried it as women in another age had carried a relic, and the not-knowing was a sort of possession, a room in her mind she could enter and stand in, dark, unlit, but hers.

At the corner of Bloomsbury Street the sun struck the pavement full, and the shadow of a chimney-pot lay across it, long and wavering, and she stepped over it as though it were a stream. A boy went by with a barrow of oranges, crying them in a voice that cracked and mended and cracked again, and the sound hung in the cold air like the smell of the fruit, bright, sharp, foreign. She thought of the girls—of little Margaret Ellis, who had asked her last week, with that terrible directness of the young, whether she had ever been in love, and she had said, "That is not a question for the classroom, Margaret," and the girl had flushed and looked down, and the whole form had seemed to draw breath together, and afterwards, in the staff-room, she had found her own hands trembling as she poured her tea. What had she meant, what had any of them meant, those girls with their secrets passed in folded notes, their giggling, their sudden silences? They imagined her, she knew, to be finished, complete, a woman with no interior, a walking rule of grammar; and she was not offended, not exactly, for had she not imagined her own teachers so? Yet the letter in her bag said otherwise. The letter said that she was not finished, that somewhere a life went on which had once touched hers and now asked—asked what? She did not know. That was the point, the pleasure, the dread of it: that she did not know.

Trafalgar Square opened before her all at once, as though a hand had drawn back a curtain, and the lions sat in the sun and the pigeons rose and fell in their endless argument with the air, and the fountains, which had been still, were suddenly alive, throwing up their thin bright plumes, and the whole place roared and glittered and was indifferent. She crossed among the pigeons, and one flew up at her very feet with a clatter of wings that made her heart jump, and she laughed at herself, alone there in the midst of the crowd, and thought: this is happiness, this sudden foolish start, this being alive in the cold sun. The Gallery stood before her, its portico heavy with shadow, and she went up the steps with the others, the clerks and the students and the two old ladies in identical grey, and the doors swung and the roar of the square fell away behind her like a sea shut out, and there was the hush, the long polished hush, the smell of stone and varnish and other people's wool.

She went to the room she always went to. She had her painting, as other women have their gardens, their cats, their grievances. It hung in the second room, on the left wall, and she came to it as one comes to a friend who does not expect to be amused, who will let one sit. It was a woman at a window, a woman in a blue dress with her back half turned, and the light fell through the window upon her hands and upon the sill, and beyond the window there was a garden, or the idea of a garden, a greenness, a blur of leaves, and the woman's face was not quite visible—turned, always turning, as though she had heard her name and were about to answer and had not yet answered, and would never answer. Miss Penhallow stood before it and let the thing work upon her. The blue of the dress was the blue of a winter evening, of a curtain drawn at dusk; the light on the hands was the light of all the afternoons of her life; and the not-answering, the turned face, the eternal almost—she felt it in her throat, a pressure, a sweetness. Why did it move her? She could not have said. It was not the subject, not the story, for there was no story; it was the paint itself, the way the light had been caught and held, as though the painter had said to time: stay, just there, just so, and time had obeyed for a moment and then gone on.
