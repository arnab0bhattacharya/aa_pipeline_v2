The morning room of the Parthenon Club, that discreet sanctuary in St James’s where the upholstery is as deep as the silence and the silence nearly as deep as the members, was at the hour of eleven but sparsely inhabited. Lord Arthur Chillingsworth sat alone by the fire, contemplating with perfect placidity a small volume of Mr. Ruskin which he had carried there for the last three weeks without once opening it. He was a young man of admirable family, unblemished reputation, and a face so handsomely vacant that one might have supposed Providence, having lavished every advantage upon him, had deliberately left the interior untenanted, that the furniture of the mind might not distract from the elegance of the façade. In society he was universally beloved, for he never contradicted, never excelled, and never explained; and it was said, with that generosity which society reserves for the wholly inoffensive, that he was the dullest man in London.

It was into this tranquil scene that there presently burst—for M. Émile Vaublanc did everything, including entering a room, with the air of a man delivering a lecture upon the picturesque—a young French journalist whose reputation for the sublime had preceded him from Paris on the wings of a thousand telegrams. He was in London to observe the English character, and had already observed it, in his newspaper, for six weeks. He had come to the club that morning expressly to meet the celebrated Lord Arthur Chillingsworth, whose wit, so the Parisian papers had it, was the wonder of the metropolis.

“My dear Lord Arthur,” he cried, seizing his hand, “at last! I have crossed the Channel for this hour. They tell me in Paris that you are the most brilliant talker in England. I have come to hear you, monsieur. I demand it. It is my profession to demand things.”

Lord Arthur, who had been under the impression that he was the dullest man in London, and had borne the burden with a fortitude that was almost heroic, regarded his visitor with the mild astonishment of a sheep accosted by a meteor.

“I am afraid,” he said, with that gentle hesitation which was his only eloquence, “that you have been misinformed.”

“Misinformed!” cried the Frenchman. “But it is magnificent! The modesty! The English modesty! It is like the fog—it covers everything and reveals nothing. Come, monsieur, say something. Say anything. I shall put it in my article.”

Lord Arthur looked at the fire, and then at the clock, and then at the fire again, and, finding no assistance in either, observed with perfect truth:

“It is very cold for the time of year.”

M. Vaublanc went into a kind of ecstasy. He whipped out a notebook. “It is very cold for the time of year!” he repeated, transcribing it with a feverish pencil. “Admirable! It is the English philosophy in a sentence—the sublime understatement, the irony of the climate, the—how do you say?—the shrug of the cosmos. But it is profound, monsieur. It is profound!”

Lord Arthur blinked. He had made that remark a thousand times, to a thousand people, and no one had ever called it profound. He rather liked it. He resolved to make it again.

“One must,” he said, venturing a second observation, “always be prepared for the weather. It is the only thing in England that is never disappointing.”

The Frenchman’s pencil flew. “The only thing in England that is never disappointing! Ah, monsieur, it is a paradox of the first order! It is—what do you call it—the epigram! You English, you conceal your epigrams in your weather. In France we have no weather; we have only climate, and it is always correct. But this—this is genius.”

And so it was that Lord Arthur Chillingsworth, who had never in his life said a thing he had not first heard at his mother’s table, discovered that he was a wit. It was a discovery of the most intoxicating kind. He said nothing more that morning, for the Frenchman, having obtained his two sentences, was satisfied to spend the remainder of the hour explaining them to him at length; but he carried away from the club a new and delightful conviction, which he examined from every angle during his walk down St James’s Street, and found to be entirely to his taste.

He tried it that evening at dinner. Lady Marchbanks, a widow of formidable intellect and a tongue that had been known to reduce bishops to silence, asked him what he thought of the new novel everyone was reading. Lord Arthur, who had not read it, and who had formerly been in the habit of saying so with an apologetic cough, now paused, looked thoughtfully at his wine, and said:

“I never read a book until it has been forgotten. One owes that much to one’s own opinion.”

There was a moment of astonished silence. Then Lady Marchbanks laughed—laughed!—and said he was wicked, and the Bishop of Wandsworth, who was present, said he had never heard anything so neatly put, and a young lady with auburn hair and a very pretty confusion declared that Lord Arthur had quite frightened her. He had never frightened anyone before. He found the sensation delicious.

Within a fortnight his reputation had undergone that transformation which is the peculiar magic of London, where a man may be dull for thirty years and then, by some happy accident of misapprehension, become the fashion, and no one will ever admit to having thought otherwise. His sayings were repeated at every table. “Chillingsworth,” people said, “is the wittiest man in town. He says nothing, and it is always the right nothing.” Invitations poured in. He was asked to dinner five nights a week, and to luncheon on the sixth, and on the seventh he was asked to a musical evening, which he declined, observing that music was the only noise that had never been improved by being set to words, and this was considered so fine that it was printed in a weekly review.
