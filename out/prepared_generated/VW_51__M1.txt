The green of the grass was so new, so violently and painfully green, that it hurt the eyes to look at it; a greenness that had been washed and rinsed by the April rains until it shone, not with the dull, mossy green of summer, but with the wet, brilliant lacquer of a new leaf. And upon this vast, trembling expanse, the life of the morning was scattered in little knots of colour—perambulators like black beetles, a flash of red, a woman’s pink hat. Cecily sat on the bench, her hands lying in her lap, feeling the sun through the thin wool of her coat, a sun that was not yet strong enough to have any warmth in it, only a kind of pale, watery promise.

He was there, her Oliver, a small, determined figure in his blue jersey, digging at the earth with a piece of stick. Dig, dig, dig. The world for him was this patch of mud; it was the entire universe, containing all its mysteries, its possibilities. He was entirely himself, entirely there, his small brow furrowed with the immense concentration of the task. And she, watching him, was not there at all. She was a dozen women at once. She was the girl who had stood on the cliffs at Cornwall and felt the whole of the future was a sea to be crossed, a girl with hair that blew in a way it no longer did, a girl who had thought—what had she thought? That words would be enough. That one could build a life out of sentences.

A robin, startlingly bold, hopped onto the path, its head cocked. Oliver looked up from his digging, his mouth a small ‘o’ of discovery. “Mummy. Bird.” He said it not to her, but to the world, to the bird, to the fact of the bird’s existence. And the word, so simple, so true, seemed to hang in the air for a second, a perfect, solid thing. Unlike her own words, which were always dissolving, turning to mist the moment they were formed. The book. It had been about a garden, or a house, or perhaps a woman in a house looking at a garden; it no longer mattered. It existed now only as a title on a blank page, a ghost of a purpose that sat on her desk at home and gathered dust. She had thought she would become a writer. Instead she had become Oliver’s mother, Edward’s wife. Were these not, too, forms of creation?

Edward. His face rose before her, not with its morning look of hurry and the newspaper, but as it was at night, in the dark, a landscape of familiar planes and shadows. A good man. A kind man. But he lived in a world of facts and figures, of the solid, the measurable. He would see the robin and think of its Latin name, its diet, its migratory habits. He would see Oliver digging and think of the boy’s constitution, his need for fresh air. He would not see, could not see, that the robin was a punctuation mark in the green sentence of the morning, or that the patch of mud was a kingdom. Their two worlds lay side by side, like this bench and the path, touching but never quite merging. She loved him, yes, but it was like loving a well-made chair, a thing of comfort and use, not a thing of—what? She could not find the word. It was the word she had been trying to write for years.

A ripple of laughter came from the bandstand, thin and silvered, and with it a snatch of a waltz, which was at once gay and sad, like a memory of a party. A group of women passed, their voices bright and brittle as glass. “No, I told her, absolutely not…” “...and the price of butter…” Their talk was a web of small, intricate things, a world she had once moved in, or thought she had. Did they, too, feel this strange doubling, this sense of being both the person who spoke and the person who watched herself speak? Or did they simply live, without this constant, exhausting commentary? Perhaps they were happy. Perhaps happiness was the absence of this second self, this shadow that sat beside her on the bench and questioned everything. She had thought she would have friends who would understand this feeling, but friendship, like everything else, had been drowned in the daily flood of meals and mending and the small, urgent demands of a child’s world. There was only Helen, and even with Helen, there were things one could not say.

“Mummy, look!” Oliver was running towards her, his face flushed, his hands held out. In his cupped palms lay a small, damp clod of earth, and embedded in it, a treasure. “A stone,” he announced, his voice full of awe. She took it. It was a pebble, grey and smooth and utterly ordinary. But in his small, hot hands it had been a jewel, a discovery. She looked at his face, at the absolute conviction in his eyes, and for a moment, the world cracked open. She saw it all as he did: the magnificent, unbelievable reality of a stone. This, then, was it. Not the book, not the great, ungrasped life, but this. This small, muddy hand. This perfect, unthinking love. The person she had thought she would become was a fiction, a character in a novel that would never be written. But this person, this woman on a bench, holding a muddy stone, was real. The sun, at last, found its warmth, and fell across them both, making a single, long shadow on the brilliant, impossible green.
