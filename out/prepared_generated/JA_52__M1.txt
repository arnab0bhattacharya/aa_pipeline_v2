Mr. Edmund Ashworth had never been so well entertained, nor so ill at ease, as he was during the month that Mrs. Eliza Vance spent at Ashworth Park. The entertainment was owing entirely to the lady herself, whose conversation was at once lively and sensible, and whose good humour seemed proof against all the petty vexations of country society. The unease was owing to his sisters, who had determined that he should marry her.

It was a determination formed with the best intentions in the world, and pursued with a perseverance that left him very little peace. His eldest sister, Mrs. Broome, a woman whose views were as long as her face, had calculated the advantages of the match to the last shilling. Miss Ashworth, the younger, had considered the matter of happiness, and concluded that Mrs. Vance’s cheerful disposition must be the very thing to make her brother’s life agreeable. They had, in short, settled the question to their entire satisfaction, and were now engaged in the task of making him as sensible of its merits as they were themselves.

That Mrs. Vance was a widow of considerable fortune, and he a gentleman whose estate, though respectable, was burdened with an entail and three unmarried sisters, was a fact too obvious to be ignored, but too indelicate to be openly discussed. It was alluded to in the frequent mention of the new roof at the Park, of the sorry state of the carriage horses, and of the ancient dowager’s jointure, which was a perpetual drain upon the family’s resources. Edmund, who was perfectly aware of these things, found such reminders a most powerful argument against the union they were meant to secure. He disliked being managed, and the more his sisters pointed to the practical advantages of the match, the more he was inclined to think only of the disadvantages to his own freedom.

His feelings for Mrs. Vance herself were of a more complicated nature. He liked her; he could not help it. She had a frankness of manner that was a relief after the studied civilities of the neighbourhood, and a quickness of wit that kept his own faculties in pleasant exercise. She had borne the loss of her husband with a becoming composure, and spoke of her situation as a widow with a freedom that was as refreshing as it was unorthodox. He had observed her, too, in her conduct to his sisters, and had been struck by the graceful patience with which she endured their attentions. She could not be ignorant of their design; yet she treated them with an unvarying civility that was, perhaps, the highest proof of her good sense.

The design was made manifest to Mrs. Vance in a hundred little ways. Mrs. Broome would speak of the future management of the Park as if Mrs. Vance were already its mistress, and Miss Ashworth would express her conviction that her brother’s taste in plantings was sadly in need of a feminine eye to correct it. On such occasions, Mrs. Vance would reply with a compliment to Edmund’s judgement, or a general observation on the difficulty of altering established habits, which served equally well to deflect the conversation and to give him a secret pleasure. She would not be a party to their scheming, and yet she did not punish him for its existence. He began to suspect that she was as much a victim of the situation as he was, and this suspicion created a bond of sympathy between them that was more potent than any of his sisters’ efforts.

One evening, when the rain kept them all within doors, Miss Ashworth produced a piece of embroidery upon which she was engaged. It was a design for a fire-screen, and represented a faithful copy of the view from the library window.

‘I have a mind to make it a wedding present,’ she observed, with a glance at her brother. ‘But I fear the colours will be too sombre. What is your opinion, Mrs. Vance? Ought there to be more brightness in the foreground?’

The question was so pointed, and the silence that followed so profound, that Edmund felt a flush of anger. He saw Mrs. Vance’s eyes rest for a moment upon the piece, and then lift to meet his own. There was no confusion in her look, but a quiet amusement, as if she were fully aware of the trap that had been laid.

‘I am no great judge of such things,’ she replied, in a tone of perfect composure. ‘But it seems to me that the scene requires a certain gravity. To add a false brightness would be to misrepresent the truth of it. The beauty is in the sobriety of the colours, is it not?’

She directed the last part of the speech to him, and he was obliged to answer.

‘I believe it is,’ he said. ‘The truth of a scene, or of a character, is not to be improved by artifice.’

Mrs. Broome gave a little cough. ‘Well! It is a very pretty thing, at all events. It will look well anywhere.’

The conversation moved on, but the moment had settled something in Edmund’s mind. Mrs. Vance had not only defended him from his sister’s importunity, but had done so with a principle that he could not help but admire. She had shown him that she valued sincerity above convenience. In that instant, he felt a stronger inclination to marry her than he had ever felt before; but it was an inclination born of respect for her character, and not of a desire to escape his sisters’ management.

He was therefore in a state of the most complete uncertainty. To propose to her would be to appear a man led by his nose; to refuse her would be to act in direct opposition to his own happiness and the welfare of his family. He was caught between the claims of affection and the indignity of submission. He resolved, at last, to take a step that was neither one nor the other. He would speak to Mrs. Vance alone, not as a suitor, but as a friend, and discover if her feelings were such as to make his own duty a pleasure rather than a sacrifice.
