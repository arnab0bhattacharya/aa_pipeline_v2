The rain had ceased, but the air in the street remained heavy with the scent of wet stone and coal smoke, a peculiarly Continental mixture that tasted of ash and damp earth. I stood by the window of my narrow room in the Rue de la Paix, watching the water drip from the eaves, when the bell at the bottom of the stairs gave a sharp, insistent rattle. It was not the hesitant knock of a servant, nor the casual tap of a neighbor, but a deliberate, clear sound that seemed to strike a chord in my chest, vibrating with a hope I had no right to harbor.

I descended the stairs, my heart beating with a strange, feverish quickness. At the door stood a woman of perhaps thirty, her face pale and angular, framed by a bonnet of deep violet silk. Her eyes, a clear, piercing grey, fixed upon mine with an intensity that made me falter.

"Mr. Thorne?" she asked. Her voice was low, trembling slightly, but with a precision of diction that was unmistakably our own. "I am Elspeth Vane. My mother was your aunt, Margaret Thorne."

The name struck me like a physical blow. Margaret Thorne—my mother’s sister, who had died before I was born, leaving only a memory of a stern, pious woman who spoke of duty as one speaks of the laws of nature. I had heard whispers of a sister in the colonies, long lost, long forgotten. But to stand before her daughter, this Elspeth, in this cold, foreign city, was a shock to my senses that left me breathless.

"Come in," I said, my voice sounding thin and strange to my own ears. "Please, come in."

She entered, shaking the rain from her cloak with a dignity that belied the shabbiness of her garments. Her dress was of a dark, faded wool, the hem worn thin, and her boots, though clean, were cracked at the toe. Yet there was a pride in her carriage, a stiff-backed rectitude that reminded me of the portraits of my ancestors hanging in the hall of our old home in Yorkshire. I led her to the small parlor, where a single candle burned on the table, casting long, wavering shadows against the whitewashed walls.

"You are surprised," she said, not as a question, but as a statement. She did not sit, but stood by the fireplace, her hands clasped tightly before her. "I know how it must seem. To receive a visitor at this hour, a stranger who claims a bond of blood. But I had no other recourse. I have no friends here, no relatives. I am alone, Mr. Thorne, and the winter is coming on."

Her words were simple, devoid of the usual social pleasantries that often veil desperation. There was a rawness in them, a bareness that cut through my defenses. I poured her a glass of the little brandy I possessed, but her hand trembled as she took it, and I saw the blue veins standing out on the back of her hand, a map of poverty and hunger.

"I have heard stories," I began, my voice faltering. "My mother spoke of your mother, but only in fragments. She said you were... distant. That you had gone away."

"Distant?" Elspeth laughed, a short, sharp sound that held no humor. "No. We were separated by circumstance, not by choice. My mother died young, and I was left with nothing but my name and my pride. I have worked, Mr. Thorne. I have taught, I have served, I have endured. But pride is a cold comfort when one is hungry, and pride is a heavy burden when one is alone in a foreign land."

I looked at her, really looked at her, and I saw not just the cousin I had imagined, but a woman of immense strength, worn down by the very world that my own life had spared. A fierce sense of injustice rose in me, a sudden, burning anger at the fate that had cast her out, that had left her to struggle while I sat in my comfortable, if lonely, exile. I wanted to embrace her, to tell her that I was glad, that she was welcome, that I would give her all I had. But I held my tongue. I am not a man of easy words, and I knew that to speak too freely now would be to betray the dignity of the moment.

"I am in difficulty, Elspeth," I said, using her name for the first time, and the sound of it in my mouth felt like a prayer. "I have little money. My salary is small, and my expenses are many. But I can spare a sum. Fifty pounds. It is not much, but it is all I have."

She looked at me, her grey eyes brightening, a flash of hope breaking through the gloom. "Fifty pounds," she repeated. "It is more than I dared hope. But it will not last long. I need time to find work, to establish myself. But I cannot stay here. This room is too small, the cost too high. I must find a lodging, however humble, where I may be independent."

"Then go," I said, the words rushing out of me. "Go, and take what you need. I will send the money to you tomorrow. But let me ask you, Elspeth—why have you come to me? You are a stranger to me. I have never met you. Why risk the rejection, the humiliation, of coming to a distant cousin whom you have never seen?"

She hesitated, and for a moment, I saw a shadow cross her face, a flicker of uncertainty, or perhaps regret. Then she straightened her spine, and her voice regained its firmness.

"Because I am of your blood, Mr. Thorne. And because I am tired of being a stranger. I have wandered for years, from city to city, always alone, always watching the door for a knock that never came. I wanted to know if there was someone who would open it. If there was someone who would care."
