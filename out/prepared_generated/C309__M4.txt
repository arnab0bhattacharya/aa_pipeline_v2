The house on Servia Street had been respectable once, when Mr. Ellwood was alive and the wool trade was kind. Now the brass knocker was tarnished green, and Mrs. Ellwood counted the days until quarter-day with the grim arithmetic of a quartermaster. The bank’s failure had taken the rest—the small annuity, the second parlour’s furniture, the girl’s music lessons. What remained was the house itself, four storeys of soot-darkened brick, and the necessity of filling its rooms with strangers.

The first lodger was a commercial traveller who smelled of porter and left rings on the mahogany. The second was a pair of sisters, milliners, who quarrelled in whispers and paid late. The third, in the autumn of the year, was a schoolmaster named Mr. Fenwick.

He came with one trunk and a box of books, a man of perhaps forty with spectacles mended at the hinge and a coat brushed threadbare at the cuffs. He taught mathematics and Latin at a grammar school on Woodhouse Lane, and he spoke to Mrs. Ellwood in the careful, measured way of a man who spent his days among boys and had forgotten the cadences of ordinary conversation.

“I keep early hours,” he said, standing in the hall with his hat in his hands. “And I should not care for company.”

“There is no company here,” said Mrs. Ellwood, “only lodgers.”

He took the back room on the second floor, which had the advantage of a window over the yard and the disadvantage of sharing a wall with the stairs. He paid a month in advance, in coin, and Mrs. Ellwood felt the weight of it in her palm like a small reprieve.

Her daughter Clara was nineteen that winter. She had her father’s height and her mother’s habit of silence, and she moved through the house with the efficiency of someone conserving a scarce resource. She had been meant for the Normal School in Ripon—there had been talk of it, before the bank—and now she taught the younger lodgers’ children their letters for sixpence a week and read whatever came to hand. She read the newspaper until it was grey, and the labels on the patent medicines, and once, in desperation, a treatise on sheep rot that the commercial traveller had left behind.

It was Mrs. Ellwood who noticed the books first. Mr. Fenwick had a copy of Euclid bound in calf, and one evening in November Clara was found in the passage outside his door, not listening but waiting, her own shabby primer of arithmetic under her arm, as though she had been about to knock and thought better of it.

“You might ask him,” said Mrs. Ellwood, drying her hands on her apron. “He is a schoolmaster. It is his trade.”

“I do not care to be a nuisance,” said Clara.

But the next week Mrs. Ellwood came up from the kitchen with the tea things and found the door of the back room ajar, and within it her daughter seated at the table with a slate, and Mr. Fenwick standing at the window with his back to her, saying, “No, no—if the angle is obtuse, you must subtract, not add. Begin again.”

Mrs. Ellwood stood in the passage a moment. The lamp was lit; the fire was low; her daughter’s head was bent over the slate, and the schoolmaster had turned and was looking at her with an expression Mrs. Ellwood could not read. She went down again without disturbing them, and in the kitchen she sat for a long while with the teapot growing cold in her hands.

She was not a foolish woman. She had been married twenty-two years and knew the look of a man who is interested. She had seen it in her own husband, once, before the wool trade had soured and the annuity had shrunk and the house had begun to fill with strangers. Mr. Fenwick was not handsome, and he was not rich, and he was perhaps too old for a girl of nineteen; but he was steady, and he was learned, and he had no wife that anyone had heard of. A schoolmaster’s wife would not starve. A schoolmaster’s wife would have a house of her own, and a position, and a name.

She began to watch. She noticed that Mr. Fenwick took his tea in the back room now, rather than the parlour, and that Clara carried it up herself. She noticed that the lessons had grown longer—three evenings a week, then four—and that Clara had begun to speak of “Mr. Fenwick says” the way she had once spoken of her father. She noticed, too, that Mr. Fenwick had taken to shaving twice a week instead of once, and that he had bought a new collar.

“He is very kind,” said Clara, when her mother ventured a remark. “He says I have a head for figures. He says I might sit the examination at the Normal School if I apply myself.”

“And what would you do at the Normal School?”

“Teach.” Clara’s voice was flat. “Girls are wanted for the board schools. It is respectable, and it pays, and I should not have to—” She stopped.

“Not have to what?”

“Nothing, Mama.”

But Mrs. Ellwood knew what she had nearly said. Not have to take lodgers. Not have to count the coal. Not have to live in a house that smelled of other people’s dinners.

In January, Mrs. Ellwood found a letter on the hall table, addressed to Clara in a hand she recognised. It was not sealed, and she held it a long moment, and then she put it back. She was not that sort of woman. But she had seen the superscription, and she knew the hand, and she lay awake that night listening to the wind in the chimney and thinking of her daughter’s future as though it were a sum she could not make balance.
