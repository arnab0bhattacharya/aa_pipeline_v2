The fog had come up from the river, thick and yellow, muffling the clatter of hooves on Cheapside and turning the gas lamps into hazy coronas of light. Clara smoothed her glove over the cold iron of the railing and looked up at the building one last time. Four storeys of soot-stained Portland stone, a brass plate beside the door that she had polished herself on more mornings than she could count. Ashworth & Pell, Solicitors. The letters were worn at the edges now, the ampersand nearly smooth.

She had a key still. Mr. Ashworth had not asked for it back, and she had not offered. It sat in her reticule now, a small cold weight against her handkerchief and the folded letter from Arthur that she had read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. Tomorrow at eleven o'clock she would stand beside him in the church at Clerkenwell and become Mrs. Arthur Rennick, and the key would be a thing she no longer had any business carrying.

The lock turned easily. The stairwell smelled of dust and coal smoke and the particular dry scent of old paper, and her footsteps on the linoleum were the only sound. She had not expected to feel anything so sharp. Ten years. She had come here at nineteen, a girl from Wandsworth with a good hand and a reference from the parish school, and Mr. Ashworth had looked at her copy of the Lord's Prayer and said, "Neat. Very neat. Can you spell 'conscientious'?" She had spelled it. She had been spelling it, in one form or another, ever since.

The outer office was dim, the blinds drawn. Her desk sat beneath the window, its surface bare except for the inkwell and the blotter, which she had not been able to bring herself to take away. The typewriter was covered with its oilcloth hood, and she stood for a moment with her hand resting on it, the way one might rest a hand on the head of a sleeping child. A Remington No. 6. She had typed on it for six years, since the old Yost had finally given up its ghost, and she knew its temperament as well as she knew her own. The way the 'e' stuck in damp weather. The way the carriage return needed a firm hand, not a rough one. She had typed thousands of letters on it — leases, affidavits, bills of sale, the endless correspondence of other people's properties and other people's quarrels — and it had never once betrayed her.

She sat down in her chair. It was a good chair, better than the one at her lodgings, with a horsehair seat that had moulded itself to her over the years. From here she could see the rooftops across the way, the chimney pots, a gull wheeling in the fog. She had looked at that view through ten years of mornings, and she had never once been sorry to see it.

Arthur did not understand this. He was a kind man — she would not have said yes to a man who was not kind — and he was steady and generous and he had a house in Islington with a garden and a piano in the front room that nobody played. He was fifteen years older than she was, a widower with a grown son, and he had asked her to marry him in the spring with a gentleness that had made her cry. But when she had said, "And my work, Arthur?" he had laughed, not unkindly, and said, "My dear girl, you won't need to work. You'll have a home to keep. That's work enough for any woman."

He had meant it as a kindness. That was the trouble. He had meant it as a liberation, and she had smiled and said nothing, because what could she say? That the thirty shillings a week she earned at Ashworth & Pell was the only money in the world that was hers, not her father's, not a husband's, not anyone's? That she had paid for her own lodgings in Camberwell for eight years, two rooms and a gas ring and a landlady who did not pry, and that the small independence of that life was the thing she was most afraid of losing? That she had once typed a letter to a Mrs. Hemmings of Brixton, a woman who had left her husband and was fighting for her own money, and that she had gone home that night and cried, not for Mrs. Hemmings but for the strange fierce gladness of typing the words "I am quite capable of managing my own affairs"?

She could not say any of it. Arthur would have been hurt, and he would not have understood, and in the end she would have said yes anyway, because she was thirty-nine years old and the world did not offer many second chances, and because she did love him, in her way. He was good to her. He brought her tea when she was tired. He did not mind that she read novels. He had once said, "You have a fine mind, Clara," and she had thought about that for a week.

But she had wanted to come here one last time. She had told Arthur she was going to see her aunt in Peckham, and instead she had taken the omnibus to the City and walked the last half mile in the fog, and now she was sitting at her desk in the dark with her gloves still on, and she did not want to leave.

She uncovered the typewriter. The keys gleamed faintly in the light from the street. She fed a sheet of paper into the carriage — there was a stack of it in the drawer, and she supposed no one would mind — and she sat for a moment with her fingers resting on the keys, the way she did before beginning a difficult letter, when the words had to be got exactly right.
