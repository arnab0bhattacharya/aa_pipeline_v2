It was upon a raw, foggy morning in the latter days of November, 1894, that I found myself standing in the front parlour of a lodging-house in Bloomsbury, watching my companion, Mr. Septimus Edge, as he subjected the carpet to the closest scrutiny. He was down upon his hands and knees, his sharp, aquiline face thrust forward, and his grey eyes darting from side to side with the intense concentration which I had learned to recognize as the prelude to some swift deduction.

The lodging-house belonged to a Mrs. Hibbert, a respectable, middle-aged widow, who stood near the door, twisting her apron strings in an agony of apprehension. She had summoned Edge to her aid upon the previous evening, and I had accompanied him, for my medical practice was slack at the time, and my interest in his singular methods was keen.

"You state, Mrs. Hibbert," said Edge, without raising his head, "that your lodger, Mr. Paul Vane, occupied this room for a full six weeks, that he paid you three months in advance, and that he left, apparently in a hurry, upon the evening of the 18th. I observe a slight fraying of the nap upon this particular board, just beneath the window. It was here, I take it, that he placed his chair each night?"

The landlady started. "Yes, sir. He would sit by the window from dusk until two in the morning, burning a candle there. It was a tall wax candle, sir, not the common tallow, and it puzzled me that a gentleman so careful in his habits should sit up so late and burn good wax when gas was laid on in the room."

Edge rose to his feet, brushing the dust from his knees with his finger-tips. "A very singular taste," he remarked. "A tall wax candle, placed in a window overlooking the street, burning until precisely two. One might almost fancy it was a signal, were it not that the blinds were invariably drawn."

"He always drew the blinds, sir, close-drawn, the moment the dark set in. And he never received a letter, nor a visitor, the whole time he was with me. Yet he was a well-spoken gentleman, quiet and civil, though dark in his looks. He wore his left glove even in the house, and I remarked that the right hand was stained brown, as if with some chemical."

I ventured an observation. "A photographer, perhaps?"

"It is possible," said Edge. "But a photographer who wears but one glove has something to conceal. Pray, Mrs. Hibbert, what did Mr. Vane take with him when he departed?"

"Only a small hand-bag, sir. He left his trunk behind." She indicated a stout, leather portmanteau which stood against the wall. "I have not opened it, sir, for I am a lone woman and it is locked."

Edge produced from his pocket a small leather case of tools. "I shall ask your permission to examine it. The key, I presume, he carried off."

In a surprisingly short time the lock yielded to his dexterous manipulation. The lid fell back, revealing a layer of neatly packed clothing. Edge lifted out coat after coat, shirt after shirt, with swift, searching fingers. At the very bottom he paused. "Ah!" he cried, and drew forth a small object wrapped in tissue paper. It was a brass cylinder, some four inches in length, fitted with a lens at one end and a delicate screw adjustment at the other.

"What is it?" I asked.

"It is the eyepiece of a theodolite," said Edge, turning it over in his long, quivering fingers. "But that is not all. You see this dark discoloration upon the brass? That, my dear fellow, is the residue of silver nitrate, and it has been applied with a small brush. He has been coating the lens itself with a sensitized solution. Yes, and here, concealed in the lining of the trunk, is a second lens, similarly treated."

Mrs. Hibbert looked bewildered. "I do not understand, sir."

"Nor is it necessary that you should," said Edge, with a reassuring smile. "But tell me, did Mr. Vane ever leave the house at night?"

"Never, sir. He went out each morning at ten, returning at one, and was not seen again until the next day. But his boots were always wet at the toes when he came in, and there was often a yellow clay upon the soles."

"Yellow clay," Edge repeated. "There is no yellow clay in Bloomsbury. But there is, if I mistake not, a quantity of it in the excavations for the new drainage works at King's Cross. And it was at King's Cross that the Great Northern mail train was held up, on the night of the 17th, by persons unknown, who removed a sealed packet from the mail-van. You will have read of it in the papers, Mrs. Hibbert."

The landlady nodded, her eyes wide. "A terrible business, sir. The guard was struck down."

"Precisely. And the packet contained a quantity of uncut diamonds, consigned from Amsterdam to a firm of Hatton Garden jewellers. Now, observe the sequence of events. A lodger takes a room on the 1st of October, pays three months in advance in gold—he paid in gold, I think?"

"Yes, sir."

"He never receives letters, he never entertains visitors. He sits up each night until two, burning a candle in the window. He wears one glove to conceal some peculiarity of his left hand—I should guess a missing finger, for the glove is stuffed with cotton-wool at the third digit—and his right hand is stained with silver nitrate. He goes out daily to King's Cross, returning with yellow clay on his boots. And on the night of the 18th, the day after the robbery, he vanishes, leaving his trunk behind him."
