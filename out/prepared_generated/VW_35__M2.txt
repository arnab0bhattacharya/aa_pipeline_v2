The light in the Reading Room did not fall so much as it hung, a pale, suspended dust of afternoon, settling on the spines of the *Times* and the *Daily Telegraph* stacked in their neat, indifferent rows. Colonel Arthur Penhaligon sat in the leather chair that had worn a hollow for his back over the last decade, a hollow as specific to him as a footprint in clay. He did not move. To move would be to admit that time had passed, and time, in the club, was a thing that was supposed to be ignored, a background hum to the ritual of the morning paper.

He unfolded the broadsheet. The ink was fresh, smelling faintly of the press, of the river, of the city beyond the window where the motor-cars were beginning to chug in their grey procession. But the words did not enter him. They sat on the surface, like rain on a waxed car, beading and sliding away. *The Crisis in the Levant. A New Play at the Lyceum. The Price of Wheat.* It was all so solid, so terribly concrete, while inside his head there was only a vast, echoing silence, a cathedral of memory where the pews were empty and the candles were long since burned down to stubs.

He remembered the mud. Not the mud of the trenches, which was a dark, sucking, eternal thing, but the mud of the garden at home, that pale, dry dust that rose in little clouds when the wind shifted. It was a trivial memory, a speck in the vastness of the day, yet it held a weight that the news of the war never had. He traced the line of a paragraph with his eye, but his mind was elsewhere, drifting back to the smell of pipe tobacco and the sound of a clock ticking in a room that no longer existed. The years had a way of smoothing things, of polishing the rough edges of grief until they became invisible, leaving only a smooth, hard surface that reflected nothing but the present.

The waiter, a boy with the air of one who had seen too much for his age, approached with the silver tray. He placed a cup of tea, still steaming, and a plate of sardines upon the small table beside the Colonel. Arthur did not look up. The clink of the saucer on the wood was a sharp, intrusive sound, a needle pricking the fabric of his reverie. He picked up a sardine. It was cold, oily, and bright with mustard. He ate it. The taste was metallic, a flash of salt on the tongue that was gone before it could be named. He ate another. The meal was not an act of hunger but of routine, a mechanical feeding of the body to keep it in its place, in the chair, in the club, while the mind wandered.

Outside, the light began to change. The pale suspension gave way to a deeper, more golden hue as the sun dipped lower in the west. The shadows in the room lengthened, stretching across the parquet floor, reaching for the legs of the tables, for the hands of the men who sat there reading, talking, sleeping. It was a time for endings, Arthur thought, or perhaps beginnings; it was hard to tell the difference. The club was a place of suspension, a bubble between the work of the day and the rest of the night, and in that bubble, time seemed to lose its linear quality. It was not a sequence of moments, but a single, continuous state of being.

He had finished the paper. He folded it, a precise, triangular shape, and placed it on the arm of the chair. He did not move to leave. There was nowhere to go. The house in Kensington was empty, a series of rooms with the doors closed, holding their breath. He preferred the company of the strangers, even the silence of it. It was a silence that was shared, a common ground.

Then, the telegram arrived.

It was not a message of war, nor of death, nor of any great event. It was a small, white slip of paper, handed to him by the same waiter, who looked at him with a mixture of curiosity and pity. Arthur took it. The paper was light, almost weightless, but it carried a gravity that made the air in the room seem to thicken.

*SON MARRIES SATURDAY. BRIDE UNKNOWN. PLEASE ACKNOWLEDGE.*

The words were stark, printed in black capitals, no room for interpretation, no softening of the blow. *Son.* The word hung in the air, vibrating. He had not spoken to him in months. No, years. There had been a falling out, a silence that had grown so thick it had become a wall, a barrier of stone and thorn. He had assumed it would last forever, that the distance was permanent, that the son was a figure in a painting, viewed from a great remove, safe and static.

But now, the paper said, the son was marrying.

*Brude unknown.*

The phrase struck him with the force of a physical blow. He did not know the bride. He had no image of her, no memory of a face, a voice, a gesture. She was a blank space, a void in the center of his life, a hole through which the wind of the future might blow. Who was she? A girl from the house in Kensington? A stranger from the city? A daughter of a family he knew, or a name he had never heard? The possibilities swirled in his mind, chaotic and formless, like dust in a sunbeam.

He looked at the other men in the room. They were reading, talking, laughing. They were alive, moving through the day, their lives intersecting and diverging in a complex web of connections. He felt suddenly isolated, cut off from that web, a single thread pulled loose. He thought of his own wife, dead these twenty years. He remembered her hands, the way she held her teacup, the sound of her laughter. But that memory was fading, becoming a dream, a thing of the past. And now, this new woman, this *unknown*, was to take her place? To fill the void?
