Mr. Edmund Vane had, for the space of an hour and a quarter, considered his dinner party at Ashby Lodge to be proceeding with a perfection which reflected the highest credit upon his own management. The company was select, the soup was excellent, and the conversation, moving from the late harvest to the vexing price of corn, maintained a level of seriousness appropriate to the gentlemen present and not entirely disagreeable to the ladies. Seated at the head of his table, with the warm light of the candles gilding the polished silver, Mr. Vane felt a serene satisfaction in the order he had created. His younger brother, Captain Thomas Vane of His Majesty’s frigate *Alacrity*, had been a source of some anxiety, his arrival being expected at any time but with no certainty as to the hour; and Edmund, a man who preferred appointments to be kept and formalities to be observed, had been prepared to be either annoyed by an untimely entrance or vexed by a complete absence.

The entrance, when it came, was of a kind he had not foreseen. It was not the quiet arrival of a traveller, but the sudden appearance of a figure who seemed to bring the very winds and waves of the Atlantic into the tranquil drawing-room. Captain Vane, his coat unbrushed and his complexion tanned to a degree that made the gentlemen of the county look like so many hothouse plants, burst in upon them with a cry of “Edmund! I knew I should find you at your dinner!” and a heartiness of greeting that set the crystal on the sideboard trembling. He clapped his brother on the shoulder with a force that threatened the stability of his host, and bowed to the assembled company with a charming, if somewhat nautical, sweep.

Edmund’s mortification was immediate and profound. He felt the eyes of Sir William and Lady Croft upon him, and saw the faint, amused smile of their daughter, Miss Eleanor Croft, the very young woman upon whose good opinion he had been carefully building a case for the past month. To have his orderly world thus invaded by a creature of chaos was a blow to his dignity from which he feared he might not recover. “Thomas,” he said, his voice tight with a civility that cost him dearly, “we had not expected you so soon. You will find that we are at dinner.”

“So I see, so I see!” the Captain replied, utterly blind to the reproof. “And a fine dinner it is, by the look of it. But a man who has been at sea for six months cannot stand upon ceremony. I have a story for you all that will make your hair stand on end—about the wreck of the *Bellerophon* off the Cape of Good Hope! We were there, you know, and but for the grace of God and a stout bit of sailing, I should not be here to tell the tale.”

There was nothing for it but to offer him a place. A footman, under Edmund’s severe direction, hastily laid another cover. The Captain, seated opposite Miss Croft, was in his element. He ignored the delicate poached fish and launched into his narrative with a gusto that commanded the attention of the entire table. He spoke of mountainous seas, of splitting timbers, of a crew clinging to the rigging as the ship went down, and of his own boat, tossed like a cork upon the waves for two days and nights before making landfall. His language was plain, his gestures broad, and his tale possessed a raw, unvarnished power that made the polite accounts of travel in the ladies’ magazines seem like so much nursery gossip.

Miss Croft, to Edmund’s utter despair, was captivated. Her eyes were wide, her lips parted, and she forgot all about her fish as she leaned forward, urging the Captain on with questions. “And were you not afraid, Captain?” she asked, her voice full of a genuine feeling that Edmund had never managed to inspire in her. “Did you think all was lost?”

“Afraid, Miss Croft?” the Captain laughed, a booming, infectious sound. “A man is too busy for fear when he is trying to save his ship and his men! But I will own that when the mast went, a cold hand did seem to clutch at my heart. It was then I thought of home, and of my brother’s good claret, and I resolved that one or the other should see me through.”

This artless compliment to Edmund’s wine did little to soothe Edmund’s spirit. He saw Sir William listening with the condescending interest of a landowner hearing a tenant’s account of a flood, while Lady Croft fanned herself with an expression of delicate horror. The Captain, warmed by his audience and the claret he praised so highly, grew ever more expansive. He told of strange lands and stranger customs, of a monkey he had acquired in the Indies and a duel he had fought in Lisbon over a misplaced glove. Each story was a fresh assault upon the decorum of the evening, a new proof of the impropriety of his brother’s character.

The dessert, a grand affair of jellies and syllabubs and a magnificent pineapple, was placed upon the table, and Edmund prayed that the change of course might signal a change in the conversation. His prayer was not answered. The Captain, having polished off a third glass of port, turned his full, beaming attention upon Miss Croft. “And now, ma’am,” he said, his voice dropping to a confidential, yet still perfectly audible, level, “I have heard from my brother that you are a young lady of sense and spirit.
