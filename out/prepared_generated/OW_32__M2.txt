The hall in Kensington was not merely a room, but a sort of architectural apology for the fact that beauty had not yet been discovered by the commonwealth. It was a long, narrow space, painted in a shade of yellow that suggested either mustard or regret, and the air hung heavy with the scent of damp wool and unspoken anxieties. At the head of the hall sat the Committee for the Encouragement of Plain Living and Moral Rectitude, a body of ladies whose faces were set with the rigid determination of women who had decided that joy was a form of laziness. Mrs. Agatha Pemberton, the chairwoman, wore a hat of such severe simplicity that it looked less like headgear and more like a warning against vanity. She adjusted her spectacles, which were small, round, and fiercely practical, and looked out over the assembled audience with the air of a woman preparing to serve a lecture on the spiritual superiority of boiled cabbage.

The audience was a mixture of the devoutly earnest and the painfully curious. They sat in rows of wooden chairs that creaked under the weight of their moral certainties. The lecture was to be delivered by Mr. Julian Vane, a man of whom they had heard whispers in the drawing rooms of Mayfair. He was said to be an aesthete of the first order, a devotee of the decorative, a patron of the grotesque, and a man who had once described a bouquet of lilies as "a crime against nature, committed by the flower itself." They had invited him not because they wished to learn, but because they wished to win. They believed that by refuting his theories, they would prove the enduring superiority of the plain and the useful. They expected him to be a monster, a creature of feathers and perfumes, who would trip over his own long hair while trying to explain the metaphysical implications of a teacup.

When Mr. Vane entered, the room exhaled a collective sigh of disappointment. He was not a monster. He was, in fact, quite ordinary in his appearance, dressed in a simple suit of dark grey wool, with a white tie that was perhaps a fraction too wide, but no more so than the fashion of the moment. He carried no flowers, wore no jewels, and his hair was cut short in a style that suggested a preference for hygiene over art. He walked to the podium with a quiet, almost apologetic grace, as if he were intruding upon a quiet conversation.

Mrs. Pemberton struck the gavel, a small wooden thing that made a sound like a broken bone. "Mr. Vane," she said, her voice crisp and clear as ice water, "we are here to discuss the virtues of plain living. You have been asked to speak against these virtues, so that we may demonstrate their truth. We expect you to argue for excess, for ornament, for the superfluous. Please, proceed."

Mr. Vane smiled. It was a gentle smile, devoid of sarcasm, which made it all the more unsettling. "Mrs. Pemberton," he began, his voice low and musical, like the sound of a distant flute, "I am afraid I have misunderstood the terms of my engagement. I was not asked to argue for excess. I was asked to speak against plain living. And I must confess that I find the task difficult, for I do not know what you mean by 'plain.' Is it plain in its simplicity, or plain in its emptiness? Is it the blankness of a canvas before the artist’s hand, or the barrenness of a field after the harvest? I suspect you mean the latter, but I am not sure."

Mrs. Pemberton frowned. "We mean plainness of diet, Mr. Vane. We mean the rejection of the lavish, the ornate, the unnecessary. We mean the return to nature, to the simple, the wholesome, the good."

"Ah," said Mr. Vane. "The return to nature. A phrase I have heard so often that it has lost all meaning. To return to nature is to return to the mud, to the rain, to the cold. It is to be hungry, to be tired, to be afraid. It is to be alive, yes, but alive in the way that a stone is alive, without the comfort of art, without the shelter of style. You speak of the wholesome, Mrs. Pemberton, as if wholeness were a virtue in itself. But a broken plate is often more beautiful than a whole one, for it reveals the pattern of the glaze. A single rose is beautiful, but a bouquet is a triumph. Why do you seek to strip the world of its ornament? Do you not see that ornament is the language of the soul? Without it, we are mute. Without it, we are naked."

The room was silent. The ladies stared at him, their mouths slightly open. He continued, his voice rising slightly, not in volume, but in intensity.

"You call your plain living a moral duty. But morality, my dear ladies, is a matter of taste. It is the taste for the right thing, the beautiful thing, the true thing. To be plain is to be dull. And dullness is the only sin that cannot be forgiven, for it kills the spirit. You wish to live simply, but simplicity is not the absence of complexity; it is the mastery of it. A plain wall is not simple; it is empty. A plain face is not simple; it is unfinished. You seek to live in a world of grey, and call it virtue. But the world is made of colour. To deny colour is to deny life. To deny ornament is to deny the joy of the eye. And a life without joy is not a life; it is a waiting room for death."
