The tempest, which had for three days lowered over the dark hills of Gloucestershire, burst forth with redoubled fury on the evening I arrived at Thornwood Hall. I had returned from the Peninsula, wearing the faded uniform of a soldier, bearing the honorable scars of Badajoz, and nursing a heart wounded far more deeply by the sudden death of my father in my absence. I was an orphan, but I was betrothed to Eleanor, a being of ethereal sensibility and unyielding virtue; and as my carriage navigated the deeply rutted lanes, bordered by ancient, groaning elms, I fondly imagined that beneath her gentle care my grief would find a balm, and my shattered spirit be restored to hope. Yet, as the heavy wheels crunched upon the gravel of the courtyard, I carried in my bosom a secret—a leaden, suffocating weight—which I had discovered among my father’s mercantile papers in London, and which threatened to transform the healing light of my anticipated union into an enduring hell.

I was shown into the library, where a cheerful fire roared in the vast hearth, casting flickering, giant-like shadows upon the oaken panels. There stood Eleanor, her pale, intellectual face illuminated by the blaze, her dark eyes reflecting a sorrow that mirrored my own. She advanced towards me, and as I clasped her hands, the contact of her pure, elevated nature sent a violent tremor through my frame. How could I, who had marched undaunted against the batteries of French cannon, now tremble like a frail child before the gaze of a woman? Because I knew, with an agonizing certainty, that I was about to shatter the sanctuary of her trust.

"You are weary, Lionel," she murmured, her voice fraught with the tenderest commiseration; "you have passed through scenes of blood and death, and the journey through this dreadful storm has exhausted you. Sit by me, and let us forget that war, or grief, or the wide world has any existence beyond these walls."

Her words, so full of soothing sweetness, pierced me to the soul. I could not accept the solace she offered while a gulf of deception yawned between us. I abruptly withdrew my hands, and turning my face towards the tempest-beaten casement, I strove to command the wild tumult of my spirits. The rain lashed against the glass, and the wind howled through the crevices of the ancient mansion, as if the elements without sympathetically echoed the desolation that was about to rend my bosom.

"Eleanor," I began, and my voice, despite my struggles, was broken and hollow, "I have come home to claim you, yet I fear I bring with me a heritage of shame, more terrible than the battlefields I have escaped. My father is dead, and his memory, which I had been taught to revere as the architect of our family’s honor, is a lie—a ghastly, festering phantom that will haunt our days."

She gazed at me with an expression of startled wonder. "What mean you, my beloved? Your father was an honorable merchant; his wealth was the reward of honest enterprise."

"Honest enterprise!" I echoed, a bitter, despairing laugh escaping my lips. "Oh, that it were so! Would that the grave could bury his secrets as it has buried his frame! But the dead, Eleanor, have a cruel power to blast the living. I have read the ledgers, the letters, the secret correspondences. The fortune that built this hall, that purchased my commission, that adorns your finger with my mother's jewels, was coined in the blood and groans of enslaved Africans. His ships, which I thought ploughed the oceans for innocent spices and silks, were fraught with human misery—chains, and whips, and unutterable woe."

At these words, Eleanor’s countenance underwent a swift and terrible transformation. The soft, roseate hue of her cheeks faded to a deathly, ashen paleness, and her large eyes dilated with a horror that seemed to freeze the very current of her life. She sank into a chair, pressing her hands against her forehead, as if to shut out some monstrous apparition. She had, from her earliest youth, dedicated her soul to the abolition of that infernal traffic; she had spoken with burning eloquence against the traffickers in human flesh; and now, the man she loved, the man she was to wed, was revealed as the heir to their accursed gains.

"Speak not so, Lionel," she whispered, her voice trembling with a suppressed agony that cut me more deeply than any sword-thrust. "Say that you are deceived—that some villain has forged these proofs to blast your name. It cannot be that the wealth which has nourished your gentle nature, which has enabled you to protect the weak and honor the brave, was wrung from the agonies of chained and bleeding men. Tell me it is not so!"

"I cannot, Eleanor; I cannot," I replied, throwing myself at her feet, while the tears, which a soldier’s pride had often sternly repressed, now gushed forth in a torrent of unmanly weakness. "I have seen the very vessels' manifests. I have read how he bargained for human souls, bartering gold for the broken hearts and shattered bodies of his fellow creatures. And this money—this blood-stained gold—it is mine. Nay, it is yours; it has paid for the very roof that shelters us. I am wealthy, yet I am a beggar, for I possess nothing that I can call my own without invoking a curse. I came hither to ask you to be my wife, but I find I am bringing you to a nuptial bed polluted by the groans of the enslaved."

The wind howled with redoubled fury without, shaking the lattice, as if the spirits of the wronged were clamoring for admittance. Eleanor sat motionless, her eyes fixed upon the embers, where the fading fire cast a baleful, lurid glow. At length she spoke, and her voice, though it was low, rung in my ears with the solemnity of a funeral knell.
