It is an axiom too well settled in the social code to be disputed by any single instance, that a young lady of unexceptionable character, when introduced to a gentleman of unexceptionable character, must find in the exchange of civilities a source of unalloyed pleasure. Yet such is the peculiar capriciousness of the human mind, that this maxim, while it governs the conduct of nine persons in ten, is liable to be suspended in the remaining one, not by any defect in the parties, but by the sudden, unbidden intrusion of memory.

Miss Eliza Harcourt, sitting in the drawing-room of her aunt, Mrs. Fairchild, at her lodgings in Belgravia, felt the full force of this suspension at that very moment. She had been engaged, with the necessary languor of a woman who has danced but once and wishes to appear indifferent, in a conversation with Mrs. Fairchild regarding the quality of the silk on the lady’s gown. But her attention had been roused, and subsequently arrested, by the arrival of a gentleman who had been announced as Mr. Arthur Thorne.

The name had struck her with a force that was, if not painful, certainly unwelcome. It was a name she had heard with a regularity that bordered on the tedious during her childhood in the country. It was the name of the man with whom her brother, the late Mr. Harcourt, had engaged in a dispute so violent and so final that it had severed the family from its nearest neighbors for the better part of a decade. Eliza had been too young then to comprehend the points of law or honor that had been contested; she remembered only the sound of raised voices, the slamming of doors, and the subsequent silence that hung over the estate like a heavy fog. She had always assumed, with the comfortable ignorance of youth, that the cause was some trivial misunderstanding of property lines or perhaps a disagreement over the management of the local game. But she had never imagined she would meet Mr. Thorne in such a light, nor that his face would be so instantly recognizable as that of a man she had seen only in the negative space of her brother’s anger.

Mr. Thorne was a man of perhaps thirty-five, with a countenance that possessed a certain austere regularity. He was not handsome, for his features were too hard, his expression too reserved, to claim that epithet; but he was distinguished by an air of quiet dignity that commanded immediate respect. He was dressed with the simplicity of a man who had no need to assert his rank by his clothes, yet his coat and waistcoat spoke of the best tailors in the city. When he was presented to Eliza by Mrs. Fairchild, who performed the duty with the brisk efficiency of a hostess who knew the value of a connection, Eliza felt her cheeks flush with a heat that was not entirely due to the crowded room.

"Mr. Thorne," said Mrs. Fairchild, with a smile that was meant to be inviting but which, in the circumstances, seemed to Eliza rather like a challenge. "I have the pleasure of introducing my niece, Miss Harcourt."

Eliza rose. She could not help but watch his face. There was no surprise in it, nor any overt acknowledgment of the past. He bowed, a deep, precise bow that spoke of perfect breeding, and took her hand with a courtesy that was cold but undeniably polite.

"Miss Harcourt," he said, his voice low and clear. "I am happy to make your acquaintance."

Eliza forced a smile. It was a dangerous moment. One word of misplaced familiarity, one too eager query, might betray the secret she had carried for so long without understanding. She must appear as every other young lady in the room: interested, perhaps, but not engaged.

"I have heard so much of Mr. Thorne," she replied, keeping her tone light and airy, "that I confess I felt a little shy of the reality. One hears so many good things, and they are often so difficult to live up to in person."

It was a safe compliment, standard fare for such an occasion, yet she watched him closely. Did he know? Did he know that she was the sister of the man he had once opposed? There was a slight tightening of the lips, a fraction of a second’s hesitation before he answered, that told her he did. He knew who she was. And yet, he chose to say nothing.

"Your flattery is excessive, Miss Harcourt," he said, with a faint, dry smile. "I am a man of very few accomplishments, and even fewer virtues. I am merely here to observe the season, as so many of us are, and to endure the music."

"And I am here," Eliza said, deciding to take a small risk, "to endure the conversation. Though I find Mr. Thorne’s silence to be far more agreeable than the noise of the orchestra."

It was a bold stroke. It was an invitation, however subtle, to speak of the things that were not being said. He looked at her then, and for a moment, the mask of public politeness slipped, revealing a depth of intelligence and perhaps a hint of amusement beneath.

"Silence is often the safest refuge," he said, his eyes holding hers with an intensity that made her heart beat faster. "But it can also be the most difficult to maintain, when one is surrounded by so much effort to be heard."

Mrs. Fairchild, who had been hovering nearby, sensing the dynamic shift, intervened with a cough. "I hope you are not being too severe, Mr. Thorne? The music is quite tolerable, I assure you. And I am sure Miss Harcourt will not let you off so easily."

Eliza felt a wave of relief. The net was being cast wider, and she was safe within it. But as Mr. Thorne turned to address Mrs. Fairchild, speaking of the pianist’s technique with a critical precision that revealed a deep knowledge of the art, Eliza found herself watching him with a new and unsettling interest.
