Arthur Pemberton had carried the image of Hester Vayne across the Mediterranean and through the echoing, vaulted corridors of embassies, as a man carries a talisman, the material weight of which reminds him that there is a steady, ordering light against the encroaching shadows of a foreign world. To return to England, then, and to the sombre, damp sweep of Thornel Park, was to anticipate the immediate translation of that long-nurtured image into the palpable substance of daily life. But the sudden intelligence that Hester had dissolved their engagement to devote her existence to nursing the sick in a London hospital had fallen upon his understanding with the perplexing shock of an anachronism. He was a man in whom the cultivated habit of public affairs had bred a profound belief in the calculability of human motives; he could not conceive that a woman, whose chief charm had hitherto lain in her pliant, domestic grace, should voluntarily exchange the spacious, ordered future of Thornel for the squalid fatigue of a ward. It appeared to him not as a solemn vocation, but as a transient and somewhat morbid enthusiasm, born perhaps of a too sensitive conscience, which a little judicious reasoning would readily disperse.

He found her in the narrow, gas-lit street of a dismal quarter, emerging from the heavy wooden door of the hospital in her plain, grey dress, looking thinner and older than the Hester of his remembrance, with a shadow of habitual exhaustion about her eyes. His own presence, in its dark, well-ordered travelling garb, seemed to strike a note of incongruous, alien elegance against the grimy London twilight. He spoke to her with a restrained impatience, arguing the case for their shared life as one might argue a clause in a treaty, balancing the advantages of his estate against the illusory merits of her new profession. She listened without resentment, her hands folded quietly, her gaze resting on the uneven paving stones as if she saw there some invisible, urgent footprint.

“I do not ask you to abandon a mere fancy, Arthur,” she said at length, her voice low, with a tremor that was not of uncertainty, but of suppressed feeling; “I ask you to come inside with me, and look for ten minutes at what I am doing, before you judge it a whim.”

He followed her into the hospital, through a stone corridor that smelled of ipecac and boiled linen, to a ward where the twilight struggled against the heavy shadows. They halted by a cot where a faded woman lay, her breath catching in a difficult, asthmatic wheeze. A child, a little sufferer of some wasting fever, was in the next bed, tossing restlessly with a low, continuous moan. Hester bent over the child, raising its head with a touch that seemed to Arthur, watching her, to possess a new and strange authority, a gentle, practical tenderness that was far removed from the mere softness he had formerly admired. She drew a cup of water, and held it to the dry, fevered lips, murmuring a low, soothing sound that fell upon the moaning as a soft rain falls upon a parched field. The child grew quieter; the woman in the next bed turned her face on the pillow and looked at Hester with eyes in which the dullness of pain was relieved by a faint, clinging trust.

Arthur stood apart, a sudden, unwonted compunction taking hold of him. He had been accustomed to dealing with life from the outside, as a sequence of events to be directed; here was life from the inside, a struggling, suffering force that defied all external arrangement. He saw, with a flash of insight that was almost humiliating to his diplomatic pride, that Hester’s heart had expanded to the measure of a great, dumb need, and that the love she had once given him was not withheld, but transmuted into a wider, sterner charity.

They passed out again into the street, and the cool air struck their faces after the close, oppressive atmosphere of the ward. He looked at her, and saw that she was trembling, not with cold, but with the reaction from the strain of her day’s work and the suppressed emotion of the interview. He felt, in that moment, that the bond between them was broken, not by her act, but by the inevitable movement of their respective lives; he saw, too, that the gentle, pliant girl he had left behind had become a woman with a will of her own, whom he could not bend to his purposes without doing violence to the very nature he had loved.

“It is not a whim, Hester,” he said, slowly and with a heavy effort. “I was wrong. But I had hoped—”

“I know, Arthur,” she interrupted gently, her eyes filling with tears. “I know what you had hoped. I had hoped it, too, once. But I cannot go back to the old, narrow life. I have seen too much of the great, terrible need of the world. My place is here, among these poor sufferers, and I must stay with them.”

He took her hand, and held it for a moment in a close, lingering pressure. “Good-bye, then, Hester,” he said; and his voice, though steady, had the note of a final surrender. “I shall not ask you again.”

“Good-bye, Arthur,” she said; and as she turned away from him, the tears fell slowly down her cheeks, but her step was firm, and her face was set toward the heavy door of the hospital.

Arthur walked away through the twilight streets, his mind filled with a new, sad consciousness of the complex, unmanageable nature of human things. He had come to London to reclaim his bride, and he was leaving it alone, with a sense of failure that was yet not without a certain dignity of renunciation. He had seen that there are forces in the human soul which cannot be bent to the purposes of worldly convenience, and that the love which gives itself to the relief of suffering is not less sacred because it is not bestowed upon one.
