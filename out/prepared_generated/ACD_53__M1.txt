It was upon a raw November evening in the year 1892 that I first had occasion to visit the chambers of Mr. Josiah Croft. The wind had a bite to it that spoke of the coming winter, and a fine, persistent drizzle had turned the pavements of Cheapside into black, gleaming mirrors. Croft, whose name was synonymous with charitable endowments and public benefactions, occupied a suite of rooms above a merchant’s office, their unassuming character a curious testament to the man’s reputed fortune.

He was a spare, sharp-featured man of perhaps sixty, with a face that seemed more accustomed to ledgers than to laughter. He sat behind a broad mahogany desk, his long fingers steepled before him. Yet for all his apparent composure, a nervous tremor in his left hand and the restless darting of his pale grey eyes betrayed a profound and consuming agitation.

“You are, I understand, Mr. Finch, a man of discretion,” he began, his voice thin and reedy. “And a man who does not allow sentiment to cloud his judgement. It is the latter quality I require.”

I inclined my head. “Discretion is the foundation of my profession, Mr. Croft. Pray, state your problem.”

He rose and paced to the window, peering down at the wet street below as if he expected to see his trouble walking there. “It is a matter of gratitude, you see. Or rather, of a debt which I find myself unable to repay. Three weeks ago, I was crossing Ludgate Hill when the axle of a hansom cab snapped. The horse bolted, and the vehicle, a great weight of iron and wood, came hurtling directly towards me. I was frozen to the spot. I should have been killed, sir, crushed to a pulp upon the stones, but for the intervention of a man.”

He turned back to me, his hands clasped behind his back. “He appeared as if from nowhere. A strong, common-looking fellow in a drab coat. He seized me by the collar and hauled me clear with a strength that was quite remarkable. The cab struck the kerb where I had stood but a second before. I was dazed, my heart hammering against my ribs. When my senses returned, the man was gone. He vanished into the crowd as completely as if the pavement had swallowed him up.”

“A commendable act of courage,” I remarked. “And you wish to find this man to reward him?”

“Precisely!” Croft’s voice rose with a sudden, almost feverish energy. “I am a wealthy man, Mr. Finch. My life is my most valuable asset, and its preservation merits a substantial recompense. I wish to settle an annuity upon him, to ensure he never wants for anything again. But I have been unable to find him. I have advertised, I have employed runners, all to no avail.”

It seemed a straightforward enough task, if a little sentimental. “Can you give me a more precise description?”

Croft hesitated, his gaze dropping to the intricate pattern of the Persian rug. “That is the difficulty. He was of middle height and build. He wore a common, dark overcoat and a bowler hat. His face… his face was strangely indistinct in my memory. A blur of exertion, I suppose. But I would know him again. There was a certain quality about him. A quiet efficiency.”

I took the case, and for the next week I pursued it with the usual methods. I questioned the cabbies who frequented the Ludgate Hill rank, the porters at the nearby warehouses, the constables on their beat. None recalled the incident with any clarity. The drizzle had kept most folk indoors, and the street was far from its usual throng. It was a frustrating business, like trying to grasp smoke.

Then, a curious note appeared in my enquiries. A barman at a public house near the Bank of England recognised my description of the man in the drab coat. He was certain of it, for the fellow had been in his establishment at the precise hour of Croft’s accident, standing at the far end of the bar and nursing a single glass of stout. The barman remembered him because he had not spoken a word to anyone and had left a coin on the counter without waiting for his change.

I reported this to Croft, expecting him to dismiss it as a case of mistaken identity. Instead, his agitation seemed to redouble. His face grew pale, and he gripped the edge of his desk until his knuckles were white.

“It cannot be,” he whispered. “It is not possible. Are you certain of the time, Finch? Absolutely certain?”

“The man was most specific, sir. He consulted the clock upon his wall to be sure of it.”

Croft sank into his chair, passing a trembling hand over his brow. “There must be some mistake. A trick of the light. It is a common face, after all.”

But it was not the end of it. Two days later, a seamstress on Fleet Street, whose window overlooked the road, told me she had seen a man matching my description standing motionless upon the opposite pavement at the very moment of the runaway cab. He had watched the accident unfold, she said, with a strange and unnerving calm, and had then turned and walked away without a backward glance.

When I laid this second, contradictory piece of evidence before Mr. Croft, he did not speak for a long time. The philanthropist’s gratitude had by now curdled entirely. The fear that had been a flicker in his eyes was now a steady, dreadful flame.

“Two places at once, Finch,” he said at last, his voice a dry rustle. “One man cannot be in two places at once.”

“Unless they are two different men,” I suggested. “A coincidence of dress and appearance.”
