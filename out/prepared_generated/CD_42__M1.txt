It was the twenty-third of December, in the Year of Grace eighteen hundred and forty-and-something, and the fog which lay upon the metropolis of London was of that particularly cheerful and social kind which seems to have been invented by Providence for the express purpose of bringing mankind together; for it drove the shivering citizens into the public-houses, and, by hiding the shops and the houses and the very sky itself, left them no other alternative but to stare into each other’s faces and the roaring fires of the tap-room. And nowhere did this fog seem more dense, more yellow, or more palpably full of the odour of stale tobacco and damp wool, than in the parlour of *The Golden Gander*, a hostelry of some repute in the Camden Town district, where the gaslights hissed like angry serpents and the sawdust on the floor was trodden into a kind of paste by the boots of the disappointed.

Now, if the reader has ever had the misfortune to belong to a Goose Club—which is a benevolent institution of the lower orders, where the members subscribe a weekly penny or twopence towards the purchase of a Christmas bird, and the publican receives the money and the thanks of the company—he will know that the spirits of such an assembly are usually of the highest order as the festive season approaches. But on this particular evening, the atmosphere in *The Golden Gander* was not one of jubilation, but of that heavy, ominous silence which precedes a thunderstorm, or, more to the point, the outbreak of a riot. For the Goose Club of *The Golden Gander* had come to a sudden and disastrous halt.

The cause of this stagnation was not the price of corn, nor the hardness of the times, nor even the frost that was even now nipping the noses of the lamplighters outside; no, the cause was the absence of Mr. Silas Flinch. Mr. Flinch, a gentleman of a pink complexion and a white waistcoat, who kept the books, the keys, and the money of the Club, had vanished. He had vanished as completely as a shadow at noon-day, leaving behind him only a locked door, a bill for coals, and a long line of disappointed subscribers who had come to claim their geese.

‘It’s a swindle, that’s what it is!’ cried Mr. Jeremiah Crump, a journeyman baker whose hands were permanently white with flour and whose temper was permanently red with indignation. He stood upon a bench, waving his hat with such violence that the gaslight flickered. ‘It’s a fraud upon the public! It’s a breach of confidence! Where is the bird? Where is the money? And where, I ask, is Flinch?’

‘He’s gone to Bath,’ said a mournful voice from the corner; ‘or perhaps to Jericho. He’s gone where the geese don’t grow, and where the subscribers can’t follow.’

‘Gone to Jericho!’ echoed the crowd, with a groan that shook the pewter mugs on the shelves.

‘And my two-and-ninepence!’ cried a poor little seamstress, with a shawl that had seen better days and a face that had seen too many bad ones. ‘I’ve denied myself sugar for it! I’ve denied myself tea! And for what? To see Flinch ride away in a cabriolet while I walk home in the slush!’

‘And my three shillings!’ roared a burly docker, shaking a fist that looked like a leg of mutton. ‘I’ll have the law of him! I’ll have the Beak on him! I’ll have his blood!’

It was at this moment, when the company was working itself up into a fever pitch of righteous fury, and when Mr. Jeremiah Crump was proposing that they should break the windows and burn the furniture to compensate themselves for their losses, that the door of the parlour opened with a slow and solemn creak, and admitted a figure which instantly commanded the attention of the room.

This was no other than Mr. Solomon Wick, the lamplighter of the district; a man of vast experience in the lighting of lamps, and of equally vast experience in the lighting of human nature. He was a tall, thin man, with legs that seemed to have been made of knotted twigs, and a face that was a perfect map of the streets he walked, seamed with wrinkles and grey with the soot of the city. He wore a coat of many capes, which he wore as a peacock wears his tail—with a certain jaunty pride—and he carried his long brass pole across his shoulder like a halberdier of old. He closed the door behind him, shook the fog from his capes like a water-spaniel shaking itself dry, and looked at the assembled company with a twinkle in his eye that suggested he knew a great deal more than he intended to say immediately.

‘Well, well,’ said Mr. Wick, leaning his pole against the wall and rubbing his hands together. ‘What a noise! What a racket! One would think the geese had grown wings and flown away of their own accord!’

‘They have flown away, Mr. Wick!’ cried Mr. Crump, jumping down from the bench. ‘Flinch has flown with them! He’s taken the pot and left us the cinders!’

‘Aye, aye,’ said Mr. Wick, nodding his head slowly. ‘So I hear. So the wind tells me as I go my rounds. The wind knows everything, gentlemen. It whispers in the gas-pipes and it howls in the chimney-pots. It says, "Flinch is a rogue," and it says, "The subscribers are fools."’

‘Fools!’ cried the docker. ‘Are we to be called fools for being robbed?’

‘You are fools,’ said the lamplighter, with sudden sharpness, ‘for thinking that a bird which is not yet hatched is worth more than a bird that is already in the pot. You are fools for putting your trust in a man with a white waistcoat and a smooth tongue, when you know that a smooth tongue is often a crooked one. But,’ he added, his voice softening as he looked at the weeping seamstress, ‘you are not fools for wanting a dinner on Christmas Day. For a dinner on Christmas Day is the right of every Englishman, and of every Englishwoman too, though the law may not say so.’
