The sunlight struck the water at an angle that made the lake, for one suspended moment, entirely the colour of bruised plums; then a breeze stirred, breaking the reflection into a thousand moving facets, bright as scattered glass. Inside the villa, the thick stone walls held the morning dew, casting a damp, cool breath across the bare polished floors. Eleanor stood by the long window, pressing her fingertips against the iron latch; she felt the heat of the garden pressing against the glass, wanting to come in. She wore a dress of pale muslin, which clung to her shoulders with the heavy, clinging moisture of the air, and she looked out at the oleanders, massed in great bloody clumps against the balustrade.

What was it that lay out there, beyond the cypresses, in the deep blue folds of the hills? One might sink, she thought, letting the heat wash over one, sink down into that incredible softness, never to rise again to the sharp, dry tap of life in England. For in England, one was always doing things; tying ribbons, paying calls, shutting doors against the draught. But here, the doors stood open, and the air rushed in, smelling of lemons and damp earth. She had been wandering, an hour ago, in the lower terrace, trailing her fingers through the unkempt boxwood, when he had appeared—Count Vittorio—stepping out from the shadow of the stone pine as if he were merely a part of the landscape that had suddenly condensed into a man. He had bowed; his eyes were the colour of the wet stones in the lake shallows, and he had spoken her name with a gravity that seemed to invest it with an entirely new, almost tragic significance.

"Miss Hilary," he had said—and the syllables had fallen into the warm air like heavy stones into deep water, sending ripples through her mind that spread out, out, to the very edges of her consciousness. She could not remember what she had replied; only that she had looked at his hands, which were very brown, and held a sprig of some wild, pungent herb that he had torn from the bank.

Now, up in the high pastures, where the chestnut woods gave way to rough scrub and the air thinned to a cutting clarity, Leo was running. He had no shirt on, and his shoulders were burnt to the colour of old terra-cotta. He ran with the wild, unthinking velocity of a boy who has escaped the trammels of governesses and nurseries, his bare feet slapping against the dry, baked earth. A lizard shot across the path; he paused, breathless, his heart thudding against his ribs like a fist against a door. The world up here was stripped bare; it was all rock, and the intense, buzzing life of insects, and the vast, indifferent blue arching over everything. He felt himself part of it—a wild, savage thing, allied to the hawks that circled above the crags. Down at the villa, they would be taking tea, spreading butter on dry biscuits, speaking in those low, clipped voices that seemed to belong to another race of beings. He hated the villa; he hated the stuffy drawing-room with its heavy velvet curtains, stolen from some palazzo, which smelled of dust and old age. Up here, he was free; he was a bandit; he was an Italian. He threw himself down on the short, springy grass, pressing his cheek against the earth, and felt the vibration of the mountain silence humming through his bones.

Meanwhile, in the shaded loggia, Mrs. Hilary sat at a small, inlaid table, her pen poised over a sheet of thin, blue-edged paper. The ink pooled, black and viscous, at the nib; she watched it with a certain detached fascination, as if the drop of ink contained the whole of the perplexing Italian summer. How could one write it down? She thought of her sister in Cheltenham, in the neat semi-detached house with the asphalt drive, reading this letter over the breakfast toast. What words could bridge that gulf?

"The weather continues exceedingly fine," she wrote, in her small, precise hand, the letters leaning slightly backward, as if retreating from the assertion. She paused, looking out over the shimmering water. A steamer was crossing to Bellagio, leaving a long, white scar on the water. "We find the local produce quite tolerable, though the butter is inevitably oily. The children are benefiting from the air."

She laid down her pen. What a monstrous, what an incredible lie! As if Eleanor were merely benefiting from the air, when the girl was being transfigured, daily, by some profound and secret alchemy that made her eyes look like the eyes of one walking in a dream; as if Leo were merely benefiting from the air, when he had turned into a little savage, coming home at dusk with his clothes torn and his eyes blazing with a strange, feral light. And she herself—was she benefiting from the air, when she sat here, hour after hour, watching the shadows lengthen on the cypresses, feeling a strange, unaccustomed loosening of the springs of her being, as if the tight, corseted structure of her life were melting in the heat? She thought of her husband, in his chambers in Lincoln's Inn, poring over briefs; she thought of the London fog, pressing against the windows. It all seemed infinitely remote, a page in a book she had read long ago and half forgotten.

"The Count Vittorio," she began, then stopped. No; she could not write that. The ink dried on the nib. She drew a line through the words, pressing hard until the paper nearly tore. How could she explain him—the dark, reserved Italian with his courtly manners, his sudden, flashing smile, his air of belonging to an older, more decadent, and more poetic race? She could not. She could only feel that the villa was full of a strange, heavy scent, as of flowers left to decay in crystal vases; and that something was happening, something irrevocable, which she could neither prevent nor hasten, but only watch, from the outside, as one watches a play through a glass case.
