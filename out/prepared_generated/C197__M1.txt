The ledger was a creature of perfect logic, its columns of debits and credits a universe of order that Arthur Penhaligon could trust. Here, in the quiet of his counting-house room overlooking the Scheldt, the world made sense. Numbers did not lie or wheedle or hide their true intentions behind a veneer of flattery. He ran a finger down the page, his mind a calm sea of arithmetic, until the door opened and the sea began to churn.

It was Mr. Van Der Hoek himself, a man whose bulk seemed to fill the doorway, his waistcoat straining against the effluvia of a successful lunch. “Penhaligon,” he boomed, his English thick with the guttural charm of Antwerp. “A word, if you please.”

Arthur rose, his posture a neat column of deference. “Of course, sir.”

Van Der Hoek settled himself onto a chair that groaned in protest, dabbing at his brow with a large silk handkerchief. “It is my sons,” he began, without preamble. “Pieter and Karel. They are… idle. Their French is passable, their Flemish is their own, but in this age, English is the language of the future. Of the steamships and the telegraph. Of commerce.” He fixed Arthur with a look of immense sincerity. “Your English is of the finest quality, Penhaligon. The Queen’s own. I wish for you to tutor them. An hour, each afternoon, after their other lessons are done.”

A cold dread, familiar and sharp, pricked at Arthur. He had seen the Van Der Hoek boys in the echoing halls of the grand house: Pieter, the elder at fifteen, a soft-shouldered youth with his father’s ruddy complexion and his mother’s weak, watery eyes; and Karel, thirteen, a feral creature with a perpetually simmering resentment in his gaze. They were not scholars. They were boys who owned their world and saw no reason to learn the language of another.

“Sir,” Arthur began, choosing his words as carefully as he would choose the correct entry for a disputed invoice. “I am, of course, honoured by your confidence. But my duties here are…”

“Your duties are what I say they are,” Van Der Hoek said, though not unkindly. He was a man used to buying what he wanted, be it bolts of silk or the time of his bookkeeper. “Your salary will reflect this new responsibility. See to it.” He heaved himself up and was gone, leaving behind the scent of expensive cigars and an impossible command.

The first lesson was a disaster, as Arthur had foreseen. It was held in the salon, a room of gilded furniture and damask curtains that seemed to mock the dusty practicality of Arthur’s own world. Pieter slouched in a velvet armchair, looking as though he might melt into its upholstery. Karel stood by the window, idly flicking a beetle from the sill onto the marble floor below.

“Good afternoon,” Arthur said, placing his book of grammar on a polished table. “Today, we will begin with the simple present tense. I am. You are. He is.”

“I am bored,” Karel said in Flemish, without turning around. Pieter giggled, a sound like a rusty hinge.

Arthur ignored it, pressing on. He was a man who believed in the slow, steady application of principle. He repeated the conjugations, his voice calm and even, but the words seemed to die in the air between them. They did not learn. They did not even pretend to listen. They existed in a state of profound, almost physical, opposition.

The true antagonist, however, arrived after twenty minutes. Madame Van Der Hoek swept into the room in a cloud of lavender and silk, her face a mask of concerned piety. “Mynheer Penhaligon,” she cooed. “How are my little doves?”

“Madame,” Arthur said, inclining his head.

“Pieter, darling, you look so tired. Is this man being very stern?” She glided to her son’s side, stroking his hair. “He does not need such difficult grammar. He needs conversation. He needs to say, ‘How do you do?’ and ‘What is the price of this wool?’”

“Madame, a foundation in grammar is essential for—”

“Nonsense,” she said, waving a dismissive, bejewelled hand. “A merchant’s son does not need to be a poet. Karel, my little tiger, come away from the window. You will catch a chill.” She turned her soft, suffocating attention to Arthur. “Perhaps you could read them a story? Something gentle. About a farm, perhaps. Boys love farms.”

The lesson dissolved into a chaotic pantomime orchestrated by the mother. Arthur’s carefully prepared exercises were abandoned for a picture book Madame Van Der Hoek produced from a nearby table. The boys, now under her protective wing, became even more intractable. Pieter yawned loudly. Karel pronounced the word ‘cow’ as ‘caw’ with deliberate, mocking slowness. And Madame Van Der Hoek would smile and say, “Excellent, my darling, excellent,” and Arthur felt like a fraud, a paid actor in a farce where the only goal was to soothe the anxieties of a doting mother.

This became the pattern for the next two weeks. Arthur would arrive to find the boys already settled with a distraction—a new pocket watch for Pieter, a set of lead soldiers for Karel—and Madame Van Der Hoek would inevitably appear, a guardian angel of ignorance, steering the lesson away from any form of discipline or structure. Arthur’s soul, a thing of order and precision, felt frayed at the edges. He would return to his ledger each evening, the clean columns a desperate comfort, the only place where his work had any real effect.

The summons came on a Friday. Van Der Hoek was in his study, a room that smelled of leather and power. He was looking over shipping manifests, but he put them aside as Arthur entered.

“Penhaligon. A report on my sons’ progress, if you please.”

This was the moment. Arthur could see the two paths laid out before him. One was smooth and paved with easy lies: *They are making excellent progress, sir. A fine foundation has been laid. Pieter has a natural ear for the vowels.* It was the path of least resistance. It would keep his employer happy, secure his position, and allow the comfortable fiction to continue.
