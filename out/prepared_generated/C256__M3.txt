The house in Belgravia was a tomb of mahogany and damp velvet, smelling faintly of beeswax and the insidious decay of old age. In the upstairs drawing room of number fourteen, Pont Street, Eleanor, the Dowager Duchess of Darrington, held court. At eighty-two, she had outlived three husbands: the Duke, who had died of a hunting fall; the Earl, carried off by a putrid fever; and finally, the Baron, who had simply expired in her bed, a fact she attributed to a weak heart and an overabundance of port. It was this final marital departure that had prompted her, she claimed, to set the record straight.

The manuscript sat on the escritoire, a towering, chaotic edifice of foolscap. To call it a memoir was generous; it was an indictment. Every great family in London was holding its breath, terrified of what the Dowager Duchess might commit to print. She had a memory like a steel trap and absolutely no regard for the living. Her nephew, Arthur Pennington, stood before the fireplace, the manuscript’s leather satchel heavy in his hands. As her sole surviving blood relation and heir presumptive to the Darrington entail, he had been commissioned—commanded, rather—to edit the sprawling beast.

"Mind you do not bleach the life out of it, Arthur," the Duchess said, her voice a dry rustle like autumn leaves. "The truth is only indecent to those who have something to hide."

Arthur bowed, the practiced, hollow smile of the season fixed upon his face. He was forty, a man of comfortable means and quiet habits, entirely dependent on the Darrington fortune to clear the staggering gambling debts he had accumulated at his club. To edit the manuscript was to secure his inheritance; to fail was to face ruin. "I shall ensure it is entirely fit for publication, Your Grace," he replied.

He retreated to the downstairs library, locked the heavy oak door, and poured himself a large brandy. He untied the satchel and began to read.

For three days, Arthur scarcely slept. The memoir was a masterpiece of venomous observation. She detailed the Duke’s congenital idiocy, the Earl’s clandestine absconding to the Continent with a French opera singer, and the Baron’s astonishing talent for forgery. She spared no one. But it was on the fourth night, in the small hours when the gas lamps flickered low, that Arthur turned to the pages concerning the summer of 1865.

He had always believed his mother, Clara, had died of a sudden influenza during a trip to the country. She had passed away when Arthur was ten, leaving him to be raised by indifferent guardians before the Duchess took him under her formidable wing. But according to the manuscript, Clara’s death was not the quiet, dignified affair society had been led to believe.

Arthur read the passage again, the brandy turning to ice in his stomach.

*It was at Venning Park, the seat of the late Lord Hollister, that the true nature of my niece’s character was laid bare. Clara, weary of her tedious marriage to Arthur’s father, had engaged in a passionate liaison with Lord Hollister’s eldest son, the rakehell Edmund. I discovered them in the orangery. The girl was in a state of undress, her morals as discarded as her shawl. When I confronted her, she did not weep or beg for forgiveness. She laughed, declaring she would sooner die than return to the suffocating dullness of her husband’s bed. A week later, she took matters into her own hands. A vial of cyanide, stolen from the photographer’s studio in the village, ensured she never had to.*

Arthur’s hands trembled. He read it again. *A vial of cyanide.* His mother had not died of influenza. According to the Duchess, she had committed suicide, driven to despair by her own indiscretions and the threat of exposure. It was a scandal of the highest order. If this were published, Arthur’s name would be dragged through the mud. The ton would whisper that his mother was an adulteress and a coward. He would be ruined, socially and financially. No one would receive him, and the doors that his future inheritance was meant to unlock would slam shut in his face.

He stared at the fire. He could simply remove the pages. He was the editor, after all. He could burn them in the grate, and the Duchess would be none the wiser. She was eighty-two, her eyesight failing, her memory sharp but her physical oversight limited. He reached for the foolscap, his fingers brushing the edge of the paper. He could feel the heat of the fire on his face.

Then he stopped. He remembered the Duchess’s words. *The truth is only indecent to those who have something to hide.* He thought of his mother, a woman he barely remembered, a ghost in a portrait gallery. He thought of the quiet, suffocating life she must have led, trapped in a marriage she despised, clinging to a moment of illicit happiness in an orangery before choosing death over captivity.

He picked up his pen. He did not strike through the passage. Instead, he began to edit. He tightened the prose, sharpened the Duchess’s barbs, and clarified the timeline. He made the scene in the orangery vivid, the confrontation crackling with tension. He did not soften Clara’s defiance or the tragedy of her end. He made it sing.

The next afternoon, Arthur carried the edited manuscript back to the upstairs drawing room. The Duchess was in her chair by the window, a cashmere shawl draped over her shoulders, staring out at the grey London sky.

"I have completed my review, Your Grace," Arthur said, placing the manuscript on the table beside her.

The Duchess picked up the first few pages, her rheumy eyes scanning the text. She turned to the chapter on Venning Park. She read in silence for a long moment, her expression unreadable. Then she looked up at Arthur.

"You left it in," she said. It was not a question.
