The rain lashed against the leaded panes of my chamber in the old college, a ceaseless and mournful rhythm that seemed to mimic the erratic beating of my own heart, which had grown so feeble and trembling that it often ceased its labor for several agonizing seconds before resuming its painful cadence. It was the night of the autumnal equinox, 1824, and the air within the room was thick with the acrid scent of burnt copper and the sweet, cloying rot of the *miasma* I had distilled from the lungs of the dead. I sat before the candle, its flame guttering in the draft, and watched the liquid in the glass vial before me swirl with a purple darkness that I had learned to associate with the very essence of the wasting fever that had claimed my sister, Elara, three winters past. Her face, pale and haggard in my memory, stared up at me from the shadows, not in accusation, but in a silent plea for the truth that I was so desperately, madly seeking. I had sworn upon her grave, in the cold mud of the kirkyard, that I would not rest until I had peeled back the veil of ignorance that shrouded the cause of this invisible assassin, for if I could name the spirit of the disease, I might perhaps bind it, or even destroy it.

I am a physician of the Royal College, a man who has walked the halls of the University of Edinburgh with the pride of one who believes that reason and observation are the only sure guides through the labyrinth of human suffering. Yet, here I am, stripped of all reputation, living in a state of secret horror, for I have begun to subject my own body to the experiments that I once performed only upon the corpses of the poor and the forgotten. I had observed that the fever did not strike all who were exposed to the same air; there was a difference in the constitution, a subtle variation in the vitality of the blood that determined whether the victim succumbed or survived. To understand this, I had to become the subject myself. I had introduced minute quantities of the infected serum into my own veins, watching with the cold detachment of a scientist and the terrified anticipation of a man standing on the precipice of ruin, to see how my body would react, how the poison would navigate the sanctuary of my flesh.

It was on this very night that I heard the soft, hesitant tapping at my door. I did not need to ask who it was; the whole world had narrowed down to the presence of Clara, my betrothed, the only being in this cold, gray city who possessed the power to anchor me to the realm of the living. I opened the door, and she stepped into the room, her eyes wide with a fear that I had never seen before, a fear not of the dark, but of me. The sight of her, so beautiful and so fragile in the flickering light, broke something within my chest that had been slowly hardening into stone.

"William," she whispered, her voice trembling as she reached out to touch my face. Her fingers were cold, and they shivered against my burning skin. "You are burning up. Your eyes are wild. What have you done to yourself? I have watched you these last weeks, disappearing into your studies, returning only to sleep in a fitful, feverish delirium. I have seen the stains on your hands, the smell that lingers in your hair. You are destroying yourself, William. You are losing your mind, and with it, your soul."

I tried to speak, to explain the logic of my pursuit, the grandeur of the discovery that lay just beyond the threshold of my understanding. I wanted to tell her that I was not mad, but that I was driven by a necessity as powerful as hunger, a duty to those who had suffered in silence. But the words died in my throat, choked by the coughing fit that seized me, a racking, violent convulsion that left me gasping and spitting blood onto the floor. Clara screamed, a sound of pure horror, and rushed to my side, wiping the crimson from my lips with her handkerchief.

"Stop!" she cried, her voice rising to a pitch of desperate anguish. "You must stop! You are killing yourself! For whom? For what? You will be dead before you have found your cure, and then who will save them? Who will save me? Look at me, William! I am here! I am alive! Is your discovery worth the price of your life? Is it worth the loss of everything we have built together?"

I looked at her, and in her face I saw the reflection of my own despair. I saw the love that I had been too arrogant to cherish, the human connection that I had severed in my pursuit of abstract truth. I wanted to say that I was close, that I could feel the edge of the truth, that if I just kept going, just one more day, one more experiment, I would hold the key to salvation in my hands. I wanted to tell her that the suffering of thousands outweighed the happiness of one, that it was my duty to suffer so that others might not. But as I looked into her eyes, I realized that my ambition had become a monstrous thing, a hungry beast that fed on my sanity and my love, leaving nothing but ash in its wake.

I reached out and took her hand, my grip weak but firm. "Clara," I said, my voice rasping, "I am afraid. I have been afraid for a long time. I thought that if I could understand the mechanism of death, I could conquer it. I thought that knowledge was a shield. But I have found only more darkness. I have looked into the abyss, and it has looked back at me, and I am beginning to see its face in my own mirror."
