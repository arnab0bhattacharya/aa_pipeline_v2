In the decrepit and muddily-splendid town of Chuzzlewick Mire, where the atmosphere tasted perpetually of spent hops and the river Sludge rolled through its banks like a sluggish, discoloured giant heavily disguised in liquid filth, the great brewing-house of Gallow and Sons reared its brick head to the weeping heavens. It was a place of vast, hissing vats and shadowy vaults, where the very walls perspired with the damp of a hundred fermentations, and where the master, Mr. Nehemiah Gallow, ruled his domain with a jowl that quivered like a bladder of lard whenever he spoke of his Christian charity. Mr. Gallow was a man of much substance, both in the waistcoat and in the counting-house, and he bore his prosperity with a smiling, oily condescension that slid over his workmen like a greasy film. He had a remarkable way of folding his plump hands over his capacious stomach, and of saying, "We must all do our little bit for the poorer brethren, Mr. Tappit; we must all do our little bit," whenever a pauper was mentioned.

Mr. Josiah Tappit, the senior cooper of the establishment, was a lean, sharp-featured man, whose face bore the permanent grain of the wood he bent, and whose hands were as hard and knotted as the oak staves he hammered into hoops. He was a widower with three small children, and his weekly wage was the sole barrier between them and the parish. He was not a man given to inward reflection, being much more accustomed to the outward driving of iron hoops; but he was a man of an honest sort, who knew a sound cask from a rotten one, and who had served the Gallow vats for twenty uneventful years.

It was upon a raw, foggy evening, when the damp crept into the very marrow of the bones, that Josiah Tappit descended into the lower cellars to draw off a sample for his master. The vaults were cavernous and dimly lit by a solitary gas-jet that flickered as though it, too, suffered from the ague. As he navigated the narrow, slimy passages, his ear caught the distinct and unmistakable sound of a brass hand-pump, laboriously wheezing in the dark. Tappit stopped short. The sound came from behind a low partition, near the great butt reserved exclusively for the union workhouse. He crept forward, his heart beating a dull, heavy tattoo against his ribs, and peered through a crevice in the damp stonework.

There, in the yellow, sickly glow of a bull’s-eye lantern, stood Mr. Nehemiah Gallow himself. The master’s sleeves were rolled up to the elbows of his fat, white arms, and he was pumping water—cold, flat, insipid river water—directly into the ale destined for the orphaned and the destitute. Beside him stood Mr. Carker, the lean and wolfish cellarman, who held a long stirring-paddle. "A little more, sir?" Carker was saying, in a voice like the rasp of a file. "A little more, and it will be a noble, charitable brew. They will think it a treat, sir; they will think it a treat."

"A little more, Carker," quoth Mr. Gallow, his jowl shaking with a silent, inward laugh. "A little more. The parish doesn't pay for London quality. Water is cheap, and charity begins at home. We must all do our little bit, Carker; our little bit." And he laughed again, a soft, wheezing, watery laugh, exactly like the gurgle of the stolen liquid as it splashed into the butt.

Josiah Tappit drew back from the stonework as though he had been struck a blow upon the head by a mallet. The master, the great Mr. Gallow, who spoke so loudly in the parish church of his benevolence, was stealing the flavour from the mouths of the starving! He ascended the cellar stairs in a dazed, mechanical fashion, and walked home through the foggy streets of Chuzzlewick Mire, seeing nothing of the mud, and hearing nothing of the distant clatter of the night-carts.

He found his three children waiting for him in the single room that served them for kitchen, parlour, and bedroom. Little Bess, the eldest, was stirring the gruel over the meagre fire; the two younger boys were asleep upon their straw pallet. The room was cold, and the gruel was thin, and the faces of the children were pale and pinched. "You’re late, Father," said Bess, looking up with a patient, wistful smile. "I kept it hot. I was frightened the fire would go out."

"Frightened, my dear?" said Tappit, sitting heavily upon the three-legged stool. "Of what?"

"Of the cold," she returned. "And of the workhouse. Mrs. Nubble at the gate was saying today that they send the children there when the fathers can't earn the bread. She says the master gives them fine ale there, Father, to keep up their spirits. Fine ale!"

Tappit groaned. He put his rough hand to his brow, and felt that it was wet with something that was not the fog. He looked at the thin gruel, and at the patient, smiling child, and at the sleeping boys. If he spoke—if he went before the magistrates and proclaimed that Nehemiah Gallow was a waterer of the workhouse ale—he would be discharged. He knew his master. The hand that fed him was the hand that could starve him; and the tongue that could speak so smoothly of charity could whisper as smoothly of ruin. He would lose his place. The children would go to the workhouse, where they would drink the very poison he had refused to brew.

But if he held his tongue—if he held it, and went on hammering his hoops, and touching his cap to the fat, smiling master—he would keep his bread. He would keep his place, and his children would be fed. It was a simple calculation. A man of the world would have made it in a moment. A man of the world would have said, "What is it to me? Let the parish drink its water. I have my own to look to." It was a simple, easy, comfortable calculation, and Josiah Tappit sat upon his stool and tried to make it.
