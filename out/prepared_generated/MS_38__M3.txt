When the last mound of earth was laid upon the silent grave of my mother, and the bleak autumnal wind sighed through the leafless branches of the elms that surrounded our desolated village, I found myself utterly alone in a world that seemed but a vast and echoing sepulchre. The pestilence had swept through our valley like a visible spirit of wrath, leaving neither hearth unbroken nor heart unstung; it had devoured the young and the old, the strong and the frail, until I, Elias, was left as a solitary monument to its terrible passage. For many days I wandered through the empty tenements of my youth, where the silence was broken only by the muttering of the streams or the distant crash of a falling roof, my soul enveloped in a gloom so profound that I often wished the earth might open and swallow me into the same oblivion as my kindred. It was during this period of unutterable despair, as I stood upon the brow of the hill overlooking the ruins of what had once been the thriving parish of Alderbrook, that I first beheld her. Clara was her name, a solitary survivor from the further end of the valley, a stranger to me save for the fleeting, distant glimpses I had caught of her in former, happier days. She approached me with a slow and measured tread, her countenance pale as the driven snow, yet illuminated by a light of unyielding resolution that seemed to mock the desolation around us; and when she spoke, her voice, though tremulous with recent grief, possessed a melancholy sweetness that penetrated the icy barriers of my sorrow. She proposed, with an earnestness that admitted of no refusal, that we should abandon this charnel-house of memories and set forth together to seek the habitations of other men, to find if perchance the plague had been confined to our lonely basin, and if anywhere the light of humanity still burned. I consented, for what had I to bind me to the scenes of my wretchedness? We gathered a few provisions and set out as the sun sank beneath the western hills, casting a lurid and baleful glare over the landscape that seemed to paint the very heavens with the colours of our despair.

We journeyed in silence for the first leagues of our progress, for the weight of our mutual bereavement lay upon our tongues and rendered speech a profanation of the sacred grief that filled our hearts. The English countryside, once so fertile and so carefully tended, now lay around us in a state of savage and melancholy wildness; the fields were overgrown with brambles and noxious weeds, the hedgerows were broken and decayed, and the roads were choked with rank vegetation that seemed to mourn the absence of the hand that had once held it in subjection. As we advanced, the aspect of nature grew more stern and forbidding; the hills rose into rugged and precipitous heights, and dark clouds gathered in the east, portending one of those tremendous storms that seem to awaken the slumbering terrors of the atmosphere. Clara walked beside me with an uncomplaining steadfastness that filled me with wonder, for her frame was delicate and her eyes bore the traces of sleepless anguish; yet I observed that she often turned her gaze backward towards the receding valley, and I knew that her thoughts were with the dead she had left behind, with the father and sister whose unburied forms she had been forced to abandon to the wolves and the elements. It was this thought that first stirred within my bosom a sense of duty towards her, a feeling that amidst the ruin of all earthly ties, there yet remained a bond of fellowship between us sufferers that we were bound to honour and sustain.

On the third evening of our journey, as we sought shelter in the roofless nave of an ancient church, the storm burst upon us with unexampled fury. The thunder rolled in long and awful peals among the hills, and the lightning played in vivid and terrifying flashes upon the ruined walls and shattered monuments around us. Clara, who had borne the fatigues of the day with a fortitude beyond her sex and years, now sank upon a broken bench and gave way to a flood of passionate weeping that shook her whole frame. I stood beside her in silence, divided between awe at the tremendous spectacle of nature and an overwhelming tenderness for the grief of this solitary being who had been thus thrown upon my protection. At length, overcoming the bashfulness and reserve that had kept us apart, I knelt beside her and took her cold hand in mine, and implored her, with such words as my heart suggested, to seek comfort in the contemplation that we were yet spared, that Providence had preserved us for some purpose, however inscrutable. She raised her streaming eyes to mine, and in that moment of extremity, a compact was silently formed between us stronger than any tie of blood or ancient friendship; we were two solitary wanderers upon the face of a blasted earth, and we owed to each other the mutual duties of guardianship and compassion.

As the days wore on and our journey carried us further from the scenes of our calamity, we began to converse more freely, and I learned the history of her sufferings, which were even more terrible than my own. She had watched, she told me, the slow and agonizing approach of the pestilence through her family, nursing each in turn with a devotion that could not avert the stroke of death, until she alone remained in a house of corpses. She spoke with an eloquence born of the deepest feeling, and as I listened, I felt my own sorrows reflected and magnified in her tale, and yet, paradoxically, I found in this communion of grief a solace I had not thought possible.
