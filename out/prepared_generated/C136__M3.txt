The Red Sea at night was a kiln, its breath ascending through the wooden decking of the SS *Mercia* to choke the lungs of Captain Elias Vane. The brass in his cabin was hot to the touch, weeping slick condensation. The porthole yielded no breeze, only the endless, rhythmic thundering of the steamer’s triple-expansion engines, churning the heavy, phosphorescent swells. Vane stood barefoot on the Persian rug, his eyes fixed not on the heat-shimmered horizon, but on the parallel rulers and dividers resting on his chart table. The mathematical truth they had just spoken was more suffocating than the equatorial heat. For seven days, the *Mercia* had been running on a false reckoning.

Vane traced the erroneous lines with a calloused fingertip. According to the logbook, meticulously kept by his Chief Officer, Arthur Pendelton, they were making a clean northerly tack through the clear, deep waters of the central Red Sea, well west of the Dahlak Archipelago. But the dawn star sights, which Vane had insisted on taking himself while Pendelton slept off a supposed bout of stomach fever, told a different story. They were fifty miles east of their plotted position, drifting dangerously toward the shoals and coral reefs of the Farsan Islands. Pendelton, a man Vane had trusted with his life for a dozen years, had been recording fabricated coordinates.

A sharp knock broke the stifling silence of the cabin.

"Enter," Vane said, his voice a low rasp.

The door creaked open, revealing the slight, hunched frame of Mr. Quigley, the Third Mate. Quigley looked terrified, his pale face gleaming with sweat in the lamplight. He stepped inside and shut the door quickly, as if afraid the wind might hear him.

"Captain," Quigley whispered, producing a glass bottle wrapped in a rag. "Found this hidden behind the spare blocks in the starboard lifeboat. It’s not the first. I found three empties stowed in the coal bunker this morning."

Vane took the bottle. It was a common gin bottle, but the stench of juniper was overpowered by a sharp, medicinal reek. He uncorked it, sniffed, and tipped a drop onto his finger. He tasted it. His jaw tightened. It was raw alcohol, cut heavily with laudanum. It was the only explanation. Pendelton, once the most precise navigator in the merchant fleet, had been dipping into the medicine chest, mixing the opiate with cheap spirits to numb whatever demons haunted him. Under its sway, his eyes had failed, his mind had wandered, and his hand had drawn lines on the chart that led them toward ruin.

"Does anyone else know?" Vane asked.

"No, sir. I keep my head down, you know that. But the men... they talk. They say Mr. Pendelton has been swaying on his watch. They think he’s ill."

"He is ill," Vane said flatly, handing the empty bottle back to Quigley. "Go back to your duties, Mr. Quigley. Speak of this to no one."

"Aye, Captain." Quigley slipped out, leaving Vane alone with the devastating geometry of the chart.

Vane returned to the table. The *Mercia* was carrying a cargo of nitrates bound for Suez, a highly combustible powder that demanded a steady hand and a clear mind. To lose the ship would bankrupt the company, ruin the crew’s wages, and likely kill every man aboard. If Vane reported Pendelton, the Chief Officer would be instantly clapped in irons, stripped of his rank, and tried for endangering the ship. He would hang, or worse, be blacklisted and left to rot in some foreign port. Pendelton had a wife in Southampton, a daughter preparing for her debut. Vane had dined at their table. He had promised Eleanor Pendelton he would bring her husband home safe.

But if Vane said nothing, the *Mercia* might strike a reef before dawn.

The whistle of the engine room sounded, signaling the change of the watch. Midnight. Vane looked at the clock. They had six hours until the sun revealed their true position. He adjusted his collar, walked out of his cabin, and climbed the iron ladder to the bridge.

Arthur Pendelton stood at the wheel, his hands resting lightly on the polished mahogany of the binnacle. The heat had reduced him to his undershirt, suspenders hanging limp at his sides. He was a shadow of the barrel-chested man who had once taught Vane how to splice a mainbrace. His skin was sallow, his eyes sunken and glassy.

"Captain," Pendelton said, turning. "You should be resting. The heat below is enough to boil a man's brains."

"I've seen enough," Vane replied, stepping into the weak light of the binnacle lamp. "We need to adjust our heading, Arthur."

Pendelton stiffened. "Adjust? The course is steady. North-northwest, as plotted. We have clear water for another hundred miles."

"You're wrong," Vane said, keeping his voice even. "The morning sights put us east of the line. We're bearing down on the Farsan Banks."

Pendelton’s face twitched. A flicker of the old, proud navigator sparked in his eyes, quickly smothered by a dull, opiate haze. "I checked my calculations myself. The current is strong here, Elias. The stars can deceive a man in this heat."

"It wasn't the stars that deceived you," Vane said softly. He reached into his coat and withdrew the empty bottle Quigley had given him. He held it up so the light caught the glass. "Laudanum and gin. A lethal mixture for a navigator."

The effect on Pendelton was instantaneous. The blood drained from his face, leaving him a ghastly, waxen white. His knees buckled slightly, and he gripped the binnacle to steady himself. He stared at the bottle, then at Vane, his lips parting in a silent, breathless plea.

"Elias," he whispered. "I... it was only to help me sleep. The pain in my gut. The surgeon gave me the laudanum, and I... I mixed it with the spirits to kill the taste. I didn't think... I didn't know I was drawing the lines wrong."
