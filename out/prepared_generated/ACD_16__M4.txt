It was on a raw November afternoon, with the fog lying so thick upon Fleet Street that the lamp-lighter had begun his rounds before four o’clock, that I found myself in the office of the *Morning Intelligencer*, listening to a narrative which, for all its commonplace beginnings, was to lead me into one of the most singular labyrinths of crime it has ever been my fortune to explore.

Mr. Josiah Crane, the editor, was a small, spare man with the harassed air of one who lives perpetually against the clock. He had sent for me, he explained, upon the recommendation of Inspector Lestrade of Scotland Yard, and he lost no time in coming to the point.

“You will think me a fool, Mr. Harding,” said he, pushing across the desk a sheet of newsprint, “but I have been twenty years in this trade, and I have learned to trust my instincts. Look at these.”

The sheet was a page of personal advertisements, such as appear daily in every London journal—requests for situations, notices of lodgings, announcements of meetings. I scanned them without at first perceiving anything remarkable.

“You observe nothing?” he asked, with a thin smile. “Look more closely at the third column.”

I did so, and presently I saw what he intended. Amid the ordinary announcements were a number of insertions which differed from their neighbours in a manner at once subtle and unmistakable. Where one would expect to read “Wanted, a Situation as Coachman” or “Lost, a Black Terrier,” there appeared instead lines of numerals—groups of figures arranged in patterns which seemed to my eye entirely arbitrary, yet which bore, in their very regularity, the stamp of deliberate design.

“They began three weeks ago,” said Crane. “At first but one or two a day. Now there are a dozen or more. I set my junior to watch them, and he tells me that the same numbers recur, though in different order, and that the advertisements are always inserted by post, always paid in advance with postal orders, and always signed with a single initial.”

“Have you formed any theory?”

“I am a newspaper man, not a detective. But I have eyes, and I have a memory. In my younger days I was on the staff of a journal in Bombay, and I saw something there of the methods of the Thugs and the dacoits. The native criminals communicated by means of marks upon stones and trees, meaningless to the uninitiated. These figures have the same look to me. They are a cipher.”

I was about to reply when there came a hurried knock at the door, and a young man entered without waiting for permission. His face was white, and his voice, when he spoke, was scarce above a whisper.

“It’s Tompkins, sir. The compositor. He’s been found in the alley off Bouverie Street. Beaten about the head something shocking. They’ve taken him to the infirmary, but the doctor says he may not last the night.”

Crane rose to his feet with an oath. “Tompkins set the personal column,” he said, turning to me. “He has been with us five years. God help me, Mr. Harding, I fear I have brought this upon him.”

I need not dwell upon the hours that followed. Tompkins, when I saw him at the hospital, was beyond speech, though his eyes were open and there was a flicker of recognition when Crane spoke his name. The police had found no weapon, no witness, and no motive—save that the man’s pockets had been rifled, though his watch and a few shillings remained. It was the opinion of the divisional surgeon that he had been struck down from behind by two men, and that the object of the assault had been not robbery but intimidation.

“They wanted something he had,” said Crane, as we drove back through the fog. “Or something he knew. He was a quiet fellow, Mr. Harding, but he had a memory for figures that was almost uncanny. He could set a column of type from memory after a single reading. If there was a key to that cipher, Tompkins would have found it.”

It was then that I recalled a name—a name which had been in my mind since Crane had first spoken of ciphers and of the darker byways of the criminal world. I had heard it spoken of at the Yard with a respect which bordered upon awe, and with a certain exasperation which I had learned to associate with men of genius who decline to be bound by regulations.

“There is a man,” said I, “who may be able to help us. He is not of the official force, and he is eccentric in his habits. But I have known him to solve problems which had baffled the whole of Scotland Yard for months. His name is Ambrose Vane, and he lodges in Montague Street, near the British Museum.”

Crane was sceptical, as practical men usually are of the unconventional. But he was also desperate, and so it was that at eight o’clock that same evening we found ourselves in a small, ill-furnished sitting-room, the air of which was heavy with the fumes of chemicals, while upon the table lay a scatter of papers, a microscope, and what appeared to be the dismembered fragments of a child’s clockwork toy.

Mr. Ambrose Vane was a tall, lean man of middle age, with a face so thin and sharp that it might have been carved from a penknife. His eyes were of a pale, cold grey, and his manner, when he chose to exert it, was that of a man who has long since ceased to be surprised by anything that human folly or human wickedness can produce.

“You interest me,” said he, when Crane had told his tale and shown the sheet of advertisements. “You interest me exceedingly. Pray let me have the numbers which have appeared during the last week, together with the dates and times of their insertion.”
