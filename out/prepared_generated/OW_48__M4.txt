The storm had passed over the valley of Alderon like a great lord in a temper, leaving behind it a broken sky of opal and tourmaline, and a wind that smelled of wet earth and crushed violets. It was on the morning after such a visitation that Corin, a shepherd of no particular consequence and of a singularly contented disposition, found the bell. He had gone out to count his sheep, a flock of rather foolish animals whose fleeces were the colour of clotted cream, and to mend a gap in the low stone wall that separated his pasture from the wilder slopes. There, half-buried in the clay and the sodden grass, a glint of silver caught his eye, as though a star had fallen from the sky and been too weary to climb back again.

He dug it out with the careful hands of a man who has learned that the earth is a miser and yields her secrets grudgingly. It was a bell, small enough to cup in his palm, of a silver so pure and ancient that it seemed to hold the moonlight within its metal. Its surface was not smooth, but was chased with strange, intertwining figures—beasts and birds and blossoms that he did not recognise—and its handle was wrought into the shape of a sleeping child. It had no clapper, yet when he lifted it, it seemed to him that it hummed with a faint, expectant music, like the silence that follows a perfect chord. He thought, in his simple way, that it would make a pretty trinket for his wife, or a plaything for the child they hoped for. And so, with a certain innocent curiosity, he took the little silver handle between his thumb and forefinger and gave the bell a gentle shake.

The sound that came from it was not a sound at all, but a presence. It was a note of such crystalline purity, so sweet and so commanding, that it seemed to enter not the ear but the very soul. It was a chord that spoke of the beginning of the world. And at that note, a strange and wonderful thing occurred. His sheep, those placid and unremarkable creatures, stopped their grazing and, one by one, folded their forelegs beneath them and sank to the wet grass, their heads bowed as if in prayer. A lark, which had been ascending the morning like a feathered fountain, ceased its song and dropped silently to the earth. The cattle in the next field knelt, and the horses in the paddock, and even the old, cynical goat that belonged to the widow Fenwick, and which had never been known to show reverence for anything, lowered its horned head and bent its knees. The whole hillside, in that moment, was a congregation of beasts, and Corin, standing alone among them, felt a terror and a wonder that were strangely akin.

The tale of the shepherd and the kneeling beasts travelled through the valley of Alderon with the speed of a scandal. It reached the ears of the lord of the valley, a certain Lord Alaric, a man of immense wealth and meticulous ennui, who collected beautiful things as other men collect debts, and who found in the world a perpetual disappointment because it could not be arranged as perfectly as his own gardens. He was a connoisseur of power, and the idea that a mere shepherd possessed a trinket that could command the adoration of the animal kingdom was not to be borne. It was an affront to the natural order, a piece of insolence in a landscape he considered his own. He summoned Corin to his hall, a vast and gloomy edifice of grey stone, adorned with the heads of stags and the tapestries of dead battles.

Corin came, dressed in his rough woollens, with the bell in a leather pouch at his belt. He stood before the lord, who reclined on a chair of carved ebony, a greyhound at his feet and a goblet of ruby-coloured wine in his hand. The hall was cold, and smelled of old wood and older pride. ‘I am told,’ said Lord Alaric, without preamble, ‘that you have found a curious toy in my fields. You will give it to me. It is not fitting that such a thing should be in the hands of a man who smells of sheep.’

Corin, who was not a fool, though he was often silent, bowed his head. ‘My lord,’ he said, ‘it was found on the common land, after the storm. It is a pretty thing, and it does no harm.’

‘Harm?’ said the lord, with a thin smile. ‘It makes a mockery of my horses and my hounds. It is a piece of vulgar magic, and I will have it for my cabinet of curiosities. Name your price. A hundred sheep? A cottage with a slate roof? You shall not be the loser.’

The shepherd looked at the lord, and then at the bell in his hand. He thought of the kneeling sheep, the silent lark, the goat of the widow Fenwick. He thought of the strange, solemn joy that had filled his heart at the sound. It was not a thing to be kept in a cabinet, he thought, like a dead butterfly under glass. It was a thing that belonged to the hillside, to the storm, to the morning. ‘My lord,’ he said, ‘I cannot sell it. It is not a thing for a cabinet. It is a thing for the world.’

Lord Alaric’s face grew dark with a cold and terrible anger. ‘You refuse me?’ he said, his voice a silken whisper. ‘You, a shepherd, refuse your lord? I could have you whipped from my lands. I could take it from you by force. But I am a generous man. I will give you one last chance. Give me the bell, and you may keep your flock and your cottage. Refuse, and you will find that the valley of Alderon is no longer a pleasant place for you.’
