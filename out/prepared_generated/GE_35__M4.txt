The library at Overleigh was a room of noble proportions, though its proportions were somewhat disfigured by the necessity of accommodating a large mahogany bureau in the most eligible situation for light—a piece of furniture which had belonged to the late Mr. Henry Ferrers, and which his son, the present owner, regarded with a mixture of filial reverence and a certain impatient sense of its obstructive bulk. It was here that young Mr. Ferrers sat, on a November afternoon, with a letter before him which he had read three times, and which he now folded with a slowness that betrayed how little his mind was occupied with the mere mechanical action. The letter was from his uncle, Mr. Theophilus Ferrers, of Birmingham, a gentleman whose fortune had been made in the hardware trade, and whose opinions upon the subject of landed proprietors were delivered with a bluntness that had something of the forge in it.

“I am sorry to hear,” the letter ran, “that you are in difficulties. I warned your father, when he would have the Overleigh property, that it was a millstone about the neck of a man who had no capital to work it. He would not listen. He was a scholar and a gentleman, and he died a poor man. I do not say it to reproach him. He was my brother, and I hope I know what is due to the dead. But I am not going to throw good money after bad. If you can show me a reasonable scheme for clearing the estate, I will consider it. But I will not pay your father’s debts to save you from the consequences of his imprudence. You are young, and you have your own way to make. I am, your affectionate uncle, Theophilus Ferrers.”

There was no anger in the young man’s face as he folded the letter, but there was a gravity that sat somewhat heavily upon features naturally disposed to gaiety. He was two-and-twenty, and he had that look of fine and somewhat delicate mould which belongs to a race that has been long accustomed to command without the necessity of asserting itself—a look which the possession of broad acres does something to create, and which the loss of them does something to destroy. He had been bred at Oxford, and had come home to find the estate encumbered, the tenants dissatisfied, and the creditors pressing. His mother had been dead some years; his only sister was married and gone into Northamptonshire; and he was alone at Overleigh with an old housekeeper and a man who did the work of gardener and groom, and who regarded the young Squire with a fidelity that was touching in its entire want of calculation.

The scheme which he had been turning over in his mind for the last fortnight was simply this—to sell the outlying farm of Bitterley, which lay at the farther end of the property, and which, though it had been in the family for three generations, was of a soil so stubborn and a situation so exposed that it had never done much more than pay its own expenses. The sale of it would clear off the most pressing of the debts, and leave him the home farm and the house, with a margin sufficient to keep up appearances. It was a scheme that had much to recommend it to a man of sense; but it had this against it, that it involved the cutting off of a limb from the family inheritance, and that it would be regarded by every old tenant on the estate as a confession of weakness. The young man had a sentiment about the land which he was half ashamed of, and which he would have found it difficult to justify to his uncle. He had walked over Bitterley on the previous day, and had stood at the gate of the old pasture, looking at the few sheep that were scattered over it, and thinking that his father had loved that field, and that he himself had ridden over it as a boy. It was not a matter of pounds, shillings, and pence to him, though he wished it were; it was a matter of a certain continuity of life, of a certain dignity that seemed to him to belong to the position of a man who held what his fathers had held before him.

“I am not a fool, I hope,” he said to himself, as he rose and walked to the window. “I know that Bitterley is a bad farm, and that the money would be better in my pocket than in the soil. But there is a feeling about it that I cannot get over. If my father had sold it, I should have thought him right. But I am not my father. I have not his learning nor his—well, I must not say his weaknesses. He was a good man, and he did his best. But he was not a man of business, and I am afraid I am not either. If I sell Bitterley, I shall have to tell the tenants that I am selling it because I cannot afford to keep it. And then they will say that the Ferrerses are going down, and they will begin to think of their leases and their repairs, and the whole thing will go to pieces. If I borrow of my uncle, I shall have to submit to be told that my father was a fool, and that I am a fool for not selling at once. And I shall have to pay him interest, and he will want to know all my affairs, and he will interfere. I would rather sell Bitterley a hundred times. But then there is my mother’s jointure, and there is the mortgage on the home farm, and there is the debt to the solicitor, which must be paid at once. If I sell Bitterley, I can pay the solicitor and the interest on the mortgage, and have a little over. If I borrow of my uncle, I can pay everything, and keep Bitterley. But I shall be in his power, and he will never let me forget it.”
