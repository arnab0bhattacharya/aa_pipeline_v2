The heat, in that part of the world, is not a mere temperature but a presence, a substance that presses upon the skull and fills the hollows of the bones, a vast and patient weight that seems to emanate not from the sun but from the very sullenness of the land itself. It was in this oppressive medium that Mr. Harcourt, a man of precise habits and a more than merely professional integrity, found himself adrift. His steamer, the *Esperanza*, a stern-wheeler of shallow draft and dubious engine, had chugged its way up the great river for three weeks, its labouring heart beating a slow, monotonous tattoo against the immense silence of the forests. The river, which he had been sent to chart, was a turbid, ochre-coloured artery flowing through a body of dark, impenetrable green. It was a world of water and vegetation, of shadow and glare, where the sun, when it appeared, did not illuminate but seared.

Harcourt’s task was a simple one in its conception: to confirm and refine the preliminary survey of a Mr. Jolliffe, whose chart, now lying spread on the table in the *Esperanza’s* hot little cabin, was the foundation of a great financial enterprise. The Central African Rivers Concession and Development Company, with its offices in a grimy London square, had already sold its shares upon the strength of that chart. The chart showed settlements—*Bwanga*, *Kasai-Falls*, *Ngoma’s Reach*—places with names that suggested commerce, labour, and the steady, predictable flow of profit. It showed the river as a commercial highway, its banks punctuated by points of human industry waiting only for the company’s agents to tap their latent wealth. Jolliffe’s ink lines were confident, his soundings regular, his villages marked with neat, reassuring squares. It was a document of pure, unadulterated potential.

But the river was not Jolliffe’s river. The villages were not there. They had passed the supposed site of *Bwanga* a week ago. Harcourt, a man who did not trust a single line he had not drawn himself, had insisted on a landing. The boat was poled towards a muddy bank where the forest leaned over the water as if to drink from it. They found only a slip of sand, a confusion of buttress roots, and the air thick with the drone of insects. There was no clearing, no smoke, no sound of human life. Only the overwhelming, indifferent presence of the jungle. Harcourt had stood there for a long time, his boots sinking slightly into the soft earth, feeling the profound and unnerving stillness. It was not merely that a village was absent; it was that the very ground seemed to deny the possibility of one ever having existed.

Since then, the pattern had repeated itself. At *Kasai-Falls*, there were no falls, only a wide, sluggish bend where crocodiles lay like logs in the shallows. At *Ngoma’s Reach*, there was no reach of a different character, no sign of a chief or a trading post, only the same unbroken wall of green and the same suffocating quiet. Jolliffe’s chart, he began to understand, was not a map of the land, but a map of an idea—an idea conceived in a distant office and imposed upon this reality with the force of capital and conviction. The villages were not errors of observation; they were, in their own way, facts. They existed in the ledgers of the company in London, in the minds of the shareholders who had purchased a future. They existed, perhaps, in the mind of the unfortunate Jolliffe, who may have been driven mad by the solitude, or who may have simply, with a stroke of the pen, created what he had been sent to find.

The moral weight of the situation settled upon Harcourt with the same physical oppressiveness as the heat. His own map, the one he was now painstakingly constructing, was becoming a document of negation. Each day, with his theodolite and his chains, he was confirming an absence. He was charting the void where Jolliffe had drawn prosperity. His own neat, careful lines—the true course of the river, the actual soundings, the blank spaces where villages were supposed to be—were a quiet, devastating refutation. He felt like a man who has been sent to inspect a magnificent palace only to find a plot of empty, weed-choked ground. His duty was to report the truth, but the truth, in this instance, was a kind of ruin. It was the ruin of an enterprise, of a fortune, of the hopes of men he would never meet. The chart he was making would not be a guide for development, but a warrant for failure.

One evening, as the sun bled a furious orange across the water, Harcourt sat with the two charts before him on the cabin table. Jolliffe’s was a work of imagination, of ambition. His own was a work of observation, of cold reality. The two could not be reconciled. To send his map back to London would be to detonate a mine under the very foundations of the Concession Company. He thought of the directors, men of substance and reputation, and of the small investors, the clergymen and widows, who had put their modest savings into this dream of African wealth. His map would be an act of profound betrayal. Yet, to alter it—to perhaps suggest a small settlement here, a possible trading post there, to soften the brutal fact of their non-existence—would be a betrayal of a different order. It would be a lie. It would be to become another Jolliffe, a creator of phantoms, a man who traded in the currency of falsehood.

The mate, a taciturn Portuguese named Ferreira, came to the doorway. "The water is quiet tonight, senhor," he said, his voice low.
