The Princess Iolanthe of Marblemere was beautiful, and knew it; and being beautiful, she was proud; and being proud, she was of a nature so exquisitely contrived for the torment of others that her courtiers declared she must have been fashioned by some divine hand in a mood of ironic gaiety, to show how perfectly a woman might be lovely and how perfectly she might be cold. Her father, King Theodorus, had built for her a palace of white stone beside the great forest, and there she held her court, and there she received her suitors, and there she dismissed them, one by one, with a smile that was like the closing of a door.

She had refused a king of the northern isles because his crown was too heavy, and a prince of the southern vineyards because his verses were too light; she had refused a warrior because his hands were stained with blood, and a philosopher because his hands were stained with ink. Each, when he departed, left behind him some gift—a necklace of opals, a mirror of beaten silver, a bird that sang in seven languages, a cup carved from a single emerald—and these she placed in a cabinet of glass, and looked at them sometimes, and yawned.

At length, when the tale of her refusals had travelled to every corner of the world, she caused a proclamation to be made: that she would marry no man who came to her with a crown or a poem, but only that man who should bring her something she had never seen. And the world, which loves a riddle, set itself to answer it.

They came, after that, in a great procession. A king of the eastern deserts brought a carpet woven of the hair of a thousand white camels, so fine that it could be drawn through the eye of a needle, and the Princess looked at it and said, “I have seen carpets.” A wise man of the mountains brought a stone that fell from the sky and burned with a cold fire, and she said, “I have seen stones.” A magician brought a glass in which one might behold the face of one’s beloved, and she looked in it and saw only her own, and said, “I have seen mirrors.” And so they went, one after another, and one after another they were dismissed, until the throne room was empty, and the Princess sat alone, and the court whispered that she would never marry at all, and the King her father grew grey with vexation.

Now in the forest that bordered the palace there lived a shepherd boy named Corin, whose father had been a shepherd before him, and whose mother had been a washerwoman, and who had never in his life seen a palace or a princess or a jewel or a wonder of any kind, except the wonders of the sky at evening and the green miracles of the wood in spring. He was poor, and he was simple, and he was happy; and when he heard the proclamation, he thought about it for a long while, sitting on a stone by the pool where he watered his sheep.

“She has seen everything,” he said to himself, “and she is tired of everything. But there is one thing she cannot have seen, for no one has ever seen it, and that is her own face as others see it, when she is not looking.” And he rose, and took from the grass a little round mirror of still water in his cupped hands—for the pool was very calm that morning—and he carried it carefully, drop by drop, all the way to the palace.

When he came into the throne room, the courtiers laughed; for his cloak was coarse, and his feet were bare, and he carried nothing in his hands but what seemed to be a little water. The Princess, however, did not laugh. She was weary of laughter, and weary of jewels, and she looked at him with the cold curiosity of one who has looked at too many things.

“And what have you brought me, shepherd?” she asked.

“A thing Your Highness has never seen,” he said.

“And what is that?”

“Yourself,” said Corin, and he held out his hands.

The Princess leaned forward, and in the little trembling mirror of the water she saw her own face—not as the silver mirrors showed it, framed and flattered, but as it was in that moment, curious and cold and a little cruel, with the shadow of the throne-room behind it and the wonder of a child in its eyes. And for an instant she did not know it. And then she knew it, and she drew back as though she had been struck.

“Take it away,” she said. “Take it away. It is a trick.”

“It is no trick, Your Highness,” said the shepherd. “It is only water. But it is the one thing you have never seen, because you have never looked at yourself without a mirror to tell you what to think.”

Then the courtiers, seeing the anger in her face, seized the boy and dragged him from the hall; and the water fell from his hands upon the marble floor, and lay there in a little pool, and in the little pool the ceiling of the throne room was reflected, and the chandeliers, and the far-off painted sky, and no one looked at it at all. And Corin was thrown out of the palace gates into the dust, and he picked himself up and went back to his sheep, and was happy, for he had never expected anything else.

But the Princess Iolanthe did not sleep that night. She lay in her chamber of ivory and rose-leaf, and she thought of the face she had seen in the water—her own face, cold and proud and lonely—and she thought of the shepherd’s words, and she was ashamed. And in the morning she rose before the sun, and she went out of the palace alone, in a cloak of grey, and she walked in the forest until she came to the pool where the shepherd watered his sheep.
