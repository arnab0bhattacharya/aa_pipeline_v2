The fog was of that particular yellow-brown complexion which belongs to London in November, and it pressed against the windows of the St. Ambrose Theatre in Dean Street as though curious to see what transpired within. I had been retained, in a quiet way, by Mr. Lionel Trenchard, the manager, and it was in the character of a minor dramatic critic for a provincial journal that I presented myself at the stage door upon the afternoon of the dress rehearsal.

Trenchard met me in the passage by the property room, his face the colour of the tallow candles which stood in a cluster upon a shelf nearby. He was a thin, nervous man, given to plucking at his lower lip, and he did so now with an agitation that spoke of a mind ill at ease.

“You have come, then,” said he, in a husky whisper. “I had half hoped you would think it a fool’s errand.”

“I never think any errand a fool’s errand until I have proved it so,” I answered. “You have the letter?”

He drew it from an inner pocket and placed it in my hand. It was a common sheet of the cheaper sort of notepaper, such as may be purchased at any stationer’s for a penny the packet, and the writing was in a rough, back-slanted hand, as though the writer had been at pains to disguise it.

*“The woman Marlow will not survive the opening night. Let those who profit by her death take warning.”*

“It is the second I have received,” said Trenchard. “The first came a week ago, and I burned it, thinking it the malice of some discharged servant. But this—this has shaken me. Miss Marlow laughs at it. She says it is the work of a rival, or perhaps of that creature Wainwright, who played opposite her at the Lyceum and has never forgiven her for leaving.”

“And what do you think?”

“I think,” said he, lowering his voice still further, “that I am a ruined man if anything happens to her. She is the whole piece. Without her the theatre closes within the fortnight. And there is another thing, Mr. —?”

“Halloway,” said I, giving him the name I had chosen.

“There is another thing, Mr. Halloway. Old Prisk, the stage doorman, has seen a stranger about the place. A man in a grey ulster, who has stood in the mews at the back, watching the stage door and the scene dock, and once—so Prisk swears—studying the trapdoor machinery through the open doors of the dock, as cool as you please, until Prisk challenged him and he walked away.”

“That is more to the purpose,” said I. “Where is this Prisk?”

“In his box. I will take you to him.”

The stage doorman was a small, wizened fellow with a face like a winter apple and eyes that had the quick, sidelong glance of a man who has spent forty years in the service of the theatre and knows every trick of it. He repeated his story with a wealth of detail that carried conviction.

“Grey ulster, sir, and a soft hat pulled low. A tall man, and thin, with a way of standing very still, like a cat at a mouse-hole. He had a notebook in his hand the second time, and he was looking at the trap and the windlass and the counterweights as though he would take their measure.”

“The trap in the stage floor?”

“The star trap, sir, that lets an actor come up through the boards as if he had sprung from the earth. It is worked by a windlass and a man at the ropes, and it wants a cool head and a steady hand, for if the catch is not true the poor soul comes down with a crash that would break every bone in his body.”

I asked to see the machinery, and Prisk led me through the dark wings to the stage, where the company were assembling for the rehearsal. The star trap was a cunning piece of work, a section of the floor cut into tapering wedges which opened outward and downward when the windlass was turned, allowing the performer to rise through the aperture upon a small platform. I examined the catch and the counterweights with some care, and I noted one thing which I kept to myself: the bolt which held the catch in its seat was bright and clean at the tip, while all about it the iron was dull with age and the grease of years.

Miss Ada Marlow was upon the stage when I came out from the wings, and a finer figure of a woman I have seldom seen. She was rehearsing the great scene of the second act, and her voice rang through the empty house with a power that made the dusty chandeliers tremble. When the curtain fell she came down to the footlights and spoke to Trenchard with a fine contempt upon her handsome face.

“Is this the detective?” said she, looking me over. “Lionel, you are a greater fool than I took you for. It is Wainwright, I tell you—or that cat of a wife of his. They would give their ears to see me fail.”

“And yet,” said I, “you do not object to the trapdoor?”

She laughed. “I have gone up through that trap every night for three weeks, and I shall go up through it on the opening night, if it pleases God. If some enemy of mine has tampered with it, it is your business to find him, not mine to tremble.”

The rehearsal proceeded, and I watched from the back of the stalls. The scene in which the trap was used came at the end of the second act, and Miss Marlow, in the character of a spirit risen from the grave, was to ascend through the boards amid a blaze of green fire. I saw the stage carpenter take his place at the windlass, and I saw the trap open and the actress rise, and the whole thing was done as smoothly as a clock.
