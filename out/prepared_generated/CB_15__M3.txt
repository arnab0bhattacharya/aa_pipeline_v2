The rain drove against the diamond-paned windows of Vanebridge Hall with a fury that seemed to echo the tumult in my own breast. I stood alone in the oak-panelled parlour, watching the swollen waters of the Aire tear at its stone bridges, and I felt—not for the first time—that the elements without were more honest than the society within. My uncle, Mr. Thorne, had that morning pronounced his final verdict upon my scheme of managing the Vanebridge estate myself, and his words still rankled, burning like acid in my mind.

"You are mad, Clara," he had said, with that slow, heavy emphasis he used when he wished to crush opposition. "Utterly, wilfully mad. Your poor father left you to my guardianship, and I shall not see the property squandered by a girl's caprice."

I had answered him with a look, and then with words that, if not prudent, were at least just. I told him that my father's will had vested the estate in me at one-and-twenty, that I was now three-and-twenty, and that what he called guardianship had become, in practice, a kind of imprisonment. He had turned the colour of old brickwork and quitted the room. I was left to the storm and my own reflections, which were neither soft nor yielding.

I had resolved, some weeks before, to visit the tenants myself, to learn the state of their dwellings, the terms of their leases, the nature of their grievances. I had discovered, through the plain questioning of the steward's daughter—a girl with honest grey eyes and a directness of speech that put me in mind of what truth might sound like if it were ever permitted to speak aloud in drawing-rooms—that the rents had been raised twice in three years, that two families had been turned out of their cottages in the dead of winter, and that the wool-combers in the lower valley were working fourteen hours a day for wages that could not feed their children. These facts, when I laid them before my uncle, he dismissed as exaggerations, the grumbling of the ungrateful poor. When I pressed him, he told me I was emotional, that I did not understand business, that I had better concern myself with my needlework and my music.

It was on the afternoon following this quarrel that the Reverend Mr. Hale called. He found me still seated at the window, my book unopened on my lap, my eyes fixed upon the grey sweep of the moor beyond the mill chimneys. He was a man of five-and-forty, neither handsome nor plain, with a countenance that bore the marks of thought and a certain dry humour that showed itself chiefly about the mouth. He had been the parish priest of Vanebridge for seven years, and in that time had earned, I believe, the respect of most and the affection of some—though affection, in this dour corner of Yorkshire, was a commodity sparingly bestowed.

"So," he said, seating himself without ceremony, for he was a man whom ceremony wearied, "I hear you have been making yourself disagreeable, Miss Vane. That is the report your uncle has circulated through three parishes."

"I am flattered," I replied, "that my uncle should think my influence extensive enough to disturb three parishes. I had supposed him indifferent to anything I might do."

"He is not indifferent. He is alarmed. There is a difference." He regarded me with an expression I could not at once interpret—grave, but not unkind. "Tell me, Miss Vane, what is it you intend?"

"I intend," I said, and my voice, I am sure, took on a steadiness that surprised us both, "to do what my uncle has failed to do. I intend to see the cottages for myself, to speak to the tenants, to examine the mill-books, and to discover why men who labour from dawn to dark in this valley cannot earn bread enough for their families."

Mr. Hale was silent. He turned his hat in his hands, slowly, as a man does when he is weighing a matter of some consequence. At last he said, "You will find, Miss Vane, that the world is not arranged to accommodate a young woman who asks such questions. You will be called eccentric. You will be shunned by the families at the Grange and at Kirkleigh Park. Your uncle will oppose you with every resource at his disposal, and he has many. You will be, in short, very much alone."

"I have been alone," I returned, "for the greater part of my life. The condition is not new to me. What is new is the discovery that I need not remain passive in it."

Something altered in his face then—a deepening of attention, a sharpening, as though he had expected compliance or at least hesitation and had found instead a will that matched his own in its quiet resolve. He did not smile, but the lines about his mouth relaxed.

"I believe," he said, "that you are in earnest."

"I have never been more so."

He rose, and stood a moment by the fire, looking down into the grate where the coals glowed with a sullen, concentrated heat. "Then I will tell you this, Miss Vane. I have been seven years in this parish, and in that time I have seen the poor of Vanebridge grow poorer and the rich more entrenched in their indifference. I have preached submission from the pulpit, and I have believed, while I preached it, that I was doing God's work. I am no longer certain. There are moments when I think that submission, in the face of such injustice, is not a virtue but a sin—the worst kind of sin, because it clothes itself in piety." He paused. "If you ride down to the lower valley tomorrow, I will go with you. I know the families there. They will speak to me where they would be silent with a stranger."
