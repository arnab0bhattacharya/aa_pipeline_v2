The steamer churned the black water into a fringe of white lace, dissolving it instantly, and the wake stretched out behind them like a road that one cannot walk down, so smooth, so deliberate, until it folded into the vast indifference of the fjord. The cliffs rose sheer, dripping with the wet of a thousand winters, their granite faces scarred and striped with the silver of unseen streams that fell from a height so tremendous they became mist before they ever reached the dark water. There was a profound silence in this, in the moving of the boat and the falling of the water and the dripping of the rocks; a silence which seemed to Eleanor to press upon her eardrums, making them ache, so that she wanted to press her hands against them and listen to her own blood, which was after all the only sound truly belonging to her.

She stood by the rail, her fingers damp with the blown spray, and watched the receding shore. There was a village there, a cluster of red roofs like dropped berries among the pines, and a church with a stubby white tower, and she thought how the people there were living their lives, marrying, dying, eating boiled fish, entirely unaware of the steamer and its trail of smoke, which from their shore must look like a dark smudge against the immense grey cliff. It was curious, she thought, how one could be so near and so far; how the water, which seemed a solid thing, a black floor, was yet a barrier more absolute than any wall, dividing them utterly from the soil, the roots, the solid earth.

Arthur Penderton came and stood beside her. He did not speak at once. He was a man who had learned the value of silence, having been married for twenty years to a woman who spoke continually, and having lost her two years ago, so that the house in Surrey was now full of a silence that appalled him, a silence that had a texture, like velvet, and a weight, like a stone on the chest. He stood now, his hands in his pockets, looking not at the cliffs but at the deck, at the grain of the wet wood, and at Eleanor’s boot, which was planted firmly upon it.

“They say the glacier is visible from the next bend,” he said, and his voice, breaking the silence, seemed to Eleanor a small, brave thing, like a bird venturing out into a vast snowfield.

“Is it,” she said. She did not turn her head. She was conscious of him, of his height, of the faint smell of wet tweed, and of the fact that he was not looking at her but at her boot, which made her feel both observed and ignored. She had never married. There had been a man, once, in her thirtieth year, who had written her letters in purple ink, and she had not married him, and the reason, if there was a reason, was something she could never quite articulate, a sense that marriage would be like closing a book one was enjoying, and reading the same page over and over. She had felt this keenly, and yet now, at forty-two, standing on the wet deck of a Norwegian steamer, she wondered if the book had not been closed anyway, only by a different hand, and if the page she was reading was not perhaps rather blank.

“Yes,” said Arthur. “A vast river of ice, they say, that moves at the rate of an inch a day.” He paused, considering this. “One cannot imagine an inch a day. It is the pace of a snail, and yet it grinds the rock to powder.”

Eleanor turned to him then. She saw that his eyes were grey, like the water, and that there were lines around them that had not been there, she imagined, when his wife was alive. Or perhaps they had; one could not know. One saw people only in their present state, like a photograph, and imagined their pasts, which were after all only stories one told oneself.

“It is the patience of it that is terrifying,” she said. “That something so slow could be so destructive.”

He looked at her, and she felt a sudden, sharp connection, as if they had both, for an instant, glimpsed the same shape in the fog. It was gone immediately. He looked away, back at the cliffs, and she felt the distance between them reassert itself, the vast, polite distance of two people who had been introduced in a hotel lobby and had exchanged names and pleasantries and had discovered nothing essential about each other at all.

Behind them, on the bench beneath the lifeboat, the young people were arguing. Their voices rose and fell, competing with the throb of the engine and the slap of the water.

“But it is the sound that matters,” said Philip, leaning forward, his dark hair falling across his forehead. He was holding a book, a slim green volume, and he struck it with his finger. “The sound precedes the sense. You cannot say that a poem means such and such, and then look at its sound as an afterthought. The sound is the meaning. It is the meaning before meaning.”

“That is absurd,” said Clara, who was sitting opposite him with her hands folded in her lap. She wore a straw hat with a ribbon, and the wind tugged at the ribbon. “If sound were all, then a nonsense rhyme would be the equal of Hamlet’s soliloquy. A baby babbling would be a poet. There must be thought. There must be a structure of ideas.”

“Ideas!” Philip exclaimed, as if the word were a fly he was swatting. “Ideas are what kill poetry. You take a living, breathing thing, and you pin it to a board with your ideas, and then you wonder why it does not fly.”
