The bus conductor on the number 12 route had begun to anticipate her boarding at the stop outside the British & Imperial Typewriter Company. She was there every morning at ten past eight, waiting on the kerb with her leather satchel held across her chest, a slip of a girl with dark hair strained back so tightly beneath her hat that it seemed to pull her features into a permanent, quiet alertness. He had seen hundreds of young women like her come up from the country since the turn of the century—pale, careful creatures who gripped their pennies and stared out of the bus windows as though the city might at any moment reach in and pluck them from their seats. This one never sat downstairs with the other typists and shop girls. She climbed to the top deck and always took the seat at the front, regardless of weather, and she watched the streets unspool beneath her with the fixed attention of someone memorising a text. He collected her fare on a Tuesday morning in October, and she held out the coins in her palm without looking at him, her gaze still fixed on the procession of carts and hawkers below. He found himself wondering, as he rang the bell and moved on, what she was looking for, whether she saw something in the city that he, after fourteen years on the route, had long ceased to see.

Mrs. Ellison, wife of the Reverend Ellison of St. Dunstan's, Saffron Hill, kept the hostel for young working women on Clerkenwell Road and had done so since 1898. She took a personal interest in each of her thirty-two residents, though her interest manifested chiefly in the enforcement of rules and the surveillance of habits. The new girl, Miss Fairchild, had arrived from Yorkshire in the first week of September, recommended by some parish connection, and Mrs. Ellison had marked her at once as the type who would either settle quietly or go home within the month. She was nineteen, perhaps twenty, and she possessed that particular quality of stillness that Mrs. Ellison had learned to read as either resilience or concealment. Miss Fairchild kept her room immaculate, returned before the ten o'clock curfew without exception, and ate her breakfast in the communal dining room in silence, her eyes on her plate. Once, passing the open door of the residents' sitting room on a Sunday afternoon, Mrs. Ellison had observed her sitting at the window with a book open on her lap, unopened for the best part of an hour, simply watching the street below with that same expression the bus conductor might have recognised. Mrs. Ellison made a note in her ledger—*Miss F. quiet, observant, no acquaintances yet*—and resolved to keep watching. She had seen girls like this fade into nothing, and she had seen them bloom. It was too early to tell which.

In the office, the junior clerk, Philip Marsden, had developed the habit of finding reasons to pass the row of desks where the typists sat. There were six of them now, recruited to handle the increasing volume of correspondence that the British & Imperial had generated since securing the government contract, and they worked in a bay partitioned from the main office by a screen of frosted glass. Miss Fairchild sat at the end of the row, nearest the window, and she typed with a speed and precision that the office manager, Mr. Cullen, had remarked upon more than once. What Marsden noticed, however, was not her efficiency but the way she carried herself between tasks. When she paused to change the paper or wait for dictation, she would sit with her hands resting in her lap and her gaze directed somewhere beyond the window, beyond the slate rooftops opposite, and there was about her a quality of self-containment that he found both arresting and faintly unnerving. He had attempted conversation twice—once in the corridor, once at the tea trolley—and both times she had answered with a courtesy so precise and final that he had been left holding the remnants of his sentence like a man who has opened an umbrella in a room. He could not decide whether she was shy or merely indifferent, and the uncertainty kept him returning to the partition, glancing through the gap where the glass panels met, watching her hands move across the keys.

In a stone farmhouse on the lane between Hawes and Askrigg, Mrs. Fairchild sat at the kitchen table reading her daughter's letters by the light of a paraffin lamp. They came every Sunday, written in a hand that had grown smaller and more controlled since leaving home, as though the paper itself were a resource to be managed. The letters were not unaffectionate, but they were spare, and Mrs. Fairchild read them the way she read the weather—looking for what lay behind the surface. *The work is agreeable. The hostel is comfortable. I have seen St. Paul's from the river and it is very fine.* Her daughter did not say whether she was lonely. She did not say whether she had made friends. She mentioned no names, described no companions, and this absence was itself a kind of information that Mrs. Fairchild received with a tightening in her chest. She wrote back at length—news of the neighbours, the price of wool at the market, her father's lumbago—but she knew that her letters arrived in London as fragments of a world her daughter had already begun to leave behind, and she folded them into their envelopes with a care that bordered on deference, as though she were addressing someone she was slowly ceasing to know.

The winter deepened and the city settled into its grey, December shape. On the bus, the conductor noticed that the girl had begun to sit downstairs on the coldest mornings, and once, when the vehicle jolted over a rut in the road, she looked up at him and smiled—an expression so brief and unexpected that he found himself smiling back before he could think about it, and then she was looking away again, and the moment folded itself into the ordinary passage of the morning.
