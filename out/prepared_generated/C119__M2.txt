The tower of Aelion was not a prison, its keepers insisted, but a sanctuary. It was a spire of white marble rising from the center of the Capital of Glass, surrounded by gardens that defied the logic of soil and season. Here, orchids bloomed in winter, and ivy climbed the walls in spirals that mimicked the turning of the stars. For eighteen years, Aelion had known only the smell of lilies, the sound of wind chimes made of crystal, and the soft, shuffling steps of his attendants. He was the Firstborn, the Light of the Kingdom, raised in the highest chamber where the air was always still and the light was always golden.

On the day he turned eighteen, the King, Oram, ascended the spiral stairs. Oram was a man of immense stature but quiet demeanor, his face a mask of serene indifference that had protected him from the turbulence of the lower city for decades. He stood before his son, who was pale as the marble floor and thin as the reeds that grew in the decorative ponds below.

"Today, Aelion," Oram said, his voice echoing slightly in the high vault, "the old laws are suspended. You are an adult. You may ask for one thing. Anything within the realm of this kingdom. A horse? A sword? A map of the coast?"

Aelion tilted his head. He had heard stories in the fables read to him by his tutors—tales of heroes and villains, of castles and caves. But he had never seen a person who was not dressed in silk, never heard a voice that did not speak in a whisper of refinement. He looked at his father, then at the window, where the sky was a perfect, unblemished blue.

"I want to see a poor man," Aelion said.

The silence that followed was heavier than the stone walls. Oram blinked, his composure cracking for the first time since his son’s birth. "A poor man? Why? What do you mean?"

"I mean someone who has nothing," Aelion said, as if explaining a simple arithmetic problem. "The books say there are people who live in the shadows of the towers. I want to see one. I want to know what it looks like to be small."

Oram’s face flushed a deep crimson. He had never seen a poor man. In fact, no one in the upper echelons of the Kingdom of Glass had. The lower city, the Undercity as it was privately called, was a blur of smoke and noise that was filtered out by the tall hedges and the sound-dampening fields that separated the royal gardens from the rest of the world. To Oram, the poor were a theoretical concept, a necessary tax bracket, not a reality.

"This is... irregular," Oram stammered. "There are no such people in the gardens. There are no such people in the palace."

"Then send someone to find one," Aelion said, his voice steady. "Send your best minister. Bring him to me. I will wait."

Oram looked at his son’s unblinking eyes. He could not refuse. The ritual of the eighteenth birthday was sacred; to deny a wish was to invite bad luck, or so the old superstitions held. More importantly, Oram was terrified. He did not know what a poor man looked like, and he was afraid that if he did not find one, his son would know that the world Aelion had been promised did not exist.

Oram turned on his heel and marched out, his heavy boots thudding against the marble. He descended to the war room, where his chief minister, Vane, was waiting with a map of the city spread across a table of obsidian.

"Find a poor man," Oram commanded, slamming his hand on the table. "Bring him to the Tower of Aelion. He must be authentic. Do not bring me a beggar from the theater. I want a true specimen of the lower class."

Vane, a man with sharp eyes and a sharper tongue, raised an eyebrow. "Your Majesty, the Lower Districts are... unregulated. The streets are narrow, the air is thick. It is not safe for a royal guard to wander alone."

"I do not care about safety," Oram snapped. "I care about appearance. Find someone who looks like they have never eaten a full meal in their life. Bring him here by sunset."

Vane sighed, folding the map. He knew this was a mistake. He had seen the lower city once, years ago, when he was a junior clerk. He remembered the smell of coal smoke and wet wool, the chaotic energy of people who had to fight for space. He remembered the way their clothes were patched and repatched, the way their eyes held a hardness that was absent in the polished citizens of the upper rings. But he had not seen one of them in twenty years. The world had changed, or perhaps he had simply stopped looking.

He sent out a squad of guards, not to the streets, but to the edge of the Upper Gardens, where the hedges grew thick. He instructed them to look for anyone who had slipped through the gaps in the fencing, anyone who had been working as a laborer in the outer fields and had not yet been sent back.

It took three hours. The sun began to dip below the horizon, casting long, purple shadows across the white marble. Oram paced the corridor outside Aelion’s room, his mind racing. What would the boy do? Would he be horrified? Would he be disgusted? Or would he be fascinated? Oram realized with a jolt of panic that he did not know. He had raised his son in a vacuum, and now he had to introduce him to the gravity of the real world.

Finally, the door opened. Vane stepped in, followed by a small figure.
