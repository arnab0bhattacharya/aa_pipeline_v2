It was a truth universally acknowledged, though never, I am sorry to say, universally acted upon, that a young man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife; but a young man in possession of nothing but the promise of a good fortune—which is to say, a young man in possession of an uncle—must be in want of patience, of hope, and of a constitution strong enough to withstand the prolonged and vexatious inactivity of waiting. Such a young man was Mr. Ferdinand Fledgeby, and such a young woman, who had by this time exhausted her own stock of the former virtues, was Miss Amelia Pemberton.

Their courtship, if so it could be called, had been a long and arid season, a sort of perpetual autumn where the harvest was always promised for the following spring, and the spring for the following year. For seven long years had Amelia Pemberton given her youth, her hopes, and her affections to Ferdinand Fledgeby, and for seven long years had Ferdinand Fledgeby given her in return nothing but the luminous and unsubstantial promise of his uncle’s demise. The uncle in question, Mr. Gideon Grimshaw, was a gentleman of Islington, whose villa, “Belle-Vue Cottage,” was as grimly furnished as his name and as far removed from any belle or vue as the imagination could conceive. Mr. Grimshaw, who was a retired vinegar-merchant and looked as though he had been personally pickled in his own stock-in-trade, was the sole obstacle between Ferdinand and a competence; and the old gentleman, with a perversity that was the very cornerstone of his character, seemed to derive a singular and spiteful pleasure from his own continued existence.

The scene of our present narrative is the front parlour of Miss Pemberton’s mother’s house, a small, respectable establishment in a street of such uniform and unrelenting brick that it seemed to have been designed by a person with a profound hatred of variety. The parlour itself was a monument to hopeful waiting. The furniture, though neat, was worn thin with anxiety; the clock on the mantelpiece ticked with a slow, monotonous, and despairing beat, as if counting not the minutes, but the years of Amelia’s devotion. It was a Sunday afternoon in November, and the rain fell upon the window-panes with a persistency that was almost personal, blurring the view of the wet flags outside and adding to the general atmosphere of damp resignation.

Amelia, a young woman whose beauty was of that gentle, fading kind which seems to require sunshine to bring it to perfection, stood by the window. Her face, which had once been as bright and hopeful as a June morning, was now touched with the faint, weary lines of a long and disappointing engagement. She was twenty-seven years old, and she felt every one of those years as a weight upon her heart. Opposite her, lounging in an easy-chair with an air of profound and unmerited confidence, was Ferdinand. He was a handsome young man, it must be admitted, with a fine head of hair and a manner that could be charming when it was not being maddeningly evasive. He was, at that moment, engaged in the delicate operation of peeling an orange, a task which he performed with the same languid optimism with which he approached all the serious business of his life.

‘Ferdinand,’ said Amelia, her voice quiet but firm, like the last, clear note of a bell before it is silenced, ‘we must speak.’

‘My dearest Amelia!’ he replied, not looking up from his orange. ‘Are we not always speaking? Is not our communion of souls a perpetual and delightful conversation?’

‘I mean a serious conversation, Ferdinand. About the future.’

At this word, Ferdinand’s hand, which was about to separate a segment of the orange, paused. The future was a subject he approached with the same trepidation as a man approaches a dentist’s chair. ‘The future, my love,’ he said, with a sigh that was meant to be profound but was merely theatrical, ‘is in the hands of Providence. And, to a certain extent, in the hands of my uncle.’

‘It has been in his hands for seven years,’ said Amelia, turning from the window to face him. The light from the grey sky fell upon her face, and he saw, perhaps for the first time, that the gentle hope which had been her chief ornament was gone, replaced by a look of resolute weariness. ‘Seven years, Ferdinand. I was a girl of twenty when you first spoke to me of our prospects. I am a woman now, and my prospects are as dim as ever. I have waited, and I have hoped, and I have believed, but I can wait no longer.’

‘Amelia, my angel, what can you mean?’ cried Ferdinand, dropping the orange in his alarm. ‘My uncle is a man of advanced years. His constitution, though stubborn, cannot last for ever. The doctors themselves have said—’

‘The doctors have been saying it for a decade!’ she interrupted, her voice rising with a passion that startled them both. ‘I have heard it all, Ferdinand! I have lived upon the crumbs of your uncle’s anticipated mortality! I have been nourished by the faint hope of his cough, and warmed by the distant prospect of his rheumatism! I cannot live on air any longer. I must have a home, a name, a certainty. I must have a husband, not the ghost of one.’

She stood before him, her hands clasped, her bosom heaving, a picture of desperate resolve. Ferdinand, who had never seen her in such a state, was thrown into a perfect panic. The comfortable edifice of his procrastination, so long and so carefully constructed, seemed to be tumbling about his ears.

‘You cannot mean—’ he stammered, rising to his feet. ‘You would not leave me?’
