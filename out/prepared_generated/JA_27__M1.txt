It is a truth very generally acknowledged, that a man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of heirs; and it is an evil not less generally lamented, that the heirs are so frequently to be found wanting in every quality which might render them deserving of the distinction. Mr. Ambrose Whitmore of Felstead Hall, in the county of Norfolk, was a gentleman whose long life had afforded him ample opportunity to confirm this observation. He had seen his relations, from the most distant cousin to the nearest nephew, display a solicitude for his health and happiness which was always in exact proportion to the vigour of his own constitution. As he had never married, the circle of his expectant relations was wide, and their attentions, long a source of quiet amusement to him, had of late become a source of considerable vexation.

The occasion for this change in his temper was a visit from his nephew, Mr. Fitzwilliam Croft, a young man whose fortune was already ample and whose ambition was amply proportioned to it. Mr. Croft, in an hour of unguarded candour, had undertaken to explain to his uncle the precise management of the Felstead estate, which he conceived to be sadly neglected. His suggestions were delivered with that particular air of condescension which is the surest mark of a shallow understanding, and which, when directed towards a man of Mr. Whitmore’s sense and experience, could not fail to produce an effect the very reverse of that which was intended.

Mr. Whitmore heard him out with a patience that was remarkable only for its brevity, and then, with a smile of the driest irony, observed, “You have given me much to consider, nephew. It is a great comfort to an old man to know that his affairs will fall into hands so capable of improving upon his own.”

Mr. Croft, taking this for a compliment of the first order, bowed with ill-concealed triumph, and took his leave, little suspecting the storm his officiousness had summoned.

The following Sunday, the family assembled at Felstead for dinner as was their custom. They were a numerous party, comprising Mr. Croft, his mother Mrs. Croft, her sister Lady Anne Gresham, and several cousins of varying degrees of connection and expectation. The conversation, as was natural, turned upon the parish, the weather, and the health of Mr. Whitmore, all subjects upon which the company could express a great deal of feeling without committing themselves to a single opinion. Mr. Croft, emboldened by his recent success, was holding forth on the subject of crop rotation when his uncle raised a hand.

“I have an announcement to make,” said Mr. Whitmore, and the silence that fell was of a quality that only the prospect of a will could produce. “I have lately been much impressed by the excellent work of the Norfolk Asylum for the Relief of the Destitute. I have therefore resolved that, upon my decease, the whole of my fortune, with the exception of a small provision for my ward, Miss Alice Thorne, shall be settled upon that institution.”

The effect of this declaration was as instantaneous as it was profound. A general gasp seemed to draw all the air from the room. Mrs. Croft turned a colour usually associated with the worst kind of bilious complaint. Lady Anne Gresham, a woman whose piety was a matter of considerable public record, pressed a handkerchief to her lips as if to stifle an exclamation of sacrilege. Mr. Croft, for his part, was the very picture of consternation; his mouth opened and closed several times, but no sound emerged, much like a fish removed from its element.

It was left to Mrs. Croft to articulate the family’s collective horror. “An asylum, Ambrose? You cannot be serious! To leave the Felstead fortune to strangers? To the *destitute*? What of your own flesh and blood?”

“I am perfectly serious, Harriet,” replied her brother, with an untroubled calm that was more infuriating than any display of anger. “I have observed that my flesh and blood, when not actively engaged in calculating the term of my life, are occupied in devising how best to spend my money. I find I prefer the gratitude of the destitute; it is less speculative.”

This piece of wit, so far from placating the company, only deepened their sense of injury. The remainder of the evening was passed in a state of genteel devastation. The relations, who had come to Felstead in search of preferment, now took their leave as if departing from a funeral. Their professions of affection, so freely offered that morning, were now replaced by a wounded silence that spoke more eloquently of their disappointment than any complaint could have done. They departed, one and all, resolved, it may be supposed, to prove their uncle wrong by withdrawing those attentions he had so lately scorned.

In the month that followed, Felstead Hall was a place of profound quiet. Mr. Whitmore, who had expected to enjoy the solitude, found it had a quality he had not anticipated. The silence was not merely an absence of noise, but an absence of life. His relations, true to their new purpose, did not call. He received no letters of concern, no offers to read to him, no awkwardly phrased enquiries after his gout. The estate, which had been a constant theatre of social ambition, was now merely a large house in which an old man sat alone. He told himself this was a victory; but a victory, when it leaves one in such complete isolation, begins to resemble a defeat.

It was therefore with a feeling for which he did not care to find a name, that he observed Miss Alice Thorne walking up the gravel drive one crisp afternoon, a small basket on her arm. She was the daughter of a deceased friend, and had been his ward for some years, residing with a respectable family in the nearby village. She was a young woman of a quiet disposition and an understanding that was not, perhaps, brilliant, but which was rendered invaluable by a singular goodness of heart.
