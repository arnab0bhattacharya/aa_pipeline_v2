It is a truth sufficiently obvious to the most cursory observer of human affairs, and yet one that must be continually re-discovered by each generation in the heat of its own particular convictions, that the most intractable of our disagreements are those which occur not between strangers, but within the circle of our own affections. For there, in the intimate precincts of home, a difference of opinion is no longer a mere abstract proposition to be weighed and discarded, but a personal affront, a betrayal of the shared blood and common history that ought to have produced a shared mind. Such a division, when it comes, does not announce itself with the clear note of a trumpet, but grows, like a slow-acting poison in the well-spring of a household, tainting every draught until the very source is called into question.

In the county town of Kingsbridge, during that turbulent spring of 1832 when the whole nation seemed to hold its breath awaiting the passage of the Great Reform Bill, such a poison was at work in the family of Mr. Josiah Croft. Josiah was a man whose substance was of the earth, earthy; he was a brewer, and his opinions, like his celebrated Stout, were the product of long fermentation and a profound respect for established ingredients. He had built his prosperity on the thirst of his neighbours and the loyalty of his customers, and chief among these was Sir Giles Bellamy, the old Member for the county, a Tory gentleman of the old school whose seat in Parliament seemed as permanent a fixture of the landscape as the spire of St. Jude’s. Sir Giles drank Croft’s Best Bitter at the King’s Head on market day, and for Josiah, this was a fact of more weight than any number of speeches delivered in Westminster. The Member’s politics were simple: things as they were were best, and any tampering with the ancient constitution of the realm was a folly that would end in ruin for honest men.

To this sturdy, practical creed, his son, Arthur, had lately become a heretic. A young man of a more nimble and bookish disposition than his father, Arthur had been to a dissenting academy in the Midlands and had returned to Kingsbridge with his head full of Mr. Brougham and the necessity of a wider franchise. He saw the world not as a settled arrangement to be preserved, but as a system riddled with injustice, awaiting the rational application of reform. His arguments, delivered with the earnest heat of a convert, were to his father not merely wrong, but a species of ingratitude. Did the boy not see the good beer that funded his education, the solid roof that sheltered him? To espouse the cause of men like their own drayman, Silas—a good enough fellow in his place, but a man who could scarcely read a placard—over the claims of a gentleman like Sir Giles, was to turn the natural order on its head.

The quarrel, which had smouldered for weeks over the breakfast table and in the quiet of the evening parlour, burst into open flame one Tuesday afternoon in the brewery yard. The air was thick with the sweet, heavy scent of malt and hops, and the great copper vats gleamed dully in the afternoon sun. Josiah, his face florid from the heat of the mash-tun, was directing the loading of a dray, while Arthur stood by, his hands in his pockets, watching the labourers.

“The men are talking of nothing else, father,” Arthur said, his voice low but insistent. “They’ve heard of the meetings in the town. They believe the Bill will give them a voice, a stake in the country they help to build.”

Josiah gave a grunt of contempt, not ceasing his work. “A voice? What voice does a man want that he hasn’t already got? He wants a voice, let him buy a pint of my Stout and have his say at the bar. That’s voice enough for any man who knows his place.”

“But it’s not a matter of place, it’s a matter of right,” Arthur pressed, his colour rising. “Why should a man’s worth be measured by the rent he pays? Silas there, he works harder than many a man with a vote, yet his opinion counts for nothing.”

At the mention of his name, Silas, a powerful man in a leather apron, paused with a barrel on his shoulder and looked over, his face impassive. Josiah turned on his son, his voice thick with a brewer’s wrath.

“Silas knows better than to fill his head with such rubbish! He knows where his bread and beer come from. It comes from work, boy, and from loyalty—not from scratching a cross on a piece of paper because some city agitator has told him he’s been wronged. Sir Giles has been a friend to this family and this town for thirty years. He drinks my beer. These Radicals—what do they promise? To pull down the fences and let the mob into the garden. They’ll ruin us all.”

“They promise him justice, father! The same justice you’d want for yourself.”

“Justice!” Josiah roared, the word a curse. He slammed a heavy mallet down on a barrel-head for emphasis. “I’ll tell you what’s justice! It’s a fair day’s wage for a fair day’s work. It’s a good brew that doesn’t sour. It’s a man standing by his friends and his betters! This Radical talk is a poison, and you, my own son, are bringing it into my yard. You would see me ruined to satisfy a fancy you’ve picked up in some pamphlet. You would sell your own father’s livelihood for a stranger’s vote.”

The injustice of the charge stung Arthur into silence for a moment. He saw the bewildered faces of the workmen, the uncomprehending loyalty in Silas’s eyes. He saw his father not as a tyrant, but as a man of his time and his class, whose world was being threatened by forces he could not comprehend. Yet the principle at stake was too large to be surrendered to filial piety.
