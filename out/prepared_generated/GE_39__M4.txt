The apothecary’s assistant, Mr. Abel Hartley, came each morning at half-past nine, and the sound of his knock—three strokes, the last two quick together, like a man who would not presume upon a fourth—had come to seem to Ellen Marrable the true beginning of the day, so that the hours before it were but a waiting, and the hours after it a species of aftermath in which the world resumed its common colour. Her mother’s sick-room at the back of the house in Coggles Lane looked out upon the river, and the light that came off the water in the early part of the morning had a tremulous quality, a restless shifting of pale gold and grey, which seemed to Ellen to partake of the uncertainty of all mortal things; and it was in this light that Mr. Hartley’s countenance, always pale and somewhat thin, took on a look of gentle gravity that was not quite melancholy, as though he had schooled himself to expect little and had found the discipline not unkindly.

He was not the doctor, but the doctor’s assistant, a young man of no fortune and of a family obscure—his father had been a schoolmaster in a neighbouring village and was dead—and there were those in Marthorpe who thought it a condescension in Dr. Nunn to employ him, though the doctor himself, a stout and easy man who loved his dinner and his whist, was heard to say that Hartley had more physic in his little finger than half the faculty in their whole bodies. However that might be, it was certain that Mrs. Marrable, who had been ailing since the turn of the year with a slow and wasting disorder of the lungs, found a comfort in his visits that she found in nothing else, and would have it that the very manner of his entering the room, with his quiet step and his careful avoidance of any loud or sudden sound, did her more good than the mixtures and the blistering. Ellen, who had nursed her with a devotion that was the more absolute for being unexamined, had at first regarded him with the suspicion which a careful housekeeper entertains for all members of a profession that must be paid; but by the time the spring had come and the willows along the river had put forth their grey-green catkins, she had ceased to think of him as a person who sent in a bill, and had begun to think of him—she hardly knew how or when—as a person whose coming she desired.

It is a subtle and gradual thing, this transfer of the heart’s allegiance from the abstract to the particular, and it is seldom accompanied by any conscious act of the will. Ellen Marrable was two-and-twenty, and had been for three years the object of a steady and respectable attachment on the part of Mr. Thomas Gedge, a corn-factor of Marthorpe, a man of five-and-thirty, with a red face, a loud laugh, and a comfortable house at the end of the High Street, whose suit was favoured by her father, a retired malster of a sanguine and practical turn, who could see no reason why a girl should not marry a man who could keep her in comfort, and who regarded the languors and refinements of sentiment as things which a well-regulated mind should put away with other childish toys. Mr. Gedge was not an unkind man; he was, indeed, generally esteemed a good fellow, and he had a way of slapping his thigh and crying “Well, Miss Ellen, and how’s the world using you?” which was meant to be hearty, and which Ellen had once thought so. But it is one of the ironies of our nature that the very qualities which commend a man to the world at large may be those which, in the closer air of a sick-room, betray their insufficiency; and Mr. Gedge, who could not enter a room without filling it, who brought with him an atmosphere of malt and merchandise and loud good-humour, and who would say, “Cheer up, ma’am, you’ll soon be about again,” in a voice that made the invalid wince, had come insensibly to seem to Ellen like a noise from the outer world which she was glad to have shut out.

Whereas Mr. Hartley, who spoke low, and who would sit by the window and read to her mother from the “Quarterly” or from a volume of Cowper, and who had a way of looking at the patient with a steady, considering gaze that seemed to take in not only her symptoms but her whole condition, had come to be associated in Ellen’s mind with all that was quiet and faithful and sustaining. She had begun to watch for the three strokes of his knock; she had begun to arrange the little matters of the room—the placing of the chair, the folding of the counterpane—with a view to his coming; and she had begun, though she would have been shocked to own it, to think of what he might be thinking of her, and to wonder whether the faint colour that came into his cheek when she spoke to him was the effect of the morning air or of something more personal.

The summer came on, and with it a change in Mrs. Marrable that was not for the better. Dr. Nunn shook his head and spoke of the necessity of keeping up the strength; Mr. Hartley came twice a day, and sometimes in the evening; and Ellen, who had slept for weeks in a chair by her mother’s bed, grew pale and thin, and yet found in her fatigue a strange exaltation, as though she were being admitted into a fellowship of suffering that had its own consolations.
