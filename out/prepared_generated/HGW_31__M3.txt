I had been exactly six weeks the medical officer of the Kerguelen whaling station when the men brought the thing in from the shelf ice. It was a desolate enough post, I assure you, a cluster of corrugated iron huts and reeking blubber boilers clinging to the black rock of the coast, with the perpetual grey soughing of the Southern Ocean for all our music. I had taken the berth largely out of a private disgust for the comfortable routines of London practice, and I found the rough, practical men of the station—and their somewhat cynical manager, Mr. Henley—excellent company enough, provided one did not expect too much refinement.

It was the harpooner, Jakes, who first sighted it, half-buried in a glacial crevasse some two miles inland. They brought it back on a sledge, a great, recumbent mass of ice, with the dark shape of the creature visible dimly through the frozen shell. They deposited it with a good deal of grunting and profanity in the empty corner of the main shed, between the disused try-pots and the spare boat-gear.

"Seal, I suppose," said Henley, standing over it with his hands in his pockets and his pipe clamped in his teeth. "A monstrous big sea-elephant, by the look of it."

"Sea-elephants don't have tails like that," said Jakes, pointing with a grimy finger through a patch of ice the men had scraped away. "It's a reptile, or I'm a Dutchman."

I must confess the thing puzzled me extremely. It was perhaps eighteen feet in length, heavily built, with a squat, powerful body and four stumpy limbs that suggested a crocodilian or saurian origin. But the head, so far as one could make it out through the murky ice, was curiously mammalian, lacking the extended snout one would expect. The tail was long and compressed, like that of a whale, but covered—so it seemed—with a rough, shaggy pelt. It was, in short, a zoological absurdity, a thing out of its proper time.

"I shall have to send word to the Natural History Museum," I said, examining it with a professional interest. "This is no modern beast."

"Indeed you will not," said Henley, turning upon me with a sudden sharpness that was quite unlike his usual phlegmatic manner. "This station isn't run by the British Museum. This beast is station property, found by station men, and station men will see the profit of it. There's a market for curiosities, and a thing like this, preserved and mounted, would fetch a thousand guineas from a showman in London or New York."

I argued the matter with him for some time, pointing out the scientific value of the specimen and the importance of proper examination, but he was immovable. He was a lean, hard-featured man, with a keen eye to profit, and the vision of a thousand guineas had utterly blinded him to any other consideration. The men, too, were on his side, for they were to share in the proceeds of the sale, and I found myself, as I had often done before in that wild place, in a minority of one.

So the thing lay in the shed, and the business of the station went on, and I thought no more about it for a day or two, save as a curious problem in natural history. It was the heat of the shed—the try-pots had been in use, and the place was stifling—that began the trouble. On the third evening, happening to go into the shed for a coil of wire, I noticed a most extraordinary smell. It was a heavy, sweetish, musky odour, quite unlike anything I had ever smelt before, and it seemed to come from the corner where the ice-block lay.

I went over to it, and saw at once that the ice had thawed considerably. A pool of water had collected about the base, and the outlines of the beast were far more distinct than they had been. The shaggy pelt, I now perceived, was of a deep, reddish-brown, and the limbs ended in great, clawed feet. The head, as the ice retreated from it, disclosed a broad, flat skull, with eye-sockets that stared blankly and horribly at the rafters. But it was not the appearance of the thing that startled me so much as the smell. It was an animal smell, the smell of a living creature—the smell of a cage in a menagerie.

I stood and looked at it, and a most disagreeable suspicion began to form in my mind. The thing was not merely thawing; it was thawing with a quite unusual rapidity, as if the heat of the shed was being drawn into the ice with a kind of hunger. I put my hand upon the surface of the block. It was wet, of course, but it was also—surely—warm. I pressed my fingers deeper into the thawed pelt, and I could have sworn that beneath it there was a faint, rhythmic pulsation.

The thought that smote me was so grotesque that I laughed aloud in the empty shed. And then, even as I laughed, I stopped, for the eyelid of the beast—the one nearest to me—flickered. It was a tiny movement, a mere quiver of the skin, but it was enough. I stood for a moment frozen with a horror that was partly physical and partly intellectual—the horror of a man who sees the impossible become real before his eyes. The thing was alive. Frozen, perhaps, for centuries in the ice, it was alive, and the warmth was bringing it back.

I ran to the door and called for Henley. He came in, wiping his mouth after supper, and I pointed to the beast without a word. He looked at it, and then at me, and I saw his ruddy face go pale.

"Good God," he said. "It's moving."

"The heat," I said. "The heat of the shed is reviving it. We must get it out into the cold again."
