The wind howled against the leaded panes of the parlor window, a low, mournful moan that seemed to vibrate in the very marrow of my bones. I sat by the dying fire, the embers shrinking into a faint, red glow, mirroring the slow extinguishing of the life I had known. For twelve years, I had been the invisible anchor of this household, the silent engine that kept the gears of Brother Arthur’s curacy turning. I had mended his clerical gowns, cooked his meals, and smoothed the wrinkles from his conscience when the weight of the parish pressed too heavily upon his shoulders. I had sacrificed my own desires, my own ambitions, my own identity, for the sake of duty and the preservation of his peace. And now, in a single, brutal stroke, that duty was revoked.

The letter lay upon the table, its seal broken, the ink still damp with the freshness of Arthur’s confession. *I have found one, Elara, who can share the burden of the house. I intend to propose to Mrs. Galloway.* The words were plain, devoid of apology, stripped of the softening euphemisms he usually employed when addressing my feelings. He did not ask for my permission; he did not even offer a consolation. He simply stated a fact, as one might state the weather or the price of coal.

I looked at my hands, roughened by the cold water of the laundry, stained by the soot of the hearth. They were the hands of a servant, not of a lady, nor of a scholar. I had no trade. I had no savings. I had no family save this man, who now cast me aside like a worn-out glove.

A sharp knock at the door shattered the silence. It was Arthur. He entered without waiting for an answer, his face pale, his eyes avoiding mine. He looked ill, as if the shock of his own decision had sickened him, yet he carried himself with the stiff, rigid dignity of a man who believed himself justified in his severity.

“You have read it,” he said. It was not a question.

“I have,” I replied. My voice was steady, though my heart hammered against my ribs like a trapped bird. “And you have decided, without a word, without a whisper of intent, to change the very foundation of this house.”

Arthur shifted his weight, his eyes fixing on a point somewhere near my shoulder. “It is a necessary step, Elara. I am tired. The work is heavy, and the house is cold. Mrs. Galloway is a good woman. She has resources. She will bring order.”

“Order,” I repeated, the word tasting of ash. “You speak of order as if I were a source of disorder. For twelve years, I have kept this house in order. I have kept you in order. I have kept you from despair, from drink, from the chaos that threatens every man who serves God in a place where He is often forgotten. And now, because I am a woman without a dowry, without a title, I am to be displaced by a stranger who brings with her only the promise of comfort?”

“Elara,” he said, his voice rising in that sharp, authoritative tone I had learned to fear. “Do not be dramatic. I am not displacing you out of malice. I am marrying because it is my duty to secure a future, and because I love this woman. You must see reason. You are thirty. It is time for you to think of yourself.”

“I have been thinking of myself for twelve years, Arthur! I have buried myself in your needs until I have no shape left of my own. Now you tell me to think of myself? With what? I have no money. I have no training. I have no friends who would take me in, for I have given all my time and affection to you. You have made me useless to the world, and now you cast me out of the only world I have.”

He flinched at the accusation, but he did not yield. “That is unfair. I have always valued your help. But I cannot live my whole life in the shadow of your sacrifice. I need a partner, not a keeper.”

The words struck me with the force of a physical blow. *Not a keeper.* That was what I was, in his eyes. A function, not a person. A vessel for his comfort, to be discarded when a more convenient vessel appeared.

I felt a sudden, fierce heat rise in my chest, a fire that burned hotter than the embers on the hearth. It was not anger, not at first, but a terrible, clarifying clarity. I had spent my life trying to be good, to be obedient, to be silent. I had believed that goodness would be rewarded, that duty would be recognized. I had been wrong. Goodness, in a world that values strength and independence, is often taken for weakness. It is exploited. It is ignored.

“And what of Mrs. Galloway?” I asked, my voice low and dangerous. “Does she know that I am leaving? Does she know that I am the one who keeps the accounts, who manages the servants, who ensures that the poor are fed and the church is clean?”

Arthur sighed, a long, weary exhalation. “We have discussed it. She is amenable to arrangements. She does not wish for conflict.”

“No,” I said, standing up. The chair scraped harshly against the floor. “She does not wish for conflict because she knows I have no power. She knows I am poor, and therefore harmless. She knows that if I leave, she takes my place, and I vanish into the shadows, where I belong, according to you.”

I walked to the window. Outside, the night was dark and stormy. The trees in the garden swayed violently, their branches clawing at the sky like desperate hands. I watched a leaf tear free and spiral down into the mud, lost and forgotten.
