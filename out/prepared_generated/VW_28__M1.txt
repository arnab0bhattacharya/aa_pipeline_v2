The light was not like English light, which lies upon the world like a damp cloth, blurring and softening; here it fell in sharp, hard planes, cutting the marble into chalk-white and blue shadow. Edith sat on the broken drum of a column, feeling the heat of the stone through the thin grey of her serge dress, and watched her brother, Arthur, who stood with his back to the Parthenon, his umbrella casting a small, precise circle of shade upon his shoes. He was looking at the frieze through a pair of spectacles, his lips moving slightly as he counted the horses—a habit he had acquired at Cambridge and never lost—and she knew, with that certainty which comes from thirty years of shared breakfasts, that he was not seeing the horses at all, but rather the concept of the horses, the grammar of their limbs, the rhythm of their procession. To him, this place was a text to be deciphered; to her, it was a sudden, blinding encounter with the world as it might be if one could only shed the weight of living.

“It is a pity,” Arthur said, lowering the spectacles and blinking at the glare, “that the west pediment is so ruinous. The Centauromachy would have been instructive.” He turned to her, his face red with the heat, his expression one of mild, scholarly regret. “Shall we go to the Acropolis Museum, Edith? There is a fragment of the Erechtheion frieze I particularly wish to see. It is catalogued as number 112.”

“Yes,” she said. “Let us go.”

But she did not move immediately. She sat there, feeling the dust of Attica settle upon her hands, and thought of the garden in Surrey where the roses grew against the wall, and how the light there was always grey-green and shifting, never still, never silent. Here, the silence was a physical thing, pressing against the ears. A lizard darted over a stone, its skin the colour of the sun-scorched earth. She watched it vanish into a crack in the marble and felt a sudden, sharp pang of envy for its small, bright life. She had come here to be useful to Arthur, to carry his notebooks and his shawls, to listen to his lectures on the Doric order, and yet, sitting here, she felt as though she had never seen anything before in her life. The blue of the sky was not a colour but a force, pressing down, and the white of the stone was not white but a radiance that hurt the eyes.

They walked down the dusty path towards the museum, Arthur’s boots crunching rhythmically on the gravel. He was talking about the restoration of the Propylaea, his voice dry and brittle as the cicadas that sang in the olive trees. Edith walked slightly behind him, watching the way the sun caught the sweat on the back of his neck. He was a good man, a kind man, who had taken care of her since their mother died, but he lived in a world of ghosts and fragments, and she realized, with a clarity that was almost painful, that she had been living there too. She had followed him through the museums of Europe, nodding at the capitals and the cornices, but she had never really looked. She had been looking at Arthur, at his opinions, at his needs.

In the museum, the cool air was a relief, but the light was dim and artificial. Arthur moved eagerly from case to case, his notebook open. “Here,” he said, pointing to a small, broken piece of stone. “Do you see the drapery? It is quite remarkable. The way the sculptor has suggested the movement of the leg beneath the cloth.”

Edith looked. She saw a piece of stone, grey and rough. She did not see the leg. She looked at Arthur’s face, illuminated by the faint light, and saw the passion there, the pure, unadulterated joy of the intellect. It was beautiful, in its way, but it was cold. She turned away and looked out of the window at the cypress trees, black against the blinding white of the hills. She felt a restlessness in her limbs, a desire to run, to shout, to break something. She wanted to feel the sun on her skin again, to be burned by it.

“I think I shall wait outside,” she said.

Arthur looked up, startled. “Outside? But the heat, Edith. You will faint.”

“I shall be quite all right,” she said. “I want to see the light.”

She left him there with his ghosts and walked out into the blinding afternoon. She did not go back to the Parthenon; instead, she walked down the slope, past the olive groves, towards the city below. The dust rose around her feet, and the heat shimmered above the roofs of the houses. She felt light-headed, almost giddy. She had no shawl, no hat; she had left them with Arthur. She was just a woman in a grey dress, walking in the sun.

She came to a small square where a fountain splashed into a stone basin. The water was green and cool. She sat on the edge and dipped her hand into it. A woman with a black headscarf and a basket of lemons looked at her and smiled, her teeth white in her dark face. Edith smiled back. She did not speak the language, but she understood the smile. It was a smile of recognition, of shared humanity. She felt a sudden surge of affection for this stranger, for the lemons, for the water, for the hard, bright light of Greece.

She thought of the years she had spent in the drawing-room, dusting the china, arranging the flowers, listening to Arthur read aloud from his papers. She had thought that was life, that was duty. But here, in this square, with the dust on her shoes and the sun on her face, she felt as though she were waking up from a long, grey sleep. The beauty of the world was not in the stones of the temples or the fragments of the friezes; it was here, in the light on the water, in the woman’s smile, in the feeling of her own blood moving through her veins.
