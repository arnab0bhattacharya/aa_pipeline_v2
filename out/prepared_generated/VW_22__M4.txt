The omnibus, heavy with wet and the smell of wool and leather, lumbered out of Hammersmith into the grey afternoon. The rain had stopped, but only in the way that rain does in London, sullenly, leaving the air so thick with moisture that the shop signs seemed to swim behind a film of water, and the pavement, when anyone stepped down, gave back a soft, sucking sound, as if the city itself were a sponge that had been squeezed and not yet wrung dry. Inside, the windows were opaque with steam and the breath of passengers, and through this fog the world appeared in glimpses—a crimson omnibus crossing at the corner, a woman’s umbrella blown inside out, the pale yellow of a gas lamp already lit though it was not yet four—fragments that came and went like the thoughts of a person half asleep.

Miss Ada Finch, a typist of three-and-twenty, sat with her knees pressed together and her gloved hands folded on her bag, and thought of Mr. Gerrard. He had said, on Friday, that he would walk with her as far as the bridge, and he had not come; and yet on Saturday he had passed her desk and had said, quite distinctly, “The weather is breaking, Miss Finch,” and she had answered, “Yes, indeed, Mr. Gerrard,” and the whole of that exchange—his voice, the way his hand had rested for a moment on the corner of her ledger, the smell of his tobacco—had been enough to fill her with a warm, confused happiness that had lasted until this morning, when she had seen him at the window of a tea-shop in the Strand with a woman in a grey coat. Grey, she thought; a dull colour, a colour without any imagination; and yet perhaps it was his sister, or his cousin, or a woman from the office in the City. The omnibus jolted over a cobble and she caught at the rail, and her thoughts, dislodged, went tumbling: if he had meant nothing by the ledger, why had he touched it? why had he said the weather was breaking, as though the weather were a secret between them, a thing that only they two had noticed? The rain, the bridge, the grey coat—it was all nonsense, she thought, and felt the tears come hot behind her eyes, and then, with a little shake of her head, she looked out at the passing shops and fixed her mind on the pattern of a hat in a window, and was calm again.

Opposite her, an old man in a black coat, green at the seams, held in his palm a little heap of coins and counted them with a finger that trembled, not with age only but with the effort of arithmetic. Twopence, a penny, a halfpenny, a sixpence worn smooth—he moved them from one palm to the other, and each coin seemed to carry a weight of its own, a history, a morning’s walk, a purchase made and regretted. He was going to see his daughter at Turnham Green, and he had not seen her for a year, and he was trying to decide whether the fare and the little gift he had bought—a china bird, wrapped in paper in his pocket—would leave him enough for the week. He counted again, and the figures would not come right, and he thought, with a sudden clarity that was almost a relief, that it did not matter, that at his age the counting of pennies was a kind of prayer, a way of holding off the dark. The woman beside him shifted her child, and the child, a boy of five or six with a flushed face and eyes that glittered, coughed—a small, tearing cough that made the passengers glance and then look away.

The mother said, “There, there, ducky,” and pressed her hand to the child’s forehead, and felt the heat there, and thought of the doctor’s fee, and of her husband, who would say it was nothing, that children were always coughing, and of the long night ahead with the fire to be kept up and the kettle to be boiled. She was not a woman given to self-pity, and she did not now indulge it, but she thought, with a kind of grim satisfaction, that she had managed before and would manage again; and she looked at the old man counting his coins and at the typist with her eyes fixed on the window, and she felt a sudden, fierce tenderness for them all, these strangers, these fellow-passengers, each locked in the small hot prison of his own thoughts. The child coughed again, and she drew him closer, and the omnibus lurched round a corner and the whole company swayed together, as though the vehicle were a single body and they its limbs.

In the corner seat, a clergyman, thin and pale, with a long nose and a collar that seemed too large for his neck, was composing a sermon. He had chosen his text—the text was “And the rain descended, and the floods came”—and he was trying to find his way from the text to the congregation, who would be, he knew, mostly women, and tired, and thinking of their dinners. He wanted to say something about the floods of the world, the floods of sorrow and of sin, and how the house built upon the rock stood firm; but the words would not come, or rather they came too easily, in a smooth, familiar torrent, and he was sick of them. He looked at the child with the flushed face, and at the mother, and thought that here was a house that would not stand firm, that was built upon the sand, and that his sermon would not help it. He felt, for a moment, a terrible dryness, a sense that the whole of his life had been a preparation for a thing that did not exist; and then the omnibus stopped, and a young man got in, shaking the rain from his hat, and the clergyman, seeing him, thought of his own youth, and of a girl he had known at Oxford, and the sermon, like a machine set going, began to turn over in his mind again, smoothly, and he was almost happy.
