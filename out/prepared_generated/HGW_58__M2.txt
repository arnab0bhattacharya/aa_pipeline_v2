The heat of the August afternoon in Hampshire was a physical thing, a heavy, wet blanket that lay upon the rolling green hills and the dry, dusty lanes. Arthur Pym, a clerk in the bicycle trade in London, was not a man accustomed to such oppressive atmospheres, nor to the leisurely pace of a holiday. He was a young man of twenty-four, with a face that was perpetually flushed from the exertion of pedaling and a mind that was perpetually occupied with the mechanical advantages of a high-wheel or the specific tensile strength of a steel spoke. He was cheerful, not by temperament, but by a sort of professional necessity; one does not sell cycles with a frown.

He had stopped his machine at the edge of a hedgerow near the village of Cuckoo’s Hollow to drink from his flask. It was a respectable day for a holiday, if one disregarded the stifling air. He was contemplating the merits of a new chain guard when he became aware of a commotion in the lane behind him. It was not the sound of a horse, for the hoofbeats were too erratic, nor the rumble of a wagon. It was the clatter of a trap, driven with a reckless disregard for the condition of the road, and the sharp, cracking voice of a man who had lost his temper.

Arthur turned on his heel. The trap was a rickety affair, painted in faded red, and it was being driven by a man who looked as though he had been chewing on a piece of raw leather for the last hour. He was a stout, ruddy-faced individual, with a beard that had been trimmed into a shape that suggested a severe geometric theorem. He was shouting at the top of his lungs, not at the horse, but at the air, or perhaps at the universe in general.

"Stop!" Arthur called out, stepping into the road. "You will run over a man, sir."

The trap skidded to a halt, kicking up a cloud of white dust. The driver’s eyes, small and dark behind spectacles that slid down his nose, fixed upon Arthur with an intensity that was almost frightening.

"Get out of the way," the man snapped. "I am pursuing a fugitive. A young woman. I have a legal right to apprehend her."

"Indeed?" Arthur said, adjusting his waistcoat. He felt a sudden, irrational interest in the matter. It was far more stimulating than a chain guard. "And where is this fugitive, if I may ask?"

"Gone!" the man shouted, pointing a thick finger down the lane. "Into the woods. She ran. She ran as soon as she saw me. You will not see her. You are a stranger. I know a stranger when I see one. A Londoner, by the look of you. A clerk, perhaps. You have that air. You look as though you spend your days counting coppers in a dim room."

Arthur blinked. "I am a bicycle clerk, to be precise. And I am on holiday. And I think, sir, that you are being somewhat uncharitable. If a young woman is running away from you, it might be worth considering why she is doing so."

The man’s face darkened. "It is not for you to question. She is under my guardianship. She is wayward. She is foolish. And now she is with a stranger. I can see it in your eyes. You are colluding with her. You are helping her to escape."

Arthur looked at the man, then at the dusty lane, and then back at the man. He was not a violent man, nor a particularly brave one, but he had a certain stubbornness about him that had served him well in the shop. "I have not seen a young woman, sir. I have been standing here drinking water for the last ten minutes. If she is with me, I am the first to know of it."

The man narrowed his eyes. "You are a liar. I know men. I know when a man is up to mischief. You will give her to me. You will turn her over. Or I will have you arrested for abduction."

"I should be surprised if I were arrested for a crime I have not committed," Arthur said, with a mildness that seemed to infuriate the driver even more. "But I will tell you this. If that young woman is running away from you, and if she is with a stranger, then perhaps you ought to look a little closer at your own conduct before you point the finger at others."

The man opened his mouth to reply, but before he could form a sentence, a figure emerged from the hedgerow. It was a young woman, perhaps twenty, with hair the color of chestnuts and a face that was pale and drawn, but not with fear. She wore a simple dress, dusty at the hem, and she was holding a small bundle in her hand. She looked at the trap, then at Arthur, and then, to Arthur’s astonishment, she smiled.

"Thank you," she said, her voice clear and steady. "I was wondering when you would stop. I have been running for three miles."

The driver’s face turned a shade of purple that Arthur had only previously seen in a bruised plum. "Margaret!" he roared. "I told you I would find you. I told you I would take you back."

Margaret stepped closer to Arthur. She was not a tall woman, but she held her head high. "You will take me back where I am not wanted, Mr. Thorne. You will take me back to a life of silence and submission. I would rather walk to the end of the earth."

Arthur looked at her, and then at the driver. He felt a sudden surge of sympathy for the girl. She was not a criminal; she was a human being asserting her right to live. And he, Arthur Pym, a clerk in the bicycle trade, found himself standing between a guardian and his ward, in a dusty lane in Hampshire, on a hot August afternoon.
