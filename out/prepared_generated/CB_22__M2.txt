The rain in Bruges was not the dry, biting wind of our northern fells, but a heavy, weeping mist that seemed to soak into the very stones of the city. I stood before the tall, narrow door of the clinic on the Oudebrugstraat, my heart beating with a rhythm that was less of fear and more of a desperate, clawing hope. I was a widow, young and alone in a foreign land, and I carried a secret so heavy it felt like a stone in my chest, ready to crush me if I should falter. I had left England to escape a past that was not my own, yet which clung to me like the damp wool of my shawl. I knocked. The door opened not with a welcome, but with a scrutiny that made my skin prickle.

It was the sister, Madame Elise Van Houten. She was a woman of sharp angles and colder blood, her face pale as the linen she handled in the clinic, her eyes dark and deep-set, like two wells in a dry earth. She looked me up and down, her gaze lingering on my plain dress, my modest bonnet, and finally, with a sneer I could feel more than see, on my face.

"You are the Englishwoman?" she asked. Her French was precise, clipped, and devoid of warmth. "Dr. Van Houten expected a housekeeper, not a beggar."

"I am here to work, Madame," I replied, keeping my voice steady, though my hands trembled at my sides. "I am capable of managing a household, and I am accustomed to hardship."

"Hardship," she repeated, as if the word were a disease. "We have plenty of that in this quarter. The poor come to us with their sores and their sorrows. We do not need another pair of hands that shake. You are a Protestant, I see. Do you think your prayers will keep the fever from spreading? Or that your God is the same as ours?"

The question struck me with the force of a physical blow. It was not just an insult; it was a test. She was probing for weakness, for the cracks in my armor. I had come here to be invisible, to be merely a function of the household, but she saw me. She saw the stranger, the outsider, the woman who had fled. I thought of the fire in my father’s study, the letters I had burned, the faces I had forgotten in the smoke. I thought of the justice I had sought in England and found only cold indifference. I would not be cowed.

"Madame," I said, and I heard the steel in my own voice, "my religion is my own affair. But my labor will be judged by its result, not by the book I hold in my hand. If you find me wanting, I will leave. But until then, I am the housekeeper. And I will keep this house clean, and warm, and in order, or I will die in the attempt."

She stared at me, her jaw tightening. For a moment, I thought she would slam the door. Then, with a grunt of dismissal, she stepped aside. "Come in. But know this: my brother is not a man who tolerates sentiment. He is a doctor, not a priest. Do not expect him to comfort you. If you weep, you will be turned out."

I followed her into the house. It was a small, stone building, narrow and deep, with the smell of antiseptic and old wood hanging in the air. The walls were bare, save for a few grim engravings of saints and anatomical diagrams. The light was dim, filtered through the grey sky outside. We passed through a kitchen that was clean but cold, into a small parlour where a fire burned low in the grate.

Dr. Van Houten was sitting in a chair by the fire. He was a large man, with a head like a boulder and hands that looked too strong for the delicate work of a physician. He did not look up as we entered. He was reading a letter, his face grave, his brow furrowed.

"Elise," he said, his voice low and rumbling, "why are you so late? The patients are waiting."

"They are waiting, Jan," she snapped. "And here is the Englishwoman. I have kept her out of the way until you could decide if she is fit."

The doctor looked up then. His eyes were dark, like the night sky over the canals, and they held a weariness that was not of the body, but of the soul. He looked at me, not with the hostility of his sister, but with a kind of weary curiosity.

"Sit," he said, nodding to a chair opposite him. "I am Dr. Jan Van Houten. I do not ask many questions. I need a housekeeper who is quiet, who is clean, and who does not pry into the affairs of the sick. Can you do that?"

"I can," I said. "I am quiet. I am clean. And I know when to close my eyes and ears."

He studied me for a long moment. "You have a hard look about you," he said. "It is good. The poor do not need softness. They need strength. And truth. Do not lie to me."

"I will not lie to you, Doctor," I said. "I am here to earn my bread. That is all."

He nodded, satisfied. "Then we are agreed. You will sleep in the room above the kitchen. You will wake at six. You will prepare the breakfast for the staff. You will keep the clinic clean. If you fail, you will go. If you succeed, you will stay. There is no middle ground in this house."

I stood up, my legs stiff from the cold. "I accept, Doctor."
