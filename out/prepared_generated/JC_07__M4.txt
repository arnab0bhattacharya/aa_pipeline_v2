I have known Captain Ibarra for the better part of twelve years, and in that time I have come to understand that a man may sail a thousand miles of coast, touching at every roadstead and river-mouth from the Gulf of Salinas to the Rio Muerte, and yet remain a stranger to the land he serves—nay, a stranger even to himself, if the land is one of those unhappy countries where the morning brings a new flag and the evening a new grave, and the people, exhausted by the mere business of living, have no leisure for the slow growth of loyalties. He was a Basque, this Ibarra, from the neighbourhood of Bilbao, a thick-set, deliberate man with a broad, weathered face and the patient eyes of one who has looked too long at the sea's monotony to expect much from the affairs of men. His vessel, the *Santa Eulalia*, was a small iron steamer of some four hundred tons, rust-streaked and foul-bottomed, which had carried the mails along that distracted coast for a quarter of a century, through revolutions and blockades, through the bombardments of ports and the burning of custom-houses, with a regularity that seemed to mock the transience of the governments she served.

It was my fortune to be aboard her on the voyage which I have in mind, having taken passage at Puerto Alvarado for the northern ports, and it was in the second night out that I found him on the bridge, in the darkness, with the smell of the land—that heavy, sweetish, faintly corrupt smell of tropical vegetation and stagnant water—coming off to us in long, warm breaths, and the lights of the coast showing here and there like the scattered embers of a fire that has burned itself out.

He spoke to me without turning his head.

"You have heard of the *comandante* Rojas," he said. It was not a question.

I had heard of him, indeed. He was one of those men—a colonel of no particular army, a leader of no particular cause—who had risen out of the chaos of the last revolution, and who now held, with a few hundred half-naked Indians and a handful of rifles, the whole of the northern province in a state of terror that was none the less real for being intermittent. His name was a byword among the peons and the small traders; it was spoken in whispers, or not at all.

"I am to land a cargo for him," Ibarra said, after a silence. "At the village of San Mateo. To-morrow night."

I asked him what cargo.

"Rifles," he said, simply. "Two hundred cases of them. And ammunition."

The word hung in the air between us, and I confess that I was startled. For thirty years, as I knew, this man had carried everything that the coast had to offer—coffee, hides, indigo, machinery, the mails, the occasional passenger, the occasional coffin—and had never, by his own account, asked what was in a crate. It was a point of honour with him, a kind of professional pride, this refusal to inquire into the nature of the goods he transported. He had carried rifles before, no doubt; he had carried them for the government, for the rebels, for whoever held the port at the moment of loading. But this was different. This was a cargo for Rojas, and Rojas was not a government, nor a cause, nor even, in any recognizable sense, a soldier. He was a bandit with a taste for blood.

"You know what he will do with them," I said.

"I know," said Ibarra.

He was silent for a while, and the *Santa Eulalia* pushed on through the long swell, her engines beating like a slow heart, and the lights of the coast sliding by, and the darkness pressing in upon us from all sides, so that it seemed to me that we were the only two men awake in a world of sleep and silence.

"I have a wife," he said at last, "in Bilbao. And two daughters. They are grown now, and married, and they write to me sometimes. They think I am a rich man. They think I have a fine ship and a fine house and a fine position. They do not know that I have been carrying the mails for thirty years and that I have nothing but the *Santa Eulalia* and the clothes I stand in. It is better so. A man should not disappoint his daughters."

He paused, and I heard him draw a long breath.

"Rojas has promised me five thousand dollars," he said. "In gold. On the beach at San Mateo. And if I do not land the cargo, he has promised to burn the *Santa Eulalia* and to hang me from the yard-arm of my own vessel. He is a man of his word, in these matters. I have seen the bodies of men who did not keep faith with him."

I asked him if he had considered the alternative—if he had thought of refusing, of turning back, of reporting the matter to the authorities at Puerto Alvarado.

"The authorities," he said, with a kind of weary contempt, "are three days' march away, and they have no soldiers, and they are afraid of Rojas. And as for refusing—" He shrugged his shoulders in the darkness. "I am a sailor, not a hero. I have a ship to think of, and a crew, and my own poor carcass. And besides—" He hesitated.

"Besides?"

"Besides," he said, slowly, "there is the matter of the five thousand dollars. It is a great deal of money. It would keep me in my old age. It would—" He stopped, and then went on with a kind of fierce bitterness: "It would buy me a little peace, a little respect, a little of the comfort that other men have earned with less risk. I have been poor all my life. I have been honest all my life. And what has it brought me? Nothing. A rusty ship and a lonely old age. And now—"
