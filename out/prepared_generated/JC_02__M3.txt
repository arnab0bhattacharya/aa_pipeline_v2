The heat of the valley of Santa Maria lay upon the land like an immense, invisible weight, pressing the breath out of the living and a mute, exhausted resignation out of the very earth itself. To Mr. Arthur Halloway, standing in the narrow strip of shade cast by the crumbling adobe wall of the municipal building, the air seemed a palpable thing, thick with the dust of the Sierra and the faint, sweetish smell of decaying vegetation from the distant riverbed. He had come to this republic, under the high-sounding auspices of the Progressista government, to drive a line of iron through the heart of those impassable mountains. He was a man of exact calculations, of transits and levels, accustomed to the stolid, unyielding nature of earth and rock; but he was discovering, with a bewildered sort of horror, that in this country the earth itself was unstable, quivering with the hidden passions of men whose faces were like masks of dark, seasoned wood.

He held a tracing of the proposed route in his hand, the clean, precise lines of his draftsmanship looking suddenly alien and absurd against the chaotic reality of the landscape. The railway was to be the great artery of civilisation, so the Minister of Public Works had assured him in a distant, echoing room in the capital, a room where the heavy air had been stirred by the languid waving of a palmetto fan. The Minister, a man of melancholy obesity, had spoken of the future with the fatalistic detachment of one who does not expect to see it, yet who is bound by some obscure, inherited duty to pretend a passionate belief in its materialisation. He had promised Halloway the protection of the Republic, a phrase which, even as it was uttered, seemed to dissolve into the surrounding shadows of the room, leaving behind a chilly impression of utter emptiness.

Now, in the village of Santa Maria, the Republic was represented by a lieutenant and a dozen soldiers, whose uniforms hung upon them in a manner suggesting a gradual disintegration, and whose eyes, restless and suspicious, seemed fixed eternally upon the dark mouths of the ravines. The railway survey was protected by their presence, and by the vast, silent conviction of the Minister that the project was inevitable. It was a protection that felt to Halloway like the shadow of a leaf held up against the sun; it was a mockery of the profound darkness waiting in the passes.

The rebel General, a man of immense prestige, whose name was spoken in whispers, held the mountains. He had sent for Halloway. The message had been brought by a silent, watchful man, who had materialised out of the heat haze on the plain, had stood regarding the Englishman with an enigmatic stare, and had then departed as silently, leaving behind a small square of paper bearing two words in a careful, sloping hand. The paper lay now in Halloway’s breast pocket, a slight bulk against his ribs, a trivial thing that yet seemed to throb with a deadly significance. Halloway, in his neat white linen, standing in the shade of the adobe wall, was acutely aware of it. It was as if he carried there not a scrap of paper, but the heavy, accumulated weight of all the silent, brooding hostility of the Sierra. He had not shown it to the lieutenant, who, with his ragged soldiers, represented the force that was to bring civilisation to this land by means of a railway. He had said nothing, because the lieutenant, a man of imbecile gaiety, was engaged in polishing the brass of his sword-belt with a rag, humming a tuneless air, and seemed to exist in a private world of his own, oblivious to the great, shadowy forces closing in around them. Halloway had looked at him, and had thought, with a grim, inner irony, of the Minister’s words. The railway was to bring civilisation, but here, in Santa Maria, it seemed merely to have brought a lieutenant polishing brass in the sun.

Halloway had gone up into the mountains, alone, on the following morning, before the sun had risen above the peaks. The coolness of the dawn was a brief respite, a fragile illusion of clarity. He had gone to meet the General, whose men held the passes. He had gone because the message had demanded it, and because he felt, with a fatalistic resignation that was growing upon him, that he was caught in a current that was stronger than any calculation of his will, a current that was sweeping him, not toward the construction of a railway, but toward some obscure, fundamental reckoning with the very soul of the land.

The trail was steep and difficult, winding through a landscape of savage grandeur, where the rocks were black and jagged, and the sparse vegetation clung to the slopes with a desperate tenacity. The silence was immense, broken only by the distant cry of some bird of prey, or the crunch of loose stones under his boots. He had walked for hours, his mind empty of thought, his eyes fixed upon the trail, his body moving with the automatic precision of a machine. He was aware of a strange, paradoxical sensation: he was alone, utterly alone, and yet he was not alone. He was surrounded by a presence, an invisible, watchful presence, that seemed to flow down from the peaks and up from the ravines, and to press upon him from all sides. He was a white man, a foreigner, a man of science and progress, and he was walking into the heart of a darkness that was not merely physical, but spiritual, a darkness that seemed to absorb the very meaning of his mission.
