The heat lay upon the mountain like a great, soft paw, pressing them, as they mounted the zig-zag path, into a common perspiration, a common subservience to the air, which tasted of crushed thyme and ancient, baked dust. Eleanor, stopping to wipe her neck with a handkerchief already dark with damp, felt the sun not merely shine but strike, boring through her grey cotton blouse into the very marrow of her bones. It was an annihilating heat, yet it did not destroy; rather, it seemed to scoop them out, leaving them hollow, translucent, so that their thoughts rang in their heads with the clarity of bells. Below them, the Bay of San Stefano had hardened into a sheet of hammered copper, and the town, with its brown roofs and prickly cypresses, resembled a shaken-out cloth, dotted, frayed at the edges where the white houses straggled into the scrub.

Cyril, a little way ahead, struck his stick against a stone, and the sharp crack roused Gerald, who had been lagging, his hands in his pockets, staring with a kind of sulky intensity at a lizard that darted across the white dust.

“We shall never get there before evening,” Gerald said, breathing hard, his face flushed to a dark, congested red. He addressed the remark to the air, but it was meant for Lucy, who was walking just behind him, carrying her sketchbook under one arm. She had tied a lilac scarf beneath her chin, and the ends of it blew out, brushing his shoulder.

“What does it matter?” Lucy said, her voice light, detached, as if she were speaking from the deck of a moving ship. She was looking upwards, where the ruins of the watchtower jutted against a sky of bleached, blinding white. “If we don’t get there, we don’t. It’s the walking—” she paused, sucking in her breath as her boot caught on a root, “—it’s the walking that is—”

“Exquisite,” Cyril supplied, turning his head to give her a smile that showed his long, yellow teeth. He was a man of forty, bald, with a stoop, but his eyes were youthful, mocking, and perpetually amused. He had the trick of making whatever he said sound slightly ironical, as though the whole fabric of life were a rather poor joke which he was too polite to laugh at.

Gerald scowled. He hated irony; he liked facts, solid, heavy, respectable, like the furniture in his father’s house in Surrey. And he hated Cyril, partly because Cyril was ironical, and partly because Lucy looked at him when he spoke, narrowing her eyes, as if she were trying to catch the exact shade of his meaning.

“You always say that,” Gerald burst out, stopping dead. “You always say it doesn’t matter. But it does matter. We started at two, expressly to see the view before the haze came up. Now it’s half-past four, and we’re not half-way. If we’d walked instead of sitting about in that wretched olive-grove—”

“Oh, the olive-grove!” Eleanor murmured. She had fallen behind, and stood now, a little apart, looking down the hillside. The words stirred something in her; a memory, not of this olive-grove but of another, years ago, in Italy, when she had been young, and a man had picked up a leaf and said—what had he said? The words had gone; only the leaf remained, grey-green, silky, with a little bend in the stalk. She saw it as clearly as if it lay in her palm. Life was like that, she thought; the events, the people, the very words, dissolved, leaving only leaves, colours, the shapes of things. She sighed, a long, soft sigh, and moved forward to rejoin the others.

Cyril had sat down on a boulder. He was taking off his boots.

“What are you doing?” Gerald demanded, outraged.

“Resting my feet. They’re on fire. I suggest you all do the same. There’s a perfectly good patch of shade under that rock. And look—there’s a stream, or at least a trickle. Lucy, come and put your feet in the water.”

Lucy laughed, a little uncertainly, and glanced at Gerald. But Gerald had turned away, and was staring fixedly at the bay, his back rigid, his hands clenched. She hesitated, then went over to Cyril, and sat down on the grass beside the trickle of water. It was barely a stream, only a thread of moisture sliding over the smooth, grey stones, but it was cool, and she dipped her fingers in it, and then pressed her wet fingers to her wrists.

“It’s delicious,” she said.

“Isn’t it?” Cyril said. He had stretched out his legs, and was leaning back on his elbows, his face tilted up to the sky. “I don’t care if I never reach the top. I don’t care if I never see the view. I’m perfectly happy here. Gerald can go on alone if he wants to.”

“Don’t,” Lucy said, in a low voice. “You know he won’t go alone.”

“Why not? He’s a grown man. He has two legs. He can walk.”

“You know what he’s like. He thinks it’s—well, a question of principle. We said we’d do it, so we must do it.”

“Principle!” Cyril snorted. “The most murderous word in the English language. More people have been killed by principle than by all the diseases put together. However, I surrender. I’ll put my boots on. But first—” he turned and looked at her, and his eyes, under their heavy lids, were suddenly serious, without a trace of irony— “first, tell me something. Why did you come?”

Lucy looked down at the water. It was very difficult to answer him, because she did not know the answer, or rather, she knew it, but it was made of so many layers, so many conflicting impulses, that to peel them all away would take hours, and the core, when reached, might be nothing at all—a mere wisp of feeling, a fancy.
