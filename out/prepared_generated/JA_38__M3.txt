Charlotte Vernon, who possessed a quickness of observation that was sometimes a blessing to her acquaintance and sometimes a trial to herself, had no sooner spent three weeks in the neighbourhood of Beechwood Park, than she became perfectly convinced that Miss Fairfax, the governess employed by Mr. Lucas, was engaged in a most deliberate campaign to secure that gentleman’s hand. Mr. Lucas was a man of large fortune and very few words, a combination which Charlotte had long observed was particularly vulnerable to the designs of an artful woman; and Miss Fairfax, who had the care of his two motherless nieces, appeared to her watchful eye to be exactly such a woman. She was pretty, in a quiet way, and had a manner of speaking with great modesty which Charlotte suspected was nothing more than calculated reserve.

Charlotte had come to Beechwood Park as the guest of her elder sister, Mrs. Carrington, whose husband had recently purchased the adjoining estate; and she had not been in the house two days before she perceived that Mr. Lucas was a man much at leisure, and that Miss Fairfax, under the convenient pretence of consulting him on the education of his nieces, was constantly in his society. She would meet him in the shrubbery with a book in her hand, or intercept him in the breakfast room with a question upon geography; and though each meeting was in itself perfectly innocent, the frequency of them could not, Charlotte thought, be entirely accidental.

“I am sure I would not say a word against her,” said Charlotte to her sister one morning, as they sat together at work in the breakfast parlour. “She is a very respectable young woman, and teaches the girls their French with great diligence. But I cannot think it right that she should be so much with Mr. Lucas. A governess has a settled sphere, and I am persuaded she would keep within it, if she had not some particular object in view.”

Mrs. Carrington, who was a good-natured woman and not much given to speculation, replied only that she supposed Mr. Lucas knew his own affairs, and that Charlotte would do well to occupy herself with her own.

This was advice which Charlotte, having a very high opinion of her own judgement, had no intention whatsoever of following. She had resolved upon interference, and she proceeded to carry it out with a steadiness of purpose which, had it been employed in a better cause, might have been entirely admirable.

Her first attempt was upon Mr. Lucas himself. Meeting him one afternoon as he rode towards the village, she entered at once into conversation upon the subject of his nieces’ education, and from thence, with very little transition, upon the subject of Miss Fairfax’s merits. She praised her industry, commended her patience, and then added, with a careful air of indifference, that she was a young woman who seemed to have a great deal of feeling.

“She is all feeling, I believe,” said Mr. Lucas, who had listened to this preamble with his usual composure. “She is everything that is excellent.”

“Indeed, yes. And one sees sometimes,” Charlotte continued, “that a young woman in her situation, thrown so much into the society of a gentleman of fortune, may be led by gratitude, or perhaps by something warmer, to form expectations which it would be unkind in him to encourage, however unintentionally.”

She paused, expecting him to look conscious or confused; but Mr. Lucas only said, “Oh,” in a tone of perfect good humour, and added some remark upon the weather, which effectually put an end to the subject.

Charlotte was not easily discouraged. She next determined to address herself to Miss Fairfax, and an opportunity presented itself the following day, when the two happened to be alone together in the schoolroom, the children having been taken out by their maid.

Charlotte had seated herself near the window with her work, and after some minutes of silence, during which she had observed that Miss Fairfax appeared perfectly contented with her own thoughts, she began: “You must find your situation very comfortable, Miss Fairfax. Mr. Lucas seems to be a most kind and liberal employer.”

“He is everything that is good,” said Miss Fairfax, with a sincerity which Charlotte immediately set down as evidence of partiality.

“A gentleman of his fortune and consequence,” pursued Charlotte, “must naturally have a great deal in his power. And I have often thought it must be a very particular advantage to a young woman in your situation to have the benefit of his advice, and the advantage of his society.”

Miss Fairfax looked a little surprised, and said she was sure she was very much obliged to Mr. Lucas for all his kindness.

“Kindness is, I fear, a word which is sometimes made to bear a great deal,” said Charlotte, with a significant smile. “But I am sure I ought not to hint at such things. I only mean that in the society of a person of so much superiority, one cannot be too much upon one’s guard.”

Miss Fairfax coloured slightly, and replied, with a gentleness which Charlotte found rather provoking, that she was very much obliged to her for her advice, though she was not conscious of being in any particular danger.

This was discouraging; but Charlotte, who had now committed herself to the business, was not to be diverted from it. She took occasion, the very next evening, when a small party was assembled at Beechwood Park, to place herself between Miss Fairfax and Mr. Lucas, and to engage that gentleman in a conversation of such length and variety upon the subject of agricultural improvement, that he had no opportunity of addressing a single word to the governess throughout the remainder of the visit. She observed, with great satisfaction, that Miss Fairfax appeared perfectly unconcerned by this arrangement, and applied herself to her work with the utmost composure; but this she attributed to a prudent concealment of her disappointment.
