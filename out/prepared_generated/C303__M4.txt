The fog off the Thames had a way of swallowing sound, and on the evening of November the fifth, 1816, it seemed to swallow the very last of Edmund Ashworth’s hopes. He stood in the shadow of the Pantheon’s colonnade on Oxford Street, the great edifice looming behind him like a tombstone, and watched the slight, stooped figure of Mr. Silas Craddock disappear into the yellow gloom. The critic’s final words, delivered with a weary, almost pitying smile, still rang in his ears: “You have a facility with language, Mr. Ashworth, that much is true. But a facility is not a fire. Your verses are correct, they are even pretty, but they are the verses of a clever mimic. There is no true feeling in them, no necessity. They have no merit.”

Edmund had clutched the sheaf of his manuscript—his life’s work, or so it had felt for the past five years—so tightly that his knuckles were white. He had wanted to argue, to quote the passages he felt were his most profound, his most original. But under the critic’s cool, analytical gaze, the words had shrivelled to ash in his mouth. He had bowed, muttered his thanks, and fled into the street.

He walked for what felt like hours, the damp cold seeping through his thin coat. He did not go home to the modest house in Clerkenwell where he lived with his sister, Clara. He could not face her hopeful, questioning eyes. She was the only one who believed in his genius, who copied his poems into a neat folio with such reverence, who spoke of his future fame as if it were a settled fact. To tell her of Craddock’s verdict would be to break something sacred. Instead, he found himself drawn back to the cramped, rented room he called his study—a space he had filled with books, with paper, with the detritus of a poet’s ambition.

The fire was still smouldering in the grate. Edmund stood before it, the manuscript in his hand. He looked at the title page, ‘Poems by Edmund Ashworth’, written in his own careful hand. He thought of the hours he had spent over each line, agonising over a single word, a subtle cadence. He thought of the poets he loved—Wordsworth, Coleridge, Byron—and the fiery, undeniable truth of their work. And he saw, with a clarity that was both terrifying and liberating, that Craddock was right. It was all imitation. It was all vanity. He was not a poet. He was a fraud.

With a cry that was half-sob, half-curse, he thrust the manuscript into the flames. The paper caught eagerly, the edges curling and blackening, the ink blistering and vanishing. He fed the fire with his notebooks, his drafts, his letters to editors, every scrap of verse he possessed. The flames roared, casting his shadow, huge and distorted, upon the wall. He watched until the last page was consumed, a fragile black ghost that drifted up the chimney. When it was done, he felt a strange, hollow peace. The poet Edmund Ashworth was dead. In his place stood a man who would deal only in facts, in certainties, in the measurable world.

The next morning, he went to his sister. Clara was in the small parlour, arranging a vase of hollyhocks. She turned as he entered, her face lighting up. “Edmund! You are back early. What did Mr. Craddock say? Is it to be published?”

He could not meet her eyes. He looked at a point on the wall just past her shoulder. “Craddock is a fool,” he said, his voice flat and strange. “And I am a greater one. It is finished, Clara. The poetry. It is all nonsense. I have burned it. All of it.”

Clara’s face went pale. The hollyhocks slipped from her fingers and scattered on the floor. “Burned? Edmund, what do you mean? Your beautiful poems…”

“They were not beautiful,” he cut in, his tone harsh. “They were the prattling of a vain boy. I am done with it. I am going to study chemistry. At the Royal Institution. I have already written to inquire about lectures. I shall be a man of science.”

He saw the hurt in her eyes, the bewilderment, and it only hardened his resolve. He could not bear her pity. He turned and left the room, leaving her standing amidst the fallen flowers.

Clara did not speak to him of poetry again. She watched him, however, with a quiet, watchful sorrow. She saw him throw himself into his new pursuit with a fervour that bordered on the manic. He rose before dawn to read treatises on pneumatic chemistry, he attended lectures by the great men of the day, he filled notebooks with diagrams of retorts and equations. He was, by all accounts, a gifted student, his sharp mind adapting easily to the rigours of empirical inquiry. Yet, Clara noticed, he never seemed happy. The light that had once animated his face when he spoke of a new metaphor was gone, replaced by a grim, determined concentration.

What Edmund did not know, what he had never suspected, was that Clara had a secret. For years, whenever he had given her a poem to read, she had not only copied it into her folio but had also kept the original drafts. She had a small, locked chest beneath her bed, and in it lay every piece of paper he had ever scribbled a verse upon, from his first clumsy attempts at age fourteen to the polished sonnets he had presented to Craddock. She had not done it out of a desire to deceive him, but from a deep, instinctive reverence for the act of creation. She believed, with a sister’s unwavering faith, that his work was precious, and that one day, when he was famous, these early drafts would be treasures. When he had burned his manuscripts, she had felt a terrible pang of guilt, as if she were a thief. But she could not bring herself to tell him. She could not add the confession of her own small betrayal to the crushing weight of his disappointment. She simply kept her silence, and her chest.
