The trouble with the world, Arthur Pomeroy was beginning to realize, is that it will not stand still for an experiment. He had set out to do a simple and noble thing: to feed humanity. He had taken the most stubborn, poverty-stricken patch of the Burnt Fen he could find—a grey, saline expanse that had refused to yield a decent crop since the Romans left—and he had, through the patient and methodical crossing of a particularly hardy Levantine durum with a native Norfolk red, produced a wheat he had named *Triticum Pertinax*. The Pertinax Wheat. It was a triumph of botanical science. It grew where nothing else would grow. It laughed at salt. It thrived in dust. It sent its roots down with the grim tenacity of a prize-fighter.

The first reports from the experimental farm at Haversham Grange were ecstatic. Yields from the barren plot were, by any sane agricultural standard, impossible. Pomeroy had written a paper for the Royal Agricultural Society. He had given a lecture in Norwich, where he had been met with polite, uncomprehending applause from men in tweed who thought science was a fine thing, so long as it didn't interfere with the price of barley. He had felt a quiet, superior satisfaction. He was not a man for grand gestures, but for the slow, inexorable march of fact.

The first sign that the fact was marching in the wrong direction came not from a laboratory, but from a muddy lane. It was old Silas Croft, a man whose face was a testament to a lifetime of squinting at the Norfolk sky, who brought the news. He stood in the doorway of the Grange, twisting his cap.

“It’s your wheat, Dr. Pomeroy,” he said, his voice flat. “It’s in my turnips.”

Pomeroy, who was in the middle of calibrating a soil hygrometer, looked up with mild irritation. “I beg your pardon?”

“Your wheat. The one you grew in the salt patch. It’s got into my top field. Choking the life out of the turnips. And the swedes. And anything else that’s there.”

Pomeroy set down his instrument. “That’s impossible, Mr. Croft. The plot is enclosed. The seed is self-contained. There is no mechanism for—”

“Mechanism or not, it’s there,” Croft said, with the unshakeable certainty of a man who knows his own land. “Come and see.”

And see he did. The field was a scene of slow-motion violence. The neat rows of turnips were being invaded by a pale green, wiry stalk of wheat, growing with a frantic, competitive energy. It was not merely growing alongside the other crops; it was weaving through them, crowding them, stealing the light and the moisture with an almost malevolent efficiency. The *Triticum Pertinax* was not just pertinacious; it was imperial.

The meeting at the village hall in Little Walsingham was not a pleasant one. Pomeroy stood before a sea of hostile faces, illuminated by the unflattering glow of two oil lamps. These were not the tweed-wearing gentry from Norwich, but the farmers themselves, men whose livelihoods were literally in the ground. At their head was Thomas Blackwood, a large man with a prosperous belly and the shrewd, suspicious eyes of a born horse-dealer.

“You tell us, Doctor,” Blackwood began, his voice a low rumble of accusation, “that this… this weed of yours was grown for poor soil. Well, it seems to have a taste for good soil as well. And my neighbour’s good soil. And his neighbour’s. It’s spreading like a plague.”

“It is not a weed, Mr. Blackwood,” Pomeroy said, his voice tight with scientific indignation. “It is a highly developed cultivar. Its vigour is its chief virtue. Its root system is designed to seek out moisture and nutrients in the most inhospitable substrates. It is simply… expressing its genetic imperative.”

A farmer at the back, a small, wiry man named Hemsworth, let out a bitter laugh. “Genetic imperative! It’s got into my prize marrowfats! I’ll show you a genetic imperative when I sue you for every penny you’ve got!”

The room erupted. Pomeroy tried to explain about wind-pollination, about the sheer volume of pollen produced by the experimental plot, about the unforeseen cross-pollination with the common *Triticum aestivum* in the surrounding fields, creating a monstrous, fertile hybrid. He tried to talk of rogue genes and dominant traits. It was like trying to explain calculus to a goat. They did not want to know about genes. They wanted to know who was going to pay for their ruined turnips.

“This is not a matter for sentiment, gentlemen!” Pomeroy shouted, his voice cracking. “This is a biological event! We are witnessing a new form of life, a species uniquely adapted to the challenges of the modern agricultural landscape! We must study it! We must harness it!”

“Harness it?” Blackwood roared, slamming a heavy fist on the trestle table. “It’s harnessing us! It’s in the hedgerows! It’s in the ditches! I found it sprouting in my own yard, by the water-butt! You’ve let a monster loose in Norfolk, and all you can talk about is your ‘genetic imperative’! I want to know your legal imperative!”

Pomeroy looked from one furious face to the next. He saw no curiosity, no wonder at the magnificent, terrible vitality of his creation. He saw only fear and anger. He saw men whose world was being invaded by a force they could not comprehend and would not respect. He had thought he was bringing them a gift, a key to unlock the bounty of their poorest lands. Instead, he had handed them a plague.

Walking back to the Grange under a sky full of indifferent stars, Pomeroy felt a profound and chilling isolation. The wind rustled in the hedges, and he could have sworn he heard in it the dry, sibilant whisper of a million stalks of Pertinax Wheat, a sound that was no longer the promise of abundance, but the quiet, patient sound of a conqueror. He had wanted to feed the world. It seemed, instead, he had merely taught it how to starve everything else.
