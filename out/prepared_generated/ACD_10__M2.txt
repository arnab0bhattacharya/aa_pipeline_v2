The rain lashed against the windows of the cramped consulting rooms on Baker Street, turning the gaslight into a hazy, amber glow that did little to dispel the chill of the November afternoon. Mr. Alistair Thorne sat hunched in the wing-back chair, his fingers twisting the brim of his silk hat until the fabric had nearly unraveled. He was a man of middle years, with a face that had once been handsome but had since been etched by anxiety into a landscape of deep furrows.

Sherlock Holmes stood by the fireplace, his long, lean form silhouetted against the dying embers. He held a small, leather-bound book in one hand, turning its pages with the casual precision of a man examining a rare insect.

“It is a peculiar case, Mr. Holmes,” Thorne said, his voice trembling slightly. “I am a man of exactitude. My ledgers are kept to the fraction of an inch. Yet, for the past fortnight, I have discovered that my most private measurements have been altered, or rather, copied. Not stolen, you understand, but replicated in such a way that the thief knew precisely where my clients kept their secrets.”

Holmes did not look up from the book. “Secrets, you say?”

“Jewels, Mr. Holmes. Pearls, sapphires, emeralds. My clients, women of the highest social standing, are accustomed to keeping their most valuable ornaments concealed within the linings of their gowns or tucked into the folds of their bodices. They trust me with these treasures while I alter the silk. It is a service I have provided for twenty years. But now, at three separate private balls, these women were robbed. The thieves knew exactly where to look. They did not rummage; they simply reached into the specific pocket or lining where the jewel lay.”

Holmes closed the book with a soft thud. He turned, his eyes bright and sharp, fixing upon the dressmaker with an intensity that made Thorne shrink back.

“Three victims,” Holmes mused. “Three balls. And you are convinced it is not a random act of petty theft?”

“Randomness does not know the difference between a sash and a pocket,” Thorne replied, his chin lifting in a spurt of indignation. “These thieves were surgical. If they had been common pickpockets, they would have taken the first thing they found. But they took only what I had recorded as being hidden in the left side-seam of the gown, or the inner pleat of the skirt. How could they know? The measurements are in my private journal, which is locked in my desk. I have not spoken of the locations to a soul.”

“Who else has access to your journal?” Holmes asked, his voice dropping to that conversational, inquiring tone that always put my nerves on edge.

“My assistant, Mr. Pemberton. And occasionally, my bookkeeper, Mr. Grimes. But Pemberton is a quiet man. He has worked for me for a decade. He is deaf in one ear and never speaks above a whisper. He is the last man I would suspect.”

Holmes nodded slowly. “And Grimes? Is he a man of means?”

“Hardly. He lives in a garret in Soho. He is a sober, dull man. He handles only the accounts. Why would he rob my clients? He could not even guess which of them wore jewels.”

“Then we must look beyond the obvious,” Holmes said, walking over to the window. He drew back the curtain and peered out at the wet, slushy street below. “You say the thefts occurred at private balls. Did the victims know each other?”

“Mostly, yes. They are all patrons of the opera. They move in the same circles. But the robberies were not simultaneous. They happened on three different nights, in three different townhouses in Belgravia.”

“And the thieves?”

“Gloved. Faceless. They came in with the other guests, or perhaps slipped in through the servants’ entrances. No one saw them. It was as if the air simply took the jewels and vanished.”

Holmes turned from the window. “You bring this to me not merely to solve the crime, but to save your reputation, I presume. If it were known that your books were compromised, your clients would flee. You would be ruined.”

“It is my honor as well as my livelihood,” Thorne said, his voice dropping to a whisper. “I would sooner face a trial for theft than a rumor of negligence.”

“Then we must find the thief before the next ball,” Holmes said. “When is it?”

“Saturday evening. The Duchess of Argyll is hosting a reception. Half my clients will be there. If the thief strikes again, it will be public knowledge.”

Holmes tapped his fingers against his temple, a habit that always preceded a burst of insight. “Pemberton, the assistant. Deaf in one ear. Quiet. Discreet. You trust him implicitly.”

“Yes, Mr. Holmes.”

“And Grimes, the bookkeeper. Dull. Poor. Soho. You dismiss him as incapable.”

“I do not dismiss him as incapable of theft. I dismiss him as incapable of *knowing*.”

“Ah,” Holmes said, a faint smile playing on his lips. “But who tells Grimes where to look? Who translates the language of inches and yards into the language of jewels? You say your journal is locked. But is the lock truly secure? Or is it merely a formality?”

Thorne frowned. “I use a good lock. A Yale.”

“Yale locks can be picked by a man with a hairpin and a steady hand. But why would Grimes, who lives in a garret, need to pick it? Unless he has already been given the key, or the information has been passed to him by someone who has access.”

Holmes walked to the mantelpiece and picked up a small magnifying glass. “Mr. Thorne, I must ask you a question that may be unpleasant. Have you ever considered that the thief is not an outsider, but someone within your own household who has turned their skills to crime? Not for money, but for the thrill of the puzzle? Or perhaps for a specific target?”
