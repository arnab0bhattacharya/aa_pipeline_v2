The drawing-room of Lady Marchmont’s house in Mayfair was a symphony in ivory and old gold, and the air was heavy with the scent of hothouse lilies and the more delicate perfume of flattery. It was a room designed for the sort of conversation that is never quite sincere and always quite delightful, where epigrams were exchanged like counters in a game no one could lose, and where the truth, if it ever dared to enter, would have been immediately shown the door by a footman.

Into this glittering aviary of fashionable birds stepped the Reverend Algernon Pryce, a young curate from a small parish in Cumberland, whose presence was due entirely to a mistake. His hostess, a woman of magnificent certainties and splendid ignorance, had seized his hand with a cry of joy. “My dear Mr. Pryce! We had quite given you up for lost in the interior! I told Marchmont only this afternoon that I was sure you had been devoured by a cannibal or, worse still, married by a native princess. And here you are, looking positively civilised! You must tell us everything.”

The Reverend Mr. Pryce, whose chief adventure in life had been the acquisition of a rather fine collection of pressed ferns, and whose knowledge of the interior was confined to the interior of his own modest lodgings, found himself propelled into the centre of a circle of expectant faces. He was a young man of a shy and studious disposition, with a pale, sensitive face and hands that were perpetually finding their way into his pockets or twisting themselves into knots of embarrassment. He opened his mouth to explain the error, to confess that he was not the celebrated explorer, Sir Peregrine Pryce, but only his obscure and unadventurous cousin, when a lady in a dress of peacock-blue velvet, who had been introduced to him as Mrs. Delamere, fixed him with a gaze of thrilling intensity.

“Do not spare us the horrors, Mr. Pryce,” she commanded, her voice a silken whisper. “We are all of us dreadfully tired of civilisation. Tell us of the savagery. Tell us of the beasts.”

And so the Reverend Algernon Pryce, who had never in his life told a lie more serious than a mild exaggeration concerning the length of his morning sermon, discovered a new and terrible talent. Looking at the eager, painted faces around him, he felt a strange and sudden impulse, a desire to be for one evening the man they thought he was. He cleared his throat, and when he spoke, his voice was not his own, but that of a man accustomed to command and to be heard above the roar of lions.

“The beasts,” he said, with a faraway look in his pale blue eyes, “are, on the whole, less terrible than the men. It is the solitude that tries the soul. There are days in the great forest, when the silence is so profound that you can hear the beating of your own heart, and you fancy it is the drum of a savage war-party. You learn to distrust the silence, you know. It is in the silence that the leopard creeps up, and the serpent uncoils.”

He paused, and the silence in the drawing-room was so complete that one could, indeed, hear the beating of several hearts, though they were not those of savage war-parties.

“I remember,” he continued, his voice taking on a dreamy, cadenced quality, as though he were reciting a poem, “one night on the banks of the great grey river, the one the natives call the ‘River of the Moon.’ My bearer, a faithful fellow named—er—Zambo, had succumbed to a fever. I was alone. The moon was a great, pale lantern in the sky, and the jungle was alive with eyes. I had my revolver, of course, but what is a revolver against the vast, indifferent eyes of the jungle? I sat by my fire, and I confess to you, Mrs. Delamere, that I felt a fear such as no man should ever know. It was not the fear of death, you understand, but the fear of being forgotten. To die there, in that immensity of green, to be erased from the book of life as though one had never been—that, I think, is the true terror.”

He had, quite unwittingly, described the sensation of being overlooked for a minor ecclesiastical preferment, but his audience was transported. Mrs. Delamere fanned herself with a languid hand. “And the natives?” she breathed. “Are they as treacherous as they are painted?”

“They are children,” said the curate, with a wave of his hand that was meant to be negligent but which, owing to a slight stiffness in his shoulder, was merely awkward. “Children of a splendid and terrible antiquity. They have a saying, which I have often pondered: ‘The man who chases two rabbits catches neither, but the man who chases the moon catches a shadow.’ It is a philosophy, you see, a whole philosophy of life, and it is more profound than any we have in Mayfair.”

A young man with a face like a rather clever and rather wicked cherub, who had been introduced as Lord Henry Wotton’s cousin, leaned forward. “And what of the great white hunter, Sir Peregrine?” he asked, with a hint of a smile. “Is he truly as fearless as they say?”

The Reverend Mr. Pryce felt a cold shiver run down his spine. He was being asked to impersonate himself. “Sir Peregrine,” he said, and a strange, sad smile played about his lips, “is a man I have known intimately for many years. He is fearless, it is true, but his fearlessness is not the absence of fear; it is the conquest of it. He once told me—and I have never forgotten it—that the bravest man is not he who feels no fear, but he who, feeling it, saddles his horse and rides on. He is a man of action, you see, and I—I am merely a man of observation.”
