The morning fog in Clapham did not merely hang in the air; it crawled, it clung, and it conspired with the damp stones to render the very soul of the district sodden and melancholy. It was a mist that seemed to have been woven from the collective sighs of a thousand brooms, a thousand leaking gutters, and a thousand hearts broken by the sheer, unadulterated banality of existence. Through this grey, clinging shroud, Miss Elara Vane walked with a step that had lost its former spring, her boots striking the pavement with a heavy, deliberate thud that seemed to ask the very cobblestones for their opinion on the matter. She was a young woman of twenty-two, possessed of a complexion that had once been the color of cream and rose, but which now, under the influence of anxiety and the London weather, had acquired a pallor more akin to old parchment. Her eyes, usually bright with the sparkle of idle amusement, were now fixed upon the muddy ground with a sort of tragic intensity, as if she expected to find, buried in the slush, the missing ledgers of her late uncle’s fortune.

For, dear reader, let us not mince matters here. Uncle Barnaby, that benevolent, portly, and profoundly mistaken gentleman who had been Elara’s guardian, had vanished from the face of prosperity as completely as a candle flame in a gale. The speculation in the Great Northern and Eastern Railway Company—a name that sounded, in those days, like a promise of gold and glory—had turned out to be a promise of ruin and shame. The shares had not risen; they had not even stayed still. They had plummeted, like stones cast into a well, until the well was full and the stones were buried under the weight of other men’s debts. The villa in Kensington, with its crystal chandeliers and its carpets thick enough to swallow a mouse whole, was gone. The servants, those silent, judgmental sentinels of the wealthy, had departed with such alacrity that they seemed to have been propelled by invisible springs. And Elara, left alone with a trunk of clothes and a heart full of indignation at the cruelty of finance, found herself a stranger in the land of her birth, compelled to seek employment as if she were a beggar asking for a crust of bread, rather than a lady seeking a place to lay down her burden.

She turned the corner into a narrow street where the houses leaned together like conspirators, their windows dark and shuttered, their facades stained by the soot of industry. At the end of this lane stood a building of such modest and unpretentious dimensions that one might have mistaken it for a large, well-kept shed. It was the residence of Mrs. Prudence Quill, a woman of formidable intellect and even more formidable temper, who had once governed the children of a baronet and now governed the souls of a dozen orphans and a few destitute boarders in a small schoolroom of her own.

Elara approached the door, her hand trembling slightly as it reached for the knocker. It was a brass beast, shaped like a lion, but worn smooth by the fingers of many anxious souls. She knocked. The sound was a dull thud, a death-knell to her last shred of dignity. The door opened not with a bang, but with a slow, creaking groan, revealing Mrs. Quill herself.

Mrs. Quill was a woman of middle years, dry as a winter leaf and twice as brittle. Her hair was pinned up in a fashion that suggested she had been arguing with it for decades and had lost. She wore a dress of dark blue, buttoned to the throat, and her face was a map of lines, each one a furrow ploughed by the ploughshare of disappointment. She looked at Elara, not with the warmth of a friend, but with the calculating gaze of an auditor inspecting a ledger.

“Miss Vane?” Mrs. Quill said, her voice thin and reedy, like the sound of a reed pipe played in a drafty room.

“Yes, ma’am,” Elara replied, her voice barely above a whisper. “I have come to inquire about the position.”

Mrs. Quill did not invite her in. She stood in the doorway, blocking the entrance to the dark, smell-of-chalk-and-ink hallway. “You know my terms, Miss Vane. I told you so, when I last saw you at the house. The salary is five pounds a month. No board, unless you can manage to live on the crumbs of the children’s lunch. The hours are from seven in the morning until six in the evening, with Sundays reserved for my private devotions, which you are expected to attend if you wish to retain my favor.”

Elara felt a chill that had nothing to do with the fog. Five pounds. A pittance. A mockery. It was less than the cost of the lace handkerchief her uncle had once thrown away because the pattern was no longer in fashion. Yet, here she stood, shivering in the damp, with no other refuge. The alternative was the workhouse, or worse, the street.

“And the duties?” Elara asked, forcing her voice to remain steady, though her knees felt as if they had been turned to water.

“Reading, writing, arithmetic, and the suppression of idle chatter,” Mrs. Quill said, her eyes narrowing. “My girls are clever, but they are wild. They require a hand of iron, wrapped in a glove of velvet. You, I am told, have a good education. But education is a poor master if one has no discipline. Can you manage the discipline, Miss Vane? Or will you find it too difficult for your delicate sensibilities?”
