The heat of that coast is a thing one does not forget, a heavy, pervasive exhalation of the earth itself, laden with the scent of mud and decaying vegetation, that hangs in the air long after the sun has sunk into the Pacific and the nightly chorus of insects has begun its monotonous, insistent litany. It was in the third year of my residence in Santa Cruz de la Mar, where I conducted a small academy for the daughters of the more respectable English and Creole families, that the matter of which I must now speak began to assume a shape, a form, a sort of brooding presence in my life, like the shadow of a cloud that will not pass.

My school was a modest establishment, a long, low house of whitewashed adobe with a patio where a single, ancient lime tree cast a circle of shade that was precious beyond rubies in the afternoons. We taught the rudiments—reading, writing, a little French, the history of a distant and, to my mind, ever more mythical England. My pupils were docile, their parents polite, and my life had settled into a routine as regular as the tolling of the cathedral bell, which marked the hours with a somnolent and slightly mournful note. I was, I suppose, a fixture, a piece of the landscape, as unremarkable and as necessary as the English consulate or the customs house. I was Miss Amelia Wainwright, and I was at peace.

This peace—a fragile thing, as I was to learn, a mere film upon the surface of a dark and troubled water—was first disturbed by a letter, brought to my door by a boy whose bare feet were caked with the grey dust of the upper town. The paper was of a coarse, yellowish texture, and the hand, though formal, was that of a man unaccustomed to the pen. It was signed by one General Ignacio Vargas, a name that was spoken in the port with a variety of inflections, ranging from the reverent to the hissed and fearful. He was the leader of the revolution—the *revolución*, as the posters on the walls had it—that had been smouldering in the interior for the better part of a year, a distant and bloody affair of which we in the town spoke in lowered voices, as one speaks of a fire in a neighbouring province, never quite believing that the wind might not shift.

The General’s request was simple, and couched in terms of a grave and almost archaic courtesy. He desired that his daughter, a girl of fourteen named Sofia, be received into my school. He wrote of his earnest wish that she should acquire the accomplishments of an English gentlewoman, that she might be fitted for a world he felt was changing, a world in which the old ways of blood and iron were, perhaps, to be superseded. He would, he added, consider it a personal favour, and he trusted to my well-known sense of justice and impartiality. The letter concluded with a flourish of loyalty to the ideals of civilization and progress.

I read it standing in the shade of the lime tree, and I confess that a certain vanity, a fluttering sense of my own importance, was stirred in my breast. To be sought out thus, by the most powerful and feared man in the province, for the education of his child! It seemed a vindication of my quiet labours, a recognition that the light of learning I tended was not so feeble after all. I thought of the girl, a creature of the wild hills, no doubt, and felt a missionary’s zeal to smooth her rough edges, to teach her the verbs and the manners, to bring her, in short, into the fold. I replied that I would be honoured to receive the Senorita Sofia Vargas.

The child arrived a week later, in the company of a silent, leather-faced woman who might have been her nurse or her jailer. She was not what I had expected. There was no savagery in her, no wildness. She was a thin, grave girl with eyes that seemed to hold a great and ancient stillness, like pools in a deep forest. She spoke little, but she watched everything with a quiet, unnerving intensity. She was neat, obedient, and learned with a rapidity that was almost disquieting. She was, in a word, a lady, and I soon felt that my task was less to instruct her than to civilize her according to a pattern she had already, in her heart, rejected.

The town, however, did not see it so. The town saw only that the daughter of General Vargas, the man whose name was a byword for rapine and sudden death, was a guest under my roof. The atmosphere in my little academy changed. The mothers of my pupils, who had hitherto been all smiles and compliments, now met me with averted eyes and lips compressed into a thin, disapproving line. Their daughters began to stay away, one by one, with excuses of illness or distant journeys. The patio, which had once rung with the chatter of young voices, grew silent, save for the dry rustle of the lime leaves and the distant, mocking cry of a street vendor.

One afternoon, Don Rodrigo Alvear, a portly, pompous man who was the chief magistrate of the town and a pillar of the government party, called upon me. He sat in my parlour, fanning himself with his hat, and spoke of the dangers of the time, of the regrettable necessity of maintaining a position of strict neutrality. “You are an Englishwoman, Miss Wainwright,” he said, his small eyes darting about the room, “and you are respected. But you must understand that in a time of civil strife, there is no such thing as a neutral ground. There is only one side or the other. The friends of order in this town are watching. They are watching you.”
