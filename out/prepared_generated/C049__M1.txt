The fever had taken the sailor by the throat and would not let go. In the cramped, steam-filled ward of the Royal Seamen’s Hospital in Portsmouth, Thomas Vance thrashed on his cot, a wraith in the ghost-ship of his own body. The chaplain, a gentle man named Mr. Finch, sat by his side, mopping his brow with a damp cloth. The air smelled of carbolic, sweat, and the looming, salt-edged finality of the sea. For hours, Vance had spoken in fragments, a mad collage of squalls and foreign ports, of a woman with eyes like the deep and a captain with a heart of stone. Then, as the grey light of a November dawn began to filter through the high, barred windows, his rambling coalesced into a single, burning point.

He clutched Mr. Finch’s sleeve with a grip of surprising strength. His eyes, wide and yellowed, fixed on the chaplain’s face. “A debt,” he rasped, his voice like stones grinding together. “A debt owed. Must be paid. Alderman Silas Croft.” He spat the name as if it were poison. “Ashworth. In the Midlands. He knows. The token… make him see.”

From beneath his pillow, he produced a small object and pressed it into the chaplain’s palm. It was a token carved from ivory, no bigger than a shilling. On one side was the intricate image of a three-masted ship in full sail. On the other, a coiled sea-serpent. It was smooth and worn, heavy with the unspoken weight of its history. Before Mr. Finch could ask another question, the light in the sailor’s eyes went out. Thomas Vance was gone, claimed by the landlocked sea of fever, leaving only a name and a carved piece of bone.

Mr. Finch, a man of principle and a quiet sense of duty, felt the weight of that token in his pocket for a week. A debt. It was a worldly matter, yet it felt like a spiritual one. A promise had been broken, a life’s final currency was this small, carved promise. He wrote to the town clerk in Ashworth, a place he had never heard of, and learned that an Alderman Silas Croft was indeed a prominent figure there, a prosperous wool merchant and a pillar of the community. Mr. Finch, taking a small stipend from the hospital’s poor box for the journey, travelled by train to the heart of England, the ivory token wrapped in a handkerchief in his breast pocket.

Ashworth was a town of soot-darkened stone and the ceaseless, rhythmic clatter of its mills. It was a place of industry and order, a stark contrast to the chaotic port he had left. Alderman Croft’s residence was a large, imposing house set back from the cobbled street, its windows gleaming. The man who greeted Mr. Finch in a cold, book-lined study was a monument to prosperity. He was stout, with a carefully tended grey beard and eyes that assessed and dismissed in the same instant. Mr. Finch explained his errand carefully, speaking of a sailor named Thomas Vance and a debt of honour. As he spoke, he watched the Alderman’s face. It remained a mask of polite bewilderment.

“A debt?” Croft said, his voice smooth and resonant. “From a sailor? My good man, my business is wool. I have dealings with merchants in Manchester and London, not with common seamen in Portsmouth. I have never heard of this Thomas Vance. There must be some mistake.” He offered a thin, condescending smile. “It is a common name, I’m sure.”

“Perhaps this will clarify matters,” Mr. Finch said, and placed the ivory token on the polished mahogany of the desk.

He saw it then. A flicker. It was not recognition, not exactly. It was a sudden, sharp tightening around the Alderman’s eyes, a momentary stillness in the hand that rested on the arm of his chair. It was the look of a man who has been shown a ghost. The mask was back in place in an instant, but the crack had been seen.

“A pretty trinket,” Croft said, his voice a fraction too loud. “But I fail to see its relevance. I am sorry you have had a wasted journey, Reverend. I trust the hospital can spare the expense.”

The dismissal was absolute. Mr. Finch left the house feeling not like a man of God, but like a supplicant who had been turned away from the gate. He knew, with a certainty that defied evidence, that Silas Croft was lying. And he knew something else. Beneath the bluster and the affronted dignity, the Alderman was afraid.

Back in Portsmouth, Mr. Finch confided in a friend, a retired Inspector of Police named Davies, who had an interest in such puzzles. Davies, a man with a face like a worn sea-chart and eyes that missed nothing, listened patiently. “A man like that doesn’t get frightened of a ghost from his past unless the ghost can still do him harm,” Davies mused. “And a debt of honour… that’s a heavy thing. Let me ask a few questions.”

Davies’s methods were subtler than Mr. Finch’s. He did not travel to Ashworth. Instead, he wrote letters, made inquiries, and spent an afternoon in the Portsmouth docks, talking to old hands and shipping agents. He learned that the ship on the token was almost certainly the *Sea Serpent*, a vessel that had made its final, ill-fated voyage fifteen years prior, disappearing with all hands off the Cape of Good Hope. The Alderman’s name did not appear on any passenger list. But Davies, through a contact at Lloyd’s, discovered something else. A young, ambitious Silas Croft had been a junior partner in a small shipping venture that owned the *Sea Serpent*. The venture had been underinsured. When the ship was lost, the firm was ruined. Croft, the junior partner, had somehow emerged with enough capital to begin his wool business. The senior partner, a man named Abernathy, had been utterly destroyed, dying in poverty a year later.
