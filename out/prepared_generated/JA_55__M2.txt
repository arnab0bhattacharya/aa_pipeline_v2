Mr. Prentice was not a man to be pitied, nor was he one to be feared, which was a rare distinction in the social orbit of Brighton, where the latter was usually the only currency that bought respectability. He possessed a fortune that was comfortable, a complexion that was pallid, and a mind that was entirely devoid of the spark which, in lesser men, might have been mistaken for wit. To observe him was to observe a well-kept but uninteresting garden; everything was in its place, the hedges were trimmed, the flowers were proper, but there was no scent, and certainly no danger.

Miss Elara Vance had arrived in Brighton with her aunt, Mrs. Gower, and her cousin, Miss Lavinia, with the specific intention of curing a mild indisposition of the nerves through the exercise of society and the consumption of sea air. Brighton, in the season of 1814, was a place of such intense activity and such profound vulgarity that it required a steady hand to navigate its promenades without appearing either too eager or too indifferent. The three ladies had secured a small lodging near the Chain Pier, from which they could observe the comings and goings of the fashionable world with the detached amusement of spectators at a play.

It was Mrs. Gower who first drew Mr. Prentice to their attention, not through any personal acquaintance, but through the more reliable medium of scandal. "He is the sort of man," Mrs. Gower declared one afternoon, as they took their tea on the balcony, "who has ruined two families in the last decade. Not by any act of violence, mind you, but by a persistence of attachment that borders on madness. He is said to be very serious, very dark, and very poor in spirit, though rich in coin. One must be on one’s guard. A man who feels too deeply is a man who will act too rashly."

Miss Elara, who had a natural inclination toward the calm and the predictable, did not feel herself in immediate danger from a man described as merely "serious." She had spent her life in the quiet counties of Dorset, where the greatest shock to the system was a delay in the post or a change in the weather. She had learned to judge a man by his steadiness, and found in Mr. Prentice’s reported character a certain rugged honesty that she secretly admired. Lavinia, on the other hand, was delighted by the prospect of a villain, for she believed that a story without a villain was like a dinner without salt.

The opportunity to test these theories presented itself sooner than anticipated. They were strolling along the promenade, the sea breeze whipping their skirts, when they encountered Mr. Prentice. He was walking alone, his hands clasped behind his back, his eyes fixed on some distant point on the horizon. He was a tall man, dressed in a sober black coat, with a face that was long and lean, and an expression of such profound melancholy that one might have mistaken him for a mourner at his own funeral.

He stopped to speak to them, not with the usual bow and flourish of the ton, but with a simple nod and a question regarding the state of the weather. "Is it likely to hold?" he asked, his voice low and steady, lacking the breathless excitement that Miss Lavinia expected.

"It is, I think," Miss Elara replied, "though the wind is picking up."

"And that," said Mr. Prentice, "is a mercy. I find the stillness of this town oppressive. There is too much noise in the mind when the air is quiet."

Miss Elara felt a sudden, inexplicable warmth toward him. He was not charming. He did not smile. He did not make a witty remark about the gulls or the architecture. He was, in fact, profoundly dull. His conversation was a series of dry, factual observations about the tides and the humidity, delivered with the monotone of a man reading a ledger. Yet, there was a kindness in his demeanor, a genuine concern for their comfort, that was entirely devoid of calculation. He offered them a handkerchief when the wind threatened to disturb Miss Elara’s hair, a gesture so chivalrous and so devoid of flirtatious intent that it was almost tedious.

As they parted, Mr. Prentice did not look back. He simply continued his walk, his head bowed, seemingly lost in the recesses of his own brooding mind.

"Well," said Lavinia, once they were out of earshot, "I expected him to be wild. I expected him to be dark and stormy, like a character in a novel. I did not expect him to be boring."

"He is kind," said Miss Elara, surprised by her own defense. "He is not unkind. That is more than can be said for many men of his station."

"Kind," said Mrs. Gower, with a sharp intake of breath. "Yes, kind. That is the most dangerous quality in a man of no principle. Kindness without wit is merely a mask for ignorance. You will find, my dear, that he is incapable of amusement. He will bore you to the point of despair. I warned you of his reputation. A man who is so serious is a man who will make you serious, and a woman cannot bear to be serious for long."

Miss Elara turned over the conversation in her mind as they walked back to their lodgings. She had anticipated a spark, a challenge, a moment of passion that would justify the caution her aunt had urged. She had prepared herself for a storm, and instead, she had found a gentle, persistent rain. It was not the dramatic downpour she had imagined, but a steady, grey drizzle that soaked through without ever becoming a flood.
