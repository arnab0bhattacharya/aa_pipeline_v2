The sea had stopped trying to kill them three days ago, and now it simply waited, a sheet of hammered brass stretching to a sky the colour of a sick man’s gums. The boat, a twenty-foot whaler’s cutter, held nine men and the ghost of a tenth. Captain Rennick lay in the stern sheets, his breathing a wet, rattling thing, his face the grey of old sailcloth. The wound in his thigh, taken from a falling spar when the *Andromeda* had struck the reef, had turned the colour of a bruised plum and smelled of something sweet and terrible.

Mr. Gorse, the mate, a lean man with a jaw like a hatchet and eyes that never stopped moving, sat forward, his hand on the tiller. He was watching the horizon to the nor’-east, where a smudge of green and grey promised land. It was the island of Pulau Kambing, and it was close. Perhaps fifteen miles. A day’s pull, maybe less with the current.

Bosun Crane, a bull of a man from Limehouse with a broken nose and forearms like knotted rope, sat amidships, his back to the mate. He was staring in the opposite direction, to the west, where the sky met the sea in an unbroken, hazy line. He was chewing on a strip of dried leather that had once been his belt.

“We can’t make Surabaya,” Crane said, not turning. His voice was a low growl, roughened by salt and silence. “It’s two hundred miles if it’s an inch. We’ve got no water but what’s in that cask, and the men are done. We’d be drinking our own piss by Tuesday and eating each other by Friday.”

“And Pulau Kambing is a death sentence,” Gorse replied, his voice tight as a fiddle string. He didn’t look at Crane, but kept his eyes fixed on the distant green. “You heard the same stories I did in Batavia. The Malays there don’t trade. They don’t parley. They take heads. The *Lively Lady* put a boat ashore there in ‘78 for water. Six men. Not a stitch of them was ever found.”

“Stories,” Crane spat, the leather strip jerking with his jaw. “I’ve heard stories about mermaids and sea serpents, too. What I see is land. Land means water. Means fruit. Means a chance. You want to row for a week on a promise and a prayer, you go ahead. I’ll take my chances with the heathen.”

A murmur went through the men. They were a rag-tag lot, their faces blistered and peeling, their eyes sunk deep in their skulls. They looked from Gorse to Crane as if they were watching a tennis match with their lives for the ball. There was Petersen, a big, fair-haired Swede who had been the sailmaker, and little Chipper, the cabin boy, whose twelfth birthday had passed unremarked two days ago. They all had their own fears, their own private calculations of hope and dread.

Gorse finally turned his head. “You’re a fool, Crane. A stubborn, bloody fool. You’d lead us to a slaughterhouse because you’re too damned scared to do the hard work of surviving.”

“And you’d lead us to a grave in the deep blue sea because you’re too damned proud to admit you might be wrong,” Crane shot back, finally twisting around. His face was a mask of grime and fury. “The captain ain’t deciding. He’s halfway to Fiddler’s Green already. So it’s you and me, Mr. Mate. And I say we go for the land we can see.”

“And I say we go for the port we know,” Gorse hissed. “Surabaya is a civilised place. There’s a harbour master, a doctor, a bloody church. Pulau Kambing is a rock full of savages who’ll have our heads on poles before we can say ‘good morning’.”

The captain stirred. A low, gurgling moan escaped his cracked lips. His eyes fluttered open, milky and unfocused. He seemed to be trying to speak, his jaw working soundlessly. Chipper, who had been detailed to sit with him, leaned close.

“What is it, Captain? What are you trying to say?”

Rennick’s hand, thin as a bird’s claw, lifted an inch from the tarpaulin. He pointed a trembling finger, not at the green island or the empty western horizon, but at the water cask. Then his hand fell, and his eyes closed again. The rattle in his chest grew louder for a moment, then subsided into the shallow, rapid panting of the dying.

The gesture was clear enough. Water. The captain’s last order was to conserve what little they had. But it decided nothing.

The argument resumed, hotter now. The men began to take sides. Petersen, the Swede, sided with the mate. He was a methodical man who believed in charts and known quantities. Little Chipper, terrified of the dark-skinned men he’d only heard of in whispers, clung to the mate’s words as if they were gospel. But most of the others, the hardened able seamen with tattoos on their knuckles and a lifetime of hardship etched into their faces, leaned towards the bosun. They were men of action, not navigation. Land was land. You could see it. You could smell it. The promise of a distant port was an abstraction, a cruel mirage.

“We’ll put it to a vote,” Crane declared, his voice ringing with authority. “All in favour of making for Pulau Kambing, raise your hand.”

Five hands went up, including Crane’s. Gorse’s stayed down, his knuckles white on the tiller. Chipper’s hand wavered, then shot up. Petersen kept his down, his big face set in a frown. That left three men unaccounted for, and the captain, who was past voting.

“It’s a tie,” Gorse said, a grim satisfaction in his voice. “Five to four.”

“Then the captain decides,” Crane said, gesturing towards the stern. “Ask him. Ask him which way to steer.”

It was a cruel joke. They all knew the captain was beyond speech, beyond reason. But Crane was a literal man. He wanted the rules followed, even if the rule-maker was insensible.
