Miss Arabella Vane was possessed of a conviction which, like a well-set stone in a ring, she turned over daily and found as unshaken as it was unbecoming. That her brother, a man of sense and a respectable, if not brilliant, income, should have been captured by the charms of Miss Clara Fitzwilliam was a source of profound and private vexation. Miss Fitzwilliam, she was persuaded, was a creature of calculation, her modesty a veil for ambition, and her affection a well-rehearsed performance for the benefit of a single, eligible audience. The marriage had been a disappointment to the neighbourhood, and to Miss Vane a calamity; for the new Mrs. Edward Vane, now mistress of Hartfield Park, brought with her nothing but a handsome face, a pleasing manner, and a family of consequence in name but not in fortune. Arabella was determined to expose the artifice she knew to be there, and she watched her new sister-in-law with the patient, unblinking attention of a cat at a mouse-hole.

The first weeks of the marriage provided a perfect stage for this observation. Mrs. Vane conducted herself with a grace that was, to Arabella’s suspicious eye, too perfect to be natural. She praised the house, which was indeed a fine one, with an air of genuine admiration that never once slipped into envy. She deferred to Arabella’s opinion on matters of the household with a sweetness that ought to have been irritating, yet was not. When Arabella, testing her, remarked upon the beauty of a neighbouring estate, known to be far greater in value than Hartfield, Mrs. Vane replied only that its grounds were considered very fine, and turned the conversation to the quality of the light in the drawing-room. It was a masterful evasion, and it confirmed everything Arabella suspected. A woman of true feeling would have shown some spark of ambition; a woman of no feeling would have been indifferent; but this perfect, unassailable propriety was the surest mark of a calculating mind, guarding its true intentions.

The situation was rendered more galling by the behaviour of the family lawyer, Mr. Davies, a man whose professional caution was mistaken by many for good sense. He was a frequent visitor to Hartfield, ostensibly to discuss the management of the estate, and he regarded the new Mrs. Vane with an approbation that bordered on the paternal. Arabella, seeing him one afternoon enjoying a dish of tea with her brother and sister-in-law, resolved to sound him out. She waited until Edward had been called from the room on some minor business, and then, with a look of studied concern, she addressed the lawyer.

“It is a great comfort,” she began, stirring her tea with a thoughtful air, “to see Edward so settled. I confess, however, that a sister’s anxiety is not so easily quieted. One always wishes to see a match founded upon the most disinterested principles.”

Mr. Davies, a man who valued precision, set down his cup. “Indeed, Miss Vane. And I can assure you, from my own observations, that Mrs. Vane’s conduct is a model of propriety. She has never once made any inquiry as to the particulars of her husband’s fortune, nor has she expressed a wish for any expenditure beyond what is perfectly reasonable. Her mind appears to be entirely occupied with his comfort and happiness.”

Arabella felt a grim triumph. “That is just what I mean! Such restraint is very admirable, of course. It is almost… professional, is it not? A little too perfect to be quite natural in a young lady of her years. It is as if she had been instructed in the art of appearing indifferent to the very thing she has so cleverly acquired.”

Mr. Davies looked perplexed. “I confess I do not follow you, ma’am. I should have said her indifference is the most genuine thing about her. It speaks to a delicacy of mind I find quite superior.”

“She is a superior actress,” Arabella replied, her voice low and certain. “And you, my dear Mr. Davies, like my poor brother, are but the audience for whom she performs.” The lawyer could only shake his head, convinced of the lady’s merit and privately lamenting the sharp, uncharitable turn of Miss Vane’s mind.

The proof, Arabella felt certain, would soon be forthcoming. Opportunity presented itself in the form of a grand ball at the house of Sir John Middleton, a gentleman whose fortune was as loud as his waistcoats. It was an event that would bring the whole county together, and where the pretensions of a fortune hunter would surely be tested. Arabella watched as Clara prepared for the evening. She chose a gown of simple white muslin, elegant but not ostentatious. She wore her mother’s pearls, which were handsome but not new. There was no attempt to outshine the daughters of wealthier men, no grasping after the sort of finery that would betray a vulgar soul. It was, Arabella had to admit, a perfectly judged performance.

At the ball itself, Mrs. Vane’s conduct was beyond reproach. She danced with her husband with an air of quiet happiness, spoke with kindness to the elderly and the plain, and showed no marked preference for the company of the richest men in the room. Arabella, observing from a seat against the wall, felt her certainty harden into a cold, bright certainty. The very absence of greed was the most damning evidence of all. This was not the simplicity of an innocent heart, but the profound cunning of a woman who understood that the greatest prize was already won, and that any further display of avarice would only serve to jeopardise it. Her indifference was not a virtue; it was a strategy.
