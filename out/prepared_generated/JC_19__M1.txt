It is a fact, sufficiently well known to those who move about the docks and the waterfront of Liverpool, that the Mersey is a river with a temper of its own, a sullen and unforgiving master to whom all must submit. To know its moods, its deep channels and its treacherous sandbanks, is a man’s whole life’s work. I had given it the best of mine. For thirty years my name, Elias Thorne, was a guarantee of a safe passage, and my judgment, which is to say my instinct, for the one is but the other refined by experience, was held in some account by the shipmasters and merchants of the port. I knew the river as a man knows the lines upon his own hand; I could feel a change in the current through the soles of my boots, and the colour of the water, the very smell of the air on a given morning, would tell me more than a dozen barometers.

There was one occasion, some fifteen years ago, which is the matter of this record. A great storm, they said, was blowing in from the Irish Sea, and the sky over the town had that bruised, yellowish look that comes before a tempest. The wind was already a fretful, keening thing in the rigging of the ships in the basin. A new steamer, the *Cassandra*, of the Blackwood Line, was due to sail on the evening tide. Her master, a nervous man with a red face and a sudden temper, was for going out, but I, who had taken her from the bar up to the quay that very morning, knew the set of the water and the weight of the wind behind it. I told him, in as few words as I could find, that to take the *Cassandra* out on that tide was to court disaster.

It was then that I first laid eyes on Mr. Abernathy. He was the owner’s agent, a man of a lean and hungry appearance, with eyes that seemed to see not the thing before him but the profit to be got from it. He stood upon the quay, his coat collar turned up against the rising gale, and he spoke to me not as a man speaks to a fellow creature, but as a master speaks to a faulty machine. He spoke of schedules, of penalties, of the faith that must be kept with the insurers. He did not speak of the sea. I remember the words I used, for they came from a place deep within me, a place where the knowledge of the water resides. “Sir,” I said, “the sea does not keep to your schedules. It is not a thing to be commanded by a contract.” He looked at me then with a coldness that was worse than any anger, and said that my refusal would be noted. I did not doubt it. The *Cassandra* did not sail that night. She sailed two days later, under a fair sky, and I took her over the bar myself. Mr. Abernathy was not there to see it.

The years passed, as they must. The incident, so vital to me at the time, became a story I did not tell, a private weight that settled in my memory. I saw Mr. Abernathy’s name in the papers, of course. It rose, as such names do, from the shipping news to the social columns, from the docks to the boardrooms. He became a man of importance, a director of this and a trustee of that, until at last the day came when he was made Chairman of the Harbour Board, the very body that licenses the pilots and governs the port. It was a grim irony, the sort that life plays upon a man, and I felt it as a change in the air, a drop in the barometer of my own fortunes. I was older then, my hands not so steady on the wheel, my eyes needing the glass more often to read the buoys in a haze.

The summons came on a Tuesday, a dry, formal document on heavy paper. I was to attend the Board to explain a certain delay. It was a small matter, a half-hour lost in the fog with a tramp steamer, the *Sea-Lark*, whose master had insisted on a speed that was not prudent. I had held her back until the Hornby Light was clear, and for this I was to be called to account. It seemed a great fuss for a little thing, and I knew, with the old pilot’s certainty, that the fog was not on the river that day, but in the mind of Mr. Abernathy.

The Board Room was a place of polished mahogany and long windows that looked out over the grey, heaving water of the river. The air inside was still and smelled of beeswax and old paper. The members sat at a long table, a row of solemn, well-fed faces, and at their head sat Mr. Abernathy. He was changed, of course; the lean hunger had been fleshed out into a solid prosperity, his hair was white, but the eyes were the same. They were the eyes of a man who has never looked at the sea, but only at the ledger-book.

The hearing was a strange and painful affair. They questioned me about the *Sea-Lark*, about the currents, the visibility, the master’s words. I answered as best I could, my voice sounding rough and foreign in that quiet room. I tried to explain the feel of the water that day, the way the fog lay in patches, the sense of a great, unseen danger lurking just beyond the bow. But my words were of no use there. They spoke of minutes and of shillings, of precedent and regulation. They were men of the land, and to them the river was a surface, a thing to be crossed, not a living force to be understood.
