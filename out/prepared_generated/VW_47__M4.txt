The street was her own, though she had never owned a brick of it; and this morning, with the sale already a bruise in her mind—the solicitor’s letter propped against the clock on the mantelpiece, its foolscap crackling whenever the fire stirred—she found that she must walk it from end to end, as a woman walks the deck of a ship she is leaving, touching the rail, counting the windows. The air was soft with that October softness which is almost a reproach, a gauze of sunlight lying over the pavement, and the plane trees in the square dropping their leaves one by one, deliberately, it seemed, so that each might be noticed, each a small brown hand waving farewell. She had her stick; she had her old grey coat; and she went slowly, for there was no hurry now, and every doorstep had become a sort of mouth, opening to speak.

Number one: the Fanning-Jocelyns, or was it Jocelyn-Fannings? There had been a parrot in the window, a green, obscene bird that shrieked at the milkman, and Mrs. Fanning-Jocelyn, all lace and lamentation, who had lost three sons in the war and spoke of them as though they were merely late for dinner. The parrot had outlived them all, she thought; she could hear it still, on certain summer evenings, a shriek like a rusty hinge swinging in the heat. And she—what had she been then? The young wife, the new arrival, with her furniture still smelling of the van, and her hopes arranged about the rooms like ornaments not yet dusty. She had stood at that very window, had she not, watching for the post, watching for a shape to turn the corner, and the shape had come, and gone, and come again, and gone for good, and the parrot had shrieked through it all, indifferent, immortal.

Number seven: Mr. Abercrombie, the stamp man, who climbed his stairs at night with a candle because he distrusted electricity, and whose rooms, she knew, smelled of gum and old paper and the faint sweet rot of things kept too long. He had shown her once, with trembling fingers, a Mauritius penny, blue as a vein, and she had understood nothing of its value except that it was the value of his whole life, and that he was offering it to her, and that she must be careful. “You see the position of the ‘P’,” he had said, and his breath had smelled of tea and toothache. She had said yes, she saw it; she had seen nothing; and he had wrapped it back in its tissue as a priest wraps the host. He died in the spring, and the stamps went to a nephew in Croydon, and the nephew sold them, she supposed, and bought a motor-car, and the Mauritius penny was somewhere now, in a drawer in Croydon, perhaps, or a museum, or lost, and Mr. Abercrombie was nowhere at all, except in her walking, in her remembering, on this soft October morning.

The houses went by, and she went by them, and it seemed to her that she was walking not down a street but through a series of rooms, each one opening into the next, and in each room a woman stood, waiting, and each woman was herself, and none of them knew the others, and all of them were patient. There was the woman of the early years, who had believed that happiness was a thing one arranged, like flowers, and who had arranged and rearranged until the flowers were dead. There was the woman of the middle years, who had learned to sit quietly in a chair and let the afternoons pass over her, and who had discovered—oh, the shame and the relief of it—that solitude is not the same as loneliness. There was the woman of the war years, who had stood in this very street on a night of fire, when the sky was a red cave and the water in the gutters ran with light, and had felt, God forgive her, a kind of exaltation, as though the world were at last as she had always suspected it to be beneath its manners. And there was this woman, the last of them, the one with the stick, who walked now past number twenty-three, where the Misses Tregaskis had lived, the two old sisters who wore identical dresses and finished each other’s sentences, and who had died within a month of one another, and whose furniture, she remembered, had been sold in the street, and a young man had bought their mirror, a tall thing with a gilt frame, and had carried it away on his shoulder, and in the mirror, as it went, she had seen the street, her street, walking away from her, upside down and small and complete.

At the corner, the public house, the King’s Arms, with its frosted windows and its smell of beer and sawdust, and the landlord, Mr. Peck, who had once, on a Christmas Eve, kissed her under the mistletoe, a wet, beery kiss, and she had laughed and wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, and he had said, “No offence, ma’am,” and she had said, “None taken, Mr. Peck,” and they had stood there, the two of them, in the yellow light, and it had been nothing, nothing at all, and yet she had remembered it for thirty years. Why? Why should the nothing things stay, and the great things go? She did not know. She only knew that Mr. Peck was dead now, and the King’s Arms was a tea-shop, and the mistletoe was gone, and the kiss remained, a small warm coin in the pocket of her memory, spent and spent again and never used up.
