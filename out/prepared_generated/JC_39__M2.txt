The rain in London, in those late autumn days of the nineties, did not so much fall as it seemed to seep out of the very bones of the city, a pervasive, grey dampness that clung to the wool of one’s coat and the spirit of one’s mind with a tenacity that defied explanation. It was a weather suited to the secrecy of the West End, where the shadows of the great houses stretched long and thin across the pavement, blurring the line between the respectable and the sordid. I stood by the window of the private office, watching the mist swirl around the gas lamps, their light fractured into trembling halos, while the heavy silence of the room pressed against my ears. It was a silence of anticipation, thick and cloying, the kind that precedes a storm or a confession.

My name is not important, though to those who knew me in the labyrinthine corridors of the Cabinet, I was merely the instrument, the quiet hand that smoothed the wrinkles in the Minister’s day. I had served Alderman Thorne for three years, a period during which I had learned that politics is not a matter of principle, but of balance, of keeping the scales from tipping into the abyss of scandal. Thorne was a man of iron will and limited imagination, a creature of habit who believed that the state was a machine to be oiled, not a soul to be saved. I respected him for his efficiency, if I could not love him for his coldness. It was this efficiency that had drawn me to him, and it was this same quality that now made me tremble as I watched the door.

The man who entered was not Thorne. It was Elias.

I had not seen Elias in fifteen years, since the days when we had shared a cramped room in a boarding house in Bloomsbury, where the walls were thin and our ambitions were loud. He was older then, I was younger; now, the years had carved a deep furrow between his eyes, and his hands, which I remembered as gentle and precise, were now gnarled and restless, tapping a rhythmic, anxious beat against the back of his chair. He wore a coat that had once been fine, now faded to the color of the London fog, and there was a wildness in his gaze that I had not known him to possess in our student days. It was the look of a man who has looked into the dark and found it looking back.

"You have not changed much," he said, his voice low, roughened by smoke or silence. "You still look as if you are calculating the cost of everything, including your own breath."

I smiled, but it felt like a mask slipping. "And you, Elias, look as if you have been running from something. Or toward it."

He laughed, a dry, brittle sound that did not reach his eyes. "There is a difference, I suppose. But in the end, the destination is the same." He sat down, not waiting for an invitation, and leaned forward, elbows on the table, his head resting in his hands. For a moment, the two of us sat in the dim light, the rain hammering against the glass, the air between us charged with the memory of shared meals and unspoken fears.

"I need to see the Minister," he said, abruptly, lifting his head. "Not to beg, not to plead. To tell him what is coming."

My stomach tightened. "Elias, you know he will not see you. You are... not a man he can comfortably acknowledge. The newspapers have been less than kind in their descriptions of your... activities."

"Let them describe what they will," he said, his voice hardening. "The truth is a dangerous thing, but it is the only thing that has weight. If he does not see me, I will make sure he hears me. There will be a demonstration at the gates. Not a riot, mind you. Just a presence. A quiet, terrible presence that will make the whole world stop and listen."

I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the draft from the window. "You are talking of treason, Elias."

"I am talking of justice," he corrected, though he did not seem to believe his own words. "Or perhaps I am talking of revenge. I have lost the distinction. It is all the same when the machine grinds you down. He is the machine, I am the gear. But gears do not have consciences. I do."

I looked at him, this ghost of my former self, and I saw the fracture in him, the place where the idealism of our youth had shattered against the hard reality of the world. He was not a villain, not in the way the police described him. He was a man who had been broken by the very system I served, and now he sought to break it in return. It was a terrible symmetry, and it terrified me.

"I will speak to him," I said, my voice steady, though my hands were shaking beneath the desk. "I will tell him that you wish to speak. But I cannot promise he will listen. And I cannot promise that your intentions will be understood as you intend them."

Elias looked at me, and for a second, the wildness in his eyes softened into something like pity. "You think you can mediate? You think you can stand between the fire and the fuel? You are a secretary, my friend. You are part of the machine. You do not have the power to stop the gears. You can only hope that they do not crush you."

He stood up, buttoning his coat, and moved toward the door. The atmosphere in the room seemed to contract, the air growing thinner, harder to breathe.
