Mrs. Thorne had lived at Ashgrove for seven years, since the death of her husband, and she had come to think of the house as hers in every way that mattered. The deeds were in Edmund's name, of course. Everything was in Edmund's name—the stables, the home farm, the linen with the TH monogram that she had embroidered herself the year she married, the rosebeds she had coaxed into health against the stubborn Shropshire clay. But Edmund had never once made her feel a guest, and she had repaid him by making herself indispensable. She supervised the kitchen, managed the household accounts, received the tenants at Christmas, and kept the parish vicar at a comfortable distance. She was, she believed, the thing that kept Ashgrove from falling into the disorder that overtook bachelor establishments. She was useful, and therefore safe.

Then Edmund had married, and everything changed.

The wedding had been in March, and the new Mrs. Thorne—Helena, a name Mrs. Thorne found both too classical and too insubstantial for daily use—had arrived at Ashgrove with three trunks, a travelling writing desk, and an expression of faint bewilderment that Mrs. Thorne interpreted, variously, as awe, gratitude, or concealed disdain. She was a dark-haired girl of two-and-twenty, the daughter of a Wiltshire clergyman, and she had come into the household the way a cat enters a strange room: silently, watchfully, and with an air of belonging nowhere.

Mrs. Thorne had prepared for her arrival with the thoroughness of a general provisioning a siege. The best bedchamber had been aired for a fortnight. New hangings had been ordered from Shrewsbury. She had selected a rose-patterned paper herself, standing in the wallpaper merchant's shop for the better part of an hour, rejecting one design after another until she found something neither too gloomy nor too frivolous. She had directed the maids in the arrangement of every chair, every candlestick, every fold of curtain. When Helena entered the room for the first time, she had looked at it for perhaps three seconds, said "How pleasant," and turned to ask Edmund whether there was a library.

A library. As though the house existed to provide her with reading material.

In the weeks that followed, Mrs. Thorne devoted herself to the task of making her daughter-in-law comfortable with an intensity that would have worn down a woman of steel. She rose early to supervise Helena's breakfast tray, selecting the preserves herself. She offered to walk the grounds with her each morning, pointing out the improvements she had overseen, the specimen trees, the new drainage ditch beyond the lower meadow. She suggested drives into Shrewsbury, called attention to the milliner's shop and the circulating library, and hinted that a new bonnet might be in order, if Helena would only say what shade she preferred.

Helena said she had no preference. Helena said the grounds were very fine. Helena said she did not require a bonnet. What Helena did, morning after morning, was take a book from the shelf in the small parlour and settle into the window seat with her knees drawn up, reading with an absorption that Mrs. Thorne found frankly alarming. She read novels. She read histories. She read a tract on the cultivation of turnips that she had found on Edmund's desk, apparently for no reason beyond the fact that it was there and she had finished the other book.

Mrs. Thorne began to feel the first cold stirring of panic. She was not wanted. The girl did not want her company, did not want her guidance, did not want her preserves or her drives or her carefully selected wallpaper. She wanted to be left alone with a book, and the implication of that desire, if one followed it to its logical end, was that Mrs. Thorne herself was surplus to the running of Ashgrove. If the new mistress could be content alone in a window seat, then she did not require a mother-in-law to manage her household for her. She would, in time, take over the keys, the accounts, the parish visits, the Christmas tenants. She would efface Mrs. Thorne by the simple method of ignoring her.

Mrs. Thorne redoubled her efforts. She began appearing in the small parlour at half past nine with a workbasket, settling into the chair opposite the window seat and applying herself to her needlework with conspicuous industry. She commented on the weather. She remarked upon the behaviour of the kitchen maid. She asked Helena whether she had slept well, whether the pillow was too firm, whether she would prefer the smaller bolster from the guest room. Helena answered each question with perfect civility and returned to her page.

One morning, Mrs. Thorne brought down the household accounts and spread them across the little table beside her chair, hoping to demonstrate the complexity of the establishment and, by extension, her own irreplacability. She read out the cost of candles, the quarterly bill from the draper, the price of mutton per pound. She asked Helena whether she had any thoughts on the matter of the kitchen garden, which had been underperforming. Helena looked up from her book—a volume of Cowper, Mrs. Thorne noted with disapproval—and said, quite kindly, that she was sure Mrs. Thorne's judgement was superior to her own, and that she had no wish to interfere.

No wish to interfere. Mrs. Thorne turned the phrase over in her mind for the rest of the day, finding new and more terrible meanings in it. The girl did not wish to interfere because she did not consider the household her concern. She was biding her time. She would interfere when it suited her, and when she did, she would do so with the authority of the mistress, and Mrs. Thorne would be relegated to the role of guest, dependent, a woman tolerated in the house of her son.
