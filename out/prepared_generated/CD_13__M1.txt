If you were to walk down towards the water at Rotherhithe, where the mud, when the tide is out, lies glistening and fat like a creature from the deep that has swallowed the moon, you would find, tucked between a chandler’s shop and a rope-walk that smelt of tar and unhappy hemp, a little establishment of the greatest importance. This was the shop of Mr. Silas Groggins, a retired sea-cook of formidable reputation and even more formidable girth, who had, at the age of fifty-three, invested the entire hoard of his life—the slow, hard-won savings from a thousand galley fires—into a single, glorious enterprise: a pie shop.

And what a shop it was! Not for Mr. Groggins the common mutton or the pedestrian beef. No, his pies were a maritime epic in crust and gravy. There was the ‘Bosun’s Bounty’, a robust affair of beef and stout; the ‘Mermaid’s Morsel’, which contained a fish of such freshness it might have been swimming in the Thames but an hour before; and the ‘Captain’s Choice’, a pie so dense with fowl, bacon, and a mysterious herb that the old cook would only call ‘sea-sage’, that it required a man of considerable fortitude to finish it. The window, from which a savoury steam perpetually fogged the glass like a ship’s porthole in a squall, was a testament to Mr. Groggins’s artistry. Pies of every description were laid out in neat, golden ranks, each one a perfect, gleaming dome, each one a promise of comfort and warmth to the shivering souls who trudged the muddy lanes.

But for all his culinary genius, Mr. Groggins was a man ill-suited to the commerce of the shore. He had the sea in his soul, and a merchant’s ledger in his hands was as foreign to him as a landlubber’s chart. He would stand behind his counter, a monument of frustration, his face as red as a boiled lobster, as the labourers from the docks and the sailors from the wharves passed his door, their pennies clinking in their pockets, their stomachs rumbling, yet their feet carrying them on to some less savoury, but more aggressive, establishment. His business, like a becalmed vessel, sat motionless upon the waters of fortune.

It was into this doldrum that there sailed, one blustering Tuesday, a boy. He was a creature of the wind and the alleyway, all sharp angles and sharper eyes, with a patch of unruly hair the colour of rust and a grin that seemed to know the price of everything and the value of nothing. His name, he said, was Toby, but he carried himself with the air of a Tiberius, a Caesar of the cobblestones.

‘Morning, Guv’nor,’ said the boy, leaning in the doorway as if he owned the passage to it. ‘Fine pies you have there. Shame nobody’s buying ‘em.’

Mr. Groggins, who was engaged in the melancholy task of dusting a particularly handsome ‘Mermaid’s Morsel’, looked up with a scowl. ‘Mind your business, you young jackanapes, and I’ll mind mine.’

‘But your business *is* my business, Guv’nor,’ said Toby, stepping inside with the confidence of a customs officer. ‘See, you’ve got the pies. A fine thing. But you ain’t got the *patter*. You ain’t got the *legs*. You’re a ship without a sail, you are. Now me, I’ve got legs enough for a centipede and a tongue that could sell a rope to a hangman. You let me take your wares out on a tray, see? I’ll sell ‘em for you. For a penny in every shilling, mind. Commission, they calls it.’

Mr. Groggins stared, his jaw agape. The audacity of the creature was staggering. And yet, as he looked from the boy’s confident smirk to the empty street, a desperate, half-drowned hope began to splash about in the old cook’s heart. What had he to lose but more silence?

‘A penny in the shilling,’ the boy repeated, holding up a grubby but determined finger. ‘And you’ll see your takings double by Saturday. I give you my word as a gentleman of the road.’

The bargain was struck. Toby was provided with a large, flat tray, a napkin, and a dozen of Mr. Groggins’s finest pies. He set off with a jaunty stride, his voice already ringing out like a bellman’s: ‘Pies! Hot pies! Bosun’s Bounty for the hungry! Mermaid’s Morsel for the soul!’

And lo! The miracle occurred. The boy was a whirlwind, a pocket-sized hurricane of commerce. He did not merely stand and cry his wares; he accosted, he cajoled, he charmed. He knew every docker by name, every sailor’s sweetheart, every clerk’s secret hunger. He would appear at the very moment a man felt the first pang of lunch, his tray held aloft like a standard. He would have a witty word for the gaffer and a roguish smile for the lass. By the time the sun began to sink behind the forest of masts in the Pool of London, the tray was empty and the boy’s pockets were heavy with coin. Mr. Groggins’s takings, as promised, had not merely doubled, they had trebled.

The days that followed were a golden age for the pie shop. Mr. Groggins, his spirits restored, baked with a new and frenzied passion. The ovens roared like a ship in a gale. Toby was a legend, a sprite of the riverside, his cry of ‘Hot pies!’ a welcome sound from Wapping Stairs to the Surrey Canal. Money flowed into the cash-box like the incoming tide, and Mr. Groggins began to see a future not of grim solitude, but of comfort and perhaps, one day, a small cottage with a view of the water.

But a seed of doubt, once planted, grows quickly in the damp soil of suspicion. Mr. Groggins, a man of simple habits, began to notice things. He noticed that Toby, after his day of selling pies, would often cross the bridge to the south side, to the labyrinthine warren of streets that was the very antithesis of honest trade. He noticed, too, that the boy sometimes carried small, un-pie-like parcels under his arm, and returned without them. The boy’s pockets, which should have been jingling with pie-money, were sometimes suspiciously silent.
