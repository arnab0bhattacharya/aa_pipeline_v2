The frost had come early to Thornfield Hollow, settling over the kitchen garden of Ashgrove Cottage like a fine layer of borrowed lace. Elias Merrick stood at the parlor window, watching the last of the autumn light drain from the sky, and considered which of his two despairs would ruin him the more completely. On the escritoire behind him lay two documents: a letter from Mr. Phipps, the solicitor in Warwick, itemizing the full extent of his father's obligations, and a deed of sale, already drafted, awaiting only Elias's signature to transfer the fourteen acres at Ashgrove to Sir George Tallis, whose land adjoined the property and who had wanted it, it was said, since before Elias was born.

The debts were staggering. Elias had known his father, the late Mr. Henry Merrick, had been careless with money, had extended credit too readily to tenants who could not pay and had borrowed against the land to fund various improvements—a new drainage ditch, a hedgerow of hawthorn, a cow byre that now stood half-finished. But it was only after the funeral, when Phipps had sat across from Elias at this same table and unfolded the ledger, that the true architecture of ruin became apparent. Henry Merrick had died owing nearly nine hundred pounds to various creditors in Warwick, Stratford, and Leamington, a sum that swallowed the value of everything Elias possessed except the land itself.

The land. Elias turned from the window and looked at it spread out beyond the garden wall, the fields running down to the copse by the river, the old oak his grandfather had planted standing bare-limbed against the dusk. The Merricks had held Ashgrove for three generations. They were not gentry, not really; they were what the village called "respectable yeomen," men who worked their own soil and sat in the parish church with their hats on their knees. Henry's father had added the orchard. Henry had subtracted nearly everything else.

Elias picked up the deed of sale and read it through again. Sir George was offering seven hundred pounds for the fourteen acres, which was a fair price, perhaps even a generous one, but it would not cover the debts entirely. Phipps had explained that the remaining two hundred could be discharged over time, in installments, if Elias retained the cottage and found work. There was a position at the grammar school in Warwick, teaching Latin and Greek to boys whose fathers had more land than sense. Elias was twenty-three. He had a degree from Oxford, taken at his father's expense, another debt that now seemed to have purchased nothing but the ability to recognize the Latin word for ruin when he saw it.

The alternative was his uncle. Nathaniel Crane, his mother's brother, had made a fortune in the Birmingham manufactories and had retired to a large house near Henley-in-Arden, where he kept a carriage and entertained men of substance. Crane had offered nothing after Henry's death—no letter of condolence, no attendance at the funeral—but then, he had never disguised his opinion of his late brother-in-law. At the only dinner Elias could remember attending at his uncle's table, Crane had raised his glass and proposed a toast "to men who understand the value of a shilling," looking directly at Henry, who had laughed good-naturedly, not realizing, or perhaps realizing and choosing to absorb the insult rather than return it.

Crane's contempt was not without foundation. Henry Merrick had been a man of extraordinary warmth and correspondingly poor judgment. He had lent money to a neighbor who was rebuilding a barn and never asked for it back. He had stood surety for a cousin's venture in Welsh slate and lost two hundred pounds when the cousin absconded to Bristol. He had given a tenant three years' grace on his rent and been genuinely surprised when the tenant left in the night, owing everything. Henry believed in people. It was a form of generosity that bordered on recklessness, and it had killed him as surely as the influenza had, leaving Elias to bury both his father and his father's good name.

For the name was still good. That was the cruelty of it. In Warwick and the surrounding villages, Henry Merrick was remembered with affection. The vicar spoke of his kindness. The tenants—weary, indebted tenants—spoke of his patience. Elias had inherited this name, this warmth that clung to the Merricks like scent to a coat, and he understood, with the clarity that poverty enforces, that the name had a value his father had never calculated. To sell the land would be to sever the last material connection between the Merricks and Ashgrove, to become a schoolmaster with a famous name and no ground beneath it. To ask Crane for the money would be to place that name in the hands of a man who had always wanted to crush it.

Elias laid the deed down and took up the blank sheet of paper beside it. He dipped the pen. The ink was thin; he would need to buy more, and the cost of ink now mattered in a way it never had before. He wrote: "Dear Uncle." Then he stopped. The phrase sat on the page like a small lie. Crane was not dear. Crane was a man who measured the world in pounds and found most of it wanting, including his sister's choice of husband.

He could imagine the letter that would follow. He could write it without thinking, because he had rehearsed it a hundred times in his head. He would explain the situation with careful honesty, would detail the debts and the value of the land, would propose a loan secured against the property, with interest at whatever rate Crane deemed fair. He would promise repayment within five years. He would be humble but not groveling, respectful but not obsequious.
