The lecture had been on the drainage of the Mesopotamian plain, or perhaps on the minor poets of the reign of Queen Anne—the subject hardly mattered now, for the room had been hot, and the lecturer had a way of lifting his chin at the end of every sentence as though he expected the ceiling to applaud, and Margaret Ellery, sitting three rows from the front with her gloves in her lap, had found herself thinking not of silt or of couplets but of the curious way the gas-jets trembled when the door at the back was opened, and of whether she had left the office window unlatched, and of nothing at all, in the end, but the heat. So that when the young man beside her—a stranger, with a thin face and a collar a size too large—leaned over and murmured, “You don’t believe a word of it either,” she had answered, “I believe every word,” though she did not, and had felt the evening take on, at once, a sharper edge, a brightness, as though someone had turned up the gas.

His name was Arthur Penhallow. He worked, he said, in the Office of Woods and Forests, and he wrote poetry in the evenings, and he did not say this as a confession but as a man states the longitude of a place he has visited. She worked for a charity in Bloomsbury, in a room with three desks and a map of the metropolis upon the wall, and she told him so, and he said that charity was the opium of the comfortable, and she said that poetry was the opium of the idle, and they stood together in the corridor, in the smell of damp coats and coal-smoke, and argued until the caretaker came with his long pole to put out the lights.

After that they met every Thursday. It was never arranged; it simply was. He would be waiting at the corner of the square, or she would find him already seated at the little table in the tea-room off the Strand, with a cup before him going cold, and his hat upon his knee, and his long fingers turning and turning a lump of sugar in his saucer until it dissolved to nothing. And they would begin again—about the Empire, about the vote, about whether a man could be said to love a woman and yet not know her opinion upon the price of bread; and the argument went on and on, and underneath it, like the river beneath the street, ran the other thing, unspoken, which neither of them named.

“You are a theorist,” she said. “You sit in your office and the forests of the Empire are numbers in a ledger, and you go home and write about—what? The moon, I suppose.”

“The moon,” he said, “is a very great deal more honest than your map of the metropolis. Your map has the workhouse in it, and the hospital, and the public-house, and it leaves out the moon entirely. That is what I object to in your charity. It is all map and no moon.”

“And what does your poetry do,” she said, “but leave out the workhouse?”

The tea grew cold between them. Outside, the rain came down, and the omnibuses went by with their wet wheels hissing, and the lights of the shops ran in long trembling streaks upon the pavement, and she saw his face across the table, thin and eager and lit from below by the little lamp, and she thought: he is wrong about everything, and I would rather be here, arguing with him, than anywhere else in London.

It was in the spring—the spring of that year when the chestnuts came out early and the German bands played in the squares and the suffrage women chalked their slogans on the walls of the Embankment—that they quarrelled in earnest. It was a Friday. The room was full of the smell of new bread and of somebody’s violet sachet, and Arthur had said, with that lift of the chin which she had come to know, that no good ever came of a cause that had to be shouted in the street; that the world was changed by thought, not by noise; and Margaret had set down her cup, and her hand had shaken, and she said, “You are a coward. You sit in your office and you are kind to me and you are kind to the trees and you are kind to the moon, and you will not put your name to anything.”

And he had gone very white, and said, “I put my name to my poems.”

“Your poems!” she said. And then, because she was ashamed, she looked away, out of the window, where a man was selling violets from a barrow, and the violets were blue and wet and beautiful, and she thought: why do I say these things? and she heard him say, quite quietly, “You are unjust.”

“I am just,” she said. “That is the trouble.”

He did not come the next Thursday. Nor the next. She sat in the tea-room with her hands folded, and the rain came down, and she told herself that she was glad, that he had been a theorist, a dreamer, that his poems were bad—they were bad, she was certain of it—and that she had work to do, real work, in the room with the map and the three desks. And the weeks went by, and the chestnuts dropped their flowers, and the days lengthened, and still she went on Thursdays to the tea-room, and sat for a quarter of an hour, and then went home, and told herself nothing at all.

It was in June, on a day of extraordinary brightness, when the whole city seemed to have been dipped in light, that she saw him. She had gone down to the Embankment to walk, because the office was stifling, and the river ran grey and gold between the bridges, and the barges went slowly, and the sun struck the water into a thousand fragments, and there, on a bench, with his hat beside him and his head in his hands, sat Arthur Penhallow.
