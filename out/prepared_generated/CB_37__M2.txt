The wind off the North Sea did not merely blow; it hunted. It tore at the hem of my skirts, whipped the dust from the carriage windows, and seemed to claw at the very glass of the world, demanding to know why I was there, and why I was so cold. We had arrived at Scarborough, that rocky, grey sentinel of the coast, three days prior, and still the chill had not left my bones. Mr. Hartley, my employer, had come for the air, for his wife’s delicate nerves, and for the social prestige of being seen at the promenade. I had come for duty, for a modest salary, and for the quiet, suffocating certainty that I was a spare part in the machinery of their comfortable existence.

I walked along the promenade, my hands tucked into my muff, my eyes fixed on the churning, slate-colored water. The beach was empty, save for a few stragglers who had braved the gale. It was there, against the backdrop of the relentless sea, that I saw her.

Eliza Vance.

She stood under a large, black parasol, though it was no longer morning, and the sun was a pale, sickly coin in the mist. She wore a dress of deep emerald silk, the color of the deepest part of the sea, and her carriage was easy, lordly, and utterly devoid of the haste that characterizes those who must answer to another’s clock. She turned as I approached, and her face, which I had known well in the sunlit, dusty rooms of our old school at Harewood, was now rounded, soft, and glowing with the health of idleness.

"Lucy!" she cried, her voice carrying over the roar of the surf. "Lucy Ashworth! Is it really you? Good heavens, what a change! You look so thin, Lucy. Are you well? Is your mother’s health improved?"

I stopped, my heart giving a violent, irregular beat. "Eliza," I said, and the word felt foreign in my mouth. "I am well. And my mother is dead, as you know. It has been two years."

A shadow crossed Eliza’s face, but it was quickly swept away by a smile of practiced sympathy. "Oh, my dear. I remember. I sent my condolences. But you are here! At Scarborough! How wonderful for you. I had no idea you were in service. I mean... I had no idea you were accompanying the Hartleys. They are very kind to you, I hope?"

"Very kind," I said, and the words tasted like ash. "They are gentlemen. They provide me with a room and a salary. It is sufficient."

Eliza sighed, a long, theatrical exhalation that seemed to ruffle the air between us. She stepped closer, the hem of her emerald skirt whispering against the stone. "Lucy, you must not be so stiff. You used to be my closest friend. We used to share our secrets, our dreams. Do you remember? We used to talk of being ladies, of having our own homes, of reading and music and travel. And now..." She shook her head, her eyes welling with sudden, performative tears. "You are a governess. You live in a stranger’s house, obeying their every whim, watching their children, correcting their faults, and all the while, your own life passes by you like that tide. You are wasting yourself, Lucy. You are so clever, so talented. To see you here, in this grey place, serving others, it breaks my heart. Truly, it does."

I looked at her. I looked at the water, which was throwing itself against the rocks with a fury that mirrored the turmoil in my chest. I wanted to scream. I wanted to ask her who she served, and what her price was, and why she had traded her spirit for that emerald silk. But I held my tongue. I am not a woman who screams; I am a woman who endures.

"Your pity is misplaced, Eliza," I said, my voice low, but steady. "I am not wasting myself. I am living. I am working. And I am free in a way that you, with your husband’s purse and your mother’s expectations, are not."

Eliza’s face hardened. The sympathy vanished, replaced by a sharp, cold look. "Free? You are bound by contract, Lucy. You have no property, no independence. You are a servant. That is the truth of it, and I think it is a sad truth. But I suppose you are too proud to admit it. You were always too proud. It is your pride that has kept you in this state. If you had been more... accommodating... more willing to use your charms as I did, you would not be standing here in the wind, looking like a ghost."

The words struck me with the force of a physical blow. *Accommodating. Charms.* She meant to sell herself. She had sold herself, and she wore her marriage like a trophy, a shield against the very life she now pitied me for living.

I felt a heat rise in my cheeks, not from shame, but from the sheer, burning indignity of her accusation. I straightened my back. I lifted my chin.

"You mistake me, Eliza," I said. "I am not proud. I am honest. And I would rather starve in my own cottage, reading my books and tending my garden, than sit in a gilded cage, smiling at men who do not see me, only seeing a commodity. You pity me because you see your own prison in my freedom, and you cannot bear to look at it. You are not pitiful, Eliza. You are merely comfortable. And comfort is a poor master."

Eliza stared at me, her lips thinning. For a moment, I thought she would strike me, or at least dismiss me with a wave of her hand. But she did not. She simply looked away, her gaze fixed on the horizon, where the sea and the sky merged in a bruised, purple line.
