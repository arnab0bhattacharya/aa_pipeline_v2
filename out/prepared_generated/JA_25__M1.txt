It was a truth very well understood in the neighbourhood of Ashworth Parsonage, that Miss Harriet Vale possessed a good deal of beauty, a great deal of sense, and not a single fortune. It was this last circumstance which had obliged her to accept the invitation of her school-friend, Mrs. Charlotte Linton, to spend the winter in London. Mrs. Linton, having married a man of considerable property, was of the opinion that every young woman of taste and merit had a right to see the metropolis, and that the expense of doing so was a trifle which no one of her generosity would allow to stand in the way.

Harriet, however, had found that the expense was not the only obstacle to her enjoyment. To be in London was one thing; to be seen in it was another. Mrs. Linton’s circle was brilliant and expansive, but it was not Harriet’s circle. She was, in effect, a piece of very handsome furniture, brought from the country to adorn her friend’s already well-furnished drawing-room. She was looked at, she was praised for her complexion and her quiet manners, and then she was forgotten. Her particular anxiety, a Mr. Edmund Alistair, whose father’s estate marched with her father’s parish, was to be avoided at all costs; and a fashionable assembly, where one might meet with half of London in the course of a single evening, seemed the very last place in which a lady should wish to find herself.

Yet it was to such a place that Mrs. Linton, in the fullness of her benevolence, insisted upon taking her. “My dear Harriet, you will be admired! You cannot help it. And who knows what excellent connexions might be made?” To this, Harriet could only offer a silent and rather melancholy assent, for she knew that admiration and connexion were not so easily distinguished, and that the one she most desired to avoid was of a family whose connexions were very excellent indeed.

The rooms at Almack’s were, as Harriet had been warned, a scene of dazzling confusion. The heat was excessive, the music loud, and the company a moving tapestry of faces in which she could discern not a single feature of familiarity. Mrs. Linton was instantly claimed by a party of friends, with a cheerful “You will not mind me for a moment, will you, my dear? I shall return before you can miss me!”—a promise which the progress of the evening soon proved to be founded on a very sanguine view of time. Harriet was left to stand near a pillar, observing the scene with the detached air of a philosopher, and endeavouring to look as though solitude were a matter of perfect choice.

She had been in this situation for perhaps a quarter of an hour, and had just concluded that a potted orange tree was a more agreeable companion than any she was likely to find, when a gentleman of an advanced age, who had been watching her with an air of amusement, drew near. “I beg your pardon, madam,” he said, with a low bow, “but I could not help observing that you have formed a most promising attachment to that unfortunate shrub. May I venture to inquire if it has returned your regard?”

Harriet, startled from her reverie, could not help but smile. “I am afraid, sir, that its conversation, though elegant, is somewhat lacking in variety. It has not yet told me a single piece of scandal.”

The gentleman laughed heartily. “A fatal defect! A fatal defect in a London acquaintance. You must allow me to remedy the deficiency. My name is Alistair, and I am, as you see, an old man with nothing to do but observe the follies of my fellow creatures. I make it my business to know a little of everyone. Who is it you are so determined not to speak to? Point them out, and I shall tell you all their secrets.”

It was a strange and forward speech, but delivered with such good humour that it seemed only droll. “You are very kind, sir,” Harriet replied, “but I am afraid I know no one to point out.”

“Ah! A new arrival. That explains the look of profound and dignified suffering. I know it well. I have felt it myself at every ball for these fifty years. Come, let us find two chairs where we may be comfortable, and you shall tell me the name of your home, and I shall tell you the name of everyone in this room who pretends to have been there.”

He led her to a quiet alcove, and for the next hour, Harriet found herself more entertained than she had been in a month. Mr. Alistair possessed the rare talent of making a perfect stranger feel like an old and valued friend. He spoke of London society with a gentle irony that did not wound, and of country life with a sympathy that did not patronize. He asked about her father’s parish, her favourite books, and the state of the roads in her county. He told her that the gentleman with the yellow waistcoat had lost a fortune at cards and was in search of a wife with one; that the lady in the pink turban had been jilted three times and considered it a point of honour to be the first to announce every engagement in the room. Harriet found herself laughing, a sound that was so rare of late it surprised even herself.

When at last Mrs. Linton appeared, flushed with triumph and apologies, Harriet felt a distinct pang of disappointment. “My dear Harriet, I had no idea the time! But I see you have not been neglected! You have found the most delightful companion in London.”

Mr. Alistair rose. “The compliment is all mine, madam. Your friend has done me a great honour in tolerating my chatter.” He turned to Harriet. “I hope I may have the pleasure of seeing you again, Miss Vale. My son is to have a small party at his house in Hanover Square on Tuesday next. I shall insist that he sends you an invitation.”
