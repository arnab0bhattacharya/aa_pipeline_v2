The mist that clung to the Wharfe valley had a peculiar, damp quality that seemed to settle into the joints of the bones, yet it was the cold that radiated from the stone of the old mill that truly chilled the blood. I stood beside Mr. Arthur Penhaligon, the chief engineer of the North Yorkshire Railway Company, watching him pace the muddy bank with the agitated gait of a man whose professional pride had been thoroughly bruised.

"It is the third time, Dr. Watson," Penhaligon said, his voice tight with suppressed anger. He was a man of precise habits, dressed in a tweed suit that was already spattered with the grime of the site, his cap held firmly in his hand. "Three times I have set the stakes by the evening. Three times I have returned at dawn to find them shifted. Not by an inch, nor by a foot, but by several yards, always in the same direction. Away from the farm of Old Grimsby."

I looked toward the structure he pointed to. It was a modest farmhouse, nestled in a hollow to the north, its windows dark against the grey morning. "And you suspect the farmer?" I asked.

"Mr. Grimsby swears his innocence," Penhaligon replied. "He claims he has not left his bed. He is a stubborn old man, Dr. Watson, but he is not a fool. Yet the company is losing patience. The survey is delayed, the costs are rising, and the directors in London are threatening to sack me for incompetence. They say I am letting local prejudices cloud my judgment. They say I am being paranoid."

"I see," I said, though I did not entirely share his despair. "But why would anyone move the stakes? To divert the line?"

"To save their land, or so the rumour goes. The proposed line would cut through the northern field of the estate. If the stakes are moved, the line swings wide, taking with it a profitable stretch of pasture belonging to a rival landowner, or perhaps saving the old man’s own property from the noise and dust of the trains. It is a simple matter of geometry and greed."

We walked toward the site of the viaduct, a skeletal framework of iron rising above the deep gorge. The workmen had gone home for the day, leaving only the silence of the valley and the distant sound of water rushing over the falls. Penhaligon stopped at the base of the surveying tripod.

"Look here," he said, kneeling in the mud. "I used a specific type of hardwood stake, marked with a bright red band. I drove them in with a sledge-hammer. The ground is compacted. It would take considerable force to remove them and replace them without leaving a mark."

I crouched beside him, examining the earth. The soil was wet and heavy, typical of the Yorkshire clay. Around the base of the stake, the mud was undisturbed, save for a small depression where the stake had been pulled out. But it was not the stake itself that caught my eye, but the shadow of the depression. It was not a clean circle. It was slightly oval, and at one end, there was a faint drag mark, a smear of clay that stretched away from the stake and toward the river.

"You see?" I said, pointing to the smear.

"See what?" Penhaligon asked, frowning.

"Whoever moved this stake did not simply pull it out. They dragged it. And they did so from the south, toward the north. But look at the angle. It is not a straight line. It curves slightly to the left."

Penhaligon straightened up, his face pale. "You think it was done from the river bank?"

"I think," I said, "that the person who moved the stake was not walking on the bank. They were on a boat, or at least, they were standing in the water, and they reached across to pull the stake."

Penhaligon laughed, a harsh, bitter sound. "A boat? In the dead of night? On the Wharfe? It is impossible. The current is too strong, and the water is too cold. No man would do such a thing."

"Impossible is a word I use sparingly, Mr. Penhaligon," I said, rising to my feet. "But let us test this hypothesis. If the stake was pulled from the water, then the mud on the south side of the depression should be disturbed in a specific way. And if the person was standing in the water, they would have left footprints in the shallows."

We walked to the river’s edge. The water was low, and the current, while swift, was not impassable. I took off my boots and waded into the shallows, the cold biting at my ankles. I scanned the muddy bottom, looking for the impression of a foot. After a few minutes, I found them. Three distinct prints, leading from the opposite bank, close to the base of the viaduct, and ending in the water’s edge.

"Look here, Mr. Penhaligon," I called out. "These are not the prints of a farmer. Look at the size. They are small. And look at the shape. The heel is narrow, the toe is broad. This is a woman’s foot."

Penhaligon waded in, his face a mask of shock. "A woman? But Grimsby’s wife is dead. His daughter is in London. Who else could it be?"

"Perhaps," I said, "it was not a woman. Perhaps it was a child. Or perhaps," I paused, looking at the prints with renewed interest, "it was a man who had been forced to wear a woman’s shoe."

"Why would he do that?" Penhaligon asked.

"Because he did not want to be recognized. And because, if he had used his own boots, the mud would have been different. These prints are clean. The mud has not been tracked into the house. This person has taken great care to avoid leaving a trail."
