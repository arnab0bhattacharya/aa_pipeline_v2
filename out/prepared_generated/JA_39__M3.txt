The news of Mr. Arthur Creswell’s marriage had reached his family at Delaford Park in Northamptonshire with all the surprise inherent in such an event, when the gentleman in question had been seven-and-thirty and deemed, by the unprejudiced portion of society, to be thoroughly established in the habits of a bachelor. His mother and three sisters had long managed his establishment with an elegance and quiet economy that left him little to desire; and when he wrote to announce he had brought home a wife from Bath, the intelligence was received with a mixture of gratified wonder and very natural apprehension.

The new Mrs. Creswell was the daughter of a city merchant of ample fortune, and had spent the last five years in acquiring every refinement of fashion which a superior income and an extensive acquaintance could bestow. She had been accustomed to the crowded drawing-rooms of Bath and the rapid intercourse of London society, and had formed a very decided opinion of her own consequence, which she was not backward in displaying. She arrived at Delaford Park in the third week of October, prepared to be charmed by the scenery and dissatisfied with everything else.

Her first evening was devoted to an examination of the house, which she performed with an air of graceful endurance that was not lost upon the family. Mrs. Creswell, the elder, received her with all the warmth which genuine kindness and a strong sense of propriety could supply; but Mrs. Arthur was not to be conciliated by warmth. She admired the size of the rooms, wondered they had not been repainted, observed that the furniture was remarkably old-fashioned, and enquired whether the carpets had not been originally chosen by some person of very singular taste. Upon being assured that they had been selected by the late Mr. Creswell, she had the grace to be silent, but her silence was more eloquent than her speech.

At dinner she found fault with nothing, which was perhaps the severest criticism she could have bestowed; for it argued a state of things so hopeless that comment would be superfluous. She ate little, drank less, and employed herself chiefly in surveying the company with an expression which seemed to say that she was making the best of a very disagreeable necessity. Her husband, who had been used to find his home comfortable and his family agreeable, betrayed some uneasiness at these silent strictures, but was consoled by the reflection that Mrs. Arthur could not be long in discovering the good sense and good breeding which formed the character of his connexions.

In this expectation he was destined to be disappointed. Mrs. Arthur had not been four-and-twenty hours at Delaford Park before she conceived a plan for its complete reformation. The family rose too early and dined too late; they walked when they might have driven, and drove when they might have remained at home. Their evening parties, when they had any, consisted of three or four neighbouring families whose conversation was chiefly of crops and parish concerns; and their music, their reading, and their amusements were all conducted upon a scale of quiet domesticity which Mrs. Arthur pronounced to be excessively tiresome. She did not say so in terms, for she was not deficient in the outward forms of civility, but she conveyed her meaning with a precision which left no room for misconception.

“I am sure,” said she, one morning at breakfast, addressing herself more particularly to her sister-in-law, Miss Creswell, “that I should be quite enchanted with the country, if it were not so remarkably retired. In Bath one has always something to look at, somebody to call upon, some engagement of importance; but here, I fancy one might live for a twelvemonth and not see a new face.”

“We are not so entirely secluded,” replied Miss Creswell, with a mildness which her sister-in-law might have known was not to be overcome. “We have a very pleasant neighbourhood, and the families about us are such as we have known all our lives. There is a satisfaction in old friendships which novelty, however agreeable, can scarcely supply.”

“Oh! I am sure I do not mean to disparage old friends,” cried Mrs. Arthur, with a quickness which proved that she had perceived the tendency of the remark. “I am excessively fond of old friends myself—when they are agreeable. But there is so much difference in old friends! Some are quite the thing, and others are—well, I shall not say what, for I am sure you must know the sort of people I mean.”

Miss Creswell did not know, and said so; but she said it with a placid smile which Mrs. Arthur found rather provoking than otherwise. She had hoped for a little opposition, or at least a little embarrassment; but the Creswell family were distinguished by a uniform composure of manner which it was impossible to disturb. They listened to her remarks with attention, replied to them with calmness, and conducted themselves throughout with such perfect propriety that she could find no handle for correction. If she proposed an alteration in the hours of meals, Mrs. Creswell the elder expressed herself obliged by the suggestion, but observed that the present arrangement had been found convenient for the servants, and she feared disturbing it might occasion some little confusion. If she recommended a more elegant style of entertainment, Miss Creswell assented to the desirableness of it in general terms, but doubted whether their neighbourhood would support the expense of such an undertaking, or their habits accommodate themselves to such a change.

Mrs. Arthur’s next effort was directed towards the individual improvement of her new relatives. She undertook to advise Miss Creswell upon the subject of her wardrobe, which she considered to be at least ten years behind the present fashion. She recommended a more costly lace, a richer silk, and a bonnet of a shape which she had seen universally worn in Bond Street; and she offered to procure these articles from London with the greatest pleasure.
