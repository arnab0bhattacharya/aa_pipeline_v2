Lady Ashworth had always believed that the essence of a perfect dinner lay not in the food, which was merely fuel for the conversation, but in the absolute, unbroken silence that existed between guests who had never before exchanged a glance. It was a game of exquisite isolation, she reasoned, where each guest was a locked room, and the hostess held the only key. To ensure this singular triumph, she had invited a Bishop, an Actress, an Explorer, and a Young Poet, all of whom were strangers to one another. The dining room in Grosvenor Square was set with silver that had seen the Crimean War and china that was thin enough to hear the soul singing through it. The candles burned with a low, conspiratorial light, casting shadows that seemed to whisper of things better left unsaid.

Lord Pemberton, the Bishop, sat at the head of the table. He was a man of considerable girth and negligible mystery, wearing a collar that looked less like a symbol of office and more like a white bandage for a spiritual fracture. Opposite him sat Mrs. Vane, the Actress, whose beauty was so sharp it might have cut the air if she had not been so carefully composed. She wore emerald silk that moved like water, and her eyes, painted with the precision of a microscopist, scanned the room for a weakness to exploit. To her right was Mr. Thorne, the Explorer, who still smelled faintly of the tropics and the particular despair of a man who has seen too much of the world to be surprised by anything. And at the foot of the table sat Julian Hale, the Young Poet, whose recent volume of verse had scandalized the critics by suggesting that virtue was a form of cowardice and that sin was the only honest emotion. He wore a shirt of pale blue, looking, as he himself would have noted, like a bruise on the wrist of the evening.

The soup was a clear consommé, tasting of nothing, which Lady Ashworth considered a triumph, for it allowed the mind to wander freely. For the first twenty minutes, the silence was a luxury. The Bishop cleared his throat, a sound like a dry leaf skittering across pavement.

“Mrs. Vane,” he began, his voice muffled by a napkin, “I confess I find the arrangement... novel. To dine with strangers is to dine with one’s own potentialities. One never knows which of them is the ugliest.”

Mrs. Vane smiled, a slow, dangerous unfolding. “It is the only way to be truly seen, my Lord. Friends see what they wish to see; enemies see what they wish to hide. But a stranger? A stranger sees the truth, because he has no stake in your lie.”

Mr. Thorne, who had been staring at a fly that had landed on the salt cellar, looked up. “Truth is a matter of perspective,” he said, his voice rough with disuse. “In the jungle, the truth is that the leopard is hungry and the gazelle is slow. In London, the truth is that the leopard is wearing a suit and the gazelle is pretending not to be eaten. I find the latter more exhausting.”

Julian Hale, the Poet, leaned forward. “You are all too literal,” he said, with the condescension of youth. “The leopard does not wear a suit; he wears a mask. And the gazelle does not pretend; she performs. We are all actors in a play that has no script, and the only audience is our own vanity.”

Lady Ashworth, who had been listening with the intensity of a hawk, interjected softly. “But who is writing the play, Mr. Hale?”

“No one,” said the Poet. “That is the horror of it. We write it as we go, and we are too busy looking at the pen to see the ink staining our fingers.”

The second course was served: a pheasant, roasted with a perfection that bordered on the obscene. The atmosphere had shifted. The initial politeness had curdled into a kind of aggressive curiosity. The Bishop, emboldened by the wine, turned to the Actress.

“Mrs. Vane, I understand that your latest role required you to portray a woman of... unconventional morals. Is it not true that to play the sinner, one must first believe in the sin? Or is it merely a matter of adjusting the lighting?”

Mrs. Vane’s fan snapped shut. “My Lord, to play the sinner, one must believe in the audience’s gullibility. As for the sin, I find it is far more comfortable than virtue. Virtue requires a constant, exhausting vigilance. Sin is merely a lapse in attention. I have never fallen into virtue; I have only stumbled out of sin.”

The Explorer chuckled, a dry, rattling sound. “I have seen women in the Congo who would make your sin look like a Sunday school activity. But I confess, I am more interested in the Poet here. I read your book. It is a fascinating document of a mind that is trying to escape the body by first destroying the body’s reputation. It is a very English form of suicide.”

Julian Hale flushed, a color that spread from his neck to his ears. “You read it?” he asked, his voice trembling with a mixture of insult and delight. “And you understood it?”

“I understood the pain,” said Thorne. “Pain is the only universal language. You write about love, but you are really writing about the fear of being known. I have spent ten years in places where no one knows your name, and I can tell you that anonymity is not freedom. It is just a different kind of prison. You are in a glass house, Mr. Hale. Everyone can see you, but no one can touch you. It is a very cold place to live.”
