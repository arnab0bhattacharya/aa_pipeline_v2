In the labyrinthine bowels of the Hôtel de Ville, where the air was a thick stew of damp stone, ancient dust, and the metallic tang of hot iron, there lived a man who was a god to a hundred beating hearts. This man was Master Thibault, a clockmaker of such renown that the very pigeons who roosted upon the belfry knew the sound of his tread and the authority of his tuning-fork. He was a small, dry man, with a face as wrinkled and brown as a walnut shell, and eyes that seemed to hold the patient, inexorable movement of the escapement wheels he so lovingly tended. For fifty years, through monarchs and ministers, through parliaments and petitions, Master Thibault had kept the great clocks of Paris to the tune of the sun, and in return, they kept the city to the tune of his own unwavering soul.

His apprentice was a boy named Luc, a quick, clever lad of sixteen, whose hands were already calloused from the file and the lathe, and whose mind was a whirlwind of the new ideas that swept through the streets like a fever. He worshipped Master Thibault, but he also worshipped the Republic, and in the spring of 1793, these two devotions were destined for a collision as sharp and final as the striking of a hammer on an anvil. For the Republic, in its boundless and terrifying enthusiasm for the New, had declared war not only on kings and aristocrats, but on Time itself. The old calendar, with its saints’ days and its royalist associations, was to be abolished. In its place came the Republican Calendar, a rational arrangement of months with names like Brumaire and Floréal, and a week replaced by a ten-day period called a *décade*. And every clock in the Republic, from the grandest to the humblest, must be set to this new, revolutionary measure.

The man who brought this edict to the clock tower was Citizen Delacroix, a Representative on Mission, whose face was as sharp and hard as a shard of flint, and whose voice had the grinding authority of a millstone. He stood before the great clock-face, a giant of brass and iron that had counted the heartbeats of the city for three centuries, and he did not see a marvel of human ingenuity; he saw an instrument of tyranny.

“Citizen Clockmaker,” he began, his voice echoing in the vaulted chamber, “the Convention, in its wisdom, has decreed that the instruments of the people shall reflect the will of the people. This clock,” he gestured with a sweep of his arm that encompassed the entire mechanism, “is to be altered. The hours shall be divided into ten, the day into ten hours. The week shall consist of ten days. You will make it so.”

Master Thibault, who had been delicately applying a drop of oil to a gear no larger than a lady’s thumbnail, did not look up. He merely wiped his hands on his leather apron, a gesture as slow and deliberate as the movement he tended. “Citizen,” he said, his voice soft but carrying a weight that made the official’s own seem thin and shrill, “it cannot be done.”

Delacroix’s eyes narrowed. “It cannot be done? Or it *will* not be done? There is a difference, citizen, and one of them is treason.”

Now Luc, who had been standing by the lathe, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs, felt a cold dread seize him. He saw the danger as plainly as he saw the glitter of the official’s cocked hat. He stepped forward, his voice trembling. “Master, perhaps we could… we could try? The mathematics are complex, but if we were to…”

“Be silent, boy,” said Master Thibault, though not unkindly. He finally turned his gaze upon the Representative. “I do not speak of treason, Citizen Delacroix. I speak of truth. This clock does not measure the whims of men or the decrees of assemblies. It measures the turning of the Earth. It is a vessel for the truth of the heavens, and its hands are slaves to the sun. To ask me to make it lie is to ask a priest to teach blasphemy from his altar. I will not do it.”

The silence that followed was heavier than the great iron weights that hung in the shadows. Delacroix’s face, which had been merely severe, now became a mask of cold fury. “You are a relic, old man. A servant of the past. You keep your clocks running for every change of government, they say, and you think this makes you neutral. It does not. It makes you an enemy of the future. You have until the next *décade* to see reason. If this clock is not striking the hours of the Republic, then your own time will be at an end.” With a final, contemptuous glare, he turned on his heel and descended the winding stairs, his boots striking the stone like a death sentence.

When he was gone, Luc burst out, his voice cracking with fear and frustration. “Master! Why? For the sake of some gears and springs? For a face of painted metal? He will have you arrested! They will call you a counter-revolutionary and send you to the Conciergerie! Is a clock worth your neck?”

Master Thibault looked at the boy, and for the first time, a deep and abiding sadness filled his eyes. He walked over to the great clock and laid a hand upon its cold, iron casing, as a father might lay a hand on the shoulder of his son.

“Look at it, Luc,” he said, his voice a low murmur. “Listen to it. It is not a mere machine. It is a promise. A promise made to every soul in this city that the sun will rise and the sun will set. That there is a rhythm to life, a truth that is greater than any committee or any king.
