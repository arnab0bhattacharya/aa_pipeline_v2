The quarrel, when it came, did not erupt from any single, grievous fault, but was the culmination of a slow accretion of small anxieties, like the fine silt that gathers in a river-bed until the current is choked and diverted. For ten years, since the sudden death of their father, Josiah and Sarah Tatham had managed the draper's shop in the market town of Navenby with a quiet, unspoken understanding that was the envy of their neighbours. Their partnership was a thing of balanced weights: his shrewdness in the wholesale markets of Boston, her unerring eye for a colour and a customer's unspoken need; his strength in the stock-room, her nimble fingers at the counter and the ledger. The bell above the door announced not just a customer, but the arrival of a new problem for their shared life to solve, and they solved them together, their harmony a kind of provincial miracle.

The disruption to this equilibrium had a name: Amelia Croft. She was the daughter of the new excise officer, a young woman whose laughter was a trifle too loud for the quiet hum of the haberdashery and whose bonnets, Josiah noted with a lover's partiality, were always of the very latest London fashion. To Sarah, however, Amelia represented a perilous lightness, a frivolity that was the antithesis of the sturdy, practical virtues upon which their livelihood depended. She saw in the girl’s sparkling eyes not a reflection of Josiah’s own worth, but a predatory gleam, a desire for the comfort and consequence of being Mrs. Tatham, without any understanding of the labour that underwrote it.

The evening the subject was finally brought into the open was a Tuesday in late October, the air thick with the smell of damp wool and the coming winter. They sat in the little parlour behind the shop, the day's takings counted in neat piles on the table between them.

“She has consented to have me, Sarah,” Josiah said, his voice rough with an emotion he could not name, a mixture of triumph and trepidation. He did not look at his sister, but at the fire, where the coals glowed with a sullen, contained heat.

Sarah’s hands, which had been sorting through a sample book of shawls, went still. She looked at her brother, at the new softness around his mouth, and felt a cold dread settle in her stomach. “Amelia Croft?” she asked, though she knew the answer as surely as she knew the price of a yard of good broadcloth.

“There is no other,” he said, and now he turned to her, his gaze pleading for an understanding she could not give.

“Josiah, you cannot,” she said, her voice low and trembling with the effort of control. “You do not know what you are doing. That girl is a dandelion clock—pretty for a moment, and then blown away by the first breath of trouble. She knows nothing of the world, nothing of work.”

“She is gentle and she is kind,” he retorted, his own voice rising. “Is it a crime to wish for some gentleness in my life? Must it be all ledgers and ledgers and the smell of calico until I am in my grave?”

“Gentleness!” Sarah’s voice was sharp now, a shard of ice. “She is shallow, Josiah. She sees the shop and the house and thinks it a fine thing to be mistress of. She does not see the early mornings, the anxious accounts, the constant, grinding care it takes to keep it all from ruin. She will be a millstone, and she will drag you down with her.”

“And what of your happiness?” he shot back, stung into a cruelty he did not intend. “Is it your happiness to have me a bachelor for ever, tied to your apron-strings? You speak of sacrifice, but it is I who have sacrificed, staying here when I might have sought my fortune elsewhere.”

The words hung in the air, ugly and unforgivable. Sarah flinched as if struck. “My happiness,” she said, her voice dropping to a near-whisper, “is in seeing you secure. In seeing this house, our father’s house, continue as it is. But you would throw it all away. The shop cannot support two households, Josiah. You know it as well as I do. If you marry, one of us must go, and it will not be me. I will not be turned out of my home for a whim.”

“Then it is you who would sacrifice me,” he said, his face pale and set. “You would have me give up the one chance of my own life, so that you may keep your own. You think my happiness depends upon your sacrifice, but it is the other way about. You think you are protecting me, but you are only protecting yourself from change.”

This was the core of their tragedy, a profound and tragic misconception wherein each, in their desperate love for the other, believed themselves the sole guardian of a shared future, and saw the other’s happiness as a treasure to be preserved at any cost—even the cost of their own heart’s desire. Josiah saw Sarah’s spinsterhood as a cage he had built for her, and marriage as the key. Sarah saw Josiah’s attachment as a precipice, and her opposition as the only barrier keeping him from destruction.

Their days thereafter were a masterpiece of silent misery. The shop became a stage for a play of profound unhappiness. They moved around each other with a painful, exaggerated politeness, their conversation confined to the price of ribbons and the arrival of a new consignment of linen. The bell above the door, once a cheerful summons, now seemed to toll for their estrangement. Customers remarked on the change, on the strained looks and the air of weary resignation that had settled over the shop like a fine dust.
