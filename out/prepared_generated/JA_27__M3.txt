Mr. Arthur Hollis, of Fenwick Hall in Norfolk, was a gentleman of very considerable fortune, and very advanced years; and as he had never married, and had no immediate descendants of his own, his wealth had long been the subject of eager speculation among a wide circle of relations. These relations, it must be owned, were not wanting in the common attentions of civility. His nephews and nieces, and their respective spouses, were assiduous in their inquiries after his health, and their visits to Norfolk were timed with a regularity that might have done credit to a more disinterested affection. They consulted his opinions on the management of his estates with a deference which they would not have thought of showing to their own fathers, and they laughed at his stories with a promptitude that bore no relation to their intrinsic humour.

Mr. Hollis, however, was not a man to be long deceived by the appearance of regard, where the reality was wanting. He had lived too long in the world, and observed too much of human nature, to mistake the anxiety of an heir for the tenderness of a kinsman. He knew that his nephews thought of his gout with a concern which was closely connected with their own prospects, and that his nieces inquired after his sleep with a reference to their own future establishment. This conviction had been growing upon him for some years, and had at length produced in him a settled indifference to them all, which was only interrupted by occasional flashes of satirical amusement.

Among his nearer relations, there were two who were considered to have the strongest claims upon his favour. His nephew, Mr. Clement Hollis, a man of good address but embarrassed circumstances, was generally looked upon as the heir presumptive; and his niece, Mrs. Brandon, the widow of a younger son, had long flattered herself that her assiduity in writing letters, and her skill in managing his domestic arrangements, would secure to her a very handsome provision. But Mr. Hollis had a way of thinking very much his own; and the partiality of a man who is determined to be guided only by his own judgment, is not always to be calculated upon by those who think they have the best right to direct it.

It was in the autumn of the year 1809, when the family party were assembled at Fenwick Hall for his birthday, that Mr. Hollis, after looking round the table with a composure which nothing could disturb, and waiting for the general hum of conversation to subside, took occasion to make a communication which was not a little startling to those who heard it. He observed, with his usual dryness, that he had for some time past been reflecting on the disposal of his property, and that he had at length come to a resolution on the subject. He was aware, he said, that it had been generally supposed he meant to leave his estate to his nephew Clement; but he was sorry to disappoint expectations, however reasonable, when they were founded on a principle which he could not approve. He had therefore determined to bequeath the whole of his fortune, with the exception of a few legacies to old servants, to a charitable institution for the maintenance of decayed gentlemen, which had lately been established in the county.

The effect of this declaration may be more easily conceived than described. Mrs. Brandon turned pale, and was obliged to leave the room; and Mr. Clement, after a few moments of silence, during which he appeared to be collecting his thoughts, entered into a very elaborate defence of his own merits, and a very warm expostulation against his uncle's decision. But Mr. Hollis was not to be moved. He heard him with perfect patience, and replied only that he was much obliged to him for his advice, but that he had already made his will, and did not intend to alter it.

From that period, a very sensible change took place in the demeanour of the Hollis family towards their aged relative. Their visits became less frequent, and their letters less elaborate. Mr. Clement found that pressing engagements in town required his presence, and Mrs. Brandon discovered that her own affairs demanded her immediate attention. The good people of the neighbourhood, who had been accustomed to see the carriages of the nephews and nieces at Fenwick Hall almost every week, soon began to observe that they were no longer to be met with in that vicinity; and the general opinion was, that Mr. Hollis had behaved with a degree of caprice and unkindness, which, however it might be palliated by his age and infirmities, was not to be justified by any principle of reason or justice.

In the midst of this general desertion, there was one person, however, who did not forsake him. This was his ward, Miss Eleanor Ashworth, a young lady of nineteen, who had been left to his care by a friend, and who had resided at Fenwick Hall since her childhood. Miss Ashworth was a girl of more observation than wit, and more good sense than pretension. She had never been a favourite with the Hollis family, who considered her as an intruder upon their inheritance; and she had too much discernment not to perceive, that whatever kindness she received from Mr. Hollis was not likely to be increased by any arts of her own. She had always been treated by him with more kindness than by his relations; but she knew that she was not included in his will, and she had too much honesty to wish to be so, and too much pride to endeavour to secure by flattery what she could not claim by right.
