The city of Bellamora is proud of many things—its violet evenings, its marble bridges, the sweet and slightly dishonest smell of its canals—but of nothing is it prouder than the cathedral of Saint Veridiana, which for three hundred years has lifted its pale spires into the sky like a prayer that has forgotten how to end. Within, the light falls through windows of garnet and amber, staining the cold flagstones with the colours of a spilled chalice, and the air is heavy with the dust of dead incense and the whispers of a thousand forgotten saints. It is a place where time seems to have fallen asleep in a chair, and where the most devout members of the congregation will tell you, in lowered voices, that the great organ in the western loft has not been played for a hundred years. It is a magnificent, silent beast of blackened oak and tarnished pipes, a monument to a music no one living has ever heard.

On the eve of the Nativity, a fine, dry snow began to fall upon Bellamora, powdering the shoulders of the stone angels and turning the city into a confection of sugar and shadow. The bishop, a man of considerable girth and greater caution, named Monsignor Ascanio Velluti, had retired early, having delivered a sermon on the virtues of silence that was universally admired and universally unheeded. The great doors of the cathedral were locked, the candles extinguished, and a profound peace settled over the nave, broken only by the furtive scuttling of a mouse and the distant, melancholy cry of a gondolier.

It was in this silence that the organ began to play.

It was not a sound that belonged to the world of men. It began as a low, subterranean tremor, a sigh from the very stones of the foundation, and swelled into a melody of such strange, crystalline purity that the few pigeons roosting in the rafters awoke and stirred with a sound like rustling silk. The music was not solemn, nor was it particularly pious. It was a thing of impossible grace, a cascade of notes that glittered and turned like a flock of silver birds, a harmony that spoke of forgotten gardens and unrequited loves, of a beauty so absolute it was almost cruel. It was the sound of a soul, and it was playing for no one.

In a small, cold cell adjoining the sacristy, a boy named Elio lay awake. He was the youngest of the cathedral choir, a child of perhaps nine years, with hair the colour of damp straw and eyes that held the unnerving, placid gaze of one who is accustomed to being overlooked. He was not a particularly good boy, nor a particularly wicked one; he was merely a boy who could not sleep. The music, when it came, did not frighten him. It seemed to him, in the way of children, a perfectly natural thing, like the blooming of a flower or the falling of a star. It was a secret, and secrets, to Elio, were the most valuable things in the world.

He slipped from his cot, his bare feet silent on the stone floor, and wrapped his thin blanket around his shoulders like a king’s ermine. He knew every loose flagstone and every creaking hinge of the old cathedral, and he moved through the shadows like a ghost, a small, determined ghost in a nightshirt. He unlatched the small door that led from the sacristy into the ambulatory, and the music grew louder, more insistent, coiling around the pillars and filling the vaulted darkness with its shimmering, impossible light.

He crept to the edge of the choir stalls and peered into the nave. The great organ was a silhouette against the faintly luminous windows, its pipes gleaming with a cold, internal fire. And there, before the console, sat a figure.

It was not a man. It was, Elio thought, a creature woven from the moonlight and the shadows, a slender form in a cloak the colour of a winter sky just before dawn. Its hands, pale as ivory, moved over the keys with a terrible, effortless precision, and its face was turned away, lost in the ecstasy of its own creation. The music was not being played; it was being remembered, summoned forth from the silence where it had slept for a century.

“You are not supposed to be here,” said a voice, and Elio started. The bishop, Monsignor Velluti, stood in the aisle, a heavy cloak thrown over his nightshirt, a silver lamp trembling in his hand. His face, usually a mask of benign authority, was pale with a fear that was not entirely holy. He had been awakened by the music, and he had come to put a stop to it, as he put a stop to all things that he could not explain in a sermon.

The figure at the organ did not turn. The music did not falter. It seemed to swell in answer, a sound of such triumphant, heartbreaking beauty that the bishop’s lamp shook and cast wild, dancing shadows on the walls.

“This is the work of the devil,” the bishop whispered, his voice thick with a terror he could not name. “It is a snare, a temptation. No one is to listen. Do you hear me, boy? It is forbidden.”

He turned his stern, frightened gaze upon Elio. “Go back to your bed,” he commanded. “Stop your ears. This is not for us. It is a vanity, a beautiful and wicked thing. It is the music of the world, and we are not of the world.”

Elio looked from the bishop’s frightened face to the solitary, shadowy figure at the organ. The music was not wicked. It was the most honest thing he had ever heard. It was more real than the bishop’s sermons, more real than the cold stone, more real than the silence that had preceded it. It was a truth that had been locked away for a hundred years, and he, Elio, a small boy in a threadbare nightshirt, was its only witness.
