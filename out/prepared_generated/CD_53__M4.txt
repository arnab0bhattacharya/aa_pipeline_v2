The mail-coach set me down at the cross-roads in the grey of a November afternoon, and I stood for a moment in the churned clay and the thin drizzle, with my carpet-bag in my hand, looking about me at a country I had not seen for fifteen years. Fifteen years! I had gone out a lad with his whole fortune in a bundle and a shilling, and I came back—so the papers had it, and so the folk of the neighbourhood were pleased to repeat—a gentleman of substance, with money in the Funds and a house in Sydney town. And yet, as I stood there, with the rain beading on the shoulders of a coat that had cost me more guineas than the whole parish could raise in a twelvemonth, I felt as small and as foolish as the boy who had once run barefoot down that very lane after a runaway pig.

The village of Wrenfold lay a mile beyond, and I walked it, for I had a fancy to come upon it afoot, as I had gone from it. The hedges were the same hedges, only older and more ragged; the pollard willows stood where they had always stood, with their knuckled fists held up against the sky; and the brook, when I came to the plank bridge, still went muttering over its stones as though it had a grudge against somebody and could not forget it. But the people—the people were changed, or I was, or the world was; and I had not gone a hundred yards into the village street before I found it out.

The first man I met was one Abel Gedge, who had been my father’s neighbour and my own companion in a hundred boyish scrapes. He was leaning over his gate now, a stout, red-faced, prosperous man, with a brood of children about his knees; and when I stopped and held out my hand, and said, ‘Abel! Abel Gedge! don’t you know me?’ he looked me up and down—at my fine coat, my gold-headed cane, my portmanteau—and he took off his hat and scraped, and said, ‘I ask your pardon, sir; I’m sure I’m very honoured; but I don’t rightly recollect—’ And when I told him who I was, and would have shaken him by the hand, he held his own behind him, and shuffled, and said that he had heard tell of my good fortune, and was sure he was very glad, and hoped I was very well; and then he called to his wife to come in out of the rain, and went in himself, and shut the gate.

I stood in the wet street, with my hand still out, as it were, and the rain running off the end of it. I had come back to Wrenfold expecting—I know not what; a welcome, I suppose; a shout, a clapping of hands, an old friend’s grip. I had forgotten that a man who goes away poor and comes back rich is a kind of walking reproach to those who stayed behind and did neither.

I went on. There was the shop where I had bought my first slate-pencil; the woman who kept it was dead, and her daughter, who did not know me, sold me a paper of pins in a voice as sharp as the pins themselves. There was the smithy, with a new smith at the anvil, who looked at me as though I were a tax-gatherer. There was the green, and the old elm with the bench beneath it, where the old men used to sit and smoke and tell lies about the wars; and there was one old man sitting there now, all alone, and he was so old that he did not so much as lift his head when I passed. And there, at the corner, was the school-house—the same low, red-roofed, whitewashed school-house, with the same twisted pear-tree at the gate, and the same cracked bell in the little wooden turret; and at the window, with a book in her hand, sat the schoolmistress.

Her name was Miss Priscilla Wardle, and she had taught me my letters, and I had not been an apt scholar. I remembered her as a tall, pale, severe young woman, with a voice that could cut like a knife, and a way of looking at a boy over her spectacles that made his very shoelaces tremble. She had rapped my knuckles a hundred times, and I had hated her for it, and had once put a live frog in her desk; and she had kept me in after school, and had made me learn a whole psalm by heart, and had then—I remember it now—given me a slice of bread and jam out of her own dinner, because, as she said, I looked hungry, and a boy cannot learn on an empty stomach.

She was sitting at the window now, and she looked up as I came to the gate, and she put down her book, and she came to the door and stood there, peering at me through her spectacles, which were the same spectacles, or their twin. She was older, of course; her hair was grey, and her back a little bent, and her gown was of a plain grey stuff, and her hands were thin; but her eyes were the same eyes, bright and quick and honest.

‘Well, ma’am,’ said I, ‘I’ve come back, you see. And I’ve brought you a book or two, and a shawl from the Indies, and—’

But I got no further, for she had stepped out into the rain, and had taken me by both hands, and was looking up into my face with a kind of wonder and a kind of joy that made my throat ache.
