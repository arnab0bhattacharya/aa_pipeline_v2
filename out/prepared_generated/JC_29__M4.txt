It was in the third year of our residence at Santa Clara that the trouble began, though the trouble, as I have come to understand such matters, had been preparing itself long before, in the slow germination of a seed that no eye could see. The estate lay in a fold of the hills, a green and somnolent pocket of the world, where the coffee trees stood in their ordered ranks like a disciplined army at rest, and the heat came down at midday with the weight of a hand pressed upon the forehead. My husband, Edward Harrowby, an engineer by training and a man of that practical, unillusioned temper which the profession breeds, had come out to manage the property for a company in London whose directors he had never seen and whose confidence he regarded as a sacred trust. He was a good man, I believe, in the way that a well-regulated clock is good—accurate, dependable, and utterly without imagination. It did not occur to him that the valley might contain other presences than those of labour and profit.

The priest came into our lives as such men do—quietly, without announcement, a figure in a dusty cassock appearing at the gate one evening when the light had gone the colour of old brass and the parrots were screaming in the ceibas. His name was Father Ignacio Mendoza, and he had been sent, he said, to serve the scattered faithful of the district, though I suspected even then that his bishop had been glad of an excuse to remove him from the capital. He was a small man, brown as a nut, with hands that had known labour and eyes that held the patient irony of one who has seen too much of human folly to be surprised by it. He spoke English after a fashion, with a rolling of the r's and a tendency to invert his sentences in a manner that gave his conversation a certain formal gravity, as though he were translating from a language older and more ceremonious than our own.

Edward did not dislike him—that would be to credit my husband with an intensity of feeling he did not possess—but he regarded him with the mild suspicion of a man who sees no use for a functionary whose duties cannot be expressed in terms of output and cost. "The man's harmless enough," he would say, "but I cannot have him about the place. The peons will be wanting holidays and saints' days and God knows what. It's bad for discipline." And so the priest came seldom, and then only in the afternoon, when Edward was in the fields, and sat with me on the verandah while the shadows lengthened across the gravel and the mountains turned the colour of violets in the fading light.

It was there, in those hours of heat and silence, that I came to know him. He had a way of speaking that was not like the speech of other men—not the brisk exchange of information nor the easy currency of gossip, but something slower, more deliberate, as though each word were a stone set carefully into a wall that must stand against weather. He told me of his youth in the seminary, of the doubts that had assailed him and the faith that had survived them, not as a triumphant certainty but as a habit of the soul, a leaning toward the light even when the light is hidden. "We are not asked to see, señora," he said once, "only to walk in the dark without stumbling. And for that, one needs—how do you say—the patience of the feet."

I do not know what I gave him in return. Perhaps only the attention of a woman starved for conversation, for the sound of a voice that dealt in ideas rather than in the price of coffee. Edward was kind, but his kindness was like his engineering—a matter of stresses and tolerances, of things properly adjusted and maintained. He did not know what to do with a wife who read novels and asked questions about the nature of God. He would pat my hand and say, "You think too much, my dear. It's the climate." And perhaps he was right. Perhaps it was the climate, the heavy air that pressed upon the mind and made it turn inward, seeking meanings where a saner atmosphere would have found only the ordinary business of living.

The revolution came to us, as such things do, not as a thunderclap but as a rumour, a distant murmur that grew by degrees into the sound of horses and the crack of rifles. The President—a man whose name I have forgotten, though I remember his portrait in the government house at the capital, a fat man with a jewelled order and eyes like a lizard's—had been overthrown by a general whose name I never learned. The peons gathered in the evenings to discuss the matter in low voices, and there were meetings in the village that Father Ignacio attended, not as a leader but as a presence, a quiet figure in the shadows whose silence seemed to give the others courage.

Edward forbade him the house on a Tuesday, in the dry season, when the dust hung in the air like a veil and the heat was a living thing that pressed against the walls. "I cannot have it," he said. "The man is a revolutionary. I have it on good authority. He is preaching sedition to the Indians, and I will not have my wife mixed up in it." I protested—I do not remember what I said, only that my voice sounded thin and futile against the weight of his certainty. He listened with the patience of a man who has already decided, and when I had finished he said, "You do not understand these people. They are children. They need a firm hand." And that was the end of it, as far as he was concerned.
