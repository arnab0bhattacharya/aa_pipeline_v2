The rain had not ceased for three days when Mr. Elias Thorne arrived at St. Cuthbert’s in Marchett. It was a town of grey stone and grey skies, its streets running with water that carried the smell of tanneries and coal smoke down toward the sluggish river. The living had been vacant for a year, and the parishioners, Thorne soon learned, had grown accustomed to their own devices. The old curate, Mr. Pashley, had held the services in a manner that suggested God was a distant landlord not to be troubled with trifles, and the churchwardens had kept the accounts with a looseness that bordered on the artistic.

Thorne was thirty-two, with a thin face and a habit of leaning forward when he spoke, as though he expected the world to meet him halfway. He had come from a curacy in the fens, where the congregation had been small and the poverty large, and he had arrived in Marchett with a head full of schemes. The first of these, which he announced to Mr. Pashley over a supper of cold mutton and boiled potatoes, was a lending library for the working men of the town.

“Books,” said Mr. Pashley, who was sixty and had the complexion of a damp wall. “For the colliers and the weavers.”

“And the framework knitters,” said Thorne. “And the clerks and the apprentices. There is a great hunger for improvement, Mr. Pashley. I have seen it. A man who reads is a man who thinks, and a man who thinks is a better Christian.”

Mr. Pashley chewed his mutton with the air of one who had heard such sentiments before and had outlived them. “You will find,” he said, “that in Marchett every gift has a string tied to it. Sometimes the string is a ribbon. Sometimes it is a rope.”

Thorne, who was young and had the confidence of the recently ordained, smiled and said he would take his chances.

The following week he called upon Mr. Josiah Crane, the town’s leading manufacturer of worsted cloth, whose mill stood by the river and whose chimneys could be seen from the church tower. Mr. Crane was a broad man with a broad accent and a way of looking at you as though he were calculating the profit to be had from your conversation. He received Thorne in his counting-house, a room that smelled of ink and wool-fat, and listened to the proposal with his head tilted to one side.

“A library,” he said, when Thorne had finished. “For the men.”

“For the men and their families, Mr. Crane. I believe it would be a great benefit to the town.”

Crane was silent for a moment. Then he said, “I will give you fifty pounds. And a room above the mill office, if you want it. It is warm and dry and the men pass by it twice a day.”

Thorne was so surprised that he nearly forgot to thank him. Fifty pounds was a fortune. He stammered his gratitude, and Crane waved a hand.

“I am a great believer in improvement,” said Crane. “A man who reads is a man who stays at home of an evening. He does not go to the alehouse and get ideas into his head about unions and the like. He improves his mind and he keeps his peace. You understand me, Mr. Thorne.”

Thorne understood him perfectly. He also understood that he had just accepted a gift with a string attached, and that the string was the colour of Crane’s worsted. But he told himself that a library was a library, and that the books would speak for themselves.

The books did not arrive all at once. The first came from Mr. Crane himself—a set of sermons by a divine whose name Thorne did not recognise, bound in calf and smelling faintly of the mill. Then came a donation from Mrs. Ashby, the widow of a former mayor, who sent a box of novels that she described as “suitable for young persons,” though Thorne found, upon inspection, that they were mostly tales of fallen women and sudden inheritances. Mr. Pashley, when he heard of the scheme, pursed his lips and donated a volume of tracts against popery, which he said had been very effective in his youth.

The library opened in the room above the mill office on a Tuesday in March. Twelve men came the first evening, and Thorne read to them from a book about the voyages of Captain Cook. They listened with the polite attention of men who were not sure what was expected of them. The next week, fifteen came. The week after that, twenty. Thorne began to feel that he had been right, that the hunger was real, that Marchett was not so grey as it looked.

It was in April that the strings began to show.

Mr. Crane sent word that he would like the library to subscribe to a periodical called The Manufacturer’s Advocate, which he described as “a very improving publication.” Thorne, who had seen a copy and found it to be chiefly an argument against the combination of workers, wrote back politely that he would consider it. The next week, Crane’s foreman appeared at the library door with a list of books that he said Mr. Crane thought would be “more suitable” for the men than the novels of Mr. Dickens, which had lately become popular. Thorne thanked him and put the list in a drawer.

Mrs. Ashby, meanwhile, had begun to take an interest in the library’s moral tone. She called upon Thorne one afternoon and spent an hour examining the shelves. She was a small woman with a sharp nose and a way of sniffing at books as though she could detect impropriety by smell.

“I see you have Mr. Fielding,” she said. “And Mr. Smollett. These are not books for working men, Mr. Thorne. These are books for—for persons of a certain class.”
