The train, which had seemed to him all the way from Rotherham a kind of fiery beast dragging him through the dark, slackened, sighed, and stood still, and there was the platform at Lavington, absurdly small, absurdly quiet, with a single lamp making a yellow puddle on the stones, and beyond it, beyond the low fence, the fields going away and away into a dusk that was not yet night—a green dusk, soft, unhurried, smelling of wet earth and something else, something sweet. He stood a moment with his bag in his hand, having forgotten, quite, what he had come for; then remembered, and straightened his tie, and felt the collar too tight, and the collar stud, which had been a source of anxiety all day, pressing into his throat like a small hard thought.

"Mr. Threlfall?" A boy had come out of the shadow—no, a man, a young man in a soft collar, with a lantern, which he did not need, and a manner so easy that it seemed to have been born with him, like his thick fair hair. "I'm Arthur. The trap's just here. Awful train, isn't it? They stop at every hedge. Give me your bag."

And the bag was taken from his hand before he could protest, and he was following the lantern down a lane where the hedges were high and dark and the sky between them a pale green ribbon pricked with stars, and the horse's hooves went clip-clop, clip-clop, and Arthur talked of the weather, of a horse that had gone lame, of a dance at Marlborough, and Threlfall said "Yes" and "Really" and "I see," and thought: this is how they live, these people, in this green sleep, and the words came off Arthur's tongue like water off a stone, smooth, without effort, while his own tongue lay in his mouth like a dry biscuit.

Then the house. It rose out of the dark—no, it did not rise, it was simply there, long and low and grey, with windows that were gold squares in the grey, and a smell of woodsmoke, and a dog barking somewhere, and a woman's voice calling "Arthur? Is that you?" and the trap stopped, and he was down, and his bag was down, and he was in a hall that was bigger than his whole schoolroom, with a fire that was bigger than the hall, and a clock ticking somewhere in the deeps of the house, and the dog, a great shaggy thing, nosing at his trousers, and Mrs. Ellwood coming forward with both hands out.

"Mr. Threlfall! How good of you to come all this way. You must be frozen. Come to the fire. Arthur, take his coat. There. Now. You're at the very heart of the fire, and you shan't move till you're warm."

She was tall, and her hair was going grey in a way that seemed deliberate, and her voice was like the fire—warm, and crackling a little, and giving off light. He stood where she put him, and felt the heat on his shins, and looked about him. There were books everywhere, and a bowl of hyacinths, and a portrait of a man in a red coat, and a case of butterflies on the wall, and a piano, open, with music on it, and a cat asleep on a chair, and the whole of it was not arranged, he thought, it had simply happened, like the fields, like the dusk, like the ease of these people. And he thought of his own room at home, with its two chairs and its gas ring and its shelf of books, and the window that looked out on the yard, and the yard that looked out on nothing.

At dinner he sat between Mrs. Ellwood and a girl called Violet, who was a cousin, and who said, "Do you hunt?" and he said, "No," and she said, "Oh," and turned to the man on her other side, and Threlfall was left with his soup. But Mrs. Ellwood asked him about his school, and he told her about the boys, and the examinations, and the scholarship he had got for one of them, and she said, "How splendid!" and he felt for a moment that he was a person, that he had done something, that the collar stud was not so tight. Then the talk went on around him, about people he did not know, and places he had not seen, and a horse called Pericles, and a ball at some house or other, and he sat and ate and listened, and the firelight moved on the silver and the glass, and he thought: this is the life, this is how it is done, and he felt a kind of hunger that was not for food.

On Saturday they walked. He was given a stick, and a pair of boots that were too large, and they went over the downs, and the sky was enormous, and the larks went up and up, and Arthur pointed out a hawk, and Violet said, "Oh, look, the first primrose!" and there was a great deal of laughing and calling to one another, and Threlfall walked a little behind, and felt the wind on his face, and thought of the mill chimneys at home, and the smoke, and the grey faces of the men coming out of the works, and it seemed to him that he had come into another world, a world that had been going on all the time, quietly, without him, and he was permitted to see it, for a little, and then would be sent back.

On Sunday evening there was a dinner party. He had not known there was to be a dinner party. He had thought it would be the family, and the fire, and perhaps a game of cards.
