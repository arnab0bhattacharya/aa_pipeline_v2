The frost lay thick upon the heath, a brittle crust that cracked under my boots with a sound as sharp as a whip-crack. The sky was a sheet of iron, unbroken by cloud, pressing down upon the desolation of the Lincolnshire moor with a weight that seemed to crush the breath from my lungs. I did not weep. To weep would be to admit that I had lost something, and I had not lost my self-respect; I had merely been cast out like a dog from the kennel, for no fault of my own, but because I was a convenient sacrifice to the vanity of a coward and the suspicion of a tyrant.

My name is Eliza Thorne, and until this morning, I was the governess at Ashby Hall. Now, I am a wanderer with a small parcel of clothes, three shillings and sixpence in my pocket, and a heart that burns with a fire that no wind can extinguish.

I had been in Ashby for only three months. Master Ashby, the son of the late Lord Ashby, was a young man of twenty, possessed of a handsome face and a soul as empty and pale as the winter sky. He had watched me with eyes that held a dull, persistent hunger, not for my mind, but for my obedience. When I rebuffed his advances—gently, firmly, with the dignity of my station—he had not raged; he had simply turned his gaze away and whispered a lie to his father. He had said I encouraged him. He had said I was a seductress, a creature of low morals who sought to entrap an heir. It was such a easy thing for him to say, and so easy for the old man to believe, for who was I but a poor woman, dependent on their charity, to challenge the word of a gentleman?

The dismissal had been brief. There was no conversation, no interrogation. The butler had handed me my wages and a note of no reference, his face a mask of polite indifference. I had walked out into the cold, my steps steady, my chin high. I would not give them the satisfaction of seeing me crumble. I would not give Master Ashby the satisfaction of seeing me beg.

But as I walked, the memory of his face haunted me. I saw him standing by the window in the library, his hands clasped behind his back, his eyes avoiding mine. He knew I was innocent. He knew the truth. And yet, he had chosen to destroy me rather than speak the word that would save me. It was not cowardice alone; it was a profound and terrible indifference to the suffering of others, a spiritual death that left only the shell of a man. I felt a fierce, almost violent anger rise in my chest, a red heat that threatened to consume me. How dare he? How dare he stand there, safe in his warm rooms, while I walked into the freezing dark?

The path wound through the gorse, the spines catching at my skirts. The wind howled in my ears, a voiceless scream of the earth. I thought of my father, a clergyman who had taught me that virtue is its own reward, that justice is the foundation of the universe. But where was justice now? It was buried under the snow, or perhaps it had never existed at all in this cold, gray world.

I reached the edge of the estate just as the sun began to set, a blood-red disc sinking behind the trees. The shadow of the great yews stretched long across the grass, like the arms of a ghost. I paused, leaning against a stone wall, and allowed myself a moment of stillness. The cold had numbed my hands, but it had not numbed my mind. I was still here. I was still myself.

A figure appeared on the path, walking towards me. It was a young man, dressed in a dark coat, his face pale and drawn. It was Master Ashby. He stopped a few yards away, his eyes fixed on me with an expression I could not read. It was not shame, not quite. It was something worse: a kind of detached curiosity, as if he were observing a specimen of rare, unpleasant insect.

"Miss Thorne," he said, his voice soft and unsteady.

I straightened my back. "Mr. Ashby."

"I did not mean to do you harm," he began, his eyes shifting to the ground. "My father... he is a difficult man. He believes what he is told. I could not... I could not stand against him."

"Could you have?" I asked, my voice low and steady. "If I had been a woman of rank, would you have spoken? If I had been a man, would you have defended my honor?"

He flinched. The question struck him like a physical blow. He looked up, and for a moment, I saw the truth in his eyes: the horror of what he had done. But it was a fleeting glimpse, quickly buried under the sediment of self-preservation.

"I am sorry," he said. "Please, let me call a carriage for you. Let me send you to town."

"I do not require a carriage," I said. "I require the truth. But you have already shown me that you do not have it."

I turned and walked on. I did not look back. I could feel his gaze on my back, heavy and accusatory, but I did not care. I was walking towards the town of Grantham, twelve miles away. The road was long, and the night was coming on, but I did not feel afraid. I felt a strange, fierce clarity. I had been stripped of my position, my home, my reputation, but I had not been stripped of my soul. And that, I knew, was the only thing that mattered.
