The coach set him down at the bottom of the Briggate, where the smell of the river met him first—that familiar compound of mud and malt and the slow rot of willow-roots, which no London air, however thick with the smoke of sea-coal, had ever quite effaced from his memory. Josiah Marston stood for a moment with his portmanteau at his feet, a slight, stooping figure of eight-and-twenty, whose town-cut coat and narrow-brimmed hat marked him already as something alien among the corn-chandlers' carts and the countrywomen with their butter-baskets; and it must be confessed that the smell of the river did not stir in him that soft tumult of home-coming which the poets, who have generally been careful to live elsewhere, assure us to be the invariable attribute of such returns. He was thinking, with the fatal facility of a mind long trained to reckon consequences, that the Briggate was narrower than he had supposed, and that the shop of Widdowson the bookseller—his father's old rival, whose window he could see already, with its dusty folios propped against the glass—had somehow shrunk in the interval, as places will that we have measured in childhood against the standard of our own importance.

His father was three days buried, and he had not been able to reach the town in time for the funeral. There was that in the circumstance which he felt to be a reproach, though nobody, he reflected, could reasonably have expected him to leave the shop in the middle of the quarter's accounts, with Mr. Thrale's temper what it was; and he had, besides, a certain consciousness—which he would not have confessed for the world, and which he set himself to analyse with the cold impartiality he was wont to apply to the motives of the persons in the books he handled—that his grief for the old man was not so much a pang as a pressure, a sense of duties suddenly thickened and complicated, like the air before a thunderstorm. He had loved his father after the manner of sons who are early sent from home: that is, he had loved him in the abstract, as a figure associated with certain maxims of thrift and certain Sunday dinners, and the vague, uncomfortable sense that one was always being weighed and found wanting.

The yard was sold. He learned it from the ostler at the King's Head, who told it him with that relish which the communication of a neighbour's misfortune never fails to produce in minds not much exercised by reflection: how the tanning business had been going down for years, since the newfangled ways came in and the big works at Nottingham undersold the little men; how the creditors had been patient, and then impatient; how the yard and the house and the drying-sheds had gone at last to a lawyer in the town, a Mr. Pickering, for a sum that would not pay the debts and leave anything for the widow. And where was his mother? The ostler thought she was with her cousin, Mrs. Hinde, in the Finkell—a street of small houses near the church, where the widows of tradesmen retired to live upon their memories and the interest of small legacies.

He found her in a parlour that was too small for the furniture she had brought into it. She was sitting by the fire, a woman of fifty-five, with the fine complexion and the delicate, querulous hands of one who had been a beauty in her day and had never quite forgiven the world for letting her grow old. She kissed him, and cried a little, and then, with that singular capacity for the immediate and practical which belongs to women whose lives have been narrowed to a point, she began at once to tell him of the difficulties of her situation: how Mrs. Hinde was kind, but had her own ways; how the tea was dearer here than in the old house, because one could not buy it in such quantity; how the parish was not what it had been, and the new vicar—a young man from Oxford, with a taste for candles—had no notion of visiting the poor of his flock as Mr. Toller used to do.

Josiah listened, and made the proper answers, and all the while there was growing in him a sense of suffocation. The room was too hot, and the tea was too strong, and his mother's voice went on and on, a thin, persistent stream of small grievances, and behind it all lay the fact which she had not named and which he could not bring himself to name—that he was now the support of this woman, and that his salary at Thrale's, which was eighty pounds a year and his keep, would not keep her in the manner to which she had been accustomed, even if he were to deny himself the books he bought, and the occasional evening at the theatre, and the subscriptions to the circulating library which were the very breath of his intellectual life.

It was not the money only. It was the whole question of what his life was to be. He had read, in the back room at Thrale's, during the long afternoons when the shop was empty, a great many books; and he had come to believe—with that touching confidence which young men of letters feel in the importance of their own impressions—that there was a work in him which the world would not willingly let die. He had planned, in the privacy of his own chamber, a history of the commerce of the Midlands, which should be no mere dry chronicle of bales and bargains, but a philosophical inquiry into the growth of towns, the manners of the trading classes, the slow transformation of the provincial mind under the influence of the printing-press and the turnpike-road. He had even written the first chapter, and had read it to a young man named Ellery, who was in the habit of writing for the magazines, and who had said it was very fine, and had advised him to send it to the *Edinburgh Review*. But the *Edinburgh Review* would not pay him for a year, if at all; and his mother must be kept from the parish.
