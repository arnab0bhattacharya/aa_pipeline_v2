The recognition came upon me—if I may put it so—not as a blow, but as a species of slow suffocation, a thickening of the already close and greasy atmosphere of that narrow Soho thoroughfare, as though the very air had turned to something foul and palpable in my throat. I had been standing, I remember, near the corner of a little street the name of which I have long since forgotten, or perhaps never knew, staring with that vacant fixity of the solitary man at the window of a shop where they sold, among other melancholy objects, stuffed birds under glass domes, and I was thinking—I was thinking of nothing at all, which is the peculiar privilege and the peculiar curse of the man whose life has been reduced to the mere act of continuing it—when I heard my name pronounced. Not the name I had borne in the days of my usefulness, but the other one, the one I had taken as a man takes a borrowed coat against the cold, and which had served me these fifteen years. It was spoken in a voice that seemed to carry with it a kind of dreadful cheerfulness, a heartiness that fell upon my ear like the sound of a door closing in an empty house.

“Why, it is you, is it not?” the voice said. “After all these years. I said to myself, I said, that is a face I know, though it has grown grey and thin, and the shoulders have a stoop to them that was not there before.”

I turned. He stood before me, a man in a decent black coat, somewhat shiny at the seams, with a soft hat pulled low over his brow, and a face that time and the prison had carved into a mask of lean and patient amiability. There was a scar, I noticed, a new one, running from the corner of his mouth to the jaw, and it gave to his smile a crooked and deliberate quality, as though the flesh itself had learned to be ironic. I knew him at once. I knew him by the way he held his head, a little to one side, as a man listens for something he expects to hear, and by the hands, which were large and still and hung at his sides with a terrible repose. His name—his name was Dobson, though that was not the name by which I had known him in the matter of the Clerkenwell affair, when he had been good enough to confide in me, and I had been good enough to confide in the police, and he had gone away for a considerable period to a place where the confidences of men are not encouraged.

“Dobson,” I said. My voice, I am afraid, was not steady. It had the thinness of a man who has lived too long alone with his caution.

“You remember,” he said, and his smile widened, and the scar whitened at its edge. “I am flattered. There are those who forget. But I do not forget. It is a faculty I have. I have had time to cultivate it, you understand. Time and quiet. And now I find myself in your neighbourhood, and I see you, and I think to myself, Dobson, here is an old acquaintance, and you have not many, and it is a cold evening, and perhaps he would do you the honour to dine with you. There is a little place, not far, where they do a tolerable steak, and the waiter is a man who knows when to be deaf. Will you come?”

What could I say? I have asked myself that question many times since, in the long nights when the house is still and the wind comes off the river with a sound like the dragging of chains. What could I say? To refuse would have been to admit a fear, and a man who admits fear to such a one as Dobson has already delivered himself into his hands. And so I said—I heard myself say it, with a kind of astonishment, as though the words belonged to another man—“I will come, Dobson. I will come with pleasure.” And we walked together, he keeping always a little to my left, so that I should not have to turn my head to see him, and he talked of the weather, and of the price of coal, and of the new buildings going up in the Strand, in the manner of a man who has all the small change of conversation in his pocket and is not afraid to spend it.

The restaurant was in a cellar, and the light was dim and yellow, and the air was thick with the smell of old fat and the sourness of wine. We sat at a corner table, and he ordered for us both, and he ate with a slow and methodical relish, as though each mouthful were a small triumph over the past. I could not eat. I drank, and the wine did nothing to warm me. He talked, and I listened, and it seemed to me that his talk was like the circling of a bird above some small and helpless creature in a field—not yet stooping, but patient, and certain of its hour.

“You are wondering,” he said at last, laying down his knife and fork and wiping his mouth with a deliberation that was almost ceremonial, “you are wondering why I have asked you here. You are thinking that I have a score to settle, and that this is a strange way to set about it. Is that not so?”

I said nothing. I could not.
