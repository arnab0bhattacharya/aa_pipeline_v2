The light, falling through the open shutters of the veranda, laid its gold in long stripes across the floor, and Miss Pargiter, who had been governess to half the good families of Shropshire, found it interfering with her embroidery. She lifted her work, a small square of linen, and held it so that the shadow fell upon it, for the sun was too bright to see the stitches by, and the blue thread was becoming a mere glare of colour. It was the hour before luncheon, when the pension at Menton, the Villa Armand, grew quiet, and the only sound was the distant, rhythmic clatter of a train passing along the coast, which seemed to beat out the very measure of the afternoon. A thought came to her—of the schoolroom at Crofton, of the slanted light on the slates, of a child’s hand, sticky with jam, pressing a primrose into her palm—and was gone again, dissolved in the warmth of the French sun. She was not unhappy; she was simply a figure in a landscape, a woman sewing, and the landscape, with its silver-green olives and its terraced hills, was content to hold her.

From his chaise-longue in the corner, Mr. Finch, who was recovering from a lung complaint and wore a shawl of grey wool even in the heat, watched the scene with the placid attention of a man to whom the world has become a slow-moving picture. His own thoughts moved no faster. They were of the same quality as the light, a slow, golden stream, in which images floated: his mother’s face, the feel of a leather-bound book, the precise way the ink dried on a page. He saw Miss Pargiter’s needle go in and out, in and out, a tiny, persistent pulse. He saw the dust motes turning in the shaft of light. He saw, beyond the veranda rail, the vast, glittering disc of the Mediterranean, which was not blue but a hundred colours, a shifting, breathing thing of silver and sapphire and jade. To be ill was to see these things, he thought, to see them with a clarity that health obscured with its business and its noise. He was grateful for the illness, in a way, for the quiet it had brought him, the long afternoons of watching, the sense of being held in a kind of amber. Mrs. Fanshawe, the widow, passed through the room then, a rustle of black bombazine and a scent of violets, and the disturbance was like a stone dropped in the still water of his mind. She paused at the rail, looking out at the sea, her back to them all, a silhouette of mourning against the glitter. Her daughter, Emily, a young woman with the unhappy, watchful eyes of a creature perpetually waiting, followed her, and stood a little behind, her hands clasped. There was a silence that was not empty but full of things unsaid—the price of coal, a letter from the solicitor, the dampness of the English winter they had fled.

It was the painter, Mr. Lyle, who broke it, not with words but with a sigh, a great, gusty exhalation of frustration. He was at the far end of the veranda, his easel set up, his canvas a muddle of grey and ochre. He had been trying all morning to catch the colour of the light on the lemons in the garden below, but the colour would not be caught. It was a living thing, it shifted and changed even as he looked, and his hand, which had once been sure and swift, now seemed clumsy, a thing of wood and bone. He scraped at the canvas with his palette knife, a sound of desecration, and felt the familiar despair settle upon him. He was failing. He knew it. The others knew it, too, he supposed. They looked at him with a kind of polite pity, the way one looks at a beautiful ruin. He saw their reflections in the window of the salon behind him: Miss Pargiter, a stooped shape of grey; Finch, a pale smudge in the corner; the Fanshawes, two dark, vertical lines. They were all waiting, he thought, waiting for the winter to pass, waiting for something to happen, waiting to die, perhaps. And he was waiting for a colour that would not come.

Then the luncheon bell rang, a sharp, silver summons that shattered the quiet. It was the signal for the small, daily drama of the pension to begin. Miss Pargiter laid her needle down, the blue thread trailing like a tiny vein. Mr. Finch drew his shawl tighter and prepared to be helped to his feet. Mrs. Fanshawe turned from the rail, her face composed into its customary expression of weary resignation. And Mr. Lyle wiped his hands on a rag, his canvas abandoned, his failure complete. They moved from the veranda into the dining room, where the long table was laid with white cloth and heavy silver, and the light was cooler, filtered through the leaves of a great fig tree. They took their places, the usual places, without speaking, for their habits were as fixed as the furniture. Mrs. Fanshawe sat at the head of the table, a queen in her own small, sorrowful kingdom. Emily sat to her right, her eyes on her plate. Miss Pargiter sat opposite, her back very straight. Mr. Finch was beside her, and Mr. Lyle took the seat at the far end, as if to distance himself from them all. The soup was served, a pale, creamy potage, and the sound of spoons against china was the only music. A thought passed from Mrs. Fanshawe to her daughter, a silent communication about the quality of the bread.
