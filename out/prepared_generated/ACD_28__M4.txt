The matter was first brought to my attention by a letter, and afterwards by the gentleman himself, who presented himself at our lodgings in Baker Street upon a wet Tuesday in the November of ’94. He was a spare, brown-faced fellow of fifty or thereabouts, with the flat back and the careful eye of one who has spent his life upon other men’s land, and he turned his cap in his hands as though he feared it might presume upon the furniture.

“My name is Adam Rusk,” said he, “and I am steward to Colonel Bewick, of Marcham Hall, near Ellesmere. I have come up on the early train, Mr. Holmes, and I would not have come at all if the Colonel had had his way. He will not have the police upon the place. He says it is a private matter, and that a fuss in the county would be worse than the loss. But I have lain awake three nights over it, sir, and I am not a man who lies awake.”

“You had best tell me the whole of it from the beginning,” said Holmes, pushing the tobacco-jar across to him. “Take your time, and do not omit the trifles, for it is usually the trifles that are worth the carriage.”

Rusk sat forward upon the edge of his chair. “There is a lake at Marcham, sir, some four acres in extent, that lies below the house in a hollow, with the old woods coming down to it on the north side. It is fed by springs, and it has never been known to fail, not in the driest summer. It is the Colonel’s pride, and there are trout in it that he has watched grow for twenty years. Now, on the night of Friday last, I was walking the home covert with my lamp, looking for a wire that had been cut, and I came out upon the upper path that overlooks the water. It was a still night, with no moon to speak of, and I heard what I took at first for the wind among the reeds. But there was no wind. It was the sound of water running, and running fast, as it runs from a sluice.”

“At what hour?”

“Close upon two in the morning. I went down through the trees quietly, for I did not know what I might find, and when I came out upon the bank I saw that the lake was down. There was a broad shelf of mud all round the western end, sir, glistening in the starlight, and the reeds were standing up out of it like the stubble in a shorn field. And yet there was no man to be seen, nor any boat, nor any pump that I could discover. I stopped and listened, and the running of the water stopped also, or seemed to. Then I went round the whole bank with my lamp, and found nothing but the mud and the wet stones. By the morning the water was back to its old level, or near enough that a careless eye would never have marked the difference. But I knew, and I measured the marks upon the stones.”

“You measured them,” said Holmes, with a quick lift of the head. “Good. How much?”

“Fourteen inches and a half at the western margin, Mr. Holmes. And the same again on the Saturday night, and again upon the Sunday, for I sat up with my lamp behind the boathouse and watched. It begins at the half after one and it is done by three. And there is never a soul to be seen.”

“You have examined the banks for the track of a pipe, or a trench?”

“I have, sir. There is no pipe and no trench. The water goes out of that lake as water goes out of a bath when the plug is drawn, and where the plug is I cannot tell you.”

Holmes sat silent for a moment, his long fingers pressed together, his eyes fixed upon the fire. “You have told me what you saw,” said he at last. “Now tell me what you have not told the Colonel.”

Rusk coloured a little. “There is a thing that has been in my mind, sir, and I am ashamed of it, for I am not a superstitious man. The oldest of the woodmen, a fellow named Cadwallader, that is near eighty and has been on the estate since he was a boy, tells me that when he was a lad there stood a chapel in that hollow, before the Colonel’s father had the valley flooded for the lake. A chapel of the old religion, sir, that had been shut up since the Reformation, and was said to have a crypt beneath it. The water was let in upon it in ’41, and the chapel was pulled down stone by stone, but the crypt, Cadwallader says, was never opened nor filled, and the water lies over it to this day. He will have it that it is the old crypt that is drinking the lake at night, and giving it back before morning, and he crosses himself when he says it.”

“And what do you say?”

“I say that water does not run uphill, sir, and that a crypt is a hole in the ground and not a pump. But I say also that I have stood on that bank at two in the morning and heard the water going out of the lake like a living thing, and I would be glad of a wiser head than mine.”

“You have done very well,” said Holmes, rising and reaching for his coat. “We shall go down to Shropshire by the afternoon train. I fancy that we shall not need to trouble the Colonel with the police, but I make no promise until I have seen the hollow.”
