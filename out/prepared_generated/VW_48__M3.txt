The heat lay upon the Nile like a pale beast breathing, and the dahabeah, which had ceased its throbbing an hour ago, seemed to Clara not so much moored as suspended — suspended in a bath of amber light that thickened towards the banks where the palms stood each in its separate column of shade. She sat beneath the awning with a book open on her lap which she had not been reading for twenty minutes. The letters dissolved. They became beetles, scarabs, the little blue figures one saw on the walls of tombs, and her eyes, lifting, followed the line of the western cliffs where the limestone caught the late sun and turned the colour of a bruised apricot, and she thought of him — of Farrant — as she had thought of him every few minutes since the morning, so that the thought had become a kind of ticking, a pulse beneath the slower pulse of the afternoon, and she could not have said whether it was the river that moved or the thought of him that moved the river.

Her father came up from the cabin, treading the boards with that careful weight he gave to all surfaces, as though testing the world's solidity. He had a letter in his hand. She saw the English stamp — the red, the grey — and felt the whole structure of the afternoon shift, as a house shifts on its foundations when a lorry passes in the street. But there was no street, only the Nile, and the mud-brick village beyond the reeds where a woman carried a water-jar in the curve of her hip.

"Edward writes," her father said, and sat down in the canvas chair beside her, crossing his legs. He drew out his spectacles, and the gesture — the deliberate unfolding of the case, the wiping of each lens with his handkerchief — was so utterly of the other world, the world of study and mahogany and the particular creak of the stair at Hartwell, that Clara felt a sickness rise in her which was not physical but temporal, as if she were being dragged backwards through months.

"He's been made partner," her father continued. He did not look at her. He looked at the letter, and then at the opposite bank. "His mother is well, and the garden — you remember the garden, the wisteria on the south wall — is coming on. He asks when we return." He paused, and the pause had a weight to it, a calculated blankness, and she knew that he was not asking but telling, and that the telling had been decided perhaps before the letter arrived, perhaps before they had left Cairo, perhaps — and here her mind turned on a dark pivot — before they had ever come at all.

"We shall leave next week," he said. "I have spoken to the captain. We can be in Cairo by Thursday and there is a P&O sailing on the twelfth."

The words fell into the heat and lay there. Clara looked at the water. A heron rose from the shallows and went up the sky in slow, deliberate strokes, and she watched it until it became a speck, and then nothing, and the sky closed over the place where it had been as water closes over a stone, and she thought: he is there, beyond those cliffs, in the valley, with the fragments, and the dust, and the painted eyes looking out of the dark. She had stood at the entrance of the tomb yesterday — was it yesterday? — and the air that came from below was dry and ancient and smelt of time itself, of centuries laid down like sediment, and Farrant had turned to her with the lamp in his hand, and his face in that light had been — she could not find the word — not beautiful, not handsome, but particular, drawn into a sharpness of attention that made every other face she had ever seen seem blurred, approximate, as if they had all been sketched in haste and only his had been finished.

"The tomb is late Eighteenth Dynasty," he had said, holding the lamp so that the light grazed the wall and the figures started out — the bearers with their trays of lotus, the dog with its curled tail, the great gold oval containing a name that had not been spoken in three thousand years. "We found the antechamber on Tuesday. The seals are intact." And then he had looked at her — not at her father, who stood behind coughing gently into his handkerchief, but at her — and had said, as if continuing a conversation they had already been having in some other place, "You understand, don't you, you see what it is — not the gold, not the things, but the hand. The hand that painted that line. Someone drew that curve of the lotus-stem three thousand years ago, and it is still the most living thing in this valley."

She had said — what had she said? Something. Something about the line, the sureness of it. But the words had been nothing, a shell thrown out to cover the enormous thing that was happening beneath, the recognition — for that is what it was, a recognition, not a discovery, as though she had known this quality of attention before and had been waiting for it, and now that she had found it she could not, would not, let it go. And yet she would let it go. She knew this too. Her father would take her home and she would marry Edward and the wisteria would flower on the south wall, and Farrant would remain here, in the dust and the painted dark, and she would — what? Carry the line of the lotus-stem inside her like a wound that could not be shown?

"Clara." Her father's voice came across the interval. "You are very quiet."
