It was in the nature of that fog, which had crept up from the river and settled over the streets of Soho like a damp, grey weight upon the chest of a dying man, to obscure not merely the physical shapes of things but also the precise moral outlines of one’s actions. I remember standing under the flickering gas-lamp at the corner of Berwick Street, watching the yellowish halo struggle against the enveloping murk, and thinking to myself, with a certain sardonic bitterness, that there was very little difference, after all, between the darkness without and the darkness within. The new Assistant Commissioner had spoken to me that morning in his office at the Yard—a bare, cheerless room with a map of the metropolis pinned to the wall like a dissected carcass—and his words had been as cold and as precise as the instruments of a surgeon. He desired an arrest. He desired, in fact, something that would satisfy the newspapers, which had been clamouring of late with an indignant and almost voluptuous fury about the foreign anarchists nesting in the heart of the Empire. He cared nothing for the slow, patient, invisible work of years, the cultivation of men whose trust was given in drops, like a bitter medicine; he cared only for the spectacle of justice, swift, visible, and photographable. And I, who had given the better part of my life to that invisible labour, found myself commanded to produce a victim.

I walked on slowly, past the shuttered fronts of the coffee-houses and the dim, steaming windows of the eating-houses, where shadows moved behind curtains like the shades of the damned in some dreary ante-chamber of the underworld. I knew every street, every alley, every dark and malodorous courtyard in this quarter, as a man knows the lines of his own hand; and I knew, too, the faces that belonged to those shadows—the dreamers, the fugitives, the desperate and the deluded, men who had fled from the prisons of one continent only to find themselves immured in the subtler, more degrading prison of poverty and suspicion in another. Among them all, there was but one whom I could plausibly arrest, one whose name would look well in the morning papers, whose vague and rhetorical associations with certain continental groups would furnish sufficient evidence for the popular mind, if not for a jury. And that man was Gregor Vassiliou.

I had first encountered Vassiliou some seven years before, in a low tavern in Charlotte Street, a place of smoke and stale beer and interminable, furious arguments conducted in three languages at once. He was a man of considerable presence, tall, gaunt, with a great dark beard and eyes that burned with the dull, persistent glow of embers in a fire that has been smothered but not extinguished. He spoke little, but when he did, it was with a weight and a deliberateness that commanded attention even from the most voluble of his comrades. I had marked him at once as a man of influence among the exiles, a man who was trusted, who knew the comings and goings, the hopes and the schemes, the secret subscriptions and the clandestine correspondences. And so I had set myself to cultivate him, with a patience that was, I suppose, in its own way, as monstrous as any treachery. I had listened to his confidences, which were given sparingly at first and then with a slow, reluctant abundance. I had learned his habits, his acquaintances, his petty vanities and his deeper loyalties. And in return, I had kept him safe. I had warned him, through oblique channels, when the net was to be cast in certain quarters; I had seen to it that his name was kept from certain lists; I had shielded him, in short, as a man shields the source of his livelihood.

For he had been my informer. He had given me names, dates, plans—enough to thwart actions that would have been bloody and senseless, enough to justify my existence in the eyes of my superiors. And yet, standing there in the fog, I was conscious of a profound and terrible irony: the very intimacy of our relationship, the very depth of my knowledge of his affairs, made him the only man I could now lay hands upon with any certainty of success. The others were phantoms, shadows without substance; but Vassiliou was real, solid, documented. I held in my desk at the Yard the record of his movements, his associations, his words—words spoken in confidence to a man he believed to be his friend. It would be the simplest thing in the world to transform those records into evidence. A word here, a phrase there, stripped of their context, would suffice to paint him as a dangerous conspirator, a fomenter of violence. And the newspapers would be satisfied, and the Assistant Commissioner would be satisfied, and the public would sleep more soundly in its beds, believing that the guardians of the law were vigilant and strong.

I reached the door of his lodgings, a narrow, grimy house in a street that seemed to shrink away from the light as if ashamed of itself. I stood for a long moment on the threshold, my hand upon the latch, and I was aware of a strange reluctance, a physical unwillingness to enter, as though the very bricks and mortar resisted my purpose. I thought of the Assistant Commissioner's face—that smooth, impassive, supremely confident face—and I thought of Vassiliou's face, with its dark, burning eyes and its expression of weary, dignified endurance. And I was conscious, too, of a deeper unease, a moral nausea that rose in me like bile. For I perceived, with a sudden and terrible clarity, that there was no escaping the logic of my situation. I had lived for years in a twilight world of deception and half-truths, and now that twilight had deepened into a darkness in which I could no longer distinguish between the hunter and the hunted, between the keeper of the law and the breaker of it.
