On the extreme edge of the Forest of Lotharingia, where the ancient oaks stood like gloomy sentinels guarding the confines of civilisation, there lived a woodcutter named Hans. He was a man of remarkably few words, which is perhaps the greatest compliment one can pay to a woodcutter, and his daughter, Elara, possessed a talent for the loom that was entirely out of proportion to her station in life. She could weave sunsets into tapestries, and trap the fleeting gold of autumn within a web of spun thread. It was said that when she plied her shuttle, the very birds paused in their singing to listen to the rhythm of the warp and the woof.

Now, the Queen of that realm, Isolde the Fair, was a woman of insatiable vanity. She was not merely content with being beautiful; she desired to be adored, which is a very different thing, and vastly more exhausting. A mirror may be bought for a few silver pieces, but the adoration of a populace requires a far heavier expenditure of artifice. Having exhausted the resources of her court jewellers, who could no longer set a diamond without provoking a yawn from the populace, she heard of Elara’s wondrous skill.

The Queen sent for the girl. The royal carriage, a monstrous gilded contraption that looked exceedingly uncomfortable and cost a great deal of money, arrived at the woodcutter’s cottage. Elara was commanded to weave a robe—a robe so magnificent that whoever looked upon it would be compelled to love the wearer. It was to be a garment that would make Isolde the most beloved queen the world had ever known.

‘You shall have a year,’ said the Queen’s Chamberlain, a man whose face was entirely composed of angles and platitudes. ‘And if you fail, your father’s wood shall be confiscated, and you yourself shall be exiled to a convent, where the garments are notoriously plain.’

Elara bowed her head. She was a creature of exquisite gravity, with eyes the colour of a bruised violet and a mouth like a crimson stain upon a lily. She did not protest, for she knew that in the presence of absolute power, silence is the only form of dignity left to the poor.

For a year, Elara sat at her loom. She sent no word to the palace, nor did she purchase any of the oriental dyes or silks that the courtiers had so generously offered. The loom was set up at the window of the cottage, where she could watch the changing seasons. She saw the green buds of spring burst forth, the heavy gold of summer, the crimson decay of autumn, and the stark, brittle white of winter. She watched the mists rise from the forest floor and the frost trace silver arabesques upon the glass. She worked the treadle, and the shuttle flew back and forth, but she used no colours. She used only the rough, unbleached wool of the mountain sheep, and a thread of grey so fine it was almost invisible to the naked eye.

When the year was up, the Chamberlain returned. He was in a foul humour, having been jostled by several low branches on the journey, which is the inevitable fate of those who ride in state through a forest.

‘Where is the robe?’ he demanded, looking around the cottage with undisguised disdain. ‘Her Majesty has commanded a festival for the morrow. The entire court is assembled. The foreign ambassadors are waiting. You have not been idling, I trust?’

‘I have not been idling, my lord,’ said Elara softly. She rose from her stool and handed him a large, folded square of cloth.

The Chamberlain took it between his thumb and forefinger, holding it at arm’s length as one might hold a dead mouse. It was grey. Not the grey of a storm-laden sky, or the grey of a dove’s wing, but a plain, unrelieved, unapologetic grey. It had no sheen, no shimmer, no embroidery. It was as devoid of ornament as a Quaker’s conscience.

‘What is this?’ he cried, his angles seeming to sharpen in his fury. ‘This is a mockery! This is sackcloth! The Queen will have you flayed!’

‘It is the robe the Queen demanded,’ Elara replied calmly. ‘She asked for a garment that would make her the most beloved queen in the world. I have woven it.’

‘You are mad! There is not a jewel, not a thread of gold, not a single shade of colour in it! Who could love a woman in this?’

‘Only those who truly love her,’ said Elara. ‘And that, my lord, is the point.’

The Chamberlain, having no taste for paradox and a profound fear of the Queen, bundled the cloth under his arm and rode back to the palace in high dudgeon. He presented the robe to Isolde with a sneer, expecting her wrath to fall upon the woodcutter’s daughter.

Isolde the Fair sat in her boudoir, surrounded by her ladies-in-waiting, who were all selected for their profound ugliness, in order that they might serve as a fitting frame for her own beauty. She unfolded the robe. A ripple of astonished laughter went through the room. It was a heavy, coarse, grey thing.

‘The wretched girl has lost her wits!’ cried the Queen. ‘She sends me a shroud! Does she think I am already dead to fashion?’

‘She says, Your Majesty,’ the Chamberlain ventured, ‘that it will make you the most beloved queen in the world.’

‘Beloved? In this?’ Isolde held it up to the light, which only served to emphasise its drabness. ‘I would look like a washerwoman! I would look like my own ghost!’

Yet, the Queen was a woman of fatal curiosity. She had exhausted every other sensation; perhaps there was a thrill in this. And so, on the night of the great festival, Isolde the Fair descended the grand staircase of the palace, wearing the grey robe of the woodcutter’s daughter.
