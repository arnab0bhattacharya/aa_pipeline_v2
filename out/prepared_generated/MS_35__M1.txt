It is with a hand that trembles, not from age, though age has its claims, but from a sorrow too vast for my poor frame to contain, that I lay down my pen to contemplate the terrible architecture of my task. For forty years I have laboured in this quiet cloister of learning, this university of Ashenfell, to erect a monument of words against the forgetting of our race. I, Aldric Fenwick, have sought to be the memory of mankind, to gather the scattered testimonies of the Great Pestilence—the Grey Shroud, as the terrified survivors named it—and to weave them into a history that would stand as a bulwark against its return. And now, as my chronicle nears its completion, I perceive with a horror that freezes my very soul, that I have not been a historian, but a prophet; and my prophecy is of doom.

My great work, this *Annals of the Grey Shroud*, was conceived in the first years of the Long Quiet, when the silence in the world was so profound that one could hear the rustle of a leaf in a forest a mile distant. The cities, those great engines of humanity, were silent tombs, their avenues choked with the slow encroachment of vine and weed. We who remained, the scattered few who had been blessed with an immunity we could neither comprehend nor replicate, were like ghosts haunting the ruins of a world that was not our own. In those days, my purpose was clear: to bear witness. I travelled through the skeletal remains of London, my heart a leaden weight in my breast, to interview the aged and the broken, to collect the diaries found in the hands of the dead, to piece together the story of our near-extinction. I wrote of the first reports from the East, of the strange fever that stole the breath and left the skin a mottled grey, of the frantic, futile efforts of our physicians, and of the great, silent dying that swallowed the world. I wrote with a scholar’s dispassion, yet every word was wrung from my own experience of loss, every sentence a scar upon my memory. My wife, my Eleanor, my gentle Elara, was taken in the first wave; my son, my brave Thomas, in the second. My history is not merely of the world; it is the very catalogue of my own heart’s desolation.

Yet, as I wrote, a strange and terrible thought began to form in my mind, a suspicion that the Shroud was not a mere accident of nature, a caprice of a blind and indifferent Providence. The patterns of its spread, the uncanny intelligence with which it seemed to seek out the last pockets of humanity, suggested a will, a purpose. I began to see it not as a pestilence, but as a predator. And my history, I resolved, must be more than a record; it must be a warning, a shield forged in the fires of past sorrows, to protect those who would come after. I laboured by day and by night, my lamp a solitary star in the great, dark firmament of the university library. I felt the weight of all the silent dead upon my shoulders, and their voices whispered to me in the rustling of the pages, urging me on, that their sacrifice might not be in vain.

It is in the pursuit of this grand, this noble, yet perhaps this hubristic design, that I first perceived the signs of our doom. It began not with a proclamation or a great cry of alarm, but with a subtle discordance in the world, a returning dissonance to the melody of our hard-won peace. Three weeks past, a young scholar of my own college, a bright and eager youth named Corwin, fell ill. He complained of a weariness in his limbs and a chill that no fire could warm. The physicians of our small community, their skills honed on the common ailments of our quiet life, dismissed it as a simple ague. But I, who had read a thousand accounts of the Shroud’s inception, knew the portents. I saw in his fevered eyes the same grey shadow I had seen in Eleanor’s, the same dreadful stillness that had preceded Thomas’s end.

I spoke my fears, but they were met with the gentle pity reserved for an old man’s anxieties. They told me I had lived too long with my ghosts, that I saw the Shroud in every shadow. They bade me rest, to cease my morbid labours. But how could I rest, when the very air had grown thick with a familiar dread? I walked the cloisters at night, and the silence, once a balm, now seemed a predator’s held breath. The wind, sighing through the ancient elms of the quad, no longer sounded like a lament for the past, but a hungry whisper of the future. And then, two nights ago, I saw it. In the infirmary, where Corwin lay, a faint, grey mist seemed to hang upon the air, a visible miasma of decay. It was a sight my mind had refused to recall, a horror I had entombed in the pages of my history, and now it had risen from the grave of my memory to stand before me.

My God, to what end have I striven? I sought to build a fortress of knowledge, only to find that the enemy is already within the walls. My great work, my *Annals*, is a fool’s testament. I have described the anatomy of a tiger to a world of deer, hoping the knowledge would protect them, never believing the tiger would return to the same forest. The past is not past. The dead are not at rest. This morning, the bells of the town did not ring for Prime; a strange and terrible silence hangs over Ashenfell, a silence broken only by a single, distant cough that echoes from the direction of the infirmary. I hear footsteps in the quadrangle below, but they are slow, and shuffling, and lack the purpose of the living.
