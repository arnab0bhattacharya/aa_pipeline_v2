The case upon which the celebrated private enquiry agent, Mr. Julian Thorne, was engaged one foggy November morning in 1893, presented itself with a certain grim simplicity. It was my privilege to accompany him, as I had on many other occasions, and to observe the workings of a mind which refused to accept any surface explanation until it had been turned over and examined from every conceivable angle. The setting was the consulting rooms of Messrs. Eldredge & Vane, in a quiet and imposing street off Harley Street, where the brass plates were polished to a mirror shine and the very silence spoke of discreet and expensive professional service.

We were shown in by a constable whose damp greatcoat left a dark trail on the marble floor of the hall. The door to the main consulting room was a formidable affair of polished oak, and it had been forced open by a local locksmith. Within, the scene was one of profound and unsettling stillness. Mr. Alistair Eldredge, a surgeon of some fifty years with a reputation for an unflinching hand and an equally unflinching manner, lay slumped in the heavy leather chair behind his desk. His head was thrown back, his lips tinged with a faint bluish hue, and a small, dark bruise was visible upon his temple. He was breathing, but shallowly, and a doctor from a neighbouring practice was already in attendance.

His partner, Mr. Cecil Vane, stood by the window, a tall, spare man whose face was a mask of professional composure, though his knuckles were white where he gripped his own wrists. “A most shocking affair, Mr. Thorne,” he said, his voice low and measured. “I found him myself not twenty minutes ago. The door was locked, as it is every night. My key was the only one, besides Alistair’s own, and that was in my waistcoat pocket.”

Thorne nodded, his pale, hawk-like eyes missing nothing. They swept the room, from the neat rows of leather-bound medical texts to the brass andirons in the fireplace. His gaze settled upon the open morocco-leather case on the desk beside the unconscious man. It was a surgeon’s kit, lined with velvet, its instruments nested in their appointed places. All, that is, save one.

“A scalpel is missing,” Thorne observed, not as a question.

Vane’s jaw tightened. “Yes. A number four scalpel, for delicate work. Nothing else appears to have been touched. No money, no correspondence. It is the most baffling thing.”

“Indeed,” said Thorne, stepping closer to the body. He did not touch the man, but bent low, his gaze fixed upon the surgeon’s hands. They were the hands of a master craftsman—long-fingered, immaculately clean, the nails perfectly manicured. “There is no sign of a struggle. The bruise on the temple is small, consistent with a single, sharp blow from a small, hard object. The handle of a scalpel, for instance.”

The constable coughed. “We’ve questioned the maid, sir. She swears no one came or went. The street door is kept locked after nine.”

At this moment, the door opened and a young man entered, hesitating on the threshold. He was thin and pale, with the anxious eyes of a student, and he clutched a sheaf of papers to his chest. This, we learned, was Mr. Fenton, the junior assistant.

“Mr. Vane,” he began, his voice trembling slightly, “I was just filing the morning’s notes when I noticed something. In the ledger for a Mrs. Anstruther. It’s been altered.”

Vane turned on him with a flash of irritation. “Nonsense, Fenton. You are overwrought. I have already told Mr. Thorne that nothing was taken.”

“It is not a matter of theft, sir,” the young man insisted, a surprising firmness entering his tone. He laid the ledger open on a small side table. “Here. The entry for her consultation last Tuesday. It has been changed. The note on her cardiac condition has been struck through and replaced with a reference to a simple gastric complaint. The handwriting is a fair imitation of Dr. Eldredge’s, but the pressure is all wrong. And the ink is fresh.”

A silence fell upon the room, thick and heavy as the fog outside. Here was a contradiction. Vane, the partner, insisted on a simple, if inexplicable, theft. The junior assistant, a man of no apparent standing, spoke of a deliberate and sinister falsification of medical records.

Thorne moved to the ledger, his movements as precise as a watchmaker’s. He examined the entry for a full minute, his face impassive. Then he straightened up and addressed the two men. “Mr. Vane, you believe your partner was assaulted by an intruder whose sole purpose was to steal a single instrument, and who then locked the door from the outside with a key he did not possess?”

“It sounds absurd when you put it so,” Vane admitted, “but what other explanation is there? Alistair is not a man to make enemies. He is respected by all.”

“Respected men are often the targets of the most venomous envy,” Thorne replied coolly. He turned to the assistant. “And you, Mr. Fenton, suggest that the motive was not theft, but the concealment of some medical error or malpractice regarding this Mrs. Anstruther, and that Dr. Eldredge was silenced to prevent his discovery of the forgery.”

Fenton swallowed hard. “I can think of no other reason for the alteration, sir. Dr. Eldredge was meticulous. He would never have sanctioned such a change.”

Thorne fell silent, his gaze drifting from the unconscious surgeon to the open case, then to the locked door, and finally to the window, which looked out upon a small, walled yard. He seemed to be weighing the two narratives in the scales of his mind. One was a simple theft that defied all logic of entry and exit. The other was a complex crime of deceit that offered a plausible motive but required a perpetrator who was already inside the locked room.
