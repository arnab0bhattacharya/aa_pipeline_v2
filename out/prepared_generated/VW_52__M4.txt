The post-cart had rattled away down the valley, leaving a silence that seemed to widen and deepen like a pool after a stone has sunk, and the three of them—Rupert, and Helena his sister, and Charles, who was no relation at all but had been Rupert’s friend since Cambridge—stood at the door of the inn at Hochsteg with their packs still on their backs, looking up. The mountains did not so much rise as remain; they had been there before the inn, before the road, before the valley, and would be there after, and the light now, at six of an evening, lay along the western faces in long bands of amber and a green so cold it was almost blue, so that the whole mass seemed less a thing of rock than of thought, of some immense and patient thought that had no need of them at all.

“Well,” said Rupert, and let his pack slip from his shoulder to the ground. He was a tall young man with a way of throwing back his head when he spoke, as though the words, once out, required room to fly.

“Well,” said Charles, who was shorter, and darker, and stood a little to one side, as he always did, so that Rupert might have the middle of the road.

Helena said nothing. She was looking at the inn—the low white wall, the shutters painted a faded apple-green, the geraniums in the window-boxes so red against the white that they seemed to have been put there for a jest, a deliberate excess—and she was thinking that she was tired, and that her boots were wet, and that Rupert had spoken to Charles and not to her, which was nothing, which was a habit, which meant nothing; and then the door opened and the woman of the inn came out with her sleeves rolled and her arms floured to the elbow, and behind her, in the dark of the passage, there was a girl.

She came forward into the light. That was all. She came forward, out of the dark passage, into the light of the doorway, and stood with her hands at her sides, and looked at the three of them without curiosity, as one looks at weather. Her hair was the colour of the honey one buys in the comb, and she wore it in two heavy braids wound about her head, and her apron was blue, and she was perhaps nineteen, perhaps twenty, and she had the kind of face that is not beautiful in any of its parts and is beautiful altogether, because it is entirely occupied with being itself, and does not know that it is being looked at. She said something to her mother in the dialect, something about the rooms, and her voice was low and had the burr of the valley in it, and then she turned and went back into the dark, and the dark took her.

Charles felt it in his chest—a small, clean blow, like the tap of a hammer on a nail, driving something home. He thought: I shall remember this. I shall remember the exact colour of the light on the wall, and the flour on the mother’s arms, and the way she turned. And then, at once, he thought: Rupert is looking at her too. And he was. Rupert had thrown back his head and was smiling—that smile, Charles knew it, it came from the very centre of him and was offered to the world like a coin, like a flower, like a flag run up—and Charles felt the nail go deeper, and turned away, and began to speak of supper, of the passes, of the weather to-morrow, in a voice that was too loud and too quick, and knew that it was too loud and too quick, and could not stop it.

They sat at a long table under the chestnut tree, and the girl served them. She served Rupert first, because he sat nearest the kitchen door, and Rupert said “Danke schön” in his German, which was bad and cheerful, and she smiled—a small smile, a mere flicker, gone before it was fairly there—and Charles, watching, saw that she smiled at the German and not at the man, that she was already thinking of the next plate, of the bread, of the door; and he was glad, and hated himself for being glad, and then she came to him and set down his plate and he said nothing at all, nothing, and she went on. The wine was sour and cold and tasted of the stone of the cellar. The mountains lost their amber and went grey, and then a deeper grey, and then the first star came out above the eastern ridge, very small and sharp, like a pinprick in a curtain, and Rupert was talking—of the glacier they would cross to-morrow, of the guide he had engaged, of a man he had met in Innsbruck who had known his father—and his voice ran on, warm and easy, and Helena ate her supper in silence and watched her brother, and Charles watched the girl.

She was clearing the next table, where two men in climbers’ coats sat over their beer. She moved among the chairs as though the chairs were not there, and once she stopped, and looked up, past the chestnut leaves, at the mountain, at the star, at nothing; and her face in that moment was so entirely her own, so shut away, so complete, that Charles felt a kind of shame, as though he had opened a door in a strange house and looked into a room where someone was praying. He looked down at his plate. The pattern on the plate was of blue flowers, and he counted them, one, two, three, four, and thought: this is absurd, this is nothing, I have known her four hours, I know nothing about her, not her name, not anything. And yet the world had altered. That was the fact of it. The world had altered, and no one knew, and Rupert went on talking about the glacier.
