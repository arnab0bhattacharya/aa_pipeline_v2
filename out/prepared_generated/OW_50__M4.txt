It was generally agreed in certain drawing-rooms—where the tea was weak, the scandal strong, and the upholstery stronger—that Mr. Lucian Quill had been born with every advantage except the one indispensable: talent. He had been a painter, and his canvases had the singular merit of being indistinguishable from the wallpaper, which at least ensured they never disturbed a room. He had been a poet, and his verses, he was told, possessed a certain music, though it was the music of a door swinging on a rusty hinge. He had been an actor, until the critics observed that his Hamlet was less a prince in mourning than a haberdasher in a temper. And so, having failed at every art, he arrived, by a logic peculiar to the age and entirely his own, at the conclusion that he should become a critic.

“For consider,” he observed to his aunt, the formidable Lady Featherstone, over a plate of cucumber sandwiches, “the painter must mix his colours, the poet must count his feet, the actor must learn his lines. But the critic—ah, the critic is the one happy idler in the republic of letters. He need only look, and sneer, and dip his pen in gall. It requires no talent whatsoever, and pays remarkably better than talent ever does.”

“My dear Lucian,” replied Lady Featherstone, who had a wit like a well-kept razor, “you have at last discovered your vocation. You have been rehearsing for it all your life.”

And so it was that Mr. Quill, with a fine new fountain pen and a heart entirely free of charity, submitted his first notice to The Yellow Lantern, a journal of art and outrage edited by a small, waspish man named Mr. Cribbins. The subject was a new exhibition at the Grosvenor Gallery, and the painter was a certain Mr. Aubrey Vane, a young man of pale Pre-Raphaelite beauty and paler Pre-Raphaelite talent, whose canvases depicted, with equal fidelity, the sorrows of Ophelia and the texture of a velvet cushion.

Mr. Quill wrote:

“Mr. Vane’s Ophelia is a lady of such exceeding pallor that one suspects she has been drowned not in a stream but in a vat of blancmange. Her expression suggests not madness but a mild indigestion, and the flowers she clutches have the air of having been purchased at a shop and arranged by a shopman with no feeling for botany. Mr. Vane, we are told, paints from the heart. We would humbly suggest he first acquire one.”

The review appeared on a Tuesday. By Wednesday, Mr. Quill was famous. By Thursday, he was infamous, which in London is the same thing with a better tailor. The offices of The Yellow Lantern were besieged by ladies demanding copies and gentlemen demanding satisfaction; Mr. Cribbins, delighted, doubled his contributor’s fee and begged for more.

“My dear Quill,” he cried, “you have the touch! You have the divine cruelty! Every artist in London wishes to be insulted by you. It is the new fashion. To be praised is to be forgotten; to be destroyed by you is to be immortal.”

And indeed, it seemed so. Within the month, invitations arrived at Mr. Quill’s modest rooms in Half-Moon Street with a regularity that suggested a conspiracy. He was asked to private views, to supper parties, to the studios of painters who wished him to see their work before the public did—and, no doubt, to be kind. He was never kind. He was brilliant, which is better. He described a fashionable portraitist’s work as “the sort of thing one sees in a dream after eating too much lobster.” He dismissed a new volume of verse as “the confessions of a soul that has never been to confession.” He remarked of a celebrated actress’s Juliet that “she played the part as though she had been told the story by a servant.”

The artists hated him, and adored him. They sent him their paintings, their poems, their plays, their souls, and he returned them all with a small, neat wound. It was said that a young composer had wept for a week after reading Mr. Quill’s notice of his symphony, and then had set the review to music, and it was the finest thing he ever wrote.

It was at the house of Lady Featherstone, on a Tuesday in June, that Mr. Quill met the one person who did not wish to be insulted by him. She was a young woman named Miss Viola Marchmont, and she was the daughter of a bishop, which should have made her dull, and the owner of a small gallery in Bond Street, which should have made her dangerous. She had hair the colour of a well-polished chestnut and eyes that seemed to be laughing at a joke she had not yet decided to share.

“Mr. Quill,” she said, as he was presented to her, “I have read your reviews.”

“Then you have read the only honest criticism in London,” he replied, with the bow he had practised before the mirror.

“I have read the only dishonest criticism in London,” she said. “You are not cruel because you care for art. You are cruel because it is the only way you have found to matter.”

There was a pause. Lady Featherstone, from the other side of the room, watched with the interest of a naturalist observing a rare specimen.

“You are very direct, Miss Marchmont,” said Mr. Quill, who was not accustomed to being direct at.

“I am a dealer, Mr. Quill. I cannot afford to be indirect. I have a gallery, and I have a painter, and I wish you to see his work.”

“And you wish me to praise it?”

“I wish you to see it. Whether you praise it or destroy it is a matter of perfect indifference to me—and to him. He is not a fashionable painter, and he does not wish to be. He paints because he must, and he will go on painting whether you approve or not. It is a novel position for an artist, and I think you will find it disconcerting.”
