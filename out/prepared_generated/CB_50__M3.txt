The salle d'examen at the Rue de l'Ancienne had been transformed for the occasion into a theatre of judgment. Garlands of stiff, unwithering laurel bound the pillars; hard chairs were set in ranks for the parents, and a higher row of velvet-cushioned seats at the front received the inspectors and the civic dignitaries. The air was close, rendered thick by the breath of too many bodies, by the scent of beeswax and lamp-oil, and by the stifling weight of expectation. I sat at the side, behind a row of older girls, my hands folded in my lap. I was the English teacher; I had no velvet cushion, no laurel. My place was to be invisible until summoned to correct, to prompt, to praise.

Madame Vasseur, the directrice, stood at the centre of the floor, magnificent in black silk and a bonnet of grey velvet. She carried her authority like a sceptre, her small dark eyes darting over the assembly to gather and gauge every impression. She was a woman who knew the market value of a graceful curtsey, a well-turned phrase, a child's rosy blush. She had built her school upon such currency. I had learned, in my two years beneath her roof, that she could be genial when it served her, and cold as the stone beneath a northern winter when it did not. She was not cruel, as some are cruel, from a delight in suffering; she was merely wholly without scruple where her reputation was concerned. She would cut off a right hand if she thought the fingers had offended a patron. I had known this; I had lived with the knowledge; I had thought myself reconciled to it.

The examination proceeded. History, geography, arithmetic—the girls in white frocks recited and were dismissed. Then came the English recitation. I had chosen the piece myself, had drilled the girl, had shaped the very words upon her tongue. It was a poem of my own writing, born of a sleepless night in my narrow attic, when the wind from the canals had rattled my casement and I had thought of the sea, of cliffs, of a home I should never see again, and had poured that longing into verse. I had not written it for the school. I had written it because the heart will speak or burst, and I had no other listener. But Mademoiselle Reine, my best pupil, had found the page in my desk—I had been careless, or provident, I do not know which—and had begged to learn it. I had consented, weakly, because I was lonely, because I wished to hear my own sorrow spoken aloud in another's voice, and so to know whether it had the ring of truth.

Reine rose. She was fourteen, pale, slender, with eyes too large for her face. She clasped her hands and began. The poem opened upon a shore at evening, the tide withdrawing, the gulls crying above black rocks; it spoke of exile, of a lamp burning in a window that the speaker could not reach. The verse was plain, but it had fire; I knew it, because the fire had been mine. Reine's voice, at first timid, gathered courage; it swelled, it trembled, it broke upon the final lines with a passion that silenced the room. When she finished, there was a hush—the kind of hush that precedes either mockery or wonder.

Then applause, warm and sustained. The inspectors nodded. Mothers murmured to one another. A grey-headed man on the front row—Monsieur the Burgomaster himself—leaned toward Madame Vasseur and said, in French, loud enough for all to hear: "Charming! What piece is that? Some English author, no doubt?"

Madame Vasseur smiled. She turned her fine head, she spread her hands, and she said: "It is my own, Monsieur. A little thing of mine, composed for the occasion."

I sat as though a stone had been dropped upon my chest. The air would not come. I saw Reine's face—pale, stricken, her eyes seeking mine across the room with a look of bewildered appeal. She knew. She had copied the lines from my hand. And Madame Vasseur knew also, for I had told her, plainly, when I submitted the recitation programme. I had told her, and she had said, "Très bien," and had turned away. I had not understood. I understood now.

The room moved on. Another pupil was called. The clock ticked. The beewax smell thickened. I sat rigid, my fingers clenched beneath my apron, and the war began within me. It was not a war of small things. It was the old war, the only war that matters: between the soul that demands its due and the body that demands its bread. I was alone in a foreign city. I had no family, no protector, no second post awaiting me. If I spoke—if I rose and said, "That poem is mine, and Madame Vasseur knows it"—I should be dismissed. Reference would be denied; the thin chain of recommendation that bound one teaching-post to another would snap. I should be left to the streets of this Belgian town, to the grey rain, to the closed doors. I knew what it was to be poor. I knew what it was to write letters that went unanswered. I knew the slow humiliation of dependence.

But I knew also what it was to be silenced. I had known it all my life—to be a woman and poor, to have a mind that burned and a voice that was not wanted, to fashion beauty in secret and see it claimed by those who had not made it. Was I to endure this? Was I to sit with my hands folded while the very sorrow of my heart was taken from me and worn as another's ornament?

I thought of the Psalm: *Open thou my lips, O Lord, and my mouth shall shew forth thy praise.* But it was not praise I wished to shew forth. It was truth. And truth, I have found, is a harder thing than praise, and costs more.
