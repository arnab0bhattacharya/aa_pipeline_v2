Sir Baskerville Trenchard had spent the better part of sixty years believing himself to be the direct and unimpeachable descendant of King Edward the Fourth. It was a belief that gave an undeniable lustre to his rather threadbare existence, illuminating the damp acres of his Wiltshire estate with the reflected glory of a vanished crown. In the drawing-room of Vetchley Hall, surrounded by portraits of men whose noses undeniably suggested a royal origin, he was accustomed to hold court with an air of serene and slightly fatigued majesty. His wife, Lady Trenchard, a woman of impeccable virtue and a complexion like unripe fruit, had woven this royal fiction into the very tapestry of her soul. She felt that to be descended from a medieval sovereign was the only true excuse for living in the country at all.

It was on a grey Tuesday afternoon, when the rain was beating a melancholy tattoo against the long windows, that the blow fell. Mr. Phipps, an antiquary of repute and a man whose countenance was as grey and dusty as the parchments he loved, had been engaged to trace the Trenchard lineage. He sat opposite the baronet and his wife, a portfolio of vellum on his knees, his expression betraying that singular absence of emotion which is the hallmark of his profession.

‘I have completed the pedigree,’ Mr. Phipps began, turning over a leaf of heavy paper with the reverence of a priest handling a sacrament. ‘It is a remarkably continuous line, Sir Baskerville. Unbroken from the present day to the reign of the monarch in question.’

Sir Baskerville smiled his slow, aesthetic smile. ‘I was always assured my grandfather was a man of peculiar exactitude. I trust the crown sits easily upon our ancestors?’

‘The crown, Sir Baskerville, never sat upon their heads,’ Mr. Phipps replied calmly. ‘It sat, rather, upon the heads of those whom they served.’

Lady Trenchard, who was arranging some particularly limp asters in a bowl of Sèvres porcelain, paused. ‘I do not quite understand you, Mr. Phipps. Are you saying there is a break in the line?’

‘A break, madam? No, the line is entirely whole. It is merely the nature of the employment that has shifted in the telling. Your ancestor, Humphrey Trenchard, was intimately connected with the royal household. He was the Master of the King’s Cookery. He boiled the royal pheasants and superintended the royal gravies.’

There was a silence in the drawing-room of Vetchley Hall—the kind of silence that precedes a social catastrophe. Lady Trenchard dropped a dahlia, and its crimson head rolled under a rosewood cabinet.

‘A cook?’ she repeated, her voice hollow.

‘The royal cook, madam,’ corrected Mr. Phipps gently. ‘It was a position of immense trust. To prepare the monarch’s food was to hold the sovereign’s life in one’s hands. The axe was near the pot, as it were.’

Mr. Phipps gathered his parchments, accepted his honorarium with a murmured word of thanks, and departed, leaving the atmosphere of the room feeling oddly depleted of oxygen.

Sir Baskerville remained in his chair, gazing at the fire. Lady Trenchard had sunk onto a tapestry sofa, her face the colour of old parchment.

‘A cook,’ she whispered, as though the word itself were a physical blow. ‘My husband, a descendant of a man who stirred pots. I shall never be able to show my face at the Duchess’s garden party. The village schoolchildren will sing ballads about us. We shall be a proverb.’

Sir Baskerville did not move. He was looking at the fire with a new, almost startled, interest.

‘Do you know, my dear,’ he said at last, his voice remarkably serene, ‘I find it the most interesting thing that has ever happened to me.’

Lady Trenchard stared at him. ‘Interesting? You are mad, Baskerville. It is a disgrace. It is a contamination. We are no better than tradesmen.’

‘On the contrary,’ Sir Baskerville replied, ‘we are infinitely better than tradesmen, for our ancestor did not sell his food; he gave it to the King. I have spent my entire life being told I am the scion of a man who wore a crown. I always found the crown a rather heavy and unimaginative ornament. It is worn by so many dreadfully dull people. But a cook! A man who understood the mystery of fire and the chemistry of flavour! That is something quite different.’

‘You cannot be serious,’ Lady Trenchard said, wringing her hands. ‘What of the portrait in the long gallery? What of the Trenchard nose?’

‘The nose, my dear, is merely a piece of cartilage that has been mistaken for a royal appendage. It is, in reality, the nose of a man who spent his life leaning over boiling cauldrons and inhaling spices. It is a connoisseur’s nose. I have always suspected I had a peculiar sensitivity to the aroma of a well-made sauce. Now I understand its true provenance.’

‘You are talking nonsense,’ Lady Trenchard cried. ‘You are talking as though it were a distinction to have had a cook for an ancestor.’

‘It is a distinction, my dear Eleanor, to have had an ancestor who created something. Kings merely rule; cooks create. A king can order a feast, but he cannot make one. He is entirely dependent upon the skill of his kitchen. What is a monarch without his dinner? A mere abstraction. But a cook is a concrete reality. He takes the crude materials of the earth and transforms them into delight. I think it is a far finer thing to have invented a new dish than to have lost a battle.’

Lady Trenchard rose, her face pale and set. ‘I cannot listen to this. I shall go to my room. I have a sick headache coming on. You are deliberately trying to humiliate me, Baskerville.’
