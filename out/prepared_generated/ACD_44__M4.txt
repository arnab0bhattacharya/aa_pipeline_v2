It was on a raw November afternoon, with the yellow fog pressing close against the windows of my consulting-room in Queen Anne Street, that my servant announced a visitor whose card bore the name of Mr. Josiah Crane. I had no recollection of the man, but the hour was one at which I was seldom troubled, and I bade her show him in.

He was a spare, brown-faced fellow of some fifty years, dressed in a decent black, with a soft hat which he turned over and over in his fingers as though it were a thing unfamiliar to him. His left hand, I observed, was gloved, and he carried it a little stiffly, after the manner of a man who has learned to live with an old hurt. His eyes, which were of a pale, washed blue, went about the room before they rested on me, and there was in his whole bearing that curious mixture of deference and wariness which one sees in men who have knocked about the world and found it a hard schoolmaster.

“Dr. Ainslie?” said he, in a voice that had the flat vowels of the North in it. “You will not remember me, sir, and I make no doubt of it. Crane—Josiah Crane, late of the 66th Foot. You took a ball out of my shoulder at Jagdalak in ‘seventy-nine, and a rare job you made of it, though I daresay you had forgotten me before the wound was healed.”

I looked at him more closely, for the name of Jagdalak stirred old and ugly memories. “I was at Jagdalak,” said I, “but I cannot say that your face is familiar. There were a great many wounds in those weeks, and not a few of them in the shoulder.”

“That is true enough, sir, and no man could be expected to carry them all in his head. But I have carried you in mine, for all that. I have had it in my mind for five years to find you and to say a word of thanks, and now that I have found you I have a further matter to put before you, which is the real reason of my coming, and I will not beat about the bush with it.”

He sat forward on the edge of his chair, his hat still turning in his hands. “There is a thing buried at Jagdalak, Dr. Ainslie, and I want your help to get it.”

I confess the words gave me a start. “Buried? What thing?”

“A box,” said he, “of no great size, that I put in the ground with my own hands on the night of the twelfth of December, ‘seventy-nine, under the north wall of the fort, where the corner stone stands. There was money in it, and papers, and a thing or two besides that I would not name. It was not mine to keep in the open, and it was not safe to carry, and so I buried it, meaning to come back for it when the country had quieted. But the country did not quiet, as you know, and I was carried down to Peshawar with this shoulder of mine, and by the time I was on my feet again the regiment was home, and I was a civilian with a pension and no means to go a thousand miles for a box in the ground. And so it has lain there these five years, sir, and I have thought of it every night of my life.”

“And what is my part in it?” I asked. “I am a physician in London, not a traveller.”

“You are the one man who could tell me the ground,” said he eagerly. “You know the fort, the wall, the corner stone. You have the map of it in your head, and I have not. I have a plan drawn rough on paper, but it is five years old and my memory is not what it was. If you would go out with me—I have the means, sir, I have come by a little money—and put your finger on the spot, I would ask no more of you, and you would have your share of what is in the box, and welcome.”

I put him off with the promise of an answer in a day or two, and he went away with many bows and protestations, leaving me more disquieted than I cared to own. For the truth is that I did remember something, though not the man. There had been a box, or talk of a box, in those last confused weeks at Jagdalak—a box that was said to hold the pay of a native regiment, and that had vanished in the rout, and that certain officers had been at pains to say nothing about. And I did not at all like the eagerness of Mr. Josiah Crane, nor the gloved hand, nor the pale eyes that went about the room before they rested on me.

I had a patient that evening in Baker Street—a gentleman of my acquaintance whose nerves had been much shaken by overwork, and who was good enough to say that my visits did him more good than all the pharmacopœia—and it was on his doorstep, as I was leaving, that I met the tall, lean figure of Mr. Sheridan Quill, the consulting detective, whose rooms were on the floor above. He had just come in from the fog, his collar turned up and his thin hands blue with cold, and he stopped on the stair to greet me.

“You are troubled, Doctor,” said he, glancing at me with that quick, sidelong look of his which seemed to take a man’s measure and his secrets in the same instant. “A patient who will not mend, or a case that will not fit?”

“Neither,” said I. “A visitor who will not be dismissed.”
