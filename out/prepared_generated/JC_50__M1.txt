The order from Batavia, when it came, was a dry thing—a sheet of grey government paper, stamped and folded with an official precision that admitted of no argument, no sentiment, and certainly no brotherhood. It had been carried up from the telegraph office by a sweating clerk, and it lay upon my desk now, under the slow revolution of the punkah’s fan, a document of pure negation. I was to refuse entry to the vessel lying off the point. She flew no flag; her name, *The Providence*, was known to no register; her purpose was suspect. She was a ghost on the water, and my duty was to see that she remained so.

From my window, I could see her, a long, dark shape against the shimmering, metallic glare of the sea. The heat of the day was a physical presence, a weight upon the chest, pressing the life out of the port of Tanahwangi. It lay in the unmoving air of the verandah, in the limp fronds of the palms, in the profound silence that had fallen over the usual clamour of the quays. Even the flies seemed to crawl with a listless, fatalistic patience. The ship was the only thing that seemed to possess a will, a quiet, patient intention that was more unnerving than any overt threat. She sat there, beyond the invisible line of my authority, a question mark written in tar and timber upon the placid water.

The boat that came ashore an hour before sunset was a simple native *prahu*, its sail a patched and sun-bleached triangle. It did not make for the main jetty, but slipped towards the small wooden steps used by the pilots. I watched it from the shade of the banyan tree, my heart beating with a slow, heavy rhythm that seemed to have no connection to the heat or the hour. A single figure stood in the stern, a man in a white jacket, his form indistinct in the long shadows stretching from the shore. He did not look up at the Residency. His purpose was singular, his movements economical. He handed a folded paper to the old harbour pilot, a man whose face was a roadmap of the treacherous currents of the archipelago, and then the *prahu* turned and was gone, swallowed by the encroaching dusk.

The pilot brought the paper to me. He placed it on my desk with a reverence that was, I thought, entirely for the office I held, and not for the man who held it. He said nothing, his old eyes clouded with a lifetime of unasked questions, and then he withdrew. The paper was coarse, the fold sharp. There was no seal, no address. I knew, before I touched it, what it contained. It was a knowledge that came not from the mind but from the blood, a cold, ancestral certainty. I unfolded it.

The hand was his. There was no mistaking it, not after twenty years. It was the same bold, impatient script I had seen scrawled in the margins of books, on the backs of letters to our father, on the flyleaf of a Bible he had taken with him when he left—a script that always seemed to be straining against its own confines, impatient for a freedom the lines on the page could not provide. “Elias,” it began, and the simple word was a blow, a key turning in a lock rusted shut by two decades of silence and the vast, indifferent spaces of the world. He did not ask for entry. He did not mention the flag or the government’s order. He wrote only of the light on the water at dawn, of the scent of spices on the wind, of a shared memory from our boyhood—the time we had built a raft from driftwood and nearly drowned in the millpond. He wrote as if we had parted only yesterday, as if the gulf of years and continents and my own carefully constructed new life were a thing of no consequence. He ended with a line that was not a request but a statement of fact, a simple assertion of a bond I had spent half my life trying to sever: “Some things, brother, are not subject to regulation.”

I read it again, and the words blurred. The punkah fan stirred the humid air, but could not cool the fire that had kindled in my veins. This was not a plea from a distressed mariner. It was a summons. It was the voice of the past, arriving not as a memory but as a physical presence, anchored just beyond my jurisdiction. The brother I remembered was a creature of impulse and grand, unworkable schemes, a man who saw the world as a stage for his own romantic dramas. He had been a merchant, a sailor, a revolutionary for causes that lasted no longer than a monsoon. He had always believed that some higher law, some moral imperative of the heart, superseded the petty statutes of men. I, on the other hand, had found my salvation in those statutes. I had built a fortress of rules and regulations around myself, a life of quiet, ordered duty in this forgotten port, and I had thought myself safe.

The sun bled into the horizon, a spectacular, violent display of orange and crimson that promised a storm, or perhaps nothing at all. The ship, *The Providence*, became a silhouette, a hole cut in the fabric of the twilight. Was he, even now, watching my window, watching for the signal lamp that would either condemn him to the open sea or grant him the sanctuary of the harbour? The responsibility was a crushing weight. To obey the order from Batavia was to perform my duty, to be the man I had made myself into: the harbour master, the functionary, the keeper of the line. To defy it was to answer to the man who had written that letter—to acknowledge that, beneath the uniform and the official seal, there was still a brother, a boy who had once believed in the unsinkable nature of driftwood and faith.
