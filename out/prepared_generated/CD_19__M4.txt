The arches beneath the viaduct at Bermondsey presented, at that era of gas and steam, an aspect which I shall endeavour to convey to you, sir or madam, as faithfully as my poor pen allows; for it was a region of perpetual twilight and perpetual drizzle, where the great brick piers rose like the legs of some monstrous antediluvian fowl, and the trains thundered overhead with a clatter that shook the very soot from the crevices and set the puddles trembling in their muddy beds. Here, where the daylight came down grudgingly and left early, and where the wind, having no other business, devoted itself entirely to the harrying of rags and the scattering of straw, there dwelt a company of boys: not such boys as you may see in the polite squares, with clean pinafores and a governess at heel, but boys of the arch and the gutter, boys whose beds were of damp straw and whose ceilings were the blackened vaults, boys who had never in their lives known the sensation of a full stomach save as a memory or a dream.

Their captain—for even among the destitute there must be a captain, or chaos comes—was a lad of some fourteen years, known to his followers as Gutteridge, a name he had bestowed upon himself in preference to the one given him at the workhouse, which he considered wanting in dignity. He was a boy of remarkable thinness, so that his jacket, which had belonged to a man of thrice his girth, hung upon him like a tent upon a pole; yet there was in his eye a certain quick, commanding light, and in his manner a swagger borrowed from the horse-dealers of the Borough, which kept his little band in excellent order. Under him served a small philosopher called Nipper, whose especial talent lay in the extraction of edibles from unwilling pockets; a boy named Tolly, whose gift was the pathetic whimper, and who could produce tears at will with a facility that would have made his fortune on the stage; and half a dozen others, ragged, sharp-faced, and hungry.

Now it happened, upon a night of particular fog and drizzle, when the gas-lamps in the streets beyond the arches burned like dim moons in a muddy sky, that the boys, returning from their evening’s industry, discovered a phenomenon in their midst: a little girl, not above five years old, seated upon a broken crate in the driest corner of the arch, wrapped in a shawl much too large for her, and regarding them with eyes of such solemn wonder that Nipper, who was first upon the scene, stopped short and let fall a purloined sausage in his astonishment.

“Hallo!” cried Gutteridge, advancing. “What’s this? A little ’un! Where d’ye come from, my dear?”

The child considered him gravely, and then replied, in a voice of the smallest and most pitiful description, that she did not know.

“Don’t know!” repeated Gutteridge. “Don’t know where you come from? What’s your name, then?”

She shook her head.

“What’s your father’s name? Your mother’s? Where d’ye live? Who brought ye here?” These questions, delivered in rapid succession, produced no other effect than a trembling of the under-lip and a silent welling of tears in the great eyes; and Tolly, observing this, was so moved—for even the hardest heart has its soft spot—that he at once sat down beside her and offered her the half of a meat-pie of dubious origin, which she accepted with a gravity that won every heart in the company.

“She’s a lost ’un,” said Gutteridge, with the air of a judge delivering sentence. “She’s a lost ’un, and she’s our’n now. We found her, and finders is keepers, as the saying is. What say you, boys?”

The boys said, with one voice, that she was their’n; and so it was settled. They called her—for she must have a name, and no name was forthcoming—they called her Posy, because Nipper declared that she was small and sweet and grew in a crack; and Posy she remained.

I pass over the particulars of that winter, which was a hard one, and would fill a volume. Suffice it to say that the boys fed her by their various arts—now a crust from a baker’s shelf, now a potato from a costermonger’s barrow, now a piece of cheese of such antiquity that it might have been dug from the ruins of Troy—and that she throve upon this diet in a manner that would have astonished any physician. She became the pet and the treasure of the arches. She mended their jackets with a needle and thread procured by means unknown; she sang them songs of her own invention, in which the words were chiefly “la” and “diddle”; and she listened to their tales of adventure with a solemnity that made the telling twice as fine. In return, they taught her the arts of the pavement: how to look hungry without appearing importunate; how to dodge a policeman with grace and economy of motion; how to sleep upon straw without permitting the rats to become familiar. It was an education, sir, of a peculiar sort, but it was the only one they had to give.

Now, upon a certain afternoon in the spring, when the fog had lifted and a pale sun was making a feeble effort to illuminate the arches, there appeared in the neighbourhood a lady of most respectable appearance: a lady in a black silk gown and a bonnet of sober grey, who carried a reticule in one hand and a small parcel in the other, and who inquired of the shopkeepers, with an anxious countenance, whether they had seen anything of a little girl, about five years old, with dark eyes and a shawl of a reddish pattern. She had lost her niece, she said; the child had wandered from her nurse in the Borough Market; she was distracted with grief.
