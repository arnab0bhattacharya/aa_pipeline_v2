It is a curious thing to observe how fiercely the English middle classes will defend the boundaries of their own respectability, particularly when those boundaries are drawn across a perfectly ordinary London suburb. I had gone down to Micklehurst on a Tuesday afternoon, driven largely by a promise to my cousin Helena, who had lately abandoned the scholastic profession to set up a school for shopkeepers' daughters. She had spent twenty years teaching the daughters of the wealthy. Now she proposed to teach the daughters of grocers, drapers, and ironmongers. The whole affair seemed to me a monument to her formidable energy, and a source of intense irritation to practically everyone else in Micklehurst.

I found her in a tall, narrow villa on Beaufort Road, a house that smelled aggressively of fresh paint, floor soap, and that peculiar, chalky scent of new educational endeavour. Helena was standing in the front parlor, directing the placement of a blackboard. She was a woman of forty-five, with the square, unyielding jaw of a Prussian general and an unwavering conviction that the multiplication table was the highest moral good. She had always been clever, but hers was a practical, organizing cleverness, utterly devoid of poetry, and I had long suspected she would eventually do something drastic. I had merely assumed she would do it with more tact.

The trouble had begun, as trouble so often does, with a letter. Helena had written to the mothers of her former pupils—wealthy, languid women in Kensington and Mayfair—to announce her new venture. She had framed it as a philanthropic enterprise. She would bring her two decades of experience among the elite to the aspiring tradesmen of the suburbs. The responses had been swift and scandalized.

I picked up one such letter from the mantelpiece, a thick, cream-coloured envelope that seemed to sweat indignation. It was from a Lady Pomeroy, whose daughter Helena had once guided through a passable French O-level. "My dear Miss Vance," it read, "I cannot conceal my astonishment. To think that a woman of your standing should lower herself to instruct the spawn of tradesmen! It is not only a betrayal of the trust we placed in you, but a profound social error. I must beg you never to mention this enterprise to my daughter again."

"She is remarkably put out," I said, showing the letter to Helena.

Helena snorted. It was an inelegant sound, but entirely genuine. "Lady Pomeroy believes the alphabet is a hereditary privilege. I am merely democratizing it."

The shopkeepers, however, were no more welcoming. I had walked from the station, and on the way I had stopped in the high street to buy a paper. The newsagent, a Mr. Bostock, was a stout, red-faced man with a gift for gossip. When I asked him about the new school, he looked at me with deep suspicion.

"It's a condescension, sir," he said, folding his arms. "That's what it is. These fine ladies coming down here, thinking they can teach our girls how to be something they're not. My Elsie doesn't need to know how to curtsy to a duchess. She needs to know how to keep a ledger and speak French to the Swiss buyers."

"But Miss Vance intends to teach French," I pointed out.

Mr. Bostock waved a hand dismissively. "It's the principle of the thing. She's used to the high and mighty. She'll look down on Elsie. Mark my words. Before the month is out, Elsie will be ashamed of her own father's shop."

I found this a remarkable display of sensitivity, but I said nothing. The suburban mind is a fortress of contradictions, and Mr. Bostock was merely manning the ramparts.

When I told Helena of this conversation, she laughed. We were sitting in the dining room, which she had converted into a classroom. It was a bleak, functional space, adorned with maps and a large, framed photograph of the Queen. The furniture was plain, the floor bare. It had the air of a reformatory.

"They think I am going to corrupt their daughters with gentility," she said. "And the gentle think I am going to corrupt them with trade. I seem to have achieved the impossible: I have united the upper and middle classes in a shared state of outrage."

"It is a remarkable feat," I agreed.

She poured me a cup of tea. It was strong, bitter, and entirely without milk, which seemed to me a perfect metaphor for her character. "The fact is," she said, "that no one knows what to do with a woman who has no use for convention. I have spent twenty years teaching girls to be decorative. I am now going to teach them to be useful. The world does not require another generation of women whose sole accomplishment is the ability to play the piano and faint gracefully."

I suggested that fainting was, perhaps, less common than it had been.

"You know what I mean," she said impatiently. "I am teaching them to think. That is the dangerous thing."

At that moment, the doorbell rang. It was a sharp, insistent sound, and it brought a look of mild alarm to Helena's face. She went to answer it, and I followed. On the doorstep stood a small, anxious woman in a brown coat. She was clutching a handbag and looking about her with the nervous intensity of a sparrow in a cage of cats.

"I beg your pardon," she said. "I am Mrs. Higgins. My husband keeps the drapery shop on the high street. I have come about the school."

Helena ushered her in. Mrs. Higgins sat on the edge of a chair in the parlor, perched forward as if ready to flee. Her eyes darted about the room, taking in the blackboard, the maps, the photograph of the Queen.

"My girl, Ethel," she said. "She's fourteen. She's a clever girl. Very good at her sums. But my husband, he doesn't hold with education for girls. He says it makes them discontented. And then there's the matter of the fees." She paused, and her grip on the handbag tightened. "We can't pay what the fine ladies pay."
