The fog had lifted to a mere haze by the time I reached the establishment of Madame Élise Vaillant in Regent Street, and the February sun, though pale and wintry, struck a kindly gleam from the brass plate upon her door. I had been summoned by a note of some urgency, delivered by hand before breakfast, and it was with a certain professional curiosity that I ascended to the first floor, where the celebrated modiste received her clients amid hangings of dove-grey velvet and the discreet perfume of orris root.

She was a small, brisk woman of perhaps five-and-forty, with dark eyes that missed nothing and hands that lay folded upon a book of patterns as though they might at any moment resume their labour. Her gown was of her own designing, plain to the point of severity, and yet so admirably cut that it betrayed the artist in every seam.

“You are very good to come, sir,” said she, when I had introduced myself. “I am told that you have a genius for the unravelling of mysteries, and I am come to a pass where I must either find a solution or close my doors.”

“I trust it is not so serious as that,” said I, taking the chair she indicated.

“It is serious enough. I have been robbed, sir—not of silks or laces, but of something which I value far more. My books have been tampered with.”

“Your account books?”

“My measurement books. Every lady who comes to me is measured, and her figure, her preferences, her peculiarities of carriage—all are entered in a volume which I keep under lock and key. It is my stock-in-trade, sir, as much as my scissors. And now I find that certain pages have been copied—not torn out, mark you, but copied with a care that argues a cool and practised hand—and within a fortnight three of my most valued clients have been robbed at private balls.”

I confess that my interest quickened at this. “Robbed of what?”

“Of their jewels, and in a manner that is most singular. Each lady wore a gown of my making, and in each case the thief—for there must be one—went directly to the place where the jewels were concealed. Lady Ashcombe had a diamond spray pinned within the folds of her train; it was taken from her very person while she stood in a crowded salon. Mrs. Delacourt-Pyne wore a ruby pendant beneath a lace bertha; it was gone before the supper dance. And last Tuesday, at the Marchmonts’ ball, the Honourable Miss Ferrers lost a pearl and emerald bracelet from a hidden pocket in her overskirt—a pocket which no eye but mine had seen, and no hand but my own had stitched.”

“You suspect one of your own people?”

“I have four assistants and a forewoman. All have been with me for years. I would have staked my life upon any of them. But I am no fool, sir, and I know that a secret is a dangerous possession. Yet there is another matter which perplexes me still more.” She rose and took from a drawer a small morocco-bound volume, which she laid open before me. “These are the pages in question. You will observe that the writing is my own, but that in the margin, against each entry, there are certain marks—a cross, a circle, a little flourish—which I did not make.”

I examined the volume with care. The marks were in ink of a slightly different hue, and they were placed with a precision that suggested a system. Against Lady Ashcombe’s name stood a cross; against Mrs. Delacourt-Pyne, a circle; against Miss Ferrers, a flourish like a curl of smoke.

“And these ladies were robbed in the order of their names?”

“Precisely. And there are other names marked in the same fashion—seven in all—which I have warned, and which have as yet escaped. But I cannot warn them forever, sir. The season is at its height. There will be a ball at the Wrenburys’ on Thursday, and Mrs. Delacourt-Pyne’s sister, the Countess of Marden, is marked with a double cross.”

I asked whether she had communicated with the police, and she shook her head with an energy that was almost contemptuous.

“The police would come to my shop, sir, and question my girls, and the story would be in every drawing-room by Friday. I should be ruined more surely by the scandal than by the thefts. No, I have come to you because you are discreet, and because I am told you have a mind that can see through a brick wall.”

I bowed, and begged for a list of all persons who had access to the room in which the book was kept. It was brief enough: the four assistants, the forewoman, and a boy who came daily to sweep and run errands. To these must be added Madame Vaillant herself and any client who might be left alone in the fitting-room—a contingency which she admitted was not impossible, though she was seldom absent from her post for more than a few minutes at a time.

I spent the greater part of the afternoon in the establishment, ostensibly as a customer in search of a gift for a female relative, and I had the opportunity of observing the staff at their work. The forewoman, a grey-haired, competent person named Miss Tredgold, moved among the tables with the air of a sergeant-major; the assistants were young, deft, and voluble after the manner of their trade. But it was the boy who drew my attention—a sharp-faced lad of fourteen or so, who came and went with the brushes and the dustpan, and who seemed to have a trick of lingering at the door of the fitting-room whenever a client of consequence was within.

I made a note of his name—Tom Blakely—and of the fact that he was employed by a firm of cleaners in the neighbouring street, and was therefore not directly under Madame Vaillant’s eye. It was a small thing, but in my experience the small things are the hinges upon which great doors turn.
