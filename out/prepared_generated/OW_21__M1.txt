In the kingdom of Valderosa, where the sun poured down upon the white terraces like molten gold, there stood a palace of porphyry and alabaster. Its courtyards were filled with the scent of orange blossoms and the cool, ceaseless chatter of water, and in the midst of the principal court lay a fountain of green serpentine marble. Within this basin dwelt a single fish of such exceeding splendour that it seemed less a creature of flesh than a thought of the sun made manifest. Its scales were of a gold so pure and burnished that the courtiers claimed it cast a light of its own, a small, swimming star in the shadow of the arcade.

One evening, as the moon rose like a pearl over the cypress trees, a Fairy of the Woods, whose business it was to govern the silent things of the earth, came to the fountain’s edge. She was weary of the silence of stones and the dumb endurance of roots. Looking upon the golden fish, she was moved by a strange caprice. ‘Little brother,’ she said, her voice like the rustling of dry leaves, ‘the world is full of pretty lies. I will give thee a voice to utter the one thing that is real.’ And she touched the water with a finger of starlight, and the fish found that it could speak.

The next morning, the Duchess of Almeria, a woman whose beauty was said to be as fragile as a Sèvres vase, was leaning over the fountain. She wore a gown of pale blue silk, embroidered with silver lilies, and she sighed with a practiced melancholy. ‘Oh, little fish,’ she murmured, ‘how I envy thy simple joy. Thou dost not know the burdens of the heart.’

The fish rose to the surface, its gills fluttering. ‘Madame,’ it said, in a voice clear and cool as the water itself, ‘thy heart is not burdened; it is empty. Thou dost envy me because thou art a hollow thing, and the sound of thy own voice is the only echo thou dost find agreeable.’

A hush fell upon the court. The Duchess turned the colour of a bruised plum, and fainted into the arms of the Baron de Valois, who was himself a man of many words and few convictions. From that hour, the fountain became the most feared and yet the most frequented spot in Valderosa. The courtiers came to it as moths come to a flame, drawn by a fatal curiosity.

To the vain Countess of Sforza, who wore a necklace of rubies that outweighed her virtue, the fish said, ‘Those stones are a confession, Madam. Thou dost wear thy greed about thy throat for all the world to see.’ To the pious Bishop of Ravello, who was discussing the merits of charity, it observed, ‘Thy talk of heaven is very pretty, but thy investments are entirely of this world. The only poor man thou dost ever consider is thyself.’

The court, which had hitherto been a garden of exquisite falsehoods, was thrown into a state of magnificent confusion. Conversations, which were once as smooth and polished as a game of croquet, became a series of dreadful ambushes. Ladies confessed to their lovers that they found them tedious; gentlemen admitted to their friends that they had cheated them at cards. The entire fabric of Valderosan society, woven from the delicate threads of flattery, deception, and polite insincerity, began to unravel with astonishing speed. The truth, it appeared, was not a cleansing fire but a corrosive acid.

At last, the matter was brought before King Alaric. The King was a man who appreciated art, architecture, and a well-made epigram far more than he appreciated reality. He sat upon his throne of carved ivory, a troubled frown upon his handsome face. ‘Your Majesty,’ cried the Duchess of Almeria, her eyes red with weeping, ‘this creature is a monster! It speaks with the tongue of a viper and the heart of a fiend!’

‘It speaks the truth,’ muttered a young lord, before clapping a hand to his mouth.

‘The truth is a matter of opinion,’ declared the Prime Minister, a man whose life was a testament to the power of ambiguity. ‘And this fish has the most disagreeable opinions I have ever encountered.’

King Alaric listened to the clamour with growing distaste. He looked at the painted ceiling of his throne room, where gods and heroes disported themselves in a cloudless, eternal sky. He looked at the graceful curve of the marble pillars. He looked at the beautiful, terrified faces of his courtiers. All this harmony, all this beauty, was being destroyed by a single, gilded fish. He made his decision. The fish must be silenced.

He descended to the courtyard, followed by a retinue of nervous nobles. The sun was high, and the golden fish was swimming in lazy circles, a perfect, living jewel. ‘Thou hast caused a great deal of trouble,’ the King said, his voice accustomed to command.

‘I have only caused a great deal of truth,’ replied the fish. ‘The trouble was already there; I merely gave it a name.’

The King was taken aback by its composure. ‘Thy gift is a curse,’ he declared. ‘Thou dost speak of things that are ugly and sordid. A palace is a place of beauty, not of fact. I command thee to be silent.’

‘And what is beauty, O King?’ the fish asked, its golden eye fixed upon him. ‘Is it the painted lie upon thy ceiling, or the unvarnished truth of a pearl? Thou dost surround thyself with beautiful things, but thou dost fear the one beautiful thing that is real. Thy court is a masterpiece of artifice, and I am the flaw that reveals the canvas.’

‘Silence!’ roared the King, his composure cracking. ‘I will have thee thrown into the moat!’

‘A fish cannot be drowned,’ the fish observed calmly. ‘And the truth, once spoken, cannot be unspoken. It is like a perfume, O King. Thou canst banish the flower, but the scent remains in the room.’
