The rain had been falling on Bristol since dawn, a steady, grey percussion that washed the soot from the windowpanes of Mr. Elias Thorne’s counting house and left streaks like black tears on the sill. Elias sat at his mahogany desk, surrounded by the ledgers that represented twenty years of solitary, unimpeachable commerce. Since the age of forty, he had been a man of immaculate reputation, a senior partner in the firm of Thorne & Wexford, trading in Cuban sugar and Virginian tobacco. But before that, he had been something else entirely. He had been a thief, and worse, a coward.

He dipped his pen into the inkwell, the scratch of the nib against the parchment the only sound in the room. It was a sound he had cultivated, this quietness. A man who makes no noise attracts no suspicion. He had learned that lesson in the cruellest possible classroom.

A knock came at the heavy oak door, startling him so that a blot of ink marred the neat column of figures he was tabulating. He frowned, setting the pen down with deliberate care. He did not tolerate interruptions. His clerk, a timid man named Poole, knew better than to disturb him before the noon bell.

“Come,” Elias called, his voice clipped and low.

The door opened, but it was not Poole. It was a woman, young, perhaps twenty, with dark hair pulled back beneath a plain bonnet that had been sadly darkened by the rain. Her gown was of modest cotton, clean but visibly mended at the cuffs. She carried herself with a rigid, upright posture that spoke of a pride sustained against considerable pressure. Elias felt a cold knot tighten in his stomach, though he did not yet know why.

“Mr. Thorne?” she asked. Her voice was clear, carrying the faintest trace of an accent that placed her origin in the west country, perhaps Gloucestershire. “I beg your pardon for the intrusion. Your clerk was away from his desk, and the door was unlatched. I have come about the position. The advertisement in the Bristol Mercury, sir. For a confidential secretary.”

Elias regarded her for a long moment. He had placed the advertisement a week ago. His eyes were not what they had been, and the late nights spent verifying the accounts of the new Cuban venture were taking their toll. He required someone with a steady hand and a discreet temperament. He had expected a man, or at any rate a middle-aged matron. Not this girl, who looked barely out of the schoolroom.

“You are young,” he said, not unkindly, but with the blunt directness he used in all commercial dealings. “The work is demanding. I require someone who can decipher poor handwriting, tally complex columns, and hold their tongue in matters of trade. It is not a position for a girl fresh from the academy.”

The young woman did not flinch. She stepped closer to the desk, her hands clasped tightly before her. “I am one-and-twenty, sir. I was educated at the charity school in Tewkesbury. I can read, write, and figure as well as any clerk in the city. And I am accustomed to holding my tongue. It has been my primary occupation for most of my life.”

There was a quiet fierceness in her words that drew Elias’s attention. He studied her face more closely. She had a strong jaw, dark eyes, and a breadth to her brow that struck a chord of recognition so deep and so sudden that he felt the blood drain from his own cheeks. He knew that face. Not hers, precisely, but its architecture. The set of the mouth, the particular spacing of the eyes. He had seen that face across a crowded courtroom, twisted in disbelief and fury, twenty years ago.

The girl was a Wexford.

Elias’s hand, resting on the desk, began to tremble. He covered it with the other, pressing both flat against the wood to still the vibration. Marcus Wexford’s daughter. She had been an infant when Elias had stood in the dock at the Guildhall and sworn on the Bible that his partner had orchestrated the entire scheme, that the forged bills of exchange were in Marcus’s hand, that the missing funds had been gambled away on Marcus’s vices. All of it lies. Elias had taken the money to cover his own debts, debts accrued in foolish speculations and private gaming hells. And when the fraud was discovered, he had laid every scrap of evidence at his partner’s door.

Marcus had been transported to Van Diemen’s Land. He had died there, Elias had heard, of fever, seven years into his sentence. The firm, stripped of its disgraced partner, had been salvaged by Elias, who presented himself as the unwitting victim of a charlatan. He had rebuilt his fortune on the ashes of his friend’s life.

And now the friend’s daughter stood before him, asking for work.

“You say you are from Tewkesbury,” Elias said, his voice sounding strange and distant to his own ears. “That is a long way to travel for a clerical position.”

“I am from Bristol originally, sir. But circumstances compelled my mother and me to leave when I was a child. My mother passed last winter. I returned to Bristol to seek my fortune, or at least my sustenance. I have no family left to claim me.” She paused, and a shadow crossed her face. “My father died before I could know him. I am told he was a wicked man, but I do not remember him so. I remember only that he was taken away, and that my mother wept for him until she could weep no more.”

Elias stared at her. She did not know. Of course she did not know. She had been a babe in arms. She saw before her only a prosperous merchant, a man of substance, a potential employer. She did not see the man who had destroyed her family and built his own comfort upon the rubble.
