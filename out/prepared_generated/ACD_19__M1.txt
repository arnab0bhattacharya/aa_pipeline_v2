It was upon a raw and gusty afternoon in the late autumn of 1893 that a lady was shown into the somewhat dishevelled chambers of Mr. Cecil Arbuthnot, the private inquiry agent. She was a woman of some forty years, with a face that might have been handsome but for an expression of settled anxiety. Her black dress was of good material but cut with a certain severity, and she carried herself with the straight-backed dignity of one who has long been accustomed to command the attention of a room. I, having called upon Arbuthnot to return a borrowed volume, found myself an unwilling audience to her tale.

“My name, sir, is Miss Helena Vance,” she began, her voice a clear and well-modulated contralto. “I am a teacher of the pianoforte. My pupil is Miss Beatrice Ashworth, who resides with her guardian at number fourteen, Portman Square.”

Arbuthnot, who had been examining a lock mechanism with a jeweller’s glass, set his tools aside and gave her his full attention. He was a man of lean and hungry appearance, with a restless energy that belied his quiet manner. “A fashionable address. And the nature of your difficulty, Miss Vance?”

“It is not my difficulty, Mr. Arbuthnot, but I fear it may soon become Miss Ashworth’s. She is a girl of seventeen, an heiress, and of a nervous and imaginative temperament. For the past month, at every lesson, she has refused to play the exercises I set for her. Instead, she plays a different tune each time—a short, simple, and utterly unfamiliar melody. When I have questioned her, she becomes agitated and will say only that she learned it from a man who hums it beneath her window at night.”

“Indeed,” said Arbuthnot, leaning forward, his long, nervous fingers steepled before him. “A man beneath her window. Has she described him?”

“Only that he is a stranger to her, and that he stands in the mews at the rear of the house. Her guardian, Mr. Silas Croft, is a man of severe and practical disposition. He dismisses the matter as fancy, a girl’s romantic nonsense. He has forbidden me to question her further on the subject and has informed me that unless she resumes her proper studies, my services will no longer be required. But I am afraid for her, sir. The girl is genuinely distressed. She plays these tunes with a sort of desperate concentration, as if they were of the utmost importance.”

Miss Vance then produced a small roll of manuscript paper from her reticule. “I have taken the liberty of transcribing them. There are six in all.”

Arbuthnot took the papers and spread them upon his cluttered desk. I peered over his shoulder. They were simple musical phrases, written in a neat, precise hand. They bore no resemblance to any classical or popular melody I knew. They were strange, modal things, with awkward intervals and a haunting, incomplete quality.

“The work of a professional musician, I observe,” said Arbuthnot, his eyes scanning the notation. “The clefs are perfect, the time signatures consistent. You have an exact ear, Miss Vance.”

“I have been a teacher for twenty years, sir.”

“Quite so. And this Mr. Croft. He is the guardian, not the father?”

“The late Mr. Ashworth was his cousin. Mr. Croft has managed the estate and the girl’s affairs for the past five years.”

“And he discourages any inquiry into these nocturnal visitations?”

“He forbids it outright. He says it encourages morbid fancies.”

Arbuthnot’s gaze was fixed upon the musical lines. “Fancies, however, have a habit of being founded upon facts. Tell me, Miss Vance, what is your impression of Mr. Croft?”

She hesitated. “He is a man of business, very exact. He is not unkind to Beatrice, but he is… cold. He watches her very closely. Too closely, I sometimes think, for a guardian.”

“Does he have any musical interest himself?”

“None whatsoever. He considers it a frivolous accomplishment for a young lady. He is a partner in a shipping firm, I believe. He is often away on business.”

Arbuthnot gathered the sheets of music. “You have done very right in bringing this to me. Leave these with me for a day. I shall make one or two inquiries. Where can I find you?”

Miss Vance gave her address at a respectable boarding-house in Bloomsbury and took her leave, her worried air seeming to have lightened a little, as if the mere act of reporting the matter had been a relief.

“An odd business, Arbuthnot,” I remarked when the door had closed. “A girl’s romance, surely. A lovesick suitor with a talent for composition.”

Arbuthnot gave a thin smile. “A suitor who composes six different tunes and hums them in a mews? It is an inefficient method of courtship, my dear fellow. No, there is a system here which I do not yet comprehend. Observe.” He laid the sheets out again. “The tunes are different in key and rhythm, yet they share a certain structural logic. They are not the work of a madman or a dreamer. They are a code.”

“A code?”

“Of some sort. Musical, perhaps, but a code nonetheless. The question is, what is the message, and who is the intended recipient? The girl, or perhaps the guardian?” He tapped a long forefinger on the manuscript. “I must see this house in Portman Square.”

The following morning, a note from Arbuthnot summoned me to a small, rather shabby coffee-house that stood opposite number fourteen. It was a handsome townhouse of the Georgian style, with a black iron railing and a polished brass knocker. Arbuthnot, disguised in the rough tweed of a countryman, sat with a cup of cold coffee before him.

“I have been here since seven,” he murmured. “The household is Miss Ashworth, the maid, a cook, and the butler. Mr. Croft departed in a hansom an hour ago. The interesting feature is the mews to the rear. I have taken a look. A high wall separates it from the garden, but there is a drain-pipe that would present no difficulty to an agile man.”
