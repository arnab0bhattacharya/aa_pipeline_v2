The morning-room of Lady Marchmont’s house in Grosvenor Square was a symphony in the key of yellow—that soft, faded, exquisite yellow of old silk and Chinese lacquer, of daffodils dying gracefully in a crystal bowl, of the last leaves of autumn clinging to the bough. It was a room in which one might discuss anything, provided one discussed it beautifully, and in which the truth was permitted only when it was dressed as an epigram.

Mr. Julian Vane, who was young, who was brilliant, and who was poor, had the good fortune to be seated beside his hostess upon a sofa of primrose satin, and the still better fortune to be listening to her ladyship’s voice, which was like the sound of a violin played very softly in a room from which all the furniture had been removed.

“You are ambitious, Julian,” she said, tapping him lightly upon the arm with her fan. “It is your only fault, and it is a fault I find entirely charming. Ambition in a young man is like a diamond in a rather shabby setting—it is the setting that makes it appear so brilliant.”

“And is my setting so very shabby, Lady Marchmont?” he asked, with the air of one who knew his own worth and found it, on the whole, satisfactory.

“You have a beautiful voice, a beautiful face, and a beautiful manner,” she replied, counting them upon her fingers as though she were enumerating the points of a horse. “You have risen at the Bar with a rapidity that is positively indecent. You have never lost a case that you deserved to win, and you have won several that you did not. You are the most talked-of young man in London. And yet—you are not in Parliament.”

“Parliament,” said Julian, with a smile that was meant to be deprecating and was in fact a little cruel, “is the last refuge of the man who has failed at the Bar, and the first step of the man who has succeeded at everything else. I confess I have thought of it. But a seat in the House is not to be had for the asking, even by a barrister with a beautiful voice.”

“It is to be had for the asking,” said Lady Marchmont, “if one asks the right person. I have a great many friends, Julian, and I have a great many interests. I could put you into a safe seat tomorrow, if I chose.”

There was a pause. The daffodils in the crystal bowl seemed to hold their breath. Julian looked at her ladyship, and her ladyship looked at the fire, and the fire, being a mere fire, had nothing to say upon the subject.

“And what,” said Julian, at last, “would her ladyship choose as the price of her—interest?”

“I have a niece,” said Lady Marchmont, still addressing the fire. “Her name is Miss Eleanor Grey. She is nineteen, she is pretty in a rather obvious way, she has a fortune of forty thousand pounds, and she is the most tiresome girl in England. She reads Ruskin, and she has opinions about the poor. She will bore you to distraction, and you will make her miserable. But she is my brother’s child, and I am very fond of her, and I should like to see her married to a man of ability. You are a man of ability, Julian. Marry Eleanor, and the seat is yours.”

Julian Vane was a man who had never in his life been at a loss for a reply, and he was not at a loss now. He was merely silent. It was a silence that did him credit. He looked at Lady Marchmont’s profile—the delicate, arrogant, exquisite profile of a woman who had been beautiful for twenty years and intended to be beautiful for twenty more—and he thought that he had never seen anything so desirable in his life as the curve of her cheek and the shadow of her lashes on her skin.

“And if I refuse?” he said at last.

“You will not refuse,” said Lady Marchmont, turning to him with a smile that was as brilliant and as heartless as a diamond. “You are far too clever to refuse. You will marry Eleanor, and you will go into Parliament, and you will make a great speech in your first session that will be quoted in all the papers, and in ten years you will be a Cabinet Minister, and in twenty you will be Prime Minister, and you will dine with the Queen, and you will be perfectly miserable, and you will thank me for it.”

“And you?” said Julian, very quietly.

“I?” said Lady Marchmont, raising her eyebrows. “I shall be a great lady, and I shall give dinners, and I shall watch you rise, and I shall say to myself, ‘I made that man.’ It is a great deal better than marrying him.”

“Is it?” said Julian.

For the first time in the interview, Lady Marchmont looked slightly discomposed. She recovered herself in an instant, but the instant had been enough.

“You are impertinent, Julian,” she said. “It is one of your charms. But it is a charm that a Prime Minister cannot afford. Go and see Eleanor. She is in the conservatory, reading something improving. You will find her very dull, and you will be very charming to her, and she will fall in love with you, and you will marry her, and you will never be in love with her, and she will never know it. That is the whole art of a successful marriage.”

“And if I were to be in love with someone else?” said Julian, rising, and standing before her with a look that she had seen in his eyes once or twice before and had always pretended not to see.

“Then,” said Lady Marchmont, “you would be very foolish, and very unhappy, and very poor. And you are none of those things, Julian. You are clever, and you are charming, and you are going to be a great man. Go and see Eleanor.”
