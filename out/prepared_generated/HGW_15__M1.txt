The barometer in Mr. Hargreaves’s study was a beautiful, irrational thing, a brass and mahogany contraption he had bought second-hand from a retired naval man in Portsmouth and subsequently modified with his own peculiar additions. Beside it stood a self-registering anemometer of his own devising, its little cups spinning in the draught from the window, and a glass tube of his own construction which, he maintained, measured the electrical tension of the air with a sensitivity that put the official instruments at Kew to shame. It was this combination of amateur science and obsessive tinkering that had led him to the Great Prediction.

“It is not merely a storm, Mr. Pennyfeather,” he had insisted, leaning over the long oak table in the parish room, his face alight with an earnestness that the assembled council found merely tiresome. “It is a meteorological event of the first order. A cyclonic depression of exceptional depth is bearing down upon us from the Atlantic. The mercury in my barograph has fallen with a precipitancy I have never before witnessed. The electrical readings are… well, they are unprecedented.”

Mr. Pennyfeather, the vicar, a man whose benevolence was matched only by his profound ignorance of anything beyond his parochial duties, smiled a placid, dismissive smile. “A bit of rain, Hargreaves? We’ve had rain before. The harvest is nearly in. A good soaking will do the turnips a power of good.”

“It will not be a soaking!” Hargreaves’s voice rose, cracking with frustration. “The wind will come from the south-east, a direction from which we are sheltered, and thus we will be lulled into a false sense of security. Then it will veer, suddenly and with tremendous force. The old trees on the common will come down like ninepins. The roofs of the cottages will be stripped. I have been calculating the kinetic energy involved, and I tell you, it is formidable. We must take precautions.”

The council, a body of farmers, publicans, and shopkeepers, exchanged glances of weary amusement. There was old Mr. Giles, whose family had farmed at Nether Compton for four generations and who considered the sky his personal barometer; there was Mrs. Gable, the postmistress, who thought Hargreaves a “flighty fellow”; and there was the blacksmith, a practical man named Thorne, who trusted only the strength of his own hammer arm. To them, Hargreaves was the village eccentric, a man who preferred the company of his dials and gauges to that of his fellow men.

“My instruments do not lie,” Hargreaves said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “I have secured my own dwelling. The cellar is sound; I have shored up the beams and stocked it with provisions. I would advise you all to consider the safety of your own families.”

A general murmur of polite, condescending dismissal went round the table. The matter was settled. Hargreaves, defeated, gathered his charts and retreated to his house on the edge of the village, a man crying in a wilderness of English complacency.

For two days, nothing happened. The sky was a placid, milky blue. A gentle breeze whispered in the elms. The village, in its quiet way, enjoyed a small joke at the expense of the frantic meteorologist. Then, on the third afternoon, the change began. The light took on a strange, brassy quality. The air grew heavy and unnaturally still. Birds ceased their singing. The leaves of the lime trees on the green turned their pale undersides to the sky, as if in dread.

By dusk, the wind had arrived. It did not begin as a gale, but as a low, searching moan that found every crack and crevice in the old houses. It was the sound Hargreaves had predicted, the first stirrings of the cyclonic monster. He stood at his window, a grim satisfaction on his face, watching his anemometer’s cups begin their frantic dance. The rain, when it came, was not rain at all, but a solid, driving wall of water that fell with a hiss and a roar. The sky, a moment before a slate-grey lid, was ripped open by a flash of lightning that bleached the world white, followed a second later by a crack of thunder that seemed to split the very timbers of the house.

Then the wind veered. It came now not from the south-east, but from the north-west, a direction for which the village was wholly unprepared. It hit the cottages and farmhouses with the force of a battering ram. Hargreaves saw the roof of the old tithe barn peel away like paper. He saw the great oak on the common, a landmark for centuries, groan, splinter, and crash to the ground. The world outside his window dissolved into a chaotic vortex of water, wind, and flying debris.

Soon, the first of them came. It was young Thomas Thorne, the blacksmith’s son, his face white with terror, hammering on Hargreaves’s door. “Mr. Hargreaves! Mr. Hargreaves, for God’s sake! The roof’s gone! The water’s pouring in!” Hargreaves, with a sense of grim duty, ushered him into the cellar. Then came Mrs. Gable, weeping and clinging to her cash box. Then a farmer and his wife, soaked and shivering. His cellar, the object of so much ridicule, was becoming a sanctuary.

And then, through the maelstrom, he saw them. Three figures, struggling against the wind, clinging to each other for support. Even through the driving rain, he recognised the stooped form of the vicar, Mr. Pennyfeather; the sturdy, stoic frame of old Mr. Giles; and the powerful silhouette of the blacksmith, Thorne. They were making for his house, their faces, when a flash of lightning illuminated them, masks of primal fear. They hammered on the heavy cellar door, their cries nearly lost in the shriek of the wind.

“Hargreaves! Open up! In the name of God, man, open the door!”
