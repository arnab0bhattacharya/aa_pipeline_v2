The light in Menton did not strike; it pooled. It lay in the courtyard like spilled honey, thick and slow-moving, catching the dust motes that danced in the stillness of the afternoon. Eleanor sat at the table, her hands resting on the white linen, feeling the fabric’s texture against her palms, a sensation that seemed to vibrate up her arms and into the quiet, dusty corners of her mind. She was waiting for the soup, or perhaps for the end of the conversation, for the two things often seemed indistinguishable when one was alone with one’s thoughts in a foreign land.

Across from her, Mr. Thorne was trying to describe the sound of the sea. He was a young man, pale as the peony petals in the garden, recovering from a fever that had left him trembling at the edges of his sentences. He spoke of the water not as a thing of power, but as a rhythm, a metronome of the soul.

“It is not loud,” he said, his voice thin, stripped of its former vigor. “It is merely… persistent. Like a clock, but without the ticking. Just the swing.”

Eleanor nodded, though her eyes were fixed on the tablecloth. She was thinking of the governess in her own mind, the ghost of a girl in a blue dress she had taught ten years ago, the way the girl had looked at the sea, not with awe, but with a kind of weary curiosity, as if expecting it to recede and reveal a road. The sea here did not recede. It stayed, breathing against the rocks with a slow, tidal patience.

Mrs. Gable, the widow, arrived with the soup. She was a woman of substantial presence, her black dress cut high in the neck, her face a mask of polite indifference that masked a deep, churning sorrow. She placed the bowl before Eleanor with a precision that spoke of long practice in maintaining order amidst chaos. Her daughter, Clara, followed, spinning a chair with a lightness that belied the heaviness of their shared history. Clara was young, sharp-featured, with eyes that missed nothing, yet seemed to see only the surface of things, like a cat watching a mouse-hole.

“You are staring at the cloth, Mrs. Vane,” Clara said, not unkindly.

“I am listening to Mr. Thorne,” Eleanor replied, lifting the spoon. The soup was thin, tasting of vegetables and sun-warmed air. “He is trying to tell us what the sea sounds like to the sick.”

“Sickness changes the ear,” Mrs. Gable said, sitting down heavily. “It makes one hear the silence. I find I hear my own heart more than the waves.”

There was a pause, a gap in the conversation where the wind from the garden rustled the leaves of the olive tree, a sound like dry paper being crumpled. They were a collection of fragments, these four. Eleanor, the retired governess, who had spent her life guiding the minds of others and now found her own mind drifting, unmoored. Mr. Thorne, the convalescent, whose illness had peeled away the layers of his former self, leaving him raw and receptive. The Gables, bound together by grief, a knot that tightened with every shared meal. And then there was Mr. Halloway, the painter, who had not yet appeared.

Mr. Halloway always came last. He sat in the corner of the garden, his easel set up before a view that had become familiar to him, yet which he could no longer capture. The light changed, the shadows lengthened, and the colors on his canvas remained dull, lifeless, like dried blood. He was failing, they all knew it, though no one said it. His brushstrokes were hesitant, uncertain, as if he were apologizing for the existence of the objects he painted.

When he finally joined them, he carried with him the scent of turpentine and exhaustion. He did not speak, but sat down, his head bowed, his hands stained with blue and ochre. He looked at the soup, then at the others, and his eyes, usually bright with a manic energy, were dull and deep-set.

“The light is wrong today,” he said, his voice raspy. “It has no depth. It is flat.”

“Perhaps it is only the hour,” Mrs. Gable said, stirring her soup. “The light changes as we do.”

But Mr. Halloway did not hear her. He was looking at the table, at the white linen, at the shadows cast by the cups. He was trying to find the thing that was missing, the essence that would make the canvas breathe. He had painted the sea a hundred times, but it had never moved. He had painted the faces of the women, but their expressions had remained frozen, like wax masks. He was trapped in a world of surfaces, unable to penetrate to the core of the experience.

Eleanor watched him, feeling a strange kinship. She, too, had been trying to capture something, to hold it in place, to make it last. But the past, like the light, refused to be fixed. It slipped through her fingers, leaving only a faint, fading impression. She thought of the blue dress, the girl’s face, the sound of the sea. It was all there, present, yet inaccessible, like a word on the tip of the tongue.

The sun began to set, and the light in the courtyard changed. It turned from gold to amber, then to a deep, bruised purple. The shadows lengthened, stretching across the table, merging with the figures of the guests. For a moment, they were all connected, bound together by the common thread of their presence, their shared isolation. The silence was no longer empty; it was full, heavy with the unspoken.

Mr. Thorne looked up, his face flushed with the warmth of the setting sun. “I think I understand now,” he said softly. “The sea does not sound like a clock. It sounds like time itself. It is the measure of our passing.”
