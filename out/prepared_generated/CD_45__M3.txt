In the matter of Mr. Silas Pocklington’s money, there was no manner of doubt, no shadow of hesitation, no microscopic speck of uncertainty, that it was very extensive indeed. It clinked, it chinked, it rustled, it accumulated, it multiplied, it bred and brought forth in a manner that was almost miraculous, and certainly very satisfactory to Mr. Pocklington himself. He was a man of a heavy countenance, of a pursed mouth, and of an eye which, though it did not exactly twinkle, had a dull, leaden glimmer in it, like a half-extinguished candle in a very dark cellar. He had taken a raw, ragged, hollow-eyed boy from the gutter of Grub Street—a boy named Julian Larkspur—and had made a gentleman of him. He had washed him, he had brushed him, he had clad him in fine broadcloth, he had put him through his paces, and he had turned him out, as it were, a finished article, a very pattern of respectability, with a smooth tongue and a smoother coat.

‘My boy,’ Mr. Pocklington had been heard to observe, with that peculiar wheeze which served him for a laugh, ‘the world is an oyster, and I am the man who keeps the oyster-knife. You shall have the knife, Julian, and you shall open it for yourself. But remember the hand that sharpened the blade!’

To which Julian, being young and grateful and very hungry, had replied with a profusion of thanks, a torrent of promises, and a perfect cataract of devotion. And so it had gone on, year after year, in the great echoing country house at Mouldering St. Mary, where the curtains were heavy with the dust of ages, and the footmen were heavy with the sleep of ages, and the very pies upon the dinner-table seemed heavy with a settled and solemn despondency.

It was upon a dreary Tuesday in November, in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and thirty-something, that Julian Larkspur, sitting in the library at Mouldering St. Mary, with a book in his hand which he did not read, and a fire before him which he did not feel, came to a very singular and very uncomfortable resolution. He had found a letter. Not a romantic letter, not a tender epistle, not a billet-doux scented with rosewater and sealed with a heart, but a business letter, folded square, sealed with a wafer, and directed in a cramped and unhandsome hand. It had fallen from a bundle of old accounts, and Julian, being curious, had opened it. He had better not. The letter was from a Mr. Ezekial Grabb, of the island of Barbadoes, and it spoke of ‘the negroes,’ of ‘the crop,’ of ‘the market,’ and of ‘the necessary chains’ with a coolness, a composure, and a callousness that made Julian’s blood run cold. It spoke of them as so many head of cattle, so many machines for the production of sugar, so many units of profit and loss. And it was addressed to Silas Pocklington, and it referred to ‘our joint interests.’

Julian sat and stared at the letter. He stared at the fire. He stared at the ceiling. He stared at the heavy curtains. He stared at everything and saw nothing. The money which had made him a gentleman, which had paid for his tutor, his tailor, his horse, his club, his very breakfast and dinner and supper, was money that had been wrung from the sweat and blood and groans of enslaved men and women. It was blood-money. It was the price of human agony. It was the wages of sin, and Silas Pocklington had paid it, and he, Julian Larkspur, had spent it.

‘Tut!’ said Julian, aloud. ‘Tut, tut, tut!’

But ‘tut’ was a very insufficient expression for the turmoil of his mind. He walked up and down the library. He walked up and down the hall. He walked up and down the garden, where the dead leaves were falling, and the wind was howling, and the naked trees looked like so many skeletons dancing a dreary jig. He walked and he walked, and the more he walked, the more he thought, and the more he thought, the more he walked, until he had worn a path in the gravel and a hole in his conscience.

He must go to London. He must see Pocklington. He must know the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth, though the truth should be as bitter as gall and as sharp as a two-edged sword. So Julian packed his portmanteau, and took his seat upon the stagecoach, and was jolted, and rattled, and bumped, and jostled, and shaken, until he was internally a jelly and externally a bruise, and so arrived in the great city.

London was very London. The fog was very foggy. The mud was muddy in the extreme. The streets were a kaleidoscope of noise, and dirt, and hurry, and confusion. The hackney-coaches jostled the carts, the carts jostled the waggons, the waggons jostled the pedestrians, and the pedestrians jostled each other. The sweeps were sweeping, the criers were crying, the beggars were begging, the thieves were thieving, and the beadles were sleeping, all after their several fashions, and all with the greatest possible energy. It was a world of smoke, and steam, and soot, and din, and Julian Larkspur, fresh from the country, felt as if he had been plunged into a boiling cauldron, or thrust into the mouth of a volcano.

Mr. Pocklington’s counting-house was in Throgmorton Street. It was a dim, dull, dusty place, with high stools and higher desks, and a smell of leather and mildew that seemed to say, ‘I am old, I am damp, I am disagreeable, and I mean to remain so.’ Mr. Pocklington sat upon his stool, like a great toad upon a stone, and he looked at Julian with his leaden eye, and he said:

‘Well?’

‘Sir,’ said Julian, ‘I have found a letter.’
