Mrs. Stanhope had the talent, not uncommon among ladies of her station, of making a certainty out of a possibility; and when she raised her voice above the general hum of conversation at the Merriweather’s dinner table, it was with the air of one who had long been in possession of the facts. "Indeed, Mr. Thorne," she said, addressing the gentleman on her right with a beaming complacency that was more alarming than any alarm, "I believe I may say, without much fear of being contradicted, that the happy event will very shortly take place. My dear Harriet has only to give her consent, which I flatter myself she will not withhold, and the whole will be settled before the winter is quite over."

The silence that followed was of that particular kind which is not silence at all, but a sudden, collective holding of breath. Every eye at the table turned, not to Mrs. Stanhope, who was too well pleased with her own announcement to notice, but to the two persons whom it most nearly concerned. Mr. Thorne, a gentleman of quiet fortune and quieter habits, looked as though he had been struck by a sudden and unforeseen chill. He set down his fork with a precision that was itself a kind of commentary, and his colour, which was naturally high, deepened to a most uncomfortable degree. He did not look at Miss Harriet Fairweather.

Harriet, for her part, felt the blood rush to her face and then retreat again, leaving her pale and cold. The mortification was so complete, so public, and so entirely without remedy, that for a moment she could only sit and stare at her plate. To contradict her mother was impossible. It would be a scene of the most vulgar and distressing kind, a confirmation of the very impropriety her mother’s words had suggested. Yet to remain silent was to give her assent to a falsehood of the most damaging nature. She was, for the space of a single, dreadful heartbeat, quite at a loss.

It was in this crisis that the principles of a good education, and a naturally sensible disposition, asserted themselves. Harriet knew that a moment of such delicacy required not force, but finesse. She took a small sip of her wine, and when she spoke, her voice was perfectly even, betraying none of the agitation which consumed her. "Mama," she said, with a gentle and affectionate smile directed at Mrs. Stanhope, "you are as always too kind, and too hasty in your predictions. You give poor Mr. Thorne no time to breathe, and you quite forget that a gentleman’s consent is of as much importance as a lady’s. We should not presume to dispose of his future for him."

This was said with such a perfect air of filial teasing, of daughterly indulgence for a mother’s warm heart, that it seemed to excuse the original error while quietly dismantling it. She had turned the subject from a declaration of intent to a question of presumption. She had, without any show of contradiction, restored Mr. Thorne to the position of a free agent.

Mr. Thorne, who had been sitting in a state of rigid discomfort, seemed to draw a breath for the first time in a minute. He looked at Harriet then, a quick, grateful glance that acknowledged her tact. "Miss Fairweather is quite right," he said, his voice a little rough at the edges. "A gentleman would not wish to be thought so precipitate. The matter, I am afraid, is far less settled than Mrs. Stanhope’s goodness would have it."

The relief at the table was almost audible. Mrs. Merriweather, a woman whose chief concern was the smooth passage of her own dinner parties, quickly seized upon the opening. "Well, well," she declared, with a laugh that was intended to wave the whole affair away. "These things will out, you know, and who can wonder at a mother’s pride? But let us not speak of settlements when the trifle is still on the table. Mrs. Stanhope, you must tell us your opinion of the new curate at St. Giles's."

The conversation, with the agility of a well-trained animal, leapt to the new topic. Mr. Thorne was saved, and Mrs. Stanhope, though a little bewildered to find her triumph so quickly superseded, was not a woman to be long out of spirits. She began at once on the subject of the curate’s sermons, which she found too long.

Harriet, however, was not so easily restored. The poison, though the vial had been removed from the table, had been spilled. She could feel the curious and pitying glances of the other guests. She saw her younger sister, Charlotte, staring at her with wide, concerned eyes. She saw Mrs. Merriweather’s daughter, Amelia, hide a small, knowing smile behind her napkin. The evening, which had begun with such promise, was ruined. Her own reputation, and Mr. Thorne’s comfort, had been sacrificed to her mother’s indiscretion. She felt a sharp resentment, not so much towards her mother, who was incapable of malice, as towards the circumstance itself.

She ate the remainder of her meal in a state of forced composure, contributing little to the talk, but managing an assent or a polite inquiry when it was required. She was conscious of Mr. Thorne’s silence beside her. He too had retreated into himself, and the easy, pleasant acquaintance they had enjoyed for the past few weeks was now marred by a painful and awkward self-consciousness. It was impossible to speak to him of anything ordinary. The great, unspoken subject lay between them, a phantom at the feast.

When the ladies withdrew to the drawing-room, Harriet felt no relief. There, under the watchful eye of Mrs. Stanhope and the other matrons, the scrutiny would only intensify. She made her way to a quiet corner, pretending an interest in a piece of embroidery left on a side table, but she was aware of the low murmur of voices, in which she knew her own name was not infrequently mentioned.
