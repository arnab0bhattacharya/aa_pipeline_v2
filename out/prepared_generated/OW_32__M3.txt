The Kensington Ladies’ League for the Advancement of Plain Living was an assembly of exceedingly earnest women who had discovered, with all the trembling delight of a puritan in a secret garden, that simplicity is the most exhausting of all the social vices. They had secured the Brompton Athenaeum for a Friday afternoon lecture, and had hung its rather grim walls with unbleached linen, a material they considered supremely moral, chiefly because it is entirely devoid of beauty. The platform was draped in harsh sackcloth, and upon it sat a small, unpainted wooden chair, a deliberate insult to the upholsterer’s art.

The purpose of the meeting was a public refutation. Lord Arthur Melcombe, a celebrated young aesthete of terrifying perfection, had been invited to speak in defence of artifice, so that the League might decisively crush him. Mrs. Arbuthnot, the president, a woman whose virtue was as aggressively unadorned as her bonnet, had arranged the spectacle. She felt that to triumph over an opponent, one must first permit him to speak, a strategic error that has been the ruin of every well-meaning fanatic since the dawn of conversation.

Lord Arthur entered the hall precisely at four o’clock. He was exquisitely dressed in a suit of dark green velvet, wearing a single orchid of such scandalous purple that it seemed to reproach the unbleached linen. He carried a slender ivory cane. He did not look like a man about to be refuted; he looked like a man who had come to forgive his refuters for their complexions.

He was greeted by a silence so heavy with disapproval that it might have been a pew. Mrs. Arbuthnot rose and introduced him with a chilling politeness, her voice possessing the cadence of an executioner who has been taught to play the harp. She expressed her hope that his arguments, however ingeniously spun, would soon be scattered by the fresh, wholesome winds of common sense. Lord Arthur bowed, smiled a smile that seemed to hold a secret entirely for itself, and placed a small, heavily gilded volume upon the unpainted table beside him.

“Ladies,” he began, in a voice of languid music, “you have asked me to defend the cult of beauty. I confess I am at a loss. To defend beauty is to confess that it is in danger. It is only the ugly that requires defending; it is only the useless that demands an apology. You have draped this hall in sackcloth. I congratulate you. Sackcloth is the only material that makes one truly appreciate silk. It is the necessary shadow that gives form to the light. But to make a religion of the shadow is a theological blunder of the gravest order.”

A murmur of indignation fluttered through the room like a frightened sparrow. Mrs. Arbuthnot looked severely at her Vice-President, Mrs. Pendennis, a lady who had renounced pearls on philosophical grounds and consequently suffered from a chronic melancholy.

“You tell us,” Lord Arthur continued, lighting a cigarette, for he found the atmosphere of virtue somewhat stifling, “that plain living is a virtue. But what is a virtue? A virtue is merely a weakness that has discovered an alibi. You renounce the comforts of life because you are afraid of them. You call it simplicity. I call it cowardice. To be entirely simple is to be entirely barren. Nature is simple, and Nature is gross. She produces mud, and insects, and the lower classes. Art is complex. Art produces jewels, and tapestry, and the exact shade of a perfectly mixed absinthe. The true aim of life is not to live plainly, but to live beautifully. A life without ornament is a sentence without punctuation; it simply runs on and on, and means nothing in particular.”

“Surely, Lord Arthur,” interjected Mrs. Pendennis, unable to contain herself, “you cannot mean to suggest that a gold candlestick is morally superior to a human soul?”

“I mean to suggest that a gold candlestick is vastly more agreeable to look at,” replied Lord Arthur, with perfect composure. “The human soul, dear lady, is a most depressing article. It is invariably in a state of moral anxiety, or spiritual exhaustion. A gold candlestick simply shines. It asks nothing, regrets nothing, and remains eternally graceful. Morality is merely the vocabulary we use to describe the things we are afraid to enjoy. When you tell me that a woman is good, I know that she is plain. When you tell me that she is wicked, I know that she is fascinating.”

“Then you believe in art for art’s sake?” asked Mrs. Arbuthnot, her tone implying she was pronouncing a fatal disease.

“I believe in myself for my own sake,” answered Lord Arthur. “And since I am the only thing in the universe that interests me profoundly, I surround myself with things that please me. A perfectly cut diamond is a far more honest companion than a social reformer. The diamond does not wish to improve me. It merely exists, brilliantly and without apology. To improve oneself is to admit that one was originally a mistake. I prefer to consider myself a masterpiece, and masterpieces do not apologize for their brushstrokes.”

He paused, adjusting the orchid in his buttonhole. The hall was silent, but it was no longer the silence of hostility. It was the silence of enchantment. The ladies had come to crush a libertine; they found themselves listening to a prophet. The unbleached linen on the walls suddenly appeared to them not as a symbol of moral purity, but as a dreary absence of imagination. Mrs. Pendennis looked down at her own unpainted wooden chair and experienced a sharp, entirely unexpected pang of aesthetic revulsion.

“You ask us to abandon plain living,” said a young woman from the back row, a Miss Arabella Vance, who had hitherto been a fierce advocate for unpolished furniture. “But what are we to put in its place? We cannot all live in palaces.”
