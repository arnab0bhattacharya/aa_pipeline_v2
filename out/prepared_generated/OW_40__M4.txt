The season was at its most brilliant, and Mrs. Vandeleur’s drawing-room, that evening, was the very temple of urbanity, filled with the soft rustle of silks and the sharper music of epigrams. It was there that Basil Hallward—no, not the painter, but a young man of equal talent for self-invention, whose life was his only masterpiece—stood with his back to the fire, making a small, exquisite scandal.

‘I assure you,’ he was saying, with the air of one imparting a scientific truth, ‘the whole thing is a matter of will. The affections, my dear Ferrers, are not a mystery; they are a problem in dynamics. One has merely to apply the proper force at the proper angle. Love is not a blind god; he is a mathematician, and his calculations are invariably correct.’

Mr. Ferrers, a young man whose principal occupation was to be seen everywhere and to see nothing, raised his eyebrows in a manner that was intended to convey a profound and weary scepticism. ‘My dear Basil, you talk of love as if it were a game of billiards. One cannot make a woman fall in love with one as one makes a cannon off the red.’

‘And why not?’ cried Hallward, with a delightful laugh. ‘It is a game, Ferrers, the most amusing game in the world, and I am the most skilful player. I will wager you, here and now, a hundred pounds to a pair of your deplorable gloves, that I can make the coldest woman in London fall in love with me within a month.’

A little ripple of interest, like a breeze over a field of wheat, passed through the group. ‘The coldest woman in London?’ said a lady with a tiara of diamonds that seemed to have been borrowed from a more magnificent epoch. ‘You are very bold, Mr. Hallward. Who is she?’

‘There can be only one,’ he replied, with a bow that was a masterpiece of mock humility. ‘The divine, the impregnable, the entirely marmoreal Mrs. Musgrave.’

The name fell upon the room like a drop of ice in a glass of champagne. Mrs. Musgrave, the beautiful widow of a very rich and very dull man, was the acknowledged queen of a certain frozen altitude of society. She was a woman of perfect features and perfect manners, who had never been known to express a preference, to betray an emotion, or to commit the solecism of being interested. She was a statue in a gallery of living pictures, and her admirers, of whom there were many, were like pilgrims at a shrine—they might leave their offerings, but they could expect no miracle.

‘Impossible,’ said Ferrers, decisively. ‘She is a woman of marble. She would listen to a declaration of love as she would listen to a report on the state of the weather—with polite indifference.’

‘My dear fellow, that is the very charm of the experiment,’ said Hallward, his eyes gleaming with the pure joy of the strategist. ‘To make a woman of feeling love you is a mere vulgarity; it is an affair of propinquity and a well-turned phrase. But to make a woman of ice, to see the thaw, to watch the first treacherous drop of sentiment fall from that faultless brow—that is a triumph of art. I accept the challenge. A month, I said. I shall do it in three weeks.’

And so the wager was made, and the news of it, as news of such delicious impertinences will, flew through the town on the wings of the morning. It was discussed over teacups in Mayfair and over brandy in St. James’s. It added a new and piquant interest to the prospect of Mrs. Musgrave’s Thursday ‘At Homes,’ to which the fashionable world repaired to pay its respects to her perfect, and perfectly unresponsive, beauty.

Hallward’s campaign was a masterpiece of subtlety. He did not besiege her with flowers or sonnets; such methods were for the sentimental. He laid siege to her mind. He discovered, through diligent inquiry, that she was a collector of antique cameos, and he became, overnight, an authority on the glyptic art. He did not present her with jewels; he presented her with his knowledge. He would stand by her cabinet of treasures and discourse, in his most musical voice, on the intaglio cutting of the Alexandrian Greeks, on the symbolism of the winged Psyche, on the curious history of the Farnese Tazza, with such a flow of effortless erudition that she, who had thought her little hobby a private and rather lonely passion, found herself listening, at first with a detached curiosity, and then with a growing and dangerous interest.

He never spoke of himself. He made her the sole subject of their conversations. He drew her out on her opinions of Balzac and of the new French poetry, on the absurdity of the modern stage, on the necessity of beauty in domestic architecture. He agreed with her with a flattering enthusiasm, and disagreed with her with an even more flattering respect. He was the perfect mirror, and she, who had been so long accustomed to admire herself in the dull glass of society’s approval, found his reflection infinitely more brilliant and more true.

The third week was drawing to its close. The wager was as good as won. He had but to speak the word. He called upon her one afternoon, in her boudoir, a room of pale grey and silver, where the light fell softly through curtains of old lace, and where the only note of colour was a single bowl of white roses. She was sitting by the fire, a book of poetry in her lap, and she looked up as he entered, and for the first time, her smile was not merely a courtesy, but a welcome.

‘I have been reading your favourite, Verlaine,’ she said, closing the book. ‘He is very strange, very modern. He makes one feel that sorrow is a form of elegance.’
