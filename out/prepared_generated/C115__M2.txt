The air in the laboratory of St. Jude’s University was thick with the scent of ozone and burnt sugar, a byproduct of the volatile compounds Dr. Arthur Penhaligon had been distilling for the better part of a decade. It was a Tuesday in late October, the kind of damp, grey English day where the sky over the Midlands seemed to press down upon the slate roofs of the town of Oakhaven, squeezing the life from the cobblestones. Arthur stood before his workbench, his eyes red-rimmed and wild, holding a glass vial no larger than a thimble. Inside, a liquid shimmered with an iridescent, oily sheen that defied the dim light of the gas lamps. It was not merely a chemical; it was a violation of the natural order. He called it Null-Gravitate.

Arthur had spent the last hour in a state of manic agitation, refusing to let the university’s official testing protocols slow him down. The theoretical framework was sound, but theory was a cold comfort when one held the key to the universe’s most fundamental law in one’s hand. He needed a control. He needed something simple, something that could not scream or run. His gaze drifted past the cluttered desk, past the stacks of journals on Newton and Hooke, to the garden chair parked in the corner of the laboratory. It was a wicker seat, slightly splintered, where his housekeeper, Mrs. Elara Thorne, often sat to wait for him to finish his lectures. She was a woman of few words and immense patience, who believed that the world was a fixed and orderly place, much like the clock on the mantelpiece.

"Mrs. Thorne," Arthur called out, his voice trembling with a mix of excitement and terror. "Come here. I need you to sit on the chair."

Elara emerged from the shadows of the bookshelf, wiping her hands on her apron. She looked at the vial, then at the chair, and finally at her employer. "Is it safe, sir? You look unwell."

"It is not a matter of safety, Elara. It is a matter of truth," Arthur said, his hands shaking as he uncorked the vial. "Sit. Do not move."

Elara sighed, the sound of a woman who had cleaned the floors of this man’s ambitions for thirty years. She walked to the chair and sat down, her posture rigid, her hands folded in her lap. The wicker creaked under her weight, a mundane sound in a room filled with extraordinary potential. Arthur approached with the vial, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. He calculated the dosage. One drop. Just one. If he misjudged it, he might not just lift the chair; he might shatter the molecular bonds of the air itself.

He brought the vial to the center of the seat, directly beneath Elara’s hips. "Close your eyes," he whispered. "Look at the stars, Elara. That is where we are going."

He tilted the vial. A single drop of the iridescent liquid fell. It struck the wicker, hissing softly, not with heat, but with a sudden, violent absence of sound. For a second, nothing happened. Elara opened one eye, ready to complain about the delay. Then, the chair lifted.

It did not fly. It did not rush. It simply ceased to be heavy. The wicker frame rose with the slow, deliberate grace of a balloon released from a child’s hand. Elara’s hands gripped the armrests, her knuckles turning white, but her feet remained in the air, hovering inches above the floorboards. The floor, the tables, the laboratory equipment—all of it began to fall away from her, dropping downward at a terrifying acceleration.

"Arthur!" she screamed, but the sound was swallowed by the rushing wind that began to build as they breached the window.

Arthur lunged for the chair, his fingers brushing the hem of Elara’s dress, but the Null-Gravitate effect was absolute. The chair was already through the glass, which shattered not with a crash but with a silent, explosive burst of prisms. Elara was gone, drifting out into the October twilight, rising above the ivy-choked brick walls of the university.

Arthur ran to the window, his face pressed against the broken frame. Below, the courtyard was empty. Above, a small, dark speck was ascending into the grey canopy of clouds. He saw the wicker chair, and in it, the silhouette of a woman in a grey dress, her arms outstretched, her apron fluttering like a sad flag.

The town of Oakhaven was quiet. The gas lamps were just beginning to flicker on, casting pools of amber light on the wet streets. Elara drifted over the roof of the town hall, the copper tiles glistening with rain. She saw the spire of the church, the chimneys of the bakeries, the slate roofs of the terraced houses. She was moving slowly, perhaps ten feet per second, a gentle ascent that was more horrifying than any fall. She could see the faces of the townspeople looking up. A boy stopped playing in the street, his mouth agape. A dog barked, but the sound was too far below to reach her ears.

As she cleared the highest point of the church spire, the wind grew stronger, cold and biting. Elara looked down at the town she had served for decades. It looked so small, so insignificant. The cobblestones were like pebbles; the people were like ants. She thought of the clock on the mantelpiece, the fixed and orderly world. It was not orderly. It was chaotic, and it was indifferent.

She drifted further, leaving the gaslight glow of Oakhaven behind. The sky above was not yet dark, but the stars were beginning to pierce through the clouds. She saw the constellations, not as myths, but as cold, distant fires. She realized then that Arthur had not just defeated gravity; he had defeated the connection. She was unmoored from the earth, from the soil, from the history of the place. She was becoming part of the void.
