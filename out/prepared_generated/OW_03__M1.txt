It was universally admitted that the Princess Seraphina of Aeloria had been fashioned by Nature in a mood of exquisite, if somewhat wilful, extravagance. She possessed the complexion of a white camellia kept too long from the sun, hair that suggested a sudden nocturnal sunset, and eyes of so profound a violet that they appeared to be perpetually mourning some lost, but quite uninteresting, god. To these charms she added a wit that was as sharp as a stiletto and considerably more dangerous, for it left no wounds that could be displayed to the public.

Her father, King Theron, was a man of simple pleasures and large appetites, who ruled the marble terraces of Aeloria with the benevolent tyranny of one who believes that gold can cure a headache. He adored his daughter with the helpless, bewildered affection one feels for a masterpiece one does not understand. Yet, the peace of the kingdom—and the King’s digestion—was constantly disturbed by the Princess’s refusal to be wed.

The suitors who crowded the alabaster halls were a tedious assortment of humanity. There was the Duke of Ombria, who wore his virtues as heavily as his velvet robes, and spoke of duty until the flowers in the conservatory wilted from sheer boredom. There was the Prince of the Eastern Isles, a creature of beautiful face and absolutely no conversation, who sat upon his divan like a handsome piece of porcelain, terrified that a sudden movement might crack his reputation. They brought her rubies that bled in the candlelight, emeralds green as the envy of the court, and fabrics so fine they seemed woven from captured mist.

Seraphina accepted these tributes with the bored grace of a cat accepting a mouse. She would hold a diamond up to the light, examine it with a critical eye, and say, "It is very bright, Duke, but I fear it lacks imagination. It merely reflects the candles. I require something that reflects the soul."

Finally, tired of the parade, she announced her decree from the balcony of the Rose Pavilion. "I shall marry," she declared, her voice silencing the wind in the cypresses, "the man who can bring me something I have never seen before."

A hush fell over the marble city. It was a condition both simple and impossible. Seraphina had seen everything. She had seen the Northern Lights painted upon the domes of the observatory; she had seen the rare white tigers of the Forbidden Forest, brought in golden cages; she had seen the tears of poets and the ledgers of merchants. To bring her novelty was to bring her the impossible.

Kings sent envoys with chests of strange treasures—clocks that struck the hours in the voices of birds, maps of lands that did not exist, and statues carved from ice that melted into music. Wise men brought wonders of science: lenses that could see the germs in a drop of water, and mechanical birds that flew but did not sing. Seraphina rejected them all. "It is merely machinery," she would say. "It is merely a trick of the light. It is merely... ordinary."

Then, one morning, as the sun turned the marble of the palace to gold and the shadows of the forest stretched long fingers toward the gates, a commotion arose in the courtyard. A shepherd boy stood there, his tunic the color of dried earth, his feet bare and stained with the clay of the riverbank. His name was Lysander, and he was poor in everything except eyes, which were as clear and grey as a winter sky.

The guards, accustomed to the silks and furs of princes, moved to drive him away with their spears. "He smells of the sheep-pen," sneered the Captain of the Guard. "He brings nothing but the stench of the fields."

"I bring a gift for the Princess," said Lysander. His voice was quiet, but it carried the strange, unpolished honesty of the forest.

The King, hearing the noise, came to the balcony. "Let him approach," Theron said, more out of a desire for amusement than hope. "It has been a dull morning. Let us see what the shepherd has found."

Lysander was brought into the Hall of Mirrors, a room designed to make the visitor feel small and the Princess divine. Seraphina sat upon her throne of ivory and pearl, looking like a lily growing in a snowdrift. She looked down at the boy with a mixture of pity and disdain.

"Well, my rustic friend," she said, her voice dripping with the honeyed sarcasm that was her favorite weapon. "Have you brought me a flower that sings? Or perhaps a stone that weeps? I warn you, I am in no mood for pastoral charms."

Lysander did not bow. He held out a small, earthenware bowl, filled to the brim with still water. "I have brought you this, Princess," he said.

The courtiers tittered. The King coughed to hide a laugh. "Water?" cried the Duke of Ombria. "He brings her water? Does he think she is a lily that needs watering?"

"It is not just water," Lysander said, stepping forward. "It is the water of the pool in the deep forest, where the ferns grow taller than men. I have carried it for three days, keeping it still, so that it would not forget what it holds."

"And what does it hold?" Seraphina asked, leaning forward, her curiosity piqued despite herself.

"It holds you," said the boy.

He walked to the foot of the throne and knelt, placing the bowl upon the marble steps. The water was perfectly still, a dark, liquid eye. Seraphina rose and descended the steps, her silk gown whispering against the stone. She looked into the bowl.

There, framed by the rough clay of the rim, was her own face.
