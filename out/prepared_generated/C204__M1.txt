In the Royal Garden of Al-Jazira, where fountains whispered secrets to the turquoise pools and the air was thick with the scent of night-blooming jasmine, lived Kamal. Kamal was a peacock, and in his own estimation, the most magnificent creature to have ever graced the earth. His existence was a symphony of colour and form, a living jewel designed to be adored. Each morning, he would begin his ritual, strutting to the edge of the great marble fountain to admire his reflection. He would tilt his crested head, admiring the perfect curve of his neck, a cascade of iridescent blue and green that shimmered like precious stones. He would turn, slowly, deliberately, to watch the thousand eyes of his tail fan open in a glorious, shimmering cascade. He was a masterpiece. The crimson hibiscus, the golden marigolds, even the peacock-blue tiles of the palace walls were but pale imitations of his splendour.

The garden was his stage, and the courtiers and handmaidens his audience. They would pause in their duties to praise him. "Behold Kamal," a gardener might say to a visiting merchant, "the pride of Al-Jazira, a vision of the divine." Kamal would accept this adoration as his due, puffing out his chest and letting his train drag regally behind him. He believed that beauty was the highest virtue, the ultimate power. What could be more important than being the most beautiful thing in the world? It was a truth as solid and unmoving as the palace itself.

His peace was disrupted by the arrival of the grey bird. She was small, unassuming, and utterly forgettable. Her feathers were the colour of ash and dust, without a single thread of gold or a hint of sapphire. She had no crest, no shimmering train, no proud stance. She was, to Kamal’s eyes, an insult to the very concept of beauty. He watched with disdain as she hopped along the gravel paths, pecking at stray seeds. He saw her one morning perched on a low branch of a gnarled olive tree, not far from the sultan’s private balcony. He expected her to simply exist, to be a dull, moving patch of grey against the garden’s vibrant tapestry. He did not expect her to sing.

The sound that emerged from her tiny beak was not a squawk or a chirp, but something else entirely. It began as a soft, silver note that hung in the morning air, then spiralled into a cascade of liquid melody. The song was a story without words; it spoke of the dawn’s first light on the desert sands, of the deep cool of an oasis, of the sorrow of a lone palm tree and the joy of a sudden, life-giving rain. It was a pure, unadorned sound that seemed to stitch the very fabric of the morning together. The garden, for a moment, grew still. The fountain’s whisper seemed crude, the bees’ hum a dull drone. All sound was held captive by the grey bird’s voice.

Kamal was indignant. He ruffled his magnificent feathers, his crest bristling. A song? What was a song compared to a visual spectacle of such breathtaking magnificence? He strutted beneath the olive tree, spreading his tail in a defiant display of unparalleled splendour. He fanned the great ocelli, each one a perfect, shimmering eye of gold and emerald, and let out a harsh, triumphant cry. He was certain the grey bird would fall silent in awe, that the entire garden would turn its gaze to him. But the song did not stop. It flowed on, a river of sound, ignoring him completely. The grey bird, it seemed, did not even notice his magnificent performance.

The true blow fell the next day. The sultan, Sultan Rostam, a man known for his appreciation of all things exquisite, stepped onto his balcony. He was a collector of rare silks, of flawless emeralds, of perfectly trained hawks. He was the ultimate connoisseur. Kamal, seeing his master, immediately began his grandest display. He fanned his tail, shimmering and turning, catching the morning sun so that a thousand points of light danced across his feathers. He was a living firework, a testament to the pinnacle of natural artistry.

But Sultan Rostam’s gaze did not fall upon him. The sultan leaned against the marble balustrade, his eyes closed, a look of profound peace on his face. He was listening to the grey bird, who was singing her heart out from the same olive branch. The sultan’s fingers tapped a gentle rhythm on the stone. A slow smile spread across his lips. "Such a voice," he murmured to his attendant, his voice carrying on the still air. "It fills the soul. More precious than all the jewels in my treasury."

Kamal froze, his train half-fanned. The words struck him like a physical blow. More precious than jewels? This… this *nothing* of a bird, with its drab feathers and its simple song? It was impossible. It was a grotesque error of judgement. He felt a hot, unfamiliar shame burn in his chest. He, Kamal, the vision of the divine, was being ignored in favour of a common songbird. He snapped his tail shut with a loud rustle and stalked away, his usual confident strut reduced to a flustered scurry. He hid behind a large flowering bougainvillea, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs.

For days, Kamal watched and brooded. Each morning, the sultan would emerge onto his balcony, not to gaze upon the garden’s colours, but to listen. The grey bird’s song became the most anticipated event of the day. The gardeners would pause their work, the guards would stand a little straighter, and the sultan would appear utterly transported. The song was the new jewel in the crown of Al-Jazira, and Kamal was merely a piece of forgotten decoration.
