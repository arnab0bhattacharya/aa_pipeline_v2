The path up Monte Scuro was less a path than a suggestion, a pale scar of crushed limestone winding through the dense, aromatic scrub. Below them, the Bay of Valletiera lay flat and dazzling, the water a hard, bright enamel that hurt the eyes to look at. The heat was a physical weight, pressing down on the four Englishmen as they climbed, drawing the moisture from their bodies and fraying the edges of their civility. It was the third week of July, 1908, and they had been on the island of Merosa long enough to tire of each other’s company but not long enough to part.

Cecil Vance led the way, his Alpine stock striking the white rock with a rhythmic, irritating clack. He was a man who believed that a holiday was merely an opportunity to apply the same discipline one exercised in the City to a more rugged landscape. He wore a tweed jacket, despite the temperature, and his neck was the color of boiled lobster beneath the brim of his hat. Behind him came his wife, Eleanor, her face hidden beneath a wide veil, her breath coming in short, quiet gasps. She had not wanted to climb the mountain. She had wanted to sit in the shaded café by the harbor and read her book, but Cecil had insisted on the expedition, and so she had come.

Trailing them by a few paces were Arthur Pendle and Miss Clara Ffoulkes. Arthur was a young man of comfortable but not excessive means, visiting Merosa to recover from an unspecified disappointment, the nature of which he had not yet disclosed to anyone. Clara was a friend of Eleanor’s, invited along to balance the numbers, a young woman with sharp dark eyes and a tendency to observe people as though they were specimens in a case. She carried a parasol but did not open it, using it instead to push aside the branches of the wild myrtle that encroached on the path.

“I fail to see,” Cecil said, pausing to wipe his forehead with a handkerchief, “why the local authorities cannot be persuaded to cut a proper road. One might make a decent ascent of the thing in a motorcar if they had any enterprise.”

“They seem to manage perfectly well without motorcars,” Arthur said, coming up behind them, his face flushed and damp. “Perhaps they don’t see the point of hurrying to the top.”

“The point,” Cecil said, turning to look at him with mild contempt, “is the view. And the exercise. One does not climb a mountain for the sake of lingering on it.”

“One climbs it, presumably, because it is there,” Clara said, smiling slightly.

Cecil gave her a look that suggested she was being impertinent, then turned and resumed his march. Eleanor glanced at Clara, a look of fleeting sympathy, and then lowered her eyes to the path.

They climbed for another quarter of an hour in silence, the only sounds the scrape of boots on stone, the distant, metallic whir of cicadas, and the occasional bleat of a goat somewhere in the hills. The vegetation grew sparser as they rose, the myrtle giving way to thyme and wild sage, and then to bare white rock that radiated heat like the wall of an oven. Eleanor stopped, pressing a hand to her side.

“I must rest,” she said.

Cecil turned, his expression caught between impatience and duty. “We’re not halfway yet, Ellie.”

“I must rest,” she repeated, and her tone was such that he did not argue. He stood awkwardly while she sat on a boulder, fanning herself with her handkerchief. Arthur offered her his flask, and she drank from it, a trickle of water running down her chin.

Clara sat down a little apart, pulling her hat off and letting the faint breeze stir her hair. Arthur went to her, standing close enough to be conversational but far enough to maintain propriety.

“You find this agreeable?” he asked, nodding toward the landscape.

“I find it beautiful,” she said. “And rather merciless. I think beautiful places are often merciless, don’t you? They don’t care whether you survive them or not.”

Arthur considered this. “I suppose that’s true. The sublime is always a little threatening. Burke would say that’s the point.”

“Burke would say anything if you paid him enough attention,” Clara said, and Arthur laughed, a sound that seemed to surprise him. He sat down beside her, and for a moment they looked out over the bay together, the water shimmering, a fishing boat leaving a slow wake of white.

Below them, Eleanor was speaking to Cecil in a low voice, something about the hotel, the heat, the way he had spoken to the waiter that morning. Cecil replied in clipped tones, and Eleanor turned her face away. Arthur and Clara pretended not to notice.

“You seem unhappy,” Clara said quietly, still looking at the view.

Arthur was silent for a long moment. “I was engaged,” he said. “It ended. In April. She decided she preferred someone else, a man with a larger estate in Yorkshire. I came here because my mother thought the sea air would be good for me.”

“And is it?”

“Not particularly. But the company has improved of late.”

Clara looked at him, her expression unreadable. “You shouldn’t say things like that, Mr. Pendle. People might think you meant them.”

“I do mean them.”

“Then you shouldn’t say them all the more,” she said, but there was no severity in her voice. She put her hat back on and stood up. “Shall we go on?”

They resumed the climb, Cecil setting a slightly slower pace, whether out of consideration for Eleanor or because the heat had finally subdued even his energy, it was impossible to say. The path grew steeper, zigzagging up the final slope, and they fell into a single file, each wrapped in private thought. The conversation died. The mountain seemed to demand silence, or perhaps it was simply that they had exhausted the small talk that had sustained them and the larger talk was too dangerous to attempt.
