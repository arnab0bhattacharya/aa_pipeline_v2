If any mortal wanderer, straying by the muddy and malodorous banks of the Thames in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and forty-something, had occasion to pass through the narrow and crooked lane known as Pickled-Herring Court, he might have stumbled upon a low-roofed, queer-smelling establishment bearing the painted sign of The Jolly Boatswain. Here, behind a pane of glass that seemed to have been dredged up from the river-bed itself, reposed the culinary triumphs of Mr. Gideon Gargoyle.

Mr. Gargoyle was a retired sea-cook. He was a man of such vast circumference and deep-sea gravity that he appeared to have been permanently expanded by a lifetime of inhaling boiling pitch and salt beef. His face was the colour of a well-smoked ham, and his voice, which boomed from the very depths of his barrel-like chest, sounded perpetually as though it were contending with a roaring nor’easter. Having survived forty years of weevily biscuit and maggoty pork, Mr. Gargoyle had invested his life’s savings—a canvas bag of heavy, clinking silver—into this pie-shop, determined to feed the landsmen of Rotherhithe with the honest crustaceans and pulpy vegetables they so sorely lacked.

But the sea, though left behind, clings to a man like a wet sou’wester. Mr. Gargoyle’s pies were nautical, tremendous, and uncompromising. They were pies built to weather a storm; pies of such stern, heavy, iron-clad pastry that to pierce one required the cutlass of a determined man, and to digest one required the iron constitution of a mariner. Eel, muscle, and mysterious globules of the deep were encased within, and though the shop was honest, the good people of the river-side regarded his provisions with a timid and respectful awe, buying but sparingly.

It was upon a damp and sleety Tuesday, when the river-fog crept up the court like a blind man’s dog, that Mr. Gargoyle first beheld the boy. He was a ragged, sharp-eyed, ferret-faced youth, with a nose that seemed built to sniff out a hot crust through a brick wall, and a cap that had once been blue, pulled down over his ears as if to smother them entirely.

‘Halloa, Admiral!’ cried the boy, tapping the glass with a forefinger that was far too knowing for its size. ‘Baked the main-sail today, have ye?’

‘What’s that you say?’ roared Mr. Gargoyle, emerging like a tidal wave from the back-parlour. ‘Speak up, boy! My ears are furred with the spray of forty latitudes!’

‘I says, are they eels or serpents in they pies?’ asked the boy, with an unblinking, impudent grin. ‘’Cos if they’re serpents, I’ll take two for the gentleman at the crossing. But if they’re eels, I’ll take six. I’m Tipples, I am. Quick Tipples. And I knows every empty belly from the bridge to the docks.’

Mr. Gargoyle, who was a tender-hearted man beneath his barnacled exterior, and who had not sold six pies in a single day since his opening, looked at the boy’s ragged elbows and felt a paternal squall rising in his chest.

‘You look as though you have been wrecked on a desert island and lived on sawdust,’ said Mr. Gargoyle. ‘Take a tray, you impudent young monkey, and if you can sell my wares, I’ll give you a penny in the shilling.’

Quick Tipples needed no second bidding. He seized a wooden tray, piled it high with the heavy, leathery pies, and vanished into the fog like a gull into the mist.

And O, what a transformation did that boy work upon the Jolly Boatswain! He was a very commercial tempest. He popped up in alleys, he haunted the wharves, he boarded the barges. He charmed the lightermen, he baffled the mudlarks, he sang the praises of Mr. Gargoyle’s pastry to the very horses dragging coal-wagons over the cobblestones. The shop’s trade doubled in a week. It tripled in a fortnight. Mr. Gargoyle, beaming like a figurehead in a following sea, rolled out pastry morning, noon, and night, blessing the day Quick Tipples had tapped upon his glass.

‘You’re a smart lad, Tipples,’ said the old cook one evening, pressing a bright shilling into the boy’s hand. ‘You’ll be an admiral yet.’

‘Aye, aye, Cap’n,’ said Tipples, touching his ragged cap. ‘Only keep the galley stoked, and I’ll keep the crew fed.’

But the river-side is a dark place, and the river itself keeps a silent account of the sins committed upon its slimy steps. Mr. Gargoyle, being a man of the open ocean, knew little of the sly, creeping wickedness of the town. He did not know that the bridge spanning the dark water to the other side was a thoroughfare not merely for honest feet, but for stolen goods; that the very fog that nourished his trade also nourished a darker commerce, presided over by a terrible old fence named Mother Muck.

Mother Muck was a woman whose soul was as damp, and dark, and corrupt as the river-mud itself. She lived in a tumble-down, half-submerged cellar across the bridge, a cavern that smelled of mildew and ancient theft, where silver spoons, pocket-handkerchiefs, and stolen watches were buried in the mire like unwholesome worms.

It was Mr. Gargoyle’s custom, upon closing his shop of an evening, to take a constitutional turn by the water-stairs to clear his lungs of the oven-heat. One Thursday, the fog being exceptionally thick, he walked further than his wont, crossing the very bridge where the gas-lamps flickered and struggled like dying souls. He saw a figure upon the stairs, and being of a sociable nature, he hailed it.

‘Ship ahoy!’ cried Mr. Gargoyle. ‘A damp night for a landsman to be abroad!’

The figure started, turned, and the light of a neighbouring lamp fell upon the sharp, ferret-face of Quick Tipples. But the boy was not alone. He was speaking in a hurried whisper to a ghastly old woman, and from his ragged pocket he drew forth—not a pie, nor a penny, but a glittering silver snuff-box, which he pressed into her claw-like hand.
