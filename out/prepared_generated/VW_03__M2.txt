The light in Paris did not fall; it flowed. It was a liquid, golden substance that pooled in the corners of the salon, catching the dust motes in slow, suspended whirls, while outside, the Seine murmured its ancient, indifferent song beneath the stone. Clara sat at the edge of the table, her fingers tight around the stem of a glass she did not wish to drink from, watching the light play over the back of the man across from her. He was speaking, or perhaps merely making the shapes of words, but the sound was distant, muffled by the sheer weight of the moment, the strangeness of being seen.

In the village, in the grey, damp rooms of the house on the hill, she had been a shadow, a function, a pair of hands that scrubbed the linens and boiled the water for her brother, Julian. She had kept the house, kept the silence, kept the small, ticking mechanisms of their lives in order since the doctors had come for their father and then, later, for their mother. There, she had been invisible, a necessary piece of furniture, acknowledged only when the tea was cold or the letters were unopened. But here, in this room that smelled of lavender and old paper and expensive wine, she was something else. She was Julian’s wife.

The thought did not shock her, as it might have; it simply settled over her, a warm, strange garment, fitting her into the space where a wife belongs. When Julian, in his white coat, his face flushed with the heat of the lecture and the wine, had turned to her and said, “Madame, will you sit?” the words had hung in the air, solid and real. *Madame.* Not *Clara.* Not *the sister.* Not *the one who mends the cuffs.* Madame. And the men around the table had looked at her with a softness, a recognition, that she had never known in the village. They listened to her. Or rather, they listened to the silence she inhabited, filling it with their own projections, their own need for a woman to be there, to complete the picture of the doctor, the intellect, the man of science.

She turned the glass in her hand. The crystal caught the light, throwing a small, sharp star onto the white tablecloth. Beside her, a woman with eyes the color of slate was speaking about the new treatments for typhoid, her voice low, melodic, a thread of sound that wove between the other conversations. Clara listened, not to the words, but to the rhythm, the way the sound moved through the room, touching the chandeliers, the walls, the faces of the strangers. They were all so alive, so intensely present, whereas back home, life had been a series of dull, repeated actions, the grinding of the millstone, the turning of the page, the washing of the dishes.

“Do you find it strange?” Julian asked, his voice low, leaning in. He was looking at her, really looking, with a curiosity that was almost painful. He was the doctor, the mind, the one who diagnosed and cured, but here, he seemed to have lost his certainty. He was looking for something in her face, perhaps the answer to a question he had not yet asked.

Clara did not answer at first. She watched a waiter move through the room, his tray balanced on one hand, his face a mask of polite indifference. The waiter was a ghost, moving through the solid world of the guests, touching nothing, belonging to no one. Was that what she had been? A ghost in her own house?

“No,” she said finally, her voice soft, barely a whisper. “It is not strange. It is… clear.”

Julian frowned, a small crease appearing between his brows. “Clear? But you have never traveled before. You have never seen Paris. You have never been to a dinner like this.”

“I have seen it,” Clara said, and she was surprised by the truth of it. She had seen it in the books, in the letters her father had written before he died, in the dreams that came to her in the small hours of the morning, when the house was still and the wind blew through the gaps in the shutters. She had seen it in the way the light fell on the street in the village, just before it turned to rain, and for a moment, everything was gold, everything was possible, and then the rain came, and the gold was washed away, and it was just mud and grey stone again.

They were talking about the disease, about the bacteria, the cells, the tiny, invisible enemies that warred within the body. The words were technical, precise, cold. But as they spoke, Clara felt a warmth rise in her chest, a sense of connection, of belonging to this group, to this moment, to the city that stretched out beyond the window, vast and humming with life. She was not the sister, the keeper, the servant. She was here, in the light, in the sound, in the conversation. She was seen.

The music began, a piano playing in the next room, the notes drifting through the open door, thin and sweet, like a thread of silver. The guests began to rise, to clear their chairs, to prepare to move. Julian stood, offering her his arm. It was a small gesture, but it felt like a bridge, a connection between two worlds. She took his arm, and for a moment, they stood still, just the two of them, in the middle of the room, while the world moved around them.

“Shall we go?” he asked.

“Yes,” she said.
