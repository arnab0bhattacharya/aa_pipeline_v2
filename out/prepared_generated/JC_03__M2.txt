The rain in London did not fall; it seeped, a persistent, grey moisture that found its way into the bones of the city and the souls of men who had lived too long in its shadows. I stood on the corner of Floral Street, watching the gas lamps flicker through the mist, their light distorted into a sickly, yellow haze that made the wet cobblestones look like the scales of some dead, slithering beast. It was a night suited to the business of concealment, where the line between the observer and the observed grew thin and treacherous.

I had spent seven years in this grey purgatory. Seven years listening to the murmurs of exiles, the whispered conspiracies of men who had lost their homelands and now sought to burn down the homes of others. I had become a collector of secrets, a man who knew the precise weight of a lie and the fragile texture of a truth. I was not a hero. I was a function. A necessary, invisible gear in the great, grinding machine of the Empire, keeping the wheels of order turning while the world outside screamed with the noise of revolution.

But machines change. The new Inspector, a man named Halloway, had arrived from the provinces with a smile that did not reach his eyes and a hunger for headlines that was almost violent. He did not understand the subtleties of the trade. He did not know that an arrest is not merely the taking of a man, but the breaking of a web; that to pull one thread is to unravel the whole tapestry. He wanted a name. He wanted a story for the *Times*. He wanted blood on his hands, however symbolic, to prove his mettle to the men who sat in the warm rooms of the Home Office.

And so, the order came. Not shouted, but delivered with a calm, bureaucratic finality that was worse than any threat. *Make an arrest. A prominent one. Something the public will understand.*

There was only one name I could give him.

His name was Koval.

I had known Koval for five years. We had met in a cramped tea room in Soho, where the air smelled of stale damp and cheap tobacco. He was a man of quiet intensity, with eyes that seemed to look through you rather than at you, searching for the flaws in your armor. I had fed him false leads, minor scraps of intelligence that were useless to him but enough to keep him loyal, to keep him coming back to me, to the safe house, to the circle of trust. In return, he gave me the names of those who were truly dangerous, the men who planned not just for protest, but for destruction. I had saved my career, perhaps my life, with his whispers. I had saved my soul, or what passed for one, by keeping my distance from the actual violence, the actual blood.

Now, I had to betray him.

I walked into the tea room. The place was empty, save for the proprietor, a heavy-set Polish woman who looked up with a mixture of suspicion and resignation. Koval was already there. He sat in the corner, his hands wrapped around a cup of tea, his posture rigid. He looked at me, and in that look, I saw a question I could not answer. He did not know, not yet. He thought I was coming to bring him the news of a police raid in Whitechapel, or a shift in the mood of the Ministry. He thought I was his friend, his anchor in the drifting sea of exile.

I sat down across from him. The chair creaked. The sound seemed loud, an accusation in the silence.

"You look tired, Inspector," Koval said. His voice was low, a rough baritone that had lost its edge. "The work is heavy."

"The work is always heavy," I replied. My voice sounded strange to my own ears, dry and brittle. "The Minister is pleased. Very pleased."

Koval smiled, a faint, sad curve of the lips. "With what? Our failures? Or our successes?"

"Both," I said. "And with something else. A need for... clarity."

I watched his face. The hope that had been there, the subtle anticipation of a shared victory, began to fade, replaced by a dawning, cold confusion. He leaned forward slightly, his eyes narrowing. "Clarity," he repeated. "Is that the word?"

"It is the word I have been given," I said.

The silence that followed was not empty. It was filled with the ticking of the clock on the wall, the distant rumble of a cart on the wet pavement, and the heavy, oppressive weight of the air. I could feel the moment stretching, thinning, like a wire about to snap. I wanted to speak, to explain, to offer some justification that might soften the blow. But there was no justification. There was only the duty of the state, the cold, indifferent logic of the machine. I was a part of it, and I was about to break.

Koval looked at me for a long time. Then, he reached into his coat pocket and pulled out a small, leather-bound notebook. He placed it on the table between us.

"Here," he said softly. "The names. The ones you asked for. The ones who are truly dangerous. Take them. Give them to Halloway. It will be enough. It will satisfy him. You will be a hero. And I will be... a footnote."

I stared at the notebook. It was a small, insignificant thing, but it held the power to destroy lives, to shatter families, to send men to the gallows. I had spent years guarding this secret, protecting Koval from the reach of the law, while he protected me from the chaos of the world. Now, I was being asked to trade one life for another, or perhaps, to trade his life for my comfort.

"I cannot take this," I said. My voice was barely a whisper.
