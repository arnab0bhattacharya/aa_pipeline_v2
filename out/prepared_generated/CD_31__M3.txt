If any conscientious chronicler of human affairs were to seek a singular monument to the unyielding tyranny of a narrow mind, let him repair without delay to Mrs. Clackport’s Lodging-House in Perchance Lane, London, where the very brass knocker seemed to shudder beneath the weight of its own respectability, and the area railings wore a rusted, reproachful frown, as if turning up their iron noses at the disreputable mud of the street. Here reigned Mrs. Clackport, a widow of a most adamantine and unyielding countenance, who, having been crossed in the gentle humanities of life, had taken a solemn vow to cross them in everybody else. Her bonnets were of an iron-grey, her silences were of an iron-grey, and her opinions, which she dispensed like unpalatable physic, were of the very darkest and most unyielding iron-grey imaginable.

Upon this formidable aunt—and she was aunt not by the tender ties of consanguinity alone, but by that sterner bond of having thrust herself into the guardianship of a forsaken nephew—had devolved the rearing of young Mr. Timothy Quirk. Timothy was a pale, lank, and wistful youth of nineteen, possessed of a singularly gentle disposition, which his aunt trampled beneath her galoshes as though it were a pernicious weed. He had been told, with that cheerful frequency peculiar to the thoroughly miserable, that his father was dead; that his father had been a wastrel; and that his father had, in departing this world, left behind him nothing but a legacy of disgrace, which Timothy was expected to wear like a hair-shirt.

But Providence, which sometimes works its miracles through the most unpropitious of channels, had lately chosen to work one through the medium of a damp and smudged newspaper, wrapping a pound of rancid butter. In this unlikely vehicle, Timothy had espied a paragraph concerning the grand opening of a new branch line at the provincial town of Muddleby-in-the-Marsh, and, more astonishing still, the appointment of a Mr. Septimus Quirk as the chief ticket-collector of that very station. The name, the Christian name, and the melancholy, half-remembered likeness conjured by the printed letters, had set Timothy’s heart beating with a wild, unaccountable hope. His father was not dead. His father was alive, and punching tickets in the Midlands!

Without a word to Mrs. Clackport—for to have announced his intention would have been to invite a volcanic eruption of iron-grey indignation—Timothy had scraped together his meagre savings, packed a single carpet-bag, and boarded the morning express.

The railway journey was a revelation to the young man. The mighty engine, snorting and tearing through the verdant countryside, seemed to Timothy a very dragon of progress, breathing fire and shaking the earth, carrying him away from the oppressive gloom of Perchance Lane toward a destiny he could not yet fathom. He pressed his nose to the cold glass of the carriage window, watching the smoky chimneys of London give way to green fields and rolling hills, and his heart swelled with a strange, sweet mixture of terror and anticipation.

Arriving at Muddleby-in-the-Marsh, Timothy found the station to be a modest, red-brick edifice, smelling powerfully of soot, damp wool, and new paint. The platform was nearly deserted, save for a few listless porters and an ancient woman selling bruised apples from a basket. The clock above the booking-office clicked its hands with a slow, deliberate rhythm, as if time itself were reluctant to depart from this unassuming spot.

Timothy stood uncertainly by the edge of the platform, his carpet-bag clutched in both hands, his heart performing a series of alarming somersaults within his breast. He scanned the faces of the few officials present, but saw no one who resembled the phantom image he carried in his mind. He was on the verge of despair, of retracing his steps to the gloomy lodging-house, when he heard a voice—a thin, reedy, yet strangely familiar voice—call out, “Tickets, please! Have your tickets ready for inspection!”

Timothy turned, and there, emerging from the shadow of a brick archway, was a man in a blue uniform with a tarnished silver badge upon his cap. The man was older, thinner, and more worn than Timothy had ever imagined, with a stoop to his shoulders and a pair of spectacles perched precariously upon a long, thin nose. But the eyes—those pale, watery, wistful eyes—were unmistakable.

“Father!” Timothy breathed, taking an involuntary step forward.

The ticket-collector paused, his punch suspended in mid-air. He peered at the young man before him, blinking rapidly behind his spectacles, as though trying to bring a blurred photograph into focus. A small, rectangular piece of pasteboard fluttered from his fingers and fell, unheeded, to the platform.

“Tim?” he said, in a voice that was barely above a whisper. “Little Tim?”

Timothy nodded, unable to speak, for a great lump had risen in his throat, threatening to choke him. The ticket-collector’s face underwent a remarkable transformation. The weary, official mask dissolved, and in its place appeared a look of such overwhelming joy, mingled with a profound and aching sorrow, that Timothy felt his eyes sting with tears.

“My boy!” cried Septimus Quirk, casting his ticket-punch upon a wooden bench and rushing forward with open arms. “My dear, dear boy!”

They embraced there upon the platform, beneath the ticking clock and the soot-stained glass, two lost souls finding one another in a world that had used them both most shabbily. The ancient apple-woman cackled knowingly, and a passing porter, mistaking the scene for a fainting fit, offered a flask of something distinctly spirituous, which they declined with trembling smiles.

“Come, come,” said Septimus, wiping his eyes with the cuff of his uniform and glancing about him with a sudden, sheepish recollection of his official duties. “We cannot have our reunion upon the public boards. I have a room—a very humble room, Tim, but clean, I assure you—just beyond the goods shed. Come, and let me look at you!”
