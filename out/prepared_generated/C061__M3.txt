The cart track wound up between the drystone walls and Joseph Callow felt every rut through his worn boot soles. He had walked this path ten thousand times before, but never so slowly. Above him the bulk of Black Tor showed its teeth against a grey sky, and below, in the dip where the land flattened out before falling to the valley, the farmhouse of Stanner Hall sat as it had always sat, smoke rising from its chimney, its barn doors open to the afternoon. He stopped where the track divided, one fork running down to the yard, the other climbing to the high pasture. He stood there a long while, watching. A figure crossed the yard carrying two pails, and even at this distance he knew her by the way she moved, the particular set of her shoulders, the slight halt in her step. Then a second figure came out of the barn and took the pails from her, and Joseph saw that it was his brother.

He had known. The letters from his mother had told him plainly, in her crabbed hand, that Mercy Belfield had married Robert Callow in the autumn of 1814, while Joseph was still in France, still wearing the King's coat, still whole in body if not in mind. He had read the letter by firelight in a billet near Bordeaux and had felt nothing he could name, only a kind of settling, as of dust after a door closes. He had folded the letter and put it in his pocket and had not read it again. But knowing it at a distance, among strangers, in a country not his own, was a different thing from standing on this track and seeing Robert's hand brush Mercy's arm as he took the pails.

Joseph went down into the yard. The dogs came first, two lurchers he did not know, barking and circling. Then Robert came out of the byre and stood still, his face working through surprise and something harder before settling on a grin that did not reach his eyes.

"Jo," Robert said. "By God, Jo."

They stood regarding each other. Robert had grown thicker in the chest and arms, the build of a man who has worked land every day for years. Joseph was leaner, harder, his face weathered by foreign suns, a scar running from his left ear down beneath his collar. He had left as a boy of nineteen and returned at twenty-four, and the years showed on both of them.

"I've come to work," Joseph said. "If there's work."

Robert looked at him. "There's work. There's always work. But Jo—"

"I know what there is to know."

Mercy appeared in the doorway. She wore a plain dress and her hair was tied back, and she stood with one hand on the frame and did not move. Joseph looked at her and she looked at him and the silence between them had a weight and texture, like something you could hold.

"Joseph," she said. "Come in. You'll be hungry."

He ate at the table where he had eaten as a boy. Mercy set out bread and cheese and a bowl of pottage and he ate without tasting. Robert sat across from him and talked, filling the air, saying that the farm had done well enough, that the price of wool had fallen, that the lower field had been drained, that their mother had died in the winter of the fever. Joseph listened and did not listen. He watched Mercy's hands as she moved about the kitchen, and he watched Robert's mouth moving, and he thought that this was the shape his life would take now: a room with these three people in it, and silence underneath the talk.

He slept in the loft above the barn, on a straw palliasse, with the horses shifting and breathing below him. The first nights were the worst. He would wake in the dark with his heart hammering, his hands gripping the blanket, and for a moment he would not know where he was. Then he would hear the horses and the wind pressing at the slates, and he would remember, and lie still until the shaking stopped.

He worked. He worked as he had been trained to work in the army, which was to say without pause and without complaint. He dug and hedged and mucked out and carried. Robert watched him for the first week, waiting for something, some sign, but Joseph gave none. He kept his head down and his mouth shut and did what was put before him. At meals he sat at the table and ate what Mercy gave him and said little. In the evenings he sat by the fire and read what few books there were, or mended harness, or simply sat with his hands on his knees and looked at the flames. Robert began to relax. He was not a cruel man, only a practical one, and he saw that his brother intended no trouble and was glad of it.

Mercy was harder to read. She moved around Joseph carefully, the way one moves around a large animal that one does not entirely trust. She gave him his food and kept her eyes on her work and spoke to him only of practical things: whether he wanted more water, whether the boots could be mended, whether the hay was in. But sometimes, when he looked up, he would find her looking at him, and in her face he would see something that was not fear and not pity but something older and more complicated, something that had no name.

A month passed. Then two. The spring came late that year, cold and wet, and the lambs died in the fields and the men worked in the rain to drag them from the ewes. Joseph worked beside Robert in the muck and the blood and the bawling of the animals, and they did not speak except to give commands, and this was a kind of closeness, the closeness of shared labour, which was the only kind Joseph could bear.
