The body of Silas Croft was discovered by the milkman, who found him crumpled against a heap of sodden sacking, his face the colour of tallow and his fingers still hooked, as if in a final, desperate act of clutching. He had died in his own yard, the place he loved more than any living soul, and the news travelled through the close-packed warrens of Clerkenwell like a spark through tinder. Within the hour, the great iron gate of Croft’s Rag and Bone Emporium, usually bolted and barred against the world, was wrenched from its hinges by a press of neighbours, their faces a mixture of morbid curiosity and avaricious gleam.

Silas Croft had been a miser of legendary proportions. A dealer in the city’s refuse, he had turned a profit from the filth of London for forty years, yet lived as though he were one step from the workhouse. His yard was his kingdom, a chaotic landscape of mounds and hillocks. There were mountains of rags, sorted by a colour only he could discern, their damp, musty scent the very perfume of poverty. There were skeletal piles of bones, stripped of their meat and carted away to the glue-works. There were towers of rusted iron, broken crockery, and the sad, headless torsos of dolls. It was a monument to decay, and now it was a treasure map.

The first discovery was made by a boy from the Seven Dials, nimble-fingered and quick. He was prising a buckle from a heap of old boots when he dislodged a loose brick from a low wall. Behind it, nestled in a canvas bag, were three sovereigns. A gasp went through the growing crowd. The hunt was on. Order, what little there was, dissolved into a frantic, scrabbling mob. They fell upon the heaps with shovels and bare hands, tearing at the sacking, sifting through the grit and grime. Money began to appear in the most unlikely places. A tin of farthings was unearthed from the heart of a pile of broken bottles. A roll of banknotes, stiff with damp, was found tucked inside the carcass of a grandfather clock. A necklace of jet beads, with a purse of shillings at its clasp, was pulled from a mound of torn petticoats.

The air filled with grunts of exertion and sharp, triumphant cries. Mrs. Gable, the baker’s wife, her formidable bosom straining her bodice, unearthed a heavy purse from a sack of what looked like nothing but floor sweepings. Mr. Finch, the pawnbroker, his sharp eyes missing nothing, calmly pocketed a handful of gold guineas he’d found nestled in a bucket of coal dust. The yard, once a place of silent, grim industry, was now a scene of pandemonium, a grotesque parody of a gold rush. The dead man’s body, now laid respectfully upon a clean sack by the constable who had finally arrived, was all but forgotten, a mere footnote to the frantic distribution of his hoard.

Amid the chaos stood Eliza. At seventeen, she was a slight, pale girl with hair the colour of dishwater and eyes that had seen too much of the world’s harshness. She had nursed Silas Croft through his final, rattling illness, spooning thin broth between his cracked lips and wiping the sweat from his brow. She had been paid in stale crusts and a place by his meagre fire. Now, she stood at the edge of the frenzy, her hands twisting in the fabric of her apron. She did not join the others. She watched, a small, still point in the turning storm.

She knew the old man. Not as the miser, the monster of local legend, but as a lonely, frightened creature who talked in his sleep of a sister he had lost in the cholera years. He had trusted her, in his way. In his fevered ramblings, he had told her things, his secrets slipping out between bouts of shivering. He had told her where the heart of his fortune lay. It was not in the coins and notes scattered like seed throughout his yard. That was his game, his way of mocking the world that would one day plunder him. He would hide his pennies for them to find, while the true prize remained safe.

She saw Mrs. Gable, her face flushed with triumph, stuffing a handful of silver into her pocket. She saw the boy from the Dials gleefully showing off a gold ring. They were all so sure they had found it all. They had scratched at the surface, but they did not know the depths of Silas Croft’s paranoia, his genius for concealment.

Eliza took a deep breath, the familiar scent of damp wool and rust filling her lungs. She walked towards the centre of the yard, towards a sight so mundane that no one had given it a second glance. It was a simple, rickety privy, its wood grey and splintered, its roof a single sheet of corrugated iron. It stood against the far wall, a source of shame and necessity, ignored by the treasure hunters who saw only the potential of the richer-looking heaps.

She lifted the simple wooden latch and slipped inside, closing the door behind her. The small space was dim and smelled of lime and earth. The others were too busy to notice her disappearance. Her heart hammered against her ribs. He had told her, his voice a dry rustle, ‘Under the boards, girl. Where the king sits. They’ll look in the rags, they’ll look in the iron, but they’ll never think to look where a man keeps his… his secrets.’

Eliza knelt on the dirt floor. There were three rough-hewn floorboards. She slipped her fingers into the gap between two of them and pulled. They were heavy, but she was stronger than she looked, her muscles honed by years of hard labour. With a groan of protesting wood, the boards came up.
