It was upon the evening of the dress rehearsal that the matter was brought to my attention by Mr. Horace Ventnor, the manager of the Criterion Theatre. I had been engaged by him earlier that day, for a letter had arrived at his office bearing a smudged E.C. postmark and a singularly chilling message. "The leading lady will not survive the opening night," it ran, the words clipped from a periodical. Ventnor was a stout, anxious man of business, more accustomed to disputes over salaries than to death-threats, and he had turned pale at the sight of the paper.

Miss Eleanor Vane, the actress in question, had dismissed the warning with a toss of her head. She was a handsome, proud woman, and her contempt for the letter was equalled only by her confidence in her own dramatic abilities. "It is some spiteful trick of Mrs. Carlyle's," she declared, naming a rival who had been passed over for the role. "She has a tongue like a vipers, but she would not dare to harm me."

Yet, there was one other circumstance which had prompted Ventnor to send for me. The old stage doorman, a man named Griggs, had observed a stranger loitering in the flies above the stage. He was a lean, dark fellow in a heavy Ulster, and Griggs had seen him examining the trapdoor machinery with more than ordinary attention. When challenged, the man had vanished down a side stair before he could be secured.

It was with these facts that I presented myself at the stage door of the Criterion. I had taken the precaution of arriving in the costume of a scene-shifter, wearing a stained canvas jacket and a cap pulled low over my eyes. The gas-jets were already flaring in the grid above, casting long, quivering shadows across the boards, and the air was thick with the smell of paint and sawdust.

Ventnor met me in the wings, looking even more agitated than he had in his office. "I am glad you have come, Mr. Hawkes," he whispered, mopping his brow with a large silk handkerchief. "Miss Vane is in a fine rage. She declares she will not be spied upon, and that we are making a mountain out of a molehill."

"You have told her nothing of the stranger seen by Griggs?"

"Not a word. I feared it would unnerve her."

"Then let us keep it so. I must see the machinery of that trapdoor for myself."

We made our way up a narrow, corkscrew staircase to the flies. The groaning of the windlasses and the shouts of the carpenters below covered our footsteps. The stage was set for the third act, which required Miss Vane to make a sudden disappearance through a trap in the centre of the stage. It was a trick that always drew applause, and Ventnor had spared no expense upon the mechanism.

Griggs, the doorman, was waiting for us beside the heavy counterweights. He was a grizzled, taciturn man, who had spent his life in the theatre and knew every rope and pulley as a sailor knows his rigging.

"It was just here I saw him, sir," said Griggs, touching his cap to me. "He had his hand upon the release-lever, and he was measuring the drop with a piece of string."

"Measuring the drop?" I asked, sharply.

"Yes, sir. He tied a knot in the string and put it in his pocket. Then he was off like a shadow."

I examined the lever. It was a simple affair, held in place by a heavy iron pin. Yet, when I looked closely, I observed that the pin had been filed nearly halfway through. A sudden strain, a sharp blow, and the pin would shear away entirely. The heavy counterweights would crash down, and the trapdoor would give way beneath Miss Vane's feet.

The drop beneath was a clear thirty feet to a concrete floor. It was a deadly fall, and there could be no doubt of the villain's intention.

"Has anyone else been near this machinery today?" I asked.

"Only the stage carpenter, Mr. Bellingham, and he was up here at noon. He said the pin looked a bit worn, and he meant to speak to the manager about it."

"Where is Mr. Bellingham now?"

"He left at six, sir. Said he had a touch of the migraine."

I turned to Ventnor. "Have you any enemies among your staff?"

"None! Bellingham has been with me for ten years. He is the most trusted man in the theatre."

It was a singular thing, but I had learned by long experience that it is the trusted man who is most to be suspected when a crime of this nature is contemplated. I looked at the filed pin again, and then at the counterweights. The pin had not been filed by a scene-shifter's rough hand. The cut was clean and precise, the work of a man skilled with metal tools.

"Go down to the stage door, Griggs," said I, "and wait for me there. If Mr. Bellingham returns, do not let him out of your sight."

The rehearsal was now in full swing. From my vantage point in the flies, I could see Miss Vane upon the stage, her voice ringing out with passion. She was a fine actress, and she moved with a grace that held the eye. Yet, as I watched her, I could not help but think of the broken pin and the dark drop that lay beneath her feet.

The third act was approaching its climax. Miss Vane moved towards the centre of the stage, her hands clasped to her breast. The cue for her disappearance was but a moment away. I gripped the railing, ready to act.

Then, from the corner of my eye, I saw a figure moving in the shadows of the grid above. It was a man in a heavy Ulster, and he was creeping towards the release-lever. His face was pale and sharp-featured, and his eyes were fixed upon the stage below.
