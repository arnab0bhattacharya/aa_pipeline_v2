I have set down elsewhere the particulars of my singular transition from the laboratory at Crowthorne to the open hillside upon which I awakened. I will not weary the reader now with the mechanism of that accident, which involved a galvanic apparatus and a newly synthesized compound of radium bromide. It is sufficient to state that I found myself lying in a field of unfamiliar grass, beneath a sun that appeared strangely tepid, and looking up at the ruins of a stone structure that I could not immediately identify.

The air was remarkably still. There were no motor-omnibuses, no clattering trains, no hum of aerial traffic such as one might anticipate in a future age. The silence was profound and pervasive, broken only by a rather mechanical wind stirring the foliage of some exceptionally tall elms. I got upon my feet, dusted my tweed trousers, and walked toward a village that lay in the hollow below.

It was a curious place. The houses were exceedingly neat, built of a white, metallic substance, and possessed of an almost aggressive cleanliness. The gardens were immaculate, though they contained no flowers, only a rather dreary and monotonous arrangement of ferns. I walked along the single street for quite ten minutes before I encountered a living soul. Then, emerging from a sort of post-office, I beheld a man.

He was exceedingly old. That was the first and most striking thing about him. His face was a wilderness of wrinkles, yet his eyes were bright and serene, and he walked with the aid of a polished stick. He was dressed in a loose, gray garment, somewhat resembling a cassock. He stopped and regarded me with a mild astonishment.

"Good morning," I said, feeling singularly foolish in my modern attire.

"Good morning," he replied. His voice was soft and deliberate, like the ticking of a very old clock. "You are a stranger. And you are—young."

"I am thirty-five," I said.

"Thirty-five," he repeated, as one might repeat a fabulous age. He looked at me with a sudden, deep interest. "We have not seen anyone of thirty-five for a very long time. I had thought there were none left. I am Harland, the keeper of the records at Dunsford. You must come with me."

He led me to a building of the same white metal, which served as a sort of town hall, and there, in a room overlooking the valley, he explained the state of things.

The gist of it was this: some two centuries before, a certain Dr. Verrall had discovered a process for the indefinite prolongation of human life. The process was physiological, involving a renewal of the cellular tissue, and it had been applied with tremendous success. Disease had been practically eliminated. The expectation of life had risen, by degrees, to two hundred years. And, in consequence, the birth-rate had fallen to absolute zero.

"We found," said Harland, in his gentle, deliberate way, "that with two hundred years to live, the world became quite full enough. Children were an encumbrance. The instinct had faded. We are a very contented people. We cultivate our gardens, we read, we converse. There is no hurry. We have time for everything."

"But," I said, "if no one has children, the race must be dying out?"

Harland nodded serenely. "Oh, yes. We are dying out. There are only some fifty thousand of us left in England. We are quite serene about it. We have had our day. The world is very beautiful, and we are leaving it very tidy."

I found this state of affairs indescribably melancholy. To live for two hundred years in a world without children, without the clamor and hope of youth. It seemed to me a dreadful negation. I said as much to Harland, but he only smiled, a little tolerantly, and offered me a glass of a light, pale wine.

In the afternoon I walked out alone, up the valley, past the white, silent houses, each with its aged occupant sitting tranquilly in the fern-garden. The whole place had the atmosphere of a vast, well-kept museum. The people were like specimens, exquisitely preserved, utterly lifeless. I found myself longing for the noise and dirt of my own century, for the crying of children, for the struggle and confusion of a living world.

It was on the hillside, near a grove of those tall elms, that I met the woman.

She was sitting on a stone bench, looking out over the valley. She was dressed in the same gray garment as Harland, but she wore it differently, with a certain impatience in the set of her shoulders. She was young—perhaps thirty. Her hair was dark, and her face had a quick, alert expression that I had not seen in any other inhabitant of this strange country.

She looked up as I approached, and I saw a flash of surprise, followed by a sudden, eager interest.

"You are the man from the past," she said. "Harland sent word."

"I am," I admitted. "And you—you are not like the others."

"No." She turned her face away. "I am not like the others. I am the last."

She told me her name was Elara. She was, she informed me, the last child born in England. Her parents, caught up in a momentary revival of the old instinct, had applied for permission to reproduce. It had been granted as a sort of experiment, a concession to a fading sentiment. She had been born when her mother was a hundred and eighty.

"They are both dead now," she said, with a certain dryness. "They lived out their two hundred years. I have a hundred and seventy left."

"And you live among these people?"

"I live among them. They are kind to me. They treat me as a curiosity. They point me out to visitors from the other districts. 'The last child,' they say. 'Is she not remarkable?' And I smile and say, 'Yes, I am remarkable.' It is very tedious."
