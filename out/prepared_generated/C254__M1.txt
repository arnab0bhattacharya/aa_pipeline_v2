The air in St James’s Park still held the damp chill of dawn, a mist that clung to the iron railings and blurred the distant shape of Buckingham Palace into a watercolour smudge. Mrs. Eleanor Vance arrived at her bench at eight o’clock precisely, as she had every morning for the past three years. It was not her bench by any official decree, but by the quiet, unspoken law of habit. It faced the lake, with a clear view of the pelicans dozing on their island and the ducks that paddled expectantly near the stone steps.

From her reticule, she produced a small, folded paper bag. The bread inside was stale, just as it should be. Arthur had always said fresh bread was a waste and did the ducks no good; they preferred the hard, crusty bits. Arthur. Her son. The memory of his voice, light and teasing as he’d said it, was a phantom ache in her chest. He used to walk here before his shift at the bank, a lanky silhouette in his tweed jacket, his long legs covering the ground with an easy stride that she watched from this very bench before he’d noticed her and waved. That was before the Somme. Before the telegram. Before the world became a place of quiet, ordered grief.

She would sit for an hour, breaking the bread into small pieces, her movements economical and precise. She would toss the crumbs to the water and watch the frantic, splashing ballet of the mallards and the coots. The activity was a small, manageable ritual, a way of tethering herself to the morning, to the world that continued to turn with or without her consent. It was a duty, like polishing his boots or keeping his letters in their box, a way of honouring a life that was no longer.

It was perhaps the second week of October when the boy first appeared. He did not come to her bench, but to the one beside it, separated by a few feet of gravel path. He was tall, just as Arthur had been at that age, but where Arthur had been all sharp angles and restless energy, this boy was still, contained. He wore a worn corduroy jacket and a flat cap pulled low over his brow. He carried a book but rarely opened it. He would simply sit, his hands in his pockets, and look out at the lake, his gaze patient and unblinking.

Eleanor felt his presence like a change in the weather. At first, she was merely aware of him, a new fixture in her landscape. Then, a strange unease began to settle in her. His age was the thing. He looked to be about eighteen, perhaps nineteen. Arthur’s age. The age he would have been this year, if not for that muddy field in France. The boy’s presence was an unwelcome echo, a living, breathing reminder of a ghost. She found herself studying him from the corner of her eye, cataloging the differences in a desperate attempt to assert them. This boy’s hair was darker. His shoulders were not quite so broad. He sat with a stillness that was not Arthur’s natural state.

A week passed. He came every morning, always taking the adjacent bench, always silent. The silence was the most unnerving part. It was a shared, yet profoundly separate, quiet. Eleanor began to anticipate his arrival, her heart giving a strange, painful lurch when he appeared around the bend in the path. She could not decide what she wanted. Part of her, the part that still woke in the night calling for her son, wanted him to speak. She wanted to hear a young man’s voice, to ask his name, to know something of his life. Was he a student? Did he work nearby? Was he happy? The questions swirled in her mind, unsaid.

But another part of her, the part that had built a fortress around her sorrow, was terrified of the very thing she craved. If he spoke, the spell would be broken. He would become just a boy, a stranger with a name and a story of his own. He would cease to be the vessel for her memory, the silent companion who, in his youth and his quiet presence, allowed her to almost believe, for a fleeting hour each morning, that Arthur was merely sitting just out of reach. His silence was a canvas onto which she could project her own longing. His voice would be a splash of black ink, ruining the portrait.

One morning, a bitter wind blew in from the east, rattling the bare branches of the plane trees. Eleanor’s fingers were stiff with cold as she broke the bread. A piece, larger than she intended, fell from her numb grasp and landed on the gravel path between the two benches. Before she could lean down, the boy moved. He unfolded himself from his bench, bent, and picked up the crust. He walked the few steps towards her and held it out.

Their hands brushed as she took it. His skin was warm. For the first time, she saw his face clearly, without the shadow of his cap. He had kind, grey eyes, and a faint constellation of freckles across the bridge of his nose.

“Thank you,” she said, her voice sounding rusty from disuse.

He gave a small, hesitant smile. “It’s no trouble. Wouldn’t want to waste it. The ducks look hungry.”

His voice was a quiet baritone, nothing like Arthur’s lighter tenor. The difference should have been a relief, but it was a fresh, sharp disappointment. This was not her son. This was just a boy. The realization was both a comfort and a profound loss.

“You come here every morning,” he said. It was not a question.

“Yes,” she replied. “It’s a habit.”
