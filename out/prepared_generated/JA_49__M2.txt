It was a truth universally acknowledged that a mother in possession of a good constitution must be in want of a crisis. Mrs. Harcourt, who had spent the preceding twenty years in the firm possession of excellent health and a superior opinion of her own medical judgment, suddenly found herself besieged by a malady of such alarming severity that immediate and exclusive attention from her daughter, Miss—now Mrs.—Ellis, was deemed indispensable. The summons arrived by post on a Tuesday, written in a hand that trembled with a delicacy which had never previously manifested itself, and stating with grave certainty that the family physician had pronounced a consumption so acute that the family could not bear the loss of her guiding hand.

Mrs. Ellis, a woman of twenty-six and settled in the comfortable dignity of her own parlor in Bath, received the letter with a mixture of duty and weary resignation. She knew her mother’s constitution was as robust as a Somerset ox, and her ailments as transient as a morning fog. Yet, to refuse the call of a parent in supposed extremity would have been an act of such moral turpitude that no amount of rationalism could excuse it. She packed her trunks with the speed of a woman who suspected the duration of her stay would be shorter than the preparation for it, and prepared to depart for the village of Chalford, where the Harcourt estate sat with an air of rural complacency that Mrs. Ellis had always found slightly stifling.

Her husband, Mr. Arthur Ellis, a man of gentle disposition and an almost uncanny ability to remain unoffended, accompanied her. This was not, as Mrs. Ellis suspected, out of any desire to share in the confinement of a sickroom, nor even out of the necessity of chaperoning, for Mrs. Ellis was a woman of unimpeachable virtue. Rather, Arthur seemed to view the journey as a necessary respite from the monotony of his ledger books, and he regarded the prospect of visiting his mother-in-law with a serenity that bordered on the suspicious.

They arrived at Chalford on a grey afternoon, the air damp and heavy with the scent of wet earth and woodsmoke. Mrs. Harcourt’s house, a sprawling structure of local stone, greeted them with an air of dignified distress. The curtains were drawn, the furniture covered in calico, and the atmosphere thick with the scent of camphor and anxiety.

Mrs. Harcourt was not in her bed, as the letter had implied, but seated in a large armchair by the fire, wrapped in shawls that suggested not so much a chill as a desire for comfort. Her color was good, her eye bright, and her voice, though adopted into a whisper, possessed a clarity that belied her claimed infirmity.

“My dearest, you look pale,” Mrs. Ellis said, forcing a tone of sympathetic horror. “The journey has been dreadful. I am ashamed to think of you suffering in this way without us.”

“I have suffered terribly, my child,” Mrs. Harcoat replied, adjusting a lace collar with a hand that did not tremble at all. “The doctor says I must have absolute quiet. No visitors, no noise, no—” she cast a significant glance at Arthur, who was already examining the fire with an air of genuine interest, “—unnecessary conversation.”

Mrs. Ellis felt a pang of irritation. She had imagined Arthur would be embarrassed by the spectacle, or at least relieved to be out of the house. Instead, he moved to the chair beside his mother-in-law, poured a glass of water from a decanter on the table, and presented it to her with a bow that was almost too gracious.

“May I have the honor of assisting in your comfort, Mrs. Harcourt?” he asked, his voice smooth as polished oak.

“Arthur!” Mrs. Ellis hissed, though it was a whisper. “Do not be ridiculous. She is ill.”

“I am not ridiculous, my dear,” Arthur replied, his eyes twinkling with a quiet, mischievous intelligence. “I am attentive. There is a great difference.”

The days that followed were a study in contrasts. Mrs. Harcourt’s illness, it transpired, was a common cold, treated with the seriousness of a plague. She required constant attendance, not for medical necessity, but for the satisfaction of being attended to. She demanded soup at odd hours, complained of the draft in the window, and recited at length the various ways in which her neighbors had neglected her in the past. Mrs. Ellis, true to her duty, provided the soup and the sympathy, but her patience was fraying like the hem of a good gown. She found herself resenting the intrusion of her mother’s self-pity into her life, and more so, the way in which her husband seemed to be thriving in the chaos.

Arthur did not read, as he usually did when confined to a room. He did not walk the grounds in melancholy silence. Instead, he engaged in long, low-voiced conversations with Mrs. Harcourt, nodding at the right moments, offering dry observations on the weather, and occasionally asking for her advice on the care of her roses. Mrs. Ellis watched him with increasing bewilderment. Was he bored? Was he mocking her? Or was there something else at play?

One evening, as they sat in the library, the fire crackling behind them, Mrs. Ellis finally lost her composure.

“Arthur,” she said, setting down her embroidery. “I must ask you. Do you find my mother’s condition amusing?”

Arthur looked up from the book he was holding, though it was open to a blank page. He smiled, a slow, gentle curve of the lips.

“Amusing, my dear?”

“Not amusing, precisely,” she corrected, her voice tight. “But you seem to take a certain pleasure in it. You are patient. You are attentive. You do not sigh, or complain, or wish yourself elsewhere. I have never seen you so... content, as I sit here feeling like a nursemaid in a farce.”

Arthur closed the book and turned to face her fully. His expression was calm, devoid of the defensive sharpness she might have expected.
