The tea had grown cold in the pot—a fact which no one remarked upon, though each, in the silences that fell between them, regarded the brown bead of the spout with a kind of reproach, as if the vessel itself had failed in its duty. Outside, the afternoon was folding itself away over the chimney pots of Kensington, and the light, that particular London light which seems to have strained through muslin and old regret, lay in long pale rectangles on the carpet, touching the brass fender, the corner of the writing desk, the hem of Miss Aldworth’s grey skirt, and passing on, indifferent, to the wall.

“But you cannot,” said Aunt Constance, and the words, though spoken quietly, had the effect of a door being shut somewhere in the house. “You cannot, Harriet. It is not done.”

“It is done,” said Harriet, “by women who have not the good fortune to possess a father and two aunts to keep them.” She had meant it lightly—or had she? The cup trembled in her hand; she set it down. The clatter was enormous.

Mr. Aldworth, in his chair by the window, did not turn. His head, with its thick white hair, was bent a little forward, as though he were reading some document invisible to the others—the air itself perhaps, or the long register of years. He had said nothing since the announcement had been made. He had looked up once, quickly, when she had spoken the word “rooms,” and then had looked down again at his hands, which were large and fine and now lay quite still upon the arms of the chair, like two old servants dismissed.

“I do not understand,” said Aunt Constance, “what has put such an idea into your head. It is that woman at the library, I suppose. Or those lectures. I said to your father at the time—did I not, Margaret?—that lectures were a mistake. They fill young heads with—”

“They fill young heads,” said Aunt Margaret, who had been gazing out of the window, “with the notion that the world is larger than the house.” She turned. She had a way of looking at one, Harriet thought, as though she were estimating the cost of the furniture. “And is it, Harriet? Is it larger?”

“It is not a question of size,” said Harriet. “It is a question of—of breathing.”

At this Mr. Aldworth made a sound. It was not a word. It was something between a sigh and a laugh, and it caused the three women to fall silent, as though a chair had spoken. He shifted in his seat; the leather creaked. He took up his pipe from the little table at his elbow, looked at it, and put it down again.

“Your mother,” he said at last, and his voice was rusty, as though it had not been used for some days, “would have understood it better than I.” He paused. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked, and the tick seemed to Harriet to be counting something out—not seconds, but the small unremarked moments of a life, the mornings, the evenings, the long afternoons of tea and silence, and she felt a sudden, terrible tenderness for him, for the room, for the very dust that floated in the light, and she thought: I am betraying them. And then, at once, with a violence that startled her: No. It is not betrayal. It is—

“Your mother,” her father went on, “was fond of saying that a woman should have a room of her own. She said it in jest. She had her room, you see. She had this house. She had—” He stopped. He raised his hand and let it fall. “She had us.”

“She had us,” said Aunt Constance, with a kind of triumph, as though the argument were now concluded. “And she was happy.”

“Was she?” said Harriet.

The question hung there. Aunt Margaret turned back to the window. Aunt Constance opened her mouth and closed it again. And Mr. Aldworth, who had written nine volumes upon the constitutional history of England, who had been made a Companion of the Bath for his services to learning, who had lectured in great halls to men who nodded and took notes, sat in his chair and looked at his daughter as though she were a text he had not been taught to read.

“I have taken the rooms,” said Harriet. “From the first of next month. Two rooms in Bloomsbury. There is a gas ring, and a window that looks upon a plane tree. I shall work in the morning at the Museum, and in the afternoon I shall copy manuscripts for Mr. Peele, who has offered me—”

“Copy manuscripts!” cried Aunt Constance. “For money!”

“For money,” said Harriet. “Yes. For money. I shall earn—” and here she heard her own voice, and it seemed to her thin and defiant, like a child’s, and she hated it, and went on all the same—“I shall earn my own bread. I shall be tired, perhaps. I shall be frightened, often. But I shall know, when I lie down at night, that I have not been—kept.”

The word fell among them like a stone into still water. Kept. It was not a word one used. It was not, Aunt Constance would have said, quite nice. And yet it had been said, and the saying of it had changed something, had let a little air into the room, or perhaps had let the cold in; it was hard to tell which.

Mr. Aldworth rose. He was tall, and in his old brown jacket he seemed to Harriet, for a moment, immense and fragile at once, like a tree that has been hollowed by weather. He walked to the bookcase and stood with his back to them, running one finger along the spines—the Greek historians, the Latin, the long row of his own works in their dark blue cloth.
