The air in the valley smelled of wet stone and dried heather, a scent that had not changed in three centuries, yet the silence was different. It was not the silence of absence, but of preservation. Arthur lay on the cold grass, his lungs burning with the crisp, sterile chill of a dawn that felt too bright, too clear. He remembered the last thing he had done: stepping off the clifftop at Whitby, the wind tearing at his anorak, the void below calling to him with a siren’s lure. He had expected impact. Instead, he had felt a lurch, a sensation like being swallowed by a heavy wool blanket, and then this: the green, the grey sky, and the profound, terrifying quiet.

He sat up, his head pounding. The landscape was familiar yet wrong. The hills of North Yorkshire rolled out before him, but the farmhouses were gone, replaced by a seamless integration of moss-covered ruins and sleek, low-lying structures that seemed to grow from the earth rather than sit upon it. There were no cars, no power lines, no birdsong. Just the wind moving through the bracken.

Arthur scrambled to his feet, his legs trembling. He was alone, but he was not unobserved. From the path leading up from the valley floor, a figure emerged. She was walking with a slow, deliberate grace, her posture perfect, her steps measured. She was young, by Arthur’s standards—perhaps thirty or thirty-five—but her face held a serenity that was unnerving. It was the face of someone who had stopped struggling against the current of time.

She stopped ten paces away. She did not look surprised. She looked, if anything, tired in a way that was deeply comforting.

"You fell hard," she said. Her voice was soft, accented in a way that was vaguely Northern but smoothed down by generations of quietude. "The fall from the Old World usually does that. Dislocates the mind before it settles the body."

Arthur stared at her. "Who are you?"

"My name is Elara," she said. "And you are the first new face I have seen in forty years. I assume you are the traveler. The stories say you come from a time when noise was a virtue and death was a surprise."

Arthur rubbed his face. The roughness of his beard was a grounding truth. "I don't know what you mean. I was at Whitby. I was going to..." He trailed off, the memory of his suicide attempt feeling absurdly distant, like a dream about another life.

"We know," Elara said gently. "We have seen the ones who jump. The ones who run out of time. You are not the first to seek an end that does not come. Here, the end is a long, slow fade. We do not rush it."

She gestured to the ruins behind her, a crumbling stone wall that once might have enclosed a sheepfold. "Come. The sun is rising, and the cold will seep into your bones. You are shivering."

Arthur moved toward her, drawn by an instinct he couldn't name. As they walked, he asked the question that had been forming in his throat since he woke. "Where are the children?"

Elara’s step did not falter, but her eyes, which were a pale, washed-out blue, grew distant. "There are no children, Arthur. There have been none for a century. I am the last."

The words hit him with the force of a physical blow. "The last?"

"The last born of the natural kind," she corrected. "We stopped, long ago. Not by decree, but by choice. We realized that the world was full enough. That the silence was preferable to the chaos of new beginnings. We aged, and we waited. And now, we are very few."

They walked into a structure that looked like a barn, but the interior was warm, lit by soft, ambient light that seemed to emanate from the walls themselves. It was filled with books, physical paper books, stacked in towers. The smell of old ink and dust was overwhelming. Elara led him to a chair by a small fire that burned without smoke.

"Sit," she said. "I will make tea. Real leaves. We grow them in the high gardens."

Arthur sat, his mind reeling. In his time, overpopulation was a crisis. In this time, extinction was a managed state. "How old are you?" he asked.

"Thirty-two," Elara said, pouring water from a clay pitcher. "I was born in 2140. My mother was ninety when I was born. She loved me fiercely, but she was also afraid. She knew I would be the last. She knew that after me, the species would simply... stop. She raised me to be calm. To be still. To appreciate the weight of things."

Arthur watched her hands. They were steady, elegant. There was no tremor of anxiety, no frantic energy. "Doesn't it hurt?" he asked. "Being the last?"

Elara paused, the teapot hovering over the cup. "Pain is a human reaction to change. I have accepted the change. I have made my peace with the silence. I spend my days tending the gardens, reading the old books, watching the sun set. I am not lonely, Arthur. I am accompanied by the memory of everyone who came before. And soon, I will be accompanied by the earth itself."

Arthur looked around the room. It was beautiful, but it was a tomb. A beautiful, warm tomb. He thought of his own world, with its traffic jams, its arguments, its messy, loud, chaotic life. He had hated it. He had wanted it to stop. But now, seeing this perfect, serene end, he felt a sudden, violent grief for the mess of it.

"What happens when you die?" Arthur asked.

"Nothing," Elara said. "We simply leave. The house will be sealed. The gardens will return to wild. The wind will take the last of us. It is a good thing. It is a rest."
