The arrival of Mr. Julian Thorne at the parsonage of St. Crispin’s had been anticipated with a mixture of dread and delirious hope that only a clergyman’s wife could sustain. Mr. Thorne, a barrister of considerable income and a cousin of no particular affection, was a man whose very presence seemed to command the air of the drawing room, filling it with a heavy, expensive silence that the household found suffocating. His purpose, whispered by the local gossips and confirmed by his own distinct lack of modesty, was to select a wife among the three Ashby daughters. The eldest, Clara, was a study in correct submission, her eyes fixed eternally on the floor or her fan, possessing a virtue so rigid it had become a form of stiffness. The middle daughter, Eliza, was a creature of anxious calculation, her fingers constantly twisting a handkerchief as she weighed the pros and cons of a London carriage against the comfort of a rural vicarage.

It was the youngest, Harriet, however, who held the room’s attention, though not in the manner intended. At fifteen, Harriet possessed a mind that moved with the speed of a pointer and a tongue that seemed to have been oiled by malice. She was not beautiful, for beauty implies a certain passivity of grace that Harriet entirely lacked; she was sharp, angular, and possessed of a laugh that was more a bark than a sound. While her sisters performed the delicate dance of attraction, Harriet engaged in a different sort of performance, one of relentless, microscopic observation.

Mr. Thorne, a man of forty with a face that had long since ceased to change expression, sat in the large chair by the fireplace, his legs crossed with an air of proprietary ease. He spoke of the law, of property, and of the inefficiency of the rural clergy, with a tone that suggested he was not merely conversing but delivering a verdict.

"Your father’s library, Mr. Ashby," Mr. Thorne said, gesturing vaguely at the shelves with a wave of his hand that brushed a stack of theological treatises, "is admirable in its humility. One sees, at a glance, the distinction between a man who reads to inform himself and a man who reads to be seen reading. The former acquires knowledge; the latter, only dust."

Clara gasped softly, a sound of wounded innocence. Eliza looked at her mother, Mrs. Ashby, who was standing by the sideboard, her face a mask of terrified politeness. But Harriet, who had been sitting on the edge of the settee with her hands folded in her lap, looked up with a bright, challenging spark in her eyes.

"Is that so, sir?" she said, her voice clear and carrying effortlessly across the rug. "Then I am curious to know which category my father falls into, as he has read half of that volume of Blackstone while I have only read the title page. If he has acquired knowledge, I have acquired a headache. If he has acquired dust, I have acquired a cold. In either case, I think the exchange has been equitable."

A silence fell, thick and immediate. Mr. Thorne’s eyebrows, which had been resting in a state of mild disdain, lifted slightly. He looked at the girl, then at her horrified sisters, and back again. The air in the room seemed to crackle, not with the warmth of the fire, but with the electric shock of a social breach.

"Equitable," Mr. Thorne repeated, the word tasting strange in his mouth. "You suggest, Miss Harriet, that the acquisition of a cold is a fair trade for the acquisition of legal knowledge."

"I suggest," Harriet replied, leaning forward slightly, "that your definition of equity is perhaps too narrow to encompass the sufferings of the young. You speak of property as if it were a fixed quantity, like a sum of money in a bank. But it is not, sir. It is a fluid thing. It moves. It changes shape. One moment it is a library; the next, it is a headache. I am merely pointing out the fluidity of the situation."

Mrs. Ashby moved forward, her hand hovering as if to strike a fly. "Harriet, my dear," she began, her voice trembling. "You must not be so... so impertinent."

"Imptinent," Harriet said, correcting the pronunciation with a sigh of exaggerated patience. "A word that is too often used to describe a truth that is merely inconvenient. If I were impertinent, mother, I would have said that your tea is too strong and your manners are too strained. But I am not impertinent. I am merely observant. And observation, sir, is the first step in the law, is it not? You cannot judge a case without observing the facts. I am merely observing the facts of this drawing room."

Mr. Thorne’s lips twitched. It was not a smile, exactly, but a relaxation of the muscles around his mouth that suggested the tension of the afternoon had been broken. He looked at Harriet with a new interest, the kind of look a jeweler might give a rough stone, seeing not the dirt, but the potential for a cut.

"Observation," he said, his voice dropping an octave, becoming smoother. "You are right, Miss Harriet. I have been speaking in abstractions. Let us speak in facts. You find the tea too strong?"

"I find it bitter," Harriet said. "And I find your silence regarding the women of this family to be even more bitter. You have come to choose a bride, sir. Yet you have not spoken a single word to any of us that is not a critique of our father’s books or our mother’s house. It is a strange courtship. It is like buying a horse without looking at it. One assumes, if the stable is clean, the horse must be good. But one cannot be certain. One might be buying a donkey."
