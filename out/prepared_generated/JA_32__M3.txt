The morning had been so remarkably mild for October, and the walk to the village so thoroughly agreeable, that Mrs. Pendleton and her three daughters were in unusually high spirits when they returned to Stockwood Lodge. They found the breakfast parlour in a state of some disorder, the tea things neglected, and Mr. Pendleton standing before the fire with a letter in his hand and a countenance of uncommon perplexity.

Mrs. Pendleton, who had been anticipating the pleasure of relating a piece of interesting village news, was obliged to suspend her narrative. She looked at her husband, then at the letter, and sat down with a degree of composure that was meant to indicate her readiness for anything.

"You are quite white, my dear," said she. "I hope it is not bad news from your sister."

"My sister has nothing to do with it," replied Mr. Pendleton, with a shortness very foreign to his usual manner. "It is from Robert."

The effect of this name upon the company was immediate. Eleanor, the eldest, who had been pouring out her father's chocolate, set down the cup with a trembling hand; Cecilia, the second, turned her whole attention from a new bonnet which she had been trimming; and Maria, the youngest, who had hitherto been more occupied with the village news than anything domestic, fixed her eyes upon her father with eager curiosity.

"Robert!" repeated Mrs. Pendleton. "Surely, Mr. Pendleton, you cannot mean your brother Robert."

"I do not know any other Robert who would write to me," returned her husband, "and I must own I am surprised at hearing from this one. He writes from Bristol, and informs me that he has lately returned from the West Indies, where he has been these fifteen years and more, and that he purposes waiting upon us next week."

"Next week!" cried Mrs. Pendleton, sinking into her chair. "Good heavens! And what are we to do with him?"

This was a question which Mr. Pendleton did not appear disposed to answer. He laid the letter on the table, walked to the window, and stood looking out at the dripping trees, as if he expected to find in the autumn landscape some solution to the difficulties of his brother's return.

The truth was, that Mr. Robert Pendleton had quarrelled with his family some sixteen years before, in a manner so decisive that all correspondence had ceased between them. What the quarrel had been about, no one at Stockwood could now say with any certainty. Mr. Pendleton himself had always been remarkably reticent on the subject, and Mrs. Pendleton, who had not been married at the time, had only the most general notion that it had involved some disagreement over money and some expressions of mutual contempt. The sisters, who had grown up in the firm belief that their uncle was a sort of wicked relation existing only to be spoken of in whispers, had each formed their own private conjectures, which they now began to impart with considerable freedom.

"I have always understood," said Eleanor, who was of a serious turn and read more improving books than her sisters, "that my uncle was a man of very dissipated habits, and that he went abroad in consequence of debts which he could not pay. If this fortune, of which he speaks, should be the result of many years of patient industry and reformed conduct, I think we ought to receive him with every mark of kindness and forgiveness."

Cecilia, who was generally allowed to be the beauty of the family, and who therefore looked at everything with reference to its effect upon appearances, had her own objections to this theory.

"My dear Eleanor, you are always supposing that people are reformed. I see no reason why my uncle should not have been always respectable, and merely unfortunate. For my part, I think it much more likely that he was treated with some injustice, and that he has come back to be reconciled. We must be very careful to show him proper attention, for if he is really rich, you know, he may be of the greatest consequence to us."

This observation, which touched upon a subject always near to Cecilia's heart, was received in silence by Eleanor, who did not entirely approve of her sister's motives, and with a laugh by Maria, who never thought of motives at all.

"I dare say he is a very agreeable old gentleman," said Maria, "and will bring us presents from the Indies. I hope he has got some shawls, at any rate. But what I want to know is, what was the quarrel about? Papa, you have never told us."

Mr. Pendleton turned from the window with an air of mingled impatience and reluctance.

"The quarrel, Maria, was about a matter which I do not choose to discuss with my daughters," said he. "It is sufficient that my brother and I differed, and that we have not spoken since. If he now wishes to renew our acquaintance, I am not the man to refuse him, but I own I did not expect it."

Mrs. Pendleton, who had been thinking all this while rather than listening, now entered upon the subject with her accustomed practical good sense.

"Well, my dear," said she, "it is no use wondering what the quarrel was about, for I am sure if you two brothers could forget it, that would be quite enough. The question is, what does he want? A man who has made a fortune abroad does not come to Somerset merely for the pleasure of seeing his relations. He must have some design. Perhaps he means to buy an estate in the neighbourhood."

"My dear," returned Mr. Pendleton, rather drily, "my brother's designs are entirely his own. He says he wishes to see me, and to renew the intercourse which has been so long interrupted. I see no reason to suppose he has anything further in view."
