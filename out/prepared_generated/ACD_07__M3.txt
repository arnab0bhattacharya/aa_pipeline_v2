It was upon a raw, sleeting afternoon in the November of 1894 that my friend found cause to remark upon the singular affair of the Gainsborough portrait. The fog lay thick upon the streets of Mayfair, turning the gas-lamps to smudged orange halos, when our hansom drew up before the iron gates of Meridian House. We had been summoned by telegram, the message bearing the frantic seal of Mr. Elias Fortescue, a man whose collection of English portraiture was the quiet envy of the nation. The matter, as it had been outlined to us, was at once bizarre and unique. The celebrated Gainsborough had vanished, and in its place hung a forgery so clever that only the eye of its owner had detected the substitution. Yet the gallery had been guarded day and night by a pair of reliable warders, and the doors had not been forced. We were admitted by a liveried footman and conducted across a marble hall to the collector’s study. Fortescue was a portly, red-faced gentleman of late middle age, possessed of a brusque, impatient manner that had plainly been sharpened by agitation. He paced the Persian rug with heavy, measured tread. “I am profoundly grateful, Mr. Holmes,” said he, extending a thick, beringed hand. “The affair is a nightmare. The police are baffled, and I confess I am at my wits’ end.” My friend inclined his head with that quiet, cat-like assurance which marked his professional demeanour. “Pray sit, sir,” said he, producing his long, black briar. “A full and frank account is the swiftest road to resolution. You have had but three visitors this month, I understand, aside from your domestic staff?” “Three, Mr. Holmes, to a man,” replied Fortescue, settling heavily into a leather armchair. “A picture restorer, a chimney sweep, and my own nephew, Mr. Reginald Fortescue. The warders, Marsh and Poole, are men of unimpeachable character. They have been in my employ for twelve years. Neither left his post, nor slept, nor admitted a soul without my written permission.” “And the window-frames?” asked Holmes, with a sudden, sharp glance toward the rain-lashed panes. “They are barred, Mr. Holmes. Every one of them. The gallery is a veritable strong-room. Yet the thing has been done, and I am ten thousand pounds the poorer.” “Let us to the gallery,” said Holmes. We followed our client down a corridor of polished oak and through a heavy, iron-bound door, which Fortescue unlocked with a key from his chain. The room was a long, panelled apartment, lit from above by a glass skylight. Upon the wall at the far end hung the picture in question. It was a portrait of a lady in blue satin, painted with that melting grace which distinguishes the master’s later years. Holmes approached it with bent back, his hands clasped behind him, and subjected the canvas to an examination so minute that I was reminded of a bloodhound upon a scent. He produced a powerful lens from his pocket and scrutinised the lower edge, the frame, and the varnish. “A most creditable piece of work,” he murmured at last. “The craquelure has been cleverly simulated with a weak solution of resin and white lead. The pigments are correct to the period. Tell me, Mr. Fortescue, who is your restorer?” “A man named Horace Veitch, of Bond Street. He came to clean the varnish from the surface three weeks ago. He worked upon it for two days, under the eyes of my warders.” “And the sweep?” “A fellow named Judson, sent by a firm in Chelsea. He swept the three chimneys in this wing, including the one in this very room. That was but a fortnight ago.” Holmes had turned his attention to the fireplace, a great, cavernous structure of carved white marble. He knelt upon the rug and thrust his long arm up into the flue, his face assuming that intense, abstracted expression which I had learned to recognize as the precursor of revelation. He withdrew his hand and examined his fingers, which were dark with soot. Then, crossing to the window, he scraped a fragment of pigment from the edge of the frame with his penknife. “Your nephew, Mr. Fortescue,” said he, turning abruptly. “What are his circumstances?” Our client’s face darkened. “Reginald is a young man of expensive tastes and slender means. He called upon me a week ago to request the settlement of a gambling debt. I refused him. He left in high dudgeon.” “And where is he now?” “He lodges in St. James’s. But you do not suspect him, surely? He was in the room for barely twenty minutes, and never once alone. The warders were present throughout.” “The presence of warders,” said Holmes, “is a very singular thing. It is a guarantee of the obvious, and a cloak for the ingenious. I think, Watson, that we shall find our solution not in what was seen, but in what was overlooked. Pray, Mr. Fortescue, have you the height of your warders?” “Marsh is a short, squarely built man. Poole is tall, perhaps six feet.” “And the sweep, Judson—was he a child or a man?” “A grown man, Mr. Holmes. A lean, wiry fellow, though not tall.” Holmes rubbed his hands together, and I perceived that spark of keen delight in his eye which heralded a successful conclusion. “I have no doubt,” said he, “that the substitution was effected upon the day of the sweep’s visit. The restorer, Veitch, is an artist of considerable talent, and it is he who painted the copy. But the manner of its introduction is the crux of the matter. You say the warders were present. Were they watching the sweep at his work?” “At every moment, Mr. Holmes.” “And yet, I perceive, your chimney flue connects directly with the adjoining coal-cellar. A man might ascend it, were he slender enough, and emerge in this very room whilst the warders’ attention was fixed upon the sweep below.” Fortescue stared.
