The wind had been howling for three days, a hollow, keening sound that seemed to scrape the very stones of Gable House. I sat by the hearth, the fire reduced to a bed of grey ashes that offered no warmth, only a faint, dying glow that mirrored the eye of my aunt, Mrs. Harker. She lay on the narrow bed in the corner, her face gaunt and yellow, her breath coming in short, rattling gasps that disturbed the silence of the room more than the storm outside. I held her hand; it was cold and light, like a dry leaf. For weeks I had watched her decline, nursing her with a devotion that left no time for reflection, no space for the cold, hard realities of our station in life. I had believed, with the blind faith of the young and the desperate, that this faithful service would be my reward, that the bond of blood and duty would secure my future. I was a fool.

The doctor had left an hour ago, his breath pluming in the freezing air, and had spoken to me in low, distinct tones while Mrs. Harker slept. He had not looked at me, but at the door, as if expecting an intruder.

"Your aunt’s affairs are settled," he said, his voice dry as parchment. "There is no provision for you, Miss Elms. The estate passes entirely to her nephew, Mr. Arthur Harker."

Arthur. The name struck me like a physical blow. Arthur, who had never set foot in Gable House, who had not written a single letter in five years, who had let the roof leak and the walls crumble while I mended them with my own hands. He was to inherit the mill, the fields, the very stones of the house where I had spent my youth, and I was to be cast out, penniless and alone, into the mercy of a winter that showed no sign of relenting.

I did not weep. I had no tears to shed; they had been dried up by the long hours of vigil and the bitter cold of my heart. I only felt a strange, terrible calm settle over me, a stillness that was more terrifying than rage. I looked at my aunt’s face, so peaceful in death, and felt no hatred, only a profound, aching sorrow for the waste of it all. For what was life, if not this? A struggle against the elements, a clinging to warmth in a house that was destined to be cold?

When the funeral was over, and the earth had closed over Mrs. Harker’s grave, I found myself standing on the moor, the wind tearing at my skirts. I had nothing. No money, no friends, no home. I was free, in the sense that I was unbound by duty, but I was also a beggar, stripped of all that had given me identity and purpose. The moor stretched out before me, vast and indifferent, its peaks lost in the swirling mist. It was beautiful, in a savage, Gothic way, but it was not a place for a woman alone. I could not stay. To stay was to freeze, or to starve, or to be swallowed by the silence.

I had only one option. The Thorne family, who lived at the manor in the next valley, had offered me a position as a companion to their young daughter, Clara. I knew the Thornes. I knew their reputation for sternness and pride, for a coldness that was as biting as the wind on the moor. They were men of order and calculation, who viewed kindness as a weakness and emotion as a vice. I had always held them at a distance, respecting their status but fearing their judgment. But now, what was there to fear? I had nothing to lose. I was already at the bottom of the pit; I could only climb.

I walked to the manor, the snow crunching under my feet. The house loomed out of the mist, dark and imposing, its windows like empty eyes staring out into the void. I knocked on the heavy oak door, and it was opened by the servant, a thin, pale woman who looked at me with a mixture of pity and disdain.

"Mr. Thorne expects you," she said, her voice flat and devoid of warmth.

I was led into the drawing room, where the fire burned bright and hot, a stark contrast to the chill I carried within me. Mr. Thorne sat by the fire, a tall, angular man with sharp, grey eyes that seemed to see through me. He did not stand when I entered. He simply looked at me, his face impassive, as if I were a piece of furniture he was considering for his library.

"Miss Elms," he said, his voice low and precise. "You are here to accept the position?"

"Yes, sir," I said. My voice was steady, though my hands trembled at my sides. "I am grateful for your offer."

"Gratitude is not required," he said. "It is merely a transaction. You will provide companionship to my daughter. You will teach her French and music. You will be discreet, obedient, and silent. You will not involve yourself in our family affairs. You will not speak of the past. You will be a shadow, Miss Elms. Nothing more."

I looked at him, meeting his gaze. I felt the old instinct to rebel, to stand up for my dignity, to demand respect. But I was tired. I was hungry. And I knew, with a clarity that was almost painful, that he was right. I had no right to demand warmth from those who had not given it to me. I had to survive. I had to endure.

"I accept, sir," I said.

"Good," he said, turning back to his book. "Your room is ready. You will dine with us at six. Do not be late."
