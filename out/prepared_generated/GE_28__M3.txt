It is a common error, perhaps the most insidious one to which the speculative mind is prone, to suppose that a strong desire once fulfilled has thereby completed its work upon the human soul. We are apt to fancy that the hungry appetite, once seated at a plenteous board, will eat and be forever silent; yet the history of our inward life tells us rather that every satisfied longing does but open the door to a new consciousness, and that the very attainment of our object is often but the beginning of a more subtle and exquisite distress. For the soul is not a vessel to be filled, but an organism whose fibres expand by what they feed on; and the nourishment that fattens one faculty into dominance may starve its neighbour into a diseased and gnawing sensitiveness.

Such, at least, was the process which had been carrying itself on in the mind of Lady Llewellyn during the two years since she had renounced the stage to become the wife of Sir Hugh Llewellyn of Bodrean Court. To the world, and particularly to that large section of it which reads the announcements of fashionable marriages in the morning papers, the affair had presented itself under the most unexceptionable of aspects. Clara Varry had been, if not the most finished, at least the most passionately felt, of our actresses; she had taken the town by storm, not merely through that physical brilliancy which commands a facile and superficial applause, but through that rare, sympathetic insight which makes the spectator feel that the actress’s own heart is being wrung afresh in every phase of the heroine’s suffering. Sir Hugh, a man of ancient family and considerable Northamptonshire estates, had seen her, had been struck, first by her beauty and then by that depth of nature which is so often hidden beneath the glitter of theatrical success, and had finally offered her his name and home. She had accepted him; and the world, with that ready cynicism which serves it for wisdom, had said it was a very good exchange for a woman who could not have hoped to play Juliet many seasons longer.

But the world, which sees the bride drive away in the carriage and the hounds meet on the lawn, does not see the slow, creeping paralysis that may be seizing upon the inward life. Sir Hugh was a man of honour, of generous and even chivalrous feeling, but his nature was one that lived entirely in the concrete, in the duties of a magistrate, the management of his estate, the sport of the field. He had married Clara because he loved her, but he had no more conception of the conditions under which her artistic soul had drawn its breath than he had of the climate of another planet. He supposed that, being married and provided with a handsome establishment, she would naturally feel herself in that state of repose which was to him the crown of existence.

Clara, for her part, had not discovered her own mistake all at once. The first months had the intoxication of a great change; the stately house, the position, the unaccustomed leisure, had all the charm of novelty. But by degrees, as the novelty wore off, there came upon her that dreadful vacancy which is the negation of all vitality to the artist-nature. The country life, which to Sir Hugh was a perfect and sufficient whole, was to her a landscape without figures; the round of duties, the visits, the management of servants, the occupations of a country gentlewoman, were so many blank hours in which the one faculty that made her life worth living was dying of inanition. She had tried to interest herself in the poor of the parish, and had succeeded in being charitable, as she had formerly succeeded in being pathetic, by a kind of mechanical effort; but the effort left her weary and unsatisfied. The fact was, she had been born an artist, and the artist’s soul cannot feed on good works any more than the poet’s can sustain itself on grammar.

It was on a grey afternoon in late October that the crisis came. She was sitting in the long drawing-room at Bodrean, looking out on the damp, brown woods, when a letter was brought to her, bearing the London postmark and the superscription of Mr. Trenchard, her old manager. Sir Hugh was out with the hounds, and the house was very still. She broke the seal with that curious tremor which comes from the sudden reopening of a past life.

Mr. Trenchard’s letter was brief and business-like, but it had a warmth in it that touched her. He was about to produce a new historical play, and he wanted her for the heroine—not for a long engagement, but for a single season, twelve weeks at the most. He knew, he said, that she had left the stage, and that her position was changed; but he believed she would not be deaf to the call of the art she had served so well. There was no one who could give the part what she could give it, and he would make any terms she pleased.

She laid the letter on her knee, and sat motionless for a long time. The autumn wind drove the dead leaves against the window, and the fire burned low. She was not thinking of the terms; she was not even thinking distinctly of the stage. What had rushed over her was a flood of memories—the smell of the gas, the murmur of the house, the hush before the curtain rose, the old, familiar, intoxicating sense of power, the consciousness that she held hundreds of human hearts in her hand, and could make them beat faster or slower at her will. It was as if a prisoner, long inured to darkness, had been suddenly shown the light of day and told he might walk in it once more—if he would pay the price.
