The rain fell in steady, relentless sheets against the windows of my little parlour, and the fire, though I had heaped it high with coals, seemed to yield no warmth; the damp of the West Country had penetrated even to the marrow of my bones, and I sat wrapped in my shawl, listening to the dreary monotony of the drops upon the glass, and to the yet more dreary monologue of my own thoughts. It was a Thursday, I remember, for on Thursdays I was wont to review the accounts of the week, and to reckon the small economies by which I maintained the respectability of my establishment upon Lansdowne Crescent. The house was mine—that is to say, it was mine so long as I could meet the quarterly payments to the mortgagee—and it was respectable, eminently respectable; the lodgers who inhabited its upper floors were persons of quality and fashion, invalids chiefly, who came to Bath for the waters, and who required quiet, cleanliness, and the appearance of gentility in their landlady. I had cultivated that appearance with the assiduity of a gardener tending a hothouse flower; my gowns were of sober silk, my caps of spotless lace, my manners a careful compound of deference and dignity. I was known in the town as Mrs. Ashcombe, the widow of a naval officer, and I had worn that name so long, and with such consistency, that I had almost persuaded myself it was my own.

The knock at the door came as the clock struck four. I heard the servant girl shuffle along the passage, and then the murmur of voices, and then the girl appeared at the parlour door with a face of some perplexity.

“A gentleman to see you, ma’am,” she said. “He gives no name, but he says he has business with you of a private nature.”

I bade her show him in, thinking it some matter of a lodger’s reference, or perhaps a tradesman with a bill. But when the door opened, and the man entered, I felt the blood forsake my cheeks, and my fingers clenched upon the arms of my chair as though I would have risen and fled, though whither I knew not.

He was not a gentleman; that was my first thought, and it was a thought of bitter irony, for I had known him in a station far beneath the one he now presumed to claim. He was dressed in a decent black coat, somewhat threadbare at the elbows, and his linen was clean but coarse; his face was fuller than I remembered it, and flushed with a colour that spoke of strong drink and coarse living, but his eyes—those pale, restless, cunning eyes—were the same, and they fixed upon me with a look of insolent recognition that turned my heart to stone.

“Good afternoon, Mrs. Ashcombe,” he said, and his voice was smooth and civil, but there was a sneer beneath it, like a serpent beneath a flower. “Or should I say Mrs. Winterbourne? Or perhaps you would prefer the name by which I first knew you—Mrs. Blackwood?”

I could not speak. The room swam before my eyes; the fire, the rain, the familiar furniture of my little sanctuary, all seemed to recede and vanish, and I was back again in that dim, shameful past which I had laboured so many years to bury.

“You are surprised to see me,” he continued, seating himself uninvited in the chair opposite mine, and stretching his hands towards the fire with the ease of one who had been long accustomed to make himself at home in other people’s houses. “I daresay you thought me safe in Van Diemen’s Land, or dead, or both. But I have returned, madam, returned to claim what is my due.”

“What do you want?” I found voice to say at last, though it was scarcely more than a whisper.

“Merely a trifle,” he replied, “a small matter of fifty pounds, to be paid quarterly, for the term of my natural life. A pension, as it were, for an old and faithful servant.”

“Fifty pounds!” I cried. “You are mad. I have not such a sum. I am a widow, a respectable woman, and I have nothing to give you.”

“Respectable!” He laughed, a low, ugly laugh that seemed to echo in the corners of the room. “Respectable! And how long, my dear lady, do you think your respectability would survive if I were to tell your lodgers—your fine invalids, your ladies of quality—that their landlady is the wife of a convicted felon? That she was married under a false name to a man who was transported for forgery? That she fled from him, changed her name, and set up as a widow of a naval officer who never existed?”

I rose from my chair, trembling in every limb. “You would not dare,” I said. “You would ruin yourself as well as me.”

“I have nothing to lose,” he answered, and for the first time a note of genuine bitterness crept into his voice. “I have spent ten years in a penal colony, madam, ten years of labour and degradation, and I have returned to find my wife living in comfort, in respectability, in a fine house in Bath, while I have not a roof to call my own. Do you think I care for my reputation? It is already in ruins. But yours—yours is a fragile thing, and it is in my power to shatter it with a word.”

He rose too, and came towards me, and I shrank back against the mantelpiece, my hand upon my heart, which beat so violently I thought it would burst.

“Consider,” he said, and his voice was soft and persuasive now, the voice of a serpent in the garden. “Fifty pounds a year is a small price to pay for peace of mind. You have a comfortable establishment; you have your lodgers; you have your good name.
