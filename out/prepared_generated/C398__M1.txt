The letter lay on the polished mahogany of the escritoire, a pale and ugly thing against the dark wood. It was sealed not with wax but with a smear of cheap paste, and the paper was so thin that the hurried, spidery ink of the address seemed to bleed through. Mrs. Eleanor Vance, the recipient, regarded it as she might a dead mouse discovered in the pantry: with a mixture of disgust and weary resignation.

Eleanor was a woman sculpted by time and loss into a figure of formidable composure. Her black silk gown was cut with severe elegance, her silver hair coiled in a perfect, unyielding knot at the nape of her neck. At forty, she was the mistress of a fine house in Mayfair, the widow of the late Mr. Alistair Vance, whose fortune in shipping had left her a life of quiet, unassailable comfort. Her past was a country she had long since closed her borders to.

The letter was from the Foundling Hospital on Guildford Street. For twenty years, she had made an annual, anonymous donation—a tithe of guilt, she called it in the privacy of her mind, sent by a solicitor to ensure her name never graced their ledgers. This, however, was a reply. Not to the solicitor, but to her. The brittle, stained paper felt like an accusation.

With a sigh that barely disturbed the still air of the room, she took up her silver letter knife and slit the seal. The script within was cramped and functional. It informed her, with the detached efficiency of an institution dealing in human cargo, that a young woman named Clara Finch, formerly of the hospital, had made an inquiry. An inquiry about a child left on their steps in the winter of 1813. A female infant, wrapped in a single woollen blanket, with no token of identification. The hospital, in its infinite bureaucracy, had somehow connected this inquiry to the anonymous donor. They wished to know if the donor had any information that might aid the young woman in her search.

Clara Finch.

The name was a stone dropped into the placid well of Eleanor’s memory. She had not given the baby a name. It would have been an attachment, a weakness. The midwife in the squalid room in Cheapside had simply called her ‘the little one’. Eleanor had held the infant for only a moment, a terrifying, squalling weight of need and life. Her own face, barely seventeen and pale with terror, had been reflected in the child’s dark, unfocused eyes. Then she had wrapped the baby tightly, her fingers numb with cold and dread, and had walked through the pre-dawn fog to the great, grim building on Guildford Street. She had placed the bundle on the stone steps, her heart a frantic drum against her ribs, and had not looked back. She had run, not walked, back to her father’s house, her reputation, her future, her entire existence, salvaged from the wreckage of a single, desperate mistake.

Now, twenty years later, that mistake had a name. Clara Finch. And she was a seamstress.

The solicitor, Mr. Abernathy, had been most discreet. A quiet word here, a coin slipped there, and the facts had emerged. Clara Finch, aged twenty, worked for a dressmaker in a cramped and airless attic in Spitalfields. She lived in a single room in a lodging house nearby. She was poor. Terribly, hopelessly poor. The information was delivered in Mr. Abernathy’s dry, precise tones, but for Eleanor, it was a series of blows. She saw the girl not as a person, but as a collection of deprivations: calloused fingers, a hollow-cheeked face, the constant gnawing of hunger. A life shaped by the very absence Eleanor had provided.

A wave of something hot and sharp—shame, perhaps, or a ghost of maternal instinct—clutched at her throat. She had left the child to the mercy of the state to save herself, to preserve the fragile, glittering life she was meant to lead. And that life had led her here: to a widow’s weeds and a silent, echoing house, to the clink of her own teacup in the oppressive quiet. She had succeeded. She was safe. She was respected. And her daughter was stitching hems for pennies, her back bent in a garret.

The choice presented itself with brutal clarity. She could do nothing. The letter could be burned, the inquiry ignored. She could go on with her life of tranquil, well-appointed misery, the secret buried once more. Or, she could answer. She could send for Clara Finch.

The thought was a dizzying plunge into a chasm. To claim the girl now would be to confess everything. Not to a priest in the safety of a confessional, but to the world. Her carefully constructed life would crumble. The women who took tea in her drawing-room, the gentlemen who tipped their hats to her in the park—they would all know. They would see not the respectable Mrs. Vance, but the disgraced Miss Albright, the girl who had borne a child out of wedlock and callously abandoned it. Her reputation, the very thing she had sacrificed the baby for, would be annihilated. She would be an outcast, a pariah in the very society she had clawed her way into.

But the image of the garret in Spitalfields would not leave her. She saw the thin shawl, the needle pricked fingers, the exhaustion etched on a young face that was, she realised with a lurch, a mirror of her own at that age. She had been so terrified of poverty then, so desperate to escape it. She had succeeded, only to condemn her own flesh and blood to it.

For three days, Eleanor Vance did not leave her house. She sat in the drawing-room, the letter on the table before her, and wrestled with the two ghosts who had come to claim her: the terrified girl of seventeen and the weary woman of forty. The girl screamed for self-preservation, for the safety of the walls she had built. The woman, worn down by years of silent, echoing guilt, felt the slow, terrible weight of a debt that could never truly be paid, only acknowledged.
