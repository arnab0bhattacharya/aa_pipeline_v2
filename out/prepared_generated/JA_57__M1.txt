Miss Harriet Elton had the misfortune of being the greatest heiress in the county, and the further misfortune of a guardian who considered this fact the most interesting thing about her. Mr. Pemberton, a gentleman of unimpeachable integrity and no imagination whatsoever, had laid the matter before her with all the gravity of a man delivering a death sentence. "Every man who comes near you, my dear," he had declared, tapping the table for emphasis, "is a fortune-hunter. It is a sad truth, but a truth nonetheless. They see the Elton estate and nothing else."

Harriet, who was nineteen and possessed of a lively understanding, received this counsel with a composure that Mr. Pemberton mistook for obedience. Inwardly, she resolved upon a course of action that she felt certain would vindicate both her judgement and her heart. She would test these fortune-hunters. A few well-placed words in the Bath circulating libraries, a sad countenance at the Pump Room, and the rumour would spread like a contagion: the Elton estate was gone, lost to a disastrous investment in a shipping venture. Harriet would be left with nothing but her good name and a competency so small as to be laughable.

The experiment was conducted with a precision that would have done credit to a military campaign. Within a fortnight, the alteration in her circumstances was the established topic of every drawing-room in the city. And the alteration in her suitors was no less remarkable. Mr. Ashworth, whose attentions had been so fervent as to alarm even Mrs. Elton’s complacent chaperone, found that he was suddenly called away to his estate in Derbyshire. Mr. Greville, who had spoken of nothing but his admiration for her mind, now found his mind occupied with the daughter of a shipping magnate. The gentlemen who had crowded her card table now found it more convenient to stand by the fireplace. Harriet observed their retreat with a satisfaction that was, perhaps, a little too cool to be entirely ladylike.

Only one of her acquaintance remained. Mr. Fitzwilliam Croft was a man of about thirty, with a small estate in Dorset, a quiet manner, and the unfortunate habit of correcting people’s Latin. He was not handsome, his conversation was seldom brilliant, and he had a way of looking at a person as if he were assessing their moral architecture. Harriet had never liked him. His presence was a constant, low-grade irritation, like a draught in a well-appointed room. He had never paid her any particular compliment, and his proposals of a walk were always framed as a matter of healthful exercise rather than romantic dalliance.

It was therefore with a sense of profound vexation that she found him waiting for her in the Pump Room one Tuesday morning, when all other swains had vanished. He bowed with his usual solemnity. "Miss Elton. I trust you are well."

"As well as can be expected, Mr. Croft," she replied, her tone implying that expectations were very low indeed.

"I was sorry to hear of your recent misfortune," he said, his eyes fixed on a point somewhere beyond her shoulder. "A most distressing business. Such reverses are a trial to the spirit."

Harriet prepared herself for the inevitable, clumsy offer of support, the transparent attempt to prove his constancy now that the prize had lost its lustre. "It has been a shock," she said, with a sigh she had practised before the mirror. "But one must learn to bear one's troubles with fortitude."

"Indeed," he agreed. "Fortitude is a cardinal virtue. However, I confess I came to speak with you on a matter of some urgency, which your change in circumstances has made all the more pressing."

Here it comes, thought Harriet, a wave of weary triumph washing over her. The final, and therefore least convincing, declaration.

"It concerns your late father's library," Mr. Croft continued, oblivious to her internal monologue. "The collection of classical texts at Elton Park. I understand from my bookseller that the family may be obliged to sell it. I have a modest income, as you know, but it is sufficient for my own needs. I have therefore taken the liberty of arranging to purchase the entire collection, and to house it in my own library until such a time as you may be in a position to reclaim it. It would be a tragedy to see it dispersed."

Harriet stared at him. The silence in the Pump Room seemed to deepen. "You... you have purchased my father's books?"

"The Virgil and the Cicero are particularly fine," he said, with a flicker of animation in his otherwise placid face. "The annotations are of great scholarly interest. I could not allow them to fall into the hands of some ignorant collector who would value them only for their binding. The sum I have paid is, of course, entirely at your disposal. I have instructed my man of business to settle the funds upon you directly. A library is a part of a family's soul, Miss Elton. It is not a thing to be sold for debt."

He then offered her his arm, his expression one of mild concern, as if he had just delivered a weather report. "You are looking pale. Shall we take a turn about the room? The air is bracing."

Harriet, for the first time in her life, was at a complete loss for words. She took his arm, her mind a whirlwind of confusion. This man, this pedantic, uninspiring man, had not offered for her hand. He had not even offered for her heart. He had offered for her father's books, and in doing so, had demonstrated a constancy and a generosity that all her other suitors, with their protestations of undying love, could not approach. He had not seen an heiress in distress; he had seen a collection of Virgil in peril.
