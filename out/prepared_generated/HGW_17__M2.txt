The machine stood in the corner of the laboratory like a piece of expensive, dead furniture. It was a tangle of brass tubes, glass lenses, and a central eye of polished quartz, wired to a battery of storage cells that hummed with a faint, insect-like vibration. It was, in the strictest mechanical sense, a success. It was, in the practical sense of the thing, a failure of the highest order.

Arthur Pym, who had built it, sat on a stool with his head in his hands. He was a man of forty, with the weary, precise appearance of a man who had spent too many years correcting the errors of other people. Outside, the rain was beating against the windows of the house in King’s Road, Chelsea, a rhythmic, dreary percussion that seemed to underscore the absurdity of the situation. Pym had intended to build a device for the observation of distant terrestrial events. The principle was simple: the transmission of light waves, or rather, a specific band of the ether’s oscillations, through a focused medium, allowing one to see, with perfect clarity, what was happening in, say, the docks at Liverpool or the fields of France, at that very moment. It was to be a scientific marvel, a tool for geography and perhaps for commerce.

But when Pym had first adjusted the focus and looked through the eyepiece, he had not seen the present. He had seen the future.

It had started subtly. He had looked at the clock on the mantelpiece. The hands were at ten minutes past four. But in the image from the machine, the hands were at ten minutes past four. He had assumed a coincidence. Then he had looked at the street outside. A woman in a grey cloak was walking past. In the machine, the woman in the grey cloak was walking past, but she was holding a red umbrella that was not in her hand at that moment. A minute later, she stopped, looked up at the sky, and pulled the umbrella from under her arm. The image in the quartz eye had shown her opening it three seconds before she did.

Pym had laughed then. He had told himself it was a trick of the refraction, a delay in the transmission, a ghost image of a past event superimposed on the present. But the ghost had not faded. The image had sharpened. And what it showed was not the past. It was the next second, the next minute, the next hour.

Now, three days later, the machine was showing him the end of the world. Or at least, the end of England.

The image was stable. It showed a vast field, brown and churned up, under a sky the color of a bruise. In the foreground, the shattered hull of a destroyer lay beached in the mud. In the background, the skyline of London was gone. Where Westminster should have been, there was a crater, smoking, vast and black. Men were running in the distance, their figures distorted by the heat haze. There was no sound, of course. The machine transmitted only light. But Pym could see the smoke rising in great, slow columns, and he could see the debris settling like snow.

He knew the date. It was not far off. Three weeks. Perhaps four. He had seen the news in the London papers before the event; a tense situation on the Rhine, a misunderstanding about naval maneuvers, a speech by a foreign ambassador that had been taken for a declaration of war. He had seen the mobilization orders in the machine before they were signed. He had seen the trains leaving the stations before the passengers were called to the platforms.

And now he saw the result.

Pym stood up. His legs felt weak. He looked at the machine, then at the door, then at the window. The rain continued to fall. The world outside was quiet, ordinary, and ignorant. He had to tell someone. He had to warn them. It was the only logical course of action. The scientific method demanded verification, but the moral imperative demanded action.

He dressed quickly, buttoning his coat with trembling fingers. He took his hat and his umbrella. He walked out into the street. The air was damp and cold. He hailed a hansom cab.

"Whitehall," he said. "The War Office."

The cab driver looked at him. "It’s raining, sir. You’ll get wet."

"Take me to Whitehall," Pym said, his voice sharper than he intended.

The cab rattled through the wet streets. Pym’s mind was racing. How could he explain it? He had no proof. He had a machine that worked on principles that were not yet fully understood, even by himself. If he went in and said, "I can see the future, and it shows your country destroyed," he would be sent to a hospital. He would be laughed out of the room. Or worse, he would be ignored.

He thought of his friend, Dr. Halloway, at the Royal Society. Halloway was a man of sound judgment, a skeptic, but a man who respected data. Perhaps he could take the machine to him. But Halloway would want to test it. He would want to know the mechanics. And Pym did not fully understand the mechanics. The quartz eye seemed to have a property that defied the known laws of physics. It did not just reflect light; it intercepted it. It caught the light that was not yet emitted. It was like seeing the shadow of an object before the object was placed in the path of the sun.

As the cab turned into Whitehall, Pym saw the crowd. It was a busy day. Men in top hats and bowler hats were hurrying along the pavement. Cabs were passing in both directions. The atmosphere was one of routine, of business, of the steady, unbroken flow of life. Pym felt a sudden, violent anger. They did not know. They did not know that in three weeks, the rain would be mixed with ash, that the cabs would be burned out, that the men in top hats would be dead or buried.
