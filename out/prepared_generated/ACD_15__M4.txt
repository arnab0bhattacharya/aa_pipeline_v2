The fog had lain heavy upon Clerkenwell since dusk, and it was with no small satisfaction that I quitted the press of Holborn and turned into the quieter thoroughfare of Sekforde Street, where the lamps burned dim and yellow through the murk. My friend had summoned me by telegram at an hour when most men of my profession are glad to be at their clubs, and the message had been characteristic in its economy: “Come at once. Bring your notebook and your scepticism. The affair of the Bramwell lamp grows curiouser.”

I found him upon the pavement outside a narrow brick premises, the windows of which were dark save for a single gleam that issued from a fanlight above the door. He was examining the lock with the aid of a small bull’s-eye lantern, and he straightened as I approached, offering me his hand with that quick, nervous energy which characterised him in the opening stages of a case.

“You are punctual, Watson, as always. I trust I did not drag you from your dinner.”

“You did not. What is it that we are to see?”

“The workshop of Mr. Elias Crane, electrician and inventor, who has been absent from his usual haunts these three days past, and whose departure, if departure it can be called, was attended by circumstances which his landlord regards as singular. The door is unlocked, as you observe, and the gas is still burning within. Let us step inside before the constable returns from his supper.”

The interior was a long, low room, cluttered with the paraphernalia of the electrical experimenter. Coils of insulated wire lay heaped upon the benches, glass vessels of acid stood in rows upon a shelf, and in the centre of the floor a dynamo of primitive design squatted like some iron idol. But it was not these that drew my eye. A travelling valise, half-packed, lay open upon a chair; a coat had been flung across the back of it, and upon the floor beside the door were scattered a handful of papers, as though they had been dropped in haste.

Holmes moved about the room with the noiseless tread of a cat, his lens applied now to the bench, now to the floor, now to the lock of a small safe which stood in the corner.

“You observe, Watson, that the safe is open and empty. You observe also that there is no sign of force. Our friend Crane, then, was either careless or he was in a hurry. The dust upon the threshold tells its own tale—a man’s boots, size eight, walking out with a heavy step and returning with a lighter one, twice in the space of an hour. The valise was packed and then abandoned. Something, you perceive, interrupted him.”

“A robbery?”

“So Mr. Crane would have us believe. He has been to Scotland Yard this morning, and has laid an information against his rival, a young man named Silas Trewin, whose workshop stands at the end of this very court. Trewin, he says, has stolen the design of his improved filament lamp—a design which was locked in that safe until three nights ago. And Trewin, as it happens, has vanished.”

I confess that I looked about me with a new interest.

“Then the fellow has bolted with the spoils.”

“It is a pleasing theory, and it has the merit of simplicity. But simplicity, Watson, is the one thing which a criminal never achieves. Let us walk down the court and see what Mr. Trewin has left behind him.”

The workshop of the rival was a smaller and poorer affair, situate at the blind end of the court, with a single window that gave upon a brick wall. The door was fastened with a padlock, which Holmes opened with the aid of a bent wire in a matter of seconds. Within, the scene was one of disorder so complete that it might have been mistaken for the aftermath of a struggle. A chair was overturned, a shelf had been swept clean, and the floor was littered with broken glass and the torn fragments of what appeared to be technical drawings. But here again my friend’s lens discovered what my eyes had missed.

“The dust upon the floor is undisturbed save in one direction,” said he, “and that direction is toward the window. The window is unlatched. A man of middle height, wearing boots that have been recently repaired upon the left heel, has climbed out and dropped into the yard. He has not gone far, however, for you will observe that the yard gate is bolted upon the inside. He has therefore returned, or he has been prevented.”

“Prevented by whom?”

“By the person who searched this room after he had gone. Look at the fragments of those drawings. They have been torn, not by the hand of a man in flight, but by the hand of a man in search of something. And they have been torn from the bottom upward, which is the method of one who is looking for a signature or a seal. Our visitor was no thief, Watson. He was a searcher.”

I was about to frame a question when a step sounded in the court without, and a young man in a tweed suit, with a pale, anxious face and hands that bore the stains of acids, came hurrying through the open door. He stopped short at sight of us, and his countenance underwent a rapid change from alarm to indignation.

“What is the meaning of this?” he demanded. “This is my workshop. Who are you?”

“My name is Sherlock Holmes, and this is my friend Dr. Watson. You, I take it, are Mr. Silas Trewin.”

The young man’s jaw tightened. “I am. And if you have come from Crane, you may tell him that I have nothing to say. He has accused me of stealing his precious design, and I tell you plainly that the design is mine—mine, I say, and I can prove it, though every safe in Clerkenwell be locked against me.”
