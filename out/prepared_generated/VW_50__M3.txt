The letter lay on the hall table, and Eleanor, passing from the morning room to the kitchen, did not pick it up. It was there; it was from him; she knew the writing—the cramped, slanting hand that had once written essays on Shelley and now wrote only dates and the weather, the weather, always the weather in France, as if the rain there were not the same rain that fell on the laurels in the garden, on the iron railings, on the wet black streets of Kensington where she walked now, in October, in the first autumn of the war, carrying parcels, thinking of him, thinking always of him, which was thinking of mud, of guns, of a boy—was he still a boy?—who had stood in the doorway last August with his kitbag, grinning, and she had thought, I must not cry, I must not cry, and had not cried, but had straightened the collar of his coat, which was absurd, because he was an officer, he had been made an officer, and did not need his mother to straighten his collar, and he had kissed her, quickly, and the door had shut, and the house had gone on being the same house, with the same clock ticking in the hall, the same curtains, the same smell of polish, but emptier, emptier, as if a wall had been removed and the wind blew through where it should not.

She picked up the letter. She took it to the window. The light was thin, the light of October, pale yellow on the brick opposite, and she read: *Dear Mother, we are moving up tomorrow. The fields are quite green still. Give my love to Catherine.* That was all. Not much. Not enough. But what could he say? He could not say what it was like—the noise, the waiting, the nights when one lay in the earth and looked at stars and thought of home, of Eleanor peeling apples at the kitchen table, of his father reading the evening paper, of Catherine, who was somewhere in a hospital, doing things he did not want to imagine, cutting bandages, holding basins, touching wounds. He could not write that. So he wrote about the fields.

Catherine, in the hospital at Rouen, wrote too. Her letters arrived on Tuesdays and Fridays, written on thin paper in a hand that had grown larger, looser, as if the hand itself had expanded, had been stretched by what it touched. *Dear Mother, we had a concert in the recreation hall. One of the orderles sang "Abide with Me" and we all cried. It is foolish to cry. Sister Marsh says one must not. But one does.* And Eleanor, reading this at the breakfast table, with the coffee cooling and the newspaper unread, thought: she is not my Catherine. My Catherine did not cry. My Catherine was fierce and quiet and read German philosophy and played the piano badly and laughed at her own mistakes. Who was this woman who cried at concerts and wrote *one does* as if she had joined some vast, suffering community from which her mother was excluded?

But perhaps she had. Perhaps they all had. The war had taken them and scattered them—Edward to the front, Catherine to the hospital, Eleanor to this house, this street, this solitary routine of waiting and reading and walking to the post office—and Arthur, Arthur to his office in the City, where he sat at the same desk he had sat at for twenty years, signing the same papers, looking at the same view of grey rooftops and the spire of St. Mary's, and thinking—what did he think? Eleanor could not say. She had never been able to say what Arthur thought. He came home at six-thirty, he drank his tea, he read his paper, he said, "Any news?" and she said, "Edward is moving up," or "Catherine writes that she is well," and he nodded, and that was all, that was their communion, their marriage, this exchange of facts over the tea things, while the maid moved in the background and the clock ticked and the war went on, and on, and on.

In 1916 Edward came home on leave. He stood in the hallway, and Eleanor saw that he had changed. He was thinner, and his eyes had a look she did not recognise—a look of distance, as if he were still watching something far away, something that moved at the edge of vision and could not be brought close. He ate enormously, hungrily, and laughed at small things, and was kind to Eleanor in a way that frightened her, because it was the kindness of a stranger, of someone who has seen too much and is being gentle because gentleness is all that is left. She wanted to ask him: what is it? What have you seen? But she could not. The words would not come. They sat together in the drawing room after dinner, and she played the piano—she played badly, as Catherine had played badly, making mistakes and laughing—and he sat in the armchair and did not laugh, but smiled, and said, "That's nice, Mother," and she thought: he is humoring me. He is humoring me as if I were a child. And she wanted to weep, but did not, because one must not weep, one must be cheerful, one must make a home for him to come back to, even if he did not come back, even if this thin, distant young man never came back.

He went. They all went. Catherine wrote that she had been transferred to a clearing station nearer the line. Arthur said, "She is very brave," and Eleanor said, "Yes," and thought: brave, brave, the word is empty, it means nothing, it is what we say because we cannot say what we mean, which is: come home, come home, I am afraid, I am alone in this house with your father who does not speak and a clock that ticks and a garden where the laurels grow too high and no one will cut them because the gardener has gone to the war too, everything has gone to the war, the gardener and the maid's brother and the boy from the post office and Edward, Edward, Edward.
