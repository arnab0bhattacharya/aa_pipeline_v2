The rain had not ceased for three hours. It beat against the diamond-paned windows of Thornecroft Hall with a fury that seemed almost personal, as though the heavens themselves had resolved to punish the old stone walls for the pride of the woman who dwelt within them. I sat alone in the great dining parlour, my book neglected on the knee, my gaze fixed upon the fire—which burned stubbornly, sullenly, as if it, too, felt the oppression of the evening and refused to give its light freely to so desolate a room. The candles were not yet brought. My aunt Mrs. Thursby had retired with a headache. The servants were distant in their quarters, and I was left to the mercy of my own thoughts, which were not gentle companions.

I had refused Sir Piers Bolton. I had refused Mr. Ainsworth of Greystones. I had refused, only last fortnight, the Reverend Mr. Cleve, whose sermonising patience and mild blue eyes had seemed to me the very emblem of a man who would bore me into an early grave—not with cruelty, but with an unrelenting goodness that would suffocate like a feather pillow pressed gently, persistently, over the face. Each refusal had been delivered with civility. Each had cost me nothing—nothing, that is, but a further hardening of that reputation for coldness which the neighbourhood had been pleased to bestow upon me since my twenty-third year. Let them call me cold. I knew what fire burned beneath the stillness, and I feared it more than any winter.

A knock came at the outer door—sharp, imperative, unhesitating. I heard Mrs. Haxby's footsteps cross the hall, heard the creak of the hinges, and then a low murmur of voices. A man's voice. Not deep, but firm, with a quality of restrained authority that I recognised before I could put a name to it, as one recognises a strain of music heard long ago in a different season of life.

Mr. Elias Marlow.

He had been my brother's tutor once, before Richard went to sea, before the fever took him at Lisbon, before the estate and all its burdens settled upon me as sole inheritor. Marlow had departed Thornecroft in the autumn of that year, and I had not seen his face since. I had heard of him only in fragments: that he had taken a position at a school near Halifax; that the school had failed; that he now kept a small establishment of his own in a village whose name I could not recall, teaching the sons of farmers and weavers for a pittance that would have shamed a journeyman. He stood now in the hall, dripping, hat in hand, his plain black coat darkened to the colour of slate by the rain. Mrs. Haxby showed him in.

He entered the parlour with a stride that was neither humble nor bold—simply direct, as a man walks who has no thought to spare upon the impression he makes. He bowed. I rose and returned the inclination, and for a moment we regarded one another in the firelight without speech. He had changed. The face I remembered had been lean and alert, marked by a certain youthful severity. The face I now saw was leaner still, scored about the eyes and mouth with lines that spoke not of age but of endurance—of a man who had been tried and not broken, yet who bore the marks of the furnace through which he had passed. His hair, once black as ink, was touched now with grey at the temples. His eyes were the same—dark, searching, unflinching.

"Miss Aycliffe," he said. "I beg your pardon for this unseasonable visit. I will not tax your patience long."

"Mr. Marlow," I answered. "You are very welcome. Will you not sit?"

He placed himself in the chair opposite mine, across the hearth, and laid his hat upon the floor beside him. He did not lean back. He sat as one ready to rise again at any moment, and his gaze, resting upon me, carried that peculiar quality of candid assessment which I had encountered in no other man of my acquaintance. It was the gaze of a man who measures not to flatter, nor to intimidate, but simply to know.

"I come on a matter of business," he said, "though it is business that touches upon old remembrances, and for that reason I have delayed it longer than I ought. I will speak plainly. The school I now keep is to be enlarged by the gift of a benefactor—a gentleman of Wakefield whose name is of no consequence. He has offered to fund the construction of an additional room and the purchase of such books and apparatus as the scholars require. But he has made his gift conditional upon my securing a counterpart sum—thirty pounds—from other subscribers. I have walked fifteen miles to-day to ask whether you, Miss Aycliffe, would consider yourself among them."

"You walked fifteen miles."

"I did. The hire of a conveyance would have consumed a sixth part of what I hope to raise."

I looked at him. The rain was still falling, driven now in sheets against the glass. I thought of him upon the open moor-road, the wind at his face, the wet seeping through that thin coat, and I felt something stir within my chest that was not pity—I did not think him pitiable—but a kind of fierce, reluctant admiration.

"You might have written," I said.

"I might. But I have learned, Miss Aycliffe, that a letter is too easily answered with a cheque and a civil phrase. I wished to be seen. I wished the request to have a face and a voice, so that it might not be dismissed as one dismisses a bill of account—paid and forgotten."
