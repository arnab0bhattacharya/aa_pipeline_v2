The appointment was for four o’clock, at a café of no particular distinction upon the Rue des Glaciers, a street which in that season of mist and early dusk seemed to lead nowhere but into the grey heart of the city’s silence. Volkov had been told to come alone, and he had come alone, though he was aware that solitude was a thing the committee prized only in its members and suspected in everyone else. He was a student of chemistry, or had been, in another life, in a city of the east whose name he no longer permitted himself to pronounce aloud; and he sat now with his hands about a glass of coffee grown cold, watching the door with the patient, hooded attention of a man who has learned that all doors open upon uncertainty.

They came in together, three of them, shaking the rain from their shoulders with the elaborate carelessness of conspirators who have rehearsed their entrance. There was Zinoviev, who was said to have escaped from a fortress and who spoke always in a lowered voice, as though the walls of Geneva were in the pay of the Okhrana; there was the woman, Vera Sergeievna, whose thin face and burning eyes suggested a saint out of some heretical icon, and who carried a parcel of pamphlets wrapped in oilcloth as another woman might carry a child; and there was the young one, Mikhail, who had the look of a seminarist and blushed when he was addressed. They took their seats about Volkov with a grave and ceremonial intimacy, and Zinoviev, leaning forward, asked him in a whisper whether he had considered their proposal.

The proposal was this: that Volkov should carry certain papers across the frontier, papers whose nature was not specified and whose destination was named only as “the north.” It was a task for which he was admirably suited, they said, because he was unknown, because his passport was in order, because he had the manner of a scholar and the face of a man who thinks only of his books. Volkov listened to the enumeration of his own virtues with a strange sinking of the heart. He had done nothing to earn this trust. He had come to their meetings out of a loneliness so profound that it had seemed to him a kind of duty, and he had sat in the back of their shabby rooms and nodded at the phrases of the revolution as a man nods at a language he is learning and does not yet speak. They had taken his silence for profundity and his foreignness for caution, and now they wished to make him their courier.

“You understand,” said Zinoviev, “that we do not ask this of everyone. There are those among us who are watched. There are those whose faces are known. But you—” he made a small gesture, as of one presenting a gift, “you are a blank page. It is a great advantage, in our work, to be a blank page.”

The woman, Vera Sergeievna, looked at him with her consuming eyes and said nothing. It was the young Mikhail who spoke next, with the eagerness of one who has not yet learned that enthusiasm is a thing to be hoarded.

“We have all read your article,” he said. “The one in the review. On the necessity of action. It was—it was magnificent.”

Volkov had written no article. He had written nothing since leaving home but letters to his mother which he did not send. He understood, then, with a clarity that was almost physical, that the committee had mistaken him for another man, a man of the same name perhaps, or a man whose name they had confused in the weary commerce of exile, where so many identities were lost and found and lost again. He opened his mouth to correct them, and in that instant the door of the café swung open and a gust of wet air entered, and he saw the three of them turn their heads as one, and he saw the fear in their faces, the naked, animal fear that was the true bond of their fellowship, and he closed his mouth again.

What was it that he felt? He would ask himself this afterward, in the long nights of his room above the baker’s shop, listening to the bells of the cathedral toll the hours in a city that was not his. It was not vanity. He was too honest for vanity. It was something more obscure, a warmth at the centre of the chest, as though a hand had been laid there, a hand that asked nothing and offered everything. These people believed in him. They believed in a version of him that did not exist, and their belief was a fire at which he had warmed his frozen hands, and he could not bring himself to extinguish it.

“There is much to arrange,” Zinoviev was saying. “The papers must be sewn into the lining of your coat. You will travel by way of Basel, and from Basel to Strasbourg, and from Strasbourg—” He went on, spinning the web of the journey, and Volkov heard himself agreeing, heard himself asking the questions that a man in his position ought to ask, and all the while a voice within him, cold and precise, remarked that he was now committed to a course of action whose consequences he could not foresee, and that the warmth at his chest was the warmth of a fire that would presently consume him.

They rose to go. Vera Sergeievna, passing him, touched his sleeve with her gloved hand, a touch so light he might have imagined it, and said, in a voice that was like the sound of a violin string plucked in an empty room, “We have waited a long time for someone like you.” Then they were gone, and he was alone with the cold coffee and the rain-streaked window, and the knowledge that he had crossed a frontier more perilous than any of which they had spoken.
