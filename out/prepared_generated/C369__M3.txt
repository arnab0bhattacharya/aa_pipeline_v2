The rain came on at half-past three, just as the omnibus lurched away from the Hammersmith terminus, and by the time it reached the corner of King Street the windows were so streaked with water that the world outside looked like a smudged charcoal sketch. The vehicle was full. It was one of the old type, painted a dull crimson, with the open staircase at the rear and the long bench seats running lengthwise, so that the passengers sat knee to knee, staring at one another or at the floor, pretending to be alone in the crowd. The smell was of wet wool and wet metal and the faintly chemical tang of macintoshes, and underneath it all the slow sweet rot of the wooden floorboards, which never quite dried.

Near the window on the left side, where a small diamond of unrainy glass had been wiped clear by someone's sleeve, sat a young woman in a navy blouse and a plain brown skirt, her hands folded in her lap over a small handbag. Her name was Edith Pell, and she was a typist at a firm of patent agents in Holborn. She was thinking about a man called Arthur. Arthur worked in the same building, on the floor below, as a draughtsman, and he had fair hair that he parted with great precision and hands that were always stained with ink. Last Thursday he had asked her whether she thought it would rain on Saturday, and she had said she thought it might, and he had said he hoped not because he was planning to walk to Hampstead, and she had said oh, that was nice, and then the lift had come and he had gone down and she had stood there with her heart beating in a way that was quite ridiculous. She had spent the whole of Saturday watching the sky. It had not rained. She did not know whether he had gone to Hampstead. She did not know whether he had gone alone. She was twenty-three years old and she lived in a room in Shepherds Bush with a gas ring and a landlady who played hymns on the piano every evening, and she had never been to Hampstead in her life.

Across the narrow aisle, an old man was counting his change in the hollow of his palm. His name was Walter Budge, though no one on the bus knew this, and he was seventy-one years old and had been a clerk at a firm of indigo merchants in Mincing Lane for forty-six years until the firm had folded in the spring and he had found himself at home on a Tuesday morning with nowhere to go. He counted the money carefully, moving each coin with the pad of his thumb: two half-crowns, a florin, two shillings, a sixpence, and four pennies. It came to eleven shillings and tenpence. He had counted it three times since leaving Hammersmith and it always came to the same, but he counted it again because the counting was the only thing that filled the hours, and the hours were very long now. His wife had died in 1903 and his daughter had married a man from Leeds and moved there, and the room he rented was in a house in Fulham kept by a Mrs. Granger, who was kind but whose kindness made him feel small. He had been on his way to Mincing Lane, not because there was anything to do there but because it was where he had always gone, and the habit of forty-six years is not broken in a season.

At the far end of the bench, pressed against the bulkhead, a young woman held a child across her lap. The child was perhaps three or four years old, wrapped in a shawl that was too large for it, and it was sleeping with the heavy, damp sleep of fever. The mother's name was Mary Toole, and she had come that morning from Camberwell to a hospital in Bloomsbury where a doctor had looked at the child's chest and listened through a glass tube and said to keep it warm and bring it back in a week. She had not understood most of what he said because he had spoken quickly and had already been turning to the next patient, but she had caught the word warm and she had caught the word week, and now she was going back to Camberwell on an omnibus that went east when she needed to go south, because it was the first one she had found and the child had been crying and she had not been able to think. The child's name was Daniel and he had been hot to the touch for four days. She pressed her lips to his forehead and felt the heat through her mouth and said nothing.

Sitting on the opposite bench, with his hat on his knee and a small notebook open against his thigh, was a clergyman named the Reverend Cedric Mowbray. He was the curate of St. Aldhelm's, Haggerston, and he was composing a sermon. He wrote in a tight, slanting hand that crept across the page like a vine, and the words he wrote were about the parable of the talents, which he had chosen because he had preached on the prodigal son two weeks ago and the sower the week before that, and the talents were next in what he thought of privately as the rotation. He had written: "We are each entrusted with something. Not the same thing. Not in the same measure. But something." He paused. The sentence seemed to him flat. He crossed out "something" and wrote "a portion of the divine." That was worse. He crossed it out and wrote "something" again. He had been a curate for nine years.
