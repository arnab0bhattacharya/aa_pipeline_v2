It was a fact noted by the more observant of the townspeople of Ridgeway—a small town lying somewhat apart from the great currents of commerce, where the mail-coach stopped but twice a week, and where a man might live and die without having his opinions disturbed by the clash of contrary minds—that the new schoolmaster, Mr. Cornelius Hale, was a person of an unsettling singularity. He had come among them in the autumn of the year, a man of middle age, with a spare, active figure, a countenance in which a certain scholarly pallor was relieved by the keenest of grey eyes, and a manner that was at once gentle and abstracted, as if his thoughts were but half engaged with the visible world. He had been appointed by the trustees of the Ridgeway Grammar School on the strength of testimonials from a distant university, and it was generally agreed that his learning was prodigious; but it was soon discovered that his method of imparting it was not such as the fathers of Ridgeway had been led to expect.

The old system, under the late Mr. Bumble, had been one of steady, if unenlightening, routine. The boys had been exercised in the declensions of Latin nouns and the conjugation of Greek verbs, in the parsing of sentences and the repetition of rules, until the whole machinery of grammar had become as familiar and as meaningless as the clatter of the mill-wheel by the river. Mr. Hale, on the other hand, was possessed by a dangerous enthusiasm for what he called the direct study of Nature. He would lead his scholars out of the close schoolroom into the lanes and meadows, bidding them observe the structure of a flower, the habits of an insect, the slow processes of germination. He brought into the classroom stones and fossils, the nests of birds, the delicate skeletons of leaves, and would discourse to his wondering audience of the great system of classification, of the laws of growth, of the interdependence of all living things, with a fervour that seemed to the solid men of Ridgeway little short of a species of madness.

The complaints were not long in finding voice. It was the grocer, Mr. Tappit, who first gave them utterance in the shop of the saddler, Mr. Pottle, where the more substantial inhabitants were wont to assemble of an evening to discuss the affairs of the nation and the town.

“I tell you what it is, neighbours,” said Mr. Tappit, setting down his glass with a decisive emphasis, “I sent my boy to school to learn something that would be of use to him in the world, and what does he come home with? A pocket full of beetles and a head full of nonsense about the sexes of plants. He can’t cast up a bill of parcels to save his life, and as for his Latin, he might as well have never seen a grammar. It’s my belief the man is a heathen, and a bad one at that.”

“Ay, you may well say it,” assented Mr. Pottle, whose son had been discovered by his father in the act of dissecting a frog upon the kitchen table, to the great detriment of the tablecloth. “There’s no discipline in the school now. The boys do pretty much as they like, and the master is as mild as a lamb. My Sam tells me that when he was idle yesterday, Mr. Hale only said, ‘Consider the ant, my boy,’ and gave him a book with pictures of insects in it. That’s not the way to make a scholar.”

The feeling thus expressed was general. The trustees, who were mostly men of business, and who regarded the school as a charitable institution for the fitting of boys to be clerks and apprentices, began to shake their heads ominously. It was whispered that unless Mr. Hale could be brought to a sense of his duty, his connexion with the school must be severed at the close of the half-year. The clergyman, the Rev. Mr. Dismal, who had a great reputation for sound doctrine, and who looked upon all innovation as a species of dissent, preached a sermon on the text of “What is man, that thou art mindful of him?” in which he took occasion to remark, with a pointedness that was not lost upon his hearers, that the mind of youth should be fed with the sincere milk of the word, and not with the vain and curious speculations of a carnal philosophy.

Amidst this general dissatisfaction, there was one person who stood, if not openly, at least silently, in the defence of the obnoxious master. This was a boy of the name of Timothy Gedge, the son of a poor widow who lived in a cottage at the end of the town, and who earned a scanty livelihood by the washing of the linen of the more prosperous inhabitants. Timothy was a small, pale, quiet boy, of a singularly retiring disposition, who had been the butt of the rougher spirits of the school, and who had seemed, under the old regime, to be hopelessly dull and unteachable. He had no aptitude for words, and the intricacies of the Latin syntax were to him a labyrinth from which he could never hope to escape. But Mr. Hale had discovered in him a faculty that had lain hidden and unsuspected. One day, in the course of a lesson on the structure of insects, he had observed the boy’s eyes kindle with a light that was not of the common order. Timothy had brought him a beetle, a rare and beautiful creature, which he had found in the hollow of an old willow-tree, and had asked a question about it with a trembling eagerness that showed how deep was his interest.
