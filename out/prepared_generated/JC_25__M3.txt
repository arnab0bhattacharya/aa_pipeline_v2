The heavy doors of the Hall of Crimson Tiles had been thrown open, though the heat of the evening, thick with the effluvia of the harbour and the damp, stealthy exhalations of the surrounding swamps, did not retreat before the shallow draft of the punkahs. They only swayed above the long table, stirring the stale, perfumed air with a motion as futile as the promises that were being uttered there. The foreign merchants, men of substantial girth and cautious, calculating eyes, sat in a nervous glitter of shirt-fronts and decorations, listening to the voice of the man who had seized the republic. He stood at the head of the table, a heavy, dark silhouette against the glare of the electroliers, and his words, delivered in a tone of heavy, paternal solemnity, fell upon the silence of the room with the hollow resonance of a stone dropped into a profound and stagnant pool.

Lieutenant Altamira stood in the shadow of a marble pillar, his hand resting lightly upon the neck of a dark bottle. He had been ordered to serve the wine, a task fitting for an officer of the presidential guard, though his true function in that house of calculated terrors was of a nature less innocent and more profound. He was a young man, but the youth had been scorched out of his face by the relentless sun of the central provinces, leaving behind a grey, ash-like pallor and an expression of brooding, cynical exhaustion. He wore his uniform of dark blue and silver with a rigid precision, as if the stiff cloth were a sort of defensive armour against the moral contagion of the house. He watched the General, whose fleshy, powerful hands rested upon the damask cloth, and he thought of the silence of the villages he had lately ridden through in the province of San Mateo.

The General was speaking of peace. His voice, a deep, rumbling baritone, rolled through the heavy air, touching upon the ancient grievances of the state, the necessity of a firm hand to guide the erring, and the profound respect he held for the commercial interests which were the lifeblood of the nation. He spoke with a heavy, impressive sincerity. His small, quick eyes, buried in folds of fat, swept the length of the table, resting upon the flushed faces of the merchants with a gaze that was at once paternal and menacing. It was the gaze of a man who has looked upon the sudden and violent extinction of human life without any particular emotion beyond a mild, administrative annoyance.

"We must forget the errors of the past," the General was saying, his words rolling out with a slow, deliberate rhythm. "The republic has suffered. She has been torn by factions, by the selfish ambitions of men who did not understand the true meaning of order. But those days are over. I have established a peace—a peace that will endure."

He paused, and a murmur went up from the merchants, a sound of polite, eager assent, as if they were willing to purchase that peace with the very coin of their credulity. There was a Mr. Holroyd, a representative of the Central American Steamship Company, a man with a red, clean-shaven face and large, white hands, who nodded with a vigorous emphasis, as though he were personally guaranteeing the General's words. Beside him sat the German consul, a man with spectacles and a pendulous lower lip, who stared at his plate with a fixed, unseeing gravity. They were all there, the men of commerce and of trade, the exploiters of the country's wealth, gathered together in the Hall of Crimson Tiles to listen to the man who had promised to protect their interests, and they were willing to believe, because they had to believe, because their money was invested in the soil and the mines and the rivers of that troubled land.

Altamira moved forward, his footsteps noiseless upon the thick carpet, and refilled the glasses. The wine was a heavy, red vintage, brought up from the cellars of the old palace, and it glowed in the crystal glasses with a rich, sombre light, like the light of a darkening fire. As he poured, he caught the eye of the General, and for a moment, a fleeting, almost imperceptible shadow passed across the General's broad, heavy face—a shadow of suspicion, of unease, as if he had seen in the young officer's impassive gaze a reflection of something he did not wish to remember. It was gone in an instant, and the General resumed his speech, his voice rising with a sudden, dramatic fervour.

"The provinces," he said, "are quiet. The rebellious elements have been dealt with. There is no more disorder. The roads are safe. The rivers are open. Your commerce will flow unhindered."

Altamira, standing back in the shadow of the pillar, permitted himself the ghost of a smile, a thin, ironical twisting of the lips that had no mirth in it. The provinces were quiet, it was true. He had seen them, those villages in the deep interior, where the mud walls were crumbling and the thatched roofs were blackened by fire, and the silence was the silence of the grave. He had ridden through the district of San Mateo, where the General's emissaries had passed like a pestilence, leaving behind them a trail of charred ruins and unburied bodies. He had seen the plaza of Santa María, where the heads of the insurgents had been placed upon poles, grinning in the sun, their dried, blackened flesh hanging in strips from the bones. The buzzards had been circling in the sky, slow, patient, and indifferent, and the air had been thick with the stench of corruption.

That was the peace the General spoke of. It was the peace of the desert, the peace of the charnel house. And these men, these fat, prosperous merchants with their white shirt-fronts and their heavy gold watches, were drinking to it, raising their glasses in a toast of congratulation, as if they were drinking the very blood of the country.
