From the street, the shop of Mr. Elias Thorne presented a face of unimpeachable respectability. Its windows, dark and polished as a winter river, held the sober spines of sermons and the gilt-lettered backs of encyclopaedias. The bell above the door, a small brass disc, announced a customer with a sound so discreet it was less a summons than a polite cough. It was a place for the quiet exchange of established truths, for the procurement of knowledge that had already been weighed, bound, and set upon the shelves of the nation. Mrs. Martha Thorne, who had presided over this calm domain for twenty years, had cultivated its silence as one might cultivate a rare orchid. She knew its every shade, its every hum.

Her knowledge, however, was a thing of surfaces. She knew the price of a second-hand Gibbon and the correct way to address a bishop’s wife who came in search of a book of meditations. She knew also, in the way one knows the existence of a cellar without ever descending its steps, that there was another life to the shop. It lived behind a green baize door at the rear of the premises, a door she never touched. From beyond it, at certain hours, would come the low, cadenced murmur of foreign tongues, a sound she had learned to hear as no more significant than the ticking of the grandfather clock in the hall. It was a sound that belonged to the life of Elias, a country she had long ago resolved not to visit. Her husband, a man whose placid exterior concealed currents of a depth she did not care to fathom, would emerge from these sessions with his face pale and his eyes holding the distant look of a man who has been staring at a great, bleak distance. She would offer him tea. He would accept. And the silence between them would re-form, solid and complete.

This silence was the architecture of their life. It was not empty; it was furnished with unspoken things, with a history of glances and deliberate omissions. She had seen the books that passed not over the counter but through the baize door: slim volumes in Cyrillic script, pamphlets with crude woodcuts of gaunt men, treatises on theories of society that seemed to her as alien as the phases of a distant planet. She had seen the men who came for them—men with haunted eyes and worn coats who spoke to her husband in a deferential, urgent English, their accents thick with the weight of another world. They were ghosts in her well-ordered house, and she had long since mastered the art of not seeing them. To acknowledge their reality would be to admit the existence of the cellar, and to admit the cellar would be to question the very foundations of the quiet street, the respectable shop, the life she had so carefully built upon the solid ground of the known.

The afternoon the police came, the light outside was the colour of weak tea, a watery, exhausted light that filtered through the Bloomsbury haze. Two men entered. They were not the familiar constable on his beat, a man whose face was as much a part of the street as the lamp posts. These men wore plain, dark suits and carried an air of official stillness that was more menacing than any uniform. The bell’s gentle cough seemed to choke in their wake. The larger of the two, a man with a jaw like a paving stone, removed his hat. The other, younger and with the sharp, restless eyes of a ferret, merely stared at the shelves, his gaze seeming to pass through the leather and paper to the secrets they might contain.

“Mr. Thorne,” the large man said. It was not a question. His voice was flat, a stone dropped into the quiet pool of the shop.

Martha felt a coldness spread from her chest outwards. It was the feeling of a dream turning sour. “My husband is occupied at present,” she heard herself say, her own voice sounding thin and foreign. “He is in the stockroom.”

The ferret-eyed man gave a small, unpleasant smile. “We know where he is, madam. We have an interest in the stock he keeps back there.” He gestured with his head towards the green baize door. The gesture was a violation, a finger pointed at a secret she had spent two decades pretending did not exist.

In that moment, the pretence fell away from her like a shed skin. It did not dissolve gently; it was torn off. She knew, with a certainty that was like a physical blow, that these men were not here for books. They were here for the men with haunted eyes, for the murmurs in the dark, for the entire, sprawling, dangerous country that lay behind that door. Her ignorance, that carefully tended garden of the mind, was revealed to be nothing but a thin veneer over a pit. She knew the names of the men who came: Karsky, the poet with the trembling hands; Volkov, the engineer who spoke of dynamite as a tool of creation; the one they called only ‘the Professor’, a frail old man whose eyes burned with a terrible, abstract fire. She knew their fears, their hopes, their fatalistic talk of a motherland drowning in blood. She had heard it all, understood it all, and had resolutely, fiercely, refused to know it.

“I… I don’t understand,” she stammered, the words ash in her mouth.

The large man’s expression did not change. “I think you do, madam. We would rather this were done without a disturbance. For the sake of the neighbourhood’s tranquillity.”
