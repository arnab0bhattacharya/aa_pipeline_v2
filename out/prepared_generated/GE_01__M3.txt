It is a peculiarity of provincial life, perhaps traceable to that slow accumulation of undisturbed usage which gives a settled character to the very stones of the street, that any novelty of intention is no sooner announced than it is assumed to be the cover for some private and anterior design. Mr. Edgar Tryan, newly come to the vicarage of Milby-under-Elm, had no sooner proposed the founding of a lending library for the instruction and rational amusement of the working men of the parish, than he found himself the object of a scrutiny so searching that it seemed to lay bare not only his purposes but the hidden structure of his coat. For in a town where the manufacture of lace provided the staple by which its bellies were filled, every inhabitant not directly engaged in the loom was held to be engaged in the far more intricate business of watching his neighbour.

Mr. Tryan was a man of mild complexion and earnest eye, whose theological education had imbued him with a desire to mend the world, beginning with Milby, though his acquaintance with the town’s interior economy was as yet chiefly confined to the view from his study window. He had conceived that a library would serve to wean the younger men from the alehouse, supposing, with the confidence of a mind accustomed to the governance of its own faculties, that the provision of wholesome literature would be met with a proportionate appetite for it. He had yet to learn that human desires are not governed by the laws of supply and demand in so simple a manner, and that the Milby working man, if offered a volume of improving history, was prone to ask what mischief the offering of it was meant to conceal.

It was into this field of latent suspicion that Mr. Tryan stepped when he sought the countenance of Mr. Jerome Bowyer, the proprietor of the largest lace-mill on the Fenside road. Mr. Bowyer was a man of heavy fortunes and a countenance to match, his grey eyes possessing the settled calm of one who has long been accustomed to compute the value of human effort in shillings and pence, and who finds the reckoning satisfactory. He received the vicar’s proposal with a deliberation that had in it nothing of enthusiasm, but everything of calculation; and after a pause during which he appeared to be casting up the moral accounts of the parish, he pronounced himself willing to subscribe fifty pounds.

But Mr. Bowyer’s liberality was not a simple effusion of the heart. He had been for some years engaged in a struggle with the older manufacturing interest of the town, represented by Mr. Grime, a struggle which, though conducted under the decorous names of market-prices and rates of wage, had its roots in that profounder antagonism which besets two men of similar ambition confined within the limits of a single provincial society. Mr. Bowyer perceived that a library, endowed by his subscription and erected under the vicar’s patronage, would serve to attach the working men to his interest, and to confer upon him that character of public beneficence which is often the most serviceable garment that ambition can assume. He did not put this to himself in terms of cold calculation; he was far too experienced in the management of his own motives for that. He believed himself to be moved by a genuine concern for the welfare of his workpeople, and so he was, in so far as that welfare coincided with his own. It is the peculiar felicity of a mind accustomed to business, that its self-interest and its benevolence are so closely interwoven that the eye of conscience cannot detect the seam.

If Mr. Bowyer’s support was thus complicated by private design, the opposition of Mr. Joshua Fode, the old curate of St. Botolph’s, was complicated by nothing so definite. Mr. Fode had been forty years in Milby, and had acquired that tenacious hold upon the town’s prejudices which is the privilege of length of service. He opposed the library not because he had examined its probable consequences and found them injurious, but because it was a new thing, and because it had been proposed by a young man. Mr. Fode’s mind was of that conservative texture which resists innovation as the soil resists the plough; not from any reasoned conviction that the old furrow is the best, but from a deep, inarticulate sense that the turning up of fresh ground must necessarily bring to light some unpleasantness which it were better to leave buried. He had seen many novelties come and go in his time, and had survived them all; which he took as a sufficient proof that they were unnecessary.

“Let ’em read the Book, I say, Mr. Tryan,” he observed, when the vicar ventured to lay the scheme before him in the dusty parlour of the curate’s house, where the smell of tallow and old bindings was as the very breath of antiquity. “The Book’s been good enough for their fathers, and I don’t hold with this newfangled notion o’ stuffin’ their heads wi’ secular books. It’ll on’y make ’em discontented, and a discontented hand’s a bad hand, as I tell Bowyer when he’s talkin’ o’ his new machinery.”

Mr. Fode’s pronunciation was of that broad Midlands character which flattens the vowels and swallows the terminations, as if speech were a process to be got through with as little expenditure of breath as possible. Mr. Tryan, whose own diction retained something of the university, was at pains to conceal his sense of the curate’s narrowness, for he was a man of genuine humility, and not unwilling to suppose that his own plans might be defective.
