The dinner party at Mrs. Fortescue’s was, by every measure of Meryton society, an unexceptionable affair. The company was numerous, the servants were attentive, and the supper, though perhaps displaying more vanity than elegance, was such as to satisfy even those ladies who came chiefly to critique it. Mrs. Vernon, a widow of comfortable fortune and uncomfortable energy, had established herself at the table with an air of triumphant composure, her eye wandering with restless satisfaction over the assembled guests, and resting, with a frequency that could not be accidental, upon the figure of Mr. Ashworth, who sat opposite.

Mr. Ashworth was a gentleman of good estate in the neighbouring county, whose recently enlarged property and unencumbered income had rendered him an object of no small speculation among the matrons of Hertfordshire since his arrival the previous month. He was a man of five-and-thirty, with a countenance more distinguished for good sense than for beauty, and a manner that was agreeable without being eager. Catherine Vernon, her mother’s eldest daughter, had not been insensible to his attention during his visits to their home; but she was too reasonable, and too well acquainted with her mother’s disposition, to build upon it. She had observed him, she had conversed with him, and she had endeavoured, with a degree of success she did not herself suspect, to conceal whatever preference his behaviour might have awakened.

It was during the interval between the removal of the cloth and the entrance of the ladies that Mrs. Vernon’s design, whatever it might have been, declared itself with a force that Catherine was wholly unprepared to withstand. Mrs. Fortescue had been expressing her admiration of the neighbourhood, and lamenting that so few gentlemen of independent fortune had settled in it of late.

“Yes,” said Mrs. Vernon, with an air of deliberate composure, “it is certainly a disadvantage. But I flatter myself we shall not long be without our share. My Catherine, I believe, will not be lost to us much longer. Mr. Ashworth has been so very particular, and I think I may venture to say that we shall soon have a celebration in the family. I have long expected it, and I am sure no one can be more deserving.”

Catherine, who had been engaged in conversation with Mrs. Fortescue’s eldest daughter, heard these words with a sensation that can scarcely be described. The colour rose to her cheeks with a rapidity she could not control, and for one moment she felt as if the whole room must be conscious of her confusion. She dared not look at Mr. Ashworth. She dared not look at any one. She applied herself to her glass of wine with an earnestness that, under other circumstances, might have appeared comical, and endeavoured to recover her composure by the only means in her power — that of not seeming to have heard.

But the words had not been spoken in vain. Mrs. Fortescue received them with a warmth of congratulation that Catherine found almost more insupportable than the original declaration. Mrs. Fortescue’s sister echoed them. Two or three ladies who sat near enough to catch the intelligence turned their heads with that peculiar expression of mingled surprise and satisfaction which such communications never fail to produce in a country society. And Catherine, who felt all the horror of a false position, was compelled to sit under the fire of so many congratulating eyes, with a smile she could not feel and a composure she could not command.

Mr. Ashworth’s behaviour during this scene was such as Catherine could neither fully approve nor wholly condemn. He looked up with evident surprise — surprise that was not unmingled, she thought, with something like displeasure — and then, as if conscious of the general observation, he withdrew his gaze and addressed himself to his neighbour with an air of deliberate composure. Whether he was more mortified or more amused, Catherine could not determine; but that he was not indifferent to the circumstance, his heightened colour sufficiently betrayed.

When the ladies withdrew, Catherine found herself the object of a scrutiny which she had no means of escaping. Mrs. Fortescue, with the kindest intentions in the world, took her arm and led her to a sofa, where she proceeded to enlarge upon the happiness of such a connexion, the respectability of Mr. Ashworth’s family, and the excellence of his estate, with a fluency that left Catherine no opportunity of reply, even had she dared to make one. Other ladies gathered round, and Catherine was obliged to listen to a discourse on settlements and wedding clothes, and to receive the compliments of women whose friendship she had never particularly valued, with a patience that cost her no small exertion.

She had, however, the satisfaction of perceiving that Miss Ashworth, the gentleman’s sister, who had been of the party, and who had been seated near enough to hear Mrs. Vernon’s communication, wore an expression of dignified reserve which seemed to declare that she, at least, was not prepared to receive the intelligence as established fact. This was some comfort. Miss Ashworth was a woman of sense, and her silence spoke more than many words.

The gentlemen appeared. Catherine’s eyes sought Mr. Ashworth’s with an anxiety she could not overcome, and she thought she perceived in his glance something of inquiry, something of concern — but he did not approach her. He seated himself at some distance, and entered into conversation with Colonel Maxwell, with an air of studied composure that Catherine knew not how to interpret.

Mrs. Vernon, mean while, was enjoying the full fruition of her achievement. She moved among her acquaintance with an elasticity of spirits that was quite her own, received congratulations with a modesty that was evidently assumed, and distributed hints and intimations with a liberality that Catherine foresaw would render the retraction, whenever it should come, a matter of infinite difficulty. She had even the audacity to whisper to Mrs. Fortescue that she hoped the business would be settled before the end of the month.
