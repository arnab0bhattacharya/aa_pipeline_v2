The wind off the Cheshire flats had a peculiar, chilling quality in late November; it did not merely blow, but seemed to scrape against the very stones of Ashgrove Hall, a low, mournful keen that found its way through the chinks of the window frames and whispered in my ear. I sat in the library, a small, dark room dominated by a dying fire and the heavy shadows of oak bookcases, trying to make myself invisible. It was a difficult task for a governess, for we are creatures of utility, visible only when required, and yet we are always present, like a stain on the glass that cannot be wiped away.

I had just placed the last of Miss Elspeth’s music books back onto their shelf when the door opened. It was not the footman, nor Mrs. Grier, the housekeeper, but Elspeth herself. She came in not with the usual demure step that her father, Mr. Thorne, so admired, but with a haste that bordered on desperation. Her face, usually so pale and composed, was flushed, and her eyes, a deep, troubled grey, held a light that I had not seen in them since we had first arrived.

“Governess,” she said, her voice low, yet carrying an intensity that silenced the crackle of the embers. “Close the door.”

I obeyed, my hands trembling slightly. I had learned, in my three months at Ashgrove, that Elspeth was a girl of contradictions; outwardly, she was the perfect picture of submission, the daughter who bowed her head when her father spoke and accepted his judgments as one accepts the verdict of a judge. But inwardly, I sensed a fire, a burning, restless spirit that chafed against the constraints of her existence.

She did not sit. She stood by the fireplace, staring into the ashes, her hands clasped tightly in front of her.

“He has come,” she said abruptly. “Lord Ferris.”

I felt a cold knot form in my stomach. Lord Ferris was a man of sixty, a widower with a fortune that would purchase three Ashgroves, and a reputation for being a tyrant in his private life, if not a gentleman in his public one. He had been a visitor for a week, and I had watched the subtle, terrifying shift in the household dynamics. The servants moved more quickly; Mr. Thorne’s eyes, usually dull with avarice, sparkled with a greed that was almost physical.

“I know,” I replied, keeping my voice steady. “He is in the dining room with your father.”

Elspeth turned to me then. The movement was sharp, violent. “Do you know what he intends? Do you know what he has said?”

“I have not heard his words, Miss Elspeth. I am but a tutor. I have no right to eavesdrop, nor to intervene in matters of family alliance.”

“Family alliance!” The words were a hiss, a venomous spittle. “It is not an alliance; it is a sale. A transaction. Like a horse, or a spaniel, to be traded for the best price.” Her voice rose, and for a moment I feared she would be overheard, but the wind outside grew louder, masking her outcry. “He speaks of a ‘marriage of convenience.’ He speaks of my ‘duty.’ He speaks of my father’s debts, which he knows, which he has likely caused, and he speaks of it as if I am a debtor myself.”

I looked at her, this beautiful, trapped bird. I thought of the scripture I had read that morning, *‘The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?’* I had applied it to myself, to my own secret hopes of a better life, but here, it applied to the man who held her future in his cold, grasping hands.

“What do you wish me to do?” I asked, though I knew the answer. I had no power. I had no standing. I was a woman of no consequence, hired for a salary, expected to teach French and geography, to suppress my own personality and reflect the propriety of the household. To speak against Lord Ferris would be to speak against the provider of my livelihood. It would be to destroy myself.

But I looked into her eyes, and I saw a mirror of my own past struggles, my own fierce, unyielding will that had been ground down by poverty and prejudice. I saw the injustice of it, the sheer, brutal unfairness of a strong man using his wealth to crush the will of a young woman who had done nothing to deserve such a fate.

“Nothing,” I said, and the word tasted like ash. “I can do nothing, Miss Elspeth. I am bound by my contract. I am bound by silence. If I speak, I am dismissed. If I am dismissed, I am destitute. And you... you remain here, under his roof, until the deed is done.”

Elspeth laughed, a sound devoid of joy, brittle as ice. “You are right. You are always right. You are the voice of reason, the voice of the world that tells us to be quiet, to be small, to accept our lot.” She stepped closer, and I felt the heat of her anger radiating from her. “But I will not accept it. I will not be a silent thing. I will not let him put his ring on my finger as if I were a piece of furniture.”

“Then what will you do?” I whispered, my heart pounding against my ribs.

“I do not know,” she said. “But I will not let him have me. I will find a way. I have a brother, in India. He is not here, but he is alive. And I have a spirit that he cannot buy. He thinks he is buying a wife; he is buying a ghost. I will haunt him. I will be a thorn in his side, a stone in his shoe, until he lets me go.”
