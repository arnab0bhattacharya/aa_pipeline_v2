It is a curious thing, this deliberate forgetfulness of one’s origin that afflicts the scholarship boy when he is suddenly plunged into the deep end of higher education. I had come up from a cramped, yellow-varnished villa in Tooting, armed with a County Council Exhibition and a smattering of Euclid and chemistry that would, my father calculated, shortly transform me into a civil engineer. My father was a man of restless, mechanical ingenuity, the proprietor of a small but perpetually threatened manufactory of patent door-hinges and gas-fittings in Battersea. He smelled indefatigably of machine oil, shrewd economies, and a desperate, baffled ambition. And I, being young and acutely sensitive to the cut of a jacket, was heartily ashamed of him.

I was ashamed of my origins, and then, turning the matter over in my solitary hours, I was consumed by a burning shame of that very shame. It seemed to me a particularly cowardly form of treason. I was at the South Kensington Technical College, surrounded by the sons of the professional classes, and I was desperately anxious to be taken for one of them.

The college was a singular microcosm of the late Victorian mind. We collected there in the great, echoing, asphalted quadrangles, a remarkable mixture of types, drawn together by the pragmatic, insistent force of Science. There were the medical students, boisterous and cynical, who seemed to regard the dissecting rooms as a particularly grim sort of gentlemen's club. There were the sleek, well-tailored sons of the suburbs, training to be electrical engineers, who talked of alternating currents and dynamos with a breezy, middle-class confidence. And there were the aristocrats, idle and elegant, drifting through the metallurgical laboratories because it seemed a slightly more virile alternative to reading for the Bar, who treated the whole curriculum with an aristocratic lightness.

Among my particular set was Merivale, a lean, languid young man who had been to Winchester, and who possessed the extraordinary facility of making the most ordinary utterance sound like a polished epigram. There was also Cullingworth, a noisy, enthusiastic, red-faced youth, the son of a Yorkshire iron-master, who mixed a fluent, vague Socialism with his thermodynamics, and argued with a boisterous cheerfulness that made up in volume what it lacked in precision. I attached myself to Merivale. I copied his colloquialisms, his easy, slightly supercilious manner with the lecturers, and I attempted, with a pathetic assiduity, to acquire his peculiar slouch.

It was Cullingworth who first introduced the topic of family into our grimy, gas-lit preparation room. We were waiting for the senior physics demonstration, a period of enforced idleness, and Cullingworth was holding forth upon the stupidity of the aristocracy.

"Your dad's a professional man, isn't he, Merivale?" Cullingworth bawled, tipping back his chair. "Lawyer or something? And mine's a maker of iron. We're the coming men, you know. Builders. What's your governor, Byass?"

My name is Byass—Arthur Byass. I flushed to the roots of my hair. "Oh, he's in business," I said, with a studied vagueness that was meant to imply vast, city-wide operations. "Commercial interests."

Which was true enough, if one stretched the definition of commerce to include a smoky little workshop where three men and a boy turned out brass fittings by the gross.

The matter might have rested there, a small, squalid lie among many, had my father not taken it into his head to visit me at the college. He was, I think, rather proud of the fact that his son was at South Kensington. He had written to me about it, in a round, laborious hand, on lined paper stamped with the name of his firm. I had not invited him, but he came.

It was on a Tuesday afternoon, in the middle of a practical chemistry class, that I saw him. We were clustered around a long, stained mahogany table, titrating solutions with delicate glass burettes, under the sharp, impatient eye of Professor Lewisham. I happened to glance up towards the high, draughty doorway, and there he stood.

He was palpably out of place. He wore his best, most Sundayest suit of black broadcloth, shiny at the elbows and tightly buttoned across his sturdy chest. His bowler hat was pushed back from his forehead, revealing a strip of red, perspiring skin, and he carried a bulging, oil-cloth bag in one hand. He looked exactly like what he was: a small manufacturer from Battersea, acutely uncomfortable in the vast, academic quiet of the place, searching for his son.

The immediate terror that seized me was, I suppose, the terror of exposure. If I went to him, if I acknowledged him, the whole elaborate, precarious edifice of my gentility would come crashing down. Merivale was standing not three feet away, carefully measuring out a pale blue fluid. If he saw me greet this shiny, oil-stained man, the illusion would be shattered. I should be Arthur Byass of Tooting, the hinge-maker's son, forever and irrevocably.

For one awful, suspended moment, I pretended not to see him. I turned back to my burette, my heart hammering against my ribs, my hands trembling so violently that the pink liquid splashed unevenly into the flask. But even as I did it, the full, sickening meanness of the action rushed upon me. I felt a hot, suffocating flush of self-disgust. What was I, that I should deny the man who had denied himself to send me here?

I looked up again. My father had not moved. He was gazing with a kind of bewildered respect at the array of retorts and Bunsen burners, his lips slightly parted. He looked pathetically small and forlorn.

"Byass!" Lewisham's voice cut across the room, sharp and irritated. "Your solution is over-titrated. You've ruined the experiment. What are you looking at?"

I set down the burette with a clumsy clatter. "I beg your pardon, sir," I said, and my voice sounded unnaturally loud in the sudden quiet of the laboratory. "My father has arrived."
