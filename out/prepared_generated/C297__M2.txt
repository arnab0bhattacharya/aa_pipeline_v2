The rain in Wessex did not fall so much as it hovered, a persistent, grey veil that turned the cobblestones of the Cathedral Close into dark, slick mirrors. Inside the Bishop’s study, the air was thick with the scent of beeswax, old paper, and the faint, medicinal odor of camphor that clung to Bishop Aldous’s skin. Elara stood by the window, her back to the room, watching the larch trees bend under the weight of the mist. She was twenty-six, a age that in the parish of St. Jude’s was considered a delicate emergency, a state of being that required immediate correction through marriage or, failing that, a retreat into the convent. But Elara had no inclination for either. She had her father’s ledgers to balance, his correspondence to filter, and his physical frailty to manage. She was the anchor that kept the ship of the household from drifting into the chaotic sea of his declining faculties.

The door opened without a knock, a breach of protocol that Elara noted with a cold, sharp precision. In walked Arthur Penhallow, the new curate. He was young, barely twenty, with ink-stained fingers and a habit of looking at the floor as if it held the answers to questions he had not yet thought to ask. He carried a leather-bound volume, his eyes still adjusted to the dimness of the corridor.

"Forgive the intrusion, Miss Aldous," he said. His voice was quiet, lacking the practiced baritone of his senior colleagues. "Your father is dozing. I was to read the homily for Sunday, but I find I cannot settle the tone while the light is so low. Might I sit?"

Elara turned. She did not invite him to sit, but she did not forbid it. She watched as he lowered himself into the high-backed chair opposite the desk, the one usually reserved for dignitaries who never actually came to call. He opened the book, but he did not begin to read. Instead, he looked at her, a strange, challenging glint in his dark eyes.

"You seem distracted, Miss Aldous. Or perhaps I am simply boring him," he said, gesturing vaguely toward the Bishop, who lay on the settee in the corner, his breathing a rhythmic, rattling thing.

"I am not distracting anyone, Mr. Penhallow. I am ensuring the tea does not go cold, and that the gas lamp does not soot the ceiling. It is a necessary task."

Arthur closed the book. "You manage the Bishop’s life with an efficiency that borders on tyranny. He is a man of God, not a prisoner. You keep him in a cage of routine."

The air in the room seemed to tighten. For years, Elara had been the invisible servant, the silent hand that smoothed the wrinkles in the bedclothes, the voice that spoke only when spoken to. Men like the Canon, or the Vicar of the neighboring town, spoke to her as one speaks to a piece of furniture—acknowledging her presence but ignoring her substance. They spoke *around* her, never *to* her. But Arthur Penhallow spoke *at* her. He treated her opinions as tangible objects that could be weighed, found wanting, and discarded. And for the first time in her life, she felt the heat of indignation rise in her chest, not as a flammable threat, but as a clarifying fire.

"Tyranny," Elara repeated, the word tasting strange in her mouth. "You are new to the Close, Mr. Penhallow. You have not seen the nights when the delirium takes him. You have not held the hand that shakes so violently it cannot hold a spoon. You have not read the letters from the parishioners who beg for miracles while their children starve. Routine is not a cage. It is the only structure that keeps the chaos at bay."

Arthur leaned forward, his elbows on his knees. "And if the structure is wrong? If the routine is a lie we tell ourselves to avoid the truth of his decay? If you are not caring for him, but merely preserving the image of him?"

Elara felt a prickle of fear, followed immediately by a surge of defiance. She walked to the desk, her heels clicking sharply on the stone floor, and picked up a letter that lay on the blotter. It was from Mrs. Gable, the head of the charity fund, complaining about the cost of the new roof tiles.

"Truth is expensive, Mr. Penhallow," she said, her voice steady, though her hands trembled slightly. "And it is often ugly. But it is better than the comfortable fiction that everything is as it should be. I keep the house in order because if I do not, it will fall. And if it falls, your 'truth' will have nowhere to stand."

Arthur looked at the letter, then back at her. He did not smile. He did not dismiss her. He looked at her with a intensity that made her feel as if he were reading the text of her soul. "Then you are the keeper of the keys," he said softly. "Not just of the house, but of the narrative. You decide what is remembered and what is forgotten. Is that not a form of power?"

"I am a daughter," Elara said, the words coming out harder than she intended. "And a daughter’s duty is to protect. If that looks like tyranny to you, then you are a very new judge."

The silence that followed was heavy, but it was different from the usual silence of the study. It was not the silence of dismissal, but of contemplation. Arthur Penhallow was not an enemy. He was not a suitor, either, in the way the local widowers had been—clumsy and predictable. He was a mirror. He was looking at her and seeing a woman, not a role. He was challenging her not to break her, but to see if she could stand on her own feet.
