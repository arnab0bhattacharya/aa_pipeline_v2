The letter came at dusk, when the gas-lamps were already beginning to blur the wet stones of the Rue des Écoles and the girls' voices, reciting their evening psalms in the chapel below, rose and fell like a tide against the walls of my room. I had been correcting exercises—French verbs rendered in a stiff English hand, the same errors repeated fifty times—and I set down my pen when the porter knocked, because the knock was different from the maid's: heavier, more reluctant, as if the man already knew what he bore. It was a thin letter, franked at Liverpool, and the handwriting on the outside was my aunt's, upright and unrelenting, the letters standing to attention like soldiers who will not be excused duty. I knew before I broke the seal. I knew, as one knows in a dream that the house is on fire, though no smoke has yet appeared. The words were few. *Edward died on the ninth, of the fever. He suffered little. He is at peace.* Then a line about the funeral, and a line about the will, and a closing sentence of such ordinary Christian comfort that it struck me like a blow: *The Lord gives, and the Lord takes away.*

I sat still a long while. Outside, the rain fell steadily on the city of which I knew nothing but its schools and its streets and the faces of my pupils, and inside me something vast and dark opened, like the mouth of a pit. Edward—my only brother, my only near relation in the world since our parents died—Edward, who had written to me every month in his careless, cheerful hand, who meant to come out to me at Christmas, who was twenty-four years old and full of plans and laughter and that particular kind of courage which belongs to the young and the good. I could not weep. The grief was too large for tears; it pressed upon my chest like a stone, and I breathed against it, and did not weep.

I thought of his last letter, received only three weeks ago, in which he described the new posting he had obtained, and the horse he meant to buy, and the girl he had danced with at the assembly rooms. *You would like her, Anne,* he had written. *She has spirit.* Now the horse would never be bought, and the girl would dance with another, and the spirit that had written those words was gone out of the world like a candle pinched between finger and thumb. Where was it gone? I asked the question with the whole force of my being, and the rain answered nothing, and the gas-lamp outside my window flickered and steadied, and the psalms below rose again: *Though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil.*

I folded the letter and put it in the drawer of my writing-desk. Then I took out the timetable for the morrow. Seven o'clock: Scripture with the third form. Eight o'clock: English grammar with the fifth. Nine o'clock: History of England with the sixth, the oldest girls, who were sharp and observant and would notice if anything were amiss. I looked at these entries as a soldier might look at orders received on the eve of a battle he has no heart to fight, and I felt a kind of anger rise in me—not at the dead, not at God, but at the necessity, the sheer grinding necessity, of composure. Who was there in this house to whom I could speak? Madame Verlot, the directress, was a woman of excellent management and no imagination; she would have offered me a day's rest and a glass of water, and thought the matter ended. The other teachers—Miss Pratt, who taught music and talked of nothing but her pupils' progress; Mrs. Hare, who was kind in a timid, fluttering way and would have been frightened by real sorrow; young Mademoiselle Duval, who was barely older than her scholars and would have wept from sympathy and made everything worse. No. There was no one. I was alone in a foreign city, among people who knew my face and my functions but not my heart, and my heart was broken.

I rose and went to the window and looked out at the rain. The city lay below me in its wet, gray beauty, the spires and roofs glistening, the river somewhere beyond the houses moving dark and silent toward the sea. It was a city of strangers, and I was a stranger in it, and my brother was dead. I said the words aloud, to hear how they sounded: *My brother is dead.* They sounded flat and false, like a sentence in a language I had learned but did not speak. I turned from the window and began to prepare for the morrow, laying out my books and my dress with the same mechanical care with which one might prepare a body for burial. The black dress. The black ribbon for my hair. The Bible, with Edward's name written inside the cover in his own boyish hand, the ink already faded. I touched the letters with my finger, and at last the tears came—not gently, not like rain, but like blood, sudden and hot and shameful, and I pressed my hand over my mouth so that the sound should not escape into the corridor where the maids were still moving about their work.

But grief, even the wildest, must have its limits, or it will devour the living as well as the dead. I wept until I could weep no more, and then I washed my face in the cold water of the basin, and said my prayers—what prayers I could find, for the accustomed words seemed mockery—and lay down on my bed without undressing, and waited for the morning.
