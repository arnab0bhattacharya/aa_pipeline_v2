It was upon a raw and gusty evening in the November of '92 that I found myself once more in the company of Mr. Sherlock Holmes. The wind had driven a fine, cold rain against the windows of our Baker Street rooms all day, and the gas lamps had flickered in their brackets as if in sympathy with the dreary weather. Holmes had been in a state of restless boredom, a condition which I had long since learned was the precursor to some new and absorbing problem. That problem, when it arrived, took the form of a card upon the salver of our landlady, Mrs. Hudson.

“Mr. Alistair Finch-Hatton, of Grosvenor Square,” Holmes read aloud, his lean fingers steepled beneath his chin. “The name is not entirely unknown to me, Watson. A man of considerable fortune and, if the society pages are to be believed, an even more considerable ego. Show him up, if you please.”

Our visitor was a portly gentleman of some sixty years, whose face, though ruddy with health, was presently clouded with an anxiety that seemed quite foreign to it. He was dressed in the height of fashion, from his silk top hat to the gloss of his patent leather boots, but he carried himself with the distracted air of a man who has misplaced his own soul.

“Mr. Holmes,” he began, once he was settled in the armchair opposite my friend, “I am come to you in a matter of the utmost gravity. I have been robbed, sir, and by a phantom.”

Holmes’s eyes gleamed. “A phantom is a most challenging adversary. Pray, be precise.”

“Precise? It is the precision of the thing that defies all reason!” Finch-Hatton exclaimed, dabbing at his brow with a silk handkerchief. “The object in question is ‘The Sorrow of Ariadne,’ a masterpiece by the Venetian master, Giorgione, which has been the jewel of my private gallery for twenty years. This morning, I went to my gallery for my customary hour of contemplation. I stood before it, and a chill, a sense of profound wrongness, stole over me. The colours were a shade too brilliant. The varnish, a fraction too new. The very soul of the piece was gone. It is a copy, Mr. Holmes. A copy of diabolical perfection, but a copy nonetheless. My Ariadne has been stolen.”

“And the security of this gallery?” Holmes inquired.

“That is what makes the matter so impossible. The room has but one door, of solid oak, which is kept locked. For the past month, my man Davies has stood guard in the corridor outside, day and night, in eight-hour shifts. The windows are barred. No one has entered or left that room without his knowledge.”

“And yet the substitution was made.”

“Just so. It is a locked room, Mr. Holmes, in the most literal sense. The thief has walked through a wall.”

Holmes leaned forward, his languor entirely gone. “Let us hear of the past month. Every detail, however trivial it may seem.”

“There is little to tell. My life is a quiet one. The only persons to have set foot in the gallery in that time, besides myself and my nephew, were two workmen. The first was a picture restorer, a man named Hargreaves, who spent a day cleaning a minor landscape. The second was a chimney sweep, a grimy fellow from the East End, who attended to the flue. And then, of course, there is my nephew, Julian. A good boy, but with an artist’s temperament and a gambler’s purse. He is in and out constantly.”

Holmes steepled his fingers again. “The sweep, the restorer, and the nephew. A most intriguing trio. And the guards? You are certain of their integrity?”

“Davies is an old soldier, utterly reliable. His relief, a younger man named Carter, is sharp and sober. They have both been with me for years. I would trust them with my life.”

“Then we shall trust them with our investigation,” said Holmes, rising. “Watson, your coat. The game, as they say, is afoot.”

A short cab ride brought us to the imposing edifice of Finch-Hatton’s mansion. The gallery was a long, high-ceilinged room, panelled in dark oak and lit by a skylight, now grey with the evening’s rain. The air was heavy with the scent of beeswax and old canvas. On the far wall, in a place of honour, hung the painting.

It was a fine work, I had to admit. A woman of classical beauty sat upon a rock, her face a mask of despair, the deep blue of the sea behind her. Yet, as I looked, I felt the same sense of dislocation our client had described. It was technically flawless, but it lacked… something. A certain truth.

Holmes, however, paid the canvas little heed. His eyes were everywhere else. He paced the length of the room, his gaze sweeping over the floor, the walls, the ceiling. He ran a long, inquisitive finger along the top of a heavy gilt frame, then examined the tip with intense concentration. He lay flat upon the polished floor, his face inches from the parquet, and peered under a large escritoire. He stood on a chair to inspect the mechanism of the skylight.

“You see the dust, Watson?” he said, not looking at me. “A fine, even layer upon the mantelpiece. But here, upon the floor behind this settee, it is disturbed. And observe this.” He pointed to a single, minute fleck of something dark upon the pale wood of the floorboards. “Soot. Not the common London soot, which is a greasy black. This is a drier, flakier substance. The product of a wood fire, perhaps, or something more specific.”

He moved to the fireplace, a grand affair of carved marble. He knelt and peered up into the black throat of the chimney. “The sweep did his work well. Too well. The flue is immaculate. Yet a man of his trade, climbing down into the soot and grime, would surely leave more than a single trace upon the floor of a pristine gallery. He would leave a smudge upon the hearth, a mark upon the rug. There is nothing.”
