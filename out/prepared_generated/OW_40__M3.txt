Lord Gerald Selsey had three remarkable gifts: an absolute mastery of the pianoforte, a perfectly tailored coat, and a profound aversion to romantic sentiment. He was twenty-four, exceedingly handsome, and entirely heartless. Society, which is always eager to forgive a man his virtues if he possesses sufficient charm, forgave him his lack of emotion. Indeed, the women of London adored him precisely because he was unattainable, mistaking his frozen detachment for a tragic depth of feeling. Lord Gerald, for his part, knew that they were mistaken, and the knowledge gave him a quiet, cynical amusement.

It was in the green drawing-room of Lady Bracknell’s house that the wager was made. Lady Bracknell was a woman of impeccable reputation, which is to say, she had never been caught doing anything interesting. Her evenings were a triumph of social management; she collected geniuses and aristocrats with the same indiscriminate zeal that others devoted to porcelain.

Lord Gerald was standing by a bank of crimson azaleas, looking with exquisite boredom at a small group of men who were debating the nature of love. Among them was the young Marquis of Dumbarton, a romantic of the most dangerous sort, because he believed in the purity of the feminine ideal.

‘Love is a divine mystery,’ the Marquis was saying, his pale, earnest face flushed with the heat of the drawing-room. ‘It transforms a man. It is the one thing that cannot be bought, or feigned, or won by strategy.’

Lord Gerald crushed a fallen azalea leaf beneath his ivory cane. ‘On the contrary,’ he said, in his low, musical voice, ‘love is merely a matter of strategy. It is the triumph of patience over common sense. Given a month, I could make any woman in London fall desperately in love with me. The colder the woman, the easier the task. The iceberg only requires the proper application of heat to melt.’

The Marquis looked at him with the tender pity of a man who has just discovered a touching weakness in a friend. ‘You are terribly cynical, Gerald.’

‘I am merely accurate. Cynicism is the only sane response to a world that insists on taking itself seriously.’

‘Then I make you a wager,’ said the Marquis, suddenly inspired. ‘I will bet you my grey Arabians that you cannot make Mrs. Vivian fall in love with you within a month.’

A murmur went through the little group. Mrs. Vivian was a widow of twenty-six, possessed of a beauty so severe it seemed almost mathematical, and a reputation for frigidity that had survived three London seasons unscathed. She had been compared to a marble statue, though statues, as Lord Gerald later observed, are at least occasionally subjected to the warming influence of the sun.

‘Mrs. Vivian?’ Lord Gerald raised an eyebrow. ‘My dear Dumbarton, you are too generous. The Arabians are already mine. Mrs. Vivian has the emotional temperature of a glacier. She declined Lord Pemberton, and Lord Pemberton has an income of thirty thousand a year and a very respectable estate in Yorkshire. To refuse a man with thirty thousand a year is the height of romance, but to refuse him and show no signs of regret is the height of impossibility. I accept your terms. A month, you say? I shall need only three weeks.’

‘A month,’ repeated the Marquis firmly. ‘And the judgment of the woman herself shall be our arbiter. She must confess her love openly.’

‘She shall confess it in front of the entire world, if you wish,’ said Lord Gerald. ‘A public declaration is the only kind worth having. It adds the spice of embarrassment to the sweetness of surrender.’

He began his campaign the very next afternoon. Mrs. Vivian was fond of walking in the park, and Lord Gerald, who had never walked in the park before, was suddenly to be found there every day, dressed with a care that bordered on the poetic. He did not approach her at first. He merely appeared, a graceful silhouette against the grey London sky, and allowed the mystery of his presence to do its work.

When he finally spoke to her, it was of the only things worth speaking of: music, orchids, and the imperfections of the Italian Renaissance. He spoke with a gentle, almost sorrowful intensity that surprised even himself. He found that Mrs. Vivian listened with an attention that was either flattering or unnerving, depending on one’s perspective.

‘Most men talk of themselves,’ she said one afternoon, as they stood beside a bed of late tulips. ‘You talk of beautiful things. It is a refreshing form of vanity.’

‘Vanity is merely the honesty of the self-aware,’ Lord Gerald replied. ‘Only the truly modest are hypocrites, because they refuse to admit what everyone else can see.’

Mrs. Vivian looked at him with her cool, grey eyes. ‘You are very clever, Lord Gerald. I distrust cleverness. It is usually a mask for insincerity.’

‘And what is stupidity a mask for?’ he asked.

‘For sincerity, unfortunately. The stupid are always sincere. It is their tragedy, and their only charm.’

He laughed, a low, delightful laugh that seemed to hang in the autumn air. He was beginning to enjoy the game. Mrs. Vivian was a worthy adversary. She was not easily moved by the common artillery of flirtation. She required a finer siege.

In the second week, he sent her a single black orchid, accompanied by a copy of sonnets by a forgotten Italian poet. He did not write a card. The flower and the book were left at her door by a discreet footman. That evening, at the Opera, she wore the orchid in her hair. It stood out against her dark curls like a secret whispered in a crowded room.

Lord Gerald saw it, and smiled. The iceberg was melting.

He pursued his campaign with the patience of a diplomat and the artistry of a painter. He was always present but never pressing. He said outrageous things with perfect composure, and she responded with a wit that matched his own. They spoke of the absurdity of society, the hypocrisy of morality, and the beauty of useless things.
