The gaslights in the corridor of the Bellhaven Institute for Working Men hissed a steady, comforting rhythm, a sound that had become the overture to Edith’s evenings. She would arrive as the city’s clerks and draymen were washing the grime from their hands, their heavy boots clumping on the worn stone stairs. Her classroom was small, smelling of chalk dust, coal smoke, and the faint, sharp tang of the men’s trades. She taught them English Literature, a grand title for an hour of stumbling through Tennyson and discussing the motives of a Shakespearean king with men whose hands were more accustomed to a ledger or a lathe.

For three terms, Thomas Avery had sat in the third row, his broad shoulders slightly hunched over the small desk. He was a printer, a fact made obvious by the permanent faint shadow of ink at the base of his thumb and the clean, almost medicinal smell of turpentine that clung to his coat. He was not the most vocal student, nor the quickest to grasp a metaphor, but his attention was absolute. When she spoke of Keats’s melancholy, his gaze would fix on her face with a quiet, unnerving intensity, as if he were trying to set her words in type, letter by letter.

She noticed him, of course. It was impossible not to. He was a solid, deliberate man with kind eyes and a gentle, rumbling laugh that he rarely used in class. Edith, at thirty-two, had long accepted the spinster’s life of tea, marking, and a small, tidy flat in Kentish Town. Her world was one of quiet routine, and she had convinced herself it was enough. But Thomas Avery began to disrupt that quiet. She found herself looking for the ink stain on his thumb, anticipating the particular way he would run a hand through his thick, dark hair when he was thinking. She would craft her lessons with him in mind, choosing passages she thought might resonate with a man who worked with words all day, not as a scholar, but as a craftsman.

One Tuesday, as they discussed *The Winter’s Tale*, she spoke of Leontes’s sudden, irrational jealousy. “It is a madness,” she said, pacing before the blackboard. “A fever of the mind that invents its own truth.”

Later, as the men filed out, their boots echoing in the stairwell, Thomas lingered. He stood by her desk, twisting his cap in his hands. “Beg pardon, Miss Croft,” he said, his voice low. “That jealousy. It doesn’t seem like a fever. More like… a stone in the boot. You can’t see it, but it changes the whole way you walk.”

His words struck her with a force that was almost physical. It was a perfect, simple, and utterly profound observation. She had smiled, a genuine, unguarded smile that felt foreign on her face. “Yes, Mr. Avery. That is exactly what it is.”

From that evening, a silent, unspoken bond seemed to form between them. It was in the way he would nod at her when he entered, a private greeting before the others arrived. It was in the small, thoughtful questions he would ask at the end of a lesson, questions that revealed a mind grappling with the material long after the others had gone home to their suppers. Edith knew he had a wife and children; she had heard him speak of a boy who was clever with his hands and a little girl who was always singing. This knowledge was a cold, hard fact she held close, a barrier she erected every morning and dismantled every night as she lay in the dark, listening to the distant clatter of a hansom cab. She told no one. There was no one to tell. To speak of it would be to give it a life it could not, must not, have. So she continued to teach him, and she loved him in the silent, disciplined way of a woman who has learned to find her joy in small, stolen things.

The last class of the winter term arrived. The air was raw with a coming frost, and the men were restless, eager for the Christmas break. She had given them a final, simple exercise: to write a single paragraph on what poetry meant to them. Most had written a few halting, dutiful sentences. Thomas had written nothing, but as the others gathered their coats, he approached her desk once more. He held a small, slim volume, bound in a deep blue cloth. He placed it on the desk between them, his ink-stained fingers resting on the cover for a moment.

“For you, Miss Croft,” he said, his voice quieter than usual. “A bit of a Christmas present. I printed it myself.”

She looked down at the book. There was no title on the cover, no author’s name. It was a private thing, a creation of his own hands. Her heart gave a strange, painful lurch. “Mr. Avery, you shouldn’t have.”

“It’s nothing,” he said, not meeting her eyes. “Just a thing I did. For the class. To say thank you.” He gave a short, awkward nod, turned, and was gone, his heavy footsteps descending the stairs for the final time that year.

Edith stood alone in the emptying room, the gaslight humming its low, constant song. She picked up the book. It was beautifully made, the stitching on the spine perfect, the pages cut clean and smooth. She opened it. The paper was of a fine, heavy stock, the kind he must have chosen himself. And there, on the first page, was a single poem, set in a clear, elegant typeface.

It was not a poem she recognised. It was about a lamp in a window, burning late into the night. It spoke of the quiet order of a classroom, the scent of chalk, and the way a voice could make the dusty past feel as immediate as the present. It was a poem about a light in the darkness, a beacon for weary travellers. And at the end, in a line that made her breath catch in her throat, it described the light not as a guide, but as something precious in itself, a small, contained universe of warmth and reason.
