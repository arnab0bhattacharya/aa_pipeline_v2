Lord Arthur Saville had spent his entire life being informed that he was the very image of his late father. The late Lord Saville, a man of impeccable breeding and entirely forgettable features, had perished in a hunting accident when Arthur was barely three. Society, which is always eager to attribute a striking countenance to a respectable lineage, had unanimously agreed that the boy was his father’s shadow. As Arthur grew into a young man possessed of dark, restless eyes, a jaw cut from pale marble, and a certain languid grace, the comparisons only deepened. He bore the name, he wore the coronet, and he was universally acknowledged as the living portrait of a man who, had he lived, would doubtless have been very glad to meet him.

It was Lady Bracknell, however, who first planted the thorn of curiosity in the fruit of Arthur’s contentment. Lady Bracknell, who remembered everything that had never occurred and forgot only the things that were inconvenient, had been surveying him through her lorgnette across the expanse of a crowded drawing-room.

"My dear boy," she had said, with that air of polished menace she reserved for the socially vulnerable, "you are wonderfully like your father. I saw him only last week, dining at the Epsom Club. He looked remarkably well for a man who has been in his grave for twenty years."

Arthur had laughed politely, supposing it to be one of her celebrated paradoxes, but her gaze held a peculiar, heavy sort of mirth. When he returned to Saville House that evening, he sought out his mother, the Dowager Lady Saville, in her boudoir. She was a woman of majestic beauty, who dressed always in tragic shades of velvet and treated her past as one treats a butler found stealing the silver—with a quiet, devastating finality.

"Mother," Arthur ventured, settling himself on a brocade footstool, "I met Lady Bracknell today. She made a rather odd remark. She said I was the image of my father, whom she saw dining at the Epsom Club last week."

Lady Saville did not look up from the lilies she was arranging in a porcelain vase. "Lady Bracknell is growing short-sighted," she murmured. "And the Epsom Club serves a very dubious consommé. I should pay no attention to anything she says, or eats."

"But she seemed so certain," Arthur pressed. "And looking in the glass this morning, I thought I saw a resemblance to the portrait in the gallery. Only, the more I looked, the less I saw of the late Lord Saville in my face."

Lady Saville’s hand trembled, ever so slightly, dropping a single white petal onto the polished floor. She turned to him, her complexion as flawless and immovable as alabaster.

"You have been looking too closely, Arthur," she said, in a voice that could have frozen a fountain in July. "The glass merely reflects what society expects to see. A son is merely an illusion of heredity, and heredity is the excuse we give to the middle classes for our eccentricities. Your father was a man of unimpeachable dullness. You, I am glad to say, are merely dull. Pray, do not attempt to be mysterious. It is a pursuit strictly reserved for those who cannot afford to be scandalous."

She dismissed him with a wave of her scented handkerchief, but Arthur was not to be deterred. The thirst for truth, which is so much more inconvenient than the thirst for champagne, had taken hold of him. He determined to uncover the identity of the man at the Epsom Club, a quest that led him, naturally, to the one man in London who knew everyone’s secrets and was willing to sell them at a reasonable price: Mr. Hubert Carstairs.

Mr. Carstairs occupied a suite of rooms in Half Moon Street, decorated entirely in yellow silk and bad taste. He was a man who had squandered three fortunes on art and the remainder on conversation, and was now forced to earn his living by dispensing both.

"My dear Arthur," Carstairs drawled, offering him a cigarette from a gold enameled case, "to discover the truth about one’s parentage in London is to attempt to find a moral purpose in a French novel. It is a tedious business, entirely devoid of style, and almost certain to end in a paragraph you would rather not read."

"I must know," said Arthur gravely.

"Must is a word one should never use except when ordering one’s tailor," Carstairs rejoined. "However, since you insist upon this tragic farce, I suggest you attend Lady Melrose’s ball tomorrow evening. I have it on unimpeachable authority that the gentleman Lady Bracknell mistook for a ghost will be present. His name is Mr. Julian Fane. He is an art collector of some repute, a man of independent means and dependent morals. But I must warn you: the truth is rarely pure and never simple. It is the one thing in the world that is invariably worse than fiction."

The ball at Melrose House was a shimmering mosaic of silk, scandal, and stringency. Orchids drooped in vast pyramids of silver, their heavy, sensuous perfume mingling with the scent of crushed velvet and expensive envy. Arthur moved through the throng with a restless, searching gaze, until he saw him, standing by the conservatory doors.

Mr. Julian Fane was a man of perhaps fifty, with hair silvered at the temples and a face of striking, melancholy beauty. He held a glass of champagne with the casual indifference of a man who has long since found the world unworthy of his serious attention. As Arthur drew near, the older man turned, and a sudden, startled recognition flashed in his dark eyes.

For a long moment, they merely looked at one another. The resemblance was not merely striking; it was an undisguised declaration. The same jawline, the same heavy lids, the same proud, weary carriage of the head.

"I see," said Fane, with a faint, bitter smile, "that the mirrors of London have finally caught up with me."
