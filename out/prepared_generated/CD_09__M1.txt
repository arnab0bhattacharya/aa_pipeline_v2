In the market town of Chilston, in the county of Kent, where the High Street was a variegated ribbon of trades and the smell of hops from the nearby oast-houses was a permanent tenant in the nostrils, the business of Mr. Elias Grimstone was conducted with a solemnity that bordered on the religious. Mr. Grimstone, a man whose face seemed to have been carved from the same sombre oak as his finest coffins, was the town’s sole purveyor of last resting-places. His shop, with its perpetual twilight within and its black crepe swags in the window, was a place of hushed footsteps and measured words. It was here that young Tom Penhaligon, aged seventeen and possessed of a heart that beat with the frantic rhythm of a stage-drummer’s sticks, was bound by articles of apprenticeship to the gentle art of the undertaker.

Tom was a lanky, awkward creature, with eyes that held a permanent gleam of distant footlights, and hands that, though deft enough with a plane and a chisel, often seemed to long for the flourish of a plumed hat or the sweeping gesture of a tragic hero. His soul, in short, was not in the coffin; it was in the play. He would pore over the tattered playbills that found their way to the town, whispering the lines of kings and villains, of witty serving-maits and lovelorn ladies, to the silent, satin-lined witnesses of his master’s trade. He saw in the grain of the wood not the finality of death, but the rich tapestry of a stage’s painted backdrop.

It was on a blustery October afternoon that the whirlwind of fate, in the form of Mr. Silas P. Pemberton’s Magnificent and Peripatetic Thespian Company, descended upon Chilston. The company’s arrival was an event of no small magnitude. A gaudy, mud-spattered caravan, drawn by a weary-looking horse and bearing the painted likenesses of actors in various states of feigned ecstasy and despair, lumbered into the yard of the Plough and Harrow inn. From this vehicle emerged a troupe of wonderfully rumpled individuals, led by Mr. Pemberton himself—a gentleman of magnificent girth and even more magnificent voice, who wore a cloak that had once been velvet and a hat that had once been fashionable.

Mr. Pemberton’s eye, ever searching for new talent—or, more accurately, for cheap and willing labour—fell upon Tom, who had abandoned his task of polishing a set of brass handles to stare, open-mouthed, at the glorious procession. The manager, with the instinct of a showman, saw in the boy’s yearning face not a coffin-maker’s apprentice, but a potential spear-carrier, a third murderer, a Roman citizen of immense and unpaid importance.

“You, my boy!” boomed Mr. Pemberton, clapping a hand on Tom’s shoulder that nearly sent him sprawling. “There is a fire in your eye! A passion unspent! Can you declaim? Can you emote? Can you, in short, act?”

Tom, who had only ever emoted over a poorly-jointed lid, could only nod, his throat as dry as a September coffin-shavings.

“Excellent!” cried the manager. “We are short a Third Citizen for our production of ‘The Tragedy of the Crimson Duke of Mantua,’ to be performed this very night! The part is small, but vital—a fulcrum upon which the first act turns! You shall have it! A shilling, and all the applause you can carry!”

The offer was a golden key, unlocking a door to a world Tom had only ever seen in his dreams. He raced back to the workshop, his heart a triumphant drum, to break the news. He found his master, Mr. Grimstone, meticulously sanding the interior of a child’s coffin, a task he performed with the grim piety of a monk at his devotions.

“Sir,” Tom began, his voice trembling with excitement, “an opportunity has arisen! A theatrical company, a great one, from London! Mr. Pemberton himself has offered me a part! A speaking part!”

Mr. Grimstone stopped his sanding. He did not look up. The silence in the workshop grew heavy, thick, and suffocating, like the air in a sealed vault. When he finally spoke, his voice was as low and resonant as a church bell tolling for the departed.

“A part,” he repeated, the word tasting of dust and ashes. “A part in a mummer’s show. A part in a charlatan’s parade of lies and frippery. Have you forgotten, Thomas Penhaligon, the sacred nature of your calling? You are an apprentice to the art of finality, to the dignity of the last journey. You are not a jack-pudding, to caper and shout for the amusement of the ignorant. You will finish your work. You will not go.”

The decree was absolute. Tom’s protestations, his passionate speeches about the nobility of the stage, about art and expression, were met with a stony, unyielding silence. He fled to his mother’s small cottage behind the workshop, seeking solace. But there, he found only a fresh wellspring of sorrow. Mrs. Penhaligon, a woman whose life had been a long and weary struggle, wept into her apron at the thought of her son’s disgraceful folly.

“Oh, my boy!” she sobbed. “To leave a respectable trade for a life of grease-paint and empty pockets! What will the town say? What will become of you? You’ll be a vagabond, a rogue, a player!” The word ‘player’ was uttered with the same horror she might have reserved for ‘murderer’ or ‘infidel.’

His last, desperate hope lay with Miss Amelia Gable, the daughter of the baker, upon whom he had set the whole of his tender and unrequited heart. He found her in her father’s shop, her arms dusted with flour, looking as fresh and wholesome as a new-baked loaf. He poured out his story, his eyes shining with the glorious future he envisioned for them both.

“And you, Amelia,” he finished, his voice full of romantic pleading, “you will be there, won’t you? You will see me, and you will know that I was meant for something more than this town, more than these boxes for the dead!”
