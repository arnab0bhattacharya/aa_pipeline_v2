The rain in Kensington, in that particular damp and gloomy corner of the world, did not so much fall as it hovered, suspended in a melancholy mist that seemed to settle upon the shoulders of the living with a weight far heavier than the leaden coffins it accompanied. It was a drizzle of the most disconsolate description, the kind that turns the gravel of a churchyard into a quagmire and the faces of mourners into masks of sallow grief. And it was into this atmospheric soup of sorrow and mud that Mr. Barnaby Pumblechoon had been hired to stand.

Mr. Pumblechoon was not, by any stretch of the imagination, a man of natural elegance or profound intellect. He was a creature of the trade, a mute hired to provide a backdrop of silent agony for the occasion. His employer, the notoriously profane and shrewd Mr. Gristle of the firm Gristle & Sons, had selected him for his peculiar physiognomy: a face that was permanently locked in an expression of profound, unutterable woe, as if he had personally witnessed the extinction of the human race. His clothes were of a black so deep it appeared to absorb the light, and his posture was that of a man bent under the yoke of a sorrow too large for his frame. He did not speak, for he had been paid handsomely to remain silent, and to weep, or at least to look as though he were weeping, with the intensity of a broken heart.

At the funeral of the late Sir Reginald Vane, a man of such immense wealth and such notoriously tedious habits that his death had been received by the town with a relief bordering on hysteria, Mr. Pumblechoon stood near the head of the coffin, his eyes fixed upon the earth with a devotion that might have been mistaken for religious fervor. He was flanked by a weeping widow, who was actually his cousin, and a young nephew, who was actually a pickpocket named Silas. The scene was one of such theatrical grandeur, so carefully orchestrated by Mr. Gristle, that it seemed less a religious service and more a performance at the Drury Lane, only with more mud and less applause.

As the cortege processed through the streets of Kensington, the rain intensified, turning the pavement into a river of grey slush. The hired carriages, black as crows, clattered over the cobblestones, their wheels splashing the onlookers with a spray that was as cold as the grave itself. Mr. Pumblechoon, standing in the lead carriage, felt the familiar chill of the damp air seeping into his bones. He was not a man who enjoyed funerals, but he was a man who enjoyed the pay, and the pay for a man of his particular talents was quite substantial. He watched the faces of the mourners as they passed, noting the fake tears of the distant relatives, the genuine relief of the servants, and the curious glances of the public, who always seemed to find a dead man’s departure a matter of great personal interest.

It was at the house of Sir Reginald Vane, a sprawling mansion of red brick and white stone that looked down upon the street with an air of imperious disdain, that the error occurred. The house was crowded with relatives, lawyers, and creditors, all of whom were eager to know who had been left what. The reading of the will was to take place in the library, a room of such gloomy magnificence that it seemed to have been designed by a man who hated light and loved dust.

Mr. Pumblechoon, having completed his performance at the grave, was preparing to slip away to the back entrance, where a carriage awaited to take him to a warm tavern and a hot meal. But he was intercepted by Mrs. Vane, the widow, a woman of such imposing stature and such acute vision that she could spot a fly on the ceiling from a mile away. She had noticed Mr. Pumblechoon’s profound sorrow during the service, and, mistaking him for a distant cousin of the deceased whom she had not seen in years, she seized his arm with a grip that was iron.

“Mr. Pumblechoon!” she cried, her voice shrill and clear as a bell. “You must come to the library. You must hear the will. I am sure you have a claim.”

Mr. Pumblechoon’s blood ran cold. He looked at Mrs. Vane, then at the crowd of staring eyes, and then at the open door of the library, which yawned before him like a trap. He tried to speak, to explain the terrible misunderstanding, to tell her that he was merely a hired mourner, a prop in the drama of death. But the words would not come. His throat was dry, and his voice, which was already weak, seemed to have vanished entirely. He could only shake his head, a gesture that, in his current state of panic, was interpreted as a sign of dignified refusal, or perhaps, deep shock.

“No, no, don’t be so modest,” said Mrs. Vane, dragging him forward. “We must all be present. It is the law, and it is the custom.”

And so, with a heart that beat like a hammer against the walls of his chest, Mr. Pumblechoon was led into the library. The room was dimly lit, the candles casting long, dancing shadows on the walls. The lawyer, a man of such severe appearance that he looked as if he had been carved from granite, began to read the will. The words flowed out in a monotone drone, listing the debts, the charities, and the bequests to the various relatives. Mr. Pumblechoon stood in the corner, his face a mask of terror, his hands trembling at his sides. He watched as the lawyer turned to the page containing the personal effects.
