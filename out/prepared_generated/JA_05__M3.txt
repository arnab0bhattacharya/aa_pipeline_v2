Charlotte Vernon had been three weeks in Upper Brook Street, and was beginning to understand that a London season was less a succession of pleasures than a continual expenditure of resolve. Her aunt, Mrs. Talbot, had brought her from Dorset with the expressed purpose of showing her the world; and though the world, as it turned out, was rather more expensive than either of them had anticipated, Mrs. Talbot was not a woman to acknowledge a difficulty before it became an absolute necessity.

The house was elegantly appointed, if not quite magnificent, and Mrs. Talbot received her visitors with a ease that spoke of long acquaintance with the forms of fashionable life. Charlotte, who had known her aunt chiefly as a cheerful, managing sort of woman, much occupied with the garden and the poor at Lyncombe, was surprised to find her so perfectly at home among ladies who wore their French muslins as though they had been born in them. Mrs. Talbot's drawing-room was frequented by a set of young women whose names Charlotte had never heard spoken in the country, but who conversed together with the kind of familiarity that implies a shared and exclusive history. They were pretty, they were lively, and they were exceedingly well-dressed; and Charlotte, who had come to London with two new gowns and a sensible dread of being conspicuous, found herself drawn into their intimacy with a rapidity that would have flattered a vainer girl.

The Misses Aldridge—three sisters, of whom the eldest, Julia, presided over the others with a sweetness that Charlotte at first admired and afterwards wondered at—had taken a particular fancy to Mrs. Talbot's niece. They called upon her, they drove with her in the park, they included her in their parties to the exhibitions and the shops, and Charlotte, whose experience of female friendship had hitherto been confined to the vicar's daughters at home, was gratified almost beyond expression. That the Aldridges should think her worthy of their notice was a compliment to her aunt's standing as much as to her own; and Charlotte, who was not unwilling to believe herself improved by London air, received their attentions with a gratitude that was perhaps warmer than the occasion strictly required.

It was Julia who first spoke of the French gloves. They were passing Sackville Street in the Aldridge carriage, and Julia had laid her hand—gloved in a pale kid so fine as to be almost translucent—upon Charlotte's sleeve, and remarked that she must on no account return to Dorset without being properly fitted. Charlotte, whose own gloves were of a serviceable leather that had done duty all winter, felt a sudden and painful consciousness of them, and agreed with a readiness that she afterwards regretted. The purchase was made; and though the sum was not in itself considerable, it was the first of a succession of small expenses that, taken together, began to weigh upon the modest allowance which Mr. Vernon had settled upon his daughter for the season.

Charlotte said nothing of this to her aunt, for Mrs. Talbot had spoken so often of the propriety of economy, and had practised it so little since their arrival, that Charlotte feared to seem reproachful. She paid for the gloves, and the ribbon that Julia admired, and the silk for the reticule that Miss Louisa Aldridge declared indispensable, and the little engravings that were pressed upon her at the exhibition, and she found, at the end of a fortnight, that she had spent more in trinkets and fancies than would have kept her mother in pocket-money for half a year at Lyncombe.

But the expense of money was nothing to the expense of uneasiness. Charlotte had begun, very slowly and very reluctantly, to perceive that the Aldridges' friendship was not altogether disinterested. They were not unkind, nor even intentionally designing; but they had the habit, common to those who have always had enough, of regarding the wants of others as a form of entertainment. They liked Charlotte because she was new, and because her admiration was sincere, and because her country simplicity afforded them a pleasing contrast to their own sophistication. They liked her, too, because she could be induced to share in their amusements without exacting any very serious return of confidence; and Charlotte, who would have walked over hot coals for Julia Aldridge, began to suspect, with a sickness of heart that she could not entirely conceal from herself, that she was valued chiefly as an audience.

The discovery was not made all at once. It came by degrees, in the manner of most unwelcome truths—through a phrase let fall by Louisa, who was less guarded than her sisters; through Julia's perfect composure when Charlotte confessed she could not join a party to Richmond; through the faint but perceptible coolness that descended upon the eldest Miss Aldridge whenever Charlotte's contributions to the common fund of entertainment fell short of expectation. Charlotte observed these things, and turned them over in her mind at night, when the noise of the street had subsided and the house in Upper Brook Street was quiet; and she arrived, by the end of the fourth week, at a conclusion that was as mortifying as it was clear: she had been taken up because she was useful, and she had been found useful because she was easy.

She might have borne this reflection with greater fortitude had it stood alone. But it was accompanied by another, which touched her more nearly. She had observed, with the sharpened perception of one who is already unhappy, that several of Mrs. Talbot's acquaintance treated her aunt with a civility that stopped short of warmth. There were calls that were not returned, invitations that were cautiously worded, and a general air of circumspection in the older ladies' manner which Charlotte, who had been bred to read social distinctions as naturally as she read the weather, could not fail to mark. Mrs. Talbot, in the country, was a woman of undisputed consequence. In London, she was a woman whose consequence was tolerated but not confirmed.
