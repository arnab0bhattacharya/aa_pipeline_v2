There are men who are born to the slow, heavy rhythm of the earth, and there are others who mistake the stirring of their own blood for the first tremor of a great event. Aurelio Valdez, editor of *La Verdad*, belonged, I am persuaded, to the latter category. He sat in the high, shuttered room of his printing press, where the air, thick with the ghosts of ink and the sweet, dry scent of ageing paper, was the only atmosphere he had ever truly breathed. Outside, the capital of the Republic of San Esteban baked in the afternoon heat, a city of white walls and dusty palms dozing under a sky of polished brass. But within those four walls, a revolution was being forged, word by corrosive word. Valdez, a man whose slight frame seemed a poor vessel for the fury he expressed, had composed an article—a proclamation, really—of such incandescent quality that he felt the very type-lead must have sweated under the pressure of the composition. He had written of the noble General Rivas, the liberator, the new Bolivar, whose brave men were even now, he believed, descending from the green mountains to scourge the corrupt regime of the old Dictator, Manriquez.

The silence in the press room was a profound thing, broken only by the distant, monotonous cry of a vendor and the creak of a wooden shutter. Valdez had not slept. The words he had written, now locked in the chase and ready for the great wooden press, seemed to hang in the air around him, a palpable presence of righteous fire. He had felt himself a conduit for the soul of the nation, a prophet crying in the wilderness of oppression. The article was to be the spark; the paper, the tinder; the Republic, the glorious conflagration. He had seen it all in his mind’s eye: the cheering crowds, the fall of the tyrant’s statues, the grateful faces of the people turned towards him, their deliverer. He was not merely a journalist; he was an architect of freedom. And the knowledge of it filled him with a tremulous, almost sacred, pride. The failure of the revolt, when the news finally came, did not arrive with the crash of artillery or the clamour of a mob. It came with the soft, final thud of a folded slip of paper pushed under the heavy door of the press. It was from a friend in the telegraph office, a man of few words and a terrified heart. Three lines. Rivas had been betrayed at the pass. His men were scattered. The Dictator’s Guard was marching on the capital. There was no signature. There was no need. Valdez read the slip by the light of a single oil-lamp, and the words swam before his eyes, meaningless and yet more terrible than any he had ever composed. The heat in the room, which had seemed the vital warmth of creation, was now the stifling air of a tomb. The silence was no longer expectant; it was the silence of a void.

He stood up and walked to the shuttered window, pressing his forehead against the warm wood. He could hear nothing but the thumping of his own heart, a frantic, foolish drum against the ribs. He had committed the one sin that power never forgives: he had been right in his desires, but wrong in his judgment. He had backed the losing horse, and in San Esteban, such a wager was paid for in blood. The thought of flight, of slipping out through the back alleys towards the river and the dubious safety of the coast, presented itself to him as a sordid scramble, an undignified scuttle. He pictured himself a fugitive, a name whispered with contempt in the very salons where he had been fêted. Then came the other vision, the one that truly chilled the marrow of his bones: the soldiers. Not the clean, abstract idea of soldiers, but the reality of them. The heavy tread on the stairs, the splintering of the door, the brutish faces under their caps, the gleam of a machete in the lamplight. They would not debate the finer points of liberty with him. They would take the man who had called their master a hyena and a thief, and they would make of him a public example. His pen, his wit, his soaring rhetoric—all would be as nothing before the simple, final fact of a length of steel.

For a long time he remained there, a man suspended between two abysses: the abyss of his own cowardice and the abyss of their vengeance. He looked at the great press, the iron monster that had been his instrument of glory and was now the chief witness to his ruin. On the composing stone lay the locked chase, his masterpiece, his death warrant. He could destroy it. He could melt the type, burn the paper, and wait for the dawn. Perhaps they would only arrest him. Perhaps he could recant, write a grovelling apology, praise the Dictator’s wisdom and mercy. The thought was a bitter gall in his mouth, a poison more deadly than any bullet. To deny his own words would be to murder the man who had written them. It would be a public vivisection of his own soul. But what was a soul worth, weighed against the cold, hard certainty of a prison cell, of a courtyard at dawn? The question was one for philosophers and martyrs, not for a newspaper editor with a dry throat and trembling hands. He was a man of ink and paper, of ideas and ideals, suddenly confronted with the appalling, irreducible reality of flesh and bone—his own. The world of words had betrayed him, for it had promised a certain order, a certain justice, that the world of men did not recognise. He felt a profound, aching loneliness, as if he were the last man on earth, condemned to witness the end of all things in this silent, ink-scented room. The darkness outside the shutters was deepening, and in the capital of San Esteban, the silence was now absolute, the silence of a city holding its breath before the coming of the storm.
