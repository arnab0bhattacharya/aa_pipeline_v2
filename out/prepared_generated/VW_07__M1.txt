The light, which was not yet light but only the ghost of it, grey and without substance, crept over the gravel and the iron railings; and Mrs. Merriweather, moving with the careful, measured tread of one who has long since ceased to hurry, felt it settle upon her cheek, a cold and indifferent touch. It was the same light, she supposed, or some cousin of it, that had fallen on the water every morning of her life, and yet this morning it seemed to have a particular quality of pity, or perhaps it was merely the dampness of November that made the world seem so close, so much a part of her own breathing. She carried the bag, the string handle cutting a little into her gloved fingers, and inside were the crusts, dry and white as bone, the only offering she had left to make. To whom she made it, or for what reason, was a question that had long ago dissolved, like a sugar lump in the tea of her consciousness, leaving only a vague sweetness behind, a sense of obligation that was as much a part of her as the ache in her knee or the grey of her hair. The ducks were already there, their shapes dark and wavering upon the water, and they turned their heads as she approached, not with the frantic greed of the pigeons that strutted upon the path, but with a certain grave expectancy, a recognition that was perhaps more intimate than any human greeting.

She reached the bench, the third from the bridge, the one where the dampness did not quite reach the seat, and she paused before sitting, looking out across the water towards the grey bulk of the Horse Guards, which stood like a memory of a more solid world. The bag rustled as she opened it, and the sound seemed to her very loud in the stillness, a shattering of the peace that the early hour had woven. She took out a crust, her fingers stiff and clumsy, and crumbled it, watching the pieces fall, white upon dark, into the water where the ripples spread out in circles that overlapped and vanished, just as the years did, she thought, just as the days did, one upon another until they were all one grey blur. And then she heard the sound of footsteps on the gravel, a steady, unhurried tread that was not the shuffle of an old man or the frantic pace of a clerk late for his office, but a sound that had a rhythm to it, a weight that spoke of youth and a body that did not yet know the limits of its own strength. She did not look up, for to look up was to invite the world in, and the world was too full of things she did not wish to see; but she felt the presence of someone stopping near the next bench, the one that was turned slightly away, and she felt the air shift as a coat was unbuttoned or a hat was lifted, and then the silence settled again, but it was a different silence now, a silence that was aware of being watched, or perhaps it was she who was watching, though her eyes were fixed upon the ducks.

He sat down. She knew it without seeing, by the way the wood of the bench creaked and then was still. And then, after a moment, he spoke. "They are greedy little things, aren't they?" he said, and his voice was low, a little rough at the edges, as if he were not used to speaking so early in the morning, or perhaps not used to speaking to strangers at all. Mrs. Merriweather did not answer at first, for she was thinking of another voice, a voice that had said something similar once, though the words were gone, leaving only the tone behind, a tone of amusement and affection that had been cut off as cleanly as a thread by a pair of scissors. She crumbled another crust, watching it dissolve in the water, and then she said, "They are only hungry." It was a flat statement, devoid of the color he had tried to give it, and she felt him shift on the bench, felt the awkwardness of the silence that followed, a silence that was heavy with things unsaid, with the weight of the morning and the cold and the strange intimacy of two people sitting in the same grey light.

He did not speak again for a long time, and she found herself listening to the sound of his breathing, which was steady and deep, a sound of life that seemed to fill the empty spaces of the park. She wondered who he was, and why he was here, at this hour, when most boys his age—she judged him to be about nineteen, perhaps twenty, though it was hard to tell with the collar of his coat turned up against the wind—were still in their beds or rushing towards the city. Was he waiting for someone? Or was he, like her, drawn to the water by some need he could not name? She turned her head slightly, just enough to see him from the corner of her eye. He was leaning forward, his elbows on his knees, looking at the ducks with an intensity that was almost painful to witness. His hands were clasped together, large and rough, and she saw a scar on his knuckle, a white line against the skin, and the sight of it made her heart contract with a sudden, sharp pang that was not quite grief and not quite hope, but something in between, a terrible, aching tenderness for the fragility of flesh.
