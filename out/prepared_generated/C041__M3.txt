Mrs. Thackeray’s request came on my second morning at Blackwood Grange, delivered across a breakfast table set with tarnished silver. She sat in a shaft of pale Yorkshire light, her hands folded so tightly in her lap that the knuckles shone white through the skin. She desired me to keep a daily ledger of young Oliver’s progress. His Latin, his French, his proficiency on the pianoforte. She pushed a leather-bound volume across the tablecloth toward me, its pages blank and faintly yellowed. I accepted the duty without hesitation. It seemed a reasonable request from a mother who saw little of her son.

For the first week, my entries were unremarkable. I recorded Oliver’s struggles with the third declension, his sudden, startling aptitude for Euclid, the way he would press his forehead to the cold glass of the nursery window and watch the drive below. The boy was nine years old, frail and watchful, with a tendency to fall silent whenever heavy footsteps sounded in the lower hall. I noted his moods, his meals, his hours of exercise in the crisp autumn air. I left the diary on my writing desk each evening, tucked beneath a copy of *The Vicar of Wakefield*.

It was on a Thursday that I noticed the book had been moved. I have a precise memory for the placement of objects, a necessity for a governess. The ribbon marker, which I always fold at a diagonal, had been smoothed flat. A faint crease marred the top corner of the page, as if it had been gripped between a thumb and forefinger. I said nothing. I simply watched. The following evening, I made a small ink blot near the bottom of the page. By morning, it had been smeared, a ghost of itself trailing across the margin.

Someone was reading the diary while I slept.

I initially suspected the housemaid, a prying woman named Mrs. Fenn, but the diary was locked in my room, and the lock showed no signs of tampering. It was Mrs. Thackeray who possessed the only other key to the schoolroom wing. The realization settled over me slowly. I began to observe her with new eyes. She was always pale, always taut with some unspoken tension, and she would look at me each morning with a sharp, questioning gaze that seemed to beg for information I had not offered aloud. I realized then that she was not searching for Oliver’s marks in arithmetic. She was searching for mention of her husband.

Mr. Thackeray was a phantom at the Grange. He was a merchant of some repute, a man whose trade took him to Hull and Leeds and, occasionally, London. His absences were long and unexplained. When he returned, he brought the scent of cigar smoke and river mud, speaking little and retreating immediately to his study. I had seen him only twice in a fortnight, a tall man with heavy brows and a coat always dusted with travel. I had not thought to write of him. But Mrs. Thackeray clearly expected me to.

I began to understand the nature of her surveillance. She could not ask the servants directly without revealing her desperation. She could not question her husband without risking his temper. But she could read the innocent observations of a governess, a woman who moved through the house with a degree of freedom and noticed things without malice. I was her spy, though I had never enlisted.

The knowledge sat uncomfortably with me for two days. I am not a deceptive woman by nature. I was raised by a clergyman who valued candor above all virtues, and the act of writing for an unseen audience felt like a kind of fraud. But I watched Mrs. Thackeray at dinner one evening, her fork motionless over her plate, her eyes fixed on her husband’s empty chair, and I felt a sudden, sharp pity. She was a woman drowning in silence, grasping at any scrap of paper that might float past.

So I began to write for two readers.

My entries became layered, careful things. The first layer was for Oliver’s benefit, or rather, for the official record of his education. I wrote of his recitations, his penmanship, the books we read together. But threaded through these observations, I began to include small details about the house and its inhabitants. I noted the sounds that drifted up from the lower floors. I recorded the times Mr. Thackeray’s horse was brought round, the hour of his departure, the direction he took on the road. I mentioned the letters that arrived, the color of the wax, the postmarks. I described the visitors who called, their dress, their expressions.

These details were woven so naturally into the narrative of Oliver’s day that they appeared incidental. I wrote that Oliver’s attention wandered during his French translation because a carriage had clattered into the drive at half past ten, bringing a gentleman in a grey coat who remained with Mr. Thackeray for two hours. I noted that the boy was restless and irritable on a Tuesday, though I attributed it to the weather, while also mentioning that Mr. Thackeray had departed before dawn, his study lamp extinguished and his desk cleared of papers. I recorded the ordinary life of the house as a painter records the landscape, with broad strokes and careful detail, allowing Mrs. Thackeray to read between the lines.

I did not invent anything. I simply made visible what had been invisible. I described the smell of brandy that clung to the lower hall on certain evenings, the sound of argument that drifted through the floorboards, the way the servants grew quiet and watchful when Mr. Thackeray was in residence. I wrote of Mrs. Thackeray herself, her pallor, her headaches, the way she pressed her hand to her temple when the front door slammed.
