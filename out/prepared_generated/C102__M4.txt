The announcement appeared in the Wednesday edition of the *Musical Gazette*, wedged between a notice of a forthcoming pianoforte recital in Bayswater and an advertisement for a patented finger-strengthening device. It occupied precisely four lines of agate type, and it read: *Mr. Aubrey Vance begs to inform his friends and the public that he has discontinued the composition of drawing-room ballads, and that his future works will be confined to music of a more serious character.*

Within a week, the effect was catastrophic. His publisher, a florid man named Mr. Pettigrew, whose offices in Paternoster Row smelled perpetually of pipe smoke and sealing wax, wrote him six letters in four days, each more agitated than the last. The ballads—"The Garden of Remembrance," "When Evening Bells Are Ringing," "My Love Lies in the Heather"—sold in the tens of thousands. They were sung at soirées in Kensington, hummed by clerks on the omnibus, and had been known to reduce entire drawing rooms to tears. Mr. Pettigrew had recently purchased a small villa in Margate on the strength of "The Garden of Remembrance" alone. He regarded Mr. Vance's announcement as a personal betrayal of the most intimate kind.

The admirers were no less wounded. Mrs. Hetherington-Cole, who had hosted a musical evening in Prince's Gate at which Mr. Vance's ballads had been performed three times in succession, wrote to him in a hand that trembled with indignation. She had, she informed him, already engaged a soprano for her autumn gathering and had promised her guests that Mr. Vance himself would accompany. She did not see how any music could be more serious than "My Love Lies in the Heather," which had made her weep for a full hour. She concluded by suggesting that he reconsider, for his own sake and for the sake of all those who had loved him.

Mr. Vance read this letter in his drawing room in Chelsea, a long, narrow room with windows facing the river, where the light came in grey and watery through the winter afternoons. He was a thin man of forty-five, with a high forehead and the slightly hunted expression of one who has spent too many years being agreeable. He read Mrs. Hetherington-Cole's letter twice, then placed it carefully on the fire and watched it curl and blacken. He had expected this. What he had not expected was the knock that came at his door on the Thursday morning, at an hour when no caller of any respectability would dream of presenting himself.

The knock was sharp and peremptory, three raps and a pause, as though the visitor had calculated the precise interval that would cause the most irritation. Mr. Vance set down his coffee cup and went to the door himself, for his housekeeper was occupied in the kitchen and he had no desire to subject her to whatever fresh indignity awaited him.

On the step stood a tall, angular man in a coat of greenish tweed, holding a walking stick with a silver knob and regarding the house with the expression of a surveyor assessing a property for demolition. This was Mr. Lionel Crane, music critic for the *Saturday Review*, a man who had spent the better part of a decade describing Mr. Vance's ballads as "treacle for the tearful," "sentimentality set to a waltz," and, in one memorable phrase, "the musical equivalent of a doily." He had never been invited to Mr. Vance's house. He had never been invited anywhere, as far as Mr. Vance could determine, and seemed to take a grim satisfaction in the fact.

"Mr. Vance," said Mr. Crane, inclining his head a fraction of an inch. "I read your announcement. I have come to see whether you have taken leave of your senses or whether, by some miracle, you have found them."

Mr. Vance stared at him for a long moment. The rain had begun to fall, a fine, persistent drizzle that beaded on Mr. Crane's tweed shoulders. There was something almost admirable in the man's insolence.

"You had better come in," said Mr. Vance.

He led the way to the drawing room, where the fire had burned low and the piano—a Broadwood grand, somewhat out of tune—stood in the corner like a patient beast. Mr. Crane took a seat without being asked, planted his walking stick between his knees, and looked about him with undisguised curiosity.

"I expected something more opulent," he said. "Given the sales of 'The Garden of Remembrance,' I had imagined velvet hangings. A gilt mirror, at the very least."

"I am not fond of gilt," said Mr. Vance.

"No. You are fond of heather and evening bells and the tears of middle-aged women. Or you were." Mr. Crane tilted his head. "What happened? Did you wake one morning and find that your soul had returned? Did you hear a late Beethoven quartet and fall to your knees in shame?"

Mr. Vance did not answer immediately. He went to the window and looked out at the river, which was the colour of wet slate. A barge was making its slow way downstream, and he watched it until it passed beneath the bridge.

"I was at a dinner party," he said at last. "Three weeks ago. In Bedford Square. There was a young woman at the piano—a Miss Ashworth, I believe—and she played a ballade of mine. 'When Evening Bells Are Ringing.' She played it very well. Everyone applauded. And I sat there and I thought: this is the music of a man who has never once been honest with himself."

Mr. Crane was silent for a moment. When Mr. Vance turned, he saw that the critic's expression had changed. The mockery had gone out of it, replaced by something more cautious, more considering.

"Go on," said Mr. Crane.

"I have written forty-three ballads," said Mr. Vance. "I could write forty-three more. I know precisely which chord to use when the lover departs, and precisely which suspension will make the maiden weep. I could do it in my sleep, and lately I believe I have been doing it in my sleep. But I have never written a single bar of music that was true."
