It is a truth generally, though often reluctantly, admitted among the middle classes of a provincial town, that the settled order of things is a species of moral furniture which we move at our peril; and when the news of Mr. Josiah Vane’s letter arrived at the house in Lindsey Row, it fell upon the household of Mrs. Vane with the shock of a heavy object dropped upon a polished floor, threatening to mar the surface of many years of respectable quietude.

Mrs. Vane, who had been for a decade and a half the accepted model of a bereaved widow, sitting in her black bombazine with a resignation that was the envy of less disciplined neighbours, was suddenly confronted not with a ghost, but with a far more inconvenient reality: a living husband who had merely been absent. To the world, and to her daughter, Hester, the tombstone of Josiah Vane had been a firm fixture in the geography of their lives; but the letter, written in a shaky hand from a lodging house in Hull, declared that the stone was a fiction, and that the man intended to occupy his own grave no longer than it took to sign a codicil to his will.

Hester, a young woman of twenty, possessed a mind that had been cultivated by the circulating library and the sermons of Mr. Broadbent into a state of acute, if somewhat rigid, sensibility. She stood by the window, the letter trembling in her hand, watching the grey mist settle over the Lincolnshire fenland that pressed against the town of Kirkby Marsh, and felt the very ground of her identity shift beneath her feet. It was not merely the scandal of a living father that agitated her, but the philosophical horror of discovering that her mother—the saint of her childhood—had been an accomplice to a deception.

"But he is dying, Mother," Hester said, turning from the window. "The letter says he has but a fortnight. Surely, whatever the past—"

"The past!" Mrs. Vane interrupted, her voice possessing that sharp, metallic quality which it acquired whenever her authority was questioned. She was a woman whose features had been sharpened by a long habit of self-denial, and she looked at Hester with the hard, bright eye of a bird defending its nest. "The past is a closed book, Hester, and I will not have you turning back the pages to read the lies written there. A man who can desert his wife and child, leaving them to the charity of his brother-in-law and the sweat of my needle, has forfeited the name of father. He is dead to us. That he should dare to write, to ask for you to go to him—to hold his hand while he makes his peace with God—is an insolence that passes all bearing."

"But if he is my father?" Hester urged, her conscience—a formidable organ in her constitution—pricking her. "Is there no duty to the dying? Does not the law of nature—"

"Nature!" Mrs. Vane cried, rising and pacing the small parlour. "Do not speak to me of nature. Nature taught him to fly from his responsibilities like a bird of prey. If you go to him, Hester, you go against my blessing. You go to a man who broke my heart and left me to the pity of a town that whispers behind its fans. I have built this life upon the rock of my widowhood; I will not have it washed away by the tears of a profligate."

Here, Mrs. Thripp, the charwoman who was engaged in the laborious business of polishing the brass fender, looked up from her knees. She was a woman of the soil, whose face was a map of the local dialects and whose understanding of human nature was derived from the observation of pigs and parish officers rather than novels.

"Aye, missus," Mrs. Thripp observed, pausing with the black-lead brush in her hand. "It be a poor heart that never rejoices, but it be a poorer one that lets a scamp back in at the door when the latch is finally bolted. I mind when my Tom run off wi' the tinker’s daughter, I said to meself, ‘Thripp,’ I said, ‘let him go to the devil his own way.’ And he did. And a good riddance it were, though I missed the help with the heavy washin'."

"You see, Hester?" Mrs. Vane said, seizing upon this proletarian corroboration. "Even Mrs. Thripp, though she lacks the advantages of education, sees the wisdom of severing a rotten limb. If you go to him, you dishonour me. You say to the world that my grievance is nothing, that my suffering is a trifle. And you tell him that he may die easy, having seen the child he abandoned, without ever paying the price of that abandonment."

Hester looked from her mother’s rigid face to the fenland outside. She felt the terrible weight of the dual commandments: Honour thy father and mother. But what if the two were at war? She saw, with the painful clarity of youth discovering the complexity of the adult world, that her mother was not asking for justice, but for revenge; and that the dying man in Hull was asking for forgiveness which he had not earned. She was the rope in a tug-of-war between two selfishnesses, and to pull in either direction was to break.

"I cannot bear it," Hester whispered, pressing her hands to her temples. "To refuse a dying man... it seems a cruelty that God will judge."

"God judges the heart," Mrs. Vane said sternly, sitting down and resuming her needlework with a ferocity that suggested she was stitching the shroud of her husband’s reputation. "And my heart tells me that a liar has no claim. If he is dying, let him die as he lived—alone. And let us keep our dignity, which is the only dowry I have to leave you."
