The young man who was shown into our lodgings upon that November evening was so palpably agitated that I feared at first he had been the victim of some street accident. His overcoat was buttoned awry, his hat crushed beneath his arm, and his hands, gloved in fine kid, trembled as he laid them upon the table. He was fair, clean-shaven, with the rounded complexion of a man reared in the country, and his eyes, of a pale blue, darted from one object to another with the restlessness of a caged animal.

"You are Mr. Plexby," said he, in a voice that he strove in vain to keep steady. "I have been directed to you by Inspector Gregson, who hinted that you were more conversant with curious inquiries than the regular force. I am in great trouble, sir."

My friend, who had been examining a residue of tobacco in his pipe, glanced up with that keen, alert expression which I knew so well.

"Pray be seated," said Plexby, with his customary courtesy. "You are, unless I am mistaken, Mr. Josiah Amberley, of Amberley & Son, the brewers. Your face has been in the illustrated papers of late, in connection with the wedding of your sister. You reside at Chislehurst, I believe, and have recently been left sole heir to the estate of your uncle, the late Mr. Horace Amberley."

Our visitor started. "You are wonderfully well informed, sir," said he.

"A trifle, a trifle," said Plexby, with a deprecatory wave of his hand. "The name of Amberley's is upon every hoarding in London. But your trouble, Mr. Amberley. Let us have it, if you please, in the order of its occurrence."

Our visitor composed himself with an effort. "It concerns a series of telegrams," he began, drawing three folded papers from his pocket. "The first arrived on the 10th of this month. It was addressed to me at my chambers in Chancery Lane, and bore the postmark of Faversham, in Kent, where our brewery is situated. It contained but two words: 'Elias Vane.'"

Plexby took the paper and glanced at it. I observed that his eye lingered but a moment before he handed it back. "Elias Vane," he repeated. "I have seen that name in the financial columns. He was your senior partner, I think?"

"He was," said Amberley. "He was a hale, vigorous man of sixty, with not a day's illness in his life. I was surprised, therefore, to receive such a message. But my surprise turned to horror when, upon the 13th, I learned that Mr. Vane had been found dead in his bed, having expired in the night from a failure of the heart. The doctor at the inquest declared it to be a natural death."

"And the second telegram?"

Amberley's face grew paler, and his fingers fumbled with the next paper. "It arrived on the 15th. It bore the same postmark, and was couched in the same terms. It contained but two words: 'Thomas Griggs.'"

"The head cooper of your establishment," said Plexby, nodding. "A man of considerable mechanical ingenuity, if I am not mistaken. I read of his death in the evening paper of the 17th. He was crushed by a falling dray, was he not?"

"He was, sir. A dray loaded with empty casks slipped its gear upon the brewery yard, and he was caught beneath it. Two men died within a week, each named in a telegram before the event. And now, sir——" Amberley's voice broke, and he passed his hand across his brow. "And now I have received the third."

He laid the paper upon the table. I picked it up and read the two words: "Josiah Amberley."

"You see, Mr. Plexby," said our visitor, in a hoarse whisper, "that my name is Josiah Amberley. I have come to you because I believe my life is in danger. I have no enemy that I know of. I am a young man, with no vices that could bring me to an early grave. Yet I cannot believe that these messages are the work of a chance. Two men are dead, and I am the third named."

Plexby sat for some moments in silence, his keen grey eyes fixed upon the fire. "Have you the envelopes?" he asked, at length.

"I have," said Amberley, producing them. "They are of the ordinary kind, such as may be bought at any post-office."

Plexby examined them with his lens, turning them over and over with the care that he bestowed upon every trifle. "They were handed in at the Faversham office," said he. "The clerk who received them would probably remember nothing. But tell me, Mr. Amberley, who benefits by the death of Elias Vane?"

"I do, in part. His share in the brewery passes to his nephew, a Mr. John Vane, but there is a clause in the partnership deed by which, upon the death of either partner, the survivor has the option of purchasing the deceased's share at a valuation. I exercised that option, and the transfer is now in progress."

"And who benefits by the death of Thomas Griggs?"

"His widow, I suppose. He had a small life insurance. But the sum is trifling."

"And who would benefit by your death, Mr. Amberley?"

The young man hesitated. "My cousin, Caleb Amberley, would inherit my uncle's estate, which is considerable. But Caleb is a harmless fellow, devoted to chemistry and entomology. He could not hurt a fly."

"I have known entomologists who were not averse to the destruction of higher forms of life," said Plexby, drily. "But let us examine the matter further. You say your cousin is devoted to chemistry. Where does he reside?"

"At Faversham, near the brewery. He has a small laboratory there, in the grounds of his father's house."

Plexby rose and reached for his coat. "Watson," said he, turning to me, "we catch the 10:15 from Victoria. Mr. Amberley, I should be obliged if you would remain in town, at your chambers, until you hear from me. I would also ask you to place yourself under the care of Dr. Watson here, who will see that no harm befalls you."
