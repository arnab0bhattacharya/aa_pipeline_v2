The earth over Mam’s grave was still dark and soft when Mrs Pugh from the farm up the hill came down to the cottage, bringing with her a loaf of bread and the full weight of her pity. Elin Tudor thanked her for the bread, though her voice sounded as though it belonged to someone standing very far away. She stood in the narrow doorway of the stone cottage, watching the autumn mist crawl over the brow of the hill, and listened to the older woman speak of the future as though it were a wild animal that needed to be tethered.

‘You cannot stay here alone, Elin,’ Mrs Pugh said, her broad face creased with a mixture of genuine concern and the unshakeable conviction of a woman who knew best. ‘You are but nineteen. What will you do? How will you live? The village will talk, and the winter will be hard.’

‘I have lived through a hard winter already,’ Elin replied, her pale hands gripping the edge of the wooden door. ‘I will stay.’

After Mrs Pugh left, shaking her head and muttering about the stubbornness of the young, Elin closed the door and leaned against it. The silence of the cottage was absolute. For three years, this silence had been an impossible luxury, shattered always by the rattling, wet breaths from the back room, the clatter of the poultices, the endless boiling of water and laundering of stained linens. Now, the quiet was so heavy it pressed against her eardrums. She walked across the beaten earth floor to the hearth. The fire had burned down to a few grey embers. She did not stir it. She simply sat on the stool where Mam had liked to sit before the sickness took her legs, and stared into the ash.

The offer of a home had come a week before the funeral, delivered in a stiff, formal letter carried by the village postboy. A distant cousin of her mother’s, a Mr Rhys, a man Elin had never met, owned a drapery shop in Carmarthen. He wrote to say that he and his wife were prepared to take Elin in as an assistant in the shop. She would have a roof over her head, he wrote, and a place at their table, and in time, perhaps, she might learn a respectable trade. It was a generous offer, the only piece of good fortune in a black month. Elin had read the letter by the light of a tallow candle, her mother’s laboured breathing the only sound in the room, and she had known instantly that she would not go.

She could not explain it to the village. They thought her strange, perhaps even mad with grief. They saw a penniless girl refusing the safety of kin. But Elin knew that to go to Carmarthen would be to let the sickness win. For three years, she had been defined entirely by the illness. She had been the girl who nursed her mother. Her own youth, her own self, had been steadily consumed by the ravenous demands of the dying. If she went to live with strangers, she would merely trade one form of servitude for another. She would be the orphaned relation, the one who must be grateful, the one who must carry the memory of her mother’s suffering into their tidy, healthy house and pretend it did not stain the air. Here, in the damp, stone-walled cottage in the village of Abercwm, she could breathe.

The first weeks were a strange, dreamlike existence. She sold the few silver spoons her mother had left her to the tinker, and with the money bought oats, barley, and a sack of coal. She learned the stark mathematics of poverty. The garden behind the cottage, which had gone to seed during her mother’s illness, yielded a meagre crop of late turnips and hardy leeks. She ate simply, often just a broth of vegetables and a crust of bread. She did not mind the hunger. It was a clean pain, a pain that belonged to her body alone, not to her heart.

The villagers watched her with wary eyes. They had respected her during the illness, admiring her devotion. Now, her solitude seemed like a rejection of them. At the chapel on Sunday, the women would press her hand and murmur condolences, but their eyes darted over her shoulder, assessing her worn shawl and her pale face. Elin stopped going. She could not bear the soft pity, nor the underlying current of judgement. She was young, and she was alone, and in the Wales of 1820, a young woman alone was an anomaly, a creature out of place, requiring either rescue or suspicion.

One afternoon, the blacksmith’s wife, a woman named Nia, knocked on the door. She brought a pot of cawl, still hot, and pushed her way inside when Elin opened the door. Nia looked around the single room, noting the bare table, the empty chair, the cold hearth.

‘You are fading away to nothing, Elin,’ Nia said, setting the pot down with a thud. ‘This is no life for a girl. You should be singing, you should be looking to the future. Mr Rhys is a good man. He would not see you want.’

‘I do not want his charity,’ Elin said, a spark of heat in her voice. ‘I want to be left alone.’

‘Alone is a dangerous thing,’ Nia replied, her tone sharp. ‘The mind turns in on itself. You have buried your mother, yes, and it is a tragedy. But you are still alive. You must live like it.’

‘I am living,’ Elin insisted, though her voice trembled. ‘I am breathing the air. I am eating. I am keeping the cottage clean. Is that not living?’

Nia shook her head, her expression sorrowful. ‘It is surviving, child. It is merely waiting for the end. You are nineteen. You should be afraid of the dark, not embracing it.’
