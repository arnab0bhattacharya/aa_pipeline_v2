She had come to Zurich in the autumn, when the lake wore a grey and wrinkled face beneath a sky of hammered tin, and the mountains stood about the town like a council of silent, disapproving elders. Her name was Vera Ivanovna Semyonova, and she carried with her a small trunk of medical books, a letter of introduction to a professor of anatomy, and that particular ardour of the Russian student which is half idealism and half a desperate hunger for the tangible world—for facts, for knowledge that could be held in the hand and used. She was twenty-two years old, with serious grey eyes and a habit of listening with her whole face, which made men think her beautiful and women think her clever, and she was, in truth, neither so much as she was earnest, which is a quality that wears better than either and is less often forgiven.

It was at the house of a certain Frau Hedwig Bäumler, a widow of independent means and radical sympathies, that she first heard the man speak. The widow kept a salon of sorts—a crowded, overheated room on the third floor of a building near the university, where on Thursday evenings one might find a dozen exiles of as many nationalities, drinking weak tea and consuming the widow's little cakes with the solemn voracity of the politically committed. There were Serbs and Czechs and one brooding Italian who never spoke; there were two Bulgarian brothers who argued perpetually about the peasant question; there was a young Frenchman of letters who affected a monocle and wrote nothing. And there was the Pole, whose name was Bolesław Miłosz, a quiet student of chemistry with a face like a closed door and hands that were always still.

But it was the other one who drew her eye that first evening—the speaker, the man they called Dmitri Pavlovich Orlov. He was tall and somewhat stooped, with a broad forehead and a beard that he kept trimmed to a point, like a revolutionary of the old school, and his voice was the voice of a man who has learned to fill a room without raising it. He spoke of Russia—of the vast, suffering, patient Russia of the villages—and his words fell into the close air of the widow's parlour like stones into still water, and the rings of them widened and widened until every face in that room was turned toward him, and Vera felt her own heart stirred by a sensation she could not name, which was part admiration and part fear, and wholly the beginning of a kind of captivity.

He noticed her, of course. Such men always notice the ones who listen with their whole faces. Afterward, when the tea was cold and the cakes were gone and the guests were putting on their coats, he came to her with a certain deliberation, as though he had all the time in the world, and said:

"You are new to us. You are studying medicine, I am told. That is good. We need doctors—we need them very much—in the work that is coming."

She asked him what work, and he smiled and said that the work was the work, and that she would understand in time, and that meanwhile she must come again, and that the widow's tea was bad but the company was necessary. He spoke with an air of kindly condescension that should have offended her and did not. She went again the following Thursday, and the Thursday after that, and each time she sat a little nearer to the corner where Orlov held his court, and each time she felt herself drawn a little further into the circle of his gravity.

It was Miłosz who spoke to her of him first. They were walking together along the Bahnhofstrasse in the thin December light, and she had been speaking of Orlov with an enthusiasm that she did not quite know how to moderate, when the Pole stopped in his walk and turned to her a face that was troubled and grave.

"You should be careful of that one," he said.

She laughed—a little sharply, for she was not accustomed to being warned.

"Because you do not like him?"

"Because I know him," said Miłosz. "Or rather—I know the kind of him. I have seen such men before. In Warsaw. In Kraków. They are very fine until they are not fine. They speak beautifully of the people, and the people are always somewhere else—somewhere far away, where the speaking costs nothing."

"You are unjust," she said. "You are jealous."

She said it lightly, as a woman says such things, but she saw his face close against her like a door slammed in a wind, and she was sorry, and would not say so.

"Perhaps," he said. "Perhaps I am jealous. But jealousy does not make a thing untrue." And he walked on beside her in silence, and she felt the weight of his silence and resented it.

The winter passed. Vera attended her lectures, dissected her cadavers, learned the Latin names of bones, and on Thursday evenings sat in the widow's parlour and listened to Orlov speak of the coming day. He spoke of it always as a thing imminent, a thing that waited just beyond the next hill of circumstance, and he spoke of sacrifice, and of the necessity of sacrifice, and of how the individual life was nothing—nothing at all—when weighed against the great current of history. She found these words beautiful. She found them so beautiful that she did not notice, at first, how often he said them, or how his eye would travel about the room while he said them, measuring the effect, weighing the faces, counting the hands that would be raised when the time came to raise them.
