The tea had been poured, and the afternoon—that long, golden, absolutely motionless afternoon peculiar to Wiltshire in late September—had settled over the drawing-room of Marchant Hall like a coat of varnish. Lady Ashcombe, a small, exquisite woman whose whole life was a triumph of arrangement, was speaking of the roses. Sir Aubrey Ashcombe, ninth baronet, stood at the window with his back to the room, and it was observed by his wife that he had not touched his cup, which was not like him, for he was a man who regarded tea as the one ceremony of the day that could not be improved upon.

‘The yellow ones by the south wall,’ said Lady Ashcombe, ‘have been a positive scandal. I have spoken to Fitch, and Fitch has spoken to the under-gardener, and the under-gardener, I am told, has spoken to Providence, but nothing has come of it. Aubrey, you are not listening.’

‘I am listening with both ears, my dear,’ said Sir Aubrey, without turning. ‘I am merely not attending. There is a difference, and it is the whole difference between a wife and a stenographer.’

‘You are in one of your moods.’

‘I am in the only mood that has ever suited me. But I am also expecting a visitor. A Mr. Cattermole, from the College of Arms. He writes that he has something for me, and he writes of it in the tone a man employs when he is about to be disagreeable in the most courteous possible manner. I confess I am curious. Curiosity is the one passion that survives the age of forty, because it is the only one that requires no exertion.’

Mr. Cattermole arrived at four precisely: a thin, dry, apologetic man, the colour of old parchment and with the manner of a physician who has come to tell you that your symptoms are trifling but your constitution is doomed. He was received with the ceremony due to a man who has spent his life among the dead, and given the second-best chair and the first-best cake.

‘You have written to me,’ said Sir Aubrey, ‘in terms that suggest I am to be either congratulated or condoled with. I beg you will do neither until I have decided which I prefer.’

Mr. Cattermole smiled the smile of a man who has smiled at a thousand baronets. He drew from his case a roll of paper, and unrolled it upon the tea-table among the china, and the thing was so beautifully drawn, so tenderly coloured, so altogether decorative in its branching and quartering, that Lady Ashcombe gave a little cry of pleasure before she had the least idea of what it meant.

‘The descent,’ said Mr. Cattermole, ‘is established beyond any cavil. From Egbert of Wessex, through the line of the Mercian kings, to Sir Roger de Ashcombe, who came in with the Conqueror and was granted these lands. The chain is continuous. There is not a link wanting.’ He paused, and the pause was a work of art. ‘There is, however, a circumstance.’

‘There always is,’ said Sir Aubrey. ‘It is the only thing that makes a pedigree worth having. A descent without a circumstance is merely arithmetic. Go on.’

Mr. Cattermole coughed. ‘The circumstance concerns the person of the founder of your house. Sir Roger de Ashcombe, as the records now indisputably show, was not, in the beginning, a knight at all. He was—I must use the plain word—the cook.’

The room was silent. Lady Ashcombe set down her cup with a hand that trembled, and the cup rang against the saucer like a small bell rung at a funeral.

‘The cook,’ she repeated, in a voice from which all the roses had withered.

‘The cook,’ said Mr. Cattermole. ‘He was afterwards knighted upon the field, for it appears he was a man of prodigious personal courage, and he died at Hastings with the King’s name on his lips. But he came over in the galley with the pots and the spits, and his hands were the hands of a scullion. The arms of your house—the three covered cups—were, I am afraid, canting arms. They were granted to him because he was a cook, and because he had a genius for the table that was spoken of in three counties. I have brought the evidence. It is complete. It is, if I may say so, rather magnificent.’

‘Magnificent!’ cried Lady Ashcombe. ‘It is a catastrophe! We have had kings in this house. I have had kings in this house. I have entertained a bishop and a duchess in the same week, and I have said to them, with my own lips, that the Ashcombes were at Hastings. I did not say in what capacity. I did not think it necessary to specify the capacity!’

‘My dear,’ said Sir Aubrey, ‘you said nothing that was untrue. He was at Hastings. He was, in fact, the only man at Hastings who was doing anything useful. The rest were merely being killed, which any fool can do. He was keeping an army fed, and he was knighted for it, and he founded a family that has lasted eight hundred years, and I find that a great deal more remarkable than if he had been a cousin of the Conqueror. Anybody can be a cousin. It requires no talent whatever. I have dozens of cousins, and I would not trust one of them to boil an egg.’

‘Aubrey, you are not serious.’

‘I have never been more serious in my life, and I am enjoying myself so much that I am obliged to sit down.’ He did so, and took up the pedigree and looked at it as a man looks at a picture he has bought and is beginning to love. ‘Think of it, my dear.
