Mr. Edward Ashworth was a young man of respectable family and moderate fortune, who having passed his youth entirely in the country, found himself, at the age of four-and-twenty, introduced by his aunt, Mrs. Ingram, to the dissipations of a London season. His father’s estate, though unencumbered, was not large, and his own expectations were confined to a comfortable independence, which could not support the frequentation of Bond Street without some degree of caution. Mrs. Ingram, a widow of fashion and some discernment, had taken him in hand, partly from affection and partly from a conviction that a handsome young man with an air of modesty might be of use in her drawing-room.

Among the various young ladies presented to his acquaintance during his first weeks in town, Miss Clara Vane soon distinguished herself in his regard. She was the daughter of a widow, Mrs. Vane, who had taken a house in Harley Street for the season, and who moved in a circle of acquaintance sufficiently extensive to suggest a degree of consequence. Miss Vane was handsome, animated, and possessed of a ease of manner which Edward, unused to the confidence of London young ladies, found entirely captivating. He was the more struck by her, as she appeared wholly unconscious of her own attractions, and received his attentions with a frankness that seemed to speak of genuine feeling rather than design.

That Miss Vane was an heiress, he had no reason to doubt. Mrs. Ingram had mentioned her father, the late Mr. Vane, as a man who had left his family in very comfortable circumstances, and Edward, whose habits of observation had been formed in the country, where one’s income is commonly known to all one’s neighbours, saw no cause to question the inference. The style in which the Vanes lived, their carriage, their furniture, the evident expensiveness of Miss Vane’s gowns, all confirmed the impression. He permitted himself, therefore, to indulge the hope that an attachment to her might be not merely a matter of the heart, but one of prudence likewise, and such a union of sentiment and interest as every young man of sense must desire.

Miss Vane, for her part, had formed a not dissimilar estimate of Mr. Ashworth. Mrs. Ingram’s establishment in Brook Street, her evident station in society, and the casual references she made to her nephew’s estate in Wiltshire, had led Clara to suppose him a man of at least three thousand a year. She was the more inclined to think well of him, as his manners were unlike those of the fashionable young men she generally met, being free from either pretension or indifference, and marked by a sincerity which she found very agreeable. Her own situation was not, in truth, what it appeared. Her father had been a man of generous disposition but imprudent habits, and his affairs, at his death, were found to be involved to a degree which had astonished even his own family. The house in Harley Street, the carriage, the establishment maintained during the season, were the result of an effort which Mrs. Vane considered necessary to the placing of her daughters, and which was supported by the gradual realisation of such property as remained to them. Clara knew that their style of living was temporary, and that when the season concluded, they must retrench, but she supposed Mr. Ashworth’s circumstances to be such as would make the disparity of fortune, even if it existed, of little consequence.

Each, therefore, continued to cherish an illusion which a single conversation might have destroyed, and each was the more anxious to conceal their own circumstances, lest the other should be discouraged. Edward, who had been taught by his aunt that a young man must not be precipitate, endeavoured to ascertain Miss Vane’s expectations by indirect means, but his enquiries were of a nature so vague that they could elicit nothing more than an equally vague reply. He asked Mrs. Ingram whether she knew what Mr. Vane had been worth, and Mrs. Ingram, who knew very little of the matter, replied that she believed him to have been a man of considerable property, and that Mrs. Vane was thought to live in a style which bespoke a handsome income. This was quite sufficient for Edward, who was not disposed to be suspicious.

Clara, in her turn, attempted to discover the extent of Mr. Ashworth’s estate by remarking upon the country with which he was connected, and by expressing an interest in Wiltshire, which she had never visited. Edward, alarmed lest she should enquire too particularly into a property which, though pleasant, was not extensive, replied in terms so general as to convey an impression of reserve, which Clara interpreted as the modesty of a man who did not wish to boast of his wealth.

They were thrown together a great deal during the following weeks, at balls, at dinner parties, and in Mrs. Ingram’s drawing-room, where Clara called frequently, and where Edward was always to be found. Their conversations were long, and of a nature to increase their mutual regard, for each possessed a degree of good sense and natural feeling which made their intercourse agreeable to both. Edward found in Clara a warmth of temper and a liveliness of mind which he had never before encountered in a woman, and Clara discovered in Edward a steadiness of principle and a gentleness of disposition which she had been accustomed to regard as existing only in books.

It was at a ball given by Lady Mercer, however, that the progress of their attachment received its most material advancement, and its most dangerous check. Edward, having secured Clara’s hand for the second dance, found himself, as they stood up together, so entirely captivated by her appearance and her conversation, that he resolved, before the evening concluded, to declare himself.
