My uncle, Josiah Meakins, was a man who had made his money out of a bottle, and he was not ashamed of it, which was the source of both my admiration and my distress. The bottle was called Meakins’ Invigorating Tonic, and it had, in the course of fifteen years, travelled from a back-kitchen in Wolverhampton to every chemist’s shop and grocers’ shelf in the kingdom. It was, I am obliged to confess, largely treacle and water, with a little gentian and a good deal of capsicum, and its virtue lay not in the mixture but in the advertisement. My uncle had a genius for advertisement. He could take a plain statement of fact—that the tonic was a warming drink for cold mornings—and so dress it in testimonials, in before-and-after portraits, in the tearful gratitude of clergymen’s widows and the robust approval of retired sea-captains, that the public came to regard it as a specific for every ill that flesh is heir to. He had made, by the time I was twenty-five, a fortune of some eighty thousand pounds, and he had built a house at Edgbaston with a tower and a conservatory, and had bought a motor-car of the kind that is started by a handle and stopped by the grace of God.

I had been his right hand in the business from the beginning. I had written the testimonials—not invented them, you understand, but arranged them, polished them, given them the little touch of human nature that made them convincing. I had drawn up the circulars, I had designed the almanacs, I had even, in the early days, carried the sample cases from door to door. And all the time I had a kind of sickness at the back of my mind, a feeling that we were selling a lie in a bottle, and that the lie was none the less a lie because it was a profitable one. But I admired my uncle. I admired his energy, his good humour, his absolute freedom from cant. He never pretended that the tonic was a medicine. “It’s a drink,” he would say, “a warm drink with a bit of a bite in it. If it does you good, that’s your affair. If it does you harm, you can’t say I didn’t warn you.” And then he would go off into a roar of laughter, and slap me on the back, and tell me I was a gloomy young dog and wanted a dose of the tonic myself.

It was in the autumn of 1906 that he announced his intention of standing for Parliament. He had been approached, he said, by the Conservative Association of Midborough, a manufacturing town in the Midlands, where the seat had become vacant through the death of the sitting member. Midborough was a place of small workshops and narrow streets, of chapels and public-houses, of a population that was largely employed in the making of boots and the brewing of beer. My uncle had been born in a village five miles from Midborough, and had started life as a grocer’s boy in the town itself. He had a sentimental feeling about the place. “I’ll go back,” he said, “and I’ll tell ’em what I think of ’em. I’ll give ’em a bit of straight talk. They’ve had lawyers and brewers and colliery owners long enough. Now they’ll have a man who’s made something out of nothing, and who knows what it is to want a dinner.”

I was appalled. I had a vision of the election address, of the posters, of the inevitable cartoons in the local papers. I could see the opposition candidate, a thin, earnest barrister with a taste for statistics, holding up a bottle of Meakins’ Invigorating Tonic and asking the electors whether they proposed to be governed by a man who had made his money out of a patent medicine. I could see the Nonconformist conscience of Midborough rising in its wrath. I could see the whole thing ending in a scandal and a defeat, and my uncle’s name a byword for quackery.

“You can’t do it, uncle,” I said. “They’ll tear you to pieces.”

He looked at me with that queer, bright, obstinate eye of his. “They can tear away,” he said. “I’ve been torn before. I’ve had the whole medical profession on my back for ten years, and I’m still here. I’m not going to be ashamed of my tonic. It’s paid for this house, and that motor-car, and your salary, my boy. If I go into Parliament, I go as Meakins of the Tonic, and if they don’t like it, they can lump it.”

The campaign began in a whirl of posters. My uncle insisted on the tonic being advertised on every hoarding in Midborough. There was a picture of a stout, cheerful working man, with a glass of the tonic in his hand, and underneath the words: “A MIDBOROUGH MAN’S FRIEND. VOTE FOR MEAKINS.” There was another, more subtle, showing a pallid, dyspeptic-looking individual—intended, I fancy, for the opposition candidate—with the legend: “HE DOESN’T DRINK MEAKINS’. DO YOU WONDER?” I protested, but my uncle only laughed. “It’s the only language they understand,” he said. “You can’t talk economics to a man who’s got a bad stomach. You give him a bottle of something that warms him up, and he’ll listen to you for an hour.”

The meetings were extraordinary. My uncle had no gift of rhetoric, but he had a gift of something better—of plain, blunt, humorous speech that went straight to the hearts of his hearers. He stood on platforms in the schoolrooms and the drill-halls, with a glass of his own tonic on the table before him, and he talked about the price of boots and the wickedness of the licensing laws and the need for a bit of common sense in the House of Commons. He told them how he had started with a barrow and a box of bottles, and how he had built up the business by hard work and a belief in the public.
