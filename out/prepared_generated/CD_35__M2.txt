The soot was not merely on him; it was of him, woven into the very fiber of his skin and hair, a testament to the chimney that had swallowed him whole when he was no taller than a sack of flour. But the boy, whose name was Tobias, had long since ceased to be a mere bundle of black dust and starved ribs. He had run, with a desperation that bordered on the divine, from the clattering, smoke-filled hells of London, and had fallen, by a stroke of fate as blind as it was benevolent, into the lap of the water. It was there, upon the narrow, floating rooms of the barge *The Iron Duchess*, that Tobias found his second birth.

The barge-master, a man of such immense girth that he seemed to be the barge’s anchor rather than its pilot, was named Silas Grubb. He was a creature of thunderous voice and sudden, inexplicable gentleness, a combination that kept his crew in a perpetual state of nervous vigilance. His daughter, Elspeth, was a girl of fifteen, sharp-eyed and sharp-tongued, with hands that were always busy and a mind that was always turning. It was Elspeth who took the soot-stained, shivering orphan under her wing, not out of charity, for charity was a word Grubb considered a waste of breath, but out of a pragmatic recognition that a pair of extra hands on the tow-path was worth more than a prayer.

For five years, the canal was Tobias’s world. It was a world of gentle motion, of the slow, deliberate creep of lock after lock, of the smell of wet willow and river mud, and of the quiet, steady rhythm of the barge’s creaking timbers. He grew, not by inches, but by leaps and bounds, his bones stretching and hardening under the open sky. He learned to read, not from the dry, dusty books of a schoolroom, but from the almanacs and broadsheets that Grubb kept in a tin box, and from the stories Elspeth told, stories of kings and ghosts and the wide, unmeasured world beyond the Midlands. Elspeth’s voice, soft and clear as the water itself, became the only music he knew, and the words she spoke became the only language that mattered.

It was on a Tuesday in late November, when the mist lay so thick on the water that the world seemed to have been erased, leaving only the faint outline of the barge and the distant, mournful cry of a curlew, that the past came knocking. They were passing the lock at Millford, a place of grim reputation and slower-than-usual gates, when a figure emerged from the fog. It was a man, tall and lean, with a face like a crumpled paper bag and eyes that shone with a cold, predatory intelligence. He wore a cap pulled low over his forehead and a coat that had seen better decades.

Tobias, who was at the helm, felt a chill that had nothing to do with the November air. He knew that face. He had seen it in the mirror of the chimney, in the eyes of the master who had beaten him for eating too slow, who had laughed when he cried, who had called him a thing of dirt and nothing else. It was Mr. Vane, the assistant to the great sweep, the man who had kept the ledger of their sufferings and the keys to their cages.

"Stop her!" Vane’s voice cut through the mist, sharp as a knife. "I know you, you little rat. I know your stink, Tobias. I know your face, even under that soot."

Tobias froze. His hands, which had been so steady for five years, began to tremble. He looked at Elspeth, who stood beside him, her face pale as the mist. He looked at Grubb, who was below, in the hold, asleep. He looked at the water, dark and still, and he thought, for the first time in years, of the chimney.

Vane stepped onto the barge, his boots slipping on the wet planks. He moved with a silence that was more terrifying than any shout. "The master will be pleased," he said, his voice dropping to a whisper that seemed to vibrate in Tobias’s bones. "He has been looking for you. He says you are worth a good sum of money. He says you have grown strong. He says you are ready."

Tobias did not answer. He looked at Vane, and for a moment, the past and the present collided. He saw himself as a boy, small and black, running from a shadow that would never end. But then, he looked at Elspeth. She was looking at him, not with fear, but with a fierce, unyielding determination. She was the only light he had known for five years. She was the only voice that had ever spoken to him as if he were a man, not a thing.

"You don't own him," Elspeth said. Her voice was low, but it carried the weight of iron. "You never did. He is free."

Vane laughed, a dry, rattling sound. "Free? He is property. He is bound by contract. He is mine."

"No," Tobias said.

It was a single word, but it was a word that had been building inside him for five years, a word that had been forged in the fire of his suffering and tempered in the cold water of his freedom. He looked at Vane, and he saw not a master, but a man. A small, mean, frightened man who was trying to hold onto a past that had already rotted away.

Tobias reached out and took Elspeth’s hand. Her hand was warm, and it was real. He looked at the water, and he saw not a trap, but a road. A road that led away from the chimney, away from the soot, away from the shadow.

"Let me go," Tobias said, his voice steady now, clear as the bell of a distant church. "Or I will push you into the water."
