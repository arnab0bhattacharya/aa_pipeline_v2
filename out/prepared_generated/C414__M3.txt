The rain had not ceased for three days. It drew a grey curtain across the market square of Avesbury, blurring the sharp edges of the coaching inn and the stone spire of St. Agnes. Inside his rented rooms above the apothecary, Herr Thomas Keller stood by the window, his breath misting the glass. Below him, the cobbles were slick and dark, reflecting the gloomy November afternoon. He listened to the muffled clatter of hooves and the distant, rhythmic drip of water from the eaves. He had lived in Avesbury for six years now, long enough for the townsfolk to have grown accustomed to his guttural vowels and his strict, unbending posture at the pianoforte. Yet, familiarity had not bred warmth. He was still the German, a foreigner who had come to teach their daughters the mysteries of counterpoint and the scales of Mozart.

A heavy knock at the door interrupted his reverie. It was not the timid knock of a pupil arriving for a lesson, nor the familiar tread of Mrs. Finch, his landlady, bringing his tea. It was a deliberate, official sound. Thomas crossed the small parlour and opened the door. On the landing stood Mr. Arthur Pendleton, a man whose presence always seemed to lower the temperature of the room. He was a magistrate, a man of substantial property, and father to Clara, Thomas’s most promising student. Beside him stood Mr. Josiah Thorne, a lean, sharp-eyed man who owned the textile mill on the river. Both men wore expressions of severe displeasure.

"Herr Keller," Pendleton began, his voice a deep, resonant baritone that carried the weight of civic authority. "We must speak with you. May we come in?"

Thomas stepped aside, gesturing for them to enter. He noted the way Pendleton’s eyes swept the small room, taking in the neat stacks of sheet music, the worn velvet armchair, and the small portrait of Beethoven that hung above the fireplace. It was an inspection, not a conversation. He closed the door and turned to face them, his hands clasped loosely behind his back.

"We shall come straight to the point," Pendleton said, refusing the offer of a chair. "There is a matter of grave concern. It concerns your character, Herr Keller, and your influence over the young ladies of this town."

Thomas felt a cold knot form in his stomach. He had been expecting something of the sort. The political winds blowing across Europe were chilly, and in a provincial English town, that chill manifested as paranoia. He had read the London papers. He knew of the arrests, the suspensions of habeas corpus, the fear of revolution. He had also heard the whispers in Avesbury, the murmured suspicions that he harboured radical sympathies.

"Mr. Thorne’s daughter, Margaret, has been asking questions," Pendleton continued. "Questions about the rights of man, about the distribution of wealth. She claims these notions came from her music lessons."

"That is absurd," Thomas said, his voice quiet but firm. "We speak only of music. Of harmony, of tempo, of the structure of a sonata."

"Nonetheless," Thorne interjected, his voice thin and reedy, "Margaret is a young woman of a delicate and impressionable nature. Her mother is quite distressed. And she is not the only one. There is talk, Herr Keller. Talk that you are a Jacobin, a troublemaker."

Thomas felt a flicker of anger ignite in his chest, but he kept his face impassive. Jacobin. The word was a relic, a bogeyman used to frighten children and magistrates alike. He was a German liberal, a man who believed in a united fatherland and constitutional government, not a bloodthirsty revolutionary. But he knew that to these men, the distinction was meaningless. A radical was a radical.

"I am a musician," Thomas said. "I teach the pianoforte. I have no interest in fomenting unrest."

"Then you will have no objection to signing a declaration," Pendleton said, producing a folded paper from his coat pocket. He held it out to Thomas, who did not take it. "A simple affirmation of your loyalty to the Crown, and a disavowal of any radical or seditious sentiments. It is a formality, really. A way to put these rumours to rest and reassure the parents of your pupils."

Thomas looked at the paper, its crisp whiteness a stark contrast to the gloom of the room. He felt the weight of the moment pressing down on him. He thought of Clara, her bright, eager face, her nimble fingers dancing across the keys. He thought of his life in Avesbury, his modest but honest living, the quiet routine of lessons and practice. If he refused, he would lose everything. The parents would withdraw their daughters. He would be ruined, forced to leave the town in disgrace. He would be adrift once more, an exile in a world that had no place for him.

He reached out and took the paper. He unfolded it and read the words, written in Pendleton’s precise, looping hand. It was a brief document, a few lines declaring his unwavering loyalty to Her Majesty the Queen, his respect for the laws and constitution of England, and his utter abhorrence of all doctrines that sought to subvert them. It ended with a space for his signature.

The silence in the room was absolute, broken only by the steady drumming of rain against the window. Thomas read the words a second time, then a third. He thought of his father, a modest court musician in Weimar, who had taught him that a man’s honour was the one thing he could never afford to surrender. He thought of the books he had read, the ideals he had held in his youth, the belief that all men were created equal and deserved a voice in their governance. He thought of the students he had taught, not just to play notes, but to listen, to think, to feel. Clara, playing a Chopin nocturne with a tenderness that had brought tears to his eyes. Margaret, struggling with a Bach prelude, her brow furrowed in concentration. These were not subversive acts. They were acts of beauty, of humanity.
