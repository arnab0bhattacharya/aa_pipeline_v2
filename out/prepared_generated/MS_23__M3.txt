It is a melancholy truth, deeply engraven upon the heart of humanity, that the earth we inhabit is a theatre of unceasing sorrow, where the brightest spirits are extinguished by the very breath of pestilence, and the fairest structures of human hope are levelled by the unrelenting hand of calamity. I had survived the great desolation—that long and terrible sickness which had emptied the towns and shrouded the once populous counties of our island in a silence deeper than that of the grave—and I was left, a lone and desolate being, in the midst of ruins. I had returned to the village of my birth, Heathmore, situated in a sequestered valley of Oxfordshire, where the ancient spire of the parish church still lifted itself, though shattered, above the blackened elms, and where the cottages, though roofless and forsaken, seemed to murmur of the days when joy and security dwelt within their walls. It was here, amid this universal desolation, that I had assumed the office of schoolmistress; not from any vain ambition, but because my heart, overflowing with grief and needing an object upon which to expend its lingering affections, found solace in the instruction of the few children who yet remained.

Every morning, as the grey dawn broke over the eastern hills, I ascended the belfry of the old schoolhouse and rang the bell. Its tones, once joyous and familiar, now echoed through the empty streets with a mournful and solemn cadence, as if it bewailed the departed multitude whose steps it had once summoned to the pursuits of knowledge and innocence. Three children only answered its call—three little ones, pale and fragile, yet bearing in their eyes the light of undying intelligence. Clara, the eldest, was a girl of surpassing sweetness; her mild blue eyes were softened by a pensiveness far beyond her years, and her voice, low and tremulous, seemed attuned to the melancholy of our condition. Arthur, a boy of ten, was bold and ardent, with a spirit that defied the gloom around him; and little Edmund, the youngest, clung to my hand with a confiding tenderness that drew tears from my eyes whenever I looked upon him. These were my pupils, my children, the sole remaining objects of my care; and for them I toiled, and for them I endured the weight of existence.

It was upon a morning in the late autumn, when the winds howled through the leafless branches and the rain beat against the shattered windows of the schoolroom, that a stranger arrived. He came on horseback, his garments drenched with the storm, and his countenance bearing the stamp of authority and worldly wisdom. He announced himself as an inspector, sent by the remnant of government that yet lingered in the southern counties, to close the school and remove the children to a central establishment. I received him in the schoolroom, where the three children sat at their humble desks, their slates before them, and their eyes fixed upon me with an expression of innocent wonder. The inspector, whose name was Manning, was a man of cold and measured speech; his face was lined with care, but it was the care of one who has learned to stifle feeling beneath the mask of policy. He looked around the room with a scrutinizing eye, and then, turning to me, said, "Madam, your devotion is laudable, but it is misplaced. The government has decreed that all scattered institutions shall be dissolved, and the children removed to a central establishment. This school must be closed."

I felt a cold shudder pass through my frame as he spoke; my heart recoiled with a horror which I could not suppress. "Close this school!" I exclaimed, and my voice trembled with the violence of my emotion. "Sir, you cannot mean it. Look at these children—look at their eager faces, their thirsty minds. You would tear them from the only home they know, from the only instruction they can receive, and consign them to a distant establishment, where they will be strangers among strangers, and where the tender care which I bestow upon them will be replaced by the cold formalities of an institution. I implore you, sir, reconsider your decree. Let the bell continue to ring; let these children continue to gather beneath this roof. It is all that remains to us of the old world—the old world of kindness and of knowledge. Will you destroy even this?"

Manning regarded me with a look of pity, but it was mingled with a stern resolve. "Madam," he replied, "the government has spoken. The scattered remnants of the population must be consolidated. The children will be well cared for; they will be educated, fed, and clothed. Your school is but a relic of a system that has passed away. You cannot cling to the shadows of the old world. The future demands a new order."

"A new order!" I repeated, and the tears gushed from my eyes. "What order can replace that of affection and duty? What future can be built upon the ruins of love? You speak of consolidation, of institutions, of systems; but you forget that the heart of man cannot be consolidated, and that the soul of a child needs not the cold hand of an institution, but the warm heart of a teacher who loves it. These children are mine—mine by the bond of sorrow and of care. You may take them from me by force, but you will never sever the tie that binds us. And as for the bell—yes, I will cease to ring it; but its tones will echo in our hearts long after you have silenced it forever."

The children, who had listened in silence, now began to weep. Clara came to me and threw her arms around my neck, sobbing as if her heart would break. Arthur stood erect, his eyes flashing with a spirit that seemed to kindle into defiance, but his lips trembled.
