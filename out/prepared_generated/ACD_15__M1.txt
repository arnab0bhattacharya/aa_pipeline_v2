It was upon a raw and drizzling afternoon in the late autumn of the year ’92 that a visitor of singular appearance presented himself at my door. He was a man of perhaps fifty, with a face like a well-worn map of some troubled country, and he held himself with the stiff, wounded dignity of one who has suffered a profound injustice. His name, he informed me, was Mr. Josiah Pargeter, and his profession, that of an inventor.

“Dr. Harker,” said he, after I had relieved him of his damp ulster, “I am a man of science, a man of method. I have spent three years and the greater part of my small fortune upon the development of a new electrical lamp—a lamp of unparalleled efficiency and steady light. I have here,” he continued, tapping a leather portfolio he carried, “the definitive drawings. They have not left my possession.”

“And the problem, Mr. Pargeter?” I asked, for his manner suggested that the portfolio was both his shield and the source of his grievance.

“The problem, sir, is a man. A man named Elias Thorne. A clever fellow, I grant you, but with the soul of a jackal. He was in my confidence. He saw my work, he handled my prototypes. And now, a mere week ago, he has vanished. Vanished like a phantom! But not before he has, I am convinced, stolen the very essence of my design.”

I confess my curiosity was piqued. “But if the drawings remain with you, how could he have stolen the design?”

Mr. Pargeter leaned forward, his eyes burning with a zealot’s fire. “By memory, Dr. Harker! By a single, perfect glance. He is a draughtsman of some skill. I believe he committed the entire schematic to memory in the space of a few moments. He is even now, I have no doubt, recreating my work and preparing to sell it as his own.”

The case, as he laid it out, seemed to possess a certain dramatic logic, yet it was built upon a foundation of the most insubstantial conjecture. It was the sort of matter that called for more than a physician’s eye; it required a mind trained in the detection of fraud and the weighing of evidence. I knew of but one man for such a task.

My friend, the consulting detective Mr. Julian St. John, received us in his chambers overlooking the Strand. He was in a state of languid repose amidst a chaos of chemical apparatus and scattered papers, but his grey eyes sharpened with interest as Mr. Pargeter repeated his story. He listened without a word, his long, nervous fingers steepled beneath his chin.

When the inventor had finished, St. John rose and began to pace the room. “A fascinating proposition,” he murmured. “A crime of pure intellect, committed without a tool or a trace. You say this Mr. Thorne’s workshop is in Clerkenwell?”

“It is. Or rather, it was. He has cleared it out.”

“Excellent,” said St. John, with a sudden briskness that transformed his entire aspect. “Then we shall begin our investigation there. The scene of the crime, or the scene of the flight, is always the most eloquent of witnesses.”

A hansom cab deposited us before a grimy brick building on a narrow, cobbled lane in Clerkenwell. The air was thick with the smell of coal smoke and damp wool. Mr. Pargeter produced a key, and we entered the late Mr. Thorne’s workshop. The sight that met our eyes was one of frantic disorder. Tools lay scattered upon the benches, sheets of paper were strewn across the floor, and a heavy iron safe in the corner stood ajar, its door hanging open on its hinges.

“You see?” cried Mr. Pargeter, gesturing wildly. “He has been here! He has taken what he needed and fled!”

St. John made no reply. He moved through the room with a cat-like quiet, his eyes missing nothing. He ran a finger along a dusty workbench, examined the scattered tools—a set of fine calipers, a jeweller’s loupe, a half-soldered brass fitting—and peered into the empty safe. I saw him stoop to pick up a single, small object from the floor, which he slipped into his waistcoat pocket with an air of finality.

“Mr. Pargeter,” he said at last, his voice calm and measured, “you are a man of method. Pray, describe for me the precise sequence of events on the evening Mr. Thorne is supposed to have viewed your drawings.”

“Well,” the inventor began, “we were taking a glass of port in my study. The drawings were on my desk. He leaned over them for perhaps a minute, no more. He made some admiring comment about the wiring, and then we spoke of other things. It was only the next day, when I heard he had not appeared at his own premises, that the terrible suspicion began to form in my mind.”

“And the safe in his workshop,” St. John continued, turning his gaze upon the open iron box. “It is a Chubb, is it not? A formidable lock. It shows no signs of having been forced. The door is not bent, the bolts are not sheared. It was opened with its own key.”

Mr. Pargeter’s face fell. “But… but he must have taken the key with him!”

“Possibly,” said St. John, a faint smile playing about his lips. “Or perhaps the key is still here.” He crossed to the mantelpiece, upon which sat a dusty clay pipe and a tin of tobacco. He lifted the tobacco tin, and there, nestled beside it, was a small, brass key. “A hurried departure is a messy business, Mr. Pargeter. It is rarely as neat as the fleeing party would wish.”

The inventor stared, his mouth agape. “I… I do not understand.”

“Let us consider the alternative,” St. John said, turning to face us both. “Let us suppose that Mr. Elias Thorne is not a thief, but a victim. A man who has been ruined and is now in hiding. A man who was confronted with an accusation so monstrous, and from a source so respected, that he saw no recourse but to flee.”
