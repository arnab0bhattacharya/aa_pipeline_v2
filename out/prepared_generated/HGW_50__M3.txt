It has sometimes occurred to me that the great driving force of human society is not the hunger for truth, but a quite unparalleled diligence in avoiding it. We are, as a species, remarkably well insulated by polite fictions, and but for a small accident of chemistry in my own laboratory, I dare say I should never have noticed the fact at all. I had been engaged for some months in isolating the alkaloids of common British flora, and it was in the course of extracting the sap of the common stinging-nettle—a plant the botanical name of which I shall suppress, for reasons that will become abundantly clear—that I stumbled upon a remarkable isomer. It was a heavy, unctuous oil, faintly greenish in hue, possessing a bitter, metallic taste not unlike that of a copper coin. I had, in the usual spirit of careless enquiry, tasted a minute drop of the substance. For several minutes I felt no ill effect whatever. Then my laboratory assistant, a decent but incurably idle fellow named Bunting, asked me whether I had been the one to break a rather favourite measuring cylinder the week before. I found myself, to my utter astonishment, saying "Yes," before I had even considered the question.

I was, I need hardly say, profoundly startled. I had fully intended to maintain my customary lie. Upon reflection, I realized I had been quite unable to formulate the falsehood. The neural impulse to speak the literal truth had entirely overridden the machinery of evasion. Further experiments upon myself and, with a certain unscientific cunning, upon Bunting’s afternoon tea, confirmed the effect. The substance, which I shall call Veritase, temporarily paralysed the higher centres of imaginative deception. One retained one's manners, one's grammar, and one's memory, but one lost, completely and irrevocably, the power to dissemble. It was a few days after this discovery that my Aunt Sophia wrote to command my presence at a dinner party at her house in Campden Hill Road, Kensington.

My Aunt Sophia was a woman of formidable respectability and considerable social ambition. She had recently maneuvered herself into the acquaintance of a certain Lady Potheringay, a person of brittle title and immense social weight, and was giving this dinner expressly to consolidate the connection. My aunt’s husband, my Uncle Thomas, was a stockbroker of comfortable but somewhat compromised ethics, and I had long suspected that a great deal of their household income depended upon transactions that would not bear a very harsh illumination. I viewed the approaching dinner with the customary dreary apprehension of a young man of small means in the presence of expensive upholstery. Then, reflecting upon the vial of Veritase in my waistcoat pocket, it occurred to me that here was a unique opportunity for a sociological experiment of the most fascinating kind.

I had dissolved a generous quantity of the oil in a small phial of sherry, and when the butler had retired to fetch the soup, I contrived to empty it into the decanter of claret which stood upon my aunt’s sideboard. The dinner was a moderately ostentatious affair. The dining room was heavy with the scent of forced roses and the faint, melancholy smell of new upholstery. Lady Potheringay sat on my aunt’s right, a large, blond woman with a somewhat imperious stare, draped in pearls that looked as though they had been strung by a determined enemy. On her left sat Sir James Potheringay, a gentleman who gave the impression of having been recently boiled. Beside me was Miss Winifred Vance, a young woman of aggressive piety and a profile like a hatchet. The rest of the party was made up of a Canon, a solicitor, and two young men in the Guards who appeared to have been imported primarily for their hair.

The claret was circulated with the roast. I watched it go round with a good deal of internal suspense. It was not, I reflected, a very fine wine, but it had an excellent bouquet, and my aunt’s guests were not, as a rule, abstemious. For perhaps ten minutes the conversation proceeded along the usual lines of Kensington after-dinner chatter. Lady Potheringay inquired of the Canon whether he did not find the Bishop of London a little trying. The Canon, who had taken a deep draught of the claret, paused with his glass halfway to his lips. "I find him an intolerable, meddling ass," he said, with a calm, clear enunciation. "He has the intellect of a curate and the manners of a shopwalker. I only tolerate him because my wife hopes he will get me a deanery."

There was a moment of perfectly frosty silence. My aunt, who had been smiling in a preparatory sort of way, looked as though she had been struck in the face with a wet fish. Lady Potheringay stared. Then Miss Vance, who had also partaken of the claret, turned to me with an expression of bright, earnest inquiry. "Do you know, Mr. Penrith," she said, "I have always thought you a remarkably plain young man. Your nose is quite the wrong shape, and your conversation is exceedingly tedious. I cannot think why your aunt invites you."

I was, I confess, so delighted by the chemical efficiency of the alkaloid that I almost forgot to be offended. I replied, with equal candour, that I found her religious views narrow and her profile distinctly hatchet-like. We beamed at one another with the cheerful, released frankness of a pair of infants. The effect upon the room was now becoming quite catastrophic. The two young men in the Guards, who had been drinking deeply, were engaged in telling my aunt, with considerable detail, precisely what they thought of her furniture, her wine, and her niece. They pronounced the furniture pretentious, the wine abominable, and the niece a fool. One of them added that he had only come because he had been told there would be a supper, and that he was dashed if he meant to stand the old girl any longer.
