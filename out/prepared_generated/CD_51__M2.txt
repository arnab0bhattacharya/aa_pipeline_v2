The fog that descended upon the estuary of the Thames in the winter of 1834 was not merely a meteorological phenomenon, but a moral condition, thick as treacle and heavy with the damp, salt-rotten breath of the sea. It obscured the muddy banks of the river, hid the jagged teeth of the mudflats, and rendered the world invisible to all but those who possessed the peculiar, unblinking stare of a cat or a customs officer. It was in this suffocating mist that young Eliza Marlowe, daughter of the ferryman, stood upon the shivering pier of Gristle Point, watching the dark water lapping at the planks with a sound like the slow chewing of a hungry dog. Eliza was a creature of the tide; her face was pale as the moon when it dared to show itself, her eyes the colour of the deep water, and her spirit, though confined to the narrow limits of a ferryman’s daughter, possessed a restlessness that the fog could not entirely smother.

Her father, old Barnaby Marlowe, was a man of few words and fewer ambitions, a fact that sat upon his chest like a stone. He desired, with the fervour of a monk who has renounced the world, nothing more than a quiet life. He wanted no quarrels, no noise, no visitors, and certainly no interference from the Crown. Yet the Crown, that great, greedy, omnivorous beast, had its eyes upon Gristle Point, and with its eyes, it had sent Mr. Josiah Pumble, a customs officer of such insufferable stiffness that he seemed to be stuffed with whalebone rather than flesh. Mr. Pumble was a man who walked as if the ground were a debtor who owed him money, and who spoke in a voice that sounded as though he were reciting a list of forfeitures. He had taken a sudden, violent, and entirely unexplained fancy to Eliza, a fancy that manifested itself in the form of stiff bowing, a perpetual clearing of the throat, and a habit of offering her gloves that smelled strongly of lavender and officialdom.

But it was not Mr. Pumble who held the truest claim upon the hearts of the estuary’s romantic observers, for there was also Silas Vane, a smuggler of considerable notoriety and a boatman of unmatchable skill. Silas was the very opposite of Pumble; where the officer was stiff, Silas was fluid; where the officer was pale, Silas was ruddy; where the officer spoke of duty, Silas spoke of the wind. He was a man of the fog, a phantom who could appear and disappear with the turn of a tide, and who looked at Eliza not with the rigid appraisal of a magistrate, but with the hungry, slanting gaze of a man who knows the value of a hidden gem.

It was on a night of such profound darkness that the stars seemed to have been scrubbed from the sky, that both men found themselves upon the pier, driven there by the twin engines of desire and necessity. The fog was so dense that one could not see one’s own hand before one’s face, yet the two men stood there, separated by a distance of perhaps six feet, and a chasm of moral character so vast it could have swallowed the river whole.

Mr. Pumble was the first to speak, his voice trembling with a mixture of cold and indignation. “Marlowe,” he cried, his voice echoing strangely in the enclosed space of the fog, “I have a warrant. I have a boat to inspect. A black-hulled craft, low in the water, moving against the current. I have tracked it for three hours. I will not be thwarted by weather, nor by sentiment, nor by the indifference of a common ferryman. I require your boat. I require it now.”

Old Barnaby, who had been standing in the shadow of the pier-post, shuffling his boots on the wet wood, looked up with an expression of profound irritation. “The boat is not for hire, sir,” he grumbled, his voice thick with the phlegm of the estuary. “And the fog is too thick for navigation. Any man who goes out in this fog is either mad or a fool. And if you are a fool, Mr. Pumble, you will drown, and it will be the end of my quiet life. I have no use for your warrants. I have no use for your boats. I have a use for sleep, and you are preventing it.”

But before Pumble could unleash a torrent of legalistic fury, a sound came from the water. It was a soft, rhythmic knocking, like a knuckle on a door, but repeated with a persistence that was almost musical. From the fog, a figure emerged, moving with a grace that seemed to defy the clumsy reality of the mud and the water. It was Silas Vane. He did not step onto the pier; he seemed to glide, his boat, a sleek, low thing that looked like a seal’s back, pressing itself against the pilings.

“Barnaby,” Silas called, his voice warm and easy, cutting through the fog like a knife through silk. “It’s a fine night for a swim, but I suspect you prefer to stay on dry land. I have a cargo of spirits, lighter than air and sweeter than honey, and I need a passage to the other side before the tide turns. I don’t need your boat, Barnaby, I have my own. But I need your eyes. The fog is thick, and the channel is treacherous. I need a guide who knows the mud. I need a man who has no fear of the dark.”

Eliza, who had been watching from the shadow of the hut, stepped forward. The fog parted around her, as if it were ashamed of her presence. She looked at Pumble, who was turning red with rage, and then at Silas, who was smiling with a charm that was as dangerous as the current below.
