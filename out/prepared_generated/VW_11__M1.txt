The light, thin as skimmed milk, lay upon the spines of the books in the drawing-room, and upon the small brass tray where the coffee things had been left; it fell, too, upon Eleanor’s hands as she smoothed the rumpled programme on the table before her, smoothing not the paper, but the thought, the worry, the whole of it, which would not lie flat. Six o’clock. The hour of the concert was a dark, solid thing on the horizon, and the hours before it were a grey, churning sea. She had been on her feet since dawn, first with the viola player, a dear, gifted man with nerves like spun glass, who was convinced the hall was too cold for his instrument, then with the chairs—two hundred of them, hard, wooden, indifferent chairs—which must be arranged not in rows, but in welcoming arcs, a gesture of hospitality to an empty room. And through it all, the other thing, the silent, heavy thing that sat with her in the cab and stood with her in the wings: the fact of Arthur’s loss. He had not told her, not in words, but the truth of it was in the way he had looked at the morning paper, in the sudden, brittle quality of his jokes at breakfast. A thousand pounds, perhaps. Or two. A sum that was not a sum, but a hole in the fabric of their lives, a cold draught that she felt on her skin though the sun was shining. She must not think of it. The viola player. The chairs. The lighting.

At the hall, the air was thick with the scent of beeswax and the cold, mineral smell of old stone. She moved through the shafts of dusty light that fell from the high windows, a ghost in her own creation. “A little more to the left, Mr. Davies,” she said, her voice sounding thin and far away, “so that the light catches the wood of the piano.” Mr. Davies, the hall-keeper, a man who had seen a thousand concerts come and go and was impressed by none of them, gave the grand piano a slight, resentful shove. It seemed to Eleanor a monstrous, sleeping beast, and she felt a sudden, absurd pity for it, for the music it would soon be made to disgorge into this great, waiting space. She thought of the money, again. The tickets, the printing, the orchestra’s fee, a river of guineas flowing steadily away from them. Arthur had said, “It’s for a good cause, my dear,” but his voice had lacked its usual conviction, and she had known then that the cause was not the hospital for children, but his own desperate need for a triumph, for a full house that would somehow, magically, fill the emptiness in his accounts.

Now, the evening. The great doors were open, and a stream of people flowed in, a current of black coats and evening capes, of scent and murmured greetings, and the whole vast, hollow place was suddenly warm and alive. Eleanor stood near the back, a small, still point in the swirling tide, and watched. She watched them take their seats, the rustle of silk, the little nods and smiles, the settling of bodies into the hard wooden chairs she had so carefully arranged. And she saw, not the people of the moment, but the people of twenty years. There was Lady Pargiter, whose face was a mask of rouged astonishment, but whose eyes were the same bright, curious eyes that had watched Eleanor’s debut; and there was Mr. Otway, who had once been young and had written her a poem, and who now sat stooped and grey, his programme held at arm’s length. She knew the history of their lives as one knows the history of a house; the marriages, the deaths, the quiet disasters, the small, enduring loyalties. They were not an audience; they were a congregation, and she was its uneasy priestess. She felt a sudden, piercing tenderness for them all, for the shared, unspoken current of years that flowed between them, and for the fragile, beautiful evening they had come here to create together.

Then the lights dimmed. A hush fell, not of silence, but of expectation, a thousand held breaths. The conductor, a small, fierce man with a great white mane, mounted the podium and raised his baton. Eleanor felt her own breath stop. The first note, a single, clear violin, hung in the darkness. It was a thread of sound, and it seemed to stitch the whole room together. She looked at the faces, illuminated now by the glow from the stage, and saw them soften, their expressions of social vigilance melting into something simpler, more vulnerable. Lady Pargiter’s mask had gone; she was just a woman listening. Mr. Otway had closed his eyes. The music flowed on, a great, dark river of sound, and Eleanor felt the worry in her chest begin to loosen its grip. It did not vanish, but it was subsumed, made a part of this larger harmony. Arthur’s loss, the cold draught, the empty seats that might have been—it was all there, but it was held within the music, given a shape, a resonance. She looked towards the stage, but she did not see the players. She saw only the light on the polished wood of the piano, and she felt, for a moment, that the music was not being made by them, but was rising up from the very floorboards, from the stone walls, from the shared, secret histories of all the people sitting there in the dark. And she was a part of it, a single, listening note in the great, unfolding chord.
