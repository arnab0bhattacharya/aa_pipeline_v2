The bells of the Cathedral of San Felipe had not stopped ringing since dawn, but their tolling had lost its triumphant pitch. What had begun as a bright, insistent brazen voice declaring a new epoch now sounded to Mateo Vargas like a frantic, hollow clanging. It was the sound of a dying animal. From the second-floor window of the *Clarion* newspaper office, Mateo watched the Plaza de Armas below. The cobblestones, washed by a sudden tropical downpour an hour prior, gleamed like dark mirrors, reflecting the fractured gray sky. In those puddles, the blood of the failed revolution was already washing away into the gutters.

Only yesterday afternoon, Mateo had stood at this same window, watching General Estrada’s rebel forces march down the Avenue of the Republic, their boots striking the wet stones in perfect unison. The men had looked like titans, invincible in their frayed uniforms and wide-brimmed hats, their rifles held high. Mateo had felt the electricity of the moment, the sheer, overwhelming gravity of history, and he had rushed back to his desk to pour his soul into lead. He had written an editorial that would, he believed, echo through the ages of the Republic of Valverde. He had titled it "The Dawn of the True Citizens."

He had called President Sotillo a decrepit tyrant, a vampire feeding on the marrow of the nation. He had praised General Estrada as the sword of justice, the inevitable reckoning for decades of corruption and greed. The article had been set in type by his trembling but eager assistant, Felipe, and the single-sheet broadside had been distributed by boys to every café, cantina, and government office in the capital of San Esteban. For a few glorious hours, Mateo had tasted the sweet nectar of vindication. The revolt had seemed unstoppable, the capital entirely in rebel hands.

But the morning light brought the distant rumble of artillery from the eastern hills, and by noon, the sound of sporadic gunfire crept closer to the city center. Estrada’s triumphant march had been a mirage. Government loyalists, reinforced by General Montoya’s brigade from the southern provinces, had counterattacked with a viciousness that caught the rebels entirely off guard. Now, as the sun began to dip behind the jagged peaks of the Sierra Madre, Mateo knew that Estrada was dead, shot against a wall in the fruit market, and Sotillo’s men were filtering back into the city like a poison seeping into the blood.

Mateo turned from the window. The offices of the *Clarion* were quiet. Felipe had gone home hours ago, his face pale with terror, muttering prayers to the Virgin. The printing press sat in the corner of the room, a hulking iron beast that had churned out Mateo’s doom. On the desk lay a copy of the morning edition, the bold black headlines screaming his treason for all the world to see. He picked up the paper, his fingers tracing the rough texture of the cheap pulp. His name was right there, beneath the title, an unambiguous signature of his rebellion.

He walked to the heavy oak wardrobe in the corner of the office and pulled it open. Inside, behind a stack of old ledgers, lay a leather satchel. It contained three changes of clothes, a false passport bearing the name of a French merchant, and a wad of American dollars he had been saving for three years. He had prepared the bag two months ago, when he had first begun to conspire with Estrada’s agents. The bag represented his escape. The southern border with Peru was only a week’s ride away. He could be in Lima by the end of the month, sipping coffee in a European café, safe from Sotillo’s wrath.

He carried the satchel to the desk and set it down heavily. He stared at it, then at the door, then back at the satchel. He thought of his wife, Elena, and his young daughter, Sofia, who were at his brother’s estate in the countryside. They were safe, for now. If he ran, they would be safe. If he stayed, Sotillo’s secret police would not only come for him; they would burn his brother’s house to the ground to ferret out any sympathizers. The calculus of survival was brutally simple.

Mateo unbuckled the satchel and took out the false passport. He looked at the photograph, a sepia-toned image of a man with a trimmed beard and spectacles, looking nothing like his own clean-shaven, sharp-featured face. He would need to shave his head, perhaps scar his cheek. He would need to become a ghost. He thought of Lima, of Paris, of New York. He saw himself walking down broad, clean avenues, a free man, a survivor. But in this vision, he was mute. He had no voice, no pen. He was a shadow, a man who had abandoned his own words.

He picked up the morning edition of the *Clarion* again. He read the first paragraph aloud, his voice barely a whisper in the empty room. "The hour of liberation is upon us. The tyrant’s grip weakens, and the true citizens of Valverde rise to reclaim their birthright." The words rang hollow now, pathetic in their optimism, but they were his. He had written them with a conviction that transcended the immediate success or failure of Estrada’s men. He had written them because he believed them, because the truth of Sotillo’s corruption was an absolute, regardless of who held the presidential palace.

If he fled, he would be admitting that his words were merely contingent, that they only held power if backed by the barrel of a gun. If he fled, he would be saying that the pen was indeed mightier than the sword, but only when the swords were on his side. The thought disgusted him. He was a writer, an editor. He had chosen this profession because he believed in the enduring power of ideas, in the slow, grinding victory of truth over brute force. To run now was to betray not just his cause, but his very identity.
