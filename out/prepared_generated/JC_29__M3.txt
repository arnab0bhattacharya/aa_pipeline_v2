She had been a year in that valley before she understood the colour of its light. It was not the light of England, which falls with a grey, forgiving equality upon all things, but a thing heavy and golden, thick with the dust of the barren hills, penetrating the very substance of the leaves. The house, a long, low building of dried mud and timber, stood on a ledge of the mountain, and below it the coffee bushes spread in dark, rigid lines down to the stream, which was no stream at all but a mere thread of sluggish moisture in the dry season, a roaring, yellow destroyer when the rains came. Her husband, Trevenna, had built the place with the immense, sordid labour of the people of the country, and he ruled over it with a fierce, sleepless concentration, as a man might rule a ship in a storm, or a prison, or some private hell of his own making. He was an engineer by trade, a man of iron furnaces and stark, smoke-begrimed realities, and he had brought her there from the decorous quietness of a London suburb, where the chimneys smoke softly and the air is full of an unresentful peace.

She had found herself alone in the profound, overwhelming silence of the hills, a silence that was not empty but heavy with the murmurs of a vast, indifferent nature. It was in this silence, which seemed to press upon the very eardrums, that Father Almeida had come to her. He was the only educated man within fifty miles, a priest of the old, weary civilization of the Spaniards, who lived in a ruinous adobe hut near the white, sun-blistered church in the village below. He was a little, frail man, with a sallow face and eyes that held the profound, pathetic melancholy of a creature accustomed to the endless, unresisted defeats of life. He spoke English with a precise, halting accent, having learned it, he said, from a Cornish miner who had died of fever in the lower valley years ago. He carried in his breast pocket a much-worn volume of Voltaire, which he read with a puzzled, ironic detachment, as if the brilliant, mocking logic of the Frenchman was a strange, exotic plant he was trying to cultivate in the arid soil of his own faith. To her, in her isolation, he became a refuge. They would sit on the verandah in the heavy afternoons, watching the mists gather in the gorges, and he would talk to her of the history of the world, of the Romans, of the English in India, of the great, unaccountable tides of human folly that sweep men away from their moorings into acts of terrible and irrevocable darkness. He never spoke of his God as a personal saviour, but rather as a vast, silent observer, a patient, sorrowful witness to the infinite cruelties of the earth. He seemed to her like a man who had once looked upon some terrible truth, and who had never quite recovered from the sight.

Then the revolution came. It did not come as a storm comes, with a gathering of clouds and a rushing of the wind, but as a creeping, insidious fever comes to a sick man, with whispers and shadows and the sudden, inexplicable barking of dogs in the night. The General, a swarthy, silent man with a scarred face and a sombrero pulled low over his eyes, arrived one morning with a ragged band of followers, their horses blown and lathered, their rifles rusty with the sweat of the tropics. They took the village, and the sound of their coming was a dull, distant popping, like the snapping of dry twigs, echoed by the mountains. The valley, which had been a place of profound, sleepy peace, was transformed into a zone of furtive, menacing glances and sudden, inexplicable silences. The Indians, who had worked in the coffee groves with a stolid, animal patience, vanished into the hills, leaving the ripe berries to rot on the bushes. The air, which had been heavy with the scent of the drying beans, became tainted with a faint, acrid smell of smoke and the peculiar, sweetish odour of decay.

Trevenna, who had received the news with a dark, lowering face, worked himself into a state of rigid, white-lipped fury. He was a man who demanded that the world be obedient to his will, and the intrusion of this chaotic, barbaric force into his ordered domain was an affront to his very soul. He had barred the shutters of the house, and had armed his half-caste overseer and two other men with rifles, and he paced the verandah like a caged animal, his eyes fixed on the village below, where a column of smoke rose straight up into the windless sky. He had spoken of the priest with a bitter, contemptuous anger, as if the old man were somehow responsible for the madness that had seized the valley. "The old fool," he had said, his voice thick with a rage that seemed to choke him. "He's been talking to them. He's been putting ideas into their heads. I'll have no more of his meddling here. The house is barred to him. Do you hear? I'll not have the fellow about the place." He had struck the verandah rail with his fist, a dull, heavy blow that made the dry wood crack. He had looked at her then, with the eyes of a man who expects a challenge, and who is prepared to crush it with the full, brutal weight of his authority.

She had said nothing. She had learned, in that year of isolation, the profound, saving wisdom of silence, the futility of opposing a force so blind and so concentrated. But she had thought of Father Almeida, with his gentle, ironical smile and his worn Voltaire, and a great, pitying sadness had come over her, a sadness that was not for the priest alone, but for all men, for the whole, tragic, stumbling pageant of humanity that moves so blindly through the brief, flickering light of its days.
