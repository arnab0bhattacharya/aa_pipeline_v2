The shop of Mr. Abel Thorne was a narrow, shadowed cavern wedged between a tobacconist and a seller of second-hand violins in that stretch of Charing Cross Road devoted to the bookish and the impecunious. It smelled of leather, dust, and the dry, sweet scent of decaying paper. It was in this quiet backwater of the metropolis that a peculiar business had begun to trouble my friend, Mr. Thorne, and he had at last applied to me for assistance.

“I am a man of books, Mr. Harwood,” he said, his long, ink-stained fingers steepled before him. He was a spare, precise man, with a face as pale and finely lined as the title page of an old folio. “My business is to preserve them, to pass them from one appreciative hand to another. It is not to play the part of a detective. And yet, I find I have no choice.”

He slid a slim volume across the worn counter. It was a copy of Purchas’s *Pilgrimes*, bound in sober calf. “This was returned yesterday by Mr. Silas Croft. A regular patron. A gentleman of quiet tastes and impeccable manners. He has been borrowing from me for three years. But lately… lately there has been a change.”

I took the book. It was, to my untrained eye, a handsome, if not remarkable, work. “A change, sir?”

“Look to page 183,” Thorne instructed, his voice low and urgent.

I did so. The page had been removed with a surgical neatness that was, in its own way, more shocking than any crude tearing. The cut was as clean as if made by a razor’s edge, so precise that one had to look closely to see the razor-thin line of the missing leaf. “Good heavens. A vandalism.”

“Precisely. And it is not the first.” From beneath the counter, Mr. Thorne produced three other volumes: a Dutch account of the East Indies, a German treatise on the Alpine passes, and a battered French atlas of the Americas. In each, a single page had been excised with the same unnerving skill. “Mr. Croft borrows a book for a week, returns it, and pays his fee without a word. But he leaves behind this… this surgery.”

“And the pages themselves? What was on them?” I inquired.

“That is what is so maddening,” Thorne said, a note of frustration creeping into his voice. “I have consulted my catalogues and my memory. The pages share no common theme. One was a description of the spice trade in Amboyna. Another, a chapter on the flora of the Tyrol. The third, a passage on the court of the Viceroy in Peru. The fourth, a map of the approaches to the city of Strasbourg.”

“A map?” I said, catching the one word that seemed to offer a purchase.

“Of course! A map!” Thorne struck the counter with the flat of his hand. “Each page, without exception, contains a map! Not always the principal subject of the page, but a small inset, a marginal sketch, a diagram of a city’s fortifications. But what can a man want with such disparate fragments? A map of a Dutch island, a mountain pass, a Spanish colonial capital, and a French river? It is a collection without rhyme or reason.”

“And Mr. Croft? What of him?”

“He is a man of about fifty, with the stooped shoulders of a scholar and the pale, soft hands of one who has never done a day’s manual labour. He dresses well, but soberly. He speaks in a low, measured tone. He is, or was, the very model of a bookish gentleman. But for the past fortnight, he has not been in. He is a creature of habit, Mr. Harwood. He comes every Tuesday and Friday. His absence is as loud as a shout.”

The problem, as I saw it, was twofold. First, to understand the purpose behind this quiet mutilation. Second, to locate the man responsible before he found another victim for his razor. I assured Mr. Thorne that I would look into the matter and took my leave, the scent of old paper clinging to my coat.

My first inquiries in the neighbourhood proved fruitless. Mr. Croft was known to the tobacconist and the violin-seller only as the quiet gentleman who frequented the bookshop. He left no trace upon the world. It was a dead end. I therefore resolved to approach the problem from the other end: the maps themselves.

I spent the next two days in the reading room of the British Museum, armed with Mr. Thorne’s mutilated volumes. I spread the four fragments before me on a large sheet of foolscap. There was the harbour of Amboyna, the Brenner Pass, the city of Cuzco, and the environs of Strasbourg. I charted their locations. They were scattered across the globe, a pin-cushion of the world’s geography. I looked for a pattern: a line of longitude, a common trade route, a historical connection. There was nothing. The only commonality was their nature as maps, and their violent removal from their parent books.

It was on the third day, as I was tracing the fortifications of Cuzco for the tenth time, that a thought occurred to me. I was not looking at the subject of the maps, but at the maps themselves as objects. I borrowed a magnifying glass from an attendant and examined the printing of each fragment. The Amboyna map was a copper-plate engraving. The Alpine pass was a woodcut. The Cuzco plan was a lithograph. But the Strasbourg map… it was a simple line drawing, printed from a type-block alongside the text.

I returned to Mr. Thorne’s shop at once.

“Mr. Thorne,” I said, placing the four books before him. “You are a man of the printing trade. Tell me, is there anything common to the production of these four specific pages?”

He adjusted his spectacles and bent over the volumes. He was silent for a long moment, his finger tracing the clean-cut edges. He looked at the paper, the ink, the impression.
