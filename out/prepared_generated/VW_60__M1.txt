The ship moved through a grey water, a water that had no colour, or rather had all colours at once, silver and pewter and the dull sheen of a fish’s belly, so that the eye, looking for a line, found none, but only a vastness that was not sky and not sea, but the space between them, where the gulls hung like scraps of paper, motionless. And the gulls were white against this grey, a sharp, cutting white, unlike the white of the sun in Bombay, which had been a blinding, a beating, a thing that pressed upon the lids and made the whole world swim in a haze of gold. Here the light did not press; it fell, it slid, it was a thin, watery substance that did not warm but only illuminated, showing the grain of the deck boards, the frayed cord of the lifeboat, the worn leather of the steward’s shoes as he passed, his footsteps making a soft, rhythmic thud that was part of the ship’s own life, the slow, deep pulse of the engine far below.

It had been a year. A year of colours. The saffron of a sari against the terracotta of a wall; the violent, impossible green of the paddy fields after rain; the black of the crows that were not crows, but something sleeker, more intelligent, that watched you from the wires. She had it all stored, not in her mind—that was too neat a place, too ordered—but somewhere deeper, in the very fibre of her being. The memory was a physical thing: the smell of dust and marigolds and the sharp, sour scent of woodsmoke, the feel of the heat on her skin like a constant, heavy blanket, the sound of the temple bells, a cascade of bronze that shattered the afternoon’s stillness. She would turn to Milly, to Georgina, to the circle of girls in the drawing-room at home, and she would say—what? She would say, "The sun was hot." But that was a lie, for the sun was not hot, it was a presence, a god, a weight. She would say, "We saw tigers." But that was a catalogue, an entry, not the truth of the thing, which was the silence of the forest, the sudden, heart-stopping stillness of the air, the flicker of striped shadow in the long grass that was not a tiger but the *idea* of a tiger, the promise of a violence that could erupt at any second.

She would tell them nothing. The thought was not a sudden one, but a slow, dawning certainty, like the coast of England itself, which was not yet a line but a thickening of the grey air, a suggestion of land, of solid things, of fields and houses and the lives that went on within them. How could she speak of the boy who had swept the veranda, whose eyes were the colour of dark honey and who had smiled at her, a smile that contained the whole history of his people, a history of patience and endurance and a sorrow she could not name? To speak of him would be to break him, to pin him to a board like a butterfly, to reduce the slow, languorous heat of the day to a sentence in a letter. Her friends would listen, their faces polite, their minds on the new hats from Bond Street, on the dance at the weekend, on the little dramas of their own small world. And she would see their incomprehension, and it would be a wall between them. She would be the girl who had been to India, the girl who was different, and in their eyes she would become a strange, exotic creature, and the truth of it all, the living, breathing reality, would die in the space between her words and their ears.

Her mother came up on deck then, pulling her shawl tight around her shoulders. "Chilly, isn't it, Eleanor? Not like Calcutta." And there it was. The phrase, so simple, so true, and yet so utterly, hopelessly wrong. It was not *like* Calcutta. It was not a matter of temperature, of degrees on a thermometer. It was a matter of being. In Calcutta, she had felt things she had no name for; she had seen a world that was older and vaster and more real than the one she had come from, and it had changed the very colour of her thoughts. Now, looking at her mother’s familiar, dear, English face, with its fine network of lines and its sensible expression, she felt a pang of such intense loneliness that she had to look away, back to the water.

"Yes, Mother," she said, and her voice sounded thin and strange in the damp air.

The ship gave a low, mournful cry, a sound that was not of the sea or the sky, but of the iron and steam of its own making. It was a sound of arrival, of ending. The grey was resolving itself now into shapes she knew: the soft, rolling curve of the South Downs, the dark smudge of a wood, the faint, pale scar of a chalk cliff. Home. The word was a comfort, and a terror. It was the place she had longed for in the stifling nights, the place of cool sheets and quiet gardens and the familiar cadences of English speech. But she was not the same girl who had left. That girl was gone, lost somewhere in the shimmering heat-haze of the East, and this new person, this woman who carried a secret world inside her, was about to step ashore. She watched the land grow clearer, more defined, more real, and she knew that she would go into that drawing-room, and she would pour the tea, and she would answer their questions about the heat and the monkeys and the Rajah’s palace, and she would smile, and she would tell them nothing. The greatest journeys, she thought, are the ones you cannot describe. The ship moved on, carrying her towards a life she knew, and away from a truth she could never share.
