The rain in London does not fall so much as it insists, a persistent, gray curtain that blurs the hard lines of the city and softens the sharp edges of its secrets. It was in such a week, late in the autumn of 1893, that I found myself summoned from the quiet confines of my study to the bustling, nerve-racked atmosphere of Whitehall. The client was Miss Elara Vance, a woman of slight stature but possesses a gaze of such piercing intensity that I felt, even before she spoke, that she was dissecting my character as a specimen under a microscope. She was accompanied by her fiancé, Mr. Arthur Penhaligon, a junior clerk in the Admiralty, whose face was drawn with the specific pallor of a man who has not slept and who knows that the noose is being tightened around his neck.

They had engaged my services not to the police, nor to the legal tribunals, but to a private inquiry. The charge was severe: the theft and potential sale of the *Codex Maritima*, a confidential report on naval dispositions in the Mediterranean. The evidence was circumstantial yet damning. Penhaligon had been seen leaving the office late. He had recently incurred a debt of honor, a trifling sum to a man of his station, but a chasm to a young clerk. And most damningly, the document had vanished from his desk while he had been, by his own admission, momentarily distracted.

"I swear to you," Penhaligon said, his voice cracking under the weight of the accusation, "that I did not take it. I looked away for no more than the time it takes to sign a letter, and when I returned, the folder was gone. I have not sold it. I have not even read it beyond the first page. But they do not believe me, Mr. Thorne. They look at me as if I am already a dead man walking."

I examined the room, noting the heavy oak desk, the brass lamp, and the air thick with the smell of stale tobacco and fear. "And who else had access to this chamber, Mr. Penhaligon?"

"Only the copying clerk," said Miss Vance, her voice cool and precise. "A new man. Silas Vane. He has been here three weeks. He is quiet, unobtrusive, and... peculiar."

"Peculiar in what way?" I asked, though I suspected I already knew the answer.

"He whistles," she said.

It was a strange answer, and I turned to Penhaligon for elaboration. The young man looked as if he were about to burst. "It is not the whistling itself, Mr. Thorne, but the *timing*. He whistles only when he is copying. He follows the rhythm of the pen. A short, sharp note for a capital letter; a low, dragging tone for a paragraph. It is mechanical, relentless, and it haunts me. It is the only sound I hear when I close my eyes."

I took the case, not because I was certain of the solution, but because the details presented a puzzle that appealed to my analytical nature. The next morning, I presented myself at the Admiralty offices under the pretense of an audit of stationery supplies. The office was a hive of activity, a labyrinth of mahogany partitions and the rustle of paper. I located Silas Vane at a desk near the window. He was a small man, with a face that seemed to have been carved from pale clay, devoid of any strong features. He wore a suit that was slightly too large, as if he were trying to disappear into the fabric.

He was copying a document, his hand moving in a steady, mechanical blur. And there it was: the whistle. It was a low, tuneless hum, vibrating in the air like a wire plucked by an unseen hand. I watched him for an hour. He did not look up. He did not speak. He simply copied, and whistled, and copied, and whistled.

I approached him as he paused to turn a page. "Good morning, Mr. Vane," I said, keeping my voice light.

He flinched, then looked at me with eyes that were startlingly bright and alert. "Good morning, sir," he said. His voice was thin and reedy. "May I be of service?"

"I am merely observing the efficiency of your work," I said, leaning casually against the partition. "It is impressive. The precision of the hand. One might say it is robotic."

Vane’s face did not change, but his fingers tightened on the pen. "I am a creature of habit, sir. The mind works best when the body is occupied with a rhythm. The whistle helps to keep the tempo."

"A curious method," I said. "And what of the *Codex Maritima*? I understand it was a document of some importance?"

The pen stopped. The whistle ceased. The silence in the office seemed to deepen, as if the very air had held its breath. Vane looked at me, and for a moment, I saw a flicker of something—fear, perhaps, or defiance.

"I do not know of what you speak," he said, but his voice lacked the conviction of the previous exchange.

"Nor do I," I replied, smiling. "I am merely a stranger with a keen eye. But I note that your left hand is trembling, Mr. Vane. A sign of anxiety, or perhaps of a cold. The weather has been unkind this week."

He stood up, his movements jerky. "I must return to my work, sir."

I left the office, but I did not leave the case. I went to the bookshop on the corner of Whitehall, where I knew the keeper of the records for the naval prints. I inquired about the *Codex Maritima*. It was not there. I then went to the pawnbrokers on Cheapside, and found nothing. Finally, I returned to the Admiralty, not to the office, but to the street.
