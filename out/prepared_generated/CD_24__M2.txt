The fog that morning did not merely hang over Clerkenwell; it clung, it oozed, and it whispered with the malicious intent of a jealous spouse who has discovered her husband’s infidelity. It wrapped itself around the eaves of the narrow houses, soaked the cobblestones until they shone like polished brass, and descended into the Yard of Mr. Silas Crump, that desolate cathedral of refuse, to settle upon the heaps of rags, the mountains of broken crockery, and the tangled carcasses of iron, as if to mourn the sudden and singular departure of the man who had ruled them. For Silas Crump was dead. He had expired in his sleep, or rather, in his waking state, for he had never truly slept, having spent the previous night in a fit of such violent, clattering anxiety that the neighbours, who were accustomed to the sounds of the yard— the shriek of the cart, the crash of the pot-sherd, the grumbling of the hogs—had feared he had attempted to swallow the very earth. But when the sun rose, pale and sickly as a invalid’s complexion, it found Silas Crump seated in his armchair, stiff as a bolt of canvas, his eyes open and fixed upon a point in the air that no longer existed, his lips curled in a sneer of such profound dissatisfaction that it might have been directed at the universe itself.

The discovery of his demise was not made by a friend, for Silas Crump had none; he had not even a dog, which would have been a pity, for the dog would have eaten him, and the neighbours would have been spared the trouble. It was made by Peter Pottle, the boy who collected the bones, who had come to fetch the master’s keys and found the master’s master gone. Word spread through the damp air of the yard with the speed of a fire in a dry hayloft. Before the body had cooled, the neighbours were upon the threshold, peering in with faces drawn in various expressions of curiosity, greed, and that peculiar, grim satisfaction which Londoners feel when a miser is finally checked by the only power that can stop him: Death.

Mrs. Gable, who lived next door and kept a shop in dry goods, was the first to cross the threshold. She was a woman of considerable girth and considerable opinions, and she viewed the scene with the critical eye of an appraiser. "My," she said, stepping over a pile of old newspapers that rustled like dry leaves, "he looked even worse than usual. I always said he was a hard man. A stone. A block of granite wrapped in rags. But I never thought he’d let himself go just like that. No, sir. He’s left us a mess. Look at the dust! Look at the rats!"

The rats, indeed, were everywhere, skittering among the bones and the broken plates, their tails flicking in the stagnant air. The yard was a labyrinth of decay and discarded things. There were heaps of tattered velvet, once the glory of some forgotten ballroom, now stained with mud and rot. There were wheels without carts, springs without beds, and pots without handles. It was a place where things went to die, and now, it seemed, the man who had collected them had died in their company.

The crowd grew. Men in dusty coats and women with shawls wrapped tight against the chill pushed their way into the yard, their eyes scanning the heaps with the intensity of treasure hunters. They knew Silas Crump. They knew he was a miser, a hoarder, a man who hoarded not just goods but gold, or at least so the rumour had it. They had seen him counting coins by the light of a tallow candle, his face illuminated like a gargoyle. They had heard him muttering to himself, his voice like gravel grinding against stone. They were certain, with the absolute certainty of the poor, that he had hidden his wealth somewhere in this yard of filth. And so they began to dig.

Mrs. Gable led the way to the pile of old tinware, kicking aside a broken mirror that caught the dim light and threw it back in jagged shards. "Here, Thomas," she called to her husband, a man whose face was as red and angry as a pepper. "Dig here. I told you he kept his money in the tin. I told you, and you didn’t listen. You always were a fool, Thomas."

Thomas, grumbling under his breath, began to turn over the tins with a crowbar. The sound was loud and harsh, echoing in the small space. The other neighbours, emboldened by the success of the first diggers, began to tear at the other heaps. A man in a top hat pulled out a bundle of old ropes and found nothing but dust. A woman with a bonnet pulled from a pile of broken chairs and found only splinters. The yard became a scene of chaotic activity, a frenzy of greed and desperation. The fog outside seemed to press in, watching the spectacle with a cold, indifferent gaze.

It was then that Eliza, the young servant who had served Silas Crump for the last two years, stepped forward. She was a quiet girl, with eyes that held a depth of sorrow that belied her youth. She had been the only one who had shown him any kindness, bringing him his tea and tending to his aching joints when the winter cold set in. She had watched him grow thinner, his eyes growing larger and more desperate, his grip on life growing weaker. She had known, with a certainty that came from long observation, that he had hidden his largest sum of money in the one place he trusted most: the bottom of the old oak barrel that had once held apples, now empty and dry, sitting in the corner of the yard, forgotten by all but him.
