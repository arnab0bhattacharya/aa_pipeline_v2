The morning light in Bath, when it chose to appear, possessed a peculiar quality of diffused grace, filtering through the lace curtains of Mrs. Pym’s drawing room with an air of calculated indifference. Miss Arabella Vane sat by the fire, her hands folded in her lap with a stillness that betrayed less composure than her countenance. She was a young woman of twenty, possessed of a fortune that was the envy of the county and a spirit that had, until the previous evening, been rather too freely indulged in the contemplation of her own worth. That worth, however, had been abruptly recalculated.

Her guardian, the venerable Mr. Thorne, had delivered his ultimatum with the precision of a surgeon. Every suitor in the circle, he declared, was a wolf in the skin of a lamb, and the lamb’s fleece was the sole object of their desire. He had advised her to test their integrity, to strip away the gold from the chain and see what remained of the link. Arabella, who had always prided herself on her discernment, had accepted the challenge with a briskness that surprised even herself. She had announced, to the small assembly gathered for breakfast, that her father’s estate had been seized by creditors, that the debt was insurmountable, and that she was now, for all intents and purposes, a beggar.

The effect had been instantaneous and, to her taste, most instructive.

Mr. St. John, whose courtship had been marked by a persistent and loud admiration for her complexion, had excused himself to attend to "urgent matters of business" before the toast had been poured. Mr. Whitmore, who had spent the last season promising to build her a library, had found his horse suddenly lame, a tragedy which he seemed to regard with far greater concern than her sudden poverty. The sisters, the Misses Clay, who had previously vied for her friendship, now exchanged glances that were sharp as needles, and their conversation, previously directed at her, had turned abruptly to the price of silk and the prospect of cheaper lodgings.

Only one gentleman remained in the room.

He was Mr. Julian Hale. He was not, by any fair estimation, a handsome man. His features were plain, his manner unassuming, and his conversation, while polite, lacked the sparkling wit that Arabella had come to expect in a suitor. He was a man of quiet industry, a solicitor of modest means and even more modest ambitions, who had been tolerated in the room largely because he was not obtrusive and because his silence allowed others to display their own accomplishments. He had sat in the corner, reading a legal treatise, and had not looked up when the news of her ruin was first whispered.

Arabella watched him now, a mixture of irritation and curiosity tightening her chest. She expected him to rise, to offer some trite consolation, or perhaps, in a fit of theatrical pity, to propose a match based solely on her remaining virtues. Instead, he closed his book, placed it on the table, and rose with a dignity that seemed out of place in a man of his station.

"Miss Vane," he said, his voice calm and devoid of the tremor of distress that had marked the departure of the others. "I trust that this change of circumstance does not alter your opinion of the value of your own person. It is a misfortune of the pocket, not of the mind."

Arabella raised an eyebrow. "You speak of my mind, sir, as if you had some knowledge of its contents."

"I speak of the mind that I have had the pleasure of observing," he replied. "It is a thing of substance, Miss Vane. I had hoped it might be found to have some worth in itself, independent of its bank account."

There was a pause. The fire crackled. Arabella felt a sudden, sharp sting of embarrassment. She had not expected to be valued for her intellect; she had expected to be valued for her dowry. The shift was disorienting.

"And now that the account is empty," she said, trying to recover the hauteur of her position, "do you find the mind still agreeable? Or is it, as I fear, merely a vessel for the gold?"

Mr. Hale smiled, a small, faint movement that did not quite reach his eyes. "I am a solicitor, Miss Vane. I deal in contracts and obligations. I have learned that the most binding agreements are those made in good faith, not those secured by collateral. If I have any claim on your attention, it is based on the former. I do not require security for my regard."

Arabella looked at him, really looked at him, for the first time. She saw the plainness of his coat, the slight stoop of his shoulders, the lack of any affectation in his bearing. It was not the picture of a romantic hero. It was not even, she admitted with a pang of disappointment, particularly charming. But it was steady. In a world that had just revealed itself to be a collection of vultures circling a carcass, his steadiness was a strange and disconcerting comfort.

"You are a strange man, Mr. Hale," she said. "Most men would have seen this as an opportunity to negotiate from a position of strength. You seem to see it as a clarification."

"Perhaps," he said. "I had hoped to know if there was anyone behind the fortune. Now I am left to wonder if there is anyone left at all."

The irony of the question hung in the air. It was a bold one, bordering on impertinence, yet it was delivered with such gentle curiosity that it lacked any sting. Arabella felt a flush rise in her cheeks, not from anger, but from a sudden, unwelcome awareness of her own vanity. She had built her identity around the idea that she was desirable because she was wealthy. If that was stripped away, what was she? A young woman with a sharp tongue and a limited understanding of the world, living in a house that was no longer hers.
