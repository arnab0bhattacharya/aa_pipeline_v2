The living of Ashcombe Parva had been bestowed upon Mr. Edmund Ferrers at the age of six-and-twenty, and he had held it now for four years without any of that restlessness which too often attends a young man’s first settlement in the country. He was fond of his parish, fond of his books, and, if the truth were known, rather more fond than he cared to acknowledge of a young lady who resided at the great house at the end of the lane.

Miss Arabella Wexcombe was the second daughter of a gentleman of good estate and better intentions, whose notions of education, though well-meant, had been somewhat irregular in their application. Having discovered, rather late in the day, that his daughter’s accomplishments were chiefly of the ornamental kind, and that her understanding, though naturally excellent, had been suffered to lie fallow, he had conceived the happy idea of engaging the clergyman to direct her reading. That Mr. Ferrers was learned, sober, and unexceptionable in his morals, was sufficient recommendation; that he was also modest to a fault, and would rather have faced a whole synod of bishops than be obliged to converse for an hour together with Miss Wexcombe, was a circumstance which did not occur to the father, and would have been of no consequence to him if it had.

The first lesson was appointed for a Tuesday morning, and Mr. Ferrers spent the preceding night in a state of agitation which he vainly attributed to the difficulty of selecting an appropriate text. He arrived at the great house a full quarter of an hour before the time, and was shown into the library, where he occupied himself in examining the backs of the books with an intensity of interest which their contents could not have justified.

Miss Wexcombe entered with composure. She was a young woman of a fair, sensible countenance, with dark eyes that observed more than she chose to say, and a manner which, though perfectly civil, was not calculated to put a nervous man at his ease. She curtsied; Mr. Ferrers bowed; and they seated themselves on opposite sides of a large mahogany table, with a volume of Mr. Addison’s Spectator between them, as though it were a fortification.

“My father tells me, sir,” said she, “that you are to direct my studies. I hope you will find me not wholly without capacity.”

“I have no doubt of your capacity, madam,” replied Mr. Ferrers, with great earnestness. “I have never entertained a doubt of it. Your capacity is—that is to say, I am persuaded it is fully equal to the task.”

He blushed; she looked at him with a species of mild surprise; and the conversation, having thus exhausted itself, was succeeded by a silence of some minutes, during which Mr. Ferrers turned over the pages of the Spectator with the air of a man who expected to find something of importance concealed between them.

“Perhaps,” said he at length, “we might begin with the paper on—on discretion.”

“As you please, sir.”

He found the paper, and read aloud a passage of some length, in a voice which he endeavoured to render steady, and which succeeded in being merely loud. When he had finished, he looked up, and found Miss Wexcombe’s eyes fixed upon him with an expression which he could not interpret.

“You do not agree with Mr. Addison, perhaps?” said he.

“I have not yet had time to form an opinion,” she replied. “You read with such rapidity that I could scarcely attend to the sense.”

“I beg your pardon. I am sorry. I shall read more slowly.”

“I should be obliged to you.”

He read the passage again, more slowly, and with a painful attention to every syllable, as though he feared that a single misplaced emphasis might be construed into a liberty. When he had done, she observed that she thought Mr. Addison’s remarks very just.

“You do?” cried Mr. Ferrers, with an animation which surprised himself. “I am very glad. I mean—I am happy to find you think so. It is a very just paper. Very just indeed.”

“You seem to have a great regard for Mr. Addison, sir.”

“I have. That is—I have the highest respect for his writings. They are—they are very improving.”

“And do you never read anything for amusement?”

“For amusement? I—I sometimes read poetry. But not often. I am afraid I do not allow myself sufficient relaxation.”

“That is a pity,” said Miss Wexcombe. “I am fond of poetry.”

“Are you? I am very glad. I mean—I am glad to hear it. Perhaps we might read some poetry together. That is, if your father approves. It might be—it might be a variety.”

“It might indeed.”

Another silence ensued, of a nature which Mr. Ferrers found it impossible to break. He was conscious of a strong desire to say something agreeable, and an equally strong conviction that whatever he said would be absurd. He therefore contented himself with looking at the clock, and calculating how many minutes remained before he could with decency take his leave.

The second lesson was not more fortunate. Mr. Ferrers had prepared a discourse on the study of history, which he delivered with a fluency that astonished him, until, happening to catch Miss Wexcombe’s eye, he forgot the thread of his argument, and was obliged to conclude abruptly with an observation on the usefulness of dates. Miss Wexcombe thanked him for his instructions with perfect gravity, and hoped she should profit by them.

“I am sure you will,” said he. “I am persuaded you will. You have a—a very good understanding.”

“You are very obliging, sir.”

“Not at all. I speak only the truth. I have always thought so. I have always thought your understanding very good indeed.”

He stopped, appalled at his own temerity. Miss Wexcombe coloured slightly, and turned her eyes to the window.

“I believe my father is coming,” said she.
