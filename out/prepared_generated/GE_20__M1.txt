It is a principle of our nature, not always agreeable to those who govern, that a man’s opinions, when they are sincerely held and grounded in a certain education, will out themselves in his bearing, his choice of words, or even in the very turn of his head, long before he has committed any act of sedition; and so it was with Herr Amsel, the music-master. He had come to the town of Ashbury some three years before, from a German principality whose name was a matter of some confusion to the townspeople, and had established himself in two rooms above the saddler’s shop in the market-place. His reputation for skill was considerable, for he could coax a melody from the most reluctant of young ladies’ fingers, and his patience with the dullness of the better sort of families was, by all accounts, inexhaustible. But his foreignness was not of the picturesque, comfortable kind that might be found in a travelling fiddler at a fair. It was a serious, thoughtful foreignness, which made his silence at the weekly assembly of the Mechanics’ Institute seem less like modesty than a quiet, profound disagreement.

The suspicion, which is a kind of social blight that settles upon a man before he is aware of it, had begun to gather about him like a miasma. It was not that he had said anything seditious; the charge against him was, in the first instance, one of omission. He had not joined the subscription for the new iron railing at the church; he had been observed to be reading a book in German on the public bench by the river, a volume whose very appearance, with its severe black binding, was enough to alarm Mr. Pomeroy, the ironmonger. And then there was the matter of his teaching. He insisted, with a Germanic earnestness that was quite lost on his pupils, on the importance of rhythm and structure, of feeling the underlying pulse of a piece rather than merely executing its decorative flourishes. To the more imaginative parents, this began to seem like a subtle form of instruction in subversion, a training of the mind to look for dangerous patterns beneath the placid surface of things.

The crisis, as is so often the case, was precipitated not by a great event, but by a small one: a daughter’s piano-playing. Miss Eleanor Vance, a young lady of seventeen whose chief beauty was a pair of earnest, somewhat myopic eyes, had been Herr Amsel’s most promising pupil. Her father, Squire Vance, was the senior magistrate of the county, a man whose character was built upon a bedrock of established order and whose face, in repose, seemed carved from the same granite as the courthouse steps. It was he who summoned Herr Amsel to his library, a room that smelled of leather, beeswax, and the unassailable authority of the county records.

The Squire did not stand on ceremony. He was a man who believed that the world was divided into those who gave orders and those who took them, and he saw no reason to obscure this simple fact with polite circumlocution. “Herr Amsel,” he began, his voice as dry as old parchment. “You are a foreigner. You have done well here, I hear. But these are troubled times. There is a fever abroad, a German fever of republicanism and atheism. I am a magistrate. It is my duty to see that the infection does not spread to Ashbury.”

Herr Amsel, who stood with his hands clasped behind his back, inclined his head. He was a small, neat man, with a high forehead and eyes that seemed to hold a perpetual, melancholic inquiry. “Sir, I am a teacher of music. My concern is with the fugue, not with politics.”

“Music is not so far from politics as you suppose,” the Squire retorted, tapping a finger on the polished mahogany of his desk. “It is a matter of discipline, of harmony. Or of discord. My daughter, Eleanor, plays a waltz of Strauss. Now she wishes to play a piece of Chopin. A mazurka. It is wild, untuneful, full of… questioning. She says you have encouraged her in this.”

“I have encouraged her to understand the composer’s intention, sir. To feel the dance within the music, even when it is a dance of sorrow.”

The Squire’s face grew grim. “This is precisely the sort of talk I mean. ‘A dance of sorrow.’ There is too much sorrow, too much questioning, in the air. I will have plain answers here.” He drew a sheet of paper from a drawer and laid it on the desk. “I have had this drawn up. It is a simple declaration. It states that you, Friedrich Amsel, are a loyal subject of Her Majesty Queen Victoria, that you abhor all revolutionary doctrines, and that you will not, by word or deed, instil such doctrines into the minds of your pupils. Sign it, and this matter is closed. You may continue your work in peace.”

He pushed the paper and a pen across the desk. The ink was black and accusatory on the white page.

For a long moment, Herr Amsel did not move. He looked at the paper, then at the Squire’s implacable face, and then past him, out of the window, to the orderly garden with its clipped hedges and gravel paths. Here was the crux of it. His living, his small, precarious dignity in this foreign land, his daily bread—all were offered to him in exchange for a few lines of ink. To sign would be a mere formality, a sop to the prejudices of a provincial tyrant. It would cost him nothing, save for a momentary feeling of self-contempt, a feeling which, he reasoned, a practical man might learn to bear. Yet he felt a profound and sudden weariness. He had fled one country where men were forced to declare their allegiance to a flag they despised; he had no wish to find the same tyranny in a more petty and domestic form.
