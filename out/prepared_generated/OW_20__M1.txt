It is one of the saddest duties of modern life to discover that one’s servants have souls. The discovery is invariably inconvenient, and almost always leads to the most regrettable complications. Sir Arthur Pembroke had always maintained that a butler should be like a well-made clock, silent, punctual, and entirely devoid of private opinion; but on that fatal Tuesday morning in May, he found that his own clock, a magnificent French affair of ormolu and malachite, had begun to speak. Not in chimes, but in chapters.

The trouble began, as all true troubles do, with a drawing-room conversation. Lady Beatrice Pembroke, a woman whose complexion was as carefully arranged as her social calendar, was entertaining the Duchess of Dorset and the notorious Mrs. Vandeleur, whose morality was as flexible as her ankles and whose opinion was therefore universally sought after.

“They say,” whispered Mrs. Vandeleur, leaning forward so that the emeralds at her throat caught the light like captive serpents, “that *The Mask of Monsieur* is the most wicked book of the century. I have read it twice. It is absolutely delightful. The portrait of the Marquis is said to be drawn from life.”

Lady Beatrice, who prided herself on reading nothing more scandalous than the *Court Circular*, smiled with a vague benevolence. “Indeed? I am afraid I am too busy with the parish bazaar to keep up with modern fiction. I hear the style is quite—quite French.”

“It is not the style, my dear,” said the Duchess, adjusting her lorgnette. “It is the butler. The butler of the Marquis in the book. He is a creature of terrifying integrity, who blackmails his master not for money, but for the sake of Art. It is quite the most modern idea I have ever encountered.”

“And the author,” chimed in Mrs. Vandeleur, “is an absolute mystery. He calls himself ‘Le Domestique.’ They say he is a man of the people, a revolutionary, perhaps a socialist who has penetrated the aristocracy to expose our vices.”

“A dangerous creature,” said Lady Beatrice, shuddering theatrically. “I hope he never enters a respectable house.”

At that precise moment, the door opened, and Hobbs, the Pembroke’s butler of fifteen years, entered with the tea. He was a man of severe aspect, with a face that looked as though it had been carved from grey marble by a sculptor who disapproved of frivolity. His whiskers were impeccable, his gloves spotless. He moved with the noiseless gravity of a ghost who has taken a vow of silence.

“Tea, my lady,” said Hobbs.

Lady Beatrice looked at him. She had looked at him a thousand times before, but never with the eyes of Mrs. Vandeleur’s gossip. She noticed, for the first time, a certain cynical droop to his left eyelid. She noticed, too, that his hand, as he poured the Darjeeling, was long and white—the hand of an artist, not a servant.

“Hobbs,” she said, her voice trembling slightly, “have you read *The Mask of Monsieur*?”

The tea in the cup did not ripple. Hobbs did not blink. “I have not had the leisure, my lady. I understand it is a work of some notoriety.”

“It is abominable,” said Lady Beatrice, testing him.

“So I have been given to understand, my lady. The consensus of the lower orders is that it lacks the moral uplift of Mrs. Humphry Ward.”

He bowed and withdrew. But as the door closed, Mrs. Vandeleur clutched the Duchess’s arm. “Did you see his eyes?” she hissed. “That man has read it. That man *wrote* it.”

The accusation hung in the air like the scent of gardenias—too sweet to be ignored, too heavy to be dispersed.

The crisis came to a head three days later. Sir Arthur, a man who dealt with difficulties by ignoring them until they either died or sued him, was finally forced to confront the evidence. It was found in the pantry, tucked behind the silver polish: a manuscript bound in morocco leather, titled *The Vices of the Virtuous*.

Sir Arthur brought it into the library, where Lady Beatrice was arranging roses.

“It is Hobbs,” said Sir Arthur, his face the colour of a boiled lobster. “He is the author. The handwriting matches the household accounts. He has used our drawing room as the setting for the scene in the conservatory. He has described your new gown, Beatrice. The pink silk. He even mentions the tear in the hem that you thought nobody saw.”

Lady Beatrice sat down upon a chaise longue, her hand at her breast. “Oh, Arthur. If we dismiss him, we admit that we know. We admit that we have read the book, or at least that we recognize ourselves in it. It would be social ruin. The Duchess would never forgive us for being interesting.”

“And if we keep him?” Sir Arthur groaned, pacing the Aubusson carpet. “He is writing a sequel. I heard him muttering to himself in the boot room. *The Son of the Butler*. It comes out next month. What if he describes the scene in the study? What if he reveals that I cannot pronounce ‘quay’ correctly?”

“We must not read it,” said Lady Beatrice firmly. “That is the only solution. We must remain in a state of noble ignorance. We must treat Hobbs exactly as before. We must not give him the satisfaction of our curiosity.”

“Curiosity!” cried Sir Arthur. “It is not curiosity I feel, it is terror! The man is a literary anarchist. He holds our reputation in his white-gloved hands.”

It was a policy of magnificent restraint. For the next week, the Pembroke household became a theatre of exquisite politeness. Hobbs served dinner with the air of a high priest officiating at a sacrifice. The Pembrokes ate their turtle soup in a silence broken only by the clinking of silver.

They did not ask him about the book. They did not ask him about the reviews in the *Times*. They treated the scandalous novel as if it were a contagious disease—present in the atmosphere, but never to be acknowledged.
