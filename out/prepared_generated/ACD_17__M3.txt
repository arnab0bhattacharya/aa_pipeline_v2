It was upon a raw November morning, with a yellow fog clinging to the pavements of Marylebone, that Mrs. Eleanor Vane was shown into my consulting-room. She was a young woman of exceptional composure, dressed in a dark grey serge that suited her pale, clear-cut features, but there was a nervous tension in her gloved hands which betrayed the agitation underlying her manner.

"You must pardon the early hour, Doctor," she began, settling herself upon the edge of the chair. "My husband, Hector Vane, is a wine merchant of considerable standing in Mark Lane. For three years our marriage has been, I believed, one of absolute confidence. Yet within the last fortnight I have made two discoveries which have shaken me profoundly, and I have come to you because I require discretion above all things. I do not fear the truth, Doctor. I fear scandal."

I had already observed several pertinent details. The left hand bore a wedding ring of plain gold, above which sat a half-hoop diamond ring of a value consistent with a prosperous trade. Her boots were of excellent leather but lightly muddied in a peculiar reddish clay, not the common grime of the City. Her umbrella, wet at the tip, told me she had walked some distance in the open air before taking a cab for the final stage of her journey.

"You speak of two discoveries," said I. "Pray be precise as to the nature of each."

"In the first place," she replied, "I found that my husband maintains two sets of account books. The first are kept at the counting-house in Mark Lane, where I have occasionally called to leave a parcel or a note. They are bound volumes, entered in the hand of his chief clerk, a Mr. Podmore. The second set I discovered by the merest accident. My husband had left a locked despatch-box in a cupboard at our house in Heathside Gardens, Hampstead, and the key had slipped behind the lining of his dressing-case. I opened it. Within, I found a thinner ledger, written entirely in my husband's own hand, containing entries in a cipher which I cannot read, and sums of money far in excess of anything his known business would account for."

"You did not confront him?"

"I dared not. There was something in the concealment that froze the question on my lips."

"And the second discovery?"

She drew from her reticule a small object wrapped in tissue paper, and laid it upon the table between us. It was a key — a Yale pattern, of bright steel, still carrying the maker's paper tag upon its ring. Upon the tag was stamped a number, and beneath it, in faded ink, an address: "27 Bellerby Street, W."

"That," said she, "I found sewn into the lining of his winter overcoat, which had been returned from the tailor's only last week, where a new velvet collar had been fitted. I know every key in our house, and every key at the counting-house. This belongs to neither. I have not heard of Bellerby Street, nor do I wish to learn of it through any channel which might expose my husband's name."

I turned the key over in my fingers. It was a new key, bright and untarnished, with no scratch upon the wards. The tag bore the name of a well-known locksmith in the Edgware Road. Here was a key that had been cut recently, assigned to a specific address, and concealed with considerable care.

"You have done wisely in coming to me," said I. "I shall make what inquiries are necessary, and you shall have my report within forty-eight hours. Until then, I must ask you to preserve an absolute appearance of normality."

She inclined her head, left a fee upon the corner of the desk, and departed with the same quiet self-possession she had shown upon entering.

Within the hour I had consulted a directory. Bellerby Street was a short, unremarkable thoroughfare running between two mews behind a terrace of substantial houses in the neighbourhood of Lisson Grove. It was not a street of residences, but of small business premises — a maker of scientific instruments, a French polisher, a dealer in second-hand furniture. Number 27 was listed as unoccupied.

I engaged a hansom and drove there directly. The fog had thickened, and the street lamps were but smudges of yellow in the murk. Number 27 proved to be a narrow-fronted building of yellow brick, with a single window on the upper floor and a solid black door at the street level. The door was new, the paint fresh, the brass knocker bright. I fitted the key. It turned smoothly, and the door opened upon a passage floored with clean linoleum.

I closed the door behind me and stood for a moment in silence. The passage was cold but not damp, and there was a faint smell of beeswax and of something else — a dry, papery scent that I associated with stored documents. I examined the premises with care. The front room was furnished as a modest private office, with a desk, a chair, and a row of new deal shelves. Upon the shelves were arranged a number of pasteboard boxes, each labelled with a date and a set of initials. I opened several. They contained correspondence, bills of lading, and invoices relating to the export of wine — but not the ordinary Bordeaux and port of Hector Vane's known trade. These were shipments of a far rarer character: vintage Madeira, old Hermitage, and sealed cases marked with the cellars of private collectors on the Continent.

I opened the desk drawers. In the second I found a letter, recently written, in a hand which I recognised from Mrs. Vane's description as that of her husband. It was addressed to a Monsieur Grenier, of Bordeaux, and its substance was this: Vane had, over the course of two years, been privately acquiring rare wines from French private cellars through agents whose methods were, to say the least, irregular.
