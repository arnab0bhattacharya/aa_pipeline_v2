The mist had not yet risen from the water; it lay along the surface in trailing, gray-white ribbons, mingling with the reflections of the bare, interlacing branches so that the pond seemed not a thing of earth, but a mere glass held up to the sky. Eleanor Craddock scattered the first crust, and the ducks came, as they always did, with that comical, waddling haste, their green necks catching a sudden, metallic gleam in the pale morning light. She scattered the bread without looking at it, her fingers breaking the hard crusts mechanically, for her eyes were fixed upon the path that skirted the water’s edge.

It was eight o'clock. Or perhaps it was five minutes past. Time, which in those days before the war had been a solid, measurable thing, marked out by the firm tread of a young man’s boots and the cheerful whistle of a tune from an opera, had now become merely a sequence of lights and shadows. The damp chill of the November air seeped through her heavy black coat, but she did not feel it. She felt only the hour, the precise hour, when he used to walk here. He had walked here, straight and tall, with his hat pushed back from his forehead, carrying his books, or perhaps merely his gloves, and looking, always looking, at the sky, or the water, or the girls going to their offices. And she, sitting at her window in the flat above the stationer’s shop, had watched him go, thinking that she must order more marmalade, or that the cook required a new saucepan. Such trivial things! And yet, how they had filled the mind, leaving no room for the prescience that might have warned her to hold him fast, to forbid him to leave.

Now, he was gone; and the Somme had swallowed him, taking not only his body, but the whole future that should have been his, the wife, the children, the slow settling into middle age. All that lay buried in the French mud, while she, Eleanor, survived, and came each morning to feed the ducks, because it was the hour he used to walk there, and the ritual was the only anchor left to her in a world that had slipped its moorings.

She scattered another crust. The ducks squabbled, their harsh, guttural quacks breaking the stillness. A cab rattled over the bridge in the distance, the hooves of the horse clattering on the stones with a sound like the dropping of dead leaves. And then, coming round the curve of the path, she saw the boy.

He was there again, sitting on the next bench, separated from hers by a patch of damp gravel and a litter of fallen leaves. He was perhaps nineteen, or twenty—a year younger than Edward would have been, had he lived; but the likeness was there, not in the features, perhaps, for this boy was fair where Edward had been dark, but in the way he sat, with his long legs stretched out before him, and his hands thrust deep into the pockets of his shabby overcoat, staring at the ground with that same brooding, inward look that had come over Edward in his last leave, when he had seemed already half to belong to another world.

Eleanor’s heart contracted. She clasped her hands in her lap, the bread falling unheeded. Why did he come? What did he want? He had no bread for the ducks; he merely sat there, day after day, a silent, disturbing presence on the edge of her grief. She felt, irrationally, that he was intruding upon her privacy; this was her place, and Edward’s, and the boy had no right to it. Yet, at the same time, a strange, aching longing seized her to hear his voice. If he would only speak! If he would only say something—anything—about the weather, or the ducks, or the time of day—she might answer him, and in answering, find some release from the terrible solitude that enclosed her like a glass bell.

But he did not speak. He sat motionless, his eyes on the ground; and Eleanor, in her turn, fell silent, watching him, while the thoughts eddied and spun in her mind. What was he thinking of, sitting there so still? Perhaps he was out of work; there were so many young men out of work now, standing on street corners, or sitting in parks, with that look of baffled, inarticulate protest. Or perhaps he was in love, and had quarrelled with his girl, and came here to brood upon her harsh words, rehearsing the things he should have said. Everyone had their own grief, she supposed; the world was full of it, a vast, grey sea of grief, on which the individual sorrow was but a fleeting wave.

The sun, which had been struggling through the clouds, suddenly broke free, and the pond was transformed. The mist vanished as if by magic, and the water sparkled, a million points of light dancing and flashing, so that the eye was dazzled and could scarcely distinguish the ducks from their reflections. The bare branches of the trees, which had looked so stark and dreary a moment before, now shone with a delicate, silvery sheen, and the grass, wet with dew, glittered as if strewn with jewels. It was so beautiful, so piercingly, almost cruelly beautiful, that Eleanor caught her breath. Life, it seemed, was still going on, still flinging out these splendours, regardless of the dead.

The boy moved. He shifted his position, and his eyes, wandering, met hers. For a moment they looked at each other; and Eleanor felt that she was seen, not merely as a widow in a black coat, but as a soul, solitary and exposed. The boy’s eyes were blue, she noticed, a pale, washed-out blue, like the sky on a winter’s evening. They held hers for a second, and then he looked away, a faint flush rising to his cheeks.
