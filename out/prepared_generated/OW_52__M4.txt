The supper-table at Lady Cheyne’s house in Mayfair was a triumph of the confectioner’s art, and Miss Honoria Vane, who had been brought up to consider a surfeit of sugar vulgar, ate three of the little pink cakes with a defiance that was almost moral. She was a girl of exquisite convictions and a small private income, and she had been taught from the cradle that wealth was a kind of leprosy, picturesque in the Bible but unmentionable in Belgravia. Her father, who had made his fortune in something disagreeable and distant, had died of a broken heart when she refused to marry a duke; her mother had died of a broken heart when she refused to marry anyone at all. Honoria had therefore been left to the care of a maiden aunt who believed in plain living and high thinking, and who had succeeded so well that the girl could not look at a bank-note without a shudder.

It was at a studio in Chelsea, where the fog hung like a soiled curtain and the models smoked cigarettes in the passage, that she first saw Mr. Lucian Quill. He was painting a picture of a beggar-woman who had been paid to look tragic, and he was doing it with a fervour that was almost religious. Honoria, who had come with her aunt to admire the beautiful poverty of the thing, was struck at once by the artist’s profile, which was that of a young saint who had not yet been canonised, and by his hands, which were long and pale and stained with something that was probably not sanctity. She said something about the dignity of the poor, and he said something about the dignity of the brush, and they were married in the spring.

For a little while it was charming. They lived in two rooms in a street that was called, with a fine irony, Paradise Row, and they ate bread and cheese and talked about the beauty of simplicity. Honoria darned his socks and felt like a heroine out of a Russian novel. Lucian painted her in a shawl and called the picture ‘The Soul Unveiled,’ and a dealer offered him ten pounds for it, which he refused with a gesture that was magnificent. Then the winter came, and the roof leaked, and the landlord was a Philistine, and Lucian began to look at the little pink cakes in the confectioner’s window with a strange, far-away expression.

‘Honoria,’ he said one evening, when the candle had guttered and the wind was making a nuisance of itself at the window, ‘I have been thinking.’

‘That is always dangerous,’ she said, smiling, for she was still young enough to think epigrams were a form of affection.

‘I have been thinking,’ he continued, ‘that it would be a very beautiful thing to be rich.’

Honoria put down her needle. ‘Lucian,’ she said, ‘you cannot be serious.’

‘I am never serious,’ he said, ‘but I am often sincere. And I am sincerely of the opinion that poverty is a great deal more picturesque from the outside than from the inside. I should like to have a house with a staircase that does not creak, and a coat that does not apologise for itself, and a dinner that does not consist of the same vegetable in three different moods.’

‘But wealth is vulgar,’ she said, with the air of one quoting the catechism.

‘So is hunger,’ he answered, ‘and yet we do not recommend it to our friends. Honoria, my love, you have been brought up to believe that money is a disease, but I assure you it is only a medicine that has been badly administered. I should like to be rich enough to be poor with dignity.’

‘You are talking like a shopkeeper,’ she cried, and there were tears in her eyes, for she had loved him for his indifference to the world, and now he was showing a most unseemly interest in it.

‘And you,’ he said, ‘are talking like a duchess who has discovered that her diamonds are paste. You do not despise wealth, Honoria; you despise the people who have it. It is a very different thing, and a great deal less Christian.’

She rose from her chair with a dignity that was somewhat marred by the fact that the chair was broken. ‘I shall teach you,’ she said, ‘the beauty of poverty. I shall show you that a man may be rich in spirit without a penny in his pocket. I shall make you see that the lilies of the field, though they toil not, neither do they spin, are nevertheless the most fashionable things in creation.’

‘And I,’ said Lucian, taking up his palette, ‘shall teach you the poverty of beauty. I shall paint you as you are, Honoria, without the shawl and without the halo, and you will discover that it is a great deal more difficult to be beautiful than to be poor.’

The next morning she began her campaign. She dismissed the servant, who had been a luxury and a bad cook, and took to doing the housework herself, which she did with a great deal of enthusiasm and very little skill. She bought a book on the simple life and read it aloud to Lucian while he painted, and he listened with a patience that was almost insulting. She made him walk in the park instead of taking a cab, and he caught a cold. She insisted on his wearing a coat that had been mended so often that it had become a kind of philosophical statement, and he caught another cold. She gave away his best brushes to a beggar, and he said nothing, but he looked at her with a gaze that was curiously like the gaze of a man who is calculating the cost of a divorce.
