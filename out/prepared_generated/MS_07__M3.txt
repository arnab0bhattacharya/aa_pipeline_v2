The pestilence had passed, leaving behind the shattered skeleton of a once-great metropolis; and in the hollows of that vast desolation, where the shattered glass of fallen towers lay mingled with the creeping ivy that had begun, even in so short a span, to veil the starkness of human ruin, a small, remnant band of survivors clung to existence. Over this solitary flock presided Valerius, a man whose early life had been devoted to the clash of arms and the stern discipline of the camp. He was fallen now, stripped of the panoply of war, yet bearing in his upright mien and commanding glance the ineradicable stamp of the soldier. He looked upon the crumbling edifices, the overgrown forums, and the dismantled engines of a forgotten age, not with the weak despair that unnerved the many, but with a sad and lofty aspiration. It was his desire, his absorbing vision, to call forth order from this chaos; to rebuild the shattered habitations; to compel the wild and desolate earth to yield again her increase to the hand of human industry; and to gather within the newly raised walls a society where peace and the gentle arts might flourish, secure from the terrors that had desolated their forefathers. To this end, he laboured with a fervent, impassioned zeal, addressing the youthful and the strong among the survivors in words of burning eloquence, appealing to their courage, their filial piety, and their innate love of life.

Yet, in the shadow of Valerius's noble endeavour, and rising in dark and portentous opposition to it, stood another figure—Elias. He had been a priest, a minister of that Deity whose worship had once crowned the city's highest pinnacles, and his mind, naturally inclined to the sombre and the terrible, had been deeply warped by the unparalleled horrors of the late visitation. To him, the swift and indiscriminating plague was not a mere sequence of natural causes, but a direct and awful judgment from on high, a fiery denunciation of the pride, the luxury, and the irreligion that had marked the latter days of the fallen civilization. He held it as a sacred, incontrovertible tenet that any attempt to rebuild the ruined city, any effort to restore the blasphemous works of the generation that had been thus smitten, was a fresh impiety, a tempting of the offended heavens, a rebellion against the divine decree that had decreed silence and desolation. He went clothed in mourning garments, his pale, ascetic face worn by vigils and fasts, his dark eyes gleaming with a wild, unearthly light; and when he spoke to the remnant, his voice, though low and tremulous, thrilled through their inmost souls with the agonizing force of a prophetic lamentation.

Between these two, the youthful spirits of the community were distracted, torn asunder by contending sympathies that left no heart in peace. There was Clara, a girl of ardent disposition and profound sensibility, whose early years had been nurtured amid the elegancies of the old world, now swept away for ever. She looked upon Valerius with an affection that partook of reverence; his heroic fortitude, his unwearied toil, his faith in the future, were to her as a beacon-star in the surrounding gloom; she clung to his vision of a restored and tranquil home with all the passionate devotion of her sex, feeling that in labouring for that end she was offering up her tears and her strength at the shrine of an ennobling hope. Yet, when Elias spoke, and painted in fearful colours the wrath that had consumed the ungodly, a chill crept over her frame; a haunting dread took possession of her spirit, whispering that the dead beneath those ruins were not appeased, and that to build upon their graves was to call down a second and more consuming vengeance. Her heart was torn between the desire to live and the terror of surviving; she listened, now to the animating promises of Valerius, now to the solemn warnings of Elias, and wept in secret over the cruel distraction of her mind.

Others, of a sterner mould, leaned towards the priest. They were appalled by the magnitude of the desolation; the silence of the vacant streets, the sudden irruption of the elements into the sacred places of human resort, seemed to them to testify of an eternal curse. They murmured against Valerius, saying that he was a man of blood, whose hands had been too often imbrued in the slaughter of the battlefield to feel the sanctity of the divine judgment; that he would lead them to fresh ruin, and provoke a fate as terrible as that which had already befallen. And Elias, seeing their wavering, addressed them with redoubled energy, his eloquence rising into a strain of impassioned declamation that seemed to borrow its sombre hues from the very clouds and tempests that gathered around the fallen city. He bade them look upon the shattered spires, the desolate hearths, the unburied bones that whitened in the sun; he called heaven and earth to witness that he sought not to rule, but to save; and he appealed to them, by all they held dear, by the memory of their lost kindred, by the awful expectation of a future retribution, to abandon the impious scheme, and to seek, in penitence and wandering, a refuge from the wrath to come.

Valerius, though he felt the influence of the priest's ascetic power, and was not insensible to the profound melancholy that pervaded his own soul, refused to yield. He stood forth in the midst of the assembled remnant, in the ruined square where the grass had already encroached upon the pavement, and raised his voice with a calm, yet commanding majesty. He acknowledged the visitation, he owned the severity of the chastisement; but he denied that the voice of Heaven demanded the extinction of the human race. He spoke of the resilience of the spirit, of the divine spark within that could not be quenched by calamity; he painted the desolation not as a final decree, but as a fiery trial through which the regenerate were to pass.
