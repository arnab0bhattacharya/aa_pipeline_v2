The rain, which had begun as a mere grey misting of the air, had now descended in earnest, washing the long, wet ribbon of the Uxbridge Road in a sheet of silver. From the top of the omnibus, the world was a succession of water-colour blurs: the ochre of a public house, the sudden, shocking crimson of a pillar-box, the huddled grey of a shop-front, all dissolving into the general damp. The vehicle itself, a great red beast, lumbered and swayed, its progress marked by the rhythmic shudder of the floorboards and the sigh of its brakes. Inside, the air was thick and warm with the smell of wet wool, of damp leather, of the faint, sweet perfume of some woman’s powder, all mingling in a fug that was the very breath of London on a Tuesday in November.

And within this fug, pressed together in the narrow seats, were the lives of these strangers, each a sealed world, yet all swaying to the same jolt and tremor. There was Miss Agnes Pryce, for instance, whose fingers, stiff with cold, held a small, damp letter. It was from him, from Arthur, and its few lines were worn thin with reading, though she held it now only for the comfort of its presence. The words themselves had become a kind of prayer, a litany against the grey afternoon. *Think of me when the lamps are lit,* he had written, and now, as the omnibus passed a street where the gas-lamps were already beginning to cast their watery haloes, she did think of him. But the thought was not of the man himself—his face, his voice, which were becoming strangely indistinct—but of a feeling, a warm, enclosed space, a room with a fire in it, into which the rain could not fall. The typewriter’s clatter was still in her ears, a frantic, metallic chatter that had followed her out of the office and into the swaying bus, but it was silenced now by this other, quieter rhythm of longing. The city outside was merely a backdrop for this inner theatre; the wet pavement was a stage for a meeting that would not happen, the flicker of a lamp a signal of a love that existed only on paper.

Just beyond her, an old man, Mr. Fenwick, was engaged in a different sort of communion. His hands, gnarled and brown as autumn leaves, were busy in his pocket, counting and re-counting the small change there. A shilling, worn smooth and featureless, a sixpence, two threepenny bits, a handful of coppers that smelled of the earth and of other hands. It was not a miser’s hoard, but a map of his small kingdom. The shilling was for the bit of bacon for his supper; the sixpence for the paper in the morning; the coppers were for the omnibus fare home again. Each coin was a stone in the path of his day, and his fingers, moving with a lifetime of habit, assured him that the path was complete, that he would not stumble. He looked out at the rain-streaked window, but he did not see the passing shops or the hurrying figures under umbrellas. He saw only the steady, comforting arithmetic of his own existence, a sum that, for all its poverty, had always come out right.

And then there was the mother, Mrs. Gable, whose world had shrunk to the small, feverish head resting against her shoulder. Her daughter, Lily, was a weight of heat and limpness, her breath coming in shallow little sighs that were barely audible above the groan of the omnibus. The mother’s whole being was a shield, curved around the child to fend off the jolts, the damp draughts that snaked through the window-frames, the careless gaze of strangers. She was not thinking of the past or the future, but only of the next moment, and the one after that. She watched the child’s lips, dry and parted; she felt the clammy heat of her brow; she listened to the fragile sound of her breathing. The great city, with its commerce and its clamour, its politics and its passions, was as remote as the moon. It existed only as an obstacle, a series of delays that stood between her and the doctor’s surgery, between her child and a cool cloth and a quiet bed. The omnibus was not a vehicle crossing London, but a vessel struggling through a hostile sea, and she its sole, desperate navigator.

Further back, a clergyman, the Reverend Mr. Harding, held a small black notebook open on his knee, though his eyes were not upon the page. His sermon was a living thing in his head, a structure of light and shadow, and he was building it still. The word ‘transience’ had come to him just now, as the bus splashed through a puddle, sending up a fan of dirty water. Transience. The fleeting face at the window, the child’s cough, the old man’s shillings, the girl’s letter—were they not all transient things, passing from one state to another, like the light on the wet road? But into what? That was the crux. His mind grappled with the great, abstract shapes of Faith and Redemption, trying to wrestle them down into the simple, homely words his congregation would understand on Sunday. The sway of the bus was a distraction, a physical reminder of the body’s frailty. He saw the girl with the letter, and a wave of something—pity, or perhaps a vicarious nostalgia—swept through him, but he checked it at once. It was a detail, a colour, a passing shadow. The sermon was the thing, the solid, enduring truth he was trying to build against the very transience he preached.
