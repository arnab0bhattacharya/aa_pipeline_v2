The fog lay thick upon the Kentish coast, a heavy, yellowish blanket that seemed to suffocate the grounds of Harrowgate House. It was a dreary November afternoon, the kind that settles into the bones and dampens the spirit, when I received the summons from Constable Arthur Penhallow. He stood in my sitting room at Baker Street, his uniform ill-fitting, his eyes red-rimmed and his hands trembling slightly as he clutched his cap.

“Mr. Holmes,” he began, his voice cracking with a distress that was unbecoming of a man of his station. “I beg of you. You must look into this. The maid, Miss Elara Vance, has been dismissed. They say she stole the pearl necklace. But I swear to you, she is innocent. I know her; she is a girl of good character. My own sister-in-law. If there is a flaw in the evidence, you will find it.”

Holmes, who had been seated by the fire, his eyes half-closed in that peculiar daze which preceded an intellectual awakening, turned his gaze upon the constable. It was a look of such piercing intensity that Penhallow shifted uncomfortably on the chair.

“Sit down, Penhallow,” Holmes said, his voice calm and precise. “And do not stand there wringing your hands. It is useless. You say the maid was dismissed. For what specific act of theft?”

“For the disappearance of a necklace of black pearls, belonging to Lady Harrowgate. It was last seen in the jewel box in the boudoir. The box was locked, but the key was found in the maid’s pocket. She was caught with the key, Mr. Holmes. What more proof can they want?”

Holmes leaned forward, his fingers steepled beneath his chin. “And the maid’s account of the key?”

“She says it was given to her by the housekeeper, Mrs. Gable, to open the box for Lady Harrowgate, who was indisposed. She says she placed the key on the mantelpiece and never touched it again. But Mrs. Gable denies it. And the necklace is gone.”

“Who else had access to the boudoir?”

“Only the mistress, the housekeeper, and the maid. The servants are not permitted in the private quarters without summons.”

Holmes nodded slowly. “And this Mrs. Gable? What is her history?”

Penhallow hesitated. “She has been there ten years. She is known to be... tight-fisted. But no one would dream of accusing her. She is the very embodiment of propriety.”

Holmes’s eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch. “Propriety is often a fine mask for prudence, Penhallow. But let us not jump to conclusions. You mentioned that you doubted the official case. Why?”

“Because of the insurance,” Penhallow said, leaning in. “I saw the papers on Lady Harrowgate’s desk before she dismissed the maid. The insurance on that necklace was increased from two hundred to five hundred pounds. That was only one week ago. Why insure it so heavily just before it is stolen?”

Holmes’s face lit up with a sudden, brilliant satisfaction. He sprang to his feet, pacing to the window and then back again.

“Five hundred pounds,” he murmured. “A significant sum for a set of pearls, unless they are of exceptional rarity or size. If the maid stole them, why would the owner increase the value? A thief does not insure his own loot. But if the item is still present, or if the theft is a pretext, then the increase in value becomes the pivot upon which the whole affair turns.”

He turned to Penhallow, his eyes sharp. “Bring me the police report. And Penhallow, you must go back to Harrowgate House. Do not confront Mrs. Gable. Instead, observe her. Note her movements, her conversations, and specifically, any correspondence she may be handling. I suspect that the key was not found in the maid’s pocket, but placed there. A classic frame-up. But why? If the necklace is missing, the insurance claim is void unless the theft is proven beyond doubt. If the necklace is not missing, then the claim is a fraud. And fraud requires a collaborator.”

Penhallow stood up, his confidence returning. “I shall go now, Mr. Holmes. I will watch her.”

“Do so. And look for a letter. A letter to a broker, or a wire to a pawnbroker. The insurance increase suggests a sale, or a replacement. If the necklace was sold, the money would have to be moved quickly. And if it was a fraud, the proceeds would be shared.”

That evening, Holmes sat in his chair by the fire, a violin in his hand, though he did not play. His mind was a whirring engine, connecting the dots of Penhallow’s report. The maid, Elara Vance, was a victim of circumstance. The real criminal was not a thief, but a forger of evidence.

The next morning, a telegram arrived from Penhallow.

*WIRE SIGNED GABLE SENT TO LONDON BROKER YESTERDAY. NECKLACE FOUND IN LINEN PRESS. MONEY TAKEN. ARRESTED. THANKS.*

Holmes smiled, a thin, satisfied curve of his lips. He picked up his violin and drew the bow across the strings, playing a brief, cheerful melody.

It was a simple case, really. The increase in insurance was the clue that had eluded the local police, blinded by the obvious guilt of the dismissed maid. Mrs. Gable, facing financial ruin, had decided to sell the necklace. But she could not do so openly. So, she fabricated a theft. She planted the key in the maid’s pocket, dismissed the innocent girl to provide an alibi for the disappearance, and then secretly removed the necklace from the boudoir, hiding it in the linen press where it would not be missed in a hurried search. The wire to the broker was her mistake. In her haste to secure the funds, she had used her own hand, and her own name, on the document.

Holmes leaned back, closing his eyes. The fog outside was beginning to lift, the sun breaking through the grey clouds to cast long, golden rays across the London streets. Another mystery solved, another innocent saved. It was a small satisfaction, but in the game of life, small satisfactions are often the only ones that matter.
