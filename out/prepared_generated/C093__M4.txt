The letter arrived on a Tuesday, the same day the first frost silvered the cobbles of Elyard’s market square. Thomas Finney had been stacking sacks of turnips outside his father’s old shop, his breath pluming in the raw air, when the post-boy handed him the folded paper. It was from his cousin, Abigail, who had gone to Liverpool three years past and married a clerk in the cotton trade. Her hand was cramped and hurried, but the words leapt from the page: *There is a place for you, Thomas, aboard the* Lady Margaret, *sailing for Quebec in April. A man of your strength and sense could do well in the timber trade, and I have spoken to a party who would take you on. You must decide quickly.*

He read it twice, standing there among the turnips, and felt something shift in his chest, a loosening of a knot that had been tied so long he had forgotten it was there. He was twenty-four years old. He had never been farther than Nottingham.

The shop bell jangled behind him, and his brother, Samuel, appeared in the doorway, wiping his hands on his apron. Samuel was nineteen, but his mind had never grown past perhaps twelve. He was tall and stooped, with the soft, wondering eyes of a child and a habit of humming tunelessly when he was anxious. Their mother had called him her lamb. She had called Thomas her strong one, and on the night she died, four winters ago, she had taken Thomas’s hand in her own thin, burning fingers and made him promise. *Look after him, Thomas. He has no one but you. Promise me.* And Thomas had promised, with the tears running down his face and the smell of sickness thick in the room.

Now Samuel came and stood beside him, peering at the letter he could not read. “Is it from Abigail?” he asked. “Does she send her love?”

“She does,” Thomas said, and folded the letter away. “She sends her love.”

He did not sleep that night. He lay in the low room he shared with Samuel, listening to his brother’s gentle breathing, and he thought about the sea. He had never seen it, but he had dreamed of it, a great grey expanse that might carry a man anywhere. He thought of the timber trade, of tall pines and cold rivers, of a place where a man’s past was not written in the faces of his neighbours. He thought of the promise.

In the morning he went to see Mr. Peck, the ironmonger, who had known their mother and who had a hard, practical way about him that Thomas respected. Mr. Peck listened, his blunt fingers laced over his waistcoat, and when Thomas had finished he was silent for a long moment.

“A promise to the dead,” Mr. Peck said at last, “is a serious thing. But your mother, God rest her, was a sensible woman. She would not have wished you to bury your life in Elyard for the sake of a promise she made you give in her last hour. She wanted Samuel safe, that was all. She wanted him cared for.”

“And if I go,” Thomas said, “who cares for him then?”

“There are places,” Mr. Peck said, not meeting his eye. “The asylum at Southwell takes in such as him. They are said to be kindly.”

Thomas flinched. He had seen the asylum cart pass through the square once, and the faces inside it had been blank and pale as paper. He thought of Samuel in such a place, humming his tuneless hum, and something in him recoiled. “No,” he said. “Not that.”

Mr. Peck shrugged. “Then you must find another way. But do not let the dead bind you, lad. The dead are past binding.”

The weeks that followed were a torment. The Lady Margaret would sail in April, and Abigail wrote again, more urgent now, saying the party in Quebec was eager for an answer. Thomas carried the letter in his pocket until the paper grew soft and grey. He went about his work, stacking and hauling and measuring, and all the while he watched his brother. He watched Samuel feed the shop cat with scraps from his own dinner, watched him help an old woman across the square, watched him sit by the window in the evening, humming, his face turned to the last of the light. Samuel was not clever, but he was good. He was the best man Thomas knew.

One evening in late February, when the thaw had begun and the gutters ran with dirty water, Thomas came home to find Samuel in a state of great excitement. He had made a discovery. He had been walking by the river, he said, and he had found a nest of young rabbits, their mother dead, and he had brought them home in his cap. He had them in a box by the fire, and he was feeding them with a rag dipped in milk. His face was alight with purpose.

“They would have died, Thomas,” he said. “They would have died, and I saved them.”

Thomas looked at the tiny, blind creatures, their fur still damp, their mouths working at the rag, and he felt the weight of his promise settle on his shoulders like a hand. He could not go. He knew it then, with a certainty that was almost peaceful. He could not leave Samuel to the kindness of strangers, or the cold mercy of an asylum. He would stay, and he would keep his word, and the sea would remain a dream.

He wrote to Abigail that night, and the writing was the hardest thing he had ever done. He told her he could not come, that his brother needed him, that he hoped she would understand. He sealed the letter and posted it in the morning, and then he went to the shop and worked until his hands were raw.
