The carriage gave a long sigh, a shuddering of all its iron bones, and then began to move; and with it moved the whole world of the platform, the soot-streaked glass of the station roof, the faces of the porters, all sliding backwards, melting into a smear of grey and brown, until there was only the smudged reflection of her own face, pale and indistinct, floating upon the window-pane. The city fell away in a litter of rooftops and chimneys, a geography of slate and brick she knew by heart, yet which seemed now, seen from this slight elevation and with this peculiar finality, a place she had only ever read about. The rhythm of the wheels began to assert itself, a steady, percussive beat against the rails, a sound that was at once the present moment and the future, a ticking away of the miles that lay between her and the thing she must face.

She had chosen the compartment for its emptiness, for the six plush seats and the little brass ash-tray and the printed notice warning against leaning from the window, a space into which she could pour the silent, formless mass of her grief. And for a while it was so. The telegraph poles flashed past, a hypnotic counting of seconds. The fields, still holding the last of the autumn’s green under a pewter sky, opened out and then closed again as the train banked into a cutting, the red earth of the banks a sudden, startling wound in the landscape. She thought of her sister, not as she had been in the end, a wisp of linen and bone in a darkened room, but as she was in a photograph on the mantelpiece, aged twelve, with a gap in her teeth and a straw hat askew, laughing at something just out of the frame. The memory was so sharp, so full of light and sound, that it seemed more real than the damp wool of her coat or the grey November afternoon.

The door slid open with a sudden, violent rattle, and a gust of cold, metallic air entered the carriage, followed by the young man. He stood for a moment in the doorway, a silhouette against the brighter light of the corridor, before pulling the door shut behind him. He was in khaki, the uniform still new and stiff, the puttees wound with a neatness that spoke of recent instruction and not yet of habit. He took off his cap, revealing a shock of fair hair, and placed it on the rack above. He was very young, she thought, his face still holding the softness of boyhood, though his eyes, when they met hers for a bare second, held a startling, watchful depth.

“Sorry to disturb you, Madam,” he said, his voice quiet, almost apologetic. He did not look at her again, but took the seat opposite, by the window, and set a small canvas bag carefully on the floor between his boots.

“It is no disturbance,” she said, the words feeling thin and useless in the quiet space between them.

They sat in silence as the train gathered speed, the rhythm of the wheels quickening into a relentless drumming. The landscape began to change, the gentle hills of the south giving way to a flatter, more severe country. He looked out at the fields, and she watched his reflection in the glass, a ghostly overlay upon the flying hedgerows. She saw the way his hand rested on his knee, the knuckles white, the fingers slightly curled. It was a hand that had been taught to hold a rifle, she supposed, a hand that had learned a terrible new grammar.

“Looks set to rain,” he said at last, his gaze still fixed on the horizon where the clouds were massing, a bruised purple.

“Yes,” she agreed. “The sky has that look about it.”

“It will be good for the ploughing, though,” he added, as if to himself. “My father will be glad of it.”

And then he was silent again, and the silence was no longer empty but filled with the unspoken image of a farm, of a man with weathered hands looking up at the same sky, a sky that would soon be raining on the fields of France as well as on the fields of home. She saw, with a sudden, piercing clarity, that he was not merely a soldier on leave. He was a son. He was going home to a place where he was still a boy, where the world was measured in furrows and rainfall and not in yards of trench and days of bombardment. The thought was a small, bright coin of warmth in the cold pocket of her day.

The train slowed, hissing into a small country station, a single platform with a bed of frost-bitten roses. The doors opened and a porter wheeled a barrow past. The young man stirred, and for the first time, she saw a small, folded paper protruding from the pocket of his tunic. It was not a letter, but a small, stiff photograph. He did not look at it, but his fingers brushed against its edge, a gesture so slight and so instinctive it was like a flicker of lightning, illuminating for a fraction of a second a whole hidden landscape of feeling. He was not just going home to a father and a farm. He was going back to a face, to a promise, to a future he was carrying with him in that small, stiff rectangle of paper. He was going to a place where someone was waiting, and the weight of that waiting was as tangible in the carriage as the weight of the black veil in her own travelling case.
