Mr. Gideon Tapp, draper, of Market Street in the town of Hanbury, was a man whose piety had the solid, unpretending texture of the Welsh flannel he sold over his counter; it was not the fine-spun merino of the pulpit, nor the glossy stuff that catches the eye of a congregation, but a serviceable warmth that wore well through the winters of a life not unacquainted with the east wind of adversity. He had risen, by diligence and the blessing of Providence, from a single shop with a shuttered window to the largest trade in linens and hosiery within ten miles of the town, and his position on the board of the Hanbury Charity School—a position he owed, in the main, to the good word of the Reverend Mr. Enoch Barrow, minister of Salem Chapel—was the visible seal of that respectability which the world, and not least the dissenting world, is apt to measure by the yard.

It was a position he prized the more because it had come to him, as it were, by the hand of his spiritual father; for it was under Mr. Barrow’s powerful preaching, in the year of the great revival, that Gideon had been brought, in his own phrase, “out of the Egypt of mere morality into the Canaan of a felt salvation,” and the tie between them was of that peculiar closeness which binds the convert to the instrument of his awakening. To sit at the same board with Mr. Barrow, to concur in his motions, to feel that his own plain sense and mercantile exactness were as a staff in the hand of the minister’s fervid and somewhat vague benevolence, was a satisfaction to Gideon deeper than any he derived from the balance-sheet, which, indeed, he never permitted himself to regard with complacency, lest it should become a snare.

The Charity School had been founded, as its prospectus declared, “for the instruction of the children of the poor in the rudiments of letters and the principles of the Protestant religion as professed by the dissenting congregations of Hanbury,” and its master was Mr. Abel Barrow, the minister’s only son. This young man had been bred, at some expense, at a seminary in the west, and had returned to Hanbury with a certain elegance of person and a fluency of speech which his father mistook for genius, and which the congregation, ever ready to honour the prophet in his seed, accepted as the promise of a second Barrow. If there were those who whispered that the schoolmaster’s discipline was somewhat sharp, and that the boys of the Charity School went in terror of his cane, such whispers were easily referred to the proverbial refractoriness of the poor, and to that godly severity which Solomon himself had sanctioned.

It was on a Thursday afternoon in November, when the fog hung low over Hanbury and the lamps in the shop windows made yellow haloes in the damp, that Gideon Tapp, having occasion to carry a small order of gray worsted stockings to the school-house in Pigeon Lane, was shown by the porter into the lobby which divided the master’s dwelling from the schoolroom, and there, waiting a moment for the master to appear, heard through the imperfectly closed door of the schoolroom a sound that made him stand still and hold his breath.

It was the sound of a cane upon a boy’s hand—not the single sharp stroke of a hasty correction, but a measured, deliberate succession of blows, six or seven in number, each falling with a dull, flat impact, and each followed by a smothered cry, as of a child who has been taught that to cry aloud is to double the offence. Then came the master’s voice, cool and didactic, with the elegant modulation of the seminary: “You will learn, Thomas Wildgoose, that idleness is a sin against the light, and that the rod is the appointed means of driving it out. Hold out your other hand.”

Gideon stood as one turned to stone. He was a man of peace, and his blood, though not hot, was honest; and there rose in him, with the suddenness of a physical nausea, a revolt which he could not have justified by any text, but which he felt to be of the same stuff as the Gospel. The door was ajar; he pushed it open, and saw the boy—a little fellow of nine, the son of a widow who kept a stall in the shambles—standing in the midst of the empty benches, his face white and his eyes dry with a terror that had gone beyond tears, while the master, a fine young man in a blue coat and a cambric stock, stood over him with the cane raised.

“Mr. Barrow,” said Gideon, and his voice sounded strange in his own ears, “I am sorry to interrupt you, but I have brought the stockings for the boys, and I would speak with you a moment in the lobby.”

The master turned, and a faint colour came into his handsome face; but he was self-possessed, and laying down the cane with a smile, as of one caught in a harmless pleasantry, he followed Gideon into the lobby and closed the door behind him.

“You will excuse me, Mr. Tapp,” he said, with an air of condescension, “but I am obliged to maintain discipline. The boy is obstinate, and the rod is the only argument that reaches his understanding.”

“Mr. Barrow,” said Gideon, and he was aware that his hands were trembling, “I have seen something this afternoon that I cannot reconcile with my conscience. That child is the widow Wildgoose’s son, and his father was a good man and a member of our congregation. I do not think our Lord would have had him beaten so.”
