The air in the Cloth Hall at Cragg’s Fold was thick with the scent of raw wool, coal smoke, and the damp wool coats of the men who filled it. To Mrs. Eliza Hargreaves, it smelled of contempt. She stood alone in the vast, echoing chamber, a black-clad figure amidst a sea of drab browns and greys. The merchants, men with hard faces and harder hands, parted before her like water around a stone, their conversations dropping to sibilant whispers as she passed. She was a widow, and worse, a woman of business. In the world of the West Riding clothiers, she was an anomaly, a creature to be pitied or scorned, but never taken seriously.

Her small, gloved hands held a sample of the last piece of cloth to come from the Hargreaves looms: a good, strong kersey, its weave tight and even. It was the work of Mr. Fell, their best weaver, and it was perfect. But perfection in the cloth was not the currency of this hall. Here, reputation and male fellowship were everything, and Eliza possessed neither.

Her eyes scanned the crowd, looking for the one face she knew would not be averted. She found him holding court near the great iron stove, Mr. Josiah Croft. He was a man built like a bull, with a broad chest and a jowly face that broke into a smug, oily smile when he saw her. He was her late husband’s partner in the Bracken Mill, and the man who had so carefully, so legally, cheated him. Arthur, with his gentle soul and his faith in the goodness of man, had signed papers he barely understood, papers that had left Croft with the lion’s share of the profits and Arthur with a mountain of debt and a broken heart. He had died of a fever last winter, leaving Eliza with nothing but a crumbling house and a single, contested share in a failing mill.

Croft had offered her a pittance for it. “A kindness, my dear Mrs. Hargreaves,” he had said, his voice dripping with a sympathy that did not reach his small, pig-like eyes. “A woman in your position… let me relieve you of this burden.”

She had refused. The refusal was all she had left of Arthur’s legacy, a final, stubborn act of defiance. In the months since, she had learned more than any clergyman’s wife ought to know. She had taught herself bookkeeping by candlelight, her fingers tracing the columns of figures until her eyes burned. She had walked to the mill, her face set against the wind and the pitying stares of the weavers, and insisted on seeing the ledgers for herself. She had discovered the subtle ways Croft was still bleeding the mill dry, selling the best cloth through his own channels and leaving the shared accounts with the dregs.

Now, she had brought the product of her stubbornness to the market. She walked directly towards Croft’s circle of associates. The men fell silent, their faces a mixture of amusement and discomfort. Croft straightened his waistcoat, a portly king in his own small domain.

“Mrs. Hargreaves,” he boomed, his voice falsely hearty. “A surprise to see you here. Has the vicarage garden proved less fruitful than you’d hoped?”

A ripple of coarse laughter went through the men. Eliza felt a hot flush of shame, but she forced herself to meet his gaze. She held up the sample of kersey. “I have brought cloth to sell, Mr. Croft. From the Bracken Mill.”

Croft’s smile tightened. “Ah, yes. The last of the stock, I presume? A sad business. I was just telling these gentlemen that I intend to buy the whole concern, lock, stock, and barrel. To put it out of its misery.” He looked at the men, seeking their approval. “The best thing for everyone.”

“It is not for sale,” Eliza said, her voice quiet but clear. “This cloth, however, is. It is a fine kersey. You can see the quality for yourselves.” She held the sample out to the man nearest to her, a thick-set clothier named Mr. Dawkins.

He looked at her hand as if it were something unclean, then at Croft. He did not take the cloth. “We do our business with men, Mrs. Hargreaves,” he grunted, turning his shoulder to her.

The snub was a physical blow. Eliza stood her ground, her arm still outstretched. She could feel the heat of a hundred eyes on her, could hear the whispers. *Poor thing… lost her senses… the parson’s widow.* She wanted to run, to flee back to the quiet solitude of the vicarage, but the image of Arthur, his face pale and defeated as he looked at the ledgers, held her fast.

She moved to the next group, and the next. Each time, the story was the same. Averted eyes, muttered excuses, the solid wall of male solidarity. They would not look at her cloth. They would not even acknowledge it. She was not a seller; she was a spectacle. Croft followed at a distance, a smug shadow, enjoying her humiliation. He was the shepherd, and these were his sheep. She was the wolf, but a toothless one.

Finally, she came to the end of the hall. There, sitting on a barrel and whittling a piece of wood, was an old man with a face like a dried apple. He was Mr. Asa Penn, a man so old and so disagreeable that he had outlived his rivals and now did business only when it suited him. He was said to have a keen eye for cloth and an even keener eye for a cheat.

Eliza hesitated, then approached him. “Mr. Penn?”

He looked up, his eyes sharp and clear. “I see you, woman. What is it you want?”

“I wish to sell this cloth,” she said, her voice trembling slightly. She held out the sample.

He ignored the sample. He looked at her face, at the desperate pride in her eyes. “You’re Hargreaves’s widow.”
