It was upon a raw, grey morning in November, with a damp mist rolling up from the chalk of the North Downs, that Mr. Jacob Carstairs presented himself at my lodgings in George Street, Croydon. He was a thickset, upright man of fifty, with a weather-beaten face and a bristling grey moustache, who carried himself with the unmistakable bearing of an old soldier. His hands, as he laid them upon my table, were coarse and blotched with the stains of his present trade, yet his back was as stiff as a ramrod and his eye as resolute.

"You will excuse the early hour, Mr. Prior," said he, "but the matter is one which will not brook an hour's delay. I am a shopkeeper now, as you may see by my hands, but I served twenty years in the 4th Dragoon Guards, and saw things in the Punjab which a man does not easily forget."

I poured him out a glass of brandy, for I perceived that he was labouring under some strong emotion, and bade him take his time.

"I keep a draper's shop at the corner of Scarbrook Road," he continued. "It is a respectable business, and I have my wife and daughter to think of. But I have had a shock, Mr. Prior, which has shaken me to the marrow. Last Tuesday afternoon, about four o'clock, a gentleman walked into my shop. He was a tall, well-dressed man, with a sallow face, a small dark moustache, and a curious droop of the left eyelid. He wished to purchase some silk handkerchiefs. My daughter served him. I was behind the counter myself, measuring a piece of calico, when I chanced to look up and catch his eye."

Carstairs paused and drank a little of the brandy.

"Well, sir," he went on, "I knew him at once, though it is fifteen years since I last set eyes on him. He was a man named Eustace Girdwood, who held a commission in a native cavalry regiment stationed at Peshawar. I was there with my troop on detached duty. One night, in the cantonment, I saw him murder his native servant—a deed as cold-blooded and deliberate as ever was done. The man had threatened to expose some irregularity in the regimental accounts, and Girdwood strangled him with his own hands in the compound. I was the only witness. I was about to go to the authorities when Girdwood was taken ill with fever, and by the time he recovered, the body had been found and the death put down to a wild beast. I had no proof, save my own eyes, and I was advised by my colonel to hold my tongue. Girdwood resigned his commission shortly afterwards and left India."

"And you recognised him on Tuesday?"

"I did, sir, as sure as I see you now. He gave a great start, and his face went the colour of tallow. He saw that I knew him. But he was a wonderful hand at controlling himself. He paid for his handkerchiefs, said something civil to my daughter, and walked out."

"Did he return?"

"No, but something else returned. I live over the shop, Mr. Prior. Last night, about two in the morning, I was aroused by a crash of glass. I seized my revolver and ran downstairs. The back window of the shop was smashed, and someone had forced an entry. I searched the place, and found that nothing had been taken—nothing, that is, save one thing."

"What was that?"

"A framed photograph which hung behind the counter. It was a group of the officers of my old regiment, taken at Meerut in '78. It was the only thing of value to a stranger that was in the shop."

I leaned forward in my chair. "You think Girdwood took it?"

"I am sure of it. He wanted to see if I was in the picture. If I am, he knows that I was at Peshawar, and that my evidence might hang him. If I am not, he has nothing to fear. That photograph is his death warrant, or mine."

I tapped the table and reflected for a moment. "You have the negative, or another print?"

"I have a small print in an album upstairs."

"Good. And what is Girdwood's present address?"

"I do not know. He gave no address. But my daughter tells me that he spoke of staying with a friend at a house called 'The Lindens,' somewhere on the Purley Road."

I took down my hat and stick. "We will go there at once, Mr. Carstairs. The matter is serious, but I think we may be in time to avert any tragedy."

It was a short drive in a fly to Purley, and we soon located "The Lindens," a substantial red-brick villa standing back from the road in its own grounds. As we pulled up at the gate, I observed a tall figure emerging from the front door. He was dressed in a dark overcoat, and carried a walking-stick. Even at that distance I could see the sallow face and the drooping eyelid.

"That is he!" whispered Carstairs, gripping my arm.

"Keep in the background," I said. "I will speak to him."

I stepped forward and accosted him. "Mr. Girdwood, I believe?"

He stopped short and looked at me with a keen, suspicious glance. "You have the advantage of me, sir," said he, in a smooth, even voice.

"My name is Prior. I am making some inquiries on behalf of Mr. Carstairs, the draper of Scarbrook Road."

A faint change came over his face, a tightening of the lips, but he answered with perfect composure: "I do not know the gentleman."

"You purchased some handkerchiefs from him on Tuesday," said I.

"It is possible. I deal at many shops. But what of it?"

"Someone broke into his shop last night and stole a photograph."

"Indeed? A singular theft. But I fail to see what it has to do with me."
