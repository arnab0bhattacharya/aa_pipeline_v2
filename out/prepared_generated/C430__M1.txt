The rain in Islington had a way of making the soot on the bricks weep, turning the streets into slick, black ribbons. Mr. Silas Croft, undertaker of Croft & Son, found the walk to Mr. Alistair Finch’s office an unpleasant one, not merely for the weather. It was the nature of his errand that made the damp chill seep into his bones. He clutched his umbrella, its black silk taut as a drum, and felt the weight of his own profession pressing down upon him. He dealt in finality, in the quiet, respectful heft of a life concluded. This… this was something else.

He found the office of the private investigator on a narrow lane off Upper Street, a place where the respectable facade of London gave way to more pragmatic concerns. A small, brass plaque read ‘A. Finch, Inquiries & Discreet Solutions’. Croft pushed open the door, the bell above it giving a dull, tired jangle. The room was a chaos of papers, maps, and strange contraptions—a camera on a tripod, a magnifying glass the size of a saucer. The air smelled of stale pipe tobacco and ozone.

The man behind the desk did not look up. He was lean, sharp-featured, and peered at a document through a monocle, his brow furrowed in concentration. “I am not purchasing almanacs, sir, nor am I in need of spiritual guidance,” he said, his voice dry as autumn leaves.

“My name is Silas Croft,” the undertaker began, his voice a low rumble. “Croft & Son, of Holloway Road. I am in need of your… inquiries.”

Alistair Finch finally lowered the monocle, his grey eyes fixing on Croft with an unnerving intensity. “An undertaker. You have no shortage of clients, I should think. What sort of inquiry could a man of your station possibly have?”

“It is a client of mine, sir. Or rather, the family of one.” Croft took a hesitant step forward, his polished boots squeaking on the floorboards. “A Mr. Abernathy. Thaddeus Abernathy. He passed away last Tuesday. Heart failure, the family said. We handled the arrangements. The funeral is today, at St. Jude’s.”

“A sad but common story,” Finch said, leaning back in his chair and steepling his fingers. “Get to the crux of it, Mr. Croft. My time is, as they say, money.”

Croft swallowed, his throat suddenly dry. “The coffin, sir. We delivered it to the church this morning. My men, two strong lads, could barely manage it up the steps. They complained of the weight. I checked the manifest myself before we left the workshop. Mr. Abernathy was a slight man, a bookkeeper by trade. The death certificate lists his weight at nine stone, four pounds. The coffin, with him inside it… it felt as though it held a man of twenty stone. More, even.”

Finch’s expression did not change, but a flicker of interest sparked in his eyes. “Coffins are heavy things, Mr. Croft. Oak, brass fittings, lead lining.”

“I know the weight of my own work, sir!” Croft’s voice rose with a tremor of indignation. “I have been in this trade for thirty years. I know the heft of a man, be he stout or thin. This was wrong. It was… dense. Like it was filled with stone.” He paused, gathering himself. “But that is not the worst of it. The family… the widow, Mrs. Abernathy, and her brother, a Mr. Davies… they were agitated. Hurried. They insisted the service be held at the earliest possible moment. And when I offered them a final moment of viewing, a customary courtesy, they refused. Quite vehemently. The brother said it was… morbid. Unnecessary.”

A silence settled in the cluttered room, broken only by the rhythmic drip of water from a leaky gutter outside. Finch picked up his monocle, polishing it on his sleeve. “A family wishes to bury their loved one quickly and without ceremony. Grief takes many forms. Some wish to linger, others to sever the tie as swiftly as possible. And a coffin feels heavier when one is grieving. A trick of the mind.”

“No,” Croft said, shaking his head firmly. “This was no trick. It was a fact of muscle and sinew. And their haste felt… not like grief, but like fear. Or guilt.”

Finch stood up and walked to the window, peering down at the wet street. “What is it you suspect, Mr. Croft? That they have buried a sack of gold with the old man? An inheritance to keep it from the taxman?”

“I don’t know what I suspect,” Croft admitted, his shoulders slumping. “That is why I am here. Something is amiss. It is a violation of my trade. A lie is being buried in my coffin, and my name is on the side of it. The grave is not yet filled. They are lowering him now, but the earth will not be returned until the mourners depart. There is still time.”

Finch turned from the window, a slow, predatory smile touching his lips. “Time, yes. And a puzzle. I must confess, Mr. Croft, this is infinitely more interesting than tracing a philandering husband or recovering a stolen pearl necklace. Very well. Describe the family to me. Every detail.”

Croft did so, painting a picture of the pale, bird-like widow who wrung her hands incessantly, and her brother, a florid, bull-necked man who smelled of gin and impatience. Finch listened, his sharp mind dissecting the information. When Croft had finished, the investigator grabbed a long, dark coat from a hook.

“Come,” he said, striding towards the door. “We shall go and pay our respects to the late Mr. Abernathy. Or, more accurately, to the heavy secret he has taken to his grave.”

They arrived at St. Jude’s churchyard as the last of the mourners, a small and sombre cluster, were making their way towards the gate. The rain had softened to a fine mist, clinging to the yew trees and the granite headstones. The grave, a dark wound in the green lawn, was indeed open. The coffin, a handsome affair of polished mahogany, rested on the lowering straps. It sat unnaturally low, its weight pulling the straps taut.
