The rain lashed against the high, narrow windows of the pensionnat in Lyon, a relentless, hissing curtain that seemed to blur the world outside into a grey, indistinct smudge. Inside the small, dimly lit study, the fire had burned down to a bed of sullen, red coals, casting long, trembling shadows across the walls where the portraits of long-dead saints hung with mournful solemnity. I sat there, clutching a cold cup of tea that I had not tasted, watching the steam rise and dissipate like the vapors of my own exhausted hopes.

It was Miss Alcott, my governess and chaperone, who had sent for me. She was a woman of fifty, with a face like a map of old scars, stern and unyielding, yet beneath that granite exterior beat a heart of fierce, protective fire. She did not speak at first. She merely pointed to the chair opposite her, and I obeyed, my legs feeling as though they were made of lead. When I sat, she fixed me with a gaze that pierced the veil of my ignorance, sharp and unblinking as a hawk’s eye scanning the valley for prey.

"Miss Elowen," she began, her voice low and gravelly, devoid of any wasted ornament. "You have been weeping again."

I looked away, my cheeks burning with a shame that was hotter than the coals. "I am not weeping, Miss Alcott. I am merely... troubled."

"Troubled," she repeated, the word landing on the floor with the weight of a stone. "A fine word for the state of a woman who has surrendered her reason to a stranger’s smile. Tell me, Elowen, what does Count de Vaucluse offer you? Gold? Power? A seat in Parliament?"

"He offers me love," I said, and the words felt both precious and foolish on my tongue. "He is charming, Miss Alcott. He is the most refined man I have ever met. He speaks of poetry, of art, of the soul. He does not care for my family’s money. He tells me he cares for nothing but me."

Miss Alcott let out a dry, humorless laugh that sounded like dry leaves scraping over stone. "Nothing but you. A dangerous currency, that. Let us examine this 'love' of his. Does he have a home? A fortune? A standing in society that would not crumble under the weight of your father’s scrutiny?"

"He is a count," I whispered, though I knew the title was all that remained of his family’s grandeur. "He says his debts are temporary. He says he has prospects."

"Prospects," she spat the word as if it were poison. "He is a beggar in a silk coat, Elowen. I have seen him. I have watched him when he thought I was not looking. He counts his coins with the anxiety of a miser and spends them with the desperation of a gambler. He is charming because he must be. It is his only trade. And you, who were sent here to learn to think, to learn to see, have chosen to be blinded by his manners."

I stood up, my heart pounding against my ribs like a trapped bird. "You are unfair! You are jealous of his attention. You are an old woman, Miss Alcott, living in this grey town, watching the rain. You have no concept of passion. You have no concept of what it is to feel truly alive."

"Passion," she said, rising slowly. She was shorter than me, but in that moment, she seemed to tower over the room, filled with a terrible, righteous fury. "Passion is not a fever to be indulged, but a fire to be controlled. If you burn down your house, you are no wiser for the warmth you felt while it was standing. You are speaking to me as if I were your mother, or a friend. I am neither. I am your guardian. And I tell you, with the weight of my own experience and the sorrow of having seen too many girls destroyed by such men, that he is a vampire. He feeds on your youth, your hope, and your innocence, and when he has drained you, he will leave you empty and discarded."

"You cannot stop me," I said, my voice trembling, though not from fear of her, but from the terrifying power of my own desire. "I am of age. I can do as I please."

"You can," she agreed, her voice dropping to a whisper that was more chilling than a shout. "But you will pay for it. And I will not watch you make that payment. I have come too far, through too much suffering, to see you throw yourself into a pit for the sake of a man who does not even have the dignity of honesty."

She reached for her desk, her movements deliberate and cold. She pulled out a sheet of paper and a pen. The scratch of the nib against the paper was the only sound in the room, a sharp, rhythmic tearing that matched the anxiety in my chest.

"What are you doing?" I asked, a sudden, cold dread seeping into my bones.

"Writing to your father," she said, not looking up. "I am telling him that you are engaged to a man of no means, no character, and no future. I am telling him that you are in danger. And I am telling him that I will not be responsible for your ruin."

"You wouldn't," I gasped. "You can't. He would disown me. He would send me home in disgrace."

"Better disgrace than destruction," she said, her eyes finally meeting mine. They were wet, but unyielding. "I would sooner see you weeping in your father’s house, safe and sound, than see you weeping in a garret in Paris, alone and forgotten, with a husband who treats you as a burden. I have loved you, Elowen. I have loved you as a daughter. And it is this love that compels me to act, even if you hate me for it. Even if you curse my name until the day I die."
