Mr. Evelyn Vance, whose slim volume of verse, *Lilies of the Night-Bloom*, had been deemed by the more adventurous critics as a work of exquisite morbidity, felt that the proper way to arrive at Ashworth Hall was not by the grimy, rattling train, but to simply materialise, perhaps on a breath of attar of roses, in the middle of the drawing-room. As it was, the journey by rail had left him feeling decidedly unsubstantial, and the sight of a liveried coachman holding a card with his name upon it was a concession to a world of brute fact that he found profoundly tiresome.

His hostess, Lady Beatrice Ashworth, was a woman whose admiration for his work was so complete and so uncritical that she had, in a moment of passion, upholstered her morning-room in a shade of mauve she had christened ‘Vance’s Violet’. She met him in the hall, a cascade of silk and sentiment. “Mr. Vance!” she cried, her hands outstretched. “You have brought the soul of the century with you! Ashworth Hall is a barren wilderness of fox-hounds and common sense; you are our first flower of decadence.”

Evelyn, who had been contemplating the rather aggressive stag’s head above the fireplace, gave a faint, weary smile. “One does one’s best, Lady Beatrice. Though I fear my soul is rather delicate for a Leicestershire draught.”

From behind a copy of the *Sporting Times* came a snort. “Soul? Draught? You’ll find the draughts are real enough in this house,” said a voice like gravel being mixed with port. This was Sir Reginald Ashworth, a man built on the same principles as his ancestral home: solid, square, and utterly impervious to ornament. He was a squire of the old school, a man who believed that the only true poetry was the cry of a fox and the only fitting conclusion to a day was the death of a pheasant. He regarded Evelyn’s lavender cravat with the profound suspicion of a man confronting a new and particularly virulent type of fungus.

“Sir Reginald,” Evelyn murmured, inclining his head. “It is an honour.”

“Is it?” Sir Reginald grunted, folding his paper. “We’re shooting tomorrow. Do you shoot?”

“I,” said Evelyn, with perfect composure, “shoot arrows. But only of the metaphorical kind. They are, I find, far more likely to find their mark.”

Lady Beatrice clapped her hands in delight. “Oh, how marvellous! Reginald, you see? He speaks in aphorisms!”

“I see he speaks in nonsense,” her husband retorted. “Aphorism is just a long word for a short excuse not to do any work.”

The weekend unfolded as a series of small, exquisite agonies. At dinner, Evelyn attempted to discourse on the spiritual significance of a peacock feather he had found in a vase, only to be interrupted by Sir Reginald, who was dissecting a grouse and holding forth on the superior ballistics of a new twelve-bore. The conversation was a battlefield where wit was no match for wellington boots. Evelyn, seeing that his delicate epigrams were being trampled underfoot like so many wildflowers, retreated into a silence of polished jade.

The next morning was the ordeal of the shoot. Evelyn was provided with a gun that felt as alien in his hands as a crucifix might in the paws of a panther. He stood amidst the swirling mist and the rustle of tweed, a splash of dark satin in a sea of drab wool. Sir Reginald’s beaters drove the pheasants over the line with great efficiency. The other guns—stout, red-faced men with names like Biffy and Tuppy—blazed away with cheerful incompetence. Evelyn, however, merely held his weapon and contemplated the aesthetic arrangement of the birds against the pewter sky.

“You are not shooting, Mr. Vance,” Sir Reginald observed, coming to stand beside him. His voice was not unkind, merely blunt. It was the voice of a man who dealt in facts, and the fact was that a gun in the hand that did not fire was a useless object.

“On the contrary, Sir Reginald,” Evelyn replied, gazing at a pheasant as it soared overhead, a jewel of crimson and emerald. “I am shooting constantly. I am shooting the moment. I am shooting the light upon its wings. To bring it down would be to destroy the very poem one has come to admire.”

Sir Reginald stared at him, his brow furrowed. “A poem? It’s a bird. A damn fine one for the pot. Are you telling me you’d rather look at it than eat it?”

“Of course,” said Evelyn. “To consume a thing is to end its beauty. To admire it is to make it immortal.”

The squire shook his head, a gesture of profound and final bewilderment. “You’re a rum cove, Mr. Vance. A very rum cove indeed. Your soup will get cold.”

That evening, as the gentlemen sat over their brandy, the air thick with the scent of cigars and masculine certainty, Sir Reginald turned to Evelyn. “My wife tells me you write about flowers and jewels and suchlike. That true?”

“I find them to be more reliable subjects than, say, the hunt,” Evelyn said smoothly. “They do not run away, and their death, when it comes, is always graceful.”

“Hah,” Sir Reginald grunted. He poured himself another measure of brandy. “I once knew a woman who was all jewels and flowers. Beautiful creature. Married a friend of mine for his money. Ruined him. Broke his heart. He took to drink and died in a ditch in Melton Mowbray.” He took a long swallow. “She had a face like a lily, he used to say. But a lily’s a funeral flower, when you think about it.”

Evelyn was silent. For the first time, he felt the weight of something other than his own exquisite sensibility. It was a coarse, heavy, painful thing, this piece of reality, and it sat in the middle of the room like a block of granite.
