I had remarked to Thorne, on more than one occasion, that the November fog seemed to bring out the singular cases. He would smile at this and fill his pipe, saying nothing. On the morning in question, however, the fog had thickened over the Thames until the lamps of the city were mere yellow blurs, and Thorne himself sat forward in his armchair with his hands clasped and his sharp grey eyes fixed on the visitor who had just risen from his chair.

The man before us was tall and broad, with the weathered complexion of one who had spent his life under harsh skies. His white hair was cut close to his head, and his left hand, resting on his walking stick, lacked two fingers. He wore a good black coat that was brushed clean of dust, and his boots, though old, were sound.

"You are Captain Bargrave," said Thorne, "late of the merchant vessel *Persephone*, retired to Greenwich this past year. You have a son from whom you have been estranged for three years, and you wish me to find him."

Bargrave stared at him. "How the devil do you know all that?"

Thorne leaned back. "Nothing very extraordinary. The tar under your nails and the particular callus on your right palm mark you as a seaman of long standing. The mourning band on your hat, not yet frayed, tells me the loss is recent. Your coat is of good cloth but cut three years ago, which suggests reduced circumstances since retirement. The letter you sent ahead mentioned only that you wished to consult me on a private matter, but you have a telegram in your breast pocket that you have been turning over since you sat down. It concerns your son, or you would not be here."

Bargrave drew a long breath. "You are right on every count. My wife died last month, and with her passing I resolved to find my boy. He left home three years ago after a quarrel. I traced him to the docks at Wapping, where he was employed by a firm called Pell and Company. I went there yesterday. They told me they had no record of him. I pressed them, and they showed me their books. His name was not in them."

"What is your son's name?" I asked.

"Stephen," said the captain. "Stephen Bargrave. He is four-and-twenty."

Thorne rose and crossed to the window, where he stood looking into the fog. "Tell me about Pell and Company. What do they ship?"

"Guano, mostly. From South American ports. They have a warehouse on Bellman's Wharf."

"And who keeps the books?"

"A man called Ferris. He is the manager. The owner is a Mr. Pell, but I did not see him. Ferris was smooth enough in his manner, but I did not care for the look of him. He had the hands of a prize-fighter and the smile of a lawyer."

Thorne turned from the window. "You said they pay their men in cash."

Bargrave looked startled. "I did not say that. But it is true. Stephen mentioned it in his last letter to his mother. He said the wages were good and always paid in coin on Saturday night. He thought it strange that no clerk was employed."

"It is strange," said Thorne. "A firm that ships guano in quantity and pays its labourers in ready money without the intermediary of a clerk is a firm that invites questions. I think we shall pay a visit to Bellman's Wharf. Captain Bargrave, you will oblige me by remaining here with Dr. Hewitt. I may need you before the day is out, but for the present I would rather approach Pell and Company without a father's anxiety in my train."

Thorne was gone for the better part of four hours. I made what conversation I could with the captain, who sat rigidly in his chair and spoke little. When Thorne returned, his boots were mired and his collar was damp, but his eyes had the bright, focused look that I had learned to associate with progress.

"Well?" I asked.

He threw his hat on the table and sat down. "I have been to Bellman's Wharf. The warehouse is a large brick building with a single entrance facing the river and a yard behind it enclosed by a wall twelve feet high. There is a counting-house at the front, where Ferris sits. He is a smooth-faced man of forty, with a scar across his knuckles that he acquired, I should think, in circumstances unconnected with commerce. I posed as a shipper seeking a carrier for a consignment of manufactured goods to Lisbon. He was courteous but guarded. He showed me the warehouse, which was half full of crates marked for various destinations. I observed two things. First, every crate bore two weight markings, one stencilled on the side and one on a paper label pasted over the first. The second marking was invariably lower. Second, there were no scales in the warehouse."

"No scales?" said Bargrave.

"None. A warehouse that ships bulk goods by weight and keeps no scales is a warehouse that does not wish its weights to be verified. I made inquiries at the customs house nearby. The clerk there, a garrulous old man named Tibbs, told me that Pell and Company's shipments are regularly lighter on arrival than on the bills of lading. The discrepancies have been noted but never pursued, because the consignees have not complained. This suggested to me that the consignees are parties to the fraud, or that the goods diverted from the shipments are of a nature that makes complaint inconvenient."

"Smuggling," I said.

"Of a sort. But there is a simpler explanation. The guano trade is legitimate and the guano itself is genuine. What is not genuine is the weight. The crates are packed, weighed, and marked correctly at departure. During transit, a portion of the contents is removed and replaced with ballast. The difference in weight represents the profit. But this would require cooperation at the receiving end, which suggests a network rather than a single rogue. And it does not account for Stephen Bargrave."
