The wind off the ice field had a way of getting into a man’s skull, not merely his clothes, and Dr. Elias Thorne had long since stopped trying to keep it out. He stood at the door of the station’s infirmary—a tarred wooden hut that smelled of carbolic and old blood—and watched the light bleed from the sky. It was a colour he had no name for, a kind of bruised pewter that seemed to press down on the world. Kerguelen’s Rest, they called this place, a grand name for a cluster of black rocks and boiling try-pots clinging to the edge of the Antarctic.

The station manager, a bull-necked man named Kincaid, was bellowing at a group of men near the flensing plan. His voice, raw with cheap tobacco and authority, cut through the constant shriek of the wind. Thorne ignored him. He was more concerned with the state of his instruments, the rust that bloomed on them overnight like a pox, and the growing sense of his own uselessness. The men here didn’t get sick so much as they got broken. Crushed fingers, gashed limbs, a lungful of something foul from the try-works. He would stitch and splint and dose them with laudanum, and they would go back out into the cold to do it all again.

It was a young hand named Petersen who found it, two days later. He came running to the infirmary, his face the colour of the rendered blubber, his breath coming in panicked gasps. He couldn’t speak, only point toward the eastern edge of the bay, where the glacier met the sea in a jumble of shattered blue ice.

“A whale, Petersen?” Thorne asked, pulling on his heavy coat. “A seal?”

The boy shook his head, his eyes wide. “No, sir. It’s… it’s a devil, sir. A frozen devil.”

Thorne followed him out, the cold immediately biting at his cheeks. They crunched over the shingle beach, past the stinking, fly-blown carcasses of the last hunt, and out onto the slick, grey-green ice. The wind was a physical presence, a hand shoving at their backs. Petersen led him to a deep crevasse, a wound in the glacier’s face that had been opened by a recent calving.

It was lodged there, perhaps twenty feet down, a dark shape entombed in the clear, ancient ice. Thorne knelt at the edge, his heart thudding a strange, slow rhythm against his ribs. At first, he thought it was a trick of the light, a shadow in the ice. But as he stared, the details resolved. It was an animal, but like no animal he had ever seen, or read of, or imagined.

It was perhaps twelve feet long, its body sinuous and powerful. Its hide was not furred, but covered in a dense, dark pelt that looked almost like felted wool. The head was the most extraordinary thing. It was not the sleek skull of a seal or the blunt head of a whale. It was almost… lupine, but the muzzle was shorter, blunter, and the jaws, frozen slightly open, were lined with teeth that were neither simple cones nor serrated triangles, but a bizarre mixture of both. And its limbs—it had limbs, four of them, not flippers—ended in wide, webbed paws tipped with formidable, curved claws. It was a creature of contradictions, a seal that had learned to walk, a wolf that had taken to the sea.

“God’s teeth,” Thorne whispered, the sound snatched away by the wind. This was a discovery. Not just for him, but for science. For the whole world. A missing link, a relic from an age of monsters.

He scrambled back from the edge, his mind racing. He had to get it out. He had to preserve it. He had to—

“Well, well. What have we here?”

Thorne spun around. Kincaid stood there, a group of his men behind him, their faces a mixture of fear and avarice. The manager’s small, piggy eyes were fixed on the creature in the ice, and Thorne saw not wonder in them, but calculation.

“It’s a specimen, Kincaid,” Thorne said, his voice tight. “An unknown animal. Of immense scientific importance.”

“Is that so?” Kincaid grunted. He spat a stream of tobacco juice onto the ice. “Looks like a lot of money to me. There’s a market for curiosities in London and New York. A thing like this… a genuine sea-devil… it’d fetch a pretty penny. More than a season’s worth of oil, I’d wager.”

“You can’t be serious,” Thorne said, a cold fury rising in him. “This isn’t a piece of ivory to be sold to a collector. This is a discovery. It belongs in a museum, in the hands of men who can study it.”

“And who decides that?” Kincaid stepped closer, his bulk blocking out the pale light. “You? A doctor with a few books and a lot of airs? I’m the manager of this station. I found it on my patch. It’s mine to sell.” He turned to his men. “Get some picks and a block and tackle. We’re hauling it out.”

Thorne watched them go, a helpless rage churning in his gut. He knew Kincaid. The man was a brute, but not a fool. He would hack the creature out of the ice, ship it north in a crate of sawdust, and sell it to the highest bidder. It would end up in some grotesque private collection, a novelty to be gawked at. The science would be lost.

That night, Thorne couldn’t sleep. The wind howled around the hut, and he imagined he could hear the creature calling to him from its icy tomb. He got up, lit a lantern, and pulled on his coat. He had to see it again. He had to document it, at least. He took his notebook and a pencil.

The moon was out, a pale, dead eye in the sky, casting a silver light on the ice. The crevasse was a dark slash in the glacier’s face. Thorne knelt at the edge, holding his lantern high.
