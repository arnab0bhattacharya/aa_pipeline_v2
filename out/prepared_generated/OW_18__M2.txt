The kingdom of Aethelgard was a place of such exquisite symmetry that it seemed less a nation than a jewel set in the crown of the earth. It was composed entirely of towers, white as bone and slender as the fingers of virgins, and these towers were connected by bridges of glass and hung with gardens that bloomed with flowers whose names had been forgotten by the rest of the world, but which the King had remembered with a pedantic fondness. There were no streets in Aethelgard, for streets are vulgar things that invite mud and muddles; there were only paths of crushed pearl, laid out in geometric perfection, leading from one spire to another. It was a kingdom without shadows, or at least without the kind of shadows that imply ignorance or poverty. The sun here was a benevolent tyrant, and it shone with a consistency that was almost moral in its rigidity.

In the highest of these towers, a room of pale blue velvet and silver mirrors, lived Prince Valerius. He was eighteen years old, and he had never seen the world outside the glass walls of his prison, for the world, his father told him, was a chaotic mess of bad manners and ill-fitting clothes. Valerius was a beautiful boy, with the kind of beauty that is often found in things that are preserved under glass: flawless, cold, and slightly artificial. He had been educated by tutors who spoke in iambic pentameter and ate their meals in silence, so that he had developed a taste for the abstract and a distaste for the concrete. He knew the chemical composition of the air, but not the smell of rain on hot pavement. He knew the history of the sun, but not the warmth of it on his skin.

On the day of his eighteenth birthday, the King descended the spiral stairs, a figure of imposing dignity, dressed in robes of emerald silk that rustled with the sound of expensive secrets. He brought with him the High Minister, Lord Castellan, a man whose face was a map of wrinkles, each one carved by the specific worry of maintaining order in a disorderly universe.

"You are of age, Valerius," said the King, his voice echoing in the vast, silent room. "And as is the custom of our house, you may ask for one thing. It shall be granted, provided it is not treasonous, nor too expensive, nor, above all, too interesting."

Valerius turned from the window, where he had been watching a sparrow that had made the fatal error of landing on the ledge. The bird was brown and awkward, and it pecked at the glass with a frantic, ugly energy. Valerius had never seen a sparrow before, and he found its existence deeply offensive. It was so uncoordinated.

"I wish to see a poor man," Valerius said.

The King paused, his hand still on the doorknob. He looked at his son with an expression of mild bewilderment, as if the Prince had asked for a wheel of cheese or a live lobster. "A poor man? My dear boy, we do not keep such things in Aethelgard. It is bad for the morale of the staff, and it ruins the aesthetic balance of the gardens."

"I do not wish to keep him," Valerius replied, his voice soft but firm. "I merely wish to see one. I have read in the old books that they are the only people who possess the luxury of despair. I find the concept fascinating."

Lord Castellan coughed, a sound like dry leaves skittering across a pavement. "Your Majesty, the Prince is young. He has been isolated. It is a phase. I would suggest we grant him a new lute, or perhaps a dog of the rarest breed. A poor man is not a commodity we can easily source. The last time we attempted to import one, the customs officers fainted at the sight of his tattered coat. It was quite distressing for everyone involved."

"Fetch him," the King said, with a wave of his hand that dismissed the subject and the Minister simultaneously. "But make it quick. I have a dinner party with the Ambassador from the Land of Flat Maps, and I cannot have the Prince brooding on poverty while I am discussing the tariffs on saffron. It ruins the digestif."

Lord Castellan bowed low and exited, his footsteps sounding like the ticking of a clock that had been wound too tight.

The search for a poor man was not a simple task. In Aethelgard, poverty was a theoretical concept, studied by philosophers but never encountered by the eyes. The ministers sent out scouts to the lower districts, but the lower districts were merely the bases of the towers, paved with marble and lined with fountains that sang in minor keys. There were no slums, for slums are ugly, and ugliness is the one crime that cannot be washed away with water or covered up with paint.

The scouts returned empty-handed, their faces pale with confusion. They had found beggars, yes, but they were beggars of a very specific type: they were actors paid to look wretched for the amusement of the tourists, and they carried hidden pockets full of gold coins that they dropped for the onlookers to pick up, a game that had become so popular that it had started a minor war in the neighboring province.

"Try the markets," the King ordered, from his throne room, which was decorated with the heads of extinct birds. "There must be someone there who has lost his wallet. Or perhaps someone who has never had one. That would be more authentic."

The High Minister sent a team to the Grand Bazaar, a place of such overwhelming splendor that it made the eye water. They found a man sitting in the shadow of a silk drape. He was wearing a coat that had been patched with the velvet from a discarded cushion, and his hat was made of woven straw that had turned yellow with age. He was eating an apple, but he was doing it with a delicacy that suggested he had never eaten one before, or that he was afraid of it.
