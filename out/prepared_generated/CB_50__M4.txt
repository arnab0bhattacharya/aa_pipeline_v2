The great hall of the Athénée de Sainte-Waudru had been scrubbed and garnished until it shone, though the day was one of those damp, grey Februaries peculiar to our Low Countries, when the sky seems a lid of hammered pewter clamped down upon the town, and the very candles sweat and gutter in the heavy air. The platform was draped in crimson cloth, and upon it, in a row of gilded chairs, sat the notables of the district: the échevins in their chains, two canons from the cathedral, and Madame la Directrice, Mademoiselle Verviers, in a gown of black silk that rustled with every self-satisfied inclination of her person. Behind her, like a dark hedge, stood the teachers of her establishment, of whom I was the most insignificant—the English mistress, engaged at a wage that would scarce keep a clerk in gloves, and regarded, I well knew, as a piece of useful furniture, necessary for the commercial value of the prospectus, but of no more account in the moral hierarchy of the school than the globe in the geography room or the skeleton in the closet of science.

I had been three years in that house. I had taught the daughters of advocates and brewers to parse the verbs of a language they would never speak, and to commit to memory the sentiments of poets whose hearts they would never understand. I had eaten my bread in a corner, and been grateful for it; I had borne the small tyrannies of Madame with the patience of one who has known sharper griefs, and had learned that a woman alone, without fortune or connexion, must purchase her daily bread with something more than her labour—she must purchase it with her pride.

The examination was the great affair of the winter term. Parents came from as far as Namur and Liège; the Bishop’s vicar was expected; and Madame had decreed that the English class should distinguish itself. To this end she had commanded a recitation—a poem, in English, of a moral and elevating character, to be delivered by the eldest pupil, Mademoiselle Célestine Duthoit, a tall, fair girl of sixteen, with a face as smooth as wax and a mind like a well-kept ledger.

The poem I had written myself. I confess it without shame, though I have never confessed it to another living soul. It was upon the text of perseverance in obscurity—the seed beneath the snow, the lamp behind the lattice, the heart that keeps its faith though no eye beholds it. I had written it in my own chamber, by the light of a single candle, after the house was still; and as I wrote, I had felt the hot tears rise, for I had been thinking of my own buried life, and of the years that were passing over me like waves over a drowned face. It was not great verse. But it was true, and it was mine.

Célestine learned it, as she learned everything, with docility. She recited it to me in the empty classroom, and her accent was like a knife in my ear, but her memory was exact. “Very well,” I said. “But you must feel it, mademoiselle. You must not merely say the words—you must feel them.”

“Feel them, mademoiselle?” she repeated, opening her blue eyes. “How does one feel them?”

I looked at her—at the smooth, untroubled brow, the hands folded so correctly, the whole composed and prosperous vacancy of her—and I said, “Never mind. Say them as I have taught you, and do not hurry.”

The hour came. The hall was full. The candles burned with a sickly yellow flame, and the breath of two hundred persons rose in a faint mist. Madame Verviers, seated in the centre of the platform, folded her hands upon her silk and smiled upon the assembly like a sovereign upon her subjects. The recitations began: a French ode upon the death of the Duc d’Orléans, delivered by a girl in ringlets; a Flemish dialogue between two shepherdesses, which provoked a gentle laugh; a pianoforte solo, executed with more precision than feeling. Then Madame rose, and the room hushed.

“We have now,” she said, in her clear, carrying voice, “the honour to present a piece of English verse, composed—I may say it without vanity—within these walls, and under the fostering care of this establishment. It is a composition which I trust will prove that we do not neglect the cultivation of the higher sentiments, even while we attend to the rudiments of grammar and the accomplishments of the toilette.” A little ripple of laughter, dutiful and brief. “Mademoiselle Duthoit will recite it.”

Célestine rose. She was pale, but composed. She wore a white frock, and her hair was braided in two thick bands. She went to the front of the platform, and for a moment she stood silent, looking out over the sea of faces. Then she began:

“The seed lies buried in the winter earth,
And knows not if the sun shall ever come;
It only knows the darkness and the dearth,
And keeps the promise of the life to come.”

Her voice was small but true. She spoke the lines as I had taught her—slowly, with a certain gravity. And as she spoke, I felt a strange and painful beating at my heart. It was my own thought, my own sorrow, my own stubborn hope, issuing from the lips of that fair, empty child, before all those strangers. I had not known it would move me so. I stood behind the row of teachers, my hands clasped before me, and I felt the blood come into my cheeks.

She finished. There was a murmur of approbation, and then the clapping began—polite at first, then warmer. Madame Verviers rose, and, advancing to the front of the platform, took Célestine by the hand and kissed her upon both cheeks. Then she turned to the assembly, and her voice was thick with a gracious emotion.
