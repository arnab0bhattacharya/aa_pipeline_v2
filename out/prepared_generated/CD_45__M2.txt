The morning fog lay thick and grey upon the estate of Lord Blackwood, a spectral shroud that seemed to cling to every ivy-covered wall and every bent limb of the ancient oak, as if the very air were mourning the impending departure of a soul that had never quite felt at home within it. Arthur Pendelton stood at the window of his study, his reflection ghostly and pale against the glass, staring out at the manicured lawns that stretched down to the river, now obscured by the murk. He was a young man of twenty-four, with a face that possessed a certain delicate refinement, the sort of beauty that belongs to those who have never known the rough hand of labor or the biting chill of want. He had been polished, scraped, and varnished by the benevolent hand of his patron, Lord Blackwood, until he shone like a piece of fine silverware, ready to be placed upon the table of high society. But today, the silver was tarnished. A single sheet of paper, yellowed with age and stained with the sweat of hands that had long since returned to the dust, lay upon the desk before him. It was a ledger, or rather, a fragment of one, brought to his attention by a coincidental accident of curiosity and a clerk’s indiscretion. The figures, stark and terrible in their plainness, told a story that no amount of polite conversation or elegant dining could obscure. The fortune that had fed him, clothed him, and educated him; the library in which he had read his Virgil; the carriage in which he had driven to the opera; the very roof over his head and the air in his lungs, all had been purchased with the blood and bone of men and women who had been sold like cattle in the Caribbean sun.

Arthur’s hand trembled as he reached for the paper. It was a strange sensation, to feel the weight of gold turn suddenly into the weight of lead, cold and suffocating. He had always believed, as is the nature of the comfortable, that his patron’s wealth had come from the honest trade of wool and the prudent management of land, perhaps with a touch of speculation in the stock exchange, which was considered a respectable vice in those days. The idea that the foundation of his own comfortable existence was built upon the broken backs of the enslaved was a thought so monstrous, so utterly at odds with the gentle, pastoral image of Lord Blackwood that Arthur had always presented to the world, that it seemed impossible. And yet, the numbers did not lie. They sat there, immutable and accusing, like stones thrown into the still water of his life, sending ripples of doubt and horror out to the edges of his being.

He looked around the room. The heavy velvet curtains, the mahogany bookcase, the bust of Cicero on the shelf, the soft leather of his chair. Each object seemed to speak to him now, not with the voice of luxury, but with the voice of accusation. Was he a friend to these things? Or was he a thief, stealing the comfort of his life from the misery of others? The question hung in the air, heavy and suffocating, pressing upon his chest until he could scarcely breathe. He thought of Lord Blackwood, a man of imposing stature and booming voice, who had treated Arthur with a paternal affection that was almost overwhelming. He remembered the old lord’s laughter, loud and resonant, echoing through the halls of the house. He remembered the way the lord had placed his hand upon Arthur’s shoulder, saying, “You are my son, Arthur, and I shall see that you want for nothing.” Now, those words sounded hollow, like the clatter of bones in a dry box. The kindness had been a shackle, and the affection a chain.

Arthur walked to the door and opened it, stepping out into the corridor. The house was silent, save for the ticking of the great clock in the hall, which seemed to mark out the seconds of his life with a harsh, mechanical precision. He could not stay. To stay was to consent. To stay was to accept the gift that was really a curse. He thought of London, with its crowded streets and its diverse faces, where a man could lose himself in the throng and perhaps find a life that was his own, unburdened by the stain of this inheritance. He thought of the work, the honest, hard work that would feed him, however poorly it might be done. He thought of the pride that would come from knowing that his bread was earned with his own hands, not bought with the blood of the innocent.

But the fear was a living thing, a cold serpent coiled around his heart. To give up this life was to step into the abyss. He was untrained for the world of labor. He knew nothing of the trade, nothing of the craft, nothing of the struggle for daily sustenance. He was a gentleman, bred to be a gentleman, and to renounce that title was to renounce his very identity. Was he not selfish to think of his own soul at the expense of his own comfort? Or was it the ultimate selfishness to remain in a house built on a foundation of lies? The questions swirled around him like the fog outside, confusing and disorienting.

He returned to the study and looked again at the ledger. The ink was faded, but the totals were clear. He picked up his pen, his hand still trembling, and began to write. He did not write a letter to Lord Blackwood, for what could he say to a man who had kept the secret so well? He did not write a protest, for who would listen to a young man with no name and no standing? Instead, he wrote a resignation.
