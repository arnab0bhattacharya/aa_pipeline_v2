Mr. Arthur Wendell had long enjoyed the satisfaction of supposing his own understanding superior to the common run of young men of his age and fortune. He was not entirely mistaken in this conceit; he possessed a lively mind, a readiness of speech, and a degree of general information that made him an agreeable companion in any drawing-room in Kent. He had read widely, if not always deeply, and could frame an argument with a neatness that frequently silenced opponents who were, perhaps, more fundamentally in the right. His sister, Miss Wendell, who resided with him at Hartwell Park, was not insensible to his merits, but she was likewise not blind to the peculiar vanity which led him to suppose himself proof against the sort of feminine artifice that had, in the past season, ensnared two of his particular acquaintances. He had remarked upon those gentlemen's folly with a degree of self-congratulation that Miss Wendell found more amusing than entirely justified, for she suspected that Arthur's immunity stemmed less from his discernment than from the fact that no young lady had yet taken the trouble to aim her powers in his direction.

This immunity was presently threatened, however, by Miss Clara Vance, a young woman of very pleasing address, who had come into the neighbourhood upon the marriage of her elder sister to Sir George Holloway, and who was now, by the convenience of that connection, a frequent visitor at every house of consequence within ten miles of Tunbridge. Miss Vance was handsome, though not remarkably so, with a countenance of great flexibility, eyes that expressed whatever sentiment the moment required, and a voice of such gentle modulation that even her most commonplace observations sounded like expressions of refined sensibility. She had the happy knack of appearing to hang upon the words of whoever she addressed, and in the case of Mr. Wendell, this appearance was rendered all the more convincing by her frequent declarations of agreement with his opinions, which she advanced with a candour and warmth that he found extremely flattering.

Miss Wendell had observed her brother's growing attachment with the concern that any sister of sense must feel, but she had, until the evening of the Hartwell dinner, contented herself with the hope that Arthur's self-assurance would, in the end, prove his own defence. He was too proud, she reasoned, to be long deceived by anyone whose understanding fell materially below his own. Yet the events of that evening shook her confidence in this reasoning considerably.

The party had been assembled in the drawing-room after dinner, and the conversation had turned, by some easy transition, to the subject of novels. Mr. Wendell, who delighted in the discussion of literature almost as much as in the display of his own taste, had delivered himself of a lengthy opinion on the merits of the fashionable novels of the day, pronouncing them, without exception, to be deficient in probability, wanting in knowledge of the human heart, and altogether inferior to the works of an earlier age. He had spoken with his usual confidence, and with rather more length than the subject strictly warranted, and Miss Vance had listened throughout with an expression of such attentive admiration that Miss Wendell, from her seat across the room, felt almost embarrassed on her behalf.

"You speak, Mr. Wendell," Miss Vance said, when he had at length concluded, "with such exactness of judgment, that I must own myself quite ashamed of my own opinion, which was, I fear, formed with far less deliberation. I had thought Miss Brudenell's last work rather affecting, but I perceive now that I was moved by mere sentiment, and not by any true estimation of its merits. I am sure I ought to read it again, with your remarks in mind, for I find I can scarcely trust my own judgment at all, when it differs from yours."

This speech was delivered with such artless sincerity that Miss Wendell could not but admire the skill with which it was composed. It conceded everything and sacrificed nothing. Miss Vance had surrendered her own opinion, certainly, but she had done so in a manner that invited Mr. Wendell to feel himself her superior in understanding, which was precisely the sort of victory he most valued. Her very confession of inadequacy was a form of flattery more powerful than any direct praise, for it appealed not merely to his vanity but to his sense of his own intellectual authority. Miss Wendell watched her brother receive this tribute and saw, with a sinking of the heart, that he was entirely beguiled. His face wore that expression of complacent benevolence which always appeared when he believed himself to be instructing someone who was both willing to learn and worthy of the instruction.

Miss Wendell was not herself without experience in the art of conversational strategy, and she recognized in Miss Vance's performance a talent that her brother, for all his cleverness, was wholly unequipped to detect. He had built his defences against the obvious arts, the direct flattery, the transparent simpering, the obvious pursuit of his attention, but he had no armour at all against the subtler weapon of having his own opinions returned to him, polished and amplified, as the reflections of a mind that merely wished to be guided aright. Miss Vance did not flatter him; she merely agreed with him, and agreed with such an appearance of reluctant conviction, of yielding her own preference to his superior judgment, that he could not but feel himself in the presence of a young woman of uncommon good sense and uncommon candour.

Miss Wendell sat with her needlework through the remainder of the evening, turning over in her mind the question of what, if anything, she ought to do. She was not ignorant of the danger of interfering in such matters.
