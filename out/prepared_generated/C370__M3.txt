The fog was a thick, grey beast that morning, pressing its damp muzzle against the windows of Pellew, Frere & Hatch on the second floor. Mrs. Agnes Tilling let herself in with the heavy iron key, turning the lock with a practiced wrist to keep the mechanism from clattering. The gas lamps on the staircase were still guttering in their brackets, casting long, unsteady shadows on the linoleum. Inside the firm’s reception room, the air smelled of cold ash, stale tobacco, and the particular, dusty melancholy of legal papers. Agnes set her bucket down by the hat stand, unwound the damp wool scarf from her neck, and struck a match to light the burner under the copper boiler she had filled at the tap on the landing.

While the water heated, she moved through the outer office, pulling up the heavy green blinds. It was barely half past six, and the city outside was a smudged charcoal sketch. Below, the Thames moved sluggishly, a vein of dirty silver under the fog. A few early barges were already out, their low horns sounding mournful and distant. Agnes rested her fingertips on the cold glass for a moment, feeling the chill seep into her bones. She thought of how the river never stopped, never rested, just carried the city’s filth out to the sea day after day.

The water in the boiler began to murmur. Agnes dumped in the harsh yellow soap and stirred it with a broken broom handle until a thick, medicinal scum formed on the surface. She wrung out her mop, the rough wooden handle familiar and smooth in her chapped hands, and began to work her way through the clerks’ room. The desks were a clutter of inkwells, blotting pads, and wire trays. As she mopped around the legs of the furniture, her eyes, sharp and quick despite her fifty-two years, caught the words on the topmost letters left out on the desks. She never touched them, never moved a single paper, but she read them as easily as she read the labels on the tins at the grocer’s.

On Mr. Frere’s desk, a heavy foolscap sheet lay open, its black ink bleeding slightly into the blotter. She read the words "Indenture of Lease," "Covenant," and "Hereditaments." On a smaller desk by the door, a clerk had left a half-finished letter: "Dear Sir, We are in receipt of your communication of the 14th inst. and beg to inform you..." Agnes paused, leaning on her mop. She wondered about the people on the other end of those letters. Did they sleep soundly, or did they lie awake worrying over their covenants and hereditaments? She supposed solicitors saw people at their worst—when they were fighting over money, or land, or the care of orphans. It was a world of sharp edges and hard lines, drawn up in ink to keep the chaos of life at bay.

She moved into the senior partner’s office, Mr. Pellew’s sanctum, which smelled faintly of cigars and leather. His desk was immaculate, save for a single envelope resting on the green leather blotter. It was airmail paper, she noted, thin and translucent, with a Canadian stamp showing a picture of a maple leaf. Agnes’s breath caught for a moment. She did not need to read the letter inside, for she knew the shape of those envelopes well enough. Her own son, William, had sent her one just like it three weeks ago. It was folded now in the pocket of her coat, read so many times the creases were turning soft as cloth.

William was in Toronto now, not in the wilds of Quebec where he had first gone after the war. He worked for a firm that made motorcar parts, writing letters in a bright, clean office that looked out over a wide avenue. He said the snow there was deeper and cleaner than in London, and that the people walked with a quicker step. He had sent her a photograph of himself standing on a bridge, looking well-fed and strong, his hair slicked back, a sharp collar bright against his neck. He looked like a stranger. He looked like a man who had forgotten the mud of Passchendaele, the shelling, the smell of gas. Agnes was glad of it. She wanted him to forget. She wanted him to become someone new, someone whose insides did not flinch at a loud noise.

She wrung out her mop again, the hot water stinging her cracked knuckles, and moved to the window in Mr. Pellew’s office. From here, the view of the river was wider. The fog was beginning to lift, shredding like wet gauze, and she could see the dark, choppy water and the far bank, where the warehouses stood like sleeping giants. She thought of the churchyard at St. Bride’s, a mile or so inland, where her husband, Thomas, lay under a flat stone. Thomas had been a clockmaker, a quiet man with clever hands and a gentle, distracted air. He had died in the influenza epidemic just after the Armistice, before William had even come home. He had gone to sleep one night with a fever and never woken up, slipping away as quietly as a clock winding down.

Agnes did not visit the grave as often as she felt she should. The churchyard was too quiet, too still, and it made the loss feel like a heavy stone in her chest. She preferred to think of Thomas in his shop, surrounded by the ticking of a hundred small hearts, the brass wheels and mainsprings spread out on his velvet mat. She imagined him in heaven, setting the angels’ watches, keeping perfect time. Here, by the river, with the fog lifting and the city waking, she felt closer to him than she did among the graves. The river was like time, she thought. It carried everything away—the letters on the desks, the men who wrote them, the boys who went to war, the husbands who stayed behind and died in their beds. It carried it all out to the vast, indifferent sea.
