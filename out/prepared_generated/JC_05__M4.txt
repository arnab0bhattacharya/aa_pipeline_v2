The river, which the natives called the Nzadi and which appeared on the company’s charts as the St. Hubert, ran brown and sullen between banks of a green so dark and uniform that it seemed less a vegetation than a condition of the air itself—a heavy, vegetable exhalation that hung above the water and shut out the sky. I had come up from the coast in a flat-bottomed stern-wheeler, with a crew of six men from the Loango shore and a headman named Makaya who spoke a little French and less English, and who regarded me, from the first, with a patience that I found more disturbing than any open insolence. We carried, besides our provisions, a theodolite in its wooden case, a sextant, two chronometers, a chest of tracing paper and drawing instruments, and a bundle of the charts prepared by my predecessor, one Ellsworth, who had died of the fever at the mouth of the river some eighteen months before, and whose work it was my business to verify and to extend.

It was understood, of course, that I was not merely to verify. The concession had been granted, the shares had been subscribed in Brussels and in Paris, and the prospectus—which I had read in a café on the Boulevard Anspach, with a certain pleasant warmth of wine in my blood—had spoken of the St. Hubert as a highway of commerce, of a chain of trading stations already established, of populous villages along the banks whose chiefs had signed treaties of amity and exclusive privilege. The chart which accompanied the prospectus was a beautiful thing, drawn in three colours, with the river winding through it like a ribbon of blue silk, and the villages marked with neat red squares, and the names in a fine copperplate hand. I remember that one of them was called St. Anne’s Landing, and another, further up, Fort Espérance. There was a mission station marked, and a factory, and a road—a road, mind you, in that country of water and mud and silence—leading inland to a district described as “of great mineral promise.”

We came to St. Anne’s Landing on the fourth day. The river had widened, and the banks had fallen back into a low, flat shore of grey mud, on which the mangroves stood on their stilted roots like a crowd of cripples. There was no landing. There was no village. There was a single hut, abandoned, with its roof fallen in, and a few stakes in the mud where a fish-trap had been, and a silence so complete that I could hear the blood beating in my own ears. I looked at the chart, and then at the shore, and then at Makaya, who stood in the bow with his arms folded, looking at nothing in particular.

“St. Anne’s Landing,” I said.

He turned his head slowly. “No, patron.”

“What do you mean, no?”

“No landing,” he said. “No village. Never was.”

I laughed, I think, or made some sound that was meant for a laugh. “The chart says otherwise.”

He looked at me with that same patience, which I had begun to understand was not the patience of stupidity but of something else—of a man who has seen many white men come up this river with their papers and their instruments, and who has learned that it is not his business to argue. “The chart,” he said, “is a paper. The river is the river.”

We went on. Fort Espérance, two days later, was a sandbank. The mission station was a clearing in which a few plantains grew wild, and a broken bell, green with verdigris, lay half-buried in the earth. I dug it out with my own hands, and it was stamped with the date 1887 and the name of a society in Lyon, and I sat on the ground beside it for a long time, while the sun went down and the mosquitoes came up from the water in a cloud, and I thought of the prospectus, and the café on the Boulevard Anspach, and the fine copperplate hand.

The villages, of course, were the least of it. Villages can be moved, or can die, or can be forgotten. But the river itself—the river which I had been sent to map—did not answer to the chart. The chart showed it running north by northwest for forty miles above Fort Espérance, with a tributary entering from the east at a place called the Kérou rapids. The river ran, in fact, west by southwest, and there were no rapids, and the tributary entered from the west, and was not a tributary but a backwater, a dead arm of the main stream, choked with lilies. I stood on the bank with my theodolite and my chronometers, and I took my readings, and I made my notes, and I knew that every line I drew was a contradiction of the paper that had been sold to the shareholders in Brussels and Paris.

What was I to do? I have asked myself that question many times since, in many places, and I have never found an answer that satisfied me. I could draw the river as it was, and send my map to the company, and the company would be ruined, and the directors would be ruined, and the shareholders would be ruined, and I would be a fool—or worse, a traitor to my employers, a man who had been sent to do a piece of work and had done it in such a way as to destroy the very thing he was paid to serve. Or I could draw the river as the chart had it, and send my map to the company, and the company would be saved, and the directors would be saved, and the shareholders would be saved, and I would be a liar—a liar in ink and tracing paper, a liar whose lies would be believed because they were drawn to scale, and who would go down to his grave with the knowledge that he had sold his soul for a salary and a pension.
