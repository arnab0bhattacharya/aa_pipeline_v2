The heat was a thing one could hold in the hand, a solid, brassy weight; it pressed upon the eyelids and made the very dust seem to glitter with a purpose of its own, as if the stones of the Acropolis, whitened and fretted by centuries, were still giving off the last vibrations of some ancient, insupportable noon. Harriet stood a little apart from her brother, fanning herself with a guidebook, and watched him. Edward, in his grey alpaca coat, his panama hat tilted just so, was in his element. He had found a fragment of a frieze, a mere block of marble with a suggestion of a horse’s flank, and was running his fingers over it with a lover’s touch, murmuring to himself in Greek, his lips shaping the syllables as if they were a kind of food.

‘You see, Harriet,’ he said, not turning, ‘the line here—the torsion—it is not merely a technical triumph, it is a moral one. The restraint. The subordination of the part to the whole. That is what the Greeks understood. That is what we have lost.’ He straightened up, his face flushed with the heat and with the pleasure of his own exposition. ‘It is a lesson in living, really.’

Harriet said, ‘Yes, Edward.’ Her voice was small, and the wind, which had begun to stir among the columns, took it and broke it up. She had said ‘Yes, Edward’ to so many things—to the superiority of the Parthenon over the Erechtheion, to the correct method of dating a kylix, to the necessity of rising at six to avoid the archaeologists and the Germans—that the words had worn smooth, like pebbles in a stream, and meant nothing at all. She looked at the horse’s flank. It was beautiful; she could see that. But it did not move her as it moved Edward. She felt, instead, a curious flatness, a sense of having been brought to the very edge of something and then told to stand still and admire the view from a description.

They had come, the two of them, because Edward had said, for years, that he must see the sites before he could finish his book. It had been a project, a shared horizon, something for the long evenings in the house at Tunbridge Wells. *When we go to Greece, Harriet.* And she had imagined it—the light, the sea, the marble—as a kind of reward, a loosening of the tight, small knot of their lives. But now that they were here, she found herself thinking of the kitchen garden at home, of the way the light fell across the flagged path at four o’clock in the afternoon, of the particular, musty smell of the linen cupboard. These were her own, and they had a weight, a texture, that this blazing, indifferent beauty did not.

That afternoon, while Edward went to the museum to compare his notes with the curator’s, Harriet was left to wander. She did not go to the museum. She walked, instead, down through the Plaka, past the little shops with their strings of sponges and their dusty icons, until she came to a small, almost forgotten Byzantine church, squeezed between two modern houses. It was cool inside, and dark, and smelled of wax and old incense. A woman was lighting a candle before a picture of the Virgin, and the flame, as it caught, sent a sudden, wavering gold across the painted face. Harriet sat down on a wooden bench and let the silence settle over her. It was not the grand silence of the Parthenon, which was really the silence of a lecture hall after the professor has left. It was a small, living silence, full of the breath of the woman and the crackle of the candle.

She thought, suddenly, of her own face in the glass that morning, the lines around her eyes, the way her hair, once a fine, pale brown, was now threaded with grey. She had never minded, not really. There had been so much to do—Edward’s meals, Edward’s correspondence, the ordering of the house, the reading aloud in the evenings. She had been the soil in which his life had grown. But here, in this little church, she felt a strange, unsettling hollowness, as if she had been a character in a book, and the book had been put down.

A week later they took the boat to the islands. Edward had his notebook out before they had even left the harbour, sketching the coastline, annotating the colour of the sea. ‘Look, Harriet,’ he cried, ‘the precise shade—that is the *oinops pontos*, the wine-dark sea. Homer had it exactly.’ Harriet looked. The water was a deep, brilliant blue, and it did, indeed, have a purple shadow in its heart. But she did not see Homer in it. She saw a colour, a light so clear and hard it seemed to ring like a glass, and she felt, for a moment, a sensation that was almost like pain—a sharp, exquisite ache that had no name and no use.

On the island of Siphos, they found a small taverna by the water, and Edward fell into conversation with a French archaeologist, a man with a beard and a passionate belief in the Minoan civilisation. Harriet, left alone, walked down to the edge of the sea. The sun was setting, and the water was turning to a sheet of beaten copper, shot through with rose and gold. A fishing boat was coming in, its sail a dark triangle against the light. She stood there for a long time, watching the men pull the boat up on the shingle, the way their brown bodies moved with a slow, economical grace. One of them laughed, a sudden, barking sound, and the others joined in. It was a laugh that had nothing to do with her, and it did not exclude her. It was simply there, like the light, like the sea.
