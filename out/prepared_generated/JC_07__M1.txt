I have often reflected, in the quiet hours of the night watch when the engines beat out their slow, indifferent rhythm against the darkness, that a man is known not by the things he has done, but by the things he has consented to know. For thirty years I had commanded the *María de la Luz*, a mail steamer of no great speed and less beauty, plying the coast of that unhappy republic from the fever-swamps of the south to the bare, wind-scoured cliffs of the north. In that time I had carried everything the world has to offer: bales of cotton that smelled of the sun, machinery for mines that would never yield, letters of credit and letters of love, and once, in a locked iron strongbox, the corpse of a deposed minister being sent home to his mother. I carried it all with the same professional indifference. A crate is a crate. The manifest states its weight and dimensions; the purser signs for it; the hold is closed. It is a matter between the shipper and God. I was the instrument of their commerce, nothing more. My world was the trim of the ship, the state of the boilers, the safety of the mails, and the steady, reliable beat of the compass. I had never asked what was in a crate. This time, I knew.

The knowledge came to me in a shuttered room in the capital, a room that smelled of damp plaster, strong black coffee, and the cold sweat of men who live by their wits. The man who called himself the Commander was not a soldier, not in any way I understood the word. He was a lawyer, I think, or perhaps a failed academic, with fine, long-fingered hands and eyes that held the flat, patient light of a reptile. He did not shout or threaten. He spoke of duty, of the people, of the historical necessity of the moment, his voice a dry rustle in the gloom. He spoke of the rifles as one might speak of medicine for a sick child. I sat opposite him, my cap on my knee, and watched a single fly crawl across the polished surface of the table between us. I felt a great weariness, the kind that settles deep in the bones and has nothing to do with lack of sleep. It was the weariness of a man who has spent his life on an uncertain sea and is suddenly shown the chart of its deepest, most monstrous currents.

He did not need to make threats. The threat was in the very air of that room, in the political tremors that had been shaking the republic for months. To refuse was to be an enemy of this new, grim reality. To accept was to become a part of it. I, who had so prided myself on my neutrality, on being a simple servant of the mails, was being asked to choose a side. The Commander slid a small, folded paper across the table. It was a draft on a foreign bank, a sum that would see me through a comfortable retirement. I looked at the paper, but I did not see it. I saw the faces of my crew, simple men with families in the Basque country, and I saw the long, grey swells of the coast, indifferent to the squabbles of men. I nodded, once. The fly had reached the edge of the table and flown away into the shadows.

The loading was done at night, in a cove far from the official port, the crates coming out from the black mangroves in silent canoes. They were not heavy, but they were dense, and the sound they made as they were lowered into the hold was a dull, final thud, like a coffin lid closing. The men worked without speaking, their faces obscured by the darkness. I stood on the bridge and watched, feeling a profound sense of trespass, as if I were violating the sanctity of my own vessel. The *María de la Luz* was no longer merely a steamer; she was an accomplice. The weight of the cargo seemed to alter her trim, to pull her down into the water with a new and sinister purpose.

The passage to the fishing village of Puerto Escondido was two days along a coast of unrelenting green. The jungle came right down to the water’s edge, a solid, breathing wall of vegetation that seemed to watch us pass. The heat was a physical presence, a wet blanket that smothered sound and thought. There was no wind. The sea was a sheet of polished brass under a white, punishing sun, and the only movement was the slow, hypnotic churn of our propeller and the lazy circling of black vultures high in the empty sky. The silence between the beats of the engine was absolute, a silence that pressed in on the ears and filled the mind with unspoken questions. My first mate, a quiet man named Etxarri, came to the bridge and stood beside me for a long time, looking at the coast.

“It is very quiet, Captain,” he said at last, his voice barely a whisper.

“The land is sleeping,” I replied, the words tasting like ash in my mouth. It was a foolish, poetic thing to say, and he knew it. He looked at me, his gaze lingering for a moment on my face, and I saw in his eyes not suspicion, but a kind of sad, shared understanding. He knew something was amiss. He had sailed with me for fifteen years and could read the set of my shoulders as easily as he read the sky. He did not ask, and I did not tell him. The silence between us was a covenant, a recognition of the line that had been crossed and the fact that we were now standing together on the wrong side of it.
