The refusal came on a Tuesday, a sheet of grey official paper that spoke of the Pharmacy Act and the ineligibility of my sex with the cold, unanswerable logic of a theorem. I had passed the examinations—by Jove, I had passed them!—and stood first in my year in Materia Medica, yet the door of the profession remained firmly shut against me. It was a peculiar sort of humiliation, to have one’s mind acknowledged and one’s hands declared unfit for the work.

So it was that I came to be in the back room of Mr. Ebenezer Crump’s establishment in the suburban sprawl of Thornton Heath. The arrangement was one of quiet, mutual convenience. Mr. Crump, a man whose ambition had long since been outsized by his girth and his fondness for port, was finding the intricacies of modern chemistry a strain on his faculties. I, Eleanor Vance, possessed the knowledge he lacked, and he possessed the legal right to sign the labels which my own hand had written. For a wage of thirty shillings a week, I was his ghost, his unseen intellect. I weighed, I measured, I compounded. He dispensed, he advised, he collected the fees. To the good people of Thornton Heath, I was merely the quiet young woman who tidied the shelves.

It was a system that worked, after a fashion. The bottles of Dr. Grew’s Soothing Syrup for Infants were of my making, as were the fever mixtures and the cough tinctures. I took a quiet, professional pride in the work, in the precise calculation of a dose, the perfect emulsion, the crystal-clear solution. My mind was engaged even if my name was not. Mr. Crump, for his part, treated me with a sort of vague, avuncular benevolence, as one might treat a clever dog that had learned a remarkable trick.

The crisis, when it came, arrived in the form of Mrs. Higgins and her small daughter, Polly. The child had a racking cough, and the doctor had sent a prescription for a linctus containing a minute, carefully calibrated dose of chlorodyne—a compound of laudanum, chloroform, and other potent substances. It was a medicine that demanded the utmost respect.

That afternoon, Mr. Crump was in a state of unusual agitation. A certain Mrs. Albright, a widow of comfortable means and uncertain temperament, had been in to discuss a nerve tonic, and the encounter had left him flustered. He was muttering about her expectations when he took the prescription from my hand to sign the day-book.

“Higgins, Higgins… a troublesome cough for the little one,” he mumbled, his eyes already scanning the ledger for Mrs. Albright’s account. “There we are. Now, Eleanor, be a good girl and see to it. I must just check the stock of quinine.”

I did as I was told. I took the fresh bottle of chlorodyne from the poison cabinet. The label, in Mr. Crump’s grand copperplate, read *Chlorodyne*. I did not, I confess, give it a second glance. Why should I? It was from our own stock. I measured the dose with my minim glass, added it to the syrup base, corked the bottle, and affixed the label with Mr. Crump’s instructions: *One teaspoonful to be taken at night.*

Mrs. Higgins took the bottle, and I thought no more of it. The wheels of our little enterprise turned as they always did.

The bell above the shop door jangled with a violence that made the bottles on the shelves tremble. It was nearly eight o’clock. Mrs. Higgins stood there, her face as white as the paper of a prescription, her eyes wide with a terror that needed no words.

“The medicine,” she gasped. “Polly… she’s gone all strange. Her breathing… it’s so slow. She won’t wake up.”

A cold dread, sharp and immediate as a hypodermic needle, pierced me. I was behind the counter in an instant. “Let me see the bottle, Mrs. Higgins. Quick now.”

She thrust it into my hand. It was the bottle I had filled. My heart hammered against my ribs. I unstoppered it and took the faintest drop on my finger, touching it to my tongue. The taste was not the familiar, slightly bitter tang of our usual chlorodyne. It was intensely, shockingly sweet, with a cloying, treacly quality that was utterly wrong.

“The stock bottle,” I said, my voice tight. I flew to the poison cabinet. Mr. Crump, drawn by the commotion, emerged from the back room, his face mottled with confusion.

“What is the meaning of this?” he demanded.

I ignored him. I seized the bottle from which I had drawn the dose. The label was the same—*Chlorodyne*—but the script was thinner, the ink a different shade. I pulled out the stopper. The odour was unmistakable. I dipped a glass rod into the viscous liquid. It was not chlorodyne. It was a bottle of concentrated tincture of squill and henbane that had been mislabelled by a wholesale druggist. A powerful emetic and narcotic, but in the dose of chlorodyne I had given, for a child of five, it was a deadly poison.

“It’s the wrong medicine,” I said, the words falling like stones into the sudden, dreadful silence. “This bottle is mislabelled. It’s not chlorodyne.”

Mr. Crump snatched the bottle from me. He stared at it, his face cycling from confusion to disbelief, and then to a dawning, terrible comprehension. He knew. He knew he had taken a delivery that morning and, in his haste to attend to Mrs. Albright, had not checked the new bottles against the invoice. He had placed the wrong stock in the poison cabinet.

Mrs. Higgins let out a moan. “Poison? You’ve given my Polly poison?”

And in that moment, the choice presented itself to me with the clarity of a chemical reaction. I could stand aside. I could let Mr. Crump bear the full weight of this. He was the registered chemist; his name was on the label, his name in the ledger. The law would hold him responsible, and rightly so. My own position, my thirty shillings a week, would be preserved. I was merely the assistant, the shadow. No one would ever know.
