It was upon a raw November afternoon, when the yellow fog had settled so thickly upon Belgravia that the lamps in Eaton Place burned like dull, smouldering coals, that I received the card of a Miss Honoria Vane. She was a woman of perhaps thirty years, plainly but decently dressed in black, and she carried in her gloved hand a small leather bag which she held with the tenacity of one who fears to lose the last thing of value in the world.

“You are the friend of Mr. Cullingford,” she said, when I had bidden her be seated. “The doctor who was with him at the hospital. They told me at the house that you had attended him in his last illness, and that you were a man of discretion.”

“I am a physician, madam, and I attended Mr. Cullingford in his final hours. But I confess I do not understand how I can serve you.”

“My brother, sir, was valet to His Excellency the Baron Gravenstein, of the Austro-Hungarian Embassy. He was found dead in a four-wheeler in Belgrave Square on Tuesday morning, and the certificate says heart failure. I do not believe it.”

I had read the paragraph in the evening paper—a brief and colourless account, such as is given to the death of an unimportant foreign servant. I said as much.

“The certificate is a lie,” she repeated, with a quiet vehemence that struck me. “My brother was as strong as a horse. He was never ill in his life. And there is this, sir.” She opened the bag and laid upon my table a small brass key. “This came to me by the afternoon post on the very day he died. There was no letter with it, only the key wrapped in a piece of tissue. It is the key of his own deed-box, which he kept at the shop of a locksmith in Ebury Street. I went there yesterday and opened it. There was nothing in it but this.”

She placed before me a single sheet of paper, closely written in a fine, cramped hand. It was in German, and my knowledge of that tongue being scanty, I could make out little beyond a date and the repetition of a name—*Kessler*—three or four times, and the words *die Papiere*.

“I cannot read it,” she said. “My brother kept his own counsel, and I know no German. But I know this, sir—that on the day after he died, a great fire was made in the yard behind the Embassy, and they burned papers until the chimney of the coach-house was black with the smoke of them. The charwoman who is my neighbour saw it with her own eyes, and she says there were three baskets full.”

I confess that the story had by this time begun to interest me beyond the common. I asked her whether she had laid the matter before the police.

“I went to the station in Gerald Street,” she answered, “and the inspector heard me with great patience, and told me that valets were often nervous men who wrote things down, and that heart failure was heart failure. He would not look at the paper. He said it was a matter for the coroner, and the coroner had already given his verdict. And then, sir, being at my wits’ end, I thought of you, who had been kind to him.”

I looked at the sheet of paper again. There was something in the very meagreness of it—a single page, a few words, a key sent on the day of death—that smacked of a man who knew himself to be in danger and took the one precaution left to him. I told Miss Vane that I would keep the paper, and that she should hear from me before the week was out.

My first step was to seek the advice of my old friend Mr. Lucian Frayne, whose rooms in Ryder Street were a very museum of the curious, and whose mind, trained upon the minute analysis of crime, had more than once been of service to the Department. He was a tall, spare man, with a face of a singular pallor and eyes that seemed to take in everything and to betray nothing. He listened to my narrative without interruption, his long fingers pressed together at his lips, and at its close he held out his hand for the paper.

He read it twice, and then he laughed—a short, dry laugh, without mirth.

“Your German is as bad as my own,” said he, “but even you might have remarked the date. It is the seventeenth of October. On the seventeenth of October, if I remember the almanac of diplomacy, there was a certain conference at the Embassy which was not reported in the press. And here is the name Kessler repeated, and *die Papiere*—the papers. Your valet was not writing a confession, my dear doctor. He was writing a receipt.”

“A receipt?”

“For a sum of money, or for a service. Observe the words *erhalten* and *quittirt*—received and discharged. It is the formal language of a man who has been paid. And the key, sent on the day of his death, was his way of telling his sister where the record lay. He was a cautious fellow, and he did not trust the post with anything but the key.”

“Then you think he was murdered?”

“I think,” said Frayne, rising and taking down his hat, “that we shall know more in Belgrave Square than we do in Ryder Street. Let us go and look at the coach-house.”

The fog had lifted a little, and the lamps of the Embassy—a great, stuccoed house at the corner of the square—shone with a hard, unfriendly brilliance. Frayne walked slowly along the railing, his eyes upon the ground, and then stopped before the archway that led to the mews behind.
