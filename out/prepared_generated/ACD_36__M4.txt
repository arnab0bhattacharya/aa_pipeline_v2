The letter which reached me at Baker Street was written upon paper of a heavy, cream-laid sort, such as one associates with the close air of a cathedral close, and it was subscribed by a dignitary whose name I had heard in connection with a learned treatise upon the Norman arches of the west country. The Dean of Saint Cuthbert’s at Wrenchester wrote with a certain guarded urgency, requesting that Mr. Sherlock Holmes should find it convenient to travel down upon the following morning, and adding that the matter was one which demanded the utmost discretion, since it touched both the reputation of a servant of the Church and the security of a piece of altar plate which had been the gift of a pious benefactor in the reign of the second Charles.

“Two problems, Watson,” said Holmes, tossing the letter across to me and resuming his violin, upon which he had been scraping with a melancholy air all the morning. “A singing organ and a swapping of silver. The one is the gossip of the choir-boys and the other is not yet the gossip of anybody, which is the more interesting circumstance of the two. Pack the small bag. We shall do very well upon the eleven-forty.”

The city of Wrenchester lies in a hollow among low green hills, and the cathedral, which is of a yellowish stone, lifts its central tower above a huddle of red roofs and crooked chimneys in a manner that I found very pleasant to the eye. We were met at the station by a small, brisk, spectacled clergyman in gaiters, who introduced himself as the Dean, and who, having shaken hands with a nervous rapidity, conducted us at once to his house in the close, where a cold collation had been laid out and where the blinds were drawn against the afternoon sun.

“I hardly know where to begin, Mr. Holmes,” said he, when we were seated. “The organ is the older story, and the foolish one, and I would not have troubled you with it at all had it not been for the other affair, which has given me some very uneasy nights. Our organist is a man named Amberley—Egerton Amberley—a person of considerable attainments, though of a temperament that I can only call excitable. For three weeks past he has complained that upon certain nights, after the cathedral is locked and the lamps are out, the great organ will sound a phrase of four or five notes, very faint, as though the keys were touched by a hand that had no weight in it. He has sat in the dark and listened to it, and he assures me that it is not the wind in the pipes, nor the settling of the building, nor any natural cause that he can name.”

“And the choir?” asked Holmes.

“The choir, sir, is delighted. You know what boys are. They have made a ghost of it, and christened it the Organist’s Tenant, and there is not a chorister in the place who would cross the close after dark for a sovereign. I have spoken to them very plainly, but a ghost is more to them than a dean, and I fear it has gone beyond my reach.”

“You have examined the organ yourself?”

“I have, with Amberley. We have gone over the keys and the stops and the bellows, and found nothing. The wind-trunk is sound, the reservoir holds, and there is no rat nor bird nor loose fitting that could produce a note. I am not a credulous man, Mr. Holmes, but I confess that I have lain awake over it.”

“And the altar plate?”

The Dean’s face grew graver. “That is the matter which has brought me to you. Upon the altar of Saint Cuthbert’s we have, or I should say we had, a paten and a chalice of parcel-gilt silver, the gift, as I have said, of a benefactor of the seventeenth century. They are not of great intrinsic value, but they are of an age and a workmanship that makes them precious to the cathedral, and they are in use at the early celebration. Upon Tuesday last, when the verger took them from the aumbry to lay them out, he found—or rather he did not find—the true paten. In its place was a thing so like it that only a close handling could tell the difference: a paten of gilt base metal, of the same size and pattern, but with the maker’s mark of a Birmingham house of forty years ago stamped small upon the underside. The chalice is untouched. The thief, whoever he was, must have had access to the aumbry, must have known the plate, and must have provided himself with this counterfeit against the day of the exchange.”

Holmes had listened with his eyes closed and the tips of his fingers together. “The aumbry is in the north choir aisle, I think?”

“It is.”

“And the organ is in the north triforium above it.”

The Dean started. “It is, Mr. Holmes. You have been in the cathedral before?”

“I have never been in Wrenchester in my life,” said Holmes, rising. “But I have looked at your cathedral as we came through the close, and a blind man could tell where the organ sits from the sound of the bellows. Let us go over. I should like to see the aumbry and the organ-loft, and I should like to speak with your verger and with Mr. Amberley—separately, if you please.”

The verger, a stout, slow man named Prickett, could tell us little beyond the fact of the discovery, and that the aumbry key hung upon a nail in the vestry, where it had hung, he said, since before his father’s time. Amberley the organist was of a different stamp altogether: a thin, pale, dark-eyed man of forty, with long hands that moved as he talked, and a manner that hovered between the aggrieved and the frightened. He repeated his tale of the nocturnal music with a wealth of detail, and when Holmes asked him whether he had ever been in the organ-loft at night alone, he answered with some heat that he was not a man to frequent a cold church after dark for his pleasure.
