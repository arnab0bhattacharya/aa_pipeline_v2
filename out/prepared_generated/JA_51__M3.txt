The village of Chaddleworth in Hampshire possessed, in the year 1813, but one decided drawback to the general happiness of its principal inhabitants, and that was the conduct of Mr. Edgar Vance. He was a young man of good fortune, pleasing address, and unexceptionable manners, who had for nearly two years past devoted himself, with very little appearance of change or abatement, to Miss Clara Fortescue. Clara was pretty, gentle, and sufficiently sensible; but the singular feature of the case was that the courtship, if courtship it might be called, had never advanced beyond a certain point. Mr. Vance rode over from his estate at Bramshott every Tuesday and Friday, sat in the Fortescue parlour, talked with great feeling of the excellence of the roads and the mildness of the season, and then rode home again, having said nothing that the most anxious ingenuity could construe into an offer. Clara, who had borne this with exemplary patience, had begun of late to look a little grave; and Mrs. Fortescue, their mother, was divided between indignation at his slowness and apprehension that any expression of that indignation would frighten him away entirely.

It was upon her younger sister Lucy, a young woman of more liveliness than discretion, that the burden of the family's impatience pressed heaviest. Lucy, who was but nineteen, possessed that sort of active understanding which cannot endure the sight of an unresolved situation. She had watched Mr. Vance through two seasons of Tuesdays and Fridays, had marked how his eyes followed Clara about the room, and had observed, with considerable annoyance, that he always contrived to sit next to her sister, and yet never once said the word that would make such a position proper. She was by no means convinced of his indifference; on the contrary, she thought him very much in love; but she held his diffidence in such contempt that she determined, about the middle of June, to bring matters to a conclusion by her own efforts.

Her scheme was not wanting in ingenuity. She had observed that Mr. Vance, whatever his hesitation in speech, was of a very jealous disposition. A gentleman from the neighbouring town of Andover, a Mr. Penrose, had lately begun to pay Clara some attention; and though Clara received it with the same tranquil civility she showed to every acquaintance, Lucy resolved that Mr. Vance should see enough of it to alarm him into action. She accordingly took pains to be walking with Clara in the lane whenever Mr. Penrose was known to be in the village, and on one occasion contrived so well that the two sisters met him at the very gate of the Fortescue cottage, where Lucy detained him in conversation for a full ten minutes, while Clara stood by with her usual composure, and Mr. Vance, riding up the lane, beheld the whole party from a distance.

The effect of this manoeuvre was not precisely what she had intended. Mr. Vance did indeed look grave, and his visit that afternoon was shorter than ordinary, and his remarks upon the weather more than usually desultory. But he made no declaration; he did not even make an enquiry. He sat, played with his gloves, drank one cup of tea, and departed with an air of settled melancholy that would have satisfied the most exacting novelist, but left Clara Fortescue precisely where she had been before.

Lucy, however, was not a young woman to be discouraged by a single failure. She next resolved upon a bolder stroke. There was to be a small evening party at the vicarage, and she contrived that Clara should sit next to Mr. Penrose during supper, while she herself engaged Mr. Vance in conversation of the most animated kind. She spoke of her sister's good spirits, of how much brighter she had seemed of late, and of the pleasure of seeing her so generally admired. She watched his face as she spoke, and saw with satisfaction that he coloured deeply and turned his cup round in his hand with an air of strong discomfort. She pressed her advantage; she hinted that Clara was of a disposition to attach herself readily where attentions were uniform, and that she hoped her sister would not be disappointed in her expectations.

Mr. Vance looked at her with an expression she could not quite read. "Miss Fortescue," said he, "has a degree of good sense that must secure her against any very serious disappointment." And then he changed the subject to the state of the vicar's shrubbery.

Lucy was now thoroughly provoked. She had given him every opportunity, and he had refused them all. She determined, therefore, upon a final and decisive measure. On the following Tuesday, when Mr. Vance arrived for his usual visit, he was informed by Lucy herself, at the door, that Clara was not at home. She had gone, Lucy said, to walk with Mr. Penrose, who had been so kind as to call for her in his gig, and they were gone in the direction of the Andover road.

Mr. Vance stood for a moment perfectly still. He said nothing; he did not even enter the house; he merely turned, mounted his horse, which stood waiting at the gate, and rode away. Lucy, who had watched him from the window, saw that he did not take the Bramshott road, but turned off in the opposite direction, towards the downs, and was soon out of sight. She was excessively delighted. She had no doubt that his feelings, thus roused, would speedily bring him back, either that very evening or by the next post, with a formal proposal.

The evening passed, however, and no letter arrived. The next morning brought nothing. Tuesday passed into Wednesday, and Wednesday into Thursday, and still there was no sign of Mr. Vance. Lucy began to feel a little uneasy, though she would not own it even to herself.
