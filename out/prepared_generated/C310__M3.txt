The fog had settled into the courtyards of Lincoln’s Inn Fields like a gray, damp wool, softening the hard edges of the iron railings and muffling the clatter of passing hansom cabs. Ezekiel Crookshanks walked through it with the measured, unhurried stride of a man who had trodden the exact same path for thirty years. He carried his worn leather satchel in his right hand, the leather polished smooth by the daily friction of his palm. In his left breast pocket, resting against his ribs, was the familiar, comforting weight of his brass watch, ticking steadily away the seconds of a life that had, until this morning, been defined by an unbroken, monotonous rhythm.

For three decades, Ezekiel had served as the chief copying clerk at Barrow, Vance, and Pell. He had never missed a day. Not when the cholera had swept through the city in ’32, leaving the streets empty and the hearses rolling day and night. Not when his own lungs had rattled with a winter fever in ’39, forcing him to wrap his neck in flannel and walk the two miles to the office through a driving sleet, his fingers so numb he could barely grip the pen. His hand had copied thousands upon thousands of legal documents—deeds, wills, briefs, and affidavits—translating the chaotic scrawl of the partners into a uniform, beautiful copperplate. His script was his pride, a testament to an unwavering constancy that he had foolishly believed the world still valued.

That morning, the managing partner, Mr. Vance, had called him into the inner office. The room smelled of pipe smoke and old paper, and the gaslight hissed softly in the globe above the desk. Mr. Vance had not asked him to sit. He had cleared his throat, adjusted his spectacles, and spoken of modernization. A younger man was to be hired. A man who understood the newfangled steel-nibbed pens and the improved copying presses, a man whose eyesight was keen and whose hand was swift. "Your service has been appreciated, Crookshanks," Mr. Vance had said, his gaze drifting toward the window. "We shall, of course, provide a month’s wages in lieu of notice."

Ezekiel had stood perfectly still, absorbing the words. He had not argued, nor had he pleaded. He had simply bowed his head an inch, turned, and walked back to his high desk in the long room. He had spent the remaining hours of the afternoon copying a lengthy conveyance, his pen scratching faithfully across the foolscap, the words blurring into meaningless shapes. He had finished the final page at half past five, capped the inkwell, wiped his pen, and placed it in the drawer. Then he had taken his satchel and walked out into the fog.

Now, as he crossed the wide expanse of Lincoln’s Inn Fields, the bare branches of the plane trees reaching up into the murk like skeletal fingers, Ezekiel felt a profound disorientation. The world he knew was dissolving around him. The firm had been his anchor, the fixed point around which his entire existence revolved. Without it, he was adrift, a sixty-year-old man with a singular talent that the world no longer required.

He turned north, beginning the familiar walk toward Pentonville. His boots struck the cobblestones with a hollow, rhythmic sound that echoed off the darkening brick facades. The gas lamps were being lit, casting pools of yellow light that struggled against the encroaching fog. He passed a pie-man crying his wares, a group of boys playing with a hoop, a woman hurrying home with a basket of vegetables. They were all part of the great, indifferent current of London life, flowing around him as if he were a stone in a stream.

As he walked, his thoughts turned to Pentonville and the small rooms he shared with his sister, Martha. The rooms were on the second floor of a narrow, four-story terrace on Amwell Street, a modest but respectable address. Martha kept the place spotless, the brass knocker polished, the hearth swept, the meager furniture gleaming with elbow grease. She was two years his junior, a spinster like him, her life equally circumscribed by duty and routine. They had lived together for twenty years, since the death of their mother, sharing a quiet, companionable existence built on unspoken understandings.

And there was Pip. The thought of the canary brought a flicker of warmth to Ezekiel’s chest. He had bought the bird five years ago from a trader in Whitechapel, a tiny, bright splash of yellow against the drabness of his days. The canary lived in a wooden cage that hung by the window of the parlor, and each morning, as Ezekiel ate his porridge, Pip would sing. The bird’s song was a small, perfect thing, a trilling cascade of notes that seemed to mock the grayness of the city outside. Ezekiel cared for the bird with a meticulous tenderness, changing its water, sweeping the bottom of the cage, and saving the best seeds from his own dinner. In return, Pip sang, filling the quiet rooms with a joy that Ezekiel could never quite articulate but which sustained him in ways he did not fully comprehend.

He reached Pentonville and began the climb up the gentle slope of Amwell Street. The lamps were lit in the hallways, and he could smell the collective suppers of the neighbors drifting through the thin walls—boiled cabbage, fried fish, the sharp tang of vinegar. He climbed the stairs to the second floor, his knees aching slightly, and put his key in the lock.

"Ezekiel?" Martha’s voice came from the kitchen. "Is that you?"

"It is," he called, hanging his coat on the peg by the door and setting his satchel on the bench.

He stood in the narrow hallway, listening to the sounds of the flat. The clatter of pots, the hiss of the kettle, and, beneath it all, the bright, clear song of Pip. The canary was singing its evening song, a cascade of notes that filled the small space with an unexpected beauty. Ezekiel closed his eyes and let the sound wash over him, feeling the tension in his shoulders ease.
