The rain in Highbury Grove did not wash things clean; it merely turned the grime into a slick, iridescent film that coated the cobblestones and the shop windows alike. Elara Vance wiped the condensation from the glass of the apothecary’s front door, her breath fogging the air in short, sharp bursts. It was November, and the chill had a teeth-gritting quality that seeped through her wool skirt and into her bones. Inside, the shop smelled of camphor, stale paper, and the distinct, metallic tang of fear.

Behind the counter stood Mr. Arthur Penhaligon, a man whose waistcoat seemed to struggle against the expansion of his belly. He was polishing a brass mortar with a rag that had long since lost its ability to absorb dirt, his movements perfunctory, his eyes darting toward the door with the nervous energy of a hawk expecting a stone. He had hired Elara three months ago, not because he needed help, but because he needed a shield. The Royal Pharmaceutical Society had rejected her application with a letter of such polite cruelty that she had framed it in her small, damp room above the chandler’s shop on nearby Mare Street. *Incompatible with the social standing expected of the profession.* The words had been printed in crisp, black type, a seal of disapproval that cost her nothing but her dignity.

Penhaligon did not care about dignity. He cared about the margin. Elara had passed her examinations with honors, her hands steady enough to measure a grain of strychnine with the precision of a surgeon’s eye. Penhaligon, conversely, had a tendency to misread labels and a profound misunderstanding of chemical stability. He was the face; Elara was the mind. To the customers, he was the Master Chemist, a man of science and authority. To Elara, he was a liability wrapped in tweed.

"Miss Vance," Penhaligon said, his voice thin and reedy. "The shelf. The blue jars. They are discolored."

Elara stepped behind the counter, bypassing the register. The shop was empty, save for the shadow of a streetlamp flickering outside. She moved to the back of the counter, where the true work was done. Here, the shelves were lined with glass and ceramic, labeled in her precise, small hand. She pulled down the jar in question. It contained a tincture of aconite, a potent preparation for heart conditions. The color was a murky amber, not the clear, pale gold it should have been.

"Contaminated," she murmured, tilting the jar to the light. "The glass was not properly sealed. The moisture from the cellar has degraded the alkaloids. It’s useless. It’s dangerous."

Penhaligon peered over her shoulder, his nose wrinkling. "Use it is not. But we can dilute it. Add more glycerin. The customer will not taste the difference. It is a strong batch, yes? Stronger is better for the heart."

Elara felt a cold knot form in her stomach. "No, Arthur. You cannot dilute a degraded alkaloid. The concentration becomes unpredictable. If a patient takes this, they could suffer cardiac arrest. It is not a matter of taste; it is a matter of life."

He waved a hand, dismissing her concern with a flick of his wrist. "You worry too much, Miss Vance. You are always worrying. I am the chemist. I sign the label. I take the responsibility. You mix. I sell."

It was the dynamic that had defined their arrangement since the day he had hired her. He took the credit, the fee, and the social standing. She took the labor, the risk, and the silence. The silence was the price of entry into a world that had barred her door. She had told herself it was temporary, a stepping stone, but the months had bled into one another, and the silence had grown heavy, like the air before a storm.

"I will throw it away," Elara said, her voice flat.

"You will not," Penhaligon snapped, his composure cracking. "That is a twenty-shilling order. Do you know what twenty shillings is? It is a week’s rent for your room. It is my profit. You will fix it. You have the skill. Use it."

Before she could protest, the bell above the door jingled. The door swung open, letting in a gust of wet wind and a figure wrapped in a heavy cloak. It was Mrs. Gable, a local widow who suffered from a weak heart. She was trembling, her face pale beneath the soot of the city.

"Mr. Penhaligon," she whispered, clutching her shawl. "I am so unwell. The doctor has said my heart is failing. He prescribed the aconite tincture. I came early to get it before the shop closed. Please, I feel I may not last the night."

Penhaligon straightened, his face shifting into a mask of professional concern. "Mrs. Gable. Of course. We have the finest preparations in the district. I shall mix it fresh for you. It will strengthen your pulse. It will save you."

Elara stepped back, her heart hammering against her ribs. She looked at the jar of tainted aconite on the counter. She looked at Penhaligon, who was already reaching for the measuring spoon. She looked at Mrs. Gable, whose eyes were wide with a terror that mirrored her own.

If she spoke now, if she revealed that Penhaligon did not know what he was doing, that he had sold her work to the board as a joke, that she was the one who had actually passed the exams, she would destroy him. But more importantly, she would destroy herself. She would be exposed as an imposter in the eyes of the community, a woman who had claimed expertise she did not have legally. The scandal would end her chances of ever working in the field, not even as an assistant. She would be cast out, branded a fraud.

But if she stayed silent, Mrs. Gable would drink the poison.

Penhaligon dipped the spoon into the murky amber liquid. The smell of rot hit Elara’s nose.
