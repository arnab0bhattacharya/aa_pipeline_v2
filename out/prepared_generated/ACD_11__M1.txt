It was a raw and gusty evening in late October when Miss Alice Carroway was shown into our rooms. The wind had swept the leaves from the plane trees in the square below and now rattled at our window-panes with an insistent, mournful finger. My friend, Sherlock Holmes, had been for three days in a state of irritable boredom, a condition which I had come to dread more than any active case, for it found expression in a singular violin-playing which was as erratic as it was brilliant. The arrival of our visitor, therefore, was a relief to me, if not to him.

She was a tall, slender girl of perhaps twenty-five, with the clear eyes and resolute bearing that spoke of a quiet strength. Her dress, though of a dark and sombre material, was well-kept, and her gloved hands, which clasped a small reticule upon her lap, were those of a lady. She sat very straight in the armchair, but there was a tremor in her voice as she began to speak.

“I have come to you, Mr. Holmes,” she said, “because I do not know where else to turn. It concerns my father, the Reverend Julian Carroway, of St. Saviour’s, in the parish of Southwark.”

Holmes, who had been leaning back with his eyes half-closed, opened them fully at the mention of the parish. “The parish of St. Saviour’s, you say? An interesting locality. Pray proceed, Miss Carroway.”

“My father is a man of the most robust and cheerful disposition,” she continued, her words now coming more freely. “He is a man of God, but he is also a man of the world, and he takes a great and practical interest in the lives of his flock. He is known for his firm hand and his kind heart. But for the past week, he has been a changed man.”

“In what way?” Holmes asked, steepling his long fingers.

“A week ago last Tuesday, he went out to make a sick-call in a poor street down by the river, a place called Rope-Walk Alley. He was gone for some hours. When he returned, he was pale—horribly pale—and he would not speak. I asked him what was the matter, but he only shook his head and retired to his study. Since that evening, he has not been himself. He has refused all food except what I bring to him, and he has told the churchwardens that he is too ill to preach on Sunday.”

“A singular result of a parish visit,” Holmes remarked. “And what else?”

“This is the strangest part of it,” said Miss Carroway, her voice dropping to a near-whisper. “Every night, he sits at his desk and writes. He writes for hours, in a feverish, agitated manner. And then, at dawn, I hear him in the grate. He has built up the fire and he burns the letter he has written. Every single night. I have seen the ashes myself. He will not tell me a word of what troubles him, and I fear for his reason. He was so strong, Mr. Holmes. To see him so… broken.”

Holmes had risen and was pacing the room, his energy renewed. “The rope-walk by the river. A classic setting. Tell me, Miss Carroway, has your father any enemies? A man in his position must make them.”

“None that I know of. He is respected and loved.”

“Has he spoken of any new acquaintance? Any face seen in the crowd that disturbed him?”

“Nothing. He is simply… haunted. Mr. Holmes, I beg of you, learn what happened in that dreadful street. Whatever it is, the knowledge cannot be worse than this torment of not knowing.”

“Very well,” said Holmes, with a briskness that was meant to be reassuring. “I shall look into the matter. Come, Watson, the air of Southwark may prove more stimulating than that of Baker Street. A turn in the fog will do us good.”

A half-hour later, we found ourselves in Rope-Walk Alley. It was a narrow, cobbled lane, hemmed in by tall, soot-stained tenements whose upper stories almost met overhead. A perpetual dampness seemed to cling to the very stones, and the air was thick with the smells of the river, of boiled cabbage, and of poverty. Holmes moved through the gloom with the purposeful stride of a hound on a scent. He paused before a small chandler’s shop, its window displaying tallow candles and coils of cheap rope.

“Information is often cheapest where it seems most costly,” he murmured, and entered. A few minutes later he emerged, having learned that the Reverend Carroway had spent a considerable time in the shop on the evening in question, speaking with the proprietor, a one-armed old sailor named Silas Blackwood. We found Mr. Blackwood in a small back room, smoke curling from a short clay pipe.

He was a hard-faced man with one eye that regarded us with a watery suspicion. “The parson?” he grunted, when Holmes made his inquiry. “Aye, he was here. A fine gentleman. He came to see a man named Tom Hackett. A sickly lad who lived up on the third floor. The parson sat with him for near an hour. When he came down, he looked as though he’d seen a ghost. Paid me for a drop of brandy for the boy and left. That’s all I know.”

The story was simple, and yet it did not satisfy my companion. As we walked back towards the main thoroughfare, Holmes was silent, his brow furrowed in thought.

“It is too simple, Watson,” he said at last. “A sick call to a dying man is a clergyman’s daily bread. It does not account for a week of torment and nightly letters to the fire. The brandy was a kindly gesture, but it is not the key. No, there is a deeper current here. Let us make one more inquiry.”
