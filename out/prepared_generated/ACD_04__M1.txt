It was upon the morning of the fourteenth of March that Mr. Alistair Finch, the celebrated manager of the Lyceum Regent, presented himself in our rooms at Baker Street. He was a man of florid complexion and nervous energy, his grey frock-coat proclaiming the prosperous tradesman rather than the artist. In one hand he clutched a letter, the paper of which was of the commonest kind.

“My dear Mr. Holmes,” he began, his voice a little tremulous, “I am here upon a matter of the utmost gravity. It concerns Miss Violet St. Claire, the leading lady of my new production, ‘The Crimson Vow’.”

Holmes, who had been observing a speck of dust upon the mantelpiece with singular interest, turned his keen eyes upon our visitor. “I am familiar with the name, Mr. Finch. Her Lady Macbeth is said to be a thing of tragic perfection. Pray, be seated and state your case without preamble.”

Finch sank into the armchair and smoothed the letter upon his knee. “This was delivered to my office yesterday. It is anonymous, of course. It states, in the plainest terms, that Miss St. Claire will not survive the first night of our play.”

He handed the missive to Holmes, who read it aloud. “‘The star that shines too brightly is destined to fall. Look to the stage, not the skies, for the trap that awaits her.’ A trifle melodramatic,” Holmes remarked, passing the letter to me. “The hand is educated, but disguised. The ink is common, the paper likewise. It tells us little, save that the writer possesses a certain theatrical turn of phrase.”

“Miss St. Claire dismisses it as the spite of a rival actress, a Miss Thorne, whom she supplanted for the part,” Finch went on, wringing his hands. “She is all courage and defiance. But I cannot be so sanguine. The threat of a ‘trap’ has a literal and unpleasant ring upon a stage.”

“A point which has not escaped me,” said Holmes. “Has anything else occurred to give colour to this warning?”

“One thing, and it was that which finally compelled me to seek you out. My doorman, a most reliable old fellow named Hackett, was on duty late last night. He saw a stranger loitering near the stage entrance. When challenged, the man said he was a scenery painter seeking work. But Hackett, who has eyes like a hawk, saw him not at the flats, but bent over the machinery for the great trapdoor in the centre of the stage. He was studying the winding gear with an intensity that was most unnatural.”

Holmes’s languor vanished in an instant. He sat bolt upright, his eyes gleaming. “Excellent! The abstract threat has acquired a tangible agent. This is infinitely more interesting. A man who studies the machinery of a trapdoor has a purpose that is likely to be as sudden as it is final. What was this stranger’s appearance?”

“Hackett says he was tall and thin, with a dark beard and a heavy coat, despite the mildness of the evening. He wore a soft hat, pulled low.”

“A description that would fit half the men in London. Yet it is a beginning. And the dress rehearsal is tonight, you say?”

“Tonight at eight o’clock. It is a closed affair, for the cast and crew only.”

“Then we shall attend,” Holmes declared, rising and beginning to pace the room. “But not as ourselves. Finch, you will procure for me a costume as a property man—something shabby, with paint stains, if you can manage it. I must have access to every corner of that theatre without arousing comment. Watson, you will accompany me as a friend of the management, perhaps a provincial critic. Your sober demeanour will lend the affair a certain verisimilitude.”

And so it was that at a quarter to eight we found ourselves at the stage door of the Lyceum Regent. The air was thick with the scent of sawdust, size, and gaslight. Finch, true to his word, had provided Holmes with a most convincing disguise. My friend, in a faded corduroy jacket and trousers daubed with the colours of a dozen forgotten dramas, was the very image of a stage-hand. He shuffled with a slight stoop, his hands deep in his pockets, his sharp features lost in the gloom of a peaked cap.

The rehearsal proceeded with the usual interruptions and exasperated cries from the director. Miss Violet St. Claire was a creature of fire and spirit, her voice ringing with a clear, bell-like tone that commanded the vast, dark space of the auditorium. I observed her closely. She moved with a dancer’s grace, yet I noted a certain tension in her bearing, a watchfulness in her fine eyes that belied her public show of indifference to the threat.

Holmes, meanwhile, had melted into the very fabric of the theatre. I saw him for a moment conferring with Hackett, the doorman, then he was gone, reappearing in the flies above the stage, then vanishing behind a stack of painted flats. He was a ghost among the mechanics of illusion.

The climax of the play, and of the rehearsal, was the scene where the villain, foiled in his treachery, plunges to his doom through the stage itself. The great trapdoor, some eight feet square, was to open swiftly, and the actor, a Mr. Garrick, was to leap into the darkness below, to be caught by a canvas sheet held by two men in the pit.

As the moment approached, I felt a cold premonition. The stage manager called for the trap. There was a grinding of gears from below, and the section of the stage floor began to drop.

“Hold!” The voice, sharp and authoritative, rang out from the shadows by the proscenium arch. It was Holmes. He had shed his stoop and moved with a panther’s speed to the edge of the trap. The stage manager, aghast, ordered the machinery to be stopped. The trap hung half-open, a black square of nothingness.
