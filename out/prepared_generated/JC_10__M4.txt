The calm had endured now nine days, and the ship, a barque of some eight hundred tons, named the *Pellucid*, of Liverpool, lay motionless upon a sea so glassy that the heavens themselves seemed to have descended and become fixed there, a vast and burnished mirror in which the idle sails hung inverted and ghastly, like the grave-clothes of a drowned thing. The sun by day was a disc of hammered brass, pitiless and without shadow, and by night the stars came out with a hard and cruel brilliancy, and the silence was such that a man might hear the blood knocking in his own temples and mistake it for the footsteps of some approaching fate. The heat entered into the very substance of the ship, into the timbers and the cordage, so that the ropes were hot to the touch and the pitch bubbled in the seams of the deck, and the air below was a stale and suffocating vapour, heavy with the smell of bilge and the sour exhalations of frightened men.

It was in this condition of things, when the water-casks were daily diminishing under the careful measurement of the mate, and the allowance had been twice reduced, that the trouble concerning the carpenter, one Elias Bunn, came to a head. He was a small, wiry man of fifty, with a face like a withered apple and a manner of great taciturnity, who had served in the ship for three voyages and was accounted a skilful workman, though of a disposition somewhat morose and given to keeping his own counsel. The charge against him was vague in its origin and dark in its suggestion. A case of preserved meat had been found broken open in the fore-hold, and certain pounds of it were missing; and since Bunn alone possessed a key to that compartment, and was known to have been seen near it on the night in question, the crew, whose nerves were frayed to the raw edge by the long inaction and the scarcity of water, had fixed upon him as the thief with a unanimity that was the more terrible because it was founded upon no evidence that a court of law would have entertained for a moment.

The captain, a man named Harrowby, of the old school, bluff and hearty in fair weather but now grown gaunt and hollow-eyed with anxiety, sent for me to his cabin on the tenth morning. I was the second mate, a young man then, and I remember how the sweat stood upon his forehead as he spoke, and how his voice, ordinarily so commanding, had sunk to a sort of husky whisper.

“Mr. Sempill,” said he, “you know these men better than I do. They are in a nasty mood. They have got it into their heads that Bunn is robbing them of their food, and I tell you plainly that if we do not find the true thief soon, or if the wind does not come, there will be murder done aboard this ship. I have spoken to Bunn, and he denies it, but he denies it in a way that makes me doubt him in spite of myself. He stands there like a stone and says nothing, and a man who says nothing is a man whom other men will hang.”

I went forward to the fo’c’sle, where the crew were gathered in the shadow of the deck-house, and I saw at once that the thing had gone beyond the stage of mere grumbling. They were seated in a half-circle, and in the midst of them stood Bunn, his back against the bulkhead, his little withered face pale and set, his eyes moving from one to another of his accusers with a look that I can only describe as one of profound and weary contempt. The boatswain, a big, red-faced fellow named Craddock, was speaking, and his words came out with a kind of heavy deliberation that was more menacing than any heat.

“We ain’t askin’ for your life, carpenter,” he was saying, “we’re askin’ for what’s ours by right. There’s a case broke open and the meat gone, and you’ve got the key. If you’re an honest man, you’ll open that locker and let us see what’s inside it.”

Bunn did not answer. He looked at Craddock, and then he looked at me, and in that look there was something which I have never forgotten—a sort of appeal, not for mercy, but for comprehension, as though he were saying: “You are a gentleman, you are an officer; can you not see that I am helpless before these men, and that my silence is the only dignity left to me?” But I was young and I was afraid of the crew, and I said nothing.

It was then that the captain appeared among us, having come forward unnoticed in the excitement. He stood for a moment looking at the scene, and then he said, in a voice that he strove to make cheerful:

“Now, my lads, this is a foolish business. You are all good men and true, and I’ll not have any man among you accused without proof. Bunn, have you anything to say?”

The carpenter turned his head slowly. “I have said what I have to say, sir,” he replied, in a low, distinct voice. “I did not take the meat. I do not know who took it. I will not open my locker to satisfy a pack of cowards who are afraid of a calm.”

A murmur ran through the crew at the word “cowards,” and Craddock took a step forward. But the captain held up his hand.

“Very well,” said he. “Then we will open the locker together, and if it is found to contain nothing but what is proper to it, I will expect every man here to shake hands with the carpenter and make his apology.”
