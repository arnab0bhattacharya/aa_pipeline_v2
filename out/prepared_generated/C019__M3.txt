The Villa Aprile clung to the side of the hill like a pale stone forgotten there by a god with an erratic arm. Below, Lake Verdana lay in the August heat, a sheet of hammered metal that hurt the eyes to look at. Inside the villa, the air was thick with the scent of jasmine and old plaster, and the flies ticked against the shutters like a clock counting down to something no one cared to name.

Margaret Eaves sat at the writing desk in the salon, her pen poised over a sheet of thin blue paper. She wrote: *The weather continues fine. The children are quite well. The villa is very comfortable, though the kitchen arrangements took some getting used to. Mrs. Hartley and I have come to an understanding about the evening meal.* She paused, looked at the sentence, and left it. What understanding? That the woman would cook what she pleased and Margaret would eat it and be grateful, because the butcher spoke no English and the vegetables arrived in a state of unwashed intimacy that would have sent her mother into a decline. She dipped her pen again. *The view from the terrace is everything we were promised. I think of you all often.* This at least was true. She thought of them in their drawing room in Cheltenham, the curtains drawn against the rain, and she thought of them with a longing that surprised her, though she could not have said whether she longed for them or for the version of herself that had existed in that room, a woman whose days had a shape she recognized.

She did not write that her eldest daughter, Cecily, had not been home before midnight in three days. She did not write that her youngest son, Leo, had come in that morning with his shirt torn and his knees bleeding and his eyes full of a wild, bright light she had never seen in them before and did not know how to answer. She did not write that her husband, Henry, had gone to bed with a headache on the second day and had scarcely risen since, lying in the shuttered room with a cloth over his eyes while the Italian sun poured down outside like something poured from a crucible. She folded the letter, slid it into an envelope, and addressed it to her sister, Anne.

Cecily was on the water. She sat in the stern of a long wooden boat while Count Aldo Serbelloni pulled at the oars with a slow, measured rhythm that made no effort and suggested no end. He was not a young man. He was forty perhaps, or older, with a face that had been handsome in the way of certain statues, precise and symmetrical, and was now settling into something more interesting, a face that had been used. His hands were brown and the veins stood out on them. He looked at her and said nothing, and she looked back and said nothing, and the boat moved across the lake toward the place where the water turned from silver to green.

She had met him at the hotel in Pescallo, where they had gone for ice because Mrs. Hartley had failed to produce any. He had been sitting at a table in the garden with two other men, and he had stood when she entered, and she had felt something shift in the room, a rearrangement of the air around her, and she had known. It was not a word she used. She did not think *love* or *passion* or any of the words the novels offered. She thought: *here is the thing that has been missing, and here it is.* He had walked her back to the villa in the dark, and at the gate he had taken her hand and raised it to his lips, and she had felt the warmth of his mouth through her glove and had gone into the house shaking.

Now, in the boat, he shipped the oars and let them drift. The shore was a dark line, the villas white among the trees. He spoke in English, which he did well, though slowly, as though selecting each word from a drawer.

"You are very quiet today."

"I am always quiet."

"Yes. But today it is a different quiet."

She did not answer. She was thinking of the letter she knew her mother was writing, the letter that would arrive in Cheltenham and be passed around the drawing room, the letter that would say nothing. She wondered if her mother knew. She thought her mother did, that her mother had always known things without being told, and that the knowing was the reason for the letters, the careful, empty letters that were a kind of architecture, a structure built to keep something out. Or in.

"I am going away next week," the Count said. "To my estate in the south. It is necessary."

She looked at him. His face was turned toward the shore, and she could not read it.

"For how long?"

"I do not know. Some weeks. Perhaps longer."

The water lapped against the hull. She felt the distance already, opening between them like the green water below, and she put her hand on the gunwale to steady herself.

"May I come?"

He turned then, and looked at her, and she saw that he had expected this and had hoped for it and feared it, all at once.

"You do not know what you ask."

"I am asking."

He took her hand. His grip was firm and dry and warm. "Then yes," he said.

Leo was on the hillside above the villa, standing in the ruins of a chapel he had found three days ago and had claimed as his own. He was eleven, thin, browned by the sun, his hair bleached to the color of straw. He had a cut on his forearm from the brambles and a bruise on his thigh from a fall, and he was happy in a way he had never been happy in England, where he had been the smallest boy in his class and the slowest at games and the one whose name the masters forgot.
