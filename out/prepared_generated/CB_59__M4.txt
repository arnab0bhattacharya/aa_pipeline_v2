The frost had not yet relinquished its hold upon the pane when Miss Ellinor Ravensworth entered the morning-room, and found the fire had been suffered to burn low in the grate; a circumstance which, in any other house, might have occasioned a sharp word to the negligent housemaid, but which, at Ravensworth Grange, was accepted with the silent resignation that attends all things in a dwelling where a single will, austere and unyielding, has long since subdued every lesser spirit to its own temper. She rang the bell herself, and stood with her back to the dying embers, her hands clasped before her, watching the pale light of a February morning struggle through the tall windows and fall in cold, cheerless patches upon the polished floor.

She was six-and-twenty, and she had refused, in the course of the last five years, no fewer than seven offers of marriage; a fact which the county spoke of variously, according to the disposition of the speaker—some calling her proud, some cold, some merely fastidious; but none, she was well aware, suspecting the truth, which was that she had never yet met the man who could look at her without first computing the value of her acres. She had begun to think that such a man did not exist; that she was condemned to pass her days in a splendid isolation, mistress of a great house and a greater fortune, and mistress of nothing else—least of all of her own heart, which, in despite of her reason, persisted in demanding something more than a settlement.

The servant announced that a person waited upon her in the library—a Mr. Ashworth, who desired a moment's speech concerning a matter of business. The name struck her with a peculiar force. She had known a Mr. Ashworth once; a grave, dark-browed young man, who had come to the Grange in her father's time to instruct her brother in mathematics and the classics, and who had gone away again, after a year, so quietly that she had scarcely marked his departure until she found that the house was duller for it. She had been but seventeen then, and he perhaps nine-and-twenty; and there had been nothing between them but a few conversations, held mostly in the presence of her brother, upon subjects remote from sentiment—the motions of the planets, the wars of the Romans, the nature of duty. Yet she had remembered them.

She went to the library with a quicker step than she was wont to use, and found him standing by the window, his hat in his hand, his figure somewhat stooped, his coat threadbare but scrupulously brushed. He was greyer than she remembered, and there were lines about his mouth that spoke of care rather than of years; but his eyes were the same—dark, direct, and utterly devoid of that obsequious softness which she had learned to expect in the eyes of every man who addressed her.

"Miss Ravensworth," said he, with a bow that was neither awkward nor servile, "I must crave your pardon for this intrusion. I am come upon an errand which I fear will seem to you importunate; but I have been told that you are the patroness of the school at Millbeck, and I am now the master of it."

"You are the master of the school?" she repeated, and felt a strange sensation, half pleasure and half pain, at the thought that he had been so near her all this while, and she had not known it.

"I am," he returned. "I took the situation a twelvemonth since, when my health failed me in the south, and I found that I must either teach or starve. The school is small, and the children are the children of labouring men; but I had rather teach them than flatter the sons of gentlemen. I have come to you because the roof of the schoolroom leaks, and the trustees will do nothing without your sanction. I have brought the estimate—it is but fifteen pounds—and I have come myself, because I would not have you think that I sent a beggar to plead for me."

She took the paper from his hand, and glanced at it, but she did not read a word of it. "You are very plain with me, Mr. Ashworth," she said, at length.

"I am plain with every one, madam," he answered. "It is the only accomplishment I possess, and I have found it a costly one. But I will not pretend to a humility I do not feel, nor to a gratitude for favours not yet granted. You have the power to grant this money or to withhold it; and I have come to ask it, not as a favour to me, but as a right belonging to the children, who are your tenants' children, and who sit in a room where the rain falls upon their books."

She looked at him steadily. "You speak as if you thought I should refuse."

"I speak as one who has learned that the rich are not often to be trusted with the happiness of the poor," he said; and then, as if recollecting himself, he added, in a lower tone, "I beg your pardon. That was unjust. I have no right to visit upon you the offences of your class."

"You have a right to your opinion," she said, "and I had rather hear it than the smooth speeches I am used to. But you do me injustice, Mr. Ashworth. I have never refused a just request in my life; and I shall not begin with yours. The money shall be sent to-morrow. But I would have you know that I do not give it because you ask it, but because the children need it."
