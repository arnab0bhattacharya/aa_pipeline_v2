The preferment offered to Mr. Arthur Grey was of a kind which, in the estimation of his mother, might have justified the sacrifice of far greater things than a young man’s fancy. The living of Compton Bassett, in the gift of Sir John Marwood, was worth nearly four hundred a year, with a house and garden of no common comfort; and Sir John, whose views upon the subject were as clear as his estate was large, had intimated that the whole might be settled upon Mr. Grey, upon the single condition of his making Miss Marwood his wife.

Miss Marwood was the niece of Sir John, and the sole expectation of his fortune; a circumstance which, as Mrs. Grey observed to her son, ought to have its weight with any rational mind. She was not, it must be confessed, a young woman of any striking attractions. Her countenance was of that negative sort which leaves the beholder to supply what it may want; her conversation, though perfectly well-bred, had not the vivacity to conceal its poverty of ideas; and her figure, though neat and unexceptionable, had none of those graces which the eye delights to follow. Yet she was gentle, obedient, and, as her uncle was careful to add, of a temper so equable as to promise the happiest domestic life to any man of sense.

It was upon a soft morning in October, while the leaves were yet upon the trees, that the matter was first brought to a decision. Mr. Grey, walking in the garden of the parsonage where he had been bred, and where he now expected to succeed his uncle, felt the conflict of his feelings with a poignancy which the mildness of the air served only to sharpen. On the one side was the living, the house, the respectability of a settled establishment, all urged upon him by the affectionate anxiety of a mother who saw the world as it is, and not as the imagination might paint it; on the other was the image of Miss Lucy Harcourt, the daughter of a neighbouring clergyman, with whom he had long maintained that species of understanding which is the most dangerous to a young man’s peace, because it has no name, and therefore no bounds.

Lucy Harcourt was possessed of no fortune; her father’s income was small, her prospects in life were of the most limited kind; yet she had a quickness of mind, a play of countenance, and a warmth of heart, which made the society of Miss Marwood appear, by comparison, a desert. It was this which now lay heavy upon him; and as he turned towards the house, he was met by his mother, whose countenance wore the expression of one who has a duty to perform, and is resolved to perform it.

‘Well, Arthur,’ said she, with a calmness which was more alarming than any reproach, ‘and have you considered what is to be done?’

‘I have considered, madam,’ replied he, ‘and I find the matter more difficult than I could have wished.’

‘That is to say, you find it difficult to be wise. There is no difficulty in the case, but what arises from your own romantic notions. Here is a gentleman of fortune and character, who offers you an establishment for life, and asks only that you should render yourself and a deserving young woman happy. What is there in this that a man of sense could hesitate about?’

‘The deserving young woman you speak of is not the one to whom my heart is attached,’ said Arthur, with a blush which he could not suppress.

‘Your heart!’ repeated Mrs. Grey, with a smile of gentle contempt. ‘And what is your heart, that it should stand in the way of your duty and your interest? Lucy Harcourt is a very good girl, but she has nothing; and with nothing, you know, a clergyman must be miserable. You would have the house, the garden, the glebe, all the comforts of a respectable life, and you would exchange them for a cottage and a wife with no provision but her own good qualities. Is this prudence? Is this even kindness?’

Arthur was silent. He knew that his mother’s arguments were such as the world would allow; yet he felt that there was something in them which the world does not always comprehend. He thought of Lucy’s smile, of the pleasure of her conversation, of the thousand nameless charms which made her presence a delight, and he felt that to give these up would be to sacrifice the very essence of his happiness.

‘But consider,’ continued Mrs. Grey, ‘how much good you might do with such a living. You might be a comfort to the poor, an example to the parish, a blessing to all around you. And with Miss Marwood, who is of so gentle a disposition, you would have no opposition, no strife, but all would be peace and propriety. Can you say as much for a marriage founded only on inclination?’

‘I believe I could,’ said Arthur, with a hesitation which did not escape his mother’s notice.

‘Then you believe what no one else will believe. However, I shall not press you further. You are of an age to judge for yourself; only remember that the opportunity which is now offered may not occur again. Sir John is not a man to be trifled with; and if you refuse his niece, you refuse the living also.’

With these words she left him to his reflections; and Arthur, walking alone in the garden, felt that the struggle in his mind was such as no words could express. He thought of Lucy, with her little fortune and her cheerful spirit; and then of Miss Marwood, with her large expectations and her tranquil air. He could not deny that the latter offered the more prudent path; yet he could not bring himself to take it.
