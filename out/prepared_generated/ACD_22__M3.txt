It was upon a raw, foggy morning in the latter days of November that Mrs. Penelope Vane was shown into our consulting room. She was an elderly lady of refined bearing, her grey hair neatly arranged beneath a bonnet of black silk, though her gloved hands twisted ceaselessly in her lap, betraying an agitation of mind that her composed features sought to conceal. Mr. Martin Hewitt—for it is of my friend and former colleague that I speak—regarded her with that quiet, attentive gaze which invited her confidence.

"I fear, sir, that you will think me a foolish old woman," she began, her voice trembling ever so slightly. "My nephew, Mr. Charles Vane, certainly does. He believes my wits are leaving me. Yet I am convinced, Mr. Hewitt, that someone enters my house each night and moves my furniture."

Hewitt steepled his fingers. "You have observed this yourself, madam?"

"I have. I live alone, save for my maidservant, Martha. My house is a modest terrace dwelling in Abingdon Lane, near Lombard Street. On three separate occasions, I have marked the exact position of my dining-room sideboard by laying a thread across the carpet from its foot to the leg of my chair. Each morning, the thread has been displaced by a full inch. The sideboard, heavy as it is, has been shifted."

"Has anything been taken?"

"That is the strangest part of it. Nothing whatsoever. Only the furniture is moved—always the sideboard, and once the mahogany bookcase against the adjoining wall. Always by an inch, never more."

Hewitt asked a few further questions, learning that the household retired early, that the doors were locked, and that no sign of forced entry had ever been discovered. He then requested permission to call at Abingdon Lane that very afternoon.

We found the house a narrow, three-storeyed building of weathered brick, hemmed in by its neighbours in the manner common to the older streets of the City. Mrs. Vane conducted us inside. The dining room was at the rear of the ground floor, a sombre apartment furnished in the heavy taste of a generation past. I noted the great mahogany sideboard against the party wall, and beside it the bookcase. Hewitt examined the wall and floor with minute attention. He pressed his ear to the plasterwork, rapped it with his knuckles, and measured the distance from the sideboard to the chimney-piece with a pocket tape. Then he drew me to the window, which overlooked a narrow paved yard.

"Notice, my dear fellow," he murmured, "that beyond this yard stands the rear wall of a substantial building. The bricks are new."

I saw that he was right. The adjoining premises, visible above the yard wall, bore signs of recent construction work—scaffolding poles still lashed against the upper storeys, and fresh mortar visible even at a distance.

"Mrs. Vane," said Hewitt, turning, "what establishment occupies the building immediately behind your house?"

"That, I believe, is the London and Colonial Bank, sir. They have been doing works there these several weeks past—enlarging their vaults, I understand, for my nephew mentioned it."

Hewitt's eyes gleamed with that peculiar brightness I had learned to associate with the gathering of a conclusion. "I shall require a few moments alone in this room, if you will be so good."

Left to ourselves, Hewitt dropped to his hands and knees and examined the skirting board where it met the sideboard. He drew a small penknife from his waistcoat and probed gently at the join. I observed that a section of the board, some three feet in length, had been fitted with concealed hinges and could be swung inward upon a cleverly disguised catch. Behind it was not solid brickwork but a rough aperture, freshly cut, opening into a space beyond.

"You see?" said Hewitt, his voice low. "A tunnel, and a recent one. The enlargement of the bank's vaults has provided both the cover and the necessity. These fellows are excavating an entrance to the bank's strong room, and they have cut through into Mrs. Vane's house to gain a shorter route, or perhaps to shore up their workings. Each night, as they work the face of the tunnel, the vibration and the pressure of the earth behind the wall push the skirting board outward by a fraction, and the sideboard, standing flush against it, is shifted by just so much."

"But why have they not simply broken through openly?"

"Because the works are conducted at night, and they require that the wall remain intact until the final night—tonight, I should judge, from the appearance of the excavation. The sideboard, being heavy, resists the pressure and shifts only an inch at a time. Mrs. Vane's vigilance has, in effect, been measuring the progress of their digging."

He straightened and addressed the room at large, as though rehearsing his report. "The shifting furniture is not the work of an intruder entering from above, but of an excavation proceeding from below and behind. The removal of earth has caused a slight subsidence of the party wall's inner face, transmitted through the concealed panel to the sideboard."

Hewitt lost no time. Within the hour, Inspector Gregory of the City Police had been summoned, and by ten o'clock that evening a small party of constables was assembled in Mrs. Vane's dining room, the furniture having been quietly cleared. The old lady herself had been persuaded to take refuge with a relative in Kensington.

It was near midnight when the sound came—a low, grating rumble, followed by the soft fall of earth. The concealed panel swung inward, and a face appeared in the aperture, grimy and anxious, a dark lantern in one hand. The man had barely set one foot upon the dining-room carpet before Gregory's men seized him. He offered no resistance, only a groan of baffled rage. Behind him, in the low, shored-up tunnel, two companions were taken with equal swiftness, along with a quantity of tools and a rough plan of the bank's vault.
