In the deepening twilight of an autumn afternoon in the year 1834, the Rectory at Tawborough seemed to slumber under the weight of its own ivy, much as its incumbent, the Reverend Francis Amcotts, slumbered under the weight of his seventy years. He was a man whose profound benevolence of disposition was matched only by a remarkable incapacity to suspect it in others; a mental constitution which, while it saved him from many a sleepless night, exposed him to the practical inconveniences of a trust that knew no boundaries. Within his study, walls lined with patristic texts and crumbling folios of the early Reformers bore witness to a lifetime of scholarly pursuit, interrupted only by the gentle demands of his parishioners. Mr. Amcotts was at this moment engaged in reconciling a passage in St. Chrysostom with a cup of weak tea, his spectacles perilously balanced on the bridge of a nose that had been kindly battered by the passage of eight decades.

Upon the vicar’s unbounded confidence in his curate, Mr. Josiah Veal, this comfortable obliviousness largely depended. Mr. Veal, a man whose broad, pallid face was rendered additionally expressionless by a certain tightness of the lips, had for six years managed the temporalities of Tawborough parish with a pen that was swift, a demeanor that was unimpeachably orthodox, and a conscience that was conveniently adjustable to the contours of his own advantage. He spoke in the measured, somewhat nasal cadence of one who considered language primarily as a vehicle for respectable evasion, a quality much admired by the farmers of the district, who liked a man who could keep the parish books without startling the sheep.

The third occupant of this moral triangle was Miss Letitia Enderby, a young gentlewoman of independent means and energetic philanthropy, whose recent return to her native Midlands had introduced into Tawborough a spirit of inquiry distressingly unlike the rector’s peaceful acquiescence. Miss Enderby was not one of those philanthropists who are content to distribute tracts and warm flannel from a distance; she carried her sympathies into the damp alleys of the town, and her eyes, sharp and dark, were given to observing the material conditions of the poor with an exactness that belonged rather to the statistical compiler than to the visiting lady.

It was on a Tuesday that Miss Enderby, having accompanied the district nurse into a particularly noisome cellar dwelling, had her attention arrested by the figure of a wasted child lying on a heap of soiled straw. The mother, a gaunt woman with the dull, patient eyes of over-driven cattle, was washing a few rags at the door.

"I dunno how it is, ma'am," the woman said, in the broad, unhurried vernacular of the region, "but t' bit o' meal as t' Rector's fund was to gi' us, it niver coom at Michaelmas. An' Muster Veal, 'e come a-walkin' by hisself, an' 'e says as how t' parish money wor all spent i' mending t' church drain-pipes. But t' drain-pipes is broke still, for t' water cooms right in wi' t' rain."

Letitia’s heart had given a violent throb, followed by that cold, clear sinking which characterizes the sudden discovery of a hidden wrong. She made no immediate reply, but pressed a coin into the woman’s hand and walked swiftly away. The mention of the church drain-pipes had fixed her suspicion; she had observed those drain-pipes but a week ago, and they were precisely in the ruinous state described. If the parish funds, augmented by a generous annual donation from her own uncle, had not repaired the drains, nor purchased the meal, where had they gone?

Upon returning to the Rectory, she had begged to see the parish account-book, a request Mr. Veal, with his accustomed urbanity, could not well refuse without betraying uneasiness. She sat in the dining-parlor and turned the pages, her finger tracing the neat, double-columned figures. The entries were unimpeachable in their calligraphy: "Repairs to Drainage, 4l. 12s. 6d."; "Distribution of Coal and Meal to Indigent Poor, 7l. 3s. 0d." But beneath the ink, a deeper scrutiny revealed a peculiar erasure in the vellum binding, a faint reduplication of strokes that spoke of a careful and deliberate falsification. She remembered the exact sum of her uncle’s last donation: fifty pounds. The book showed only thirty-five pounds allotted to the poor. Fifteen pounds had vanished into Mr. Veal’s capacious pockets.

Now, as the evening shadows stretched across the Rectory garden, Letitia stood by the gate, the heavy account-book pressed against her side, feeling the full, oppressive weight of her discovery. The problem that lay before her was not merely one of evidence, but of those subtle moral algebraic equations in which the unknown quantity is human sorrow. She knew Mr. Amcotts’s nature as well as she knew the contours of the hills above Tawborough. To tell him that Josiah Veal, the man he had loaded with his confidences, the man who had bowed at his dinner-table and echoed his platitudes, was a thief, would be to strike a blow at the very center of the old man’s universe. It would not merely anger him; it would shatter the image of a benevolent world which he had spent seventy years constructing.

She reflected, with that analytical tendency which belongs to minds trained in observation, upon the mixed nature of human motives. Mr. Veal was not a melodramatic villain; he was a man of narrow intellect and expensive tastes, who had yielded to the temptation of an opportunity, softening his conscience by the easy doctrine that the poor were always improvident, and that a few pounds diverted from their misuse would do no great harm. He had probably begun with small defalcations, and the ease of his success had bred that fatal audacity which is the usual sequel to undiscovered wrongdoing. Yet, if the deception were allowed to continue, the poor of Tawborough would continue to starve, and the stream of benevolence, meant to irrigate a barren parish, would run perpetually into the stagnant pool of one man’s selfish luxury. Letitia felt the imperious demand of justice, but she felt also the yearning pity that is the foundation of all true charity.
