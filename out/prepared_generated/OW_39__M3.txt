In the city of Val di Lago, where the bridges are as numerous as sins and infinitely more picturesque, there lived a woman named Serafina. She was a maker of lace, a craft that in Val di Lago was considered the sister of prayer, as both involved a great deal of kneeling and produced very little money. Her lace was woven from the white breath of the sea-mists, or so the merchants claimed when selling it to foreigners, though Serafina herself knew it was woven from thread and despair. She was terribly poor, and therefore possessed the one advantage denied to the rich: she could not afford to be anything but herself.

Each morning, Serafina would carry her finished work to the great iron gates of the Palazzo Azzurro, where the nobility of Val di Lago disported themselves in a perpetual twilight of gilded mirrors and scandal. It was there that she was observed by the Duchess of Bellacqua, a woman whose beauty was a matter of public record, and whose vanity was a matter of private triumph. The Duchess was exquisitely made up; she looked like a miniature painted on hot porcelain, and she had managed to preserve her youth not by living wisely, but by living indifferently.

Pausing beneath the arch of the gate, where the southern sun struck the cobblestones and turned them to molten gold, the Duchess beckoned Serafina. The poor woman approached, holding out her wares—collars delicate as frost, and handkerchiefs so fine they seemed woven from the very air.

"Who made these?" asked the Duchess, her voice possessing the charming flatness of those who have never been contradicted.

"I did, Your Grace," Serafina murmured, her eyes cast down, for she had been told that to look at a duchess directly was to steal a glance, and glances, like jewels, were heavily taxed in Val di Lago.

"They are tolerable," said the Duchess, adjusting a diamond hairpin that cost more than the entire borough in which Serafina slept. "But they lack ambition. I am weary of mediocrity. I shall commission a veil. It must be finer than any ever made. It must be so delicate that it will be mistaken for a sigh, so intricate that the priests will weep to see it, and so utterly unique that I shall be the only woman in Val di Lago envied at the Cathedral of the Seven Bridges."

Serafina bowed her head. "It will take time, Your Grace. A year, perhaps. The thread must be spun from the finest spider-silk, and the pattern requires a thousand eyes."

"You have two," replied the Duchess, with the flawless logic of the aristocracy. "Use them. I shall pay you in gold, and in the privilege of knowing that your hands have served my face. The veil must be ready for the Carnival of the White Peacocks."

For twelve months, Serafina toiled in her narrow room above the canal. The southern kingdom was kind to the rich, offering them perpetual sunshine, but it was merciless to the poor, offering them only the glare of the sun on the water to blind them. Serafina sat at her cushion, her fingers flying over the ivory bobbins, weaving a pattern that was less a design and more a confession of the soul. She wove the secrets of the lagoon into the border, and the silence of the midnight bridges into the center. She worked by the light of tallow candles that smoked and wept, and as the lace grew, her sight shrank.

It is a curious thing about beauty: it demands the sacrifice of the artist, though it never asks the spectator to pay. The Duchess desired a miracle of thread, and Serafina paid the inevitable price of miracles. By the eleventh month, the world had become a blur of water-colors, and by the twelfth, she could see nothing at all. But the veil was finished. It lay upon the table like a flake of sea-foam, a gossamer web of such breathtaking fragility that it seemed to exist only as a rebuke to the grossness of matter.

Her young neighbor, a boy with a voice like cracked crystal, led her to the palace gate. The Duchess received them in her morning room, a chamber hung with pale blue silk and filled with white azaleas that smelled of expensive funerals.

"Is this it?" asked the Duchess, lifting the veil. It was so light it seemed to float upward, yearning for the ceiling.

"Yes, Your Grace," Serafina whispered, her sightless eyes fixed on the darkness. "It is the finest thing I have ever made. It will make you look like an angel."

"Angels are terribly dull," yawned the Duchess, draping the veil over her auburn hair. "They never have any adventures. But I suppose it will do. It makes me look tragically beautiful, which is exactly the effect I wished to achieve for the Carnival." She turned to her mirror, examining the effect. The veil clung to her features, softening her sharp cheekbones and casting a mist over her bright, hard eyes. "Yes, it is quite perfect. A masterpiece of illusion."

"I have gone blind making it, Your Grace," Serafina said softly. She did not say it to reproach the Duchess, for one cannot reproach a force of nature, but merely to state a fact, as a sailor might note a change in the wind.

The Duchess did not turn from the mirror. "Blindness is a great gift for an artist," she said, with the gentle cruelty of the very elegant. "It prevents one from seeing how ugly the world really is. You should be grateful. Besides, to look upon the veil I am about to wear is to see the last beautiful thing on earth. It is best to quit while you are ahead."

She dropped a heavy purse of gold into Serafina’s trembling hands. The metal was cold, and clinked with the hollow cheerfulness of money. The boy led the blind lace-maker away, and the Duchess prepared herself for the Carnival of the White Peacocks.
