The quarrel at the brewery had begun, as such quarrels will, with a trifle so small that neither party could afterwards remember it truly—whether it was the price of malt or the impudence of a drayman—but it had swollen by degrees into a difference of principle, and principle, once invoked in a county town during the Reform agitation, is a wind that blows where it lists and cares nothing for the damage done to household peace. Mr. Theophilus Gedge, brewer and maltster, of the Bridge Brewery at Cottesford, was a man of substance in that town, churchwarden and poor-law guardian, who had voted at every election for five-and-twenty years as his father had voted before him, and who held it for an axiom that a borough which had returned the same family to Parliament since the reign of Queen Anne could not be greatly in need of mending. His son Adam, a young man of four-and-twenty, lately returned from a year in London with a head full of statistics and a heart full of ardour, had taken up the cause of the Reform candidate with a vehemence that his father regarded as a species of domestic treason, aggravated by the circumstance that the young man aired his opinions in the brewhouse itself, among the very men whose votes, if the Bill passed, would become as good as Mr. Gedge's own.

“I tell you, Father,” said Adam, on the morning of the nomination, standing in the yard with his hands behind him and his hat pushed back, in the attitude of a man who has arguments in reserve, “you may call it agitation, but it is justice, and nothing less. Look at these men—” and he nodded toward the brewhouse door, whence issued the sound of coopers at work and the thin vapour of hot liquor—“honest fellows, who have served you ten years and more, who would be at your back in any trouble. Why should their voice count for nothing in the government of the country that taxes their beer?”

“Their voice!” said Mr. Gedge, with that species of laugh which is intended to convey contempt without the trouble of argument. “Their voice is very well in its place, and its place is the brewhouse. They know their business and I know mine; and if they had the franchise to-morrow, Adam, they would be driven to the poll like sheep by the first man who stood them a dinner and a pot of ale. I know the men of Cottesford, I believe, a trifle better than you do, having paid their wages and their doctor's bills these thirty years.”

“That is the very reason,” returned Adam, warmly, “that you should trust them the more. You say you know them; then show it. The Reformers ask no more than that a man who pays rates shall have a voice in the choosing of the man who spends them.”

“And who is to be the judge of the paying?” said Mr. Gedge, with a shrewdness that was not without its force. “There is your Mr. Vandeleur, the candidate, a London attorney, who knows no more of Cottesford than he does of the interior of Africa, promising every man a vote as freely as if votes were ha'penny buns at a fair. Promises are cheap in election time, Adam; I have seen a good many of them, and they are not worth the paper they are printed on.”

“Mr. Vandeleur is an honest man,” said Adam, “and would serve the town faithfully.”

“Honest!” cried the elder, his colour rising, for the name of the candidate was as a red rag to him. “Honest, is he? He dined at the Crown with a parcel of radical shopkeepers, and drank the health of 'the people,' and never once named the old member, who has sat for Cottesford since before that attorney was breeched. Honest! I tell you, sir, I will not have that name spoken in my yard. There is no honesty in setting class against class and son against father. And as for you,” he continued, his voice thickening with that anger which is half sorrow, “you that I have bred up to inherit this business and stand in my shoes—you to be the one to bring this shame upon me, and to teach my own men to look sour at the hand that feeds them! Get out of my sight, sir, and do not come into this yard again with your Vandeleur and your Reform till you have learned what is due to your father and your father's house.”

Adam's face flushed and then paled, for he was proud, and the words stung him in the tenderest part of his pride—the sense that he was acting honourably and was understood by none. He would have spoken, but his father had turned from him and was walking toward the counting-house with a step that betrayed the agitation he would fain have hidden; and Adam, left standing in the yard, became suddenly aware that the noise of the coopers had ceased, and that three or four of the men were gathered at the brewhouse door, looking on with the mingled curiosity and embarrassment of those who have overheard what was not meant for their ears. There was old Mudge the foreman, who had been in the brewery since he was a lad of twelve, and who now stood with his leathern apron in his hand, turning his cap round and round as if the secret of the universe were written in its lining; and there was Mrs. Mudge, the foreman's wife, who did the fine laundering of the town and had a tongue that went like a mangle, standing at the yard gate with a basket on her hip and her mouth open.
