The rain had not ceased for three days. It tapped against the shutters of the apartment on the Rue Vauquelin with the same relentless rhythm it had kept since Tuesday, turning the narrow street below into a slick, grey channel that reflected the dull light of the gas lamps. Inside, the apartment smelled of boiled cabbage, beeswax, and the particular mustiness that settles into rooms where the windows are rarely opened. Jadwiga Kwiatkowska sat in the chair by the window, her hands folded in her lap, listening to the clock on the mantelpiece mark the minutes she no longer counted. She had been a widow for eleven years and a mother without a son for two, and in that time she had learned that waiting was not something one did but something one became.

The knock came at half past four. She did not startle. She was past the age of startling. She rose from the chair slowly, pressing her hands against the armrests, and crossed the room with the careful, deliberate steps of a woman who did not trust her own ankles on the uneven floorboards. The hallway was dark. She opened the door.

He stood in the stairwell, dripping. He was young, perhaps twenty-five, though the hollows beneath his cheekbones made him look older. His coat was too thin for the weather and hung from his shoulders as though it belonged to a larger man. His hair, dark and plastered to his forehead, dripped onto the collar of a shirt that had once been white. He held his cap in both hands, turning it slowly, squeezing the rain from it in a gesture that seemed less nervous than habitual, the way a man might handle a thing he had carried for a long time.

"Mrs. Kwiatkowska?" he said. He spoke in Polish, but with the flattened vowels of someone who had lived among Russians.

"I am she."

"My name is Piotr Żak. I shared a cell with your son, Stanisław, in Warsaw." He swallowed. "In Mokotów. I have brought you a message from him."

She stood aside. She did not invite him in with words, but the gesture was clear enough. He entered the hallway and stood on the mat, water pooling around his boots, and she saw that he could not meet her eyes. His gaze fixed itself somewhere over her left shoulder, on the row of hooks where her late husband's coat still hung, though it no longer carried his smell and she kept it only because she could not bring herself to give it away.

"Come to the kitchen," she said. "You are wet."

She set the kettle on the stove and lit the burner with a match that trembled only slightly. He sat at the table on the chair her son had once sat in, and she noticed that he kept his hands flat on the tabletop, fingers spread, as though he were trying to press himself into the wood, to become flat, to take up less space. She had seen this before, in the faces of men who had come to Paris from the east, men who carried papers and stories and messages for the mothers and wives who had been left behind. She had received two such visitors before. One had brought a letter she later learned was forged. The other had brought nothing but a story that changed each time she asked him to repeat it.

She poured the tea. She set the cup in front of him. She sat across from him and waited.

He drank. He held the cup with both hands, and she watched the steam rise between them and thought of how her son used to hold his cup the same way, as though the warmth were a thing he was afraid of losing. She pushed the thought aside. Sentimentality was a trap, and she had fallen into it too many times.

"Tell me," she said.

Piotr set the cup down. He looked at the table. He looked at his hands. He looked at the window, where the rain ran in crooked lines down the glass. He did not look at her.

"Stanisław asked me to find you," he said. "If I got out. He said, 'Find my mother in Paris. Tell her what I could not tell her myself.'"

"And what could he not tell me?"

"That he was sorry. That he thought of you. That at the end, he was not afraid." He paused. "He said you would understand what he meant by that. He said you would know that he was not just saying it to comfort you, that he meant it. That he had made his peace."

Jadwiga folded her hands on the table. She looked at the young man's face, at the way the muscle in his jaw tightened and released, at the way his throat moved when he swallowed. She wanted to believe him. The wanting was physical, a pressure behind her sternum, a tightness in her throat that she recognized as the same feeling she had carried for two years, the feeling of a woman who has been holding her breath without knowing it.

"You shared a cell with him," she said.

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"Fourteen months. From the January arrests until the transfer in March."

"And what was he like, in the cell?"

Piotr's fingers curled slightly against the table. "He was strong," he said. "Stronger than the rest of us. He organized the reading circles. He shared his rations with the older men. He kept a journal, hidden in the lining of his coat. He wrote poems. He—" He stopped. His jaw tightened again.

"He what?" Jadwiga said.

"He talked about you," Piotr said. "About this apartment. About the kitchen. He said the light came in through the window in the afternoon and turned the walls the colour of honey. He said you kept geraniums on the sill."
