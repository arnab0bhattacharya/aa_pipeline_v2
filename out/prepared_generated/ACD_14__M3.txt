It was upon a raw, foggy morning in the latter days of November, 1894, that my friend, Mr. Bardell Edgecumbe, was persuaded to turn his singular faculties toward a problem as curious as any in his varied experience. We sat in our dingy quarters above a hatter’s shop in Whitechapel High Street, a district which provided Edgecumbe with a steady stream of the anomalous, when a sharp rat-tat sounded at the door.

Our visitor was a man of a most unmistakable calling. He was stout, wore a heavy plaid waistcoat, and carried about him that indescribable mustiness which clings to the atmosphere of a pawnbroker’s establishment. His face was pale and flurried, and he clutched a small object in his coarse, red hand.

“Mr. Edgecumbe,” said he, “I am Jacob Mowcher, of Mowcher’s Repository in Commercial Street. A most perplexing affair has come my way, and Inspector Vane, knowing your turn for the unusual, bade me seek you out.”

Edgecumbe, who had been lounging in his armchair with an expression of profound boredom, sat up abruptly, his grey eyes gleaming. “Pray be seated, Mr. Mowcher,” said he. “Lay out your puzzle upon the table, and let us have the facts in the order they occurred. You have brought the object in question, I perceive.”

The pawnbroker nodded, and, laying down his burden, placed a silver watch upon the table. It was a handsome piece, somewhat tarnished, but bearing the unmistakable hallmarks of fine craftsmanship. Edgecumbe seized it with his long, nervous fingers, and examined it through his powerful lens.

“A hunter-case watch,” he murmured. “Eighteen-carat gold, though silver-gilt upon the inner plate, a common enough economy of the wealthy. The maker’s name is Tobias, of Clerkenwell. The hinge is slightly worn, suggesting a habit of opening it with the right thumb. But what is this upon the inner case?”

He turned the watch over, and I leaned forward to look. Engraved upon the silver was a small but elaborate crest—a falcon striking, upon a wreath of laurel.

“The crest of the Harpenden family,” Edgecumbe observed quietly. “An old Suffolk house. I read the obituary of young Lord Arthur Harpenden with sorrow a little over two years ago. He was drowned in a boating accident in the Bay of Naples. Now, Mr. Mowcher, how comes this watch into your possession?”

“It was pledged yesterday evening,” said the pawnbroker, “by a ragged street boy. He came in just as I was closing, and offered it for a loan of ten shillings. I knew the crest at once, for I had once appraised the Harpenden plate, and I was struck dumb. I questioned the boy. He was a sharp-looking urchin, though his clothes were mere rags, and his hands were black with the grime of the streets. He told me that a tall gentleman, dressed in a heavy travelling ulster and a soft felt hat, had given him the watch to carry. The gentleman, he said, had engaged him to take a message to a certain address, but the boy could not read, and the address was written upon a slip of paper which he had lost. The watch, the gentleman said, was a token of his good faith, and he promised the boy a further half-crown upon the successful delivery of the message. The boy, finding himself hungry and the gentleman gone, came to me instead.”

Edgecumbe smiled. “A pretty tangle,” said he. “And where is the boy now?”

“I left him in the care of my assistant below. I did not dare let him go.”

“Excellent. I must see him at once. But first—” Edgecumbe continued his scrutiny of the watch. “You say this watch was given to the boy by a tall gentleman in an ulster. Did the boy describe him further?”

“He said he had a heavy, brown beard, and that he walked with a limp.”

“With a limp, eh? And a brown beard! Come, Watson—pardon me, Dr. Thornton—we may begin to weave our web. Let us have the boy up.”

The urchin was accordingly brought before us. He was a bright-eyed, intelligent lad of perhaps twelve, with all the cockney assurance of his station, though somewhat cowed by his surroundings.

“Now, my boy,” said Edgecumbe, in his kindly, reassuring manner, “do not be frightened. Tell me exactly what happened. Where did you meet this gentleman?”

The boy scratched his head. “At the corner of Thrawl Street, sir. I was selling matches, and he comes up to me, quick and quiet-like. ‘Boy,’ says he, ‘I want you to carry a message. It is of the utmost importance, and I will pay you well.’ He presses the watch into my hand, and says, ‘Take this as security for your honesty. Deliver the message to the address on this paper, and I will meet you there and pay you your due.’ I was to go to a house, he said, but I lost the paper out of me pocket in the crowd at the corner of the Whitechapel Road. When I went back to look for him, he was gone, and I was mortal hungry, so I popped the ticker.”

“You lost the paper,” Edgecumbe repeated, his eyes narrowing. “Are you quite sure of that?”

“Yes, sir, certain sure.”

“Did you notice anything about the gentleman’s hands?”

The boy looked puzzled. “His hands, sir? Why, he wore thick woollen gloves, and one of them—the right one—was torn at the tip, and I could see he had a bandage on his finger.”

Edgecumbe rose and tossed me the watch. “Thornton, observe the bow of the pendant,” said he. “You will perceive a faint but distinct mark. It is the impression of a small metal ring, such as is used to suspend a watch from a chatelaine or a fob chain. The watch has been worn upon the person of a woman. Now, Mr. Mowcher, you are certain of the Harpenden crest?”

“As certain as I am of my own name,” the pawnbroker replied.
