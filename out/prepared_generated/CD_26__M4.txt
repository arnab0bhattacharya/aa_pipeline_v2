Mrs. Tabitha Crane, relict of the late Mr. Jeremiah Crane, pawnbroker, of Cinnamon Court, Somers Town, sat in the back parlour of her establishment with her foot upon a hassock and her mind upon a grievance. The foot was bandaged, swollen, and of a purple so decided that it might have been dyed to match the curtains; the grievance was of longer standing, and had to do with the general ingratitude of a world which she had spent forty years in charging a shilling in the pound for. She had fallen—slipped upon a piece of orange-peel at her own doorstep, an accident which she considered a personal affront on the part of Providence—and had been carried, swearing softly and genteelly, into the little room behind the shop, where the three golden balls hung in the fog outside like three moons in a bottle of porter.

It was a room in which everything was nailed down and nothing was given away. The clock had been pledged and never redeemed, and told the hour with a certain sulky reluctance, as though it knew it was not paid for. The chairs were of the sort that pinch, the fire was of the sort that smokes, and about the whole apartment hung that peculiar odour, half leather, half old silver, half despair, which is the atmosphere of pawnbrokers’ parlours all the world over. Mrs. Crane, in her chair of state, with a shawl about her shoulders and a black bonnet upon her head—for she would have considered it indecent to be found unbonneted, even by a doctor—received the condolences of the Court with a countenance in which pain and triumph were curiously mingled, and spoke of her injury as a judgment upon somebody, though upon whom she left delicately uncertain.

Now, it is a remarkable circumstance in the affairs of this life, and one which Mrs. Crane had never before had occasion to remark, that the man whom she had most flatly refused, most publicly insulted, and most profitably forgotten, was the very man who came to her in her affliction. Mr. Simon Pottle, the bird-fancier at the corner—a small, round, grey, bustling man, with a face like a friendly potato, and a shop full of canaries that sang louder than the church bells and cost less to keep—had come to Mrs. Crane, some three years before, to borrow fifteen pounds upon a stock of cages and a lease nearly expired. Mrs. Crane had looked at him over her spectacles as she might have looked at a bad sixpence, had told him that she lent no money to men who kept singing-birds, had observed that birds were a luxury and luxuries were for the quality, and had sent him out of her shop with a phrase so neat and so cutting that it had been quoted in the Court for a twelve-month afterwards. Mr. Pottle had gone home, had sold his watch, had borrowed of his brother-in-law, had weathered the storm, and had prospered; and the first use he made of his prosperity was to come round to Cinnamon Court with a covered basin of beef-tea, and a face of such unaffected kindness that Mrs. Crane could have struck him with her crutch.

“I have brought you a little strengthening broth, ma’am,” said Mr. Pottle, setting it down upon the table with a gentle clatter, “and I have brought you likewise a couple of my birds, that you may have the singing of them while you are laid by; for I have always found, ma’am, that a canary is a very cheerful companion, and asks no interest for his company.”

Mrs. Crane regarded him with a dark and suspicious eye. “I want no birds,” said she, “and I want no broth. I want my foot to be let alone, and my character to be respected. I am not a pauper, Mr. Pottle, nor yet a person to be fed with a spoon by the neighbours.”

“No, ma’am,” returned Mr. Pottle, cheerfully, “you are not; and if you were, ma’am, there’s not a person in Somers Town would be more ready to do it than I. But it’s only broth, ma’am, and it’s only birds; and there’s no shame in either of ’em, that I know of.”

And with that he took up the poker and mended the fire, which was smoking in the sulky manner of the clock, and went out, leaving the broth behind him. Mrs. Crane, after a decent interval of indignation, drank the broth. It was very good broth. She was so angry with it for being good that she drank every drop, and then sat looking at the empty basin as though it had insulted her.

The days that followed were days of trial. Mr. Pottle came every morning with his basin and his cheerful face, and every morning Mrs. Crane received him with a fresh accession of severity. She reminded him, in a voice trembling with gout and dignity, that she had refused him fifteen pounds; she dwelt upon the circumstance with an emphasis which would have been cruel in a less injured woman; she told him that she knew very well why he came—that he came to crow over her, to see the pawnbroker’s widow brought low, to have his revenge in broth. And Mr. Pottle, who had no revenge in him at all, and whose only desire was that the poor lady should not starve in her own back parlour, would reply mildly that he had forgotten all about the fifteen pounds, and that the broth was nothing, and that she must not talk so, for it would do her no good, and then would go and feed the canaries in the shop, and whistle to them, and the canaries would answer him, and the whole Court would ring with the sound of it, and Mrs. Crane, lying alone with her foot upon the hassock, would feel the singing in her bosom like a reproach.
