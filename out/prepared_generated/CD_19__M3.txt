If any one of the small, ragged, and deeply-soiled fraternity of boys who dwelt beneath the great brick viaduct of the London and Greenwich Railway in the year of grace 1843, had been asked by a benevolent philosopher what capital he had laid by for a rainy day, he would have answered, with a sniff and a scratch of his woolly head, that he had a sharp eye, a quick hand, and a hollow arch to keep the wet from the sharp eye and the quick hand. It was a neighbourhood of mud and mystery, of tangled iron and dripping soot, where the great trains overhead went roaring and thundering like iron giants in a perpetual fury, shaking the very bottles on the dusty shelves of old Mother Mottle’s gin-shop across the street. Underneath these arches, where the gas-lamps of the main road cast long, yellow, and exceedingly devious fingers into the damp gloom, the boys slept in hollows of brick-rubble, wrapped in such scanty garments as the charity of the surrounding dust-heaps had bestowed upon them.

They were governed by a boy of fourteen, known to the neighbourhood and the beadle as the Artful Skip; a name bestowed upon him for his singular dexterity in vanishing from the grasp of authority. The Skip had a theory of life, which was, that the world owed him a living, and that he was the appointed collector of the debt. He spoke with a lisp, which he considered highly genteel, and wore a battered beaver hat, which he considered highly imposing. "The thircumstance of being born," the Skip was accustomed to observe, "is a mithtake that requires a deal of eating to put right."

It was upon a raw and dripping Tuesday evening, when the fog crept in from the river like a thief, that the gang found the little girl. She was sitting on a discarded paving-stone in the third arch, a mere atom of humanity, gazing with a stony, vacant wonder at the great black pipes above her head. She was very small, very pale, and very dirty; but she had a pair of great brown eyes, which seemed to hold the whole of the melancholy fog and a great deal of unshed tears within their depths.

The gang surrounded her, and the Skip, planting his hands on his knees, bent down to inspect her.

"What'th your name, thmall one?" demanded the Skip.

The little girl looked at him, and her lips trembled, but she gave no answer.

"Ain't thhe got a tongue?" demanded a boy of twelve, known as Squeezer, from his habit of extracting the last drop of moisture from a stolen orange.

"She’th got a tongue, thure enough," said the Skip, reflectively scratching his chin. "But it don't theeem to be on thpeaking termth with her memory. Where do you live, thmall one?"

The child only shivered and crept closer to the cold brickwork.

"She'th a lotht one," pronounced the Skip, with judicial gravity. "The ith lotht, and her name ith gone after the retht of her. We mutht call her the Lotht one."

So the Lost One she became, and the gang adopted her into their society. They were thieves and beggars, every man Jack of them, as hardened a set of young reprobates as ever snatched a pastry from a cook’s window; yet they took this small, silent burden upon their shoulders with a tenderness that would have astonished the beadle. The Skip, with an air of profound sagacity, appointed himself her guardian. Squeezer, who had a weakness for sweet-eels, brought her half of a stolen one, wiping the grease carefully on his sleeve before presenting it to her. A third boy, known as the Angel, because of his unmitigated depravity, gave her his only waistcoat—a garment so full of holes that it was more like a fishing-net than a vest—to keep her warm.

They fed her by begging, when begging was prosperous, and by theft when it was not. The Skip would take her by the hand, and, assuming an air of virtuous melancholy, would parade her before the doors of the worthy inhabitants of Bermondsey. "Pray, thur," the Skip would say, lifting his battered hat, "behold my thithter! The dithtreth of a motherleth family!" And the worthy inhabitants, deceived by the child's great brown eyes, would bestow halfpence, which the Skip would carefully divide, giving the largest portion to the Lost One.

But the winter advanced, and the fog thickened, and the trains roared louder, and the cold crept into the very marrow of the arches. The Lost One grew thinner, and her cough, which had begun as a mere whisper, became a hacking, tearing sound that echoed in the hollow arches like the cough of a clockwork skeleton. The Skip, listening to it in the night, would bury his head in his brick-rubble pillow, and whisper, with a strange moisture in his sharp eyes, "Don't cough, thmall one, don't cough. It maketh a hole in the night."

It was upon a Saturday, when the frost was sharp and the gas-lamps burned with a frosty halo, that a new and alarming element entered into the life of the gang. The Angel, returning from a reconnaissance in the main road, brought news of a well-dressed lady.

"She’th a-asking," said the Angel, breathless and excited, "she’th a-asking at every shop and every corner, about a lotht child!"

"Lotht child?" said the Skip, sharply. "What lotht child?"

"A little girl," said the Angel. "With great brown eyeth. The thays she dithappeared from a carriage in the Thtrand. The thays there ith a reward."

A reward! The word ran through the gang like a flame through straw. A reward meant bread, and meat, and warmth. A reward meant a whole winter's comfort. The boys looked at each other, and then at the Skip. The Skip stood in the centre of the group, his hands in his pockets, his battered hat on the back of his head, and a look of dark and portentous thought upon his face.
