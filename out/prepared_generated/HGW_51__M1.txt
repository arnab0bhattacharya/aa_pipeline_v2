My dear Father,

I write to you from a place so different from the high, clean air of Khurum that I feel I must be reporting from another planet, or perhaps from the interior of some vast and terrible machine. You warned me that England was a land of many people and great industry, but nothing in your sermons or our geographies prepared me for the reality. The noise is the first and most terrible thing. It is not the sound of a few men shouting or a river falling, but a constant, grinding, roaring din, as though the whole earth were being chewed by some great iron jaw. When I stepped from the train—a monstrous iron worm that vomits steam and screams upon its arrival—I was struck by a wave of sound and stench so overwhelming that I nearly fainted. The air, thick with the breath of a million coal-fires, is a perpetual yellow fog, and the sky is the colour of a dull pewter dish.

These Englishmen are a strange people. They move with great purpose and haste, yet they seem to see nothing. They rush through their streets as though pursued by demons, their eyes fixed upon some private destination, oblivious to the monstrous contraptions that thunder past them. I have seen omnibuses, great double-decked houses on wheels, packed with human cargo, pulled by a team of horses whose eyes are wide with the terror of their labour. And yet, the men and women inside sit as placidly as if they were in their own parlours. They have grown so accustomed to their cages that they no longer see the bars. I saw a man yesterday, standing on a street corner, calmly eating a sausage from a piece of paper while a great engine of war, a black cannon on wheels, rumbled past not ten feet from him. He did not even turn his head. What manner of stoicism is this? Or is it, as I suspect, a kind of madness that comes from living in such a place?

I am now lodged with a Mr. Abernathy, a friend of the Missionary Society, in a house in a part of the city called Bloomsbury. The house is a marvel of strange customs. We eat our meals at fixed hours, with a great number of implements—forks with two, three, and four prongs, knives of varying shapes, spoons for soup and spoons for sweets—and every man and woman must know the precise use for each. To use the wrong one is a crime more heinous than theft. They do not eat with their hands, which seems to me a great loss of pleasure and intimacy with one's food. And they speak a language of elaborate indirectness. When Mr. Abernathy wishes to know if I am cold, he says, "The fire seems to have dwindled somewhat, has it not?" instead of asking me plainly. It is as though they are afraid of their own meanings.

Yet, for all their strangeness, I cannot help but be fascinated by their contraptions. Yesterday, Mr. Abernathy took me to see a great exhibition of electrical science. There, in a vast hall, they had captured lightning in glass tubes. Men with wild hair and earnest faces demonstrated how a current could make a bell ring, or cause a metal filament to glow with the light of a captive star. One fellow, a Mr. Tesla's disciple, showed us a chair from which lightning crackled in blue bolts to the hand of a brave volunteer. The crowd of savages—these very same Englishmen who walk unseeing through their streets—gaped and gasped like tribesmen at a shaman's trick. They do not understand the principles, you see. They only worship the power. They have traded the spirits of the river and the mountain for the spirits of the wire and the dynamo, and I do not think they are any wiser for it.

The children here are another puzzle. In Khurum, the children run wild and free, learning the ways of the world from the world itself. Here, they are dressed in stiff, uncomfortable clothes and sent to vast buildings called schools, where they are taught to sit still for hours and memorise facts from books. They are like young animals being trained for a circus. They learn the names of kings and the dates of battles, but they do not learn which berries are poisonous or how to read the weather in the clouds. They are being filled with knowledge, but emptied of wisdom. I saw a little boy crying in a park because his kite, a beautiful thing of paper and wood, had flown into a tree. His nursemaid scolded him for getting his shoes muddy, showing no pity for his lost toy. In Khurum, we would have climbed the tree. Here, they punish the boy for the laws of gravity and the dampness of the earth.

I find myself thinking of our valley with a longing that is almost a physical pain. I dream of the silence, broken only by the cry of a hawk or the rush of the wind over the grass. I dream of the faces of the people, direct and open, and of the simple, honest work of the day. This London is a great and terrible wonder, a testament to what man can build when he forgets the sun and the soil. But it is a cold, hard, noisy place, and its people, for all their cleverness with wires and wheels, seem to me more savage in their hearts than any tribe I have ever read of. They have conquered nature, and in doing so, they have conquered themselves.

Your dutiful son,
Elias Thorne
