The sky over old England was the color of a bruised plum, and Kael walked beneath it as she had walked for seventy-three days, her boots leaving prints in the dust that no one would ever see. The road she followed was cracked and pale, a dead vein running south through a landscape that had forgotten the hand of man. In the fields beside her, abandoned orchards grew wild, their bitter apples falling to rot in the tall grass. Once, her brother had told her, these roads were so full of cars that a person could not cross them. Now the only traffic was the wind, and the occasional fox that watched her pass with yellow, unconcerned eyes. She pulled her coat tighter. In her pack she carried four days' worth of dried food, a water filter, and a map drawn on cloth that was wearing thin at the folds. She carried, too, the weight of a year's silence, which was heavier than all the rest.

She had last heard from Tommen in a voice message, scratchy and brief, transmitted over the fading radio network. He was in the south, he had said, near the ruins of the old power station at Callow Hill, where people were trying to build a generator from salvaged parts. He had sounded tired but hopeful. Then nothing. The network had failed entirely that winter, and the letters she had entrusted to passing traders had never brought a reply. She had waited through a spring that never warmed, watching the lane that led to their cottage in the hope of seeing his tall figure walking home, until waiting had become a kind of slow death, and she had decided that she would rather walk and know.

In the late afternoon of the seventy-fourth day, she came upon a bridge over a river that the map called the Stillwater, though the water was anything but still, running brown and fast over a bed of broken concrete. On the bridge sat a man with his legs hanging over the edge, fishing with a line made from braided wire. He was perhaps fifty, with a face weathered to the texture of old bark, and when he saw her he raised a hand in a gesture of greeting that seemed to carry no surprise, as though he had been sitting there for years simply waiting for someone to wave at. She approached and saw that his line was tied to a stick, and that beside him on the stones lay three small fish, silver and staring. "You're the first person I've seen in nine days," he said. "I'm Edric." She told him her name, and he offered her a fish, and because the rules of the road demanded exchange, she gave him two of her precious matches. He cooked the fish on a flat stone over a small fire, and they ate in a silence that was not unfriendly, looking out over the darkening water.

Edric had been a teacher once, in a town called Merrow, before the second wave of the sickness had taken the children. He spoke of this plainly, without drama, as a man speaks of something he has carried so long it has become part of his posture. His wife had died in the first wave, and his daughter in the third, and now he fished and walked and sometimes met people on the road and fed them fish, and this seemed to be enough. "I don't look for anything anymore," he said, banking the fire with his boot. "I just keep moving. It's when you stop that the quiet gets in." Kael nodded, because she understood the quiet he meant. It was the quiet of a house with one too few voices, the quiet of a morning when you set out two cups out of habit and then stand very still, looking at the second cup, unable to put it away.

She left Edric in the morning, and he gave her a fish wrapped in dock leaves for the road, and she thought of him for many miles afterward, a solitary figure on a broken bridge, casting his line into brown water. The road led her through a dead village where ivy had pulled down the walls of the church and saplings grew through the floor of the pub. In what had been a schoolyard, she found a swing still hanging from a rusted frame, and she pushed it and watched it sway, back and forth, the chains groaning, until it stopped.

Three days later, in a valley choked with fog, she met the woman and the boy. They were sitting beside a campfire at the edge of the road, and the woman was singing softly, a song Kael did not recognise, in a language that might have been old Welsh. The boy was perhaps eight, thin and pale, wrapped in a blanket too large for him, and he watched Kael approach with large dark eyes that held no fear, only a weary curiosity. The woman, who introduced herself as Maren, offered tea made from pine needles, and Kael sat with them and drank the bitter, warming liquid and felt, for a moment, something almost like peace. Maren was travelling to the coast, she said, where she had heard there were boats and people building a life around fishing and trade. The boy was not hers, she explained. She had found him alone in a house in a town called Thriston, sitting beside a woman who had been dead for several days, and she had taken him because there was nothing else to do. "He doesn't speak," Maren said, and the boy looked at Kael and then looked away, and she saw in his face the same expression she had seen in her own mirror during that long, silent spring: the face of someone who has been left behind.
