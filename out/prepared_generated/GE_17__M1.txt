The history of a mind is often a history of its silences, and in the small cathedral town of Ketherminster, the silence of Miss Priscilla Wynter had become, for many years, a kind of civic institution. It was not the silence of a vacant or timid nature, but a profound and deliberate quietude, a medium through which the various currents of provincial life flowed, depositing their secrets in its still depths. From her handsome but somewhat faded house in the Minster Close, Miss Wynter had for two decades presided over the town’s private economy with a discretion so complete that it was mistaken for simple generosity. She lent money. Not in any formal, documented fashion, but with a quiet, unobtrusive kindness that seemed less a transaction than a natural dispensation, like the falling of rain upon the thirsty fields. To be in her debt was to be in a state of mild, comfortable obligation, a debt that one felt one could almost forget, so little was it ever pressed. She was, in the common estimation, a woman of independent fortune, and this perception was the very bedrock of her influence. It was not until the winter of 1837 that this bedrock was discovered to be built upon a most perilous sand.

The news, when it broke, did not arrive as a thunderclap but as a slow, creeping frost. It began with the failure of the great railway speculation, a venture into which Miss Wynter’s entire capital, it was rumoured, had been unwisely but trustingly poured. Mr. Finch, the solicitor, whose face was a professional mask of regret, confirmed the worst. Miss Wynter was not merely embarrassed; she was, to use the dreadful, unambiguous term, ruined. The house in the Close must be sold, its contents dispersed. The quiet, anchoring presence that had been a fixture of their lives was to be uprooted, and in its place was to be a void, and with it, a strange and unlooked-for liberation. For the debtors, the news brought a complex and disquieting sensation, a mixture of pity for the lady and a guilty, exhilarating relief. The silent pressure was gone. The obligation, which had never been spoken of, could now be allowed to evaporate into the general misfortune of the times.

The matter was first debated in earnest at the Wheatsheaf Inn, where Mr. Abel Pardoe, a master wheelwright with a reputation for shrewdness, held court. “A sad business for the old lady,” he began, wiping ale from his moustache. “But a man must look to his own family first. My little loan was a gentleman’s agreement, you might say. Nothing on paper. Now that she’s nothing to her name, well, the slate’s wiped clean, isn’t it? It’s not as if I were a common tradesman with a bill to be settled.” He looked around for assent, and found it in the nodding heads of several farmers and lesser men, who felt the lifting of a weight they had not fully acknowledged. Their obligation had been a sentiment, and with Miss Wynter’s fortune gone, the sentiment could be honourably retired.

But another voice, older and rougher, spoke up from the corner. It was old Gammer Slaney, who sold herbs and country simples from a basket, and to whom Miss Wynter had once advanced the money for a new roof after a storm. “A gentleman’s agreement, is it, Mr. Pardoe?” she cackled, her eyes bright and sharp as flint. “Aye, and a poor woman’s blessing, that’s what it was. She never asked for it back, not when my boy was sick, nor when the harvest failed. ‘When you can, Martha,’ she’d say. Well, I can. And a debt’s a debt, whether it be wrote down in a book or writ in the heart. Her being poor now don’t make me rich, nor does it make me honest to keep what I owe.” Her speech, delivered in the broad, unvarnished dialect of the surrounding farms, fell into a sudden silence. It was not that she had persuaded them, but that she had given a name to the faint, persistent ache of conscience that a few of them had been trying to soothe with Mr. Pardoe’s logic.

The question was thus thrown open, a quiet agitation that ran beneath the surface of the town’s affairs. It was a test not of solvency, but of character. To repay was to acknowledge a moral truth that existed independently of one’s own convenience; to withhold was to accept a worldly logic that was, in its own way, perfectly defensible. The town began to divide itself, not by wealth or creed, but by this new, invisible line. Miss Wynter, meanwhile, remained in her house, packing her books and linens with the same methodical quietude that had governed her lending. She received visitors with a calm dignity that offered no hint of her own state of mind, asking after their children and their health as if she were not the subject of their pity and their debate. It was this very composure that made her case the more poignant; she did not plead, and in not pleading, she seemed to lay a far greater claim upon the honour of her debtors. The true measure of a debt, it became clear to some, is not the creditor’s power to collect, but the debtor’s freedom to forget. And in Ketherminster, that freedom was proving to be a far more burdensome thing than anyone had supposed.
