In the fetid and labyrinthine heart of Seven Dials, where the very bricks seemed to sweat a grimy perspiration and the air was a thick compound of cabbage-water, soot, and forgotten hopes, there stood a court of singular disrepute known as Cinder-Spinney. It was a place where the houses leaned together like drunken cronies swapping sordid secrets, their cracked and grimy windows staring at one another in a kind of hopeless intimacy. In this court, in a room that was more a cupboard than a chamber, lived Mrs. Polly Tattle, a widow of five children and a washerwoman by trade, whose hands were as red and chapped as a winter apple, and whose heart was, by some strange and unaccountable perversity of nature, as soft as a dumpling.

Her trade was a hard one, and her family a hungry one. The five little Tattles—Mick, Sal, the twins Bob and Ben, and the baby, who was called Trot because he had a way of moving that suggested a little, unsteady cart—were a perpetual and clamorous demand upon her energies. Their diet was chiefly potatoes and the scent of other people’s dinners, and their raiment was a sort of animated patchwork, a walking museum of the cast-off clothing of the parish. Yet Mrs. Tattle, though she scrubbed for the quality and the would-be quality of the neighbouring streets, and came home each night with her arms steaming and her back like a bent hoop, never failed to find a morsel for a stray cat or a copper for a blind beggar.

It was upon a Tuesday, a day of drizzle and general despondency, that she returned from a particularly trying engagement with the linen of a certain Major Pipkin, a gentleman of such precise and punctilious habits that he insisted his shirts be ironed with a smoothness that bordered on the miraculous. Her arms ached, her head throbbed, and her soul was heavy within her. She let herself into her single room, which served as parlour, kitchen, and dormitory, and was about to collapse upon the one rickety chair the establishment possessed, when her eye was caught by a circumstance of the most singular nature.

Her copper, a great, black, soot-encrusted vessel that stood in the corner like a squat and brooding idol, was the centre of her domestic economy. In it she boiled the clothes of half the parish, and in it, on occasion, she boiled the family's potatoes. Now, as she looked, she perceived a pair of small, bare feet protruding from its open top, and from within its cavernous depths came a sound that was not the bubbling of water or the hiss of steam, but a low, regular, and pathetic snore.

Mrs. Tattle, a woman of prompt decision in a crisis, seized the poker and advanced upon the copper. “Halloa, there!” she cried, in a voice that was meant to be stern but trembled with alarm. “Come out of my copper, whoever you are! It’s not a hotel for the quality, nor yet a lodging-house for the gentry!”

The snoring ceased. There was a stirring within, a scrambling, and then a face appeared over the rim of the copper—the face of a boy, so thin and pale that it seemed a mere sketch of a face, a face that had been drawn with a trembling hand and then half-rubbed out by the cruel sponge of poverty. His eyes, which were large and of a startling, luminous blue, were wide with terror. He was all bones and rags, a little scarecrow of a boy, and he looked at Mrs. Tattle as a mouse might look at an owl.

“Please, mum,” he whispered, his voice a dry rustle, “I was only sleeping. I didn’t mean no harm. I was that cold.”

The poker fell from Mrs. Tattle’s hand with a clatter. The sternness in her face melted away as if it had been a lump of wax before a fire, and was replaced by a look of such profound and tender pity that it would have done credit to a Madonna. “Cold!” she echoed. “And hungry, I’ll be bound. When did you last eat, you poor little sparrow?”

The boy’s answer was a silent, eloquent, and utterly devastating look of longing. Mrs. Tattle needed no further explanation. In a trice she had him out of the copper, had wrapped him in a threadbare blanket, and had set before him the better part of a loaf and a cup of small beer. The boy fell upon the food with a ferocity that was terrible to behold, and Mrs. Tattle, watching him, felt her heart swell with a great and foolish tenderness.

His name, he said between mouthfuls, was Nim. He had run away from the workhouse of St. Lazarus-in-the-Fields, where the master was a man of flint and the gruel was a watery ghost. For three days he had wandered the streets, sleeping in doorways and under stalls, until, drawn by the warmth of Mrs. Tattle’s fire, he had climbed into the copper as into a nest.

“And a fine nest it is, my dear,” said Mrs. Tattle, her eyes glistening, “and you shall have it for as long as you like. You shall be my nephew, come all the way from the country. My poor sister’s boy, orphaned and left alone in the world. That’s what you shall be, Nim, for I’ve a great fancy to have a nephew, and you’re as like my poor sister’s child as ever was.”

And so it was settled. Nim became, by a fiction as bold as it was charitable, a member of the Tattle household. The five little Tattles, after a brief and noisy council of war, accepted their new cousin with the stoicism of children who are accustomed to the unexpected. He shared their potatoes, their rags, and their bed, and he repaid them with a quiet and watchful gratitude that was more eloquent than any words.
