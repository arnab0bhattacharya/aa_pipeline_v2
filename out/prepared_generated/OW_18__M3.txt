In the tall white tower of Aurelia the young Prince Lucien had spent his eighteen years, guarded from the world's roughness by walls of marble and by tutors whose wisdom was as carefully selected as the fruits that adorned his porcelain dishes. The tower stood in a garden of silver fountains and black cypress trees, where peacocks walked with a solemnity befitting their jewelled throats, and where no wind that had not been first examined by the head gardener was suffered to stir the petals of the damask roses. For the King, his father, held that sorrow, being an ugly thing, was unfit for the contemplation of the young, and that beauty, being the only truth worth knowing, should be served exclusively.

On the morning of Lucien's eighteenth birthday, the King came to him in his chamber, which was hung with tapestries depicting the hunts of gods and the languors of nymphs, and said, 'My son, you have been preserved from the sordidness of the common world as a jewel is preserved from tarnish. Yet now you are of age, and I will grant you one request, whatsoever your heart desires. Ask for a kingdom beyond our borders, and I will fit out an armada. Ask for a bride, and I will send ambassadors to the forty courts of the earth. Ask for wisdom, and I will summon the philosophers of the silver mountains.'

Lucien, who had been gazing from his window at the precise and beautiful geometry of the gardens below, turned and said, 'I would like to see a poor man.'

The King started, as a man starts when he is told something he ought to have known but has somehow neglected to learn. 'A poor man?' he repeated. 'What an extraordinary request. I myself have never seen one. They are, I am told, unpleasant to look upon. They lack the raiment that gives dignity to the body, and they are afflicted with expressions of grievance that would distress your temperament, which has been kept as delicate as a lily of the valley.'

'Nevertheless,' said the Prince, 'I would see one. For the tutors speak of them in the histories they read to me, and they seem to occupy a great deal of the world's attention. If they are so numerous, surely one may be found.'

The King sighed, for he had hoped his son would ask for something reasonable, such as a sceptre of ivory or a horse with a mane of spun gold. 'It shall be done,' he said, and he descended from the tower and summoned his ministers.

Now the ministers of Aurelia were men of considerable refinement, who wore velvet the colour of pomegranates and carried staves of ebony inlaid with lapis. They had been appointed to their offices on the strength of their complexions and the elegance with which they poured wine, and they were entirely unacquainted with the lower quarters of the city, which lay beyond the ivory gates and the avenues of flowering lime. It was said among them that the lower quarters existed only in rumour, propagated by pessimistic poets.

The King commanded them to search the city and bring back a poor man, that the Prince might look upon him and be satisfied. So the ministers put on cloaks of grey silk, which they imagined to be the garb of the common people, and went down into the streets.

They walked through the merchants' quarter, where men of substance sat in their counting-houses amid coffers of spice and bales of silk, and they saw no one who could be called poor, for even the apprentices wore linen of a decent whiteness. They crossed the quarter of the armourers, where the sound of hammers was like the persistent chiming of brazen bells, and there they saw men with soot upon their hands and faces, but the ministers agreed that soot was merely a occupational consequence and not a true index of destitution. They passed through the market of the cloth-sellers, where women bargained over woollens and cottons with a vivacity that the ministers found disturbing but not indigent.

At last they came to a quarter where the houses leaned against one another as if for support, and where the gutters ran with water the colour of weak tea. Here the ministers halted, for the smell was not what they were accustomed to, and the light, filtered through ragged awnings, took on an indecent brownish hue. They peered into a doorway and saw, sitting on the bare stones, a man who was mending a basket. His garment was of a brown so faded that it seemed to have forgotten what colour it had originally been, and his hands were knotted with labour. His face was lined, as a field is lined with furrows, yet his eyes, when he raised them, held a quietness that the ministers found unsettling, for it was not the quietness of submission but of something else they could not name.

'Here is a poor man,' said the chief minister, with the air of a naturalist who has discovered a specimen. 'See how he lacks velvet and silver. See how he sits upon the ground as if chairs were unknown to him. He is undoubtedly the thing we seek.'

They addressed the man and told him that the Prince desired his presence in the tower. The man looked at them and said, 'What does a Prince want with a poor man?' And the chief minister replied, 'That is not for you to question. You are to come.' So the man rose and followed them, and as he walked through the streets the people of the lower quarter watched, for they had never seen men of such evident magnificence in their quarter before, and they marvelled that their neighbour should be escorted by them.

When they came to the tower, the ministers presented the man to the King, who examined him with the detachment of a connoisseur inspecting a piece of pottery. 'He will do,' said the King. 'Take him to the Prince.'
