The Misses Ashforde were reckoned, by their mother, to possess between them every accomplishment that could reasonably be expected in a family of moderate fortune and immoderate ambition; and if the world did not always agree with Mrs. Ashforde, the world was not, in her opinion, the best judge of such matters. The elder daughter, Harriet, was two-and-twenty, with a countenance rather too sensible to be fashionable and a tongue rather too honest to be agreeable. The younger, Louisa, was but nineteen, and had the good luck to be thought pretty by everyone who had not the leisure to observe her closely, and to be thought charming by everyone who had.

It was upon a Tuesday in September, when the garden at Beckworth Lodge was at its most forgiving and the weather at its most obliging, that Mr. Edmund Wexcombe came to dinner, and with him the whole business of Louisa’s future. Mr. Wexcombe was a young man of good family and better opinion of himself, who had read a great deal at Cambridge and had not yet discovered that reading and understanding are not the same thing. He was apt to say, in company, that he trusted he was not easily imposed upon; that he had made the human character his study; and that he flattered himself he could see through a design as well as most men. He said this so often and with such gravity that his sister, Mrs. Delacourt, who was older than he by six years and had long since ceased to be amused by him, had formed the habit of looking out of the window whenever he began.

“You will find Miss Louisa Ashforde a most agreeable creature,” said Mrs. Ashforde, as she handed him into the drawing-room. “She has read everything you have read, and admired it before you had the chance to recommend it.”

Mr. Wexcombe bowed, and Louisa curtsied, and within a quarter of an hour he had discovered in her a mind of uncommon penetration. She had not, it is true, read the books he spoke of; but she had read the reviews of them, which is often more serviceable in conversation, and she had the art of agreeing with a gentleman so warmly that agreement seemed to become her own original thought. When he observed that the modern novel was a frivolous species of composition, she confessed she had always felt it so. When he allowed that Richardson, whatever his merits, was tedious, she wondered that anyone had the patience. When he pronounced that a woman’s understanding was best shown in her silence, she blushed and said nothing for a full minute, which he found the most eloquent thing she had said all evening.

“She is a very sensible girl,” said Mr. Wexcombe to his sister, as they drove home. “A very sensible girl, and no affectation.”

“She is very assiduous,” said Mrs. Delacourt.

“Assiduous! You do her injustice. There is no art in her. She speaks from the heart.”

“I dare say she does. But I would not swear the heart is her own.”

Mr. Wexcombe laughed at this, as a man laughs who believes the joke to be his own, and said that his sister had grown cynical in the country. Mrs. Delacourt let it pass. She had learned, in the course of a long widowhood, that there are arguments which only confirm the person you wish to convince, and that a brother in love is the most confirmed of all.

The acquaintance prospered. Mr. Wexcombe rode over to Beckworth Lodge three times in the first week, and four times in the second, and by the third week he had begun to speak of Louisa’s taste in music, her delicacy of feeling, and the remarkable felicity with which she entered into his opinions on every subject. Mrs. Delacourt, who had been invited to accompany him, went once, and observed a great deal. She observed that Miss Louisa never expressed an opinion until her brother had expressed his, and then expressed it better; that she laughed at his jokes a moment before the point arrived, as if she had anticipated it, which is the most flattering laugh of all; that she praised his horses, his watch, his taste in waistcoats, and even his manner of carving a joint, with a warmth that no joint ever merited. She observed, too, that when Mr. Wexcombe was out of the room, Louisa’s face settled into something very like calculation, and that she asked a great many questions about his estate in Norfolk, and whether the house was large, and whether his mother lived there, and whether she was of a sociable temper.

“You are very quiet, Susan,” said Mr. Wexcombe, as they drove home. “Have you nothing to say to Miss Ashforde?”

“A great deal,” said Mrs. Delacourt. “But I doubt you would like to hear it.”

“You are prejudiced. You formed your opinion before you saw her.”

“I formed it while I saw her, which is the better method.”

He frowned. “I wish you would not be so determined to find fault. She is the most open creature in the world.”

“She is open enough,” said Mrs. Delacourt, “to let me see the bottom of her.”

There was a silence. Mr. Wexcombe whipped the horse, though the horse had done nothing to deserve it, and said that he had not expected his own sister to be the enemy of his happiness. Mrs. Delacourt said nothing, and they drove the rest of the way in a temper on his side and a meditation on hers.

She considered the matter that night, and for several nights after. She was fond of her brother—fonder, perhaps, than she was willing to own to a man who had once told her that her understanding was masculine—and she was not insensible to the danger of a marriage contracted in the heat of self-conceit. Yet she knew him well enough to know that a warning would be received as an insult.
