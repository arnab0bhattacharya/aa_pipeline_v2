The dining-room of Mr. Henry Leveson’s house in Bolton Row was filled with that subdued, amber light which a profusion of wax candles and dark mahogany diffuses, creating a pleasant dusk in the upper regions of the lofty room, where the shadows of the wine-coolers loomed like peaceful monsters. It was an atmosphere in which the minor irritations of existence might be supposed to vanish, and where conversation, if not brilliant, was at least guaranteed to be weighty. Among the guests seated around the long table was Mrs. Eleanor Meredith, a woman of seven-and-twenty with clear grey eyes and a certain firm composure of mouth which was apt to deepen into gravity. She was known to the assembled company as the sister of the reclusive Mr. Arthur Meredith, whose novel, "The Weary Watch," had been the most discussed book of the season. Mr. Leveson, a critic of considerable authority, had been its chief eulogist in the *Quarterly*, and it was at his particular request that Mrs. Meredith had come to represent her absent brother.

She sat midway down the table, between the elderly jurist Sir James Hartop and a young rising poet named Mr. Vance. Her mind was not entirely on her immediate neighbours, for she was engaged in that quiet, internal combat which is the peculiar burden of concealed authorship. The novel, which had drawn from Mr. Leveson praise for its "masculine breadth of thought and virile grasp of human motive," had been the slow, secret travail of her own brain, written in the early hours before her household stirred. To sit now and hear her own work discussed as the production of a man, and to feel the subtle flattery of being treated as the intelligent sister of a genius, was a psychological ordeal which demanded no small portion of irony and self-command.

Mr. Leveson, who sat at the head of the table, was a man of large intellectual appetites and a somewhat dictatorial manner. His face was heavy and pale, lighted by eyes of that restless intelligence which is perpetually classifying and judging. He had the look of a man who had read everything and found most of it wanting. Turning towards Mrs. Meredith, he addressed her with a courteous but slightly patronizing air.

"I was saying to Hartop," he began, in a voice trained to carry the exact pitch of intellectual authority, "that your brother’s book is one of the few novels of late that one can read without a sense of shame for the craft. There is a severity in his handling of the Darnell episode—a refusal to indulge in the merely sentimental—which speaks well for his future. He does not write as one who is seeking to please, but as one who is compelled to exhibit the truth."

"I will convey your approbation to him," said Eleanor, quietly. "He is much occupied at present with another conception, and such encouragement is precious."

"Tell him," continued Leveson, leaning back and surveying the rim of his glass, "that what the public wants, and what I shall look for, is a further refinement of that masculine method. The danger in these times is a certain softness, a creeping effeminacy in literature, which weakens the fibre of the national mind."

A slight pause followed. Sir James Hartop, who cared little for literature but much for good claret, nodded vaguely, while young Mr. Vance looked up with the earnestness of a man who has not yet made his own dogmas.

"You mean," said Vance, "the predominance of emotional over structural elements? The desire to weep rather than to understand?"

"Precisely," said Leveson, accepting this amplification with a gracious nod. "It is the inevitable consequence of women invading the literary sphere. They write, and the public reads, and a standard is established which is fundamentally unrobust. Women see life through the medium of their own sensations; they are incapable of that disinterested, philosophic survey which alone produces great art. Their minds are excellent for the domestic interior, for the subtleties of drawing-room strategy, but ask them to deal with the broader currents of human motive, with the clash of ideas, with the tragic complexities of the will, and they are entirely at sea. They are, by nature, deficient in the sense of justice, which is the foundation of all true criticism."

Eleanor had been listening with that outward attention which masks an inward tension. Her first impulse was a hot, unreasoning indignation, such as rises in the throat and threatens to betray itself in blush or trembling; but she had learned, in the solitary discipline of her writing-table, that strong feelings are better servants than masters. She looked at Leveson with a calm, measuring glance, noting the easy confidence of his brow, the slight wave of his hand as he dismissed half the human race from the precincts of intellect. It was not malice, she perceived, but a colossal complacency—a provincialism of the mind, which, for all his learning, he had never transcended. He had read history, but had not observed life; or rather, he observed life only through the spectacles of his own prejudices, and called the distortion vision.

"I suppose," she said, with a slight smile, "that you would say the same of women’s capacity for history, for science, for any field which demands a comprehensive view?"

"Undeniably," said Leveson. "I do not deny them cleverness—a sort of intuitive nimbleness, which may produce agreeable verse or lively correspondence. But the architectonic faculty, the power of building a solid, coherent world out of the raw material of human experience—that belongs to the masculine intelligence alone. When a woman attempts it, she is simply playing at being a man, and the result is a monster."
