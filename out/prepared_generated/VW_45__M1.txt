The bell above the door gave its thin, apologetic ring, and with it the whole atmosphere of the shop changed; the dust, which had been settling in a brown November light upon the spines of forgotten histories, was stirred, lifted, and made to dance. Mrs. Ainsworth looked up from the ledger where she had been recording the sale of a slim volume of essays to a man in a grey suit, and saw that Mr. Vance had entered. He stood for a moment on the threshold, shaking the rain from his hat—a gesture that seemed almost too large for the narrow aisle between the Biography and the Poetry—and the wetness on his coat gleamed like the scales of some fresh, exotic fish brought up from the depths of the Thames.

"Horrid weather," he said, his voice booming slightly against the silence, "quite the end of the world."

But he was looking at her, and not at the weather; he was looking at her with that particular kind of kindness which is heavy and warm, like a velvet cloak thrown over the shoulders. She felt the weight of it settle upon her, and she smiled, arranging her features into the polite mask of the shop assistant, though beneath it something fluttered—a moth of vanity, perhaps, or simply the pleasure of being seen.

"Good afternoon, Mr. Vance. We have just received a new consignment from the continent. Some very fine bindings."

"Bindings," he repeated, nodding his head slowly. He did not look at the books. He looked at her hands as they rested on the counter, the fingers long and stained faintly with ink from the morning’s cataloguing. He saw the smallness of the room, the way the shelves pressed in upon them, the meagre gaslight that flickered overhead. "You should not be shut up here, Miss Ainsworth. It is a tomb. A beautiful tomb, I grant you, but a tomb all the same."

She laughed, a sound that was swallowed immediately by the paper and leather surrounding them. "It is my trade, Mr. Vance. And I like the silence."

"Do you?" He stepped closer, and the scent of wet wool and expensive tobacco filled the space between them. "I should like to give you noise. The noise of a motor car. The noise of the sea at Bournemouth. The noise of a dinner party where people talk of things that matter—politics, art, the future."

The future. The word hung in the air, shimmering like the light on a soap bubble. She thought of her room in Camden Town, the gas meter that had to be fed with shillings, the thin soup of the evening meal. She thought of the kindness in his eyes, which asked for nothing but to be allowed to give. It was a temptation not of the flesh, but of the spirit—the desire to be taken out of the grinding machinery of the daily round, to be set down in a place where the light was brighter and the hours were not counted in pennies.

"I shall take these," he said, gesturing vaguely at a stack of books he had not looked at. "Whatever they are. And perhaps... perhaps you would allow me to drive you to Richmond on Sunday? The leaves are turning. It would be a shame to miss them."

She opened her mouth to speak, to refuse or to accept, but the bell rang again, and the moment shattered.

It was Felix. He stood in the doorway, dripping wet, his hair plastered to his forehead, looking like a drowned rat that has crawled out of the sewer of the city. He did not see Mr. Vance at first; he saw only Margaret, and his face lit up with a sudden, desperate intensity, as if she were the only fire burning in a frozen landscape.

"Margaret," he breathed. "I have it. I have finished the stanza. The one about the gulls. Listen—"

He stopped. He saw the man in the grey coat. He saw the pile of books on the counter, the money being exchanged. He saw the way Mr. Vance stood, solid and immovable, occupying the space with the confidence of ownership. Felix’s face, so open a moment before, closed up like a fist. The light went out of his eyes, leaving only the wet, grey misery of the November afternoon.

"Mr. Vance," Margaret said, her voice sounding thin and reedy to her own ears. "This is Mr. Shaw. A poet."

Vance turned, benevolent and puzzled. He looked at the ragged coat, the trembling hands. He smiled the smile of the rich man who pities the poor but does not understand them. "A poet? How fascinating. Do you write about nature?"

Felix did not answer. He looked at Margaret, and his gaze was a physical thing, a wire stretched taut between them. *Don't do it,* his eyes said. *Don't sell the silence. Don't sell the ink.*

"I must be going," Vance said, sensing the draught of hostility. "Sunday, then, Miss Ainsworth?"

"Yes," she said. "Sunday."

The door closed. The bell rang. The silence rushed back in, but it was no longer the peaceful silence of the books; it was a heavy, suffocating silence.

Felix moved to the counter. He picked up one of the volumes Vance had bought— *The Songs of the South Seas*—and held it as if it were a dead thing.

"He knows nothing," Felix said. His voice was rough, scraped raw. "He buys books to weigh down his tables. He buys you to... to decorate his life."

"He is kind, Felix."

"Kind?" Felix laughed, a harsh, barking sound. "It is the kindness of the butcher to the sheep. He wants to take you away from this. From us. From the words."

"And what do you offer?" she cried out, the frustration of the month, the year, rising in her throat. "Poverty? Cold fingers? A future where we starve in a garret and no one reads your poems?"
