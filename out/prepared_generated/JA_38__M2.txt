It was a truth universally acknowledged, or at least so firmly believed by Miss Clara Harcourt, that a woman of governess’s rank who dwells within the walls of a private estate, and who possesses a complexion of such unassuming delicacy and a mind of such quiet intensity, must be actuated solely by the most predatory designs upon the fortunes of her employer. Clara, who had spent the previous three days in a state of nervous agitation, had finally resolved that the peace of Ashworth Park could no longer be preserved without her intervention. Her neighbor, Mr. Arthur Penhaligon, was a man of considerable property, a title, and a disposition that Clara had long admired for its gentle, if somewhat inert, stability. He was, in her estimation, a prize too valuable to be left unguarded against the encroachments of a female whose education was, at best, middling, and whose origins were, at worst, suspiciously humble.

The governess in question was Miss Elara Vance. She was a woman of twenty-three, with a habit of retreating into the library at odd hours and a tendency to speak in sentences that were too well-constructed for her station. It was these very traits that had alarmed Clara. One did not expect a woman of that class to possess such a command of language, nor did one expect her to sit for hours in the corner of the room, reading with an absorption that suggested not merely literary taste, but a deeper, more calculating focus. Clara had observed, with the sharpness of a hawk, that Miss Vance’s eyes often lingered on the portrait of Mr. Penhaligon’s father, or perhaps, Clara fancied, on the young man himself when he passed the open door. The coincidence was too frequent to be accidental. The gaze was too steady to be innocent.

Clara’s plan was simple, if fraught with the danger of appearing meddlesome, a risk she deemed necessary for the sake of propriety. She would visit Ashworth Park under the guise of returning a borrowed volume, and in the course of conversation, she would gently but firmly suggest to Mr. Penhaligon that his household required a closer scrutiny. She would hint at the impropriety of a governess’s intimacy, perhaps allude to a rumor of a previous scandal in a similar situation, and thereby force him to dismiss the creature before the mischief was done.

The journey to Ashworth was a short one, but Clara spent the time in the carriage arranging her features into an expression of benevolent concern. She did not wish to seem jealous, for that would be unforgivable in a woman of her age and position; she wished only to appear wise, and protective of a friend.

Upon arriving, she was received with the usual deference by the butler and led into the drawing room, where Mr. Penhaligon was to be found. He was standing by the fireplace, holding a letter, and did not immediately turn. Clara seized the moment to observe the room. It was tastefully furnished, lacking the ostentation of the capital but possessing a warmth that suggested a genuine appreciation for comfort. And there, seated at a small table near the window, was Miss Vance. She was not looking up. She was engaged in a task of such domestic simplicity—repairing a torn page of a book—that Clara felt a sudden, violent surge of indignation. How dare she? How dare she occupy the same space, the same air, the same silence?

“Mr. Penhaligon,” Clara said, her voice projecting a crispness she had not intended. “I hope I do not intrude upon a moment of private correspondence.”

Mr. Penhaligon turned, a smile of genuine pleasure breaking across his face. “Miss Harcourt! I am charmed to see you. Indeed, you interrupt nothing but my own reflection. Please, sit. Miss Vance, you may leave us if you have no business; otherwise, do remain. I find the company of a good scholar delightful.”

Clara’s heart sank, but she recovered her composure with the skill of a veteran. She took a chair opposite the fire, ensuring that Miss Vance was positioned between her and the gentleman. “I trust you are well, Mr. Penhaligon. I have come to return the *Quarterly Review*, and also to discuss a matter of some local interest. It is said that the roads to the village are improving, which is a boon to those of us who must travel to market.”

Mr. Penhaligon nodded, but his eyes drifted past her, toward the window where Miss Vance stood, now holding the repaired book. “The roads are indeed improving. A pity, perhaps, that they make it easier for us to escape the confines of this county. One feels, at times, that the world outside is very small, and the world within even smaller.”

Clara watched him with narrowed eyes. This was the moment. She leaned forward, her voice dropping to a confidential tone. “You speak of the world within, Mr. Penhaligon. I hope you are aware that such a concept can be dangerous for a man of your standing. There are those who look upon a household not as a sanctuary, but as a market place. I have heard whispers, though I cannot vouch for their truth, that certain individuals in your employ are less concerned with the education of your children than with the advancement of their own prospects.”

Mr. Penhaligon looked at her, his brow slightly furrowed. “Whispers, Miss Harcourt? I am not accustomed to listening to such things. My children are in good hands. Miss Vance is a lady of great integrity and intellect. I value her opinion highly.”

“Opinion,” Clara repeated, the word tasting like ash in her mouth. “Intellect is a fine weapon, sir, but it is often used to conceal a heart that beats for other than its master. Have you not noticed how she watches you? How she lingers when she should be at her duties? It is a common trick, sir. The serpent hides in the garden of the house.”
