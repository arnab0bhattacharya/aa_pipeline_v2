It was a maxim of Lord Arthur Savile that a man might be forgiven for a bad conscience, or even a bad tailor, but never for a missing buttonhole. To appear in society without a flower was, in his estimation, the unpardonable sin of the uninteresting. He had cultivated a reputation for a flawless lapel as an orchid cultivator might cultivate a rare hybrid—by devoting to it every hour that could be spared from the equally demanding tasks of dining well and avoiding the earnest.

On a particular Sunday morning in the season of 189-, however, a tragedy of monumental proportions befell him. He was to attend the wedding of the Duke of Berkhamstead at St. George’s, Hanover Square. The Duke was marrying an American heiress, a young woman whose complexion resembled a pale peach and whose fortune resembled a national debt. It was an event that demanded the strictest adherence to aesthetic ceremony.

As Lord Arthur stood before his cheval glass, his valet, Phipps, proffered a silver tray. Upon it lay a single, perfect carnation, dyed a profound and melancholic green. Lord Arthur placed it in his buttonhole with the reverence of a priest administering a sacrament. He turned to survey the effect. It was charming. He was beautiful. He was entirely satisfied.

He stepped into his hansom cab. The morning was unusually bright, a circumstance that invariably irritated the aristocracy, who prefer their skies to match their silver spoons. As the cab lurched over the cobblestones of Mount Street, a sudden jolt threw Lord Arthur forward. He heard a soft, rustling sigh, the sound that a flower makes when it decides to abandon its post. He looked down. The green carnation had slipped from his lapel, bounced off his knee, and fallen through the half-open door into the muddy thoroughfare.

Before he could summon a cry, the heavy wheels of a passing brewer’s dray ground the blossom and stem into the mire. It was gone.

Lord Arthur sat back in the hansom, his face ashen. To arrive at a wedding without a buttonhole was to declare oneself a clerk, a Nonconformist, or worse, a man of earnest moral purpose. He directed the cabman to drive instantly to Bond Street.

Every shutter was drawn. It was Sunday. London, which on a weekday is a vast emporium of beautiful vanities, had transformed itself into a desert of enforced piety. The florists, those high priests of modern romance, were locked in their Sabbath slumbers.

Lord Arthur alighted and walked with a feverish elegance down the deserted pavement. He encountered a crossing-sweeper, a boy of singularly unpleasant aspect, who was regarding the sky with the bovine stupidity of the truly destitute.

“You,” cried Lord Arthur, tossing a sovereign into the dirt. “Find me a flower. A carnation, a rose, a violet—anything that possesses a petal and a pulse.”

The boy looked at the coin, then at Lord Arthur. “I knows where there’s a mortuary, sir,” he replied, with the cheerful morbidity of his class. “But they don’t ‘ave no blooms there. Only them as is stiff.”

“I do not require a bouquet for a funeral,” Lord Arthur snapped, “but to prevent one. Away with you.”

He returned to the cab and commanded the driver to traverse the length of the city. They drove through the somnolent majesty of Belgravia, where the area railings looked like the bars of a prison for the exceedingly well-bred. They rattled past the British Museum, where the dead languages lay entombed in stone, and into the clamorous labyrinth of Whitechapel.

In the East End, the Sabbath was observed with a different flavor. The air was thick with the scent of stale gin and fried fish, a combination that, while possessing a certain piquant realism, was hardly conducive to aesthetic contemplation. Lord Arthur, his pearl-grey gloves resting lightly on his knees, peered through the window with the detached curiosity of an explorer in a foreign land.

“Stop,” he cried suddenly.

He had spotted a barrow at a street corner. It was piled not with cabbages or second-hand boots, but with flowers. They were somewhat wilted, it was true, and their colors had the garish, unapologetic brightness of the lower classes. A woman in a plaid shawl was presiding over them.

Lord Arthur stepped out. The crowd on the pavement parted for him as the Red Sea is said to have parted for the Israelites, though with less subsequent consequence.

“Good woman,” said Lord Arthur, “I will give you five pounds for that white carnation.”

The woman stared at him, her face a map of the harsh London weather. She looked at the flower in question, a slightly battered specimen that leaned drunkenly against a bunch of diseased-looking chrysanthemums.

“Five quid?” she echoed, her voice flat with suspicion. “For a bloom? You’re ‘aving a game, sir.”

“I assure you I am entirely serious. The flower, if you please.”

The woman, however, was not to be rushed. She had been born into a world where sudden generosity was invariably a prelude to betrayal. She took the carnation in her rough, red hand.

“It’s a lovely little thing,” she murmured, stroking its petals with a tenderness that surprised Lord Arthur. “I was going to take it home to me daughter. She’s got the fever, and she do love a flower.”

“Here is ten pounds,” said Lord Arthur, producing a banknote. “Give it to your daughter. I only require the blossom.”

The transaction was completed. Lord Arthur, with a sigh of profound relief, pinned the somewhat disreputable carnation to his immaculate lapel. It sat there, a touch of the gutter in the heart of Mayfair, but it was a flower. He was saved.

He arrived at St. George’s just as the congregation was spilling out onto the pavement, a shimmering tide of silk, velvet, and moral laxity. The wedding was over.

Lord Henry Merton, a man celebrated for his ability to say perfectly outrageous things with an air of absolute innocence, spotted him and sauntered over.
