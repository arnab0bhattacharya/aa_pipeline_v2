The path up the mountain had begun as a convenience, a mere white thread laid upon the hillside for the pleasure of the English, but it had long since given up all pretence of being a path and had become instead a series of assertions—a stone here, a scuffle of dust there—as though the mountain itself were reluctant to admit them. They climbed in single file, and the single file had become, without anyone quite noticing, a kind of statement. Mr. Ashworth led, because he always led, his panama hat a small pale disc against the greater pallor of the sky. Behind him came his wife, who had not spoken since the second bend, and behind her the two who were not married and were not, everyone agreed, going to be—Miss Verney, whose parasol had begun to droop like a tired flower, and Mr. Thorne, who carried no hat at all and whose face had taken on the colour of the baked earth.

The heat was not a thing that fell upon them from above so much as a thing they walked through, thick and resistant, like water that had forgotten how to move. It pressed upon the backs of their necks. It gathered in the folds of their clothing. It made of every stone a small oven and of every shadow a rumour. And yet the mountain was beautiful—one had to keep saying so—for the sea below them had turned the colour of a grape held up to the light, and the island itself, spread out like a crumpled cloth, showed here and there the white flash of a house, the green stab of a cypress, the slow brown turning of a road.

“One might,” said Mr. Ashworth, stopping so suddenly that his wife nearly walked into him, “one might almost be in Italy.”

“We are not in Italy,” said Mrs. Ashworth, and the words came out flat and dry, like the sound of a spoon struck against a cup.

“My dear—”

“We are on an island. There is a difference.”

The difference, whatever it was, seemed to require no further comment. They stood a moment, the four of them, strung out along the path like beads on a broken string, and the silence that came down was not the silence of the mountain but something more particular, something that had been carried up with them and now sat heavily in the heat. Miss Verney opened her parasol a little wider, though the sun had not moved, and Mr. Thorne looked at the sea as though he had never seen it before and hoped never to see it again.

It was Mrs. Ashworth who broke first, though not in the way one might have expected.

“I have been thinking,” she said, and her voice had in it a curious lightness, almost a gaiety, as though she had just discovered something and was not yet sure whether to keep it, “I have been thinking that when we go down—when we go down, I shall not be the same person who came up.”

Her husband turned to look at her. His face, beneath the panama, was pink and puzzled.

“Of course you won’t,” he said. “You’ll be tired. We shall all be tired.”

“No,” she said. “That is not what I mean.”

And she would not say what she meant. She began to walk again, and the others followed, and the path, which had been a series of assertions, became a series of concessions, each step a small surrender to the mountain. The air grew thinner, which is to say it grew more precious, and with each breath they took less of it and wanted more.

At the summit there was a cairn of stones and a view that had been described in the guidebook as “magnificent” and was, in fact, beyond all description, being nothing but light and distance and the slow blue breathing of the sea. They stood in a row and looked at it, and for a moment they were united in the way that people are united by beauty—which is to say, not at all, but side by side.

Then Miss Verney said, to no one in particular, “I should like to throw something over.”

“Throw something?” said Mr. Thorne.

“A stone. A coin. Something of mine. To make a mark.”

“You can’t make a mark on that,” said Mr. Ashworth, with a wave of his hand that took in the whole of the Mediterranean.

“No,” she said. “That is why I should like to.”

And she took from her finger a ring—a small thing, a garnet—and held it up, and the light went through it and made a red star in the air, and then she threw it, and they watched it fall, and it became nothing, and the sea took it without a sound.

“There,” she said, and her voice trembled a little, and she turned away.

It was then that Mr. Thorne, who had said almost nothing all afternoon, began to speak. He spoke to Mrs. Ashworth, as though the others were not there, and his voice was low and hurried, as if he had been keeping the words for a long time and could not now stop them.

“You said you would not be the same. I have been thinking the same thing. Only it is not the going down. It is the being here. I have been here—we have all been here—for three weeks, and I have not written a line. Not a line. I sit at the little table in my room and I look at the paper and I think of all the things I might say, and they seem—they seem like the things one says at a party, when the windows are shut and the lamps are lit and everyone is being clever. And then I come out here and the light is so—it is so indifferent. It does not care whether I write or not. And I think, what is the use of being clever in the face of that?”
