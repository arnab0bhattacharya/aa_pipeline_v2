It is a fatal thing, and one that leads to the most disastrous of conclusions, to believe that a rich man is a poor man with money. Miss Araminta Ponsford, who had been brought up on the very best principles—that is to say, the worst—held this to be an article of faith more immutable than the doctrines of the Church. Her father, a financier whose soul had been so thoroughly mortgaged to his ledgers that he could not redeem it even for ready money, had died leaving her a fortune so vast that to speak of it was in the worst possible taste; therefore, she spoke of it constantly. She had been taught that wealth was vulgar, and she had accepted the proposition with the serene delight of one who finds a universal rule that admits of only one exception: herself.

Her drawing-room in Mayfair was a masterpiece of studied poverty. There was a single, beautiful Persian rug upon the floor, a testament to the fact that one perfect thing is better than many fine ones. The walls were hung with silk the colour of a faded rose, and upon them hung three pictures: a Whistler, a Botticelli, and a portrait of Miss Ponsford herself by an artist who had captured her so perfectly that she had been obliged to dismiss him. She believed that beauty, like good news, should be rationed, and she administered her surroundings with the severity of a Spartan queen.

It was in Chelsea, that paradise of pretension and the birthplace of so many bad verses and good intentions, that she discovered Mr. Julian Vane. He was painting in a studio that smelled of turpentine and ambition, a young man with the face of a poet and the hands of a dreamer. His canvases were riotous explosions of colour—peonies bleeding into pomegranates, sunsets clashing with the sea—and he himself was a study in delightful destitution. His coat was of a foreign cut and a domestic threadbareness, and he spoke of light and shadow as other men speak of dividends.

Miss Ponsford fell in love instantly. It was, of course, the only logical thing to do. He was beautiful, he was poor, and he was an artist; he was, in short, a living embodiment of all the principles she so dearly espoused. She saw in him the perfect companion for her life of elegant austerity, a man who would appreciate the subtle poetry of a dry crust and the profound philosophy of an unpaid bill.

She bought three of his canvases at a price that made him gasp and her heart sing with the joy of a martyr paying for her own stake. She invited him to her house, where he stood, dazzled, upon the Persian rug. He admired the Botticelli; he was captivated by the Whistler. Then, his eyes, accustomed to the riot of his own palette, fell upon the vast, empty spaces of her walls.

‘My dear Miss Ponsford,’ he said, a note of genuine horror in his voice, ‘this is all very well, but where is the rest of it?’

‘The rest of what, Mr. Vane?’ she asked, pleased by his perceptiveness.

‘The art! The colour! The life!’ he cried, gesturing wildly. ‘This room is not a home; it is a mausoleum for good taste. With a house like this, a man could be a prince. With a fortune like yours, a man could own every beautiful thing in London. He could fill these walls with masterpieces. He could live…’ He paused, and a look of profound longing came over his face. ‘He could live magnificently.’

Araminta felt a chill, as if a window had been opened upon a winter of the soul. ‘You wish to be rich?’ she whispered, the words tasting of ash.

‘Of course!’ he declared, with the unthinking candour of a child. ‘What is the use of painting the beauty of the world if one cannot own a little of it? I am tired of poverty, Miss Ponsford. It is a charming costume for a season, but it is a dreadful thing to be buried in.’

In that moment, Miss Ponsford’s love curdled into a noble and terrible resolve. He was a savage who had been shown a jewel and desired only its market value. He had the soul of a grocer and the ambition of a chimney sweep. It was her duty, her sacred mission, to teach him the beauty of poverty. She would instruct him in its exquisite textures and its sublime silences. She would show him that true wealth was a spirit unburdened by possessions.

Julian, for his part, had seen the chill in her eyes and had formed a resolve of his own. He had met many beautiful women, but never one so wilfully, so extravagantly, so magnificently ignorant of the world. She treated her fortune as if it were a disease, and her own exquisite beauty as a mere accident of nature. He would teach her the poverty of beauty. He would show her that a lovely face, like a lovely picture, was but a perishable thing without the gilded frame of circumstance to give it meaning and permanence.

And so their courtship began, a battle of opposing pedagogies. She took him to the East End, to a wretched little room where a family of five subsisted on hope and the occasional herring. She pointed out the noble simplicity of their lives, the dignity of their threadbare blankets. Julian, who had lived in such rooms, merely remarked that the damp was very bad for the constitution and that the children’s coughs sounded like a death rattle. ‘One cannot eat dignity, Miss Ponsford,’ he said, ‘and it is a poor sauce for a potato.’

He, in turn, took her to the opera, not in her own box, but in the pit, where they could see and be seen. He pointed out the Duchess of Dorset, a woman whose face was a ruin of rouge and regret, but whose diamonds were the talk of Europe. ‘Behold the triumph of art over nature,’ he murmured. ‘Her beauty is gone, but its memory is preserved in paste and platinum. That is the only immortality worth having, my dear Araminta.’
