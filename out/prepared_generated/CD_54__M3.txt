If any traveller, chancing in the year eighteen hundred and fifty-something to be benighted upon the prodigiously muddy and entirely unpicturesque high road between the towns of Cogden Bridge and Muddleton, were to inquire of a passing rustic as to the name of the settlement whose chimneys darkened the very heavens before him, he would be told, with a melancholy shake of the head, that it was Splaxton. And if the same traveller were to ask what manner of place Splaxton might be, the rustic would likely reply that it was a place of smoke, a place of bricks, a place of everlasting clatter, and, above all, a place belonging to Mr. Grindstone.

Mr. Grindstone was, in his own estimation, the making of Splaxton; and as Splaxton was, in the estimation of Mr. Grindstone, entirely indebted to him for the blessing of being unendurably miserable from five o’clock in the morning until nine o’clock at night, it may be readily supposed that he considered himself a public benefactor of the highest water. He was a man of a hard, red face, as if he had been boiled and then ironed; of a stiff, square figure, as if he had been built, like his own machinery, to run on certain unyielding lines; and of a voice that sounded, in its ordinary conversational key, very much like the grating of a heavily laden coal-cart upon loose stones.

To this agreeable spot came, on a damp Tuesday evening in November, the Thespian and Dramatic Association of the United Kingdoms, headed by its founder, manager, and leading actor, Mr. Septimus Quivers. Mr. Quivers was a gentleman of a singularly lank and melancholy aspect, who wore a velvet cloak so completely worn away at the elbows that it resembled a garment of tatters more than a vestment of art, and who spoke, upon all occasions and in all weathers, as if he were addressing a crowded gallery from the boards of Drury Lane. It was the misfortune of Mr. Quivers, and of his devoted company, that the axle of their conveyance—a machine of antiquated and infirm constitution, answering to the name of the Triumph—had snapped clean in two within a quarter of a mile of Mr. Grindstone’s gates, depositing the entire theatrical wardrobe, together with three actresses of varying ages, a property crown, and a papier-mâché dragon, in a ditch of considerable depth and singularly cold water.

Here, then, was a predicament! The company was stranded, the axle was irreparable by any ordinary means, and the innkeeper at the Soaked Sprocket—a man of a countenance like a damp sponge—would accept no theatrical credit, however pathetically or eloquently advanced by Mr. Quivers. But Mr. Quivers was a man of resource. Mr. Quivers, rising from the ditch, and wringing the ditch-water from his velvet cloak with an air of grand desolation, declared that the drama should not perish in Splaxton; that the muse should not be unstrung by a mere mechanical fracture; and that he would, that very night, hire the long room of the Soaked Sprocket, and present to the generous and enlightened populace of Splaxton a piece of heart-stirring domestic tragedy, the receipts of which should defray the expense of a new axle.

The piece selected was one of Mr. Quivers’s own compositions, entitled "The Iron Heart; or, The Cruel Master of the Mill." It was, in its way, a masterpiece of dramatic irony; for the cruel master, Sir Bludel Greed, was depicted as a fiend of such unmitigated blackness, grinding the faces of the poor, turning widows into the snow, and subsisting entirely on a diet of gold and the tears of orphans, that one might have thought him a creature of mere fable. Mr. Quivers, it must be owned, had not written it with any particular reference to Splaxton; he had written it because Sir Bludel died in the third act of an apoplexy, which was always a very telling effect, and allowed Mr. Quivers to exhibit his celebrated dying-scene, wherein he tore his hair, reproached his ancestors, and expired in a paroxysm of remorse, all within the space of four minutes.

But Mr. Grindstone, hearing of the intended performance from his head-clerk, a pale young man with a cough, and learning, with a shock that turned his red face to a deeper purple, the very title of the play, resolved to put a stop to the business forthwith. He issued, therefore, a proclamation—nay, a decree—posted upon the great gates of the mill, and affixed to the very pump in the yard, to the effect that any hand, operative, workman, woman, or child, in his employ, who should presume to attend the theatrical representation at the Soaked Sprocket, should be instantly discharged, and turned, without character or reference, into the unfeeling streets.

This edict produced a profound sensation in Splaxton. The mill-hands, who had looked forward to an evening of tragic delight as to a glimpse of paradise, were cast into the deepest gloom. They stood about in knots, looking at the handbills, and then at the mill, and then at their own ragged coats, as if comparing the relative claims of art and bread, and finding bread, as is too often the case in this world, the heavier weight. Mr. Grindstone, from his counting-house window, chuckled consumedly to himself, and rubbed his hands with a sound like the filing of brass.

Mr. Quivers, however, was not a man to be daunted by a tyrant. Mr. Quivers, on learning of the prohibition, was filled with that righteous indignation which is the life-blood of the drama. He declared, in the bar of the Soaked Sprocket, his voice trembling with heroic fury, that the cause of Thespis was the cause of liberty; that he would play the piece if but a single spectator could be mustered, even if that single spectator were a deaf old woman or a blind old man; and that he would teach Mr. Grindstone, by the force of histrionic example, that the human heart could not be fenced in by factory-gates.
