The letter lay upon my writing-desk like a serpent coiled amid the innocent papers of my daily life; and indeed its words had stung me to the very soul. It was from a stranger, a physician of a private asylum near London, who wrote to inform me that my mother, whom I had believed for two-and-twenty years to be mouldering in her grave, was yet alive, and had been these twenty years a prisoner within those walls. My father, he wrote, had consigned her thither in the first year of their marriage, under circumstances which the writer did not presume to judge, but which he felt it his duty, now that the hand of death was upon him, to disclose to the only child of that unhappy union. I read the letter again and again, and each perusal seemed to burn the words more deeply into my brain. My mother living!—living, while I had wept over the tale of her death as over a sacred sorrow; living, while my father's grief had been the theme of all who knew him, and his faithful widowhood the model of connubial constancy! The whole fabric of my past seemed rent asunder; the tender recollections of my childhood, the reverential love I had borne my father, the melancholy pride with which I had cherished the memory of a mother whom I never knew—all, all were shaken, and I stood amid the ruins of my affections, aghast and trembling.

I will not dwell upon the agonies of that night. The morning found me pale and haggard, but resolved. I would go to her; I would see this injured being, whose wrongs I but half comprehended, and whose voice I had never heard but in the tales of others. I ordered my horse, and set forth alone, for I could not bear that any eye should witness the emotions which must overwhelm me. The road to the asylum lay through a country of singular desolation; the autumn had stripped the trees of their foliage, and the wind, sweeping over the barren heath, drove before it the withered leaves in eddying whirlwinds, that seemed to me the fit emblems of human hopes and joys, scattered and trodden under foot by the relentless storms of fate. The sky was low and heavy, and the rain fell in fitful gusts, as if nature herself wept over the sorrows of the world. I rode on in silence, communing with my own troubled spirit, and striving to prepare myself for the interview which awaited me.

The asylum stood upon a rising ground, a gloomy pile of dark stone, surrounded by high walls and a moat of stagnant water, whose sluggish surface reflected the leaden heavens above. The very air seemed heavy with the sighs of the wretched, and the silence was broken only by the distant moaning of the wind among the trees, or the occasional shriek of some poor maniac, that pierced the ear like a knell of despair. I dismounted at the gate, and, giving my name to the porter, was conducted through a long and dreary corridor to the apartment of the physician. He was a man advanced in years, with a countenance worn by thought and suffering, and a manner that bespoke the sympathy he dared not openly express. He received me with a melancholy kindness, and, after some preliminary discourse, in which he strove to soften the shock he was about to inflict, he related to me the sad history of my mother's wrongs.

She had been, he said, a woman of singular beauty and sensibility, endowed with a mind of the highest order, and a heart overflowing with tenderness. In the first year of her marriage, a misunderstanding had arisen between her and my father,—a misunderstanding, he hinted, rather than a crime,—but my father, jealous of her affections, and stung to madness by suspicions which had no foundation in truth, had taken advantage of a temporary derangement of her faculties, brought on by a fever, to confine her in this living tomb. There she had remained for twenty years, her reason restored, but her liberty denied her; her letters intercepted, her prayers unheard, her very existence concealed from the world and from her child. The physician had attended her through the whole period of her captivity; he had watched over her with a father's care; and now, feeling his own end approaching, he could no longer endure to be the keeper of so cruel a secret.

I heard him to the end in silence; but when he had done, a torrent of emotions, long suppressed, burst forth, and I wept like a child. I wept for my mother, for her blighted youth, her wasted years, her lonely sufferings; I wept for my father, whose crime had been expiated by a lifetime of remorse; I wept for myself, the unconscious inheritor of so much woe. Then a fierce indignation took possession of my soul. I demanded to be led to her immediately; I could not, I would not, wait another hour. The physician, seeing my agitation, endeavoured to calm me; but I broke from him, and, following the direction he gave, I rushed along the winding passages, and ascended the narrow stair that led to my mother's cell.

The door was open. I paused upon the threshold, and beheld a female figure seated by a narrow window, through which the pale light of a November afternoon fell dimly upon her. She was clothed in black, and her hair, once dark as the raven's wing, was now thickly sprinkled with grey. Her face was turned towards the light, and I could see that it was still beautiful, though worn by sorrow and seared by long years of solitary grief. She was reading in a little book, and her lips moved as if in prayer. I stood motionless, unable to speak, unable to advance; my heart beat so violently that I thought it would burst its prison. At length she raised her eyes, and beheld me. A sudden flush overspread her pale cheek, and she rose from her seat, and stretched out her arms towards me with a cry that rent my very soul.
