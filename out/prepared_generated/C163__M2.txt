The rain in Lambeth did not fall so much as it hovered, a grey, suspended mist that seeped into the bones of the old brickwork and settled in the lungs. It was a Tuesday evening in November, 1887, and the house at 44 Blackfriars Road stood empty, its windows boarded up against the damp, its interior a cavern of silence broken only by the tick of a clock that had long since stopped.

Inspector Arthur Penhaligon of the Metropolitan Police stepped over a pile of rotted floorboards, his boots squelching in the stagnant water that pooled in the corners of the parlor. He looked tired, his face a map of lines carved by decades of low-level drudgery. Beside him, the constable, a young man named Miller, held his lantern up, casting long, trembling shadows against the peeling wallpaper.

"Strange, sir," Miller whispered, his voice echoing in the hollow room. "No signs of forced entry. The windows are latched from the inside. And no furniture. Not a chair, not a table. Just the body."

Penhaligon crouched beside the deceased. The man was middle-aged, dressed in a suit that had been fashionable perhaps a decade prior, now stained with dust and mud. His face was pale, the skin slack, and his eyes were open, fixed on a point somewhere in the middle distance. There was no wound. No poison could be smelled, though the constable had already sent for a doctor.

"What’s this?" Penhaligon asked, pointing to a sheet of paper lying on the bare floorboards, just inches from the dead man’s outstretched hand.

Miller leaned in, lowering the lantern. It was a newspaper, folded neatly into a square. It was not a London paper, nor a paper from any major city Penhaligon recognized. The masthead was faded, the ink brown with age.

*The Ohio Gazette*, it read.

"American?" Miller frowned. "Why would a man die in an empty house in Southwark with an American newspaper?"

Penhaligon picked up the paper with a pair of tweezers, though he did not have any, and used his handkerchief instead. He unfolded it carefully. The date at the top was clear: *Columbus, Ohio, October 14, 1867.*

"Twenty years ago," Penhaligon muttered. He looked at the man’s face again. "Who is he?"

Miller shrugged. "No identification on him. No wallet, no watch. Nothing."

Penhaligon felt a chill that had nothing to do with the damp. He recognized the town. He had read about it in the old files, the ones that were supposed to be buried but never quite were. The Great Columbus Bank Heist. A robbery so brazen, so meticulously planned, that it had made headlines across the Atlantic. Three men had walked into the central bank, emptied the vault, and vanished into thin air. No one had ever seen them again. It was a ghost story for bankers, a legend for detectives.

He should have called it in. He should have waited for the forensics, for the autopsy, for the proper channels. But Penhaligon was an old dog in a new trick, and something in the way the newspaper lay, perfectly flat, perfectly centered, felt like a message. A signature.

He left the house with Miller, instructing him to secure the scene and send for the morgue. But as he walked into the misty street, his mind was elsewhere. He had a contact, a man named Silas Vane, who was not a police officer, but a private investigator of dubious reputation and exceptional knowledge. Vane lived in a cramped flat in Bermondsey, surrounded by maps and old ledgers. He was the kind of man who knew the names of every smuggler, every forger, and every man who had crossed the ocean with a past he wished to forget.

Vane’s flat smelled of pipe smoke and stale tea. He sat in a high-backed chair, his legs crossed, watching Penhaligon enter with a pair of eyes that seemed to look through him rather than at him.

"You look like you’ve seen a ghost, Arthur," Vane said, not looking up from the book he was reading.

"I’ve found a body," Penhaligon said, closing the door behind him. "Empty house. No ID. American newspaper. *The Ohio Gazette*, dated 1867."

Vane’s hand paused on the page. He looked up, his expression shifting from boredom to sharp interest. "Columbus?"

"Yes."

"The bank robbery?"

Penhaligon nodded. "The man who died. He was holding it. Ringing the date with a circle of ink. It’s a calling card, Silas. Or a confession."

Vane set the book down. He moved to the window, looking out at the dark street. "If it’s a confession, he’s dead. If it’s a calling card, someone else is coming."

"Who would do that?" Penhaligon asked. "Why kill a man who is already a ghost?"

"Perhaps he wasn't a ghost," Vane said softly. "Perhaps he was a witness. Or perhaps he was the one who got away."

Vane walked to a cabinet and pulled out a leather-bound notebook. He flipped through the pages, his finger tracing the names of men who had vanished. "There were three men in the robbery. A planner, a driver, and a gunman. The driver was caught in New York. The planner died in a fever in 1870. The gunman... no one knows. But there were rumors. Rumors that he didn't go back to the States. That he came here. To London."

"Why?"

"London is a city of shadows, Arthur. It’s easy to disappear. And the currency... well, if you can launder the gold, you can live like a king in the back rooms of the city."

Penhaligon stared at Vane. "You think this man is the gunman?"

"I think this man is the key," Vane said. "And the newspaper isn't just a memento. It’s a map. Look at the date. October 14, 1867. That’s the day of the robbery. But look at the edition. It’s the evening edition. The one that carried the news of the theft. If he ringed the date, he’s marking the moment. But why kill him?"
