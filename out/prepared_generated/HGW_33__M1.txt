The night over Barrowfield was a strange, unwholesome yellow, a colour that had nothing to do with the setting sun and everything to do with the smoke of a hundred chimneys lying under a low ceiling of cloud. It was the sort of sky that promised a wet Tuesday, the sort of sky in which the whole of a man’s life could be predicted. Arthur Penhaligon, standing on the steps of his mill, felt its familiar weight pressing down upon him. Below, the town was a dark seam in the valley, its lights already pricked out against the gloom. The looms in the mill behind him had fallen silent an hour ago, a silence that felt more ominous than their ceaseless, metallic thunder.

Then, the world changed.

It was not a sound, at first, but a light. A sudden, impossible glare from the west, a sheet of white fire that bleached the yellow sky and threw the long, sharp shadows of the mill’s chimneys across the cobbled yard. Penhaligon shielded his eyes, his mind immediately sorting through the possibilities—a gas main, a powder magazine, a catastrophic boiler explosion. But the light was too high, too clean. A low rumble followed, a sound like a giant’s gravel cart being tipped over, and then came the rain—not of water, but of fire.

Streaks of incandescent matter, like tracer bullets from some celestial war, tore through the clouds. They were not meteors in the romantic, wish-granting sense; they were hot, angry stones, and they fell upon the high moors that cradled the town. The peat and the dry heather, tinder after a long spell of fair weather, caught at once.

Within the hour, the western horizon was a line of fire. The wind, which had been a mere whisper, began to draw upon the blaze, and a column of ruddy smoke stood against the stars, a monstrous pillar marking the ruin of the moorland. The town held its breath. Then, the town bell began to toll, a frantic, cracked summons that had not been heard since the great flood of ‘88.

The summons was for the fire brigades, but there were only two engines in Barrowfield, and the moors were vast. It was the Mayor who, in a state of considerable agitation, gave the order that was echoed from the steps of the mill: all able-bodied men were to take up spades and buckets and proceed to the moor. Mill hands, quarrymen, carters—all were wanted. The fires had to be beaten out before the wind turned and brought them down into the valley, upon the houses and the mills themselves.

Penhaligon, a man of action as much as a man of capital, saw the logic of it. He gave the order to his foreman, and a stream of men, their faces grim and curious in the fiery light, began to pour out of the mill yards and up the steep track to the moor. Among them, he noted with a flicker of irritation, were the men from the picket line. The strike was three weeks old, a bitter affair over a new, faster loom and a corresponding cut in wages. He had blacklisted the ringleaders, a necessary measure to protect the interests of the company. Now, the company’s interests were the town’s interests, and the distinction had become inconvenient.

The scene on the moor was one of controlled chaos. The air was thick with the smell of burning peat, a choking, earthy perfume. The fire brigades were directing their feeble jets of water onto the most immediate threats, but the real work was with the spades, men digging trenches in the heather and beating at the flames with wet sacks. The heat was intense, a dry, roaring presence that sucked the moisture from a man’s throat. And there was no command. Men worked in frantic, isolated groups, their efforts as likely to fan the flames as to smother them.

Penhaligon stood by a gate, shouting directions that were swallowed by the wind and the crackle of the fire. He was a man used to the ordered world of the mill, where every man had his station and every machine its rhythm. This was a different kind of battle. He felt a rising tide of frustration, the uselessness of a commander without an army.

It was then that he saw the young man.

He was not large, but he had the wiry strength of the loom-tenders, and he moved with a purpose that was utterly absent in the others. He was Tom Garside, the most vocal of the strikers, a man whose name had been on the blacklist for a fortnight. Penhaligon had seen him only across a picket line, his face set in a mask of sullen defiance. Now, he was a different creature entirely. He had climbed onto a great millstone that lay half-buried in the heather, and his voice, a clear, carrying tenor, cut through the din.

“Stop! Stop that, you fools! You’re just spreading it! Dig a firebreak here! A line, from that rock to the old sheepfold! Spades, all of you! You two, with the sacks, stand ready to beat out any sparks that cross the line!”

His voice was not that of a striker making demands. It was the voice of a foreman, a man who understood the physics of a problem and the management of men. And, to Penhaligon’s astonishment, the men obeyed. They turned from their frantic, useless labour and, under Garside’s shouted instructions, began to dig. The boy who had been on the picket line, shouting for a shilling more a week, was now organising a defence of the entire town.

Penhaligon felt a hot flush of something that was not anger, but a profound and disorienting humiliation. He, the owner of the mill, the man whose capital employed these very men, was standing uselessly by a gate. This blacklisted agitator, this troublemaker, was taking command. He could see it in the glances of the men as they worked—glances of relief and respect directed at the young man on the millstone. They were no longer a mob of mill hands; they were a fire brigade, and Garside was their captain.
