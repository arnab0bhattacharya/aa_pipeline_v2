If any wandering traveller, or seafaring man inclined to a damp bed and a moist conscience, had found himself stumbling through the narrows of the town of Brécourt on that sour and squalid night in the autumn of the year 1793, he would have found it, with its shutters barred, its snuffling gendarmes, and its dismally howling sea-wind, to be a most miserable and God-forsaken refuge. The very houses appeared to have turned their backs upon the street, shrinking into their own rotten foundations as if to avoid the gaze of the revolutionary patrol; and the street itself, deep in a black mud that seemed to be compounded of the washings of the guillotine and the scraping of the bottom of the Channel, yawned like a hungry grave.

It was into this scene of unutterable desolation that Captain Amos Bowdge, a man of a broad and weather-beaten aspect, carrying about his person an atmosphere of salt beef, tar, and stubborn English independence, thrust his hands deeper into his pea-coat pockets and swore a solemn and comprehensive oath against the whole Republic of France. Captain Bowdge was the commander of the brig *Mumblety-Peg*, a trim little vessel then lying at anchor in the roads, and ostensibly engaged in the lawful and laudable business of carrying Newfoundland cod; but which, on that particular night, was waiting with a silent and hungry hold to receive a very different cargo—a cargo of terrified aristocrats, packed as closely and as secretly as the very cod they pretended to be.

The Captain had been led to a low tavern of a profoundly evil smell, kept by a one-legged man named Bouchard, who crawled about his own tap-room like a gigantic and disreputable spider. Here, in a dismal back-parlour that was hung with damp cobwebs and smelt strongly of stale garlic and old rope, the business of the night was to be transacted.

"I tell you what it is the matter, Mounseer," said Captain Bowdge, addressing a thin, anxious Frenchman who sat at the table. "I tell you, and I say it plain, that my hold is full. I am chock-a-block. I haven't so much as a spare inch of deck for a ghost, let alone a living soul. If you want to ship a dozen more, you must get another bottom, or swim for it!"

"But, my dear Captain," urged the Frenchman, whose name was Lemoine, and who spoke English with a fearful and trembling accent, as if the very words might betray him to the lamppost, "it is not a dozen. It is but one. A child. A little child. And his nurse."

"A child!" roared the Captain, bringing his great hand down upon the table until the pewter pots danced a frantic jig. "A child, and a nurse! And what next, I should like to know? A French dancing-master? A poodle dog? A child is just the sort of screaming, squalling, telling little devil that will bring the whole boiling down upon us. A child can't hold its tongue. It hasn't got the art of it."

"But this one—he is dumb, sir. He is struck without a sound. He has not spoken since they took his father and his mother in the cart, three days gone. He is a little ghost, sir. And the nurse—she is faithful as a dog. She will not leave him."

"And who, in the name of all the blue devils, is the nurse?" demanded the Captain.

At this juncture, a door at the back of the parlour, which had before been closed, creaked slowly open, and there entered a woman who instantly struck the Captain with a peculiar and profound uneasiness. She was a tall, gaunt, square-shouldered female, with a face like a sheet of yellow parchment, upon which the red cap of Liberty was stuck at a fierce and defiant angle. She carried in her arms a child of some four or five years old, a pale and beautiful boy, dressed in rough peasant's clothes, but with an air of languid refinement about his small, weary limbs that betrayed his blood as surely as if he had worn his father's coronet. The child's head rested on the woman's shoulder, and his large, dark eyes stared at the candle-flame with a blank and awful fixity.

The nurse herself was a spectacle to make the blood run cold. She wore a coarse apron, stained with what might have been wine or might have been something darker; and her eyes, which were small, green, and set very close together, slid about the room with a restless, prying glance, as if she were taking an inventory of every crack in the wall and every stain on the floor. She had a curious habit of drawing her lips in, as if she were tasting something sour, and she spoke in a deep, gruff voice, with a rasping cough that she coughed at the end of every sentence, as if it were a necessary part of the grammar.

"I am the nurse," said she, with a curtsey that was more like a mockery of a curtsey. "Citizeness Grognard. I am to go with the little aristocrat. It is my duty to the Republic."

"Your what?" said the Captain, eyeing her with intense suspicion.

"My duty to the Republic," she repeated, drawing her lips in and giving her cough. "The Committee of Public Safety has ordained that the child shall live. But he must have a guardian. I am that guardian."

The Captain looked at Lemoine, and Lemoine looked at the Captain, and the child looked at neither, but continued to stare at the candle. The Captain's mind was made up in a flash. He had seen many a strange sight on the high seas, and many a strange passenger in his time, but he had never seen a woman who looked more like a spy than this same Citizeness Grognard. He was as certain that she was a spy as he was that the tide was coming in; and he was as certain that she carried in her capacious apron a list of the very aristocrats he was about to smuggle away, as he was that his own ship had two masts. But he was a man of resource.
