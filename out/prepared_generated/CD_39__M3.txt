It was a general persuasion in the town of Grimesdyke, that if you were to take a chisel to Mr. Pecksniff Grout, you would find a column of figures running clean through to his marrow. He was the first cotton master in those parts to reduce a human sigh to a fraction, and he could calculate the exact pecuniary exhaustion of a widow’s tear to the sixth decimal place. He was a man of weight, both in the market and on the parish scales; a man who, when he walked down the High Street, seemed to be perpetually auditing the very cobblestones beneath his boots, summing up the dirt, the stray dogs, and the honest people into one comprehensive, unassailable balance-sheet.

Mr. Grout had lately taken to auditing Mrs. Millicent Tottle.

Millicent was the relict of the late Mr. Joshua Tottle, ironmonger, a man of substantial and unyielding iron, who had departed this life leaving his wife with a well-stocked shop, a heavy ledger, and a profound silence. The shop, with its dark and glittering caverns of nails, hammers, and heavy hinges, stood in Cropper Street, breathing a metallic and sooty breath of its own. Its interior was a twilight forest of iron, where the kettles hung like dark, extinguished moons, and the shovels stood in ranks like melancholy sentinels awaiting the very fires of perdition. Here, Millicent ruled, a woman of two-and-thirty, with a pair of steady grey eyes, a resolute chin, and a heart that had been wrapped in brown paper, sealed with iron filings, and put away on the highest shelf of the warehouse, directly behind the patent door-latches.

‘A most providential arrangement, my dear Mrs. Tottle,’ Mr. Grout was in the habit of saying, as he stood before her counter, his large, flabby form seeming to absorb what little light struggled through the grimy shop-window. ‘A most providential arrangement, this conjunction of the textile and the metallic! Iron gives the pivot, cotton gives the fleece; the one supports, the other envelops. We are a merger, my dear Mrs. Tottle, waiting to be ratified.’

Mr. Grout had a peculiar way of expressing himself. He spoke not of courtship, nor of love, nor of the tender affections; he spoke of mergers, of consolidations, of net profits, and of overlapping interests. He courted Millicent as he would negotiate a purchase of raw cotton, with a strict eye to the market price and a firm determination not to pay a farthing over the intrinsic value.

‘You have the fixtures, my dear madam; I have the fluid capital. You have the iron will, if I may be permitted the commercial jest; I have the soft goods. A union of our establishments could not fail to produce a most handsome dividend. I am prepared to offer you the honourable title of Mrs. Grout, a substantial jointure, and a complete absorption of your present liabilities—which, I have observed, are beginning to press somewhat heavily upon the iron trade.’

Millicent looked at him from the other side of the counter, where her hand rested on a cold, newly-forged poker. She did not tell him that the liabilities of the ironmongery were perfectly manageable, and that Joshua Tottle, though dead, had left his books in a state of methodical perfection. She merely looked at him, and thought how exceedingly like a suet pudding he looked, a suet pudding that had been left too long in the steam of the cotton-mill, and had grown heavy, and greasy, and entirely devoid of any spiritual nourishment.

‘I am greatly obliged by your proposal, Mr. Grout,’ said Millicent, with a quiet dignity that made the very copper saucepans seem to ring in sympathy. ‘But I am yet in my mourning.’

‘A most sensible and prudent consideration,’ returned Mr. Grout, nodding his large head with an air of profound approval. ‘Mourning is an expense, and economy is the root of wealth. We shall defer the final settlement until the expiration of the twelvemonth. The terms, however, may be considered as firmly established. I am not a man to fluctuate with the market.’

With which assurance, Mr. Grout took his departure, leaving Millicent to her hammers and her silence. She stood for a long time in the dim shop, listening to the distant, muffled roar of his cotton mills, that great beast of machinery which ate up the hours and the hands of the town, and ground out, with a monstrous and unceasing regularity, the vast wealth of Mr. Pecksniff Grout.

She thought of the merger. She thought of the great, red-brick house on the hill, where Mrs. Grout would sit in a parlour, draped in silks that smelled of the loom, and pour out tea for other cotton masters, and listen to talk of percentages and the price of bales. She thought of the security of it, the warmth, the freedom from the sharp east winds that rattled the windows of the ironmongery, and the sharp creditors who sometimes rattled at the door. It was a fortress, that red-brick house; and she was very tired of standing sentinel at the gate of her own little fortress, with nothing but a counter between her and the world.

But then, as she looked at the ranks of bright, cold steel, and the honest, heavy iron, a feeling of repulsion crept over her, which was not merely a dislike of Mr. Grout’s person, but a profound and stubborn revolt against his arithmetic of the soul. He had spoken of her heart as if it were an asset, of her loneliness as a liability, of her future as a dividend. He had reduced the grand and terrible mystery of human companionship to a cold commercial transaction, a merger of stocks, a consolidation of interests.
