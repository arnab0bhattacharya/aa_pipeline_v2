The rain in Geneva did not fall so much as it hung, a grey curtain that turned the cobblestones of the Rue de la Rive into a mirror of mud and shadow. Inside the lodging house, the air was thick with the scent of damp wool, boiled cabbage, and the sharp, metallic tang of fear. I sat in the corner of the common room, my hands wrapped around a cup of tea that had gone cold an hour ago. Across the table, the committee of three sat with the rigid posture of men who had already written their verdict and were merely waiting for the ink to dry.

Viktor Alekseyev, a man whose beard was as tangled and bitter as his outlook, sat at the head of the table. Beside him was Pyotr, a former seminarian whose eyes held a hollow, fanatical light, and behind them, the silent observer, a young man named Dmitri who looked at the floor as if seeking answers in the grain of the wood. They were not looking at me. They were looking at the door.

I was accused of being a spy for the Okhrana, the secret police. The evidence was circumstantial, a fact that mattered little to men who believed that innocence was a luxury reserved for the tsar’s court. I had been seen talking to a man named Kolya in the park three weeks prior. Kolya had been arrested the next day. Therefore, I was the mole. The logic was simple, brutal, and entirely flawed, but in the cramped, suffocating world of the Russian exile in Switzerland, logic was often the first casualty of paranoia.

The door creaked open. The room exhaled.

Kolya walked in.

He was a small man, unremarkable in every way, dressed in a coat that had seen better decades. He carried a wet umbrella that dripped onto the floorboards, a rhythmic *plip, plip, plip* that seemed to mark the heartbeat of the room. My stomach tightened. Kolya was the denouncer. Or so the committee believed. Kolya had walked into the local police station in Zurich and pointed a finger at me, saying I had provided names. It was a lie, a malicious fabrication born of a petty grudge over a shared room and a debt of three francs. But to Viktor and Pyotr, Kolya’s testimony was the hammer that broke the nail.

Kolya stopped in the center of the room. He looked at us, his eyes wide and watery, not with the pride of an accuser, but with the confusion of a man who had woken from a nightmare and could not remember the dream.

"Is he here?" Viktor asked, his voice low and gravelly.

Kolya nodded, pointing a trembling finger at me. "Yes. That is him."

Pyotr leaned forward, his lips thinning. "You said it was you who saw him meeting the agents. You saw the exchange of the list."

Kolya’s face twisted. A sweat broke out on his forehead despite the chill in the room. "I... I saw him talking to a man. In the park."

"And you are certain it was an agent?" Viktor pressed.

"I was told it was," Kolya whispered. "By... by someone."

"Who?"

"I do not know. A stranger. In the hotel lobby. He said you were a spy. He said if I reported it, I would be safe. I was afraid, comrades. I was so afraid."

The room went silent. The rain tapped against the window pane. I watched Kolya’s hands. They were shaking violently, clutching the fabric of his coat. He was not the confident agent of the police. He was a frightened, broken man.

Viktor’s eyes narrowed. He looked at me, then back at Kolya. "You are admitting you were coerced?"

"I was afraid," Kolya repeated, his voice rising in a panic. "I did not know if it was true. I just wanted to be safe. I wanted to leave. I thought if I helped... if I pointed a finger... I would be free."

Pyotr scoffed, a sharp, ugly sound. "Convenience is a powerful drug, Kolya. But we are not here to judge your fear. We are here to judge his guilt." He pointed at me. "You saw the exchange of papers. You saw the bribe."

Kolya looked at Pyotr, and for the first time, his eyes hardened. Not with fear, but with a sudden, dawning clarity. "No," Kolya said.

The word hung in the air, sharp and unexpected.

"No?" Viktor repeated, leaning back.

"I did not see any papers," Kolya said, his voice steadying, though it was thin. "I did not see any bribe. I saw him talking to a man who looked like a student. A young man. He was arguing about philosophy. I heard the word 'Nietzsche.' I heard him say, 'You cannot buy a soul with rubles, you can only corrupt it.' They were arguing. That was all."

My heart hammered against my ribs. I remembered that day. I had been arguing with a young radical who was obsessed with Nietzsche. We had been shouting, our voices raised in the park, drawing the attention of passersby. It had been a ridiculous, heated debate about the will to power and the state. I had no idea a stranger had been watching, let alone that the observation would be twisted into treason.

Pyotr’s face darkened. "You lie. You are recanting. You are part of the conspiracy."

"I am telling the truth!" Kolya shouted, slamming his hand on the table. The tea cups rattled. "I am a coward! I am a liar! But I am not a killer. I did not see a list. I saw two men arguing. I was afraid of being alone in this city, and I was afraid of the police, and I threw a man under the bus because it was easier than facing the dark. But I did not see what you say I saw."
