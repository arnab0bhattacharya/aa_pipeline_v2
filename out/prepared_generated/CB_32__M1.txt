I had set my hand to the plough, and I would not turn back. With the sum of one hundred and twenty pounds—a legacy from a great-aunt who had died in a fit of indigestion, and to whom I owed nothing but this last, accidental kindness—I took the lease of a house in Netherfold. It was a narrow stone building, squeezed between the chandler’s and the saddler’s, with a prospect of the churchyard and the grey, brooding fells beyond. I had it cleaned and whitewashed until it shone like a bleached bone. I bought twelve small desks, a map of the world, a globe, and a set of slates. Above the door I hung a board, freshly painted in my own hand: *Miss A. Vance, Academy for Young Persons.*

My ambition was not grand; it was fierce. I would teach these girls—daughters of the shopkeepers and the better sort of weaver—what I myself had had to beg and scrape for: the solid ground of grammar, the clear currents of history, the living truths of Scripture. I would not deal in the flimsy accomplishments that make a young lady a prize for a husband and a burden to herself. I would make them think. I would make them strong. For the first month, I had nine pupils. Nine! And I taught them as if they were ninety, my heart swelling with a righteous fire every time little Mary Shuttleworth mastered her declensions, or when pale Jane Sowerby spoke up with a flash of intelligence in her usually timid eyes. My schoolroom was my kingdom, and its purpose my religion.

Then, one Monday in October, a new board appeared across the cobbled street. It was broader than mine, and painted with a flourish I could never afford. *The Rector’s Academy for the Daughters of Gentlefolk.* The letters were a deep, unctuous black. It was Mr. Thorne’s doing. Mr. Thorne, the rector of St. Jude’s, a man whose piety was as broad and smooth as his face, and whose conscience was as carefully tailored as his coat. He had looked upon my little enterprise not as a work of merit, but as an insolence. A single woman, without patronage or connections, daring to open a school within sight of his own church? It was a challenge to the natural order of things.

The effect was as if a dam had burst. My nine pupils dwindled to four, then to two. Mrs. Shuttleworth, a woman whose bonnets grew more elaborate with every passing week, called one afternoon to withdraw Mary. She could not, she said, with a simpering apology, “go against the Rector’s wishes.” The injustice of it struck me like a physical blow. It was not that her school was better—her teachers were the faded, half-educated daughters of minor gentry, who taught embroidery and water-colours and called it learning. It was that it was *safe*. It had the blessing of the church, the nod of the gentry. My school had only my own desperate, grinding will behind it.

I stood at my window and watched the girls, in their bright bonnets and warm cloaks, troop into the Rector’s establishment. A storm was gathering over the fells, the sky turning the colour of a bruise. The wind, coming down from the moors, rattled the window-pane and moaned in the chimney. I felt a surge of hatred so pure and hot it frightened me. It was the hatred of the powerless for the powerful, of the just for the unjust. I was alone, and the world was arranged to crush me. I could go to him, I knew. I could walk across that street, enter his comfortable, book-lined study, and beg. I could plead my poverty, my need, my right to a living. But the very thought of it made the blood rush to my face and my hands clench into fists. To beg of that man would be to admit he was my master, to confess that my cause was a lost one. It would be to bow the knee to a Baal of social order, and I would not do it.

The last two pupils, the Sowerby sisters, were withdrawn before the week was out. Their father, a weaver whose hands were permanently stained with indigo, came to the door, his cap in his hands. “I’m sorry, Miss Vance,” he mumbled, his eyes fixed on the floor. “But he’s the Rector. He has the ear of the magistrates. A man in my position cannot afford to cross him.” I saw the fear in his face, and my anger against him died, replaced by a colder, harder fury against the system that made him a serf. I dismissed him with a nod, not trusting my voice, and closed the door on my empty schoolroom.
