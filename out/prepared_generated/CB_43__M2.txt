The rain lashed against the window-panes of the schoolroom, a ceaseless, furious drumming that matched the turmoil within my breast. I had been teaching the catechism, or so it appeared to the class; but my mind was far away, haunted by the sight of little Thomas Harker’s face. It was a face I could not forget: bruised, swollen, and pale with a terror so profound it seemed to have stripped the very soul from the child. He had whispered, in a voice so thin it was barely audible above the wind, that the overlooker had beaten him for spilling a measure of lint. “He struck me, teacher,” the boy had said, his eyes wide and fixed on some distant horror, “and Mr. Vane said it was good discipline.”

Good discipline. The words burned in my memory like a branding iron. I could not stay. The duty of the teacher is to instruct the mind, but the duty of the human heart is to protect the weak from the strong. I left the room, closing the door softly behind me, and descended into the street. The air in Millhaven was thick with the smell of wet wool and coal smoke, a heavy, oppressive blanket that settled on the lungs. I wrapped my shawl tighter around my shoulders, not from cold, though the November chill bit sharply, but from a shudder that ran through my bones. I walked towards the mill, my heart beating a frantic, irregular rhythm against my ribs.

The mill loomed against the grey sky, a vast, soot-stained monstrosity of brick and iron. Its wheels turned with a heavy, monotonous clank, a sound that seemed to grind the life out of the workers who shuffled in and out of its shadows. I paused at the gate, my hand trembling on the iron bar. Was it madness to go? I was a woman of no consequence, a governess in all but name, dependent on the goodwill of the household that employed me. Yet, if I turned back now, I knew I would never forgive myself. I would have betrayed the trust of that poor, battered child. I pushed the gate open and stepped onto the cobbled path.

I was shown into a waiting room that smelled of damp wool and stale tobacco. I sat there for what felt like an eternity, watching the dust motes dance in the shafts of weak light that pierced the curtains. Finally, a servant appeared and led me to a door at the end of a long corridor. It opened into a library, richly furnished but cold, the fire in the grate long dead. A young man sat by the window, his back to me. He turned as I entered.

It was Arthur Vane. He was a handsome man, with a face of classical correctness, though his eyes held a certain detached sharpness that I did not care for. He rose from his chair, bowing with a grace that felt performative.

“Mrs. Reed,” he said, his voice smooth and polite. “I was told you wished to see me. Please, sit down. The draught is unpleasant in this room.”

I did not sit. I stood rigid, my hands clenched at my sides. “I do not wish to sit, Mr. Vane. I wish to speak of an act of cruelty committed under your father’s roof.”

Arthur Vane’s eyebrows raised slightly, a flicker of amusement or perhaps irritation crossing his features. “Cruelty? That is a strong word. Pray, be precise. Who is the offender, and who is the victim?”

“Thomas Harker,” I said, my voice steady despite the tremor in my hands. “He is a boy in my Sunday class. He is twelve years old. Last night, the overlooker, Mr. Gird, beat him. He struck him with his hand, Mr. Vane, again and again, until the boy could no longer stand. And when Thomas cried out, Mr. Gird called it necessary correction.”

Arthur Vane leaned back in his chair, steepling his fingers. “I am sorry to hear that. I trust Mr. Gird is not aware of the extent of the injury. If the boy is merely bruised, it is a regrettable incident, but not a criminal one. Discipline, however harsh, is often necessary in a mill. The work is demanding; the children must be kept in check.”

“Discipline?” I cried, the word rising in my throat with sudden, fierce heat. “You call the beating of a child discipline? You call it necessary? If a man of your station were struck by a servant, you would not call it discipline. You would call it assault. You would demand justice. But because the victim is a child, and because the perpetrator holds a position of power, it is to be dismissed as an accident of the trade?”

Mr. Vane’s face hardened. The polite mask slipped, revealing a cold, imperious stare. “Mrs. Reed, you are overstepping your bounds. You are a teacher, not a magistrate. You have no authority here. My father runs this mill. He knows the character of his men. Mr. Gird is a trusted employee. To suggest otherwise is to cast aspersions on the management of this house, which I am not prepared to tolerate.”

“I am not casting aspersions,” I said, my voice dropping to a low, intense register. “I am speaking the truth. I saw the boy’s face. I saw the blood. I saw the fear. And I tell you, Mr. Vane, that there is a God who sees it too. He sees the bruised cheek and the broken spirit. He sees the hand that strikes and the heart that permits it. Do you think He is blind to such injustice? Do you think He does not hold the accounts of the rich and the powerful?”
