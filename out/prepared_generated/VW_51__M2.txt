The light in Kensington Gardens did not fall so much as it hovered, a pale, trembling gold that seemed to suspend the dust motes in the air, making the world appear both immense and impossibly fragile. Lila sat on the wooden bench, her fingers tracing the grain of the wood, feeling the roughness of it, the way it had weathered the years, just as her own skin had weathered the last two years of silence and sudden, violent noise. Her son, Arthur, was three feet away, or perhaps ten; distance was a fluid thing here, measured not in yards but in the intensity of his laughter. He was chasing a white butterfly, a blur of yellow wool and brown hair, his small legs pumping with a frantic, purposeless energy that reminded Lila, with a pang that was less of pain than of recognition, of a bird caught in a wire.

She watched him, and in watching him, she saw her own mother. It was a trick of the light, or perhaps a trick of the mind, but for a moment the little boy with the red face was not Arthur but her mother, young, before the illness, before the withdrawal into that grey room where the curtains were always drawn. Her mother had loved the park, or so Lila remembered, though the memory was hazy, edged in mist. She had loved the smell of the damp earth, the way the willows dipped their heads like penitents. And now, looking at Arthur, Lila felt a strange, disjointed sorrow, not for what was, but for what might have been.

What might have been. The phrase floated in the air, unmoored, like the leaves that were just beginning to tremble on the maples. The book. She had meant to write it. It was not a grand ambition, not a thing for the world to judge, but a private necessity, a way of ordering the chaos of the soul. She had bought the paper, the good, heavy cream-colored sheets, and the ink had been a deep, midnight blue. She had sat at the table in the flat on Sloane Street, the light coming in through the window, and she had waited for the words. They had not come. They had gathered, perhaps, in the back of her mind, a crowd of ghosts pressing against the glass, but they would not step through. Instead, there was the washing, the cooking, the endless, circular dance of domesticity that swallowed the hours whole.

And then there was Julian. She thought of him not with anger, for anger was a hot, bright thing, and what she felt was colder, a kind of grey numbness. He was at the office now, or perhaps at the club, drinking sherry with the men who spoke of politics and horses and the price of grain. He was a good man, they all said. He was kind. He brought her flowers, sometimes, a single rose, pink as a sunset, which he would place on the mantelpiece and then forget, letting it wilt and drop its petals onto the carpet. Lila would pick them up, one by one, and place them in a jar on the sill, a small, sad archive of missed connections. He did not understand the book. He did not understand the need to say the things that could not be said, to capture the fleeting, elusive moment when one was truly alive. To him, life was a structure, a thing to be managed, a balance sheet to be balanced. To Lila, it was a river, and she was trying to catch the water in her hands, only to watch it slip through her fingers, leaving behind a cold, wetness that evaporated before it could be named.

Arthur stopped running. He stood still, his breath coming in little puffs of white in the cool air, and he looked at her. His eyes were wide, dark, and serious, holding a depth that was impossible for a child of his age. For a second, Lila felt a shock, a vibration that ran through her bones. He was not just a child; he was a bridge, a link to a time she had not yet lived, a future that was already present in the curve of his spine, the set of his jaw. She wondered who he would be. Would he be like Julian, solid, grounded, or would he be like her, drifting, searching for a shape that was never quite there? She wondered if he would remember this morning. He would not. This moment, with the light and the silence and the weight of her thoughts, would dissolve into the ether, replaced by the next, and the next, until the thread was pulled so tight it snapped.

A woman in a green dress walked past, holding a basket of flowers. She hummed a tune, a fragment of a waltz, and the sound hung in the air, fragile and sweet. Lila followed the sound with her eyes, watching the woman’s back, the sway of her hips, the casual grace of her movement. It was a life, another life, moving along its own track, parallel to hers, never intersecting. They were all islands, Lila thought, floating in the sea of time, separated by water, connected by the same sky.

The wind picked up, rustling the leaves, making a sound like whispering. Arthur turned and began to run again, this time towards the pond, where the ducks were paddling in the green water. Lila watched him go, and she felt a sudden, sharp desire to follow, to be part of the movement, to escape the stillness of the bench. But she did not move. She sat, her hands in her lap, her fingers intertwined, feeling the pulse in her wrist, a steady, rhythmic beat that was the only thing that was real, the only thing that was constant.
