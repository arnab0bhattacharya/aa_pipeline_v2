For the bell had struck one; and here she sat, eating her sandwiches, with the crumbs falling softly, rather softly, onto her lap, and the pigeons, plump and grey, edging nearer with their ridiculous, mechanical nods, as if they knew, as if they had calculated to the very crumb the precise moment she must drop a morsel. The bread was dry; the butter tasted faintly of the paper in which it had been wrapped all morning in the drawer of her desk at Brenchfield & Smale’s; but it was food, and the air was sweet, surprisingly sweet, for a city churchyard in October. Overhead the branches of the plane trees were stripping themselves, leaf by leaf—yellow, and some quite brown, curling at the edges—and the sky above the spire was a pale, washed-out blue, the sort of blue one sees in old watercolours left too long in the sun. And she was twenty-one; and it was her lunch hour; and she had been asked to the theatre.

She bit into the sandwich (it was egg and cress) and chewed slowly, watching a pigeon strut across the gravel with an air of immense self-consequence, its throat catching the light in a sheen of purple and green, iridescent, absurdly vain for a bird that lived on crumbs and cigarette ends. There were clerks eating too, scattered about the benches, hunched over their paper bags, and one old man in a threadbare coat sitting quite still with his hands folded on his knees, gazing at nothing—or at the war memorial, perhaps, at the names carved there, though his eyes seemed too clouded, too far away, to be reading anything so precise as names. He might have been thinking of Flanders, or of nothing at all; one could not know. That was the thing about people—you saw them for a moment, fixed in some attitude, and they seemed entire, complete, a whole life contained in the droop of a shoulder or the angle of a hat; and then they moved, or the light changed, and they became strangers again, opaque, inaccessible. She wiped her fingers on her handkerchief. The old man did not move.

She had been asked to the theatre. That was the thing. Not by anyone she had known long—only by a young man called Martin, Martin Reeve, who had come to the office on Tuesday with a delivery of carbon slips from Craddock & Vine’s and had lingered, leaning against the doorframe of the typing room, twisting the string of his parcel round and round his finger, until Miss Pringle had looked up over her spectacles with that expression she wore, the one that could freeze the ink in the fountain pens. He had asked her on Wednesday. He had stood by the filing cabinets, smelling very faintly of cigarettes and something else, something warm and sharp, like damp wool, and had said, “I don’t suppose—” and then had stopped, and started again, “That is—I’ve got two tickets, for Saturday, if you’d care to—” And she had said, “I’ll think about it,” which was absurd, because what was there to think about? One went to the theatre or one did not. It was not, after all, a matter of any great consequence. A play. Two seats in the gallery, probably, or the pit. An evening. And yet she had said she would think about it, and she had been thinking about it ever since, turning it over in her mind the way one turns a coin, examining first one face and then the other, and finding on neither side anything that could properly be called a reason.

For what did she know of him? That he was tall, or rather, that he stood in a way that made him seem tall, inclining forward slightly, as if always on the point of leaving. That he had brown hair, cut short, and a way of smiling that showed his lower teeth, which were slightly crooked. That he worked at Craddock & Vine’s, which was not Brenchfield & Smale’s but was respectable enough—she thought so; she hoped so. That he had laughed at something she had said about Mr. Brenchfield’s moustache, a remark she had not realised was funny until he laughed, and then it had seemed funny indeed, terribly funny, so that she had laughed too, and Miss Pringle had looked up again, and the room had contracted to its usual size, and she had felt the heat rise in her cheeks. Was that enough to go on? Was that enough to build an evening on, to sit beside him in the dark and watch figures moving on a stage, speaking words written by someone else, while beside her his knee would be, perhaps, an inch from hers, and she would be conscious of it the whole time, the warmth of it, the nearness, so that the play would become a blur, a noise, and she would not be able to say afterwards what it had been about?

A pigeon had got hold of a crust and was dragging it across the stones with a kind of desperate, comical determination, and another pigeon was following, hoping, no doubt, for a share; and the old man on the bench had shifted at last, was fumbling in his pocket for something—a coin? a sweet?—and had brought out a small piece of bread, which he threw with a gesture at once gentle and absent-minded, as though his hand remembered a kindness his mind had forgotten. The pigeons converged. The gravel was suddenly alive with them, a heaving, pecking confusion of grey and purple and white.

And if she went? If she put on her good blouse, the cream one with the lace at the collar, and met him at half past six at the corner of Bouverie Street—if she did that, then what?
