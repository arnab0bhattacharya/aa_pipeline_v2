The rain fell upon the slate roofs of Millbrook with a relentless, drumming persistence, as if the sky itself were agitated by the same restless energy that churned within my breast. I sat at the window of the small, damp room I shared with my fellow apprentices, the needle hovering in my fingers, the thread trailing like a pale vein of light against the grey curtain. It was late November, and the wind howled through the chasms of the old mill-wheel, a sound that seemed to echo the hollow ache in my own chest.

Mrs. Gable, my mistress, had come in an hour ago, bearing a tray of cold tea and a look of such weary sympathy that it nearly broke me. She was a woman of no great beauty, her face lined by the same poverty that bound us all, yet her eyes held a gentleness that was rarer than gold. She had placed a shilling on the table—not for me, but for the coal-bill that was overdue. She had said nothing of the letter that had arrived that morning, a letter bearing the seal of Lady Harrow, but I had seen it. I had seen the way her hands trembled as she tore the envelope, and I had heard the sharp, strained quality of her voice when she read the terms aloud to the empty room.

"To be a companion," she had whispered, the words hanging in the cold air like smoke, "and to have her own room. No needle-work. No scrubbing. Just... reading. Conversation."

I had looked up then, my heart beating so violently I feared it might burst through my ribs. It was a dream, surely. A fever-dream brought on by the hunger that had gnawed at my stomach since the last meal. To leave the institution where I had been raised, that dark, stifling place where my youth had been traded for board and a coarse education, to enter a house of light and leisure? It was too good to be true. It was a trap.

Lady Harrow was a figure of local legend. A widow of considerable means and eccentric temperament, she was said to be cruel in her kindness, testing the souls of her servants with subtle cruelties, rewarding devotion with silence and punishing a misplaced glance with icy disdain. To accept her patronage was to step into a web from which there might be no escape, save in the heart.

"Ellen," Mrs. Gable said, her voice soft but firm, cutting through my reverie. She stood by the door, her shawl pulled tight against the draft. "You have not answered me. Are you to go?"

I set down my needle. The click of the steel against the wooden table sounded obscenely loud in the quiet room. "I do not know, Mrs. Gable. I am afraid."

"Of what?" she asked, sitting down heavily on the edge of my bed. The mattress sagged under her weight, a familiar, depressing sound. "Of Lady Harrow? Or of leaving us?"

"Of both," I admitted. "And of neither. I feel... unmoored. If I stay, I remain in the dark, but the dark is known to me. If I go, I step into a light that I cannot see. What if I am not worthy of it? What if I am only a curiosity to her? A pet to be caged and admired, then discarded when the novelty fades?"

Mrs. Gable reached out and took my hand. Her fingers were rough, calloused from years of mending and washing, but her grip was warm. "Ellen, you are not a curiosity. You are a woman with a mind that does not let her rest. I see it in the way you read those tattered books by the fire when you think no one is looking. I see it in the way you speak of justice, even when the world offers you nothing but injustice. Lady Harrow may be strange, but she is not blind. She chose you. Why do you doubt her sight more than your own worth?"

"I have been taught that gratitude is a debt to be paid in service," I said, my voice tightening. "And I have no desire to pay a debt I did not incur. But Mrs. Gable, if I leave, who will help you? Who will mend the rags when your own hands are too swollen to hold the needle?"

She looked away, towards the window where the rain streaked the glass. "I shall manage. I have always managed. That is the burden of the kind, isn’t it? We must endure, so that others may rise above us. But Ellen, do not mistake endurance for happiness. I do not ask you to stay out of pity. I ask you to go, if you believe you can bear the risk."

The risk. The word sat heavy in the room. I thought of the years at the institution, the cold baths, the sharp tongues of the matrons who saw in us not children, but assets to be managed. I had survived by keeping my spirit locked in a high tower, accessible only to me. To open that tower now, to offer it to a stranger, to a woman who might be kind or might be cold, was a terrifying leap.

But what was I to do? Stay here, in this damp room, my hands stiffening from the cold, my mind slowly dulling like a blade left in the rain? I had seen the future in the eyes of the older apprentices: a life of quiet resignation, of unloved marriages or spinsterhood, of watching the world pass by while they mended the tears in their own souls. I was not made for that. I had a fire in me that burned too brightly for such a small hearth.

"I will go," I said. The words came out before I had fully formed the thought, sharp and decisive.
