I had taken a small house in Flask Walk, Hampstead, for the season, chiefly on account of my wife’s nerves, which the London particular—that yellow-brown canopy of fog and smoke—had sadly disordered. It was a quiet, genteel neighbourhood, of the sort where a man may walk abroad of a morning in his slippers without fear of being thought eccentric, and where the chief topics of conversation are the price of coals and the misdemeanours of the local sweep. My own practice—for I was then in the third year of a modest general surgery—permitted me to be much at home, and it was thus that I came to observe the small drama which I shall here set down.

Our immediate neighbours were a Mr. and Mrs. Aldous Crane. Crane was a wine merchant of some standing, with a warehouse in the Borough and a counting-house off Cheapside. He was a florid, hearty man of perhaps five-and-forty, given to loud laughter and the wearing of a heavy gold watch-chain, and he passed for a good fellow among the merchants with whom he dined. His wife, Edith, was some fifteen years his junior, a pale, thoughtful woman with a habit of watching her husband when he was not looking, and of falling silent whenever his name was mentioned. They had been married, I understood, some four years, and there were no children.

It was on a Tuesday in early November that Mrs. Crane called upon us. My wife was out; the maid showed her into my consulting-room, and I saw at once that she had come upon a matter of some gravity, for her hands were trembling and she had the air of one who has rehearsed a speech a hundred times and forgotten it at the crucial moment.

“Dr. Merriwether,” said she, “I am told you are discreet, and I have no one else to whom I can turn. It is not a medical matter. It is—it is my husband.”

I begged her to be seated and to compose herself, and in a little while, with many hesitations and one or two starts towards the door, she told her story.

Some weeks previously, while seeking a receipt among her husband’s papers—he had promised to settle an account with the draper, and had forgot—she had come upon a small japanned deed-box in the bottom of his wardrobe, a box she had never seen before. It was locked, but the key was in the lock, and her curiosity, she confessed, overcame her scruples. Within were two sets of account books. One set she recognised as the books of the business, which she had occasionally seen upon his desk. The other set, bound in the same fashion and kept with the same care, bore entries of a wholly different character: sums of money in and out, in a hand she did not know, with initials and dates and no names. There was, besides, a folded plan of some building, and a bundle of letters tied with tape, which she had not the courage to open.

“And the key?” I asked.

“That is the strangest part of all, Doctor. Upon the same ring as the key of the deed-box there was a second key—a heavy, old-fashioned key, such as opens a warehouse or a strong-room. I have tried it upon every lock in the house, and it fits nothing. I have asked Crane, in as careless a manner as I could manage, whether he had ever lost a key, and he laughed and said he had lost a dozen in his time, and thought no more of it. But I saw his face when I said it, and there was a flicker in it—a watchfulness—that frightened me more than any anger could have done.”

“You fear—” I began.

“I fear scandal,” she said, and her voice was steady now, and very low. “I fear disgrace, and the ruin of his name, and the shame of it in the newspapers. I do not fear the truth, Doctor, whatever it may be. It is the not knowing that is unendurable. If he is in some difficulty—some entanglement of business—I would help him if I could. But I cannot ask him, for he would lie to me, and I should know that he lied, and after that there would be nothing left.”

I confess that my first thought was to counsel her to go home and say nothing, for I am not by nature a meddler, and the affairs of a husband and wife are a country into which a stranger enters at his peril. But there was a dignity in her distress which forbade the easy answer, and I bethought me of a man who had once done me a service—a man, moreover, whose peculiar gifts were exactly suited to such a tangle.

“I know of an investigator,” said I, “who is not of the common stamp. He is not a policeman, nor a private enquiry agent of the vulgar sort. He is a philosopher who has made the study of evidence his life’s work, and he is the most discreet man in London. His name is Sebastian Vane. If you will permit me, I will write to him this afternoon.”

She permitted me, and two days later Mr. Sebastian Vane came down to Hampstead.

He was a tall, spare man, with a long, thin face, a high forehead, and grey eyes that had a way of fixing upon a thing—a chair, a glove, a smudge upon a wall—as though it were a text and he a scholar deciphering it. He wore a soft felt hat and a cape-coat of a brownish grey, and he carried a walking-stick which he used rather as a pointer than a support. I had prepared tea in my consulting-room, and Mrs. Crane was present, having come by the garden gate to avoid observation.

Vane listened to her recital without interruption, his chin sunk upon his hand, his eyes half closed. When she had done, he asked for the key.
