It was exactly half-past two on a bright Tuesday afternoon when young Cubberley sneezed. He was standing at the time on the marble paving of the Grand Concourse in Whitestone, a perfectly proportioned young man of twenty-four, engaged in the altogether wholesome business of buying a new hat. The sneeze was a violent one. It jerked his head backward, caused him to drop his silk parasol, and produced a fine, moist spray that settled in a barely perceptible dew upon the polished shoulder of the municipal automaton standing nearby.

Cubberley himself was rather shocked. He looked about him with wide eyes, dabbed at his nose with a handkerchief, and then, feeling a strange tickling irritation at the back of his throat, coughed. It was a rattling, uneven cough, quite unlike the polite clearing of the throat one might use to attract a shop assistant’s attention. The automaton, a shining contrivance of brass and glass tasked with directing pedestrian traffic, merely swiveled its optical lenses toward him and clicked its internal cogs in mild, programmed reproof.

People did not sneeze in Whitestone. They had not sneezed, or coughed, or run a temperature, for the better part of a hundred years. The last recorded instance of a febrile condition had been logged in the Central Registry at Guildford in the year of the Great Purge. Since then, the entire apparatus of medical science—which had once loomed so large in the national life, with its hospitals, its surgeries, and its endless, fussy practitioners—had been dismantled. The buildings had been converted into gymnasiums and lecture halls for mechanical engineering. The very vocabulary of illness had faded into a quaint antiquity, retaining only a metaphorical significance. When a person said they felt “liverish,” they meant they were mildly out of sorts with the weather; nobody had the faintest notion where the liver actually was.

By the time Cubberley reached his rooms in the Seventh Quadrangle, however, metaphor was quite inadequate to his condition. He had walked the distance of a mile under a perfectly agreeable sun, yet he found himself shivering violently. His joints, particularly his knees and the knuckles of his hands, ached with a dull, persistent throb. His forehead, when he pressed the back of his hand to it, felt uncommonly hot. He sat down heavily in his armchair, entirely forgetting the new hat, and stared at the wall with a sort of bewildered indignation.

“What the devil,” said Cubberley to the empty room, “is the matter with me?”

By evening, the matter was considerably worse. His throat felt as though he had swallowed a quantity of broken glass, and his temperature had climbed to a degree that made his vision swim. He sent a pneumatic message to his cousin, Mrs. Bunting, who arrived shortly after eight o’clock, looking distinctly alarmed.

“You look perfectly dreadful, Arthur,” she said, standing well back from his chair. “Your face is the color of a ripe tomato. What have you been eating?”

“Nothing out of the ordinary,” Cubberley managed, his voice a mere croak. “I am hot, Polly. I am hot, and I am cold, and my bones ache. I think I must have been poisoned.”

“Nonsense,” said Mrs. Bunting, though she did not advance. “Nobody is poisoned nowadays. The food inspectors are entirely too efficient. You must have sat in a draught.”

“There are no draughts in Whitestone,” Cubberley retorted, irritably. “The ventilation is calculated to a fraction of a cubic foot. I tell you, I am ill.”

The word hung in the air between them like an obscenity. Mrs. Bunting’s eyes widened. She took another step backward.

“Ill?” she repeated, in a whisper. “You can’t be ill. There isn’t such a thing.”

“Then what do you call this?” he demanded, and was seized by another racking cough that left him gasping.

Mrs. Bunting did not know what to call it. She did what any sensible, modern woman of the era would do under the circumstances: she sent for the Secretary of the Local Board of Hygiene.

The Secretary, a Mr. Voules, was a brisk, practical man with a bald head and an absolute faith in municipal regulations. He arrived at ten o’clock, wearing a heavy overcoat against the evening chill, and carrying a notebook. He looked at Cubberley, listened to his chest with a small, metallic stethoscope he had once seen in a museum and subsequently purchased as a curiosity, and then sat down at the table to write.

“Temperature elevated,” Mr. Voules muttered, scribbling. “Pulse rapid. Respiration labored. Discoloration of the mucous membranes.” He looked up. “Have you been tampering with any old drains, Cubberley? Disturbing ancient refuse?”

“I have been buying a hat,” said Cubberley, faintly.

“H’m,” said Mr. Voules. “Well, it is certainly some form of disorder. I suppose we must isolate you. I shall have the Quadrangle sealed by morning.”

“Isolate me?” Cubberley protested, sitting up with a sudden, feverish energy. “I don’t want to be isolated. I want someone to make me well. Isn’t there anyone who knows how to treat this?”

Mr. Voules looked genuinely puzzled. “Treat it? My dear fellow, there are no treatments. There are no treatments because there are no ailments. We abolished them all, you know, in the ’Twenties. The whole science of therapeutics was declared obsolete by the Imperial Council. There is a great deal of literature on the subject of municipal sanitation, but as for putting things right once they’ve gone wrong with the human body—well, I shouldn’t know where to begin.”

“But I am in pain!” Cubberley cried.

“Yes,” said Mr. Voules, thoughtfully. “Yes, I can see that you are. Most extraordinary. I shall have to report it to the Central Office in London. They will probably send down a commission.”

The commission arrived the following day, traveling by the express pneumatic tube from the capital. It consisted of three elderly men in dark uniforms, carrying leather cases filled with instruments whose uses they could only vaguely conjecture. They entered Cubberley’s rooms wearing thick rubber gloves and masks of gauze, which gave them the appearance of gigantic, spectrally pale insects. Cubberley, whose fever had now reached a dangerous pitch, watched them from his bed with bloodshot, terrified eyes.
