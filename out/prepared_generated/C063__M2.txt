The air in the sitting room of the house on Waverley Crescent hung thick with the scent of beeswax and stale lavender. Outside, the London rain lashed against the sash windows, blurring the gaslights of Bayswater into soft, weeping halos of amber. Inside, the only light came from a single tallow candle burning in the center of the mahogany table, its flame trembling not from a draft, but from the sheer, suffocating tension of the three men seated around it.

Aunt Cordelia stood at the head of the table, her eyes rolled back into her skull, her lips moving in a silent, frantic prayer. She was a woman of sharp angles and sharper opinions, dressed in a mourning dress that had been fashionable two decades prior. To the outside world, she was the eccentric widow of a minor diplomat; to her family, she was a nuisance who insisted on communicating with the dead. Her nephews, Arthur, Julian, and Silas, had come for the weekend to attend the funeral of a distant cousin, but they had been dragged into this ritual by Cordelia’s unyielding demand for a "spiritual cleansing."

"Be still," Cordelia whispered, her voice sounding like dry leaves skittering over pavement. "The veil is thin. He is coming."

Julian, the eldest, shifted in his chair, the leather creaking loudly in the silence. He caught Silas’s eye across the table. The younger brother was pale, his fingers drumming a nervous rhythm on the mahogany. Arthur, the quietest of the three, simply stared at the candle, his face a mask of polite skepticism. They had all been raised in the shadow of Grandfather Aldous, a man of immense wealth and terrifying sternness who had died five years prior. His memory was a mix of cold dinners and strict discipline. The idea that his spirit would return to demand confessions was, to them, absurd. It was a trick. Someone was playing them.

Cordelia’s hand began to tremble. Then, without warning, it shot out, rigid and pointing directly at the empty corner of the room where the grandfather’s old armchair sat, draped in a sheet.

"Who is there?" Julian asked, his voice steady, though his heart hammered against his ribs.

The room temperature seemed to drop ten degrees. The candle flame turned blue. A voice, low and gravelly, filled the air. It did not come from any specific direction; it seemed to vibrate in the walls, in the floorboards, in the very bones of the men.

"I am Aldous. And I am not pleased."

Cordelia gasped, dropping to her knees, tears streaming down her face. "Father," she sobbed. "I have called you."

The voice paused. The silence that followed was heavier than the rain outside. "You have called me," the spirit said, "but you have not cleansed them. Look at them. They are heavy with secrets. They carry the weight of my house, and they are rotting it from the inside."

Arthur leaned forward, his brow furrowed. "Grandfather," he said, his voice calm. "If you are truly here, then you know we are your grandsons. We have done nothing to offend you."

The voice laughed, a dry, rattling sound. "Nothing? Is that what you think? Sin is not always a grand crime, boy. It is the lie told to protect the self. It is the resentment swallowed. It is the truth left unspoken. Confess."

Julian scoffed, leaning back in his chair. "This is ridiculous. Aunt Cordelia, who is doing this? Is it the new maid? The gardener? We have a parlor trick going on here."

"The spirit does not lie," Cordelia wailed, still on her knees. "You must confess, or the house will be cursed. I feel the chill. I feel his anger."

Silas, who had been pale, now looked terrified. He looked at his hands, then at his brothers. "I..." he started, then stopped. He swallowed hard. "I stole from the family account. Three years ago. I gambled it all away in Paris. I told you I had lost it in a business venture. I have been paying it back slowly, but I never told you the truth."

The room went silent. The candle flame snapped back to a bright, steady yellow.

"Silas," Julian said, his voice cold. "You told us you were broke. You said the venture failed."

"I lied," Silas whispered, his head bowed. "I am ashamed."

The spirit’s voice returned, softer now, but no less commanding. "One more. Two more."

Arthur looked at Julian. Julian looked at the floor. The atmosphere in the room had shifted. The suspicion that someone was hiding in the shadows had faded, replaced by a creeping, genuine dread. If Silas’s confession had been real, then the voice was real. Or, at least, the pressure was real.

"I have resented him," Julian said, his voice barely audible. He pointed at Silas. "I have resented him for being the favorite. I have resented you, Arthur, for being the obedient one. I have spent years crafting stories to make myself look better, to make you look worse. I have manipulated situations to ensure I was the one who got the approval. I am proud of nothing, but I am proud of my lies."

Arthur stared at his hands. He had always been the quiet one, the one who observed. He had never spoken against the family. But in the darkness of the room, with the ghost of his grandfather demanding truth, he realized how hollow his silence had been.

"I have judged," Arthur said, his voice cracking. "I have judged everyone in this room. I have judged my aunt for her madness. I have judged Silas for his weakness. I have judged Julian for his arrogance. I have kept my distance, not out of shyness, but out of a belief that I was superior to them. I have watched you suffer and said nothing, because I thought your pain was your own fault."
