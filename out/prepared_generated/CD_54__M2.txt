The axle of the great touring wagon, which had carried the illustrious company of *The Rattling Bell* across the breadth of England with a dignity that suggested it was merely transporting the crown jewels rather than a prop forest and a few barrels of sawdust, had surrendered its spirit with a crack like a pistol shot. This occurred on the muddy, sludge-choked outskirts of Grimsworth, a town so thoroughly impregnated with the soot of its own industry that even the rain, when it deigned to fall, seemed to arrive already grey and weary, as if it had been scrubbing floors in the basement of the world. The company, led by the flamboyant and perpetually flushed Mr. Barnaby Vane, found themselves stranded in a yard that smelled of wet ash and despair, with their playbills fluttering in the wind like the broken wings of unfortunate birds.

It was Mr. Vane, with a voice that could shatter a teacup at fifty paces, who proposed the solution. He stood upon a broken crate, addressing his shivering actors with the gravity of a general before a siege. "We shall play," he declared, his eyes twinkling with a manic light that was either inspiration or a sign of impending madness, "we shall play *The Tyrant of the Loom!* It is our masterpiece. It is our salvation. It is, I dare say, the only thing in this wretched town that has not yet been sold, seized, or broken."

The play, it must be understood, was a melodrama of such potent venom that it made the air in the theatre feel thick as porridge. It depicted a mill owner of such insatiable greed and such grotesque physical proportions that he resembled a sausage stuffed with coal, who starved his workers, beat his wife, and ultimately drowned his own son in a vat of dye because the boy had the audacity to ask for an extra half-penny. It was a piece of art so specifically tailored to the grievances of the working man that it seemed less a creation of the imagination and more a transcription of the collective nightmares of Grimsworth.

The company set about their preparations with a frantic energy. The stage, which had been stripped from the wagon, was erected in the open square, where the cobblestones were slick with the grime of the mill chimneys. The backdrop, a painted representation of a factory interior, was hung between two leaning poles, and the actors, dressed in rags that were intended to represent poverty but looked suspiciously like their own discarded Sunday best, rehearsed their lines with a fervour that bordered on religious ecstasy.

Into this chaotic scene strode Mr. Silas Blackwood, the proprietor of the Grimsworth Iron and Cotton Works. He was a man of medium height, medium width, and medium intelligence, possessing a face that was not ugly, exactly, but was arranged in such a way that it suggested a permanent state of mild irritation at the very existence of the sun. He wore a top hat that sat slightly askew, as if it had been placed there by a careless hand, and he carried a cane of blackthorn which he tapped against the pavement with the rhythmic precision of a metronome counting out misery.

"Vane!" he bellowed, his voice cutting through the rehearsal like a knife through cold butter. "You insolent, itinerant vagabond! I have heard that you intend to perform this... this *farce* in my town."

Mr. Vane turned, his face a mask of theatrical indignation. "Sir, it is not a farce. It is a mirror. It is a reflection of the truth, polished and presented for the edification of the public. We are here to pay for the repair of our axle, and we do so through the honest labour of our art."

"Art!" Blackwood sneered, the word seeming to sour in his mouth. "You call it art. I call it sedition. I call it poison. I forbid it. I forbid every man, woman, and child in my employ from setting foot within that wretched enclosure. If any of my hands are seen watching your performance, they shall be discharged. Immediately. Without pension. Without reference. They shall be cast out into the street to beg for the very crumbs that you are so eager to distribute."

The actors stared at him, their mouths open in silent, comic shock. This was a threat of such magnitude, such absolute and crushing power, that it silenced even the most heated of the ensemble. The crowd, which had begun to gather at the edges of the square, fell silent, a hush so profound that one could hear the distant, grinding roar of the mill wheels, a sound that served as the heartbeat of the town.

Mr. Vane looked at Blackwood. He looked at his actors. He looked at the broken axle of the wagon, lying in the mud like a severed limb. And then, with a smile that did not reach his eyes, he turned back to Blackwood.

"Very well, sir," Vane said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper that somehow carried to the back of the crowd. "If you forbid them, then we shall not play. We shall remain here, in the rain, and we shall do nothing. But I give you my word, and I am a man of my word, though I am a poor one, that the play will be written on the walls. The story of the Tyrant of the Loom will not be confined to the stage. It will be in the eyes of the men who work for you. It will be in the silence of the women who wait for them. It will be in the very soot that coats their lungs. You can stop the play, Mr. Blackwood, but you cannot stop the truth. And the truth, sir, is a thing that grows in the dark, and it feeds on silence."
