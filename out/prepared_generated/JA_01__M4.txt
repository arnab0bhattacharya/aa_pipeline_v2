The business of the morning at Hartwell Place was scarcely begun before Mrs. Ellison had arranged the whole of her daughter’s future to her own satisfaction, and had begun to lament, with a mother’s tenderness, that the eldest Miss Ellison should be so little considered by Providence as to have reached three-and-twenty without a single offer. It was a complaint she made often, and always in the presence of the young lady in question, as though repetition might at last provoke the heavens into amendment.

“For my part,” said Mrs. Ellison, settling her shawl against the morning chill, “I do not know what young men are coming to. In my day, a girl with half your sister’s understanding would not have wanted for admirers above a twelvemonth. But there—we live so out of the world at Hartwell.”

“We live very comfortably, ma’am,” said Miss Ellison, without looking up from her work.

“Comfortably! Yes, comfortably. That is a word for it.” Mrs. Ellison sighed, and turned her attention to her younger daughter, who was standing at the window in an attitude of great apparent absorption in the drive. “And you, Harriet, need not imagine that your own prospects are any better for all your fine airs. Mr. Ashby is to dine with us, and I desire you will be civil to him.”

“I am always civil,” said Harriet.

“You are always something,” returned her mother, “which is not quite the same thing.”

Mr. Ashby was a gentleman of very considerable property in the neighbouring parish, of a personable figure and a most unexceptionable pedigree, whose conversation had the singular quality of being at once inexhaustible and entirely without incident. He had dined at Hartwell Place a great many times, and on every occasion had spoken to Harriet of the weather, of the roads, of the price of wheat, and of a certain grey mare of his which he was pleased to consider a phenomenon. He was universally esteemed a very worthy man, and Harriet had long since concluded that worthiness, however excellent a thing in the abstract, might be rendered intolerable by an excess of application.

The curate was of a different order. Mr. Leighton had come to the parish some eight months before, with a small stipend, a great deal of learning, and a manner which the neighbourhood found it convenient to call odd. He was not handsome, and he was not rich, and he had a habit of saying exactly what he thought, which Mrs. Ellison pronounced to be a want of address and which Harriet had privately decided to be the most agreeable quality a man could possess. When he spoke to her—which he did seldom, and never for long—she was sensible of a quickness in her own mind which no other company had ever produced; and when he was gone, the drawing-room at Hartwell seemed to her a very large and very empty apartment.

“I cannot think,” said Miss Ellison, when her mother had quitted the room, “why you encourage Mr. Leighton as you do. It is not kind to raise expectations which can never be answered.”

“I have raised none,” said Harriet. “He has never spoken to me of anything but books and the parish.”

“And that, in your opinion, is conversation.” Miss Ellison smiled, and smoothed her work upon her knee. “My dear Harriet, you are too romantic. A man with three hundred a year and a curacy may talk of books as long as he pleases; it will not furnish a house, nor keep a carriage, nor make you the equal of your neighbours. Mr. Ashby, on the other hand, has everything that a reasonable woman could desire.”

“Everything but the power to entertain me for a quarter of an hour together.”

“Entertainment!” cried Miss Ellison, with a little laugh. “You speak as though marriage were a play. I assure you, a woman of sense learns to be entertained by her duties. Mr. Ashby is a man of excellent principles; he is well received everywhere; and his estate is not entailed. I wonder you do not see it.”

Harriet did see it, and saw, moreover, a good deal more than her sister intended. For it had not escaped her observation that Mr. Ashby, in the course of his visits, had more than once addressed himself to Miss Ellison with a warmth which that lady had received with great composure and no discouragement whatsoever; and that since his attentions had fixed themselves upon Harriet, her sister’s solicitude for her welfare had increased in exact proportion to her own disappointment. The advice was disinterested in appearance only. It was the advice of a woman who had rather see her sister married to a fool than not married at all, provided always that the fool were rich enough to make the spectacle supportable.

The dinner passed as dinners at Hartwell Place were wont to pass. Mr. Ashby sat at Mrs. Ellison’s right hand and discoursed upon the grey mare, and upon a new turnpike road, and upon the remarkable mildness of the season; and Harriet, seated opposite to him, found her thoughts wandering with a persistency she could not check to a small, plain parlour in the village, where a man with a grave face was at that moment, she did not doubt, reading by a fire of his own making. Once or twice she caught her sister’s eye, and read in it a satisfaction which she was at no pains to conceal.

“You are very silent, Miss Harriet,” said Mr. Ashby, when the cloth was drawn. “I hope you are not indisposed.”

“Not at all, sir. I was only thinking.”

“Thinking! Ay, that is a dangerous employment for a young lady.” He laughed heartily at his own wit, and Mrs. Ellison laughed too, and Miss Ellison smiled, and Harriet smiled also, because there was nothing else to be done.
