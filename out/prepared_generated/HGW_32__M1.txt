The first sign of the anomaly was not a symptom, but a sound. It was a dry, rasping cough that echoed with a peculiar intimacy across the polished expanse of the Great Hall of Computation in New London. The sound was so alien, so utterly out of place in the serene hum of the pneumatic tubes and the gentle whir of the calculating engines, that every head turned.

The source of the disturbance was a young man named Arthur Penhaligon. He was employed in the Archive of Historical Texts, a post of some prestige that involved the careful dusting of actual paper books, a task most considered a quaint and rather unhygienic pastime. Arthur was a model citizen of the year 2342: tall, clean-limbed, his face unblemished, his movements efficient. He was, until this precise moment, indistinguishable from any other of the ten million souls inhabiting the Greater London Conurbation.

He coughed again, a deeper, more convulsive sound this time, and brought a hand to his mouth. When he lowered it, the pale skin of his palm was flecked with a startling, brilliant crimson. A collective gasp, a sound of pure, undiluted shock, went through the hall. No one had seen such a colour outside of a historical drama or the butcher’s synthetic protein dispensary.

Arthur stared at his hand, his brow furrowed not with pain, but with sheer, analytical confusion. He felt a strange warmth in his chest, a tightening of his lungs that was utterly novel. He looked up at the sea of horrified, perfectly healthy faces and felt a sudden, profound sense of isolation.

Within the hour, the Sanitary Inspectors arrived. They did not walk, but glided on silent conveyors, their white uniforms seeming to absorb the light. They wore sterile masks and gloves, not to protect themselves from Arthur, but to protect the world from him. He was not a patient; he was a contamination.

“Subject Penhaligon,” one of them intoned, his voice filtered and metallic. “You are exhibiting signs of organic malfunction. You will accompany us to the Assessment Centre.”

There were no doctors. The very concept was as archaic as alchemy. Humanity had, through a century of rigorous genetic hygiene, atmospheric filtration, and universal nutrient paste, eradicated the very possibility of disease. The human body was a solved equation. Arthur’s body was presenting an impossible new variable.

They took him to a sterile white chamber, not unlike the decontamination units used for meteorite fragments. His sister, Elara, a junior bio-technician, was permitted to see him through a sheet of reinforced plasteel. Her face, usually a mask of placid contentment, was a knot of bewildered anxiety.

“Arthur, what is it?” she asked, her voice trembling slightly. “Is it a… a malfunction of the respiratory filter?”

“I don’t know,” he said, his voice hoarse. “It’s just… an ache, Elara. A tightness. And the coughing. It’s as if my lungs are trying to clean themselves of something that isn’t there.”

“The Inspectors are talking of ‘pathogens’,” she whispered, using a word she had only ever encountered in the historical archives. “They’re reading old texts. They say it’s a… a biological agent. Something that makes the body attack itself.”

Arthur managed a weak smile. “It feels less like an attack and more like an argument. A disagreement between my chest and the air.”

But to the world outside, it was a plague. News of the ‘Penhaligon Contagion’ spread with the speed of a data-stream. Panic, a forgotten emotion, began to flicker in the public consciousness. People wore filtration masks in the streets. Transport from the sector was suspended. Arthur’s apartment was sealed and incinerated. His colleagues at the Archive were quarantined and subjected to humiliatingly thorough biological scans.

The Assessment Centre’s directors, a committee of grim-faced technicians and public health officials, had no precedent to follow. Their instruments could measure his temperature, his blood-oxygen levels, the cellular degradation in his lung tissue, but they could offer no cure. The knowledge of medicine, of pharmacology, of surgery, had been deleted from the active data banks as obsolete clutter. It was considered a barbaric science, like trepanning or bloodletting.

Arthur became a specimen. They took samples of his blood, his saliva, his sweat. They bombarded him with harmless but strange energies, hoping to ‘re-calibrate’ his cellular matrix. He grew weaker. The fever that was a mere statistical anomaly on their charts was, for him, a raging fire. The coughing fits left him breathless and exhausted.

Elara, using her clearance, delved into the forbidden archives. She bypassed firewalls and ignored civic warnings, her fingers flying across the console. She searched for words like ‘influenza,’ ‘pneumonia,’ ‘antibiotic.’ She found them, buried in digitized texts from the 20th and 21st centuries, surrounded by a language of barbarism and chaos. She read of hospitals, of doctors, of nurses—people who dedicated their lives to fighting this very kind of organic decay. She read of medicines, strange chemical concoctions that could kill these invisible ‘pathogens.’

She printed a section of a text describing supportive care: hydration, rest, and the management of fever. She smuggled the flimsy, archaic paper to the Assessment Centre.

“This is nonsense,” the Chief Inspector said, holding the printout with a pair of tongs as if it were toxic. “The subject requires systemic re-alignment, not… hot broth and blankets.”

“It’s all we have,” Elara pleaded, her voice raw. “The ancients knew how to fight this. They lived with it. They survived it. You are just letting him die!”

Her outburst was considered an emotional malfunction. She was escorted away. But her words, and the sheer, baffling persistence of Arthur’s condition, forced the committee to reconsider. They had a duty to preserve the integrity of the human stock, and here was a piece of that stock failing in a way they could not comprehend. Perhaps, they reasoned with deep reluctance, some of the old, crude methods had a logic to them.
