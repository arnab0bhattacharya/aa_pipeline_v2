If any honest chronicler of the human heart were to set forth the exact degree of soot, smoke, and general desolation that accumulated in the stagnant air of Cinderford, he might write a hundred pages and yet fail to make the reader’s eyes water as severely as the town itself did. The great spinning mills of Mr. Grindstaff dominated the place, a monstrous monument of soot-stained brick that ground the hours and the cotton and the people into one uniform, relentless dust.

It was on a damp and dismal Tuesday in November, when the fog hung about the weeping chimneys of Cinderford like a wet blanket upon a feverish patient, that Mrs. Bessie Tulliver sat in her kitchen, nursing her youngest and looking at a crust of bread as though it had personally insulted her. Bessie was a thin, pale woman, worn threadbare by the unceasing loom, by the damp Lancashire winters, and by the heavy grief she carried for a babe lost in the autumn. She was a woman who had once possessed a bright, tidy comeliness, but the mill had taken her youth and spun it into yarn.

Her husband, Jabez Tulliver, was a hand-loom weaver of the old, stubborn, tragic sort, who believed that to bend was to break, and who wore his poverty like a particularly scratchy coat of mail that he refused to take off even when it chafed him to the bone. He was a man perpetually indignant, and his indignation was mostly directed at the mills, the masters, and anyone who offered him a ladder out of his ditch, on the grounds that the ladder was not made of the correct wood.

“It’s a mort of bread, Bess,” said Jabez, glaring at the table, “but I’ll not have my family fed on the scraps of the Grindstaffs.” Jabez had a way of saying “Grindstaffs” as if he were biting into a particularly sour gooseberry. “I’ll not have it, I say. Let them keep their charity for them as wants it.”

“But, Jabez,” pleaded Bessie, her voice a mere whisper of exhaustion, “it isn’t charity. It’s a place. Housekeeper to the widowed brother.”

“A place!” cried Jabez, striking the table. “A place, says she! A place in the enemy’s camp! To be the servant of the brother of the very man that grinds us all to powder in his mills! No, Bess! My pride is my own, and I’ll not sell it for a kitchen and a fireside, though we starve by inches!”

Here was the cruel paradox of the virtuous poor: they had pride in abundance, but nothing else, and their pride was a very hungry guest. The three children, who sat huddled on a damp sack in the corner, looked up with hollow eyes. The youngest whimpered, a thin, reedy sound like a faulty valve in a steam-engine. Bessie drew her shawl tighter around the infant, her heart swelling with a bitterness that was almost a match for her husband’s. She was worn out by work and grief, and the children were hungry, and the brother was kind.

The brother in question was Mr. Septimus Grindstaff, a man as different from his mill-owning sibling as a soft plum pudding is from a brickbat. Where Mr. Grindstaff the elder was all fire, iron, and the relentless accumulation of capital, Septimus was a gentle, melancholy man who lived in a tall, narrow house on the edge of the moor, a house which seemed to lean away from the smoke as if trying to escape it. Septimus had been left a widower, and his quiet house had fallen into a gentle, harmless sort of chaos, populated by stacks of unread periodicals and a great many cats. He was a man who spoke softly, and when he had offered Bessie the place, he had done so with a genuine, trembling kindness that made Bessie’s eyes smart.

“It is not charity, Jabez,” Bessie repeated, a sudden fire in her own tired eyes. “It is wages for honest work. Must we all be drowned in the slough because you will not take a hand from the bank?”

“I’ll not take a hand!” roared Jabez, his voice cracking. “My father was an independent weaver, and his father before him! We have never been beholden to the masters, and we shall not begin now! I’ll not have my wife a slave in a Grindstaff kitchen!”

And so the matter rested, as it had rested for three days, with Jabez growing more adamant and the children growing more thin. The argument filled the small, damp room, bouncing off the bare walls and making the very air taste of strife.

It was on the following Thursday, when the frost had turned the gutters of Cinderford into rigid, ugly scars of ice, that Mr. Septimus Grindstaff himself came knocking at the Tulliver door. He did not come in a grand carriage, but on foot, wrapped in a shabby greatcoat, his boots white with the damp snow.

He entered the kitchen with an air of profound apology, as if he were deeply sorry for taking up any space in the world at all. He looked at Jabez, who stood by the cold fireplace with his arms folded, looking like a very grim and underfed monument to defiance. He looked at Bessie, who sat with the baby. He looked at the three children, whose eyes seemed to grow larger as their faces grew thinner, till they resembled nothing so much as three little owls perched upon a winter branch.

“Mr. Tulliver,” said Septimus, in his soft, hesitating voice, “I beg your pardon for the intrusion. I am a man of few words, and those are but poorly chosen. But I am told you object to my offer.”

“I do, sir,” said Jabez, stiffly. “I’ll not be beholden.”
