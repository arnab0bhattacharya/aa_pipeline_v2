The rain had beat against the windows of Thornfield Grange for three days without ceasing, and the house had grown as damp and sour as a well. I had come to this place in autumn, when the leaves were still copper on the bough, and now the year had rotted into winter, and I with it. My pupil, Miss Aurelia Vane, was a child of nine years, quick and restless, with a habit of watching me as though she expected me to perform some trick. Her mother, Lady Vane, was a woman of pale, exquisite coldness, who spoke to me only when she wished to correct my grammar or my posture, and who regarded my presence in her house as a species of necessary inconvenience, like the drains.

It was on the fourth day of the rain that Mr. Edmund Vane arrived.

He came in a flurry of wet greatcoat and scattered easels, a man of perhaps thirty years, with dark hair that fell across his brow and eyes that seemed to take the measure of a room before he entered it. He was cousin to Sir Richard, the master of Thornfield, and he had come, I was told, to paint the family portraits—a task that would occupy him through the winter months. I thought little of it. I had learned, in my twenty-six years, to expect nothing from the arrival of gentlemen.

But he sought me out.

I was in the schoolroom, attempting to instil the rudiments of French into Aurelia’s unwilling mind, when the door opened and he stood there, a brush in his hand like a sceptre. “You are Miss Ellery,” he said, not a question. “I have heard you speak. You have a voice that carries. I should like to paint you.”

I set down the book. “I am a governess, sir. I am not a subject for portraiture.”

“All the more reason,” said he. “The ladies of this house have faces like porcelain—pretty, smooth, and empty. Yours has been lived in.”

I confess the words stung me, not with pleasure but with suspicion. I had known flattery before, in the servants’ hall and the drawing-room alike, and it had always been a coin spent to purchase something I was unwilling to sell. “You are very free with your compliments,” I said. “I wonder what you hope to gain by them.”

He laughed—a short, surprised sound. “Nothing, Miss Ellery. I am an artist. I see, and I wish to record what I see. Is that so very strange?”

“It is strange in this house,” I replied, “where every word is weighed and every glance is measured. I have learned to distrust what comes too easily.”

He studied me a moment, and his expression changed—less the gallant, more the man. “You think I mean to make you a gift of your own vanity. I do not. I mean to paint you as you are: plain, fierce, and honest. It will not be a pretty picture. It will be a true one.”

I do not know what moved me—whether it was the novelty of hearing the truth spoken plainly, or the weariness of a life spent in dissimulation. I consented.

The sittings began the next day, in a small parlour that faced the garden. He set me by the window, where the grey light fell across my face, and he worked in silence for long stretches, his eyes moving from me to the canvas and back again with an intensity that I found difficult to bear. I am not a woman accustomed to being looked at. I had spent my life looking—at my betters, at my charges, at the world that passed me by—and to be the object of such scrutiny was to feel myself turned inside out.

“You have a remarkable face,” he said, on the third day. “I have painted duchesses and actresses, and none of them had what you have.”

“And what is that?” I asked, though I did not wish to know.

“A soul that has been tried in the fire and has not been consumed. You have suffered, Miss Ellery. It is written in the set of your mouth and the shadow beneath your eyes. I do not say this to flatter you. I say it because it is true, and because I cannot paint a lie.”

I looked at him sharply. “You are very bold, sir.”

“I am honest,” he said. “There is a difference.”

“Is there?” I rose from my chair, for I could not sit still under such words. “You come into this house a stranger, and within a week you presume to tell me what I am. You know nothing of me. You know nothing of what I have suffered or not suffered. You see a governess with a plain face and a sharp tongue, and you weave a romance about her, because it pleases you to think that beneath her dull exterior beats a heart of fire. It is a pretty fancy, Mr. Vane, but it is not the truth. The truth is that I am tired, and cold, and I have no one in the world to care whether I live or die. That is not remarkable. That is the common lot of women like me.”

He had stopped painting. He held his brush in the air, and his eyes were fixed on me with an expression I could not read. “You are wrong,” he said quietly. “I do not weave romances. I observe. And I have observed that you are not like the others in this house. You do not simper. You do not scheme. You do not pretend to be what you are not. That is rare, Miss Ellery. That is worth painting.”

I turned away. My heart was beating too fast, and I did not trust it. “You will finish your picture,” I said, “and then you will leave, and I will remain. That is the way of the world.”
