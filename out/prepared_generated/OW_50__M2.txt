Alfred Pym had tried to be a poet, but the Muse, in her infinite wisdom, had looked at his rhymes and decided they were merely clumsy attempts to rhyme "slate" with "fate." He had tried to be a painter, but the canvas rejected his brushstrokes with a silence more profound than any silence he had ever heard. He had even attempted the stage, only to be driven off by a single, stifled yawn from the manager, which was, in itself, a tragedy of the highest order. Thus, when the world offered him the option of becoming a critic, Alfred accepted it with the relief of a man escaping a burning building, not because he loved art, but because he assumed that to destroy it required no talent at all.

It was a theory of terrible simplicity. If one could not build a house, one was at least safe in one’s ability to point out where the roof leaked. Alfred settled into a modest flat in Kensington, where the wallpaper was a shade of cream that suggested either old age or a mild form of melancholy. He spent his days observing the world with the detached curiosity of a cat watching a fly, and his evenings preparing his first verdict. He did not read the plays he was to review; he merely read the reviews of those who had read the plays. He found that criticism was less about the work and more about the critic’s ability to dress up his own boredom in the robes of intellect.

The play in question was *The Gilded Cage*, a melodrama by a minor dramatist named Horace Fink. It concerned a bird who wanted to be a fish, a plot so thin it was almost invisible, like the hope of a starving man. Alfred sat in the front row, wearing a tie of such aggressive red that it seemed to be shouting at the audience. He watched the performance with a stillness that was unnerving, his eyes fixed on the stage as if he were waiting for a bomb to fall. When the curtain fell, the audience applauded with the polite, rhythmic beating of wings that belongs to those who have given up on soaring.

Alfred’s review appeared the next morning in the *Morning Echo*. It was not long, nor was it particularly structured. It was, however, a masterpiece of surgical precision. He did not say the play was bad; he said it was "aggressively mediocre," a phrase that stuck to the tongue like a thorn. He did not say the acting was poor; he described the lead actress as "suffering from a terminal case of sentimentality that had spread to her entire face." He did not mention the plot holes; he merely stated that the story had the structural integrity of a wet paper bag. The tone was not angry, but cool, detached, and utterly fatal. It was the verbal equivalent of a gaslight being extinguished.

By noon, the review had been copied into every drawing-room in London. By evening, it had been recited in clubs, debated in pubs, and whispered in the corridors of the Royal Academy. Horace Fink was not merely criticized; he was annihilated. The public, who had been politely bored by *The Gilded Cage*, suddenly found themselves enraged on its behalf. They did not defend the play; they defended the critic. To be insulted by Alfred Pym became the highest form of social distinction. If a painter’s work was deemed "derivative," his value doubled. If a novelist was called "prosaic," his books were bought in triplets. Alfred had discovered that in a society obsessed with status, the stamp of a discerning critic was worth more than the gold leaf on a gilded frame.

Within a week, Alfred’s modest flat was besieged. Letters arrived in such volume that the postman began to look at him with a mixture of fear and respect. They were not letters of apology; they were letters of invitation. The Duchess of Ashworth asked him to review her latest poem, a collection of sonnets on the theme of "The Transience of Roses." The Duke of Pembroke wanted him to judge his new sculpture, a marble bust of his horse, which the Duke insisted was "spiritual." Even the Prime Minister sent a note, inquiring if Alfred would be so kind as to critique the Prime Minister’s recent speech on trade, a speech that Alfred had not heard and which, he suspected, did not exist.

Alfred found himself in a position of uncomfortable power. He had not sought fame, but fame had come to him like a parasite, attaching itself to his skin and feeding on his indifference. He realized that he was no longer a critic; he was a brand. His name had become a currency, a mark of quality that was applied to things that had no quality. He was the seal of approval for the disapproved.

One evening, he was invited to dine with Lord and Lady Castlemere, a couple of artists who had recently painted a portrait of a dog that was said to be "the most honest thing ever put on canvas." Alfred went, not because he cared for the dog, but because he knew that by ignoring the dog, he would make it famous. He sat at the table, surrounded by crystal glasses and silver plate, and listened to the host talk about the "soul of the beast." Alfred smiled, a thin, polite curve of the lips that revealed nothing. He waited for the moment to strike.

"The dog," said Alfred, finally, his voice soft and clear, "is a masterpiece of emptiness. It captures the very essence of nothingness. It is the portrait of a void, and in that void, we see our own reflection. It is not a painting of a dog; it is a painting of the absence of a dog. And that, my dear Lord, is the only thing worth painting in this age."
