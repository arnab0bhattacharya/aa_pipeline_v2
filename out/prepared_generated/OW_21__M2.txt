The fountain in the courtyard of the Palace of Solara had been dry for three days, not from a lack of water, but from a lack of imagination. The courtiers, a tribe of velvet and silk who had long since confused their own reflections with the sun, gathered around the stone basin in a state of exquisite boredom. In the center of the dry marble lay a single fish, its scales the color of liquid gold, its eyes holding the dull, reflective patience of a jewel that knows it is being admired but not understood.

A fairy, small as a dewdrop and dressed in the iridescent wings of a mayfly, descended upon the lip of the fountain. She did not speak; she merely touched the waterless stone with a finger that smelled of ozone and burnt rosemary. A sound like a silver bell cracking echoed through the courtyard. The golden fish opened its mouth, and out poured not water, but words, crisp and cold as ice.

“I am tired of being a metaphor,” the fish said, its voice thin and high, yet carrying with the authority of a verdict. “You look at me and see wealth, power, and eternal youth. You are mistaken. I am only hunger, and you are only vanity. Let us see which is the more durable illusion.”

The Duke of Vane, a man who wore his mustache like a weapon and his opinions like a coat of armor, stepped forward. “A fish that speaks of philosophy,” he sneered, adjusting his cuffs. “It is a common trick. The creature is merely repeating the thoughts of the philosopher who last fed it. I, myself, have never been fed by a thinker.”

“Ah, Vane,” the fish replied, turning its head with a slow, serpentine grace. “You are not fed by a thinker, for you have none. You are fed by the fear of others. You do not lead the court; you frighten it. Your grandeur is a shadow cast by your own mediocrity. You are loud because you are empty, and you are cruel because you are afraid of being perceived as what you actually are: a man who mistakes volume for substance.”

A murmur rippled through the group of ladies in their emerald and sapphire gowns. The Duchess of Alara, who had previously been discussing the price of silk, now watched the fish with the wide-eyed curiosity of a cat observing a mouse that had begun to recite poetry. “How dreadful,” she whispered to her maid, though her voice was not unpleased by the scandal. “It is so much more interesting than the Duke’s latest speech on taxation.”

The King, who had been sitting on the steps of the fountain, reading a book he had not opened in a decade, looked up. He was a tall, pale man, with the kind of face that is beautiful only in repose, for in motion it revealed a profound lack of conviction. He closed the book and looked at the fish.

“Silence it,” the King said, his voice flat and devoid of inflection. “A fish should not know the secrets of men. It is an affront to nature, and nature is the only thing that has ever treated me with dignity.”

“But Your Majesty,” said the fish, ignoring the command with the casual arrogance of the truly powerful, “if you silence me, you must also silence the mirrors in the ballroom. For I am merely the mirror that speaks. You have spent your life polishing the glass, but you have never dared to look at what is reflected. You are not a king; you are a caretaker of your own decline. You rule this court not because you are wise, but because they are too afraid to be bored without you.”

The King’s face flushed a deep, arterial red. He stood up, and the courtiers scattered like leaves in a gale, though they tried to do so with the dignity of swans. The Duke of Vane, realizing that the fish had struck a nerve that ran deep into his own insecurity, shouted, “Burn the basin! Drown the creature! It is a demon of the deep, sent to mock our sovereignty!”

“Burn the basin,” the fish agreed, with a shrug that rippled through its golden scales. “But remember, Vane, that fire reveals the impurities in metal. You will find that you have no core. You are all surface. And when the surface is gone, what remains? Nothing. Just as you are now.”

The King drew a dagger from his belt. It was a fine blade, inlaid with rubies, but his hand trembled. He approached the edge of the fountain. The fish did not flee. It lay still, its gills flaring in a rhythm that seemed to mock the King’s erratic heartbeat.

“You are afraid,” the fish said, its voice dropping to a whisper that carried across the silent courtyard. “Not of me, but of the truth. You have built this palace, this court, this life, as a cage to keep the world out. But the world is already inside you, King. It is the doubt that wakes you at midnight. It is the emptiness that no banquet can fill. I am not the enemy. I am the witness. And witnesses are dangerous, not because they can hurt, but because they cannot be bribed.”

The King raised the dagger. The courtiers held their breath. The Duchess of Alara closed her eyes, not in horror, but in anticipation. She had always known that the King was a fraud, but to hear it said by a fish was a different order of experience. It was art. It was the ultimate epigram.

If he killed the fish, he would be the tyrant who silenced truth. If he spared it, he would be the fool who listened to a pet. There was no third option. There was never a third option for those who have confused power with personality.
