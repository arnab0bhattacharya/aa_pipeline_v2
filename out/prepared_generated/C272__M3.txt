The letter arrived on a Tuesday, brought up to the schoolroom by the concierge's boy while Clara Whitmore was hearing the younger girls translate from Caesar. She saw her name on the fold—her mother's hand, cramped and tilting downward as it always tilted when the news was bad—and she placed the letter face down beneath her grammar text and told the boy to go. She continued with the lesson. Cecile Dornier was struggling through the passive periphrastic, and Clara corrected her twice with the same patient precision she would have used on any other morning, because the morning had not yet changed, because the letter was still only paper and ink and not yet what it would become once she opened it.

She opened it after the midday meal, in her small room above the courtyard, with the window open to the November air of V——, a city of steeples and damp stone that had never become hers in the three years she had lived in it. The letter said that Edward had died on the fourth of the month, of a fever contracted in the autumn rains, and that he had been attended by Dr. Graeme and had wanted for nothing, which Clara understood to mean that he had died in pain and her mother could not write the word. She read the letter twice. She folded it and placed it in the drawer of her writing desk and sat on the narrow bed with her hands in her lap, and the sounds of the school came up to her through the floorboards—the clatter of the refectory, the pitch pipe in the music room, Sister Anne-Marie calling the little ones to their afternoon walk in the garden—and these sounds continued as though the world were a clock that did not stop when one of its smaller parts fell still.

She did not go down to supper. She told herself she would compose herself by morning, and the telling was a kind of work, a task with a shape and a limit, and she could perform tasks. She had performed them since arriving at the École de Sainte-Agnès with her trunk and her references and her one good black dress, teaching English to girls who already spoke three languages and considered a fourth a mild inconvenience. She had learned to stand before them with her hands clasped behind her back and her voice pitched to carry over their whispering without ever rising to anger. She had learned to eat alone in the small parlour set aside for the Protestant instructors and to smile when Madame Laurier, the head of the French department, made her careful, laboured conversation in English, as though the effort of listening were a courtesy Clara ought to repay. She had learned all of this, and composure was only one more thing to learn, and she would learn it by morning, and the learning would cost her nothing that she could not afford.

She slept, or did not sleep. The room was cold. The canal below the school wall smelled of standing water and the last leaves blown from the elms along the Rampartstrasse. At some hour she woke from a dream she could not remember and found that her face was wet and that she had been making a sound she did not recognize, very low, the sound of an animal in a place it should not be. She pressed her face into the pillow and made herself stop. She lay still and breathed in the rhythm she used before entering the schoolroom—four counts in, four held, four out—and this was absurd, she knew it was absurd, to regulate her breathing as though grief were a thing to be managed like a classroom of restless girls, but she did it because she could do it, because it had a shape.

The bell rang at half past five. She rose and dressed and braided her hair and pinched her cheeks, which were hollowed and grey, and she put on her black dress, which was the same black dress she always wore and therefore told nothing. She went down the stairs with her books under her arm and entered the long corridor where the older girls assembled before the first lesson, and they parted for her with their usual mild deference, and one of them—Louise, the banker's daughter from Geneva—said, "Good morning, Mademoiselle," and Clara said, "Good morning, Louise," and her voice was steady, and the morning had begun.

The first lesson was with the middle division, girls of thirteen and fourteen, and they were reading the third act of *The Merchant of Venice*, which Clara had chosen for its speeches and its manageable moral questions. She opened her copy and asked them to begin at Portia's entrance, and Berthe Holzer, who was diligent and humourless and read everything as though it were an instruction manual, began: "You see me, Lord Bassanio, where I stand, such as I am." Clara listened. She corrected the pronunciation of *suited* and the emphasis on *fair*. She walked between the desks with her hands behind her back, the way she always walked, and the girls did not look up at her because there was nothing to see. She was the same as she had been yesterday. She would be the same tomorrow. This was the thing she was building, the edifice of sameness, and each correct pronunciation was a brick in it, and each steady step was mortar, and she built and built and the morning held.

Between lessons she stood in the corridor and looked out through the tall window at the garden, where the gardener was raking the dead leaves into heaps along the wall. The elms were nearly bare. She thought of Edward at twenty, walking through the orchard at home with his coat open and his hair damp, coming in late for supper because he had been reading by the river, always reading, always coming in late, with that look of slight surprise as though the world's expectations were a thing he had genuinely forgotten.
