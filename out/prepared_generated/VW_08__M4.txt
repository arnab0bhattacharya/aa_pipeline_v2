The sun had gone off the terrace, though the light still lay in a broad, flat slab upon the sea, and the chairs, the little iron tables, the palms in their green tubs, seemed to wait, as if for a signal, in a pause of extraordinary gentleness. Mrs. Ashcombe, who had come down late, after the long afternoon of her husband’s sleeping, sat with her hands folded in her lap, watching the shadows lengthen across the courtyard flags. It was at this hour, when the English went up to dress and the hotel was quiet, that the girl came with her basket.

She was there now, at the far corner by the arch where the bougainvillea hung in a great purple cascade, a small figure in black, her basket on her arm, not importuning anyone, only standing, as though she had grown there out of the stones. Mrs. Ashcombe had watched her for three days before she had made up her mind to buy; and even then she had bought hurriedly, with a shyness that was almost guilt, a dozen yards of lace which she did not want, and had felt, as she put the coins into the brown palm, a curious sense of having been the one who received.

“You are always here,” she said this evening, in her slow, careful French. “Do you never rest?”

The girl smiled. Her teeth were very white in the brown face; her eyes, which were large and black and rather melancholy, took in Mrs. Ashcombe with a grave attention, as though she were reading a page. “I rest in the morning, madame. In the afternoon the English ladies come down. It is the best time.”

“And in the evening?”

“In the evening I go home.” She shifted the basket. “It is up there.” She nodded vaguely towards the hill behind the town, where the white houses climbed one above another among the vines and the grey crags, and a convent, they said, and a church with a blue-tiled tower.

“Far?”

“Not so far. Half an hour—if one walks.” She said it with a little pride, as of a distance achieved. “My mother is there, and my brothers, and my sister who is married, and her children. There are nine of us in the house.”

“Nine!”

“It is nothing,” said the girl, with the same faint pride. “My aunt has fourteen. And my grandmother is with us also. She is very old—she does not see well—but she knows everything. She sits by the window and tells us what to do.” She laughed, a low, soft laugh, and then, as if recollecting herself, glanced at Mrs. Ashcombe and was silent.

The sea beyond the terrace had turned to the colour of pale pewter; a steamer, far out, was moving south with a thread of smoke behind it. Mrs. Ashcombe, sitting very still, felt all at once the enormous weight of the island behind her—the hill, the climbing houses, the nine in one room perhaps, the grandmother by the window, the fourteen of the aunt, the vines, the crags, the convent—and it seemed to her that she had never in her life known what it was to be so many, so crowded, so warm.

“And you,” said the girl presently, with a shy boldness, “you are alone here? You and the gentleman who is ill?”

“My husband. Yes.”

“He does not come down?”

“He is not strong enough. The doctors sent him here for the winter. He sits in his room, and reads, and sometimes he sleeps.” She heard her own voice, thin and flat, describing it; and it occurred to her that she had said these words, or words like them, a hundred times, to a hundred people, in a hundred hotels, and that they had never before sounded so small.

“It is sad,” said the girl simply.

“Oh no—not sad. He is better here. The warmth—” But the girl was not listening to the reasons; she was looking at Mrs. Ashcombe with her grave, reading eyes, and Mrs. Ashcombe stopped.

“When I am married,” the girl said, “I shall not let my husband sit alone in a room. We shall sit in the doorway, in the evening, and the children will play in the street, and my mother will come, and my aunt, and we shall talk.” She spoke as one describing a thing long meditated. “I shall have a house with a balcony, and vines over it, and a little stove in the winter. And I shall sell lace only in the morning. In the afternoon I shall sit on the balcony.”

“And your husband?”

“He will be a fisherman. Or he will work in the wine. It does not matter. He will come home in the evening and I shall have the soup ready.” She paused, and then, with a sudden look at Mrs. Ashcombe, half shy, half proud: “There is a young man now. He speaks to my father. But my father says we must wait. There is no money.” She said it without bitterness, as one states the weather. “So we wait. Perhaps next year. Perhaps the year after.”

The light was nearly gone. A servant came out with a taper and lit the lamp in the hall, and the flame, yellow and uncertain in the dusk, made a little pool of brightness that did not reach them. The girl’s face had grown dim; only her white teeth showed when she smiled, and the white of her eyes.

“You must go home,” said Mrs. Ashcombe. “It is getting dark.”

“Yes, madame.” But she did not go. She stood turning her basket a little, as if there were something more she wished to say, and could not. At last: “Madame is kind,” she said. “The other ladies—they buy, and they go. Madame stays and talks. It is not the same.”
