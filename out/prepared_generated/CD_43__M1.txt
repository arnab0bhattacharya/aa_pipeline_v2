The morning that saw the downfall of Mr. Augustus Pengelly was ushered in, not by the cheerful chirping of the sparrows upon the verandah of his villa at Clapham, nor by the slanting sunbeams that so lovingly caressed the gilded capitals of his Corinthian columns, but by a shriek—a shriek so piercing, so utterly devoid of all decorum, that it rattled the very silver on the breakfast-table and caused the fat, contented goldfish in the hall to leap, for once, from their serene and silent world.

The shriek proceeded from the lips of Mr. Pengelly himself, who, at that precise moment, was not in the breakfast-room at all, but in his study—a room of dark mahogany and heavy curtains, where the air smelt of leather and despair—and was staring at a document with eyes that seemed to have forgotten how to blink. The document was a telegram, a modern contrivance of wire and lightning, which, upon this occasion, had delivered a message of such thunderous and absolute ruin that the very paper seemed to tremble in his grasp.

“Gone!” cried Mr. Pengelly, in a voice that was now a mere husk of its former booming self. “The ‘Imperial Grand Junction and Trans-Continental Railway’—gone! The shares—my fortune—my all—vanished into thin air! Oh, the villainy! The audacious, the insatiable, the bottomless villainy of it all!”

He was a man of comfortable girth and florid complexion, who had hitherto believed the world to be a well-oiled machine, constructed chiefly for the benefit of gentlemen like himself. Now, the machine had not merely broken down; it had exploded, leaving him clutching at the charred and worthless remnants. He sank into his chair, a monument to commercial catastrophe, and buried his face in his hands.

It was in this state of utter desolation that his niece, Miss Arabella Finch, discovered him. Arabella was a young woman of twenty, whose life had been as smooth and untroubled as the surface of a millpond on a windless day. Her education had been conducted by a succession of governesses, her dresses had been procured from the most fashionable establishments, and her chief daily anxieties had hitherto concerned the proper curling of her hair and the correct deportment required for a garden party. She was, in short, a perfect product of a sheltered and prosperous existence; a hothouse flower, suddenly and cruelly exposed to the biting frosts of reality.

“Uncle!” she cried, rushing to his side. “What is it? What terrible news?”

Mr. Pengelly raised his head, and the look in his eyes struck a chill to Arabella’s heart. It was the look of a man who has seen a ghost—the ghost of his own future.

“Arabella, my child,” he began, his voice trembling. “The sun has set upon our fortunes. The railway—that great artery of progress, that iron road to prosperity—has been a pit of swindlers and charlatans! We are ruined. Utterly, completely, and irrevocably ruined!”

The words fell upon Arabella like stones. Ruined! The word was one she had read in novels, but it had never seemed real. She looked around the study, at the solid mahogany desk, the shelves of leather-bound books, the portraits of serene ancestors gazing down from the walls. How could these things be illusions? How could this solid, comfortable world be dissolving like sugar in hot tea?

“But, Uncle,” she stammered, “surely there is something… some savings… some property…?”

“Nothing!” he roared, with a sudden, wild energy. “The house is mortgaged to the roof-tiles! The furniture will be seized by creditors before the week is out! We shall have nothing—nothing but the clothes upon our backs and the bitter taste of failure in our mouths!”

And so it was. In the space of a single week, the villa at Clapham, with its Corinthian columns and its contented goldfish, passed into the hands of a stern-faced gentleman from the City. Mr. Pengelly, his spirit broken and his girth mysteriously diminished, took a room in a dreary lodging-house in Bloomsbury, where the wallpaper was peeling and the landlady’s voice was a perpetual acid drip of complaint.

For Arabella, the change was a descent into a world she could not comprehend. The silks and muslins were replaced by a single, sensible grey dress. The delicate pastries and French chocolates were supplanted by buttered bread and weak tea. And the great, yawning chasm of the future stretched before her, a terrifying wilderness of unknown toil and humiliation. She must find work. The very word was an affront. Work! It was for other people—for the shop-girls she had once observed with a detached curiosity, for the seamstresses whose bent backs she had barely noticed from the carriage window.

It was in this state of desperate resolve that she remembered Miss Priscilla Gumm. Miss Gumm had been one of her governesses years ago—a thin, sharp-featured woman with a voice like a file and a nose that seemed to be perpetually scenting out some failure of discipline. She had since, Arabella recalled, opened a school for young ladies of limited means in a part of London that was spoken of in hushed, fearful tones.

Summoning all her courage, Arabella wrote a letter. The reply came swiftly: a terse, ink-blotched note inviting her to call at the ‘Academy for the Propriety and Improvement of the Female Mind,’ located in a grimy street off the Tottenham Court Road.

The Academy proved to be a narrow, three-story house, squeezed between a baker’s shop, which exhaled a warm, yeasty smell, and a printer’s, which clattered and groaned like a thing in pain. The door was opened by Miss Gumm herself, who was thinner and sharper than ever, and whose nose seemed to have developed an even greater capacity for detecting deficiency.

“Miss Finch,” she said, her eyes scanning Arabella from head to toe. “You are punctual. That is a start. Come in.”
