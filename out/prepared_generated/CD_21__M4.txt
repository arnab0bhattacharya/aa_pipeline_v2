Mr. Obadiah Grubb, parish clerk of St. Sepulchre’s Without, was a man in whom the love of order had hardened, as it will in some natures, into a positive passion for parsimony; so that he walked the world as though it were a ledger, and every soul he met a sum that might be reduced. He was a small, dry, clicking man, with a head as bald and polished as a brass knocker, and hands that seemed perpetually engaged in the act of rubbing together, whether in satisfaction at a saving made or in anticipation of one to come. His voice was a sort of cheerful rasp, like a file upon a hinge, and his favourite word—which he used on all occasions, whether of joy or commination—was “Method.”

“Method, sir,” he would say, “method is the poor man’s capital. Give me method, and I will undertake to feed the hungry with the crumbs of a rich man’s table, and to warm the shivering with the very parings of a coal-cellar.”

It was in the bitter January of eighteen hundred and thirty-something, when the frost had lain upon the London roofs like a white scurf for three weeks together, and the gutters were stopped with ice, and the very cats went about with a pinched and philosophical air, as if they had solved the problem of existence by expecting nothing—it was then that the vestry of St. Sepulchre’s Without, being moved by the importunities of certain charitable ladies and the plain evidence of their own senses, resolved upon a distribution of coals to the poor of the parish. And who so fit to administer the fund, to keep the lists, to weigh the sacks, to detect imposture, and to make a little go a very long way indeed, as Mr. Obadiah Grubb? The vestry thought so; Mr. Grubb thought so; and the charitable ladies, who did not know him, thought so too.

The coal-yard behind the church was a scene of the most admirable confusion—that is to say, it was confusion in the eyes of the crowd, but method in the mind of Mr. Grubb. There he stood, in his greatcoat and his spectacles, with a list in one hand and a piece of chalk in the other, while the sacks were ranged before him like a regiment of black dwarfs, and the poor of the parish were marshalled in a long and shivering line that wound out of the yard and down the narrow passage by the sexton’s house.

“Name?” said Mr. Grubb, with his chalk poised.

“Betsy Crumble, sir, widow, two children, one a cripple.”

“Method,” said Mr. Grubb, making a note. “Half a sack. Next.”

“Timothy Shanks, sir, out of work these five months, and the wife down with the fever.”

“Method. Half a sack. Next.”

“Old Mrs. Grubb, sir, of the almshouses in Pickle-herring Court.”

The chalk stopped in the air. Mr. Grubb looked up from his list, and then down at his list, and then up again at the woman who had spoken—a small, bent, shawled figure, with a face like a withered apple and eyes that were still bright, though sunk deep in their sockets.

“What name?” said Mr. Grubb, sharply.

“Grubb, sir. Mrs. Abigail Grubb. I am on the list, sir, as I understand.”

Mr. Grubb went very red, and then very white, and then he made a great business of consulting his papers, though every eye in the yard could see that he was not reading a word of them.

“There is some error,” he said at last. “There must be some error. The list is made out with method. I will not have the method disturbed. Stand aside, my good woman; stand aside.”

But the woman did not stand aside. She looked at him with a strange, patient, penetrating look, as though she were reading him as he read his list, and finding him, perhaps, a shorter sum than he supposed.

“Obadiah,” she said, quietly, “do you not know me?”

The crowd began to murmur. The sexton, a fat man with a face like a full moon, put his hand to his ear. Mr. Grubb felt that the eyes of all the parish were upon him, and that his method was in peril.

“I know you,” he said, with a desperate attempt at dignity, “as a claimant upon the charity of this parish, and I tell you that your name is not upon my list. There is no Mrs. Grubb in Pickle-herring Court. There is no such person. The list is correct. The list is made out with method.”

“I have lived in Pickle-herring Court these seven years,” said the old woman, “and I have not seen you once, Obadiah, though it is but a step from your own door. I did not think to see you now, and to be told I am not myself.”

At this there was a great stir in the yard, and a stout woman in a red cloak cried out that it was a shame, and a thin man with a squint said that he had always thought the clerk a hard one, and a boy at the back began to whistle. Mr. Grubb saw that the mob was rising, and the mob was the one thing in the world that his method had never taught him to manage.

“Silence!” he cried. “Silence, I say! The distribution is suspended for the space of one quarter of an hour, by reason of an irregularity. Let no man stir.”

And with that he took his mother by the arm—her arm, so thin that it was like a stick wrapped in a rag—and drew her into the vestry-room, where the coal-dust lay thick upon the ledgers, and the parish registers stood in their presses like a row of coffins.

“Mother,” he said, when the door was shut, “this is a most unseemly interruption. You have compromised the method. You have brought the whole scheme into contempt.”
