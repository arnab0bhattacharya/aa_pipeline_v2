Mrs. Clementina Arkwright was a lady of such profound convictions and such singular hats that it was impossible to doubt her sincerity, however much one might doubt her sanity. She believed, with an ardour that was almost a form of indigestion, that the veil between this world and the next was of the flimsiest silk, and that she, with the aid of a planchette and a quantity of black velvet, could lift a corner of it at will. Her drawing-room in Bayswater, a veritable grotto of bric-à-brac and faded damask, was perpetually draped in twilight, not from any aesthetic preference, but because she felt strong light was disagreeable to the spirits.

Her nephews, Cecil and Arthur Dorian, were young men of the late nineteenth century, which is to say, they were possessed of a certain fashionable languor and a complete absence of faith. Cecil, the elder, was a poet of minor reputation and major pretensions, who affected a weariness with the world that was, in itself, quite exhausting. Arthur, who had recently left Oxford with a first in Classics and a profound belief in the superiority of the Clubland cigarette, regarded his aunt’s enthusiasms with the gentle, pitying amusement one reserves for a rather charming, but fundamentally absurd, piece of antique furniture.

‘My dear boys,’ Mrs. Arkwright announced one Tuesday evening, her turban of peacock feathers nodding with the gravity of an owl, ‘I have had a communication. The Beyond is restless.’

‘I am not surprised, Aunt,’ said Arthur, accepting a cup of tea. ‘The rent in Kensington must be astronomical.’

Cecil merely sighed, examining a small, carved ivory frog on the mantelpiece. ‘The material world is so dreadfully loud. It is a wonder the spirits can get a word in edgeways.’

‘Tonight,’ his aunt continued, ignoring them with the serene determination of the truly convinced, ‘we shall hold a séance. The conditions are propitious. The moon is in Scorpio, and Mrs. Higgins has made a seed cake, which always seems to please them.’

The séance took place in the back drawing-room, which Mrs. Arkwright called her ‘Psychic Sanctum’. The curtains were drawn, a single lamp with a rose-coloured shade cast a blushing, insufficient light, and the air was thick with the scent of jasmine and impending nonsense. At the centre of the room stood a round table of polished mahogany, upon which were placed a planchette, a glass of water, and a small silver bell. Mrs. Arkwright, arrayed in a black silk gown that rustled with every gesture, sat at the head of the table. Her nephews took their places on either side, their expressions a carefully cultivated blend of indulgence and boredom.

‘We must join hands,’ she whispered, her voice trembling with anticipation. ‘The current must be unbroken. Think pure thoughts.’

‘I shall think of the new French novel I am reading,’ murmured Cecil. ‘It is impure in the most aesthetically pleasing way.’

‘I shall think of the Derby,’ said Arthur. ‘It is pure enough, and considerably more probable.’

They joined hands. Mrs. Arkwright closed her eyes and began to hum a low, tuneless dirge. For several minutes, nothing happened, save for the ticking of a long-case clock in the hall and the faint sound of Arthur attempting to stifle a yawn. The atmosphere was not so much spiritual as merely stuffy.

Then, the temperature in the room dropped with the suddenness of a social faux pas. The rose-coloured lamp flickered and died, plunging them into a profound gloom. A low, sepulchral moan seemed to emanate from the very walls.

‘Oh!’ gasped Mrs. Arkwright. ‘He is here!’

From the deepest shadows in the corner of the room, a figure began to coalesce. It was a man of imposing stature, dressed in the severe frock coat and high collar of the mid-Victorian era. His face was pale and stern, his hair white and neatly parted, and his eyes held the fixed, disapproving glare of a man who has just been presented with a bill he considers outrageous.

‘Good heavens,’ whispered Arthur, his boredom instantly evaporated. ‘It’s Uncle Barnaby’s tailor, come to haunt us for his bill.’

‘Silence, boy,’ boomed the apparition, in a voice that seemed to vibrate through the floorboards. ‘I am not a tradesman. I am your grandfather.’

Cecil and Arthur exchanged a look of profound scepticism. Their grandfather, Sir Gideon Dorian, had been a man of legendary rectitude and even more legendary boredom, a pillar of the City who had regarded pleasure as a form of moral turpitude. He had been dead for fifteen years.

‘Grandfather?’ said Cecil, a note of artistic interest in his voice. ‘How dreadfully novel. I had always imagined you as a sepia photograph.’

‘You will speak when you are spoken to,’ the spirit intoned. ‘I have come from a place of eternal reckoning to confront you with the state of your souls.’

‘Oh, I do hope not,’ sighed Arthur. ‘I’ve had a very trying week, and my soul is really not up to an audit.’

‘You are frivolous,’ the spirit declared, pointing a translucent finger at Arthur. ‘You waste your substance on idle pursuits, on horses and cards and the company of foolish women. You are a disgrace to the Dorian name.’

‘And you, Cecil,’ the spirit continued, turning its baleful gaze upon the elder brother. ‘You wrap yourself in a shroud of aestheticism to conceal the emptiness within. You write verses about lilies while your heart is a barren desert. You are a hypocrite.’

Mrs. Arkwright was weeping with joy. ‘Oh, the truth of it! The terrible, wonderful truth! Confess, my boys, confess!’

‘This is a trick,’ said Arthur, his voice hard. ‘Aunt Clementina, who is behind the curtain?’

‘There is no curtain!’ wept his aunt. ‘It is the spirit of Sir Gideon!’

‘A trick, is it?’ roared the apparition. ‘Then let me prove my identity. Cecil, when you were seven, you did not break the Chinese vase in the hall. You were covering for Arthur, who was attempting to fly a kite indoors.’
