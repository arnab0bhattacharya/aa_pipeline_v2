It was a raw and gusty morning in the latter part of October when I found myself rattling over the moor road in a dog-cart, bound for the little village of Highfell. My companion, the celebrated private agent Mr. Alistair Vance, sat in silence, his grey eyes fixed upon the grim, heather-clad slopes that rose on either side of us. The case which had brought us from London was as singular as any I have encountered, and it concerned the construction of the new North Yorkshire & Cleveland Railway’s branch line to the coast.

The engineer in charge of the works, a Mr. Arthur Pendelton, had met us at the station. He was a man of about thirty, lean and capable-looking, but with a nervous tic about his eyes that spoke of considerable strain. He had laid the whole affair before Vance in our compartment.

“It is the most confounded business, Mr. Vance,” he had begun, running a hand through his already disordered hair. “For three weeks now, every morning I find my surveying stakes have been moved. Not pulled up and thrown down, you understand, but carefully repositioned. Always the same way. Always nudging the line of the viaduct a little to the west.”

“And the effect of this persistent nudging?” Vance had inquired, his long fingers steepled before him.

“Disaster, sir. Utter disaster. The line is being diverted towards Hardcastle Crag. A sheer drop. To build there would be impossible without a fortune in extra works, and even then the gradient would be wrong. The company’s directors are breathing fire. They suggest I am incompetent, or worse. They threaten to dismiss me and seek damages for the delay.”

Vance had listened with an air of placid interest. “And who, in your opinion, is responsible?”

“There is only one man it can be: old Silas Croft. His farm, Thistlethwaite, lies directly in the path of the original survey. The company has served him notice to treat for the land. He is a stubborn, cantankerous fellow who has sworn he will not see his fields cut in two by a ‘fire-spitting serpent,’ as he calls the train. I have confronted him, but he denies all knowledge, and laughs in my face.”

This, then, was the problem we had been engaged to solve. As our cart crested the final rise, the valley of the River Swale opened below us, a great scar of raw earth and scaffolding marking the progress of the railway. Beyond it lay a patchwork of small fields, and among them, a stone-built farmhouse that squatted under the lee of the moor like a watchful beast. That, I knew, was Thistlethwaite.

We found the works in a state of disarray. Pendelton led us to the point of contention. A line of wooden stakes, daubed with red paint, marched across the landscape. Vance did not look at the stakes themselves, but at the ground around them. He walked slowly, his head bowed, for the better part of an hour. He examined the turf, the angle of the morning light, and the distant, dark bulk of the farmhouse.

“Mr. Pendelton,” he said at last, “the man who moves these stakes is not a farmer. A farmer wears heavy boots with hobnails. The prints here are from a lighter shoe, a boot of good leather, but not for field work. Furthermore, see how the grass is bent here, and here. The man is left-handed. He approaches from the east, works quickly, and leaves. He is not Silas Croft.”

Pendelton looked crestfallen. “Then who, in heaven’s name, can it be?”

“That,” said Vance, “is what we must discover. I should like to speak with Mr. Croft.”

The farmer was exactly as Pendelton had described him: a wiry man with a face like a dried apple and eyes that held a deep and abiding suspicion of all outsiders. He stood in his farmyard, a pitchfork in his hands, and listened to our questions with a sour expression.

“Moved your little sticks, have they?” he grunted. “Well, it’s no work of mine. I’m a simple man. I mind my own business. But I’ll not weep to see your railway come to grief. It’s a noisy, filthy thing, and it’ll spoil the valley.”

“You have no knowledge of the matter?” Vance pressed gently.

“None,” Croft snapped. “And if you’re thinking to look for footprints in my fields, you’ll find naught but the tracks of my own two feet and those of my beasts. Now be off with you. I’ve work to do.”

We left him glowering after us. Back at the works, Vance requested a lantern and a coil of stout twine. As dusk began to fall, he led me to the line of stakes.

“The method is simple, Watson,” he murmured, his voice low. “We shall let the villain come to us. I have noted the precise angle of the diversion. If he is consistent, the next stake he moves will be that one at the end of the line. We shall make it difficult for him.”

He proceeded to tie the twine from the final stake to a nearby fence post at ankle height, then stretched another length a few yards away, creating a subtle tripwire. He then extinguished the lantern, and we settled down behind a clump of gorse, the cold of the Yorkshire night seeping into our bones.

For two hours we waited in silence. The wind moaned across the moor, and a thin drizzle began to fall. I was beginning to think the affair a wild-goose chase when Vance touched my arm. A faint, rhythmic sound reached my ears—the soft tread of someone walking with great care.

A figure emerged from the gloom, moving with a strange, loping gait. It was a man, of middling height, dressed in a dark coat and a flat cap. He carried a small mallet and a measuring rod. He did not look towards the farmhouse, but kept his eyes fixed on the line of the proposed viaduct. He reached the final stake, and as he bent to work, his foot caught in Vance’s twine. He stumbled forward with a sharp cry of annoyance.
