The steam rising from the mutton stew curled into the dim air of the dining room, carrying with it a scent that was less of dinner and more of memory. It was the smell of waiting, of holding one’s breath while the world outside the window of 14 Elmsworth Road settled into its nightly rhythm. Elara sat at the head of the table, the only one, for her brother, Julian, was not there to eat. He was at the University of London, a fact that weighed on the tablecloth like a second, invisible plate.

Her mother, Mrs. Halloway, placed the final bowl of soup before Elara. Her hands were red and raw, the knuckles swollen from the day’s work scrubbing floors in the high street shops. She did not sit down. She remained standing, a rigid figure in a dress that had seen better decades, her eyes fixed on the middle distance as if addressing an audience that did not exist.

"I have finished the accounts," Mrs. Halloway said. Her voice was thin, stretched tight over the silence. "The butcher’s bill is up. The coal merchant has sent a note. We cannot keep the fire burning through the night if we are to keep the roof over our heads."

Elara looked down at her hands. They were clean, unmarked by labor, save for the faint, permanent stain of ink on her left thumb from the last three years of study. "I passed the matriculation, Mama. With distinction. The headmaster said—"

"I know what the headmaster said." Mrs. Halloway cut her off, her voice hardening. "I have read the letter. I have read it four times, Elara. I have read it until the words blurred into a single, meaningless line. And I have looked at the ledger. I have looked at the numbers, and they do not lie. They are cold, and they are final."

Elara looked up. The gaslight flickered, casting long, dancing shadows across the wallpaper, which was peeling slightly at the corners. "You are sending Julian to Cambridge next term. I saw the enrollment fee in your purse."

The silence that followed was louder than the clinking of cutlery. Mrs. Halloway’s jaw tightened. "Julian is a boy. He has a future in the law. He will make us proud. He will secure this family."

"And I am what?" Elara asked. The question hung in the air, brittle and sharp. "I am a girl. I am a burden. I am the variable to be subtracted so that Julian’s equation balances."

"You are not a burden," Mrs. Halloway said, though her voice lacked conviction. "You are a daughter. But you are also a girl of nineteen who has no trade, no skills, and no money. If you continue to sit in that house, studying Latin and history, you will starve. Or you will remain a spinster, dependent on your brother’s charity, which he does not have, and will not have for years. I have found a position. At Mr. Gable’s haberdashery on Sanderstead Road. It opens at seven in the morning. It pays four shillings a week."

Elara felt a coldness spread through her chest, unrelated to the drafty window. "A shop cashier. I am to count pennies and hand out ribbons. I am to smile at men who will look through me as if I am made of glass."

"It is work," Mrs. Halloway insisted. "It is honest work. And it will pay for your board while you save for... for whatever you wish to do next. But it must start Monday."

"I have a scholarship application pending," Elara said, her voice trembling. "The bursary from the Charity for Women’s Education. I wrote the essay. It was on the industrial revolution. I poured everything I had into it."

Mrs. Halloway sighed, a sound of profound exhaustion. "And I have a mortgage to pay. I have a son who needs his fees. I cannot gamble with our survival on a bursary that I do not know if we will get. We must be practical, Elara. We are not aristocrats. We are not even solidly middle-class. We are struggling. And when one struggles, one cuts. One cuts the fat. And right now, in my heart, your education is the fat."

Elara stood up. The chair scraped against the floor, a harsh, jagged sound that seemed to tear the fabric of the room. "You are killing me," she whispered. "Not my body. My mind. You are cutting it out."

"I am keeping us alive!" Mrs. Halloway shouted, her composure finally shattering. Tears welled in her eyes, but they did not fall. She wiped them away with the back of her hand, leaving a streak of grey on her cheek. "Do you think I want this? Do you think I want to send my daughter to stand behind a counter while my son reads poetry in the library? I would rather be dead. But I cannot be dead. I must be alive. I must be here. And to be here, we need money. We need Julian to succeed. Because if he fails, we are nothing. We are dust in the wind."

Elara looked at her mother, really looked at her, for the first time in years. She saw the hollows under her eyes, the grey hairs that had recently appeared at her temples, the way her shoulders had slumped inward, as if trying to make herself smaller, to take up less space in the world. She saw the fear. It was a raw, bleeding thing, exposed by the gaslight.

"I am not a variable," Elara said, her voice quiet but steady. "I am not a number to be balanced. I am your daughter. And I am smart. I am capable. And I am angry."

"I know," Mrs. Halloway said. "I know you are angry. And I am sorry. But the world is not fair, Elara. It does not care about your distinction. It does not care about your essays. It only cares about what you can do, and what you cost. And right now, you cost too much."
