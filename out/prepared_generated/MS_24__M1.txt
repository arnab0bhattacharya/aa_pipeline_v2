It was a grievous truth, which I now carried in my breast like a poisoned arrow, that the very ground upon which I stood, the ancient oaks of Ashcombe that sheltered our repose, and the grey stones of the house itself, were all cemented with a wealth I dared not name. The fortune, which my late father had amassed with such industry and acumen, and which had purchased for me a commission in the glorious service of my King and country, was not the fruit of honest commerce in the Levant, as I had been led to believe from my youth. No; the ledgers I had discovered, hidden in the secret drawer of my father’s escritoire, told a different and a more terrible story. They spoke of ships with names like *The Prosperity* and *The Christian’s Venture* plying a triangular and infernal course; they spoke of human souls, catalogued as chattel, and of fortunes wrung from the misery of the wretched in the plantations of the West Indies.

My father, whose image I had so long held as a paragon of English virtue and rectitude, was in truth a merchant of blood and tears. He had bought this very estate, this symbol of landed respectability, with the price of shackled men and women, whose names were recorded only as numbers in his cold, precise hand. I, who had marched through the scorched plains of Spain and Portugal, who had witnessed the ravages of war and the noble sacrifice of men for the cause of liberty, was now a beneficiary of the very tyranny I had fought against. The hypocrisy of my position was a brand upon my soul. The very medals on my breast felt like leaden weights of shame. I had returned from the Peninsula a hero, my conscience clear, my future bright; now I was an inheritor of infamy, a man living upon a foundation of corruption.

And it was this corruption that stood between me and Eleanor. My dear, my beloved Eleanor! Her soul was a temple of pure and ardent virtue. Her mind, illuminated by the noblest principles, had dedicated itself to the sacred cause of Abolition. With what fire did she speak of the enslaved! With what tears did she lament their suffering! Her voice, which to me was the sweetest music, became a trumpet of righteous indignation when she described the horrors of the Middle Passage. She had read the works of Clarkson and Wilberforce; she had attended the great meetings in London; her heart bled for the oppressed. To her, the slave trade was the one unpardonable sin of the age, a stain upon the national character that must be scoured away with the tears and labours of all good men and women.

How could I, who loved her with a passion that had survived the cannon’s roar and the privations of the march, now approach the altar with her? How could I place a ring upon her finger, a ring bought with the price of chains, and vow to cherish her, when I was hiding from her the very essence of my being? The deceit was a living thing within me; it poisoned my every glance, it twisted my every word. When she spoke of her abhorrence of the planters, of the cruel masters of the colonies, I felt her words strike me as physical blows. I, who should have been her comfort and her shield, was the living embodiment of the thing she despised most in all the world. I was a wolf in sheep’s clothing, a villain cloaked in the uniform of a gentleman.

Last evening, we walked in the garden as the sun bled its last colours across the western sky. The air was heavy with the scent of jasmine, and the only sound was the gentle murmur of the fountain and the distant call of a nightingale. It was a scene of perfect, untroubled peace, a peace that mocked the turmoil in my own breast. Eleanor took my arm, her touch light and trusting, and spoke of our impending union, of the happy life we should lead here at Ashcombe. Her words were a dagger in my heart.

“Arthur,” she said, her face upturned to mine, her eyes shining with a love so pure it was almost painful to behold, “I feel that Providence has brought us together. That from this house, we shall be able to do so much good. Your influence, your fortune, can be a great instrument for the cause of justice.”

Her words were a sentence of condemnation. “My fortune,” I stammered, my voice thick with unspoken agony. I could not bear it. The weight of the secret, the horror of my position, became an intolerable burden. I felt my heart swell against my ribs, as if it would burst from its prison of flesh. I must speak, I must confess, or go mad.

“Eleanor,” I began, my voice breaking, “I must tell you something. Something about my family… about this estate…”

She looked at me, her expression shifting from love to gentle concern. “My dear Arthur, what troubles you so? You have been a shadow for days. Is it the memory of the war? You must know you can tell me anything.”

Her trust was the final turn of the screw. “No, it is not the war,” I cried, pulling my arm from hers and turning to face her. The last light of day caught the anguish in my face. “It is a matter of honour, of conscience! The fortune that maintains this house, that bought my commission, that you believe can be used for good… Eleanor, it was not made in the Levant. It was gathered from the horrors of the slave trade. My father was a slaver. I am the inheritor of a blood-soaked legacy.”
