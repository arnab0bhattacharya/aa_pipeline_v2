The sea at Trennick Cove was the colour of a bruised plum on the day she found the mermaid’s purse. She was seven, and her name was Elara, a name her father said was too fanciful for a Cornish girl, though he had been the one to choose it. The purse was a tough, black, horny thing, empty and dry, and she held it in her palm as if it were a jewel. Her mother, sitting on a plaid rug with her hat tied down against the wind, said it was the egg case of a skate and that she should throw it back. But Elara kept it, burying it deep in the pocket of her pinafore, where it clicked against a smooth white pebble she had also decided was a treasure. That evening, she placed both on the windowsill of their rented cottage, and the last light of the sun, a thin, watery gold, made the pebble glow and the purse look like a piece of the deep, dark sea brought up for her inspection. She fell asleep to the sound of the waves dragging at the shingle, a sound that would, for the rest of her life, mean safety.

The garden at Wren’s Hollow was a riot of unkempt roses and lavender that hummed with bees. Elara was twenty-three, and the man beside her on the stone bench was named Laurence. He was a schoolmaster with kind eyes and hands that were never quite still. He had been talking for some time about the future, about a small house near the school, about the merits of a well-kept ledger. She watched a ladybird crawl across the back of his hand as he spoke, its tiny red shell a spot of brilliant colour against his pale skin. Then he stopped talking and asked her a question, his voice suddenly shy. She did not answer at once. The air was thick with the scent of the lavender, and she felt a strange, disloyal pang for the sea at Trennick, for the cold, clean smell of it. But Laurence was looking at her, his kind eyes full of a hope that was both fragile and persistent. She thought of the ledger, of the small house, of a life that was orderly and good. She smiled and said yes, and he took her hand, and the ladybird flew away.

The house in London was narrow and tall, and the rain on its windows sounded like a thousand tiny fingers drumming. Elara was thirty-five. Her daughter, who was called Iris, was down with a fever, and Elara had not slept properly in two days. She sat by the child’s bed, a cool cloth in her hand, listening to the rattle in Iris’s chest. Laurence was at a meeting of the school board; he would be home soon, with a paper bag of hot chestnuts, a small ritual he had kept up since their courtship. In the dim light of the gas lamp, Elara watched her daughter’s flushed face and felt a terror so profound it was a physical weight on her chest. She thought of the mermaid’s purse, of how she had held it as a child, believing it held some secret power. Now she felt she understood the emptiness at its heart. The door clicked open downstairs, and she heard Laurence’s familiar, heavy tread on the stairs. He entered the room, smelling of damp wool and the outside air, and placed the warm bag of chestnuts on the bedside table. He did not speak, but simply stood behind her and placed his hands on her shoulders. The weight of them was solid and real, and for a moment, the terror receded.

The park in Sussex was a wide, green expanse, and the deckchair was uncomfortable, but Elara was glad of the sun. She was sixty-two. Beside her, her grandson, a boy of six named Timothy, was methodically destroying a daisy. He had red hair and a serious, intent expression. ‘Grandma,’ he said, without looking up from his work, ‘what’s the best thing you’ve ever done?’ Elara considered the question. She thought of the mermaid’s purse, of the lavender garden, of the long nights by Iris’s bedside. She thought of the years of schoolmaster’s dinners, of the careful keeping of the ledger, of the quiet, steady love of Laurence, who had died three years before. She thought of the small, unremarkable acts of her life: the meals cooked, the socks darned, the stories read aloud. ‘I think,’ she said slowly, ‘the best thing I ever did was to sit on a beach and find a mermaid’s purse.’ Timothy looked up, his interest caught. ‘What’s a mermaid’s purse?’ he asked. And she told him, watching his eyes grow wide, and for a moment, she was seven again, the wind in her hair and the sea in her ears.

The room in the nursing home in Bexhill was small and smelled of disinfectant and boiled cabbage. Elara was eighty-nine. She spent most of her days in a chair by the window, watching the grey sea, which was not the sea of Trennick Cove but would have to do. Iris came to visit every Tuesday, bringing soft fruit and news of Timothy, who was now a man with a family of his own. Elara listened, but the words seemed to come from a great distance, like voices from another room. Her memories were not a continuous story but a collection of bright, disconnected fragments, like the pieces of a shattered vase. The smell of lavender. The rattle of a chest. The warmth of a bag of chestnuts. The feel of a smooth white pebble in her hand. She could not string them together. They did not seem to belong to one person. They were just moments, floating in the vast, dark emptiness of time. She looked at her own hands, thin and spotted, resting on the blanket. They were the hands of a stranger.
