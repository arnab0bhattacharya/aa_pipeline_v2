The rain had stopped by noon, leaving the cobbles of Fennelbury’s market square glistening like a seal’s back, and Mr. Abel Quill, auctioneer, appraiser, and the town’s most cheerful repository of secrets, was in his element. He stood on the steps of the old Ashworth house, a fine Georgian building whose windows stared blankly at the street, and addressed the gathering crowd with the practised gusto of a man who had sold everything from prize heifers to a bishop’s library.

“Lot forty-one, ladies and gentlemen! A set of six mahogany chairs, solid as the Bank of England, though perhaps a trifle more comfortable! Who’ll start me at fifteen shillings?”

The crowd, a motley collection of dealers from Birmingham, curious neighbours, and the merely idle, responded with the languid interest of a wet Tuesday. The Ashworth family’s descent had been swift and spectacular. Old Mr. Ashworth, a man of grand visions and poor arithmetic, had sunk the family fortune into a scheme to build a canal that, unfortunately, went nowhere. Now his effects, and those of his late wife, were being scattered to the four winds.

Quill knew them all. He knew that the china tea service had been a wedding gift from a jilted suitor who had never quite forgiven Mrs. Ashworth. He knew that the library’s copy of *Paradise Lost* had a pressed violet in it from a summer long past. He knew, too, that the Ashworth’s eldest son, a decent but dull-witted fellow, was now clerking for a solicitor in Wolverhampton, and that the daughter had taken a position as a governess in a household where the master was known to be a pinch-penny. Quill felt a genuine, if theatrical, pang of sympathy for them all. He was a man who thrived on the foibles of others, but he was not without a heart.

The sale wore on. The crowd thinned as the rain returned, a fine, persistent drizzle that made the air smell of wet wool and damp brick. Quill, his voice growing hoarse, finally reached the last items, a collection of oddments from the study.

“And here, gentlemen, we have a job lot of miscellaneous papers, correspondence, and what-have-you. A veritable Pandora’s box of the late Mrs. Ashworth’s personal effects. Ideal for lighting a fire, or for the incurably curious. What am I bid? A shilling? Sixpence?”

A dealer from Coventry, a man with a face like a crumpled paper bag, offered a shilling. Quill’s gavel was raised when a voice from the back, quiet but firm, cut through the murmur.

“Two shillings.”

Quill squinted. It was Mr. Percival Wren, the town’s most respected banker, a man whose very gait suggested the solidity of a vault. He was a churchwarden, a magistrate, and a pillar of such unassailable probity that the very idea of him attending a house clearance was a scandal in itself. He stood apart from the others, his umbrella dripping a neat circle on the flagstones, his eyes fixed on the bundle of papers Quill held.

“Two shillings from Mr. Wren!” Quill boomed, delighted by this unexpected turn. “A discerning bid! Any advance on two shillings?”

The Coventry dealer shook his head, sensing a private matter. The gavel fell. “Sold to Mr. Wren for two shillings!”

Later, in the back room of the King’s Head, where Quill was enjoying a restorative glass of porter, Mr. Wren found him. The banker’s usual composure was frayed at the edges. He held the bundle of letters, tied with a faded ribbon, as if it were a live rat.

“Quill,” he said, his voice low. “I must speak with you on a matter of some… delicacy.”

Quill wiped a froth moustache from his lip. “But of course, Mr. Wren. A fine purchase. Family history, is it? The Ashworths were a rum lot.”

Wren placed the packet on the table between them. “These letters… they were not meant for public consumption. They were written by Mrs. Ashworth to her sister. They contain… certain allegations. About myself.”

Quill’s eyebrows, which were large and expressive, rose a full inch. “Allegations, sir? Of what nature?”

Wren’s face was a mask of anguish. “They concern a transaction, many years ago, before I was established. A loan I facilitated for Mr. Ashworth. The terms were… usurious. He was desperate. I was young and ambitious. She knew. She wrote it all down. If these were to become public, my reputation, my position as magistrate, my very standing in this town…” He trailed off, the words hanging in the smoky air.

Quill understood immediately. The whole edifice of Percival Wren, the church pew, the bank, the magistrate’s bench, was built on a foundation of unimpeachable character. A whiff of scandal would bring it all down. The Ashworths, in their ruin, held the match that could light the fuse.

“What would you have me do, Mr. Wren?” Quill asked, his cheerfulness for once entirely absent. “The sale is final. The letters are yours.”

“I know that, you fool,” Wren hissed, his composure cracking. “But you are the auctioneer. You have a duty. I am asking you to… to advise me. To help me. I could pay you. Handsomely.”

Quill looked at the packet. He thought of Mrs. Ashworth, a proud woman he had once seen chase a stray dog from her garden with a parasol, her dignity intact even in a moment of absurdity. He thought of her daughter, the governess, and her son, the clerk, trying to rebuild their lives from the wreckage. The sale of these letters, if they were sold to a scandalmonger, would be a final, cruel twist. But Wren’s offer was tempting. A hundred pounds, perhaps more. It would be enough to retire his own small debts, to buy his wife a new winter coat.
