The rain lashed against the leaded panes of the study window, a relentless, hissing hiss that seemed to mock the silence of the room. I sat rigid in the high-backed chair, my hands folded tightly in my lap to conceal their trembling. Across the desk, the Headmistress, Madame Verhaeghe, sat with her back to the storm, her face a mask of polite, immutable disapproval. Behind her, in the shadows of the alcove, stood Elise. The girl was sixteen, with eyes like dark, wet stones, and she did not weep; she simply watched me, her gaze heavy with the terrible weight of accusation.

"You must know," Madame Verhaeghe said, her voice soft, precise, and utterly devoid of warmth, "that the trust placed in you, Madame Halloway, is not a toy to be trifled with. Elise speaks of letters. Private correspondence. She says you read them."

I opened my mouth, but the words came out in a foreign tongue, clumsy and broken. *No. Je n'ai pas. I did not.*

Madame Verhaeghe sighed, a sound that carried the fatigue of a woman who had dealt with such things for decades. "We will speak in English, Madame Halloway, for your sake. But know this: the school has a reputation. It is built on the honor of its pupils and the integrity of its staff. If you cannot defend yourself clearly, I must assume the truth lies in what is before me."

I looked at Elise. The girl’s chin was lifted, a defiance in her posture that I had never seen in her before. She was a quiet child, usually shrinking from attention, yet now she stood as a pillar of cold accusation.

"Elise," I said, my voice steady despite the hammering of my heart. "Do you swear it? On your life and your honor?"

"I swear it," she replied, her accent thick but her tone unyielding. "I saw you with my letters. You read them in the library, while I was at dinner. You laughed."

The memory of the incident struck me like a physical blow. I had not read her letters. I had been in the library, yes, but I had been waiting for a friend, Mrs. Van Daele, who had run late. I had seen a stack of envelopes on the table, unattended, and I had turned them over to see if they were addressed to my own name, a habit born of solitude. I had seen only a fragment of handwriting, a name, and then I had set them down. I had not opened them. I had not read a word. But how could I say this? How could I explain the confusion of that moment, the rain, the fog, the isolation of my life in this strange, damp city?

"Madame Hallowey," Madame Verhaeghe continued, "you have no friends here. You are alone. You have no family to vouch for you. When a pupil accuses you, and you have no one to speak for you, the scales are not even."

Injustice. The word rose in my throat, bitter and sharp. It was the same injustice that had always haunted my childhood, the sense of being trapped, of being defined by the opinions of those who held power over me. I was not a child now, yet I felt the same suffocating pressure. I was a woman, educated and capable, yet here I was, reduced to a defendant in my own life, unable to speak the language of the court, unable to command the respect that should have been mine.

"I ask you," I said, forcing the words to be clear, "to check the library ledger. I was there from four to five. Mrs. Van Daele arrived at five. She can testify to my presence. I was not alone."

Madame Verhaeghe’s eyes flickered to Elise. "And you, Elise? Where were you?"

"I was at dinner," Elise said simply. "I left my letters on the table. I returned, and they were gone. Then I saw Madame Hallowey holding them."

It was a lie. I knew it was a lie. I could see it in the slight tension of her jaw, in the way she avoided my gaze. But she was a pupil, and I was the accused. In the hierarchy of this school, her word was worth more than mine.

I stood up. My chair scraped against the floor, a harsh, jarring sound.

"Madame Verhaeghe," I said, my voice rising, "you are asking me to accept a lie. You are asking me to abandon my position, my livelihood, my dignity, based on the word of a child who is lying. I will not stand for it. I have done nothing wrong. I have kept my distance, I have taught my classes with care, I have treated every student with respect. And now, because of a moment’s confusion, because of a girl’s jealousy or cruelty, I am to be cast out."

"Calm yourself, Madame Halloway," Madame Verhaeghe said, her voice hardening. "Passion is no substitute for evidence. You are making it worse."

"Passion!" I cried. "Is it not passion that drives me to speak? Is it not the fire of truth that burns in me? You sit there, safe in your authority, and you call it passion. But I, who have no authority, who have no power, I am expected to be silent? I am expected to accept your judgment without question?"

The storm outside seemed to grow louder, the wind howling through the eaves, a ghostly moan that echoed my own inner turmoil. The fire in the grate crackled, throwing long, distorted shadows on the walls. I looked at Elise again. She was pale, but her eyes were hard. She had made her choice. She had chosen to destroy me, and in doing so, she had destroyed herself.

"I will not resign," I said, my voice low now, but steady. "I will wait for Mrs. Van Daele. I will wait for the truth. And when it comes, you will see that I have been wronged."
