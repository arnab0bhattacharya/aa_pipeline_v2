The rain had not ceased for three days, and the house in Wetherby Lane—a tall, narrow house of dark stone, with windows that looked upon the dripping sycamores of the chapel yard—seemed to gather the greyness of the sky into its very walls. Within, in the parlour that served Miss Aldworth for her schoolroom and her sanctuary both, a fire burned with a fierce, concentrated heat, as though it warred against the damp that crept under doors and through the ill-fitting sashes. Before it sat the mistress of the house, her hands—thin hands, but strong, the knuckles prominent—folded upon the black stuff of her gown, her eyes fixed not upon the flames but upon the young woman who stood at the window, watching the rain streak the glass.

“You will not be persuaded, then,” said Miss Aldworth at length. Her voice was low and dry; it had the quality of a door that has been shut many years upon its hinges.

Edith Fanshawe turned. She was seven-and-twenty, and her face, though not beautiful, had a clearness and a resolution in it that beauty often lacks; her eyes were grey and steady, and her mouth, which could be soft, was now set in a line that bespoke a will not easily bent. “No, Aunt Margaret. I shall not be persuaded. The vessel sails on Tuesday, and I shall be upon it.”

“Aunt Margaret.” The elder woman repeated the name with a curious intonation—half mockery, half pain. “You have called me that since you were a child of ten, when your father left you at my door with his blessing and his debts, and went to his grave without another thought for either. I have been your aunt in kindness, Edith, though there was no blood between us. And now you will go to the other end of the earth, and I shall never see you again.”

“You might have come with me,” said Edith quietly. “I asked you. I ask you still.”

Miss Aldworth’s lips tightened. “To New Zealand? To a colony of savages and sheep-farmers? To teach the daughters of shopkeepers and transported felons? I am sixty-three years old, Edith. I have kept this school for thirty years. I have buried a husband and a child in this town, and I have built what I have with my own hands. I will not leave it to die in a wooden hut at the world’s end.”

“And I will not stay to die in a stone one,” returned Edith, and her voice, though quiet, had an edge that cut. She came forward from the window and stood before the fire, her hands clasped before her, her figure straight and taut as a bowstring. “You speak of what you have built. Do you think I do not know it? I have watched you these fifteen years. I have seen you rise at five and labour until midnight; I have seen you deny yourself warmth, and rest, and company, and every softness of life, that the school might prosper and your name be respected. I have seen you refuse offers of marriage—two that I know of—because you would not surrender your independence to a man. You have been a brave woman, Aunt Margaret. I honour you for it. But you have paid for your independence with your heart, and you have never once confessed the price.”

Miss Aldworth rose from her chair. She was a tall woman, and in her youth had been handsome; the ruins of that handsomeness were still visible in the fine bones of her face, the clear line of her brow. “You are insolent,” she said.

“I am truthful,” said Edith. “And I am going away, so I may be truthful at last. You have never forgiven me for not being the daughter you lost. You have never forgiven me for having a father who was weak and a mother who died, and for coming to you with nothing but a pair of frightened eyes and a hunger for love. You fed me and clothed me and taught me, and you never once told me that you loved me. You could not. It was not in your nature, or it was not in your creed. And so I have learned to live without it. I have learned to make my own way, as you made yours. And now I am going to a country where there is room to breathe, where a woman is not measured by the breadth of her parlour or the number of her pupils, where I may build something of my own—not a school, perhaps, but a life. And you call it folly, and you call it desertion, because you cannot bear that I should succeed where you have only endured.”

The words fell into the silence like stones into deep water. Miss Aldworth stood very still. The firelight played upon her face, and for a moment—one moment only—something moved in her eyes that was not anger. Then she turned away and walked to the window, and looked out upon the rain.

“You think me a hard woman,” she said at last, and her voice was not quite steady. “Perhaps I am. I was not always so. When I was your age, Edith, I loved a man who was a schoolmaster in this town—a gentle man, with a soft voice and a scholar’s hands. He asked me to marry him. I refused him, because I had seen my mother die in childbed, and I had seen my father break his heart over her grave, and I was afraid. I told myself I was afraid of losing my freedom. I was afraid of losing myself. And so I let him go, and he married another woman, and I have lived alone ever since. I have made my school my child, and my reputation my comfort, and I have told myself that it was enough. But it is not enough. It is not enough, Edith, and I know it, and I have known it for thirty years, and I have never said it aloud until now.”
