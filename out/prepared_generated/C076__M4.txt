Mr. Ezekiel Groat, parish constable of St. Botolph’s-Within, was a man who carried his authority not so much as a mantle but as a full suit of armour, clanking it at every opportunity. He was a large, solemn fellow with whiskers that seemed to have been pasted on with the same stubbornness he applied to the law, and a voice that he pitched to a permanent, official baritone. His sense of his own importance was a palpable thing, a third presence in any room he entered, demanding a chair and a dish of tea. It was this very quality that drew him, with the gravity of a planet settling into orbit, to the cookshop of Mrs. Prudence Vellacott on Candlewick Lane.

The shop was a low-ceilinged, steamy place, redolent of boiled beef and fresh bread, a kingdom of savoury warmth presided over by its widow-queen. Mrs. Vellacott was a woman of fifty with a face like a well-used frying pan—lined, seasoned, and utterly practical. Her late husband had left her the business and a tidy sum besides, all of it carefully hoarded and none of it squandered on frivolity. She was respected in the parish, but it was a respect of the sort given to a reliable clock: one noted its function, not its person. This, to her, was a deficiency. She saw in Mr. Groat, with his constable’s staff and his ponderous pronouncements on vagrancy and the licensing of hackney coaches, a chance to acquire not just a husband, but a title. Mrs. Constable Groat. It had a fine, solid ring to it, like a good brass door-knocker.

He, for his part, saw in her a comfortable home, a constant supply of hot dinners, and, most crucially, the substantial savings he was certain were tucked away in the tea-caddy. He imagined himself, after a decent interval, retiring from the rigours of parish duty to a life of ease, his wife’s fortune funding a small establishment and perhaps a pony-trap. Their courtship was a masterpiece of mutual, unspoken calculation. He would sit in her shop of an evening, sipping a glass of her porter, and orate on the importance of a well-ordered household, the value of thrift, and the sacred duties of a wife. She would nod, wipe down a table, and murmur her agreement, her eyes fixed on the solid silver buckles of his shoes, imagining them as a down-payment on respectability.

Their engagement was announced with little fanfare and much private satisfaction on both sides. The wedding was set for a Tuesday morning at the parish church, a quiet affair to be followed by a cold collation in the cookshop. As the day approached, a certain tension, thin as a hairline crack in a teacup, began to show. Mr. Groat, while ostensibly helping with the arrangements, had taken to questioning Mrs. Vellacott about her ledgers, suggesting that a man of his administrative experience might, in future, take a more active hand in the business’s finances. Mrs. Vellacott, in turn, had begun to drop hints about the dignity of her future position, and how a constable’s wife must be seen to be a woman of substance, perhaps with a new bonnet from a fashionable milliner and a girl to help in the kitchen.

The wedding morning dawned grey and damp. Mr. Groat, resplendent in a bottle-green coat that was a little too tight across the shoulders, arrived at the cookshop early. He found his bride-to-be in the parlour, already in her best plum-coloured silk, a formidable sight. The air was thick with the smell of a ham boiling for the collation. He cleared his throat, a preparatory rumble that heralded a pronouncement.

“Ahem. Prudence. My dear. On this auspicious morn, I believe it is meet that we should have a clear understanding of the practicalities that shall govern our union.”

Mrs. Vellacott, who was adjusting a jet brooch at her throat, gave him a sharp look in the mirror. “Practicalities, Mr. Groat? I thought we were to be married.”

“Indeed, indeed,” he said, waving a hand as if to dismiss such trifles. “But a man in my position must think ahead. Your little business here… it has been a credit to you, but it is hardly a fitting enterprise for the wife of a parish constable. I have been giving the matter my most profound consideration. I believe we should sell the lease. I have a mind to invest the capital—along with your other… shall we say, accumulated funds—in a more genteel concern. A stationer’s, perhaps. It would be more becoming.”

He beamed at her, expecting her to be dazzled by his foresight. Instead, she turned slowly from the mirror, her face a mask of disbelief. “Sell the shop?” she repeated, her voice dangerously low. “And what ‘accumulated funds’ do you refer to, sir?”

“Why, your savings, my dear,” he said, a trifle impatiently. “The money you have put by from the profits of this… this cookery. It is our money now, you understand, and I am best placed to manage it for our mutual benefit.”

Mrs. Vellacott’s laugh was a short, sharp sound, like a cracker being pulled. “Our money? Mr. Groat, you have taken lodgings with me for six months and paid your rent with a punctuality I can only commend. But I have seen your accounts, sir, in the letters you leave open on your table. You have a constable’s salary and a pile of debts from your last post in Southwark. It is not my savings that will be invested, but my savings that will be paying for your new coat and your pony-trap, I’ll be bound!”

Mr. Groat’s jaw went slack, his whiskers seeming to droop. “My dear Mrs. Vellacott, I am shocked! Shocked and wounded! I offer you the protection of my name, the respectability of my office! A woman in trade, a widow, alone in the world—you should be on your knees in gratitude! I am marrying you for your own good!”
