The dining room at the Villa Rosalba was filled with the heavy scent of overcooked meat and the faint residue of yesterday’s lavender polish. Miss Anning, who had spent thirty years teaching the daughters of country squires to play the piano with stiff, unyielding wrists, sat before her portion of grey fish, seeing not the fish but the shimmering, evasive quality of the light that fell through the slatted shutters. It broke against the white tablecloth in long, striped tremors, making the silver salt-cellars seem to swim. She was aware, as one is aware of the ticking of a clock in a room where one is lying awake, of the precise angle at which Mrs. Thorney’s daughter, Agnes, was holding her spoon. The girl possessed a tragic thinness, a pallor that seemed to absorb the very colours of the room, and Miss Anning thought, as she often thought in these morning hours, how completely the dead weight of Mrs. Thorney’s widowhood pressed upon the girl’s shoulders.

Mrs. Thorney, indeed, sat there like a heavy stone in the centre of a stream, forcing the current of life to divide and flow around her. She wore a brooch of black jet that caught the light and threw it back with a hard, mechanical gleam; she spoke of the temperature, which was mild for February, though the mistral had been biting the night before, rattling the glass doors that led out to the garden. But the words were merely the surface of things, the thin, brittle ice over the deep water. Beneath it, Miss Anning felt the vast, unspoken sorrow of the woman, the way she continually hoarded her grief, keeping it sharp and polished, as if to let it dull would be a betrayal of the man who had died in Shropshire two winters past.

“Yes, quite mild,” said Mr. Vivian, from the far end of the table. He spoke with the polite, slightly breathless tone of a man whose lungs have recently been compromised. He had come to Menton to recover from a fever, and the illness seemed to have left him transparent, stripped of all superfluous flesh, so that the blue veins at his temples stood out like the rivers on an antique map. He was looking at the fish, but his mind was wandering among the oleander bushes in the garden, where a single, late bloom was shedding its pink petals onto the dry, dusty earth. He felt a sudden, overwhelming pity for the petals, which seemed to him infinitely vulnerable, and then for himself, and then for Agnes Thorney, who was swallowing her tea with a motion that conveyed a profound, unspoken weariness.

It was at such moments, when the clatter of forks against porcelain seemed to expand until it filled the entire room and then contracted into a sudden, breathless silence, that the painter, Mr. Crane, would push his chair back. He was a large man, unkempt, with paint stains on his cuffs that the French laundry could never entirely eradicate. He looked at the world with a furious, dissatisfied intensity, as though it had promised him something which it continually failed to deliver. He had been trying for a week to capture the exact hue of the mountains at dusk—that peculiar, powdery blue that melted into violet, which was not the blue of pigment but the blue of distance, of air, of time itself. He failed, continually; and each failure left a mark upon his face, a deepening of the lines around his mouth, a shadow in his eyes that made Miss Anning, watching him from beneath her grey eyelashes, feel a strange, maternal tenderness mixed with fear.

“Will you walk this morning, Mr. Vivian?” Mrs. Thorney asked, her voice cutting across the silence with the precision of a pair of scissors.

“I think I shall,” he replied. “The air by the sea wall…”

He did not finish the sentence. There was no need to finish it; they all knew the sea wall, the way the waves broke against the limestone blocks, leaving dark, wet patches that dried white in the sun, and the way the pines grew at angles, bent by the relentless pressure of the wind. They all knew it, and the knowledge of it bound them together, making the Villa Rosalba not merely a pension but a small, self-contained universe, drifting through the winter months on the edge of the Mediterranean.

Later, when the morning had ripened into a hazy, white heat, Miss Anning found herself walking along the path that skirted the cliffs. The sea was not blue today but green, a deep, glassy green, like the inside of a bottle, with flashes of silver where the fish darted beneath the surface. She walked slowly, her boots crunching on the gravel, and she thought of the years she had spent in other people’s houses, teaching other people’s children, and how now, at the age of fifty, she was finally, terrifyingly, alone. The thought did not depress her; rather, it gave her a sensation of space, of air rushing into a vacuum. She stopped and looked out at the water, and the water seemed to look back at her, vast, indifferent, and beautiful.

A little way ahead, Mr. Vivian was sitting on a bench, his hat in his hand, letting the breeze ruffle his hair. He looked fragile, like a plant that has been kept too long in a dark room and is suddenly exposed to the light. Beside him sat Agnes Thorney, and they were not speaking. They were watching a small boat with a brown sail that was tacking across the bay, making its way towards the harbour. Miss Anning saw that their hands, resting on the wooden slats of the bench, were almost touching, but not quite. There was a space between them, perhaps an inch, and that inch seemed to Miss Anning to contain more life, more unspoken possibility, than all the words that had ever been spoken in the Villa Rosalba.
