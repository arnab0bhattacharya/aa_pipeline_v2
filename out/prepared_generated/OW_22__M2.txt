The rain in London does not fall; it hovers, a grey curtain drawn across the stage of the world to hide the actors from the audience. It was such a night when Julian Vane stood before the fire in the library of Lady Ashworth, watching the flames dance with a lethargy that mirrored his own mood. He was a young man of twenty-eight, possessed of a face that critics called “classically handsome” and clients called “deceptively calm,” and a mind that moved with the precision of a Swiss watch and the treachery of a double-edged sword. He had risen in the legal profession not by brute force, but by the subtle application of wit, which is the only weapon that never dulls because it is never used in anger, only in amusement.

Lady Ashworth entered the room. She was a woman of fifty, draped in velvet the colour of crushed violets, her hair pinned so high that it seemed to defy gravity, much like her own position in society. She carried with her the scent of old roses and old money, a perfume that Julian found more intoxicating than any young woman’s freshness.

“You have a look of profound boredom, Mr. Vane,” she said, her voice a low contralto that vibrated in the very stones of the floor. “Or perhaps it is the look of a man who is calculating the cost of a meal he has not yet eaten.”

Julian turned, his eyes bright with the particular sparkle that preceded his most devastating parries. “My Lady, I assure you, my boredom is strictly intellectual. I am merely pondering the definition of freedom. I have discovered that it is a very expensive commodity, and one that is usually sold in instalments to those who cannot afford the lump sum.”

Lady Ashworth smiled, a expression that was half amusement and half pity. “Freedom is for beggars and poets, Julian. The rest of us have obligations. And you, my dear fellow, have a very specific obligation hanging over your head like a sword of Damocles, though I suspect the rope is made of silk.”

He knew then what she intended. The air in the room grew thick, charged with the electricity of an unspoken proposition. “You speak of my future,” Julian said, keeping his tone light, as if discussing the weather. “I had thought it was to be spent in the Court, arguing cases that were less interesting than this conversation, for clients who are less charming than you.”

Lady Ashworth moved to the window, her back to him, silhouetted against the grey gloom. “I am going to offer you a seat in Parliament. It is a seat I have coveted for ten years, and one that I will not share with a man who lacks ambition. But the seat has a condition. It is not a condition of political allegiance, though I shall expect that as a matter of course. It is a condition of domestic arrangement.”

Julian felt a cold thread of anticipation run down his spine. “And that arrangement?”

“Marriage,” she said simply. “To my niece, Clara. She is a girl of twenty, with a fortune that is adequate, a temper that is manageable, and a face that is pleasant enough to pass for attractive in the right light. She is, in short, the perfect vessel for a man of your talents to store his reputation in.”

Julian laughed. It was a short, dry sound, devoid of warmth. “A perfect vessel? My Lady, one does not marry a vessel. One marries a person. Or, in my case, one might marry a situation.”

“Clara is both,” Lady Ashworth replied, turning to face him. Her eyes were dark and deep, holding a secret that Julian could feel but not quite grasp. “She is a person of no particular significance, which makes her ideal. She will not interfere with your intellect. She will not demand your time. She will provide the cover, the respectability, the social armor that allows you to do what you truly wish to do.”

“I am not certain I know what I truly wish to do,” Julian said, though his heart was beating with a rhythm that was far from calm. “I suspect I wish to remain exactly where I am, in this library, listening to your voice.”

Lady Ashworth’s expression did not change, but a shadow flickered across her face, a momentary lapse in the mask of aristocratic composure. “That is a dangerous sentiment, Julian. It is the sentiment of a man who mistakes the shadow for the substance. Clara is the substance. I am the shadow. Marry the substance, and the shadow will follow. Refuse, and you will have neither.”

The silence that followed was not empty, but full of the weight of unsaid things. Julian looked at her, really looked at her, seeing not the patron, not the aunt, but the woman who had, in the quiet hours of the night, stolen his peace with the mere beauty of her mind. He loved her. It was a fact as plain and as useless as the fact that the rain was falling. He loved her with the passion of a man who knows he is doomed to watch a beautiful thing from a distance, never daring to step forward and shatter its perfection.

“If I refuse,” Julian said, his voice low, “do I lose the seat?”

“You lose everything,” she corrected. “The seat, the prospects, the admiration of the class that I represent. You will remain a brilliant barrister, which is a title that sounds impressive but amounts to very little in the grand scheme of things. You will be a cog in a machine, polished and useful, but ultimately replaceable.”

“And if I accept?”
