If any dispassionate observer had stood at the crossroads of Mudley-in-the-Marsh on that fateful autumn morning, he might have observed the town to be in a state of tremulous agitation. The bells of St. Botolph’s were ringing with a fervour that suggested the kingdom had been delivered from a foreign yoke, the mud of the high street was being frantically swept by a dozen charwomen, and the very sparrows seemed to chirp with a civic consciousness. For this was the day appointed for the grand banquet to celebrate the erection of the New Town Hall; an edifice which Alderman Sputterwick had vowed would make Mudley-in-the-Marsh the envy of the surrounding shires, and which had been promised to possess a roof of such magnificent solidity that the rain might beat upon it in vain.

But Nature, indifferent to aldermen and their vanities, had gathered her forces. The sky was of a leaden, bloated aspect, swelling with unshed water, and the wind blew with a damp, biting malice. It was against this theatrical backdrop that Mr. Job Pumblechook, the contractor, was discovered to be missing. He was not merely absent from his lodgings; he had vanished, taking with him his trunk, his family, and the very substantial sum of public money advanced for slate, timber, and the wages of sundry artisans. The roof of the New Town Hall, a grand skeleton of rafters, stared openly at the heavy sky, as if inviting the clouds to do their worst.

When this intelligence was conveyed to Alderman Sputterwick, that pompous functionary was in his parlour, brushing his civic robes with a small fox-hair brush and humming a tune. The messenger was Mr. Grubble, the town clerk, a man whose natural complexion was that of a pale jelly, and who now trembled so violently that his papers rattled.

"Gone, did you say, Grubble?" quoth the Alderman, not pausing in his brushing. "Gone where?"

"To London, sir, or to the Americas, or to the bottom of the river," stammered Grubble. "He left in the night, sir. And the roof—the roof is but a lattice of bare spars. The slaters downed their tools and walked away, declaring they were unpaid. The rain comes, sir!"

Alderman Sputterwick turned upon him a face of thunder. "Rain, Grubble? What is rain to the municipal enterprise of Mudley-in-the-Marsh?" He puffed out his chest until the buttons of his waistcoat strained. "The banquet is ordered. The Mayor of Brixby is coming. The Member for the Division is coming. We shall not be moved by the flight of a vagabond or the impertinence of the weather. The hall is built. The walls are up. If the roof is... unfinished, it is merely an architectural choice, sir! An open-air court, a tribute to the classical forums of antiquity! We shall preside in the refreshment of the breeze!"

Grubble, who knew his master's temper, swallowed his alarm and nodded, retreating to his office to draft a series of increasingly desperate memorandums.

By four o'clock, the guests began to assemble in the principal street, their carriages splashing through a thoroughfare that had turned to a thin, brown soup. The clouds, which had merely threatened all day, now burst. The rain did not fall in drops; it fell in sheets, in solid masses of water, driven sideways by a wind that howled up the street and rattled every shutter. It was a rain that seemed to have a personal grievance against the town of Mudley-in-the-Marsh.

Undeterred, Alderman Sputterwick took his place at the head of the long table, set upon a temporary platform in the centre of the roofless hall. The walls of bare brick rose around them, offering the illusion of shelter, but the sky above was as black and wet as the inside of a water-butt. The guests, in their silks and broadcloths, took their seats with forced smiles and damp feet. The Mayor of Brixby, a choleric gentleman named Choke, mopped his bald head and whispered that it was a disgrace; but Alderman Sputterwick merely tapped his glass with a spoon.

"Gentlemen!" he cried, his voice rising above the drumming of the rain upon the tables. "We are met to celebrate a monument to our civic progress. The elements themselves conspire to join our festivities! We are not afraid of a little water, gentlemen! It is the lifeblood of the earth!"

"Amen," muttered the Member for the Division, as a rivulet of cold water ran down the back of his neck.

The banquet commenced. The turtle soup was served, thin and lukewarm, for the kitchen fire had been somewhat dampened by a cascade from the rafters. The guests ate in a subdued, splashing manner, mopping their brows and their plates with napkins. The very candles upon the table guttered and hissed in the damp, casting wild, flickering shadows upon the walls, making the brickwork seem to heave and breathe. The rain fell upon the table with a steady, drumming persistence, filling the wine glasses with a pale, diluted vintage, and turning the bread to a soft paste.

Alderman Sputterwick, however, ate and drank with a determined grandeur. He was seated beneath a large green umbrella, which his man held above him, and from beneath this canopy he dispensed compliments and speeches. The meat was carved by Mr. Grubble, whose spectacles were so clouded with mist that he could not distinguish a leg of mutton from a knuckle of veal, and who frequently apologised to the joint itself.

"Gentlemen," continued the Alderman, "I perceive that some of you are looking upwards. You are admiring the noble rafters, no doubt! The honest English oak, sir! What could be more fitting than to sit beneath the boughs of our native timber, unadorned by foreign slate? It is a design, gentlemen, a design of my own conception, to bring the citizens of Mudley closer to nature, to the very clouds that water our prosperous fields!"
