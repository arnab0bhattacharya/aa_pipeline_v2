The situation of Mr. Henry Ellerton, of Winstone Hall in Derbyshire, was generally allowed to be one of the most pitiable that a young man of good family and agreeable person could possibly be placed in. He had inherited, at the age of three-and-twenty, an estate which every neighbour agreed to be the finest in the county, a house of handsome proportions, and a park of such extent and beauty as to draw admiring comparisons from all who beheld it; and he had inherited, with these advantages, a burden of debt so considerable, and so entangled in its character, that the income of the property, though ample, was scarcely sufficient to discharge the annual interest, and left him nothing whatever wherewith to live. To sell was impossible without disgrace; to remain was impossible without ruin; and Mr. Ellerton, who was a gentleman of very amiable disposition and very little resolution, had spent two years in the agreeable occupation of lamenting his fate to his friends, and in the somewhat less agreeable occupation of avoiding his creditors, before it occurred to him that there was one course still open to him, which had the double recommendation of being both respectable and effectual.

It was his aunt, Mrs. Harcourt, a widow lady of strong understanding and stronger opinions, who first pronounced the word which had been hovering upon every tongue but his own.

“You must marry money, Henry,” said she, with the decision of a woman who had long since given up the attempt to be delicate. “There is nothing else for it. You cannot sell the estate; you cannot let it; you cannot live upon it; and you cannot, I hope, be content to remain a burden upon your friends. Marry a fortune, and the whole is settled.”

Mr. Ellerton received this advice with the graceful melancholy which distinguished him. He had always, he said, been of a domestic turn; he had no objection whatever to matrimony; indeed, he had often thought that a wife would be the greatest comfort to him in his difficulties. But where was he to find a lady who would accept a man whose very estate was a species of mortgage, and whose person was encumbered with a debt of honour which he could not name without a blush?

“There is Miss Ashby,” said Mrs. Harcourt, without hesitation.

Mr. Ellerton looked up. Miss Ashby was the only daughter of Mr. Ashby of Millford, a manufacturer of cotton, whose wealth was reported to be immense, and whose origin was reported to be nothing at all. The family had lately purchased a small estate in the neighbourhood, and had taken their place in the county with a quietness which some called modesty and others impudence, according to the temper of the speaker. Miss Ashby was said to be handsome, accomplished, and proud; and her father was said to be worth a hundred thousand pounds.

“Miss Ashby!” repeated Mr. Ellerton, with an air of gentle surprise. “My dear aunt, she would never look at a man in my situation.”

“She will look at you very particularly,” replied Mrs. Harcourt, “if you present yourself with the proper assurance. You are a gentleman, Henry, and the possessor of Winstone; and these are things which money cannot buy, and which the Ashbys, to do them justice, are likely to value. Go and pay your respects to her. You have nothing to lose.”

Nothing to lose was, in Mr. Ellerton’s estimation, the strongest argument that could be urged; and accordingly, within the week, he presented himself at Millford, in a new coat and with a countenance composed to the most interesting degree of gentle dejection. He was received by Mr. Ashby with a civility which was almost cordial, and by Miss Ashby with a composure which he found it difficult to interpret. She was a young woman of three-and-twenty, with a fine complexion, a pair of very steady dark eyes, and a manner which was neither shy nor forward, but which seemed to be the result of a settled habit of thinking for herself, and of having her thoughts attended to. She conversed with him on the weather, on the county, on the pleasures of a country life; and Mr. Ellerton, who had prepared a variety of elegant speeches upon the subject of his own misfortunes, found himself, to his great surprise, unable to introduce a single one of them.

He returned a second time, and a third; and upon each occasion he was received with the same civil composure, and found himself compelled to talk upon subjects which had no connexion whatever with his debts. He began to be of opinion that Miss Ashby was a very agreeable young woman, and that her fortune was almost a secondary consideration. He was not, however, a man to be hurried into forgetfulness of his own interests; and upon the fourth visit, he resolved to bring matters to a point. He had prepared a speech, in which his situation was to be disclosed with a delicacy that should soften the disclosure, and his affection declared with a warmth that should excuse it; and he had just reached the most affecting passage of this composition, when Miss Ashby, who had been listening with the utmost attention, interrupted him.

“Mr. Ellerton,” said she, “I am very sensible of the honour you do me; but before you proceed further, I think it right to tell you that I am perfectly acquainted with your situation.”

Mr. Ellerton started. “My situation, madam?”

“Your estate,” said Miss Ashby, with the same steady composure, “is encumbered to the amount of forty thousand pounds. The interest upon the mortgages is regularly paid, I believe, but the principal is not to be commanded; and your income, after these payments, is barely sufficient for the necessary expenses of the property. You have, besides, a debt of honour of five thousand pounds, contracted at Bath, which you have never been able to discharge, and which is the more pressing of the two.
