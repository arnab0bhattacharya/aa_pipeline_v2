The first thing I noticed was the silence. Not a true silence, you understand, for there was the wind in the lime trees and the distant hum of some manner of machinery, but a silence of the human sort—an absence of the cries of children, of the clatter of carts, of all that random, cheerful uproar that spells life in a place where people have things to do. I had awakened, so far as I could determine, in a bed of singular comfort in a room of admirable cleanliness and no particular character, and had spent some hours in a state of profound bewilderment before a tall, grey-haired man in a suit of sober blue entered and addressed me with a civility so entire and so remote that it chilled me more than any violence could have done.

His name, he said, was Mr. Elton, and he was my—the word he used escapes me; it was some compound of “custodian” and “physician”—my attendant, let us say. He explained that I had been found, insensible and curiously dressed, upon the downs near the town of Ashcombe Regis, and that I had been brought hither for my own comfort and safety. He spoke of my “displacement” as one might speak of a sprained ankle, and when I demanded the date, he gave it with a readiness that suggested the question was not unexpected.

“The year is two thousand and ninety-one,” he said, “by the old reckoning. We keep no other.”

I confess I laughed. It was the laugh of a man who feels the ground shifting under him and clutches at the absurd. But Mr. Elton did not laugh. He regarded me with a polite concern, as one might regard a guest who has made a mild but regrettable breach of taste, and presently he began to speak of the world into which I had stumbled, in the manner of a lecturer addressing a not very intelligent audience.

The great fact, he said, was this: that the human span had been extended, by a combination of what he called “metabolic husbandry” and certain hereditary adjustments, to something between one hundred and eighty and two hundred and twenty years. The second great fact was that the birth-rate had declined, quietly and persistently, through the latter half of the twenty-first century, until it had reached zero. There had been no child born in England for close upon seventy years.

“But surely,” I said, “there must be some—”

“There are none,” said Mr. Elton, with the same placid finality. “The last was born in the year two thousand and twenty-one. A girl. She is alive still, and in excellent health. Her name is Miss Verity Anstruther, and she lives in the town. I have taken the liberty of sending word to her, as she is always interested in—in anomalies.”

He said the word without malice, but it stung. I looked at my own hands, at the coarse wool of my sleeve, and felt myself to be indeed an anomaly—a survival, a freak, a thing out of a museum.

The town of Ashcombe Regis, when I walked through it that afternoon, was beautiful and terrible. The streets were clean, the gardens immaculate, the houses of a quiet and harmonious design. The people I passed were courteous and unhurried, and they were all, without exception, old. Not infirm—there was no infirmity, no palsy, no wheezing decrepitude—but old with a settled, polished, ageless old age, their faces smooth and their eyes remote. They nodded to me with a mild curiosity and passed on. There were no perambulators, no schools, no shouts, no games. The very dogs seemed elderly and contemplative. It was a world of reasonable, kindly, slowly dwindling ghosts.

I found Miss Anstruther in a garden behind a low stone wall, pruning roses. She was thirty years old—the youngest person in England, perhaps in the world—and she looked, at first glance, like a woman of five-and-forty, her hair already grey at the temples, her face grave and composed. But her eyes were not the eyes of the others. They had a restlessness in them, a hunger, a something that had not yet been smoothed away by the long, level years.

“You are the man from the past,” she said, and held out her hand with a directness that was almost shocking. “I have been expecting you. Mr. Elton is a great one for preparing one for shocks, but he need not have troubled. I have been shocked all my life.”

We walked together in the garden, and she talked, and I listened. She talked of her childhood—the strange, solitary childhood of the last child, petted and studied and watched by a whole nation of the elderly, a little specimen of a vanishing race. She had been educated by tutors who were themselves the product of an education designed for a world that no longer existed. She had been given every advantage, every kindness, every indulgence—and no companions.

“They meant it well,” she said. “They were the most benevolent of jailers. But you cannot imagine what it is to be the only young thing in a world of the old. They have no urgency, you see. They have no sense that anything must be done before it is too late. They have all the time in the world, and they have arranged their world so that nothing ever needs doing at all. And I—I have been running down like a clock that nobody winds.”

“But surely,” I said, “there are others of your generation—people of fifty, of sixty?”

“A few,” she said. “A very few. But they are old already in their minds. They were born into the twilight and they have accepted it. They do not feel the want of children, because they have never known what it is to live among them. I am the last who remembers—not remembers, no, I was too young—but I am the last who was ever meant to be part of a world with a future. And now there is no future. There is only a long, comfortable, interminable present, and a slow diminution.”
