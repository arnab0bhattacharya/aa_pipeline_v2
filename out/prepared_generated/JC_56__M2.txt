The heat in Valparaíso did not simply exist; it oppressed. It lay upon the quay like a damp, suffocating blanket, pressing the breath out of the lungs and turning the air into a substance as thick and sluggish as oil. From the veranda of the small, whitewashed house that served as the *Colegio de Santa Clara*, Mrs. Elspeth Vane watched the harbor. The water was a dull, leaden expanse, broken only by the slow, listless undulation of the tide and the occasional glint of sunlight that seemed to burn with an angry, yellow intensity, as if the sun itself were suffering from the same fever that gripped the city. Below, the port was a hive of indifferent activity: sailors cursing in a dozen tongues, mules groaning under loads of nitrate, and the rhythmic clanging of the cranes. It was a sound that had worn itself into the very stones of the street, a ceaseless, grinding noise that spoke of commerce, of extraction, of a life that went on regardless of the moral upheavals that might be brewing in the shadows of the alleys.

Elspeth had been in this place for three years. Three years of dusty classrooms, of the sharp smell of chalk and the even sharper scent of the sea, and of a quiet, dignified isolation that had become, in its own way, a form of protection. She was an Englishwoman, a fact that in this republic was often interpreted as a mark of stability, of a certain cold, orderly reliability. It was this reputation, more than her teaching, that had drawn the attention of Señor Aramburu. He had come to her on a night when the wind was blowing hard from the mountains, carrying with it the scent of rain and impending violence. He was a tall man, gaunt as a heron, with eyes that held a peculiar, hollow brightness—the look of one who has seen too much and believes in too little. He did not ask; he stated. He required a tutor for his daughter, Isabela. He required a woman who would not ask questions, who would provide structure, who would be a shadow rather than a voice.

Elspeth had accepted. It was a mistake, though she did not know its magnitude then. It was merely a job, a way to fill the long, empty hours of her life with the mechanical labor of instruction. But in the world of Valparaíso, actions were rarely neutral. To teach the daughter of the revolutionary leader was to align oneself with the cause, or at least, to become a target for those who opposed it. The conservative factions, who held the reins of the republic with a grip that was both iron and brittle, viewed her with a suspicion that was cold and calculating. They were men of the old guard, officers and merchants who spoke of order and duty with a fervor that masked their own deep-seated fears. To them, Elspeth was not a teacher; she was a conduit for subversion, a link in a chain that threatened to unravel the fabric of their society.

She felt the shift in the air before it was spoken. The teachers at the college began to avoid her, their eyes sliding off her face as if she were a stain on a clean tablecloth. The students, those young girls who had once looked up to her with a mixture of admiration and fear, now watched her with a wary, distant gaze. They were children, yes, but they were also the daughters of men who held power, and they had learned, early and well, to read the currents of danger. Elspeth would walk through the corridors of the school, and the silence would follow her like a second shadow. It was a heavy silence, full of unspoken accusations.

One afternoon, as the sun began to dip below the horizon, painting the sky in shades of bruised purple and dull gold, she was summoned to the office of the Director. It was a small, cramped room, smelling of stale tobacco and old paper. The Director, a thin man with a nervous twitch in his left eye, did not look up from his desk.

"Mrs. Vane," he said, his voice flat and devoid of inflection. "There are... concerns. About your associations. About where you go in the evenings."

"I go to my home," Elspeth replied, her voice steady, though her heart was beating a frantic, irregular rhythm against her ribs. "I have a student to tutor. It is private."

"Private," the Director repeated, the word tasting in his mouth like ash. "In this city, there is no privacy, Mrs. Vane. There is only duty. And there is loyalty."

He looked up then, and his eyes were hard, flinty things. "You are an Englishwoman. We value our friends. But we do not value those who harbor our enemies. This is a warning. Do not make us regret your presence here."

Elspeth left the room without another word. The walk back to the classroom felt like a journey through a dream, a surreal landscape where the walls seemed to lean in closer, where the light from the windows was thin and sickly. She thought of Aramburu, of his hollow eyes, of the weight of his silence. She thought of Isabela, a girl of twelve, who had been quiet, almost ghostly, in her presence. There had been something in the child’s eyes, a depth that was not of her years, a awareness of the storm that was gathering around them both.

That evening, when Elspeth arrived at Aramburu’s house, the streets were empty. The usual bustle of the port had vanished, replaced by a stillness that was more terrifying than noise. The house was dark, save for a single candle burning in the window of the study. She rang the bell, and the sound echoed sharply in the night, a thin, piercing note that seemed to cut through the thick air.
