The Reverend Mr. Ashworth was a man whose mind, by its very constitution, was better fitted for the pursuit of a lost Greek root than for the consideration of a household dinner. It was this amiable deficiency which had led him, on a Tuesday afternoon in the latter part of October, to stop the post-chaise of Mr. Evelyn on the road from Woodstock, and to press upon him, with a hospitality as sincere as it was untimely, the invitation to dine at the Parsonage at Little Barford.

Mr. Evelyn, a gentleman of some property and a most agreeable countenance, had accepted with a pleasure that was, in its way, as untimely as the invitation itself, for he had been expected at his cousin’s house in Banbury that evening. But the Reverend’s kindness was of a sort that could not be easily refused, and so it was settled. Mr. Ashworth, having thus secured his guest, rode home in a state of great satisfaction, and entirely forgot the matter until five o’clock, when, upon seeing Mr. Evelyn’s horse being led to the stables, it all came back to him with the force of a sudden revelation.

He found his wife and daughter in the drawing-room, his wife engaged in her embroidery, and his daughter, Miss Clara Ashworth, reading aloud. “My dear,” said he, with a look of cheerful consternation, “I have done a very thoughtless thing. I have invited Mr. Evelyn to dinner.”

Mrs. Ashworth, a lady whose talent for household management was surpassed only by her talent for being vexed by its disruption, laid down her needlework with an air of profound alarm. “Mr. Evelyn! To dinner! To-night! And what, pray, is there for him to eat? The larder, as you know very well, is to be cleared to-morrow, for Martha to turn out the pantry. We have nothing but a cold mutton bone and the end of a cheese.”

Clara, who had looked up from her book with a mild surprise, now felt a more lively emotion. She saw the whole situation with a clarity that her father lacked and her mother, in her distress, was unable to command. To confess the emptiness of their larder to a guest, and a gentleman of Mr. Evelyn’s consequence, was a humiliation too great to be borne. Her father, she knew, would only offer apologies of such a general and abstruse nature as would make the matter worse. Her mother would fall into a flutter of apologies that would border on the impertinent. The management of the evening, therefore, must fall to her.

“There is no occasion for alarm, Mamma,” said Clara, in a voice of perfect composure. “We shall do very well. Pray, do not give it another thought.” She then turned to her father. “Papa, you must go down and entertain Mr. Evelyn. I will speak to Martha directly.” Her tone was so decided, and her manner so tranquil, that the Reverend, relieved of all responsibility, gladly obeyed.

Mrs. Ashworth, however, was not so easily pacified. “But what can you possibly say to her, Clara? What can we do? A mutton bone! It is not to be thought of.”

Clara gave her mother a reassuring smile. “It is not what we have, Mamma, but how we present it. Leave it all to me.” She then descended to the kitchen, where she found the cook, Martha, in a state of mind that mirrored her mistress’s.

“Martha,” said Clara, with an air of calm authority, “we have a guest for dinner. It is a sudden engagement, and we must manage with what we have.”

Martha, a woman of few words and much feeling, merely shook her head and pointed a floury finger towards the bare shelves. Clara surveyed the scene not with despair, but with the keen eye of a general surveying a difficult field of battle. “The mutton bone,” she said, “will make a very respectable soup. There are onions and carrots, I see, and a little parsley. It will be a *consommé*, and we shall call it such. The cheese, a little toasted, with some dried fruit from the pantry, will do for a second course. We have eggs, I suppose?”

“A few, Miss.”

“Then we shall have an omelette. It is a dish of great elegance when properly prepared. And the bread is fresh?”

“Baked this morning, Miss.”

“Excellent. We are not so destitute as we feared. Now, we must have a fire in the dining-room, and the best candles. The impression of warmth and light is a great aid to digestion.” Having thus issued her orders with a precision that left no room for doubt, she returned to the drawing-room, where she found her mother still in a state of nervous agitation.

The evening began, and Clara watched it unfold with the vigilance of a diplomat at a congress of nations. Mr. Evelyn, whose good manners were a true reflection of a good heart, seemed to suspect nothing. He conversed with the Reverend on a point of Livy with great animation, and complimented Mrs. Ashworth on the tasteful arrangement of her drawing-room. When they were called to dinner, Clara held her breath.

The *consommé* was served in the best china, and was received with an appreciation that did credit to Mr. Evelyn’s palate. “A most delicate preparation, Mrs. Ashworth,” he said. “It has a clarity and a savour that is quite remarkable.” Mrs. Ashworth, who had not been able to bring herself to eat a spoonful, could only murmur her thanks, her eyes fixed on her daughter’s face.

The omelette, light and perfectly cooked, followed. “You have a cook of great talent,” observed Mr. Evelyn, turning to Clara. “To produce something so simple and yet so perfect requires a certain artistry.”

Clara inclined her head. “Martha has a natural gift for the culinary arts. She believes that the quality of an ingredient is best revealed by the simplicity of its preparation.” It was a sentence she had composed in her mind while watching the onions being chopped, and she was pleased with its philosophical sound.
