The shop of Mrs. Selby, seamstress, stood on a narrow lane in the town of Marrowgate, its single window a murky eye that looked out upon the cobbles and the ceaseless drizzle. Inside, the air was thick with the smell of damp wool, coal smoke, and the faint, sweet scent of lavender water used to freshen the bolts of cloth. For Eliza Thorne, who had just turned seventeen, this small, dim space had become the whole of her world. For three months she had bent her head over hems and buttonholes, her fingers, once clumsy with a needle at the Fellsworth Charitable Institution, now moving with a quiet, practiced rhythm.

Mrs. Selby herself was a woman carved from the same durable, unglamorous material as her trade. Her back was permanently curved from a lifetime of stooping, and her eyes, though kind, were perpetually weary. She was not a mistress given to grand speeches or effusive praise, but she paid Eliza her small wage on a Saturday without fail and had, on the first cold snap of autumn, presented her with a thick, hand-knitted scarf of undyed wool. “No sense in you catching a chill,” she had muttered, thrusting it into Eliza’s hands. “A sick apprentice is a useless one.” It was, Eliza knew, the closest thing to affection the woman had shown, and she treasured it more than the coins she earned.

Their work was humble. They mended the rough shirts of weavers, turned worn sheets sides-to-middle, and fashioned sturdy, unlovely dresses for the wives of shopkeepers and farmers. The cloth was often coarse, the colours drab, the patterns nonexistent. Eliza’s dreams, when she allowed herself to have them, were filled with the silks and muslins she had only ever glimpsed in the fashion plates that occasionally wrapped a parcel from a larger town. But such dreams were a luxury, and a dangerous one. They were the stuff of the institution’s cautionary tales, of girls who had reached for a glittering prize and fallen into ruin. Stability, Mrs. Selby’s quiet presence seemed to say, was a prize in itself.

The disruption to their quiet routine arrived on a Tuesday, in a carriage that was far too fine for their lane. It was a glossy black affair, its wheels sending up a spray of muddy water that spattered the shop’s already grimy window. Eliza, who had been dispatched to the door to shake out a tablecloth, watched, frozen, as a liveried footman descended and opened the carriage door. A woman stepped out, her cloak of deep blue velvet a slash of impossible colour against the grey afternoon.

She was Mrs. Arabella Fitzroy, a name that was spoken in Marrowgate with a mixture of awe and speculation. A widow of independent means, she had come to the town a year prior, taking up residence in the grand old house on the hill, a place that had stood empty for a decade. She was known for her patronage of the arts and her charitable works, but there was a whispered-about edge to her philanthropy, a suggestion that her motives were not always as pure as they seemed.

Mrs. Fitzroy entered the shop, and the small space seemed to shrink around her. She brought with her the scent of rosewater and something else, something clean and expensive that Eliza could not name. She smiled at Mrs. Selby, a dazzling, practised smile. “Mrs. Selby, I presume? I have a commission. A gown for the Michaelmas assembly. I am told you are a woman of discretion and skill.”

Mrs. Selby, her face a mask of wary neutrality, simply nodded. The work was to be a gown of midnight-blue satin, a fabric so rich and fluid it seemed to hold its own light. As Mrs. Fitzroy described the cut—the low shoulders, the fitted bodice, the fall of lace at the sleeve—her eyes did not rest on Mrs. Selby, but on Eliza. She watched the girl’s hands, her nimble fingers, her straight back and the way the dim light from the window caught the gold in her hair.

“And who is this?” Mrs. Fitzroy asked, her voice a silken drawl.

“My apprentice, Miss Thorne,” Mrs. Selby replied, her tone clipped.

“Eliza,” the woman repeated, as if tasting the name. “A pretty name. And a pretty hand with a needle, I’ll wager.” She stepped closer to Eliza, who felt a flush creep up her neck. “You have an eye for detail, child. I can see it in the way you hold yourself. You are not meant for mending weavers’ shirts.”

The words, spoken so casually, struck Eliza with the force of a physical blow. It was as if the woman had reached into her secret heart and pulled out her most guarded hope. For the next two hours, as Mrs. Fitzroy was measured and draped in a length of cheap calico, she spoke to Eliza, not as a mistress to an apprentice, but as a patron to a protégée. She spoke of London, of the great houses she had visited, of the finest modistes whose creations were sought after by duchesses. She spoke of a world where a woman’s skill with a needle was not a trade, but an art.

When the carriage had finally rolled away, leaving behind a small fortune in satin and a lingering scent of rosewater, the shop felt emptier and shabbier than ever before. Mrs. Selby sat in her chair, her hands still. “She wants you,” she said, her voice flat. “She’ll offer to take you on. To ‘finish’ your education. To make you her own personal seamstress.”

“She was only being kind,” Eliza murmured, her heart hammering against her ribs.

“Kindness is a bauble for the rich to play with,” Mrs. Selby retorted, a rare flash of bitterness in her tone. “They take it out, admire it, and put it away when they tire of it. You have a place here, Eliza. It is not grand, but it is solid. You will always have work, and you will always have a roof. With her…” She trailed off, shaking her head. “With her, you will be a beautiful thing she has made. And what happens when she tires of her creation?”
