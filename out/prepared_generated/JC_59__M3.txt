He had brought it down at last—the iron road, driven through the absurd and stubborn anarchy of the Cordillera. For five years Martin Hallowes, a mining engineer possessed by a vision of civic virtue, had wrestled with the treacherous, sliding shale of the San Esteban range, fighting a silent war against an indifferent universe. He had believed, with the earnest simplicity of a man unacquainted with the darker ironies of human commerce, that he was forging a conduit for progress into the heart of the republic. He had imagined the bright ore of the interior descending to the sea, and in exchange, the mercantile blessings of the coast flowing upward to the starving, sun-baked villages of the plateau. It was to be a great uplifting. He had said so himself, often, in the lamplit offices of the foreign concession, surrounded by the cynical, heavy-lidded stares of the local functionaries who had granted him the charter. They had smiled, and stroked their thin mustachios, and signed the papers, and Hallowes had mistaken their indolence for trust, their silence for awe.

Now he stood upon the wooden platform of the newly completed terminus at Puerto Garrano, a dreary, sun-bleached settlement sweating under the oppressive heat of the Pacific seaboard. The air was thick, vibrating with the drone of invisible insects and the faint, corrosive smell of salt marshes. The town itself seemed a mere temporary excrescence upon the mud—a cluster of flimsy corrugated iron roofs huddled beneath the immense, brooding shadow of the mountains whose jagged silhouette cut sharply into the bleached sky. Hallowes stood quite alone near the edge of the planks, his hands clasped behind his back, his pith helmet tilted slightly forward to shield his eyes from the blinding glare. He was waiting for the first train.

The morning had been one of extraordinary, oppressive stillness, as if the whole tormented land were holding its breath in anticipation of the event. Hallowes felt a profound, hollow fatigue—the spiritual exhaustion of a creator who has given too much of his own substance to his creation. He had poured his youth, his restless energy, his very moral substance into those cuttings and embankments. He had lived among the half-naked, sullen peons, directing their feeble, uncomprehending efforts with a fierce, silent patience. He had endured the fevers, the mutinies, the agonizing delays when the rains washed away months of toil in a single night of roaring, tawny water. Through it all, the vision had sustained him. He saw the railway as a great artery of life, carrying the red blood of civilization to the dying extremities of the land. It was a noble, romantic delusion, and he had held it close to his chest like a talisman against the creeping, tropical despair that had broken stronger men than he.

Beside him, a little withdrawn into the meager shade of a corrugated iron awning, stood Don Carlos Rivas, the political agent of the Minister of the Interior in Valverde. He was a small, dapper man, excessively neat in his white linen suit, with a face that seemed to have been carefully cleansed of any troubling expression. He carried a cane, which he tapped rhythmically against the board, and he smoked a thin, black cigar. He was the sole representative of the government present to witness the inauguration of the line. There were no other dignitaries, no cheering crowds of grateful citizens. The handful of coast dwellers who had gathered at the far end of the platform stood in a sullen, mute group, regarding the empty track with a passive, animal indifference.

Hallowes turned his head slightly, catching the eye of the agent. "It is a great day for the Republic, Don Carlos," he said, his voice subdued, with an effort of conviction that seemed to die away in the heavy air. "The interior is opened at last. The villages up there will know prosperity."

Don Carlos removed the cigar from his lips and examined the glowing tip with a delicate, deliberate attention. "Without doubt," he murmured, in his soft, slightly foreign English. "The Presidente is deeply mindful of the achievement. It changes the complexion of the country." He paused, and a faint, ironic glimmer, utterly devoid of warmth, crossed his smooth features. "Yes. It changes everything."

A faint, tremulous vibration ran through the wooden platform, communicating itself to the soles of Hallowes’s boots. It was a deep, rhythmic pulsing, growing rapidly in intensity, as if some vast, subterranean heart had begun to beat beneath the crust of the earth. Then, sweeping round the curve of the barren hillside that shut in the bay, a dark smear of smoke appeared against the sky, followed by the blunt, protruding snout of the locomotive. It came with a harsh, sustained panting, a clangor of iron and a furious hissing of steam that seemed violently to profane the ancient, sun-baked silence of the coast. It was a small, powerful machine, aggressively new, its brass fittings glittering with a cruel, mechanical ostentation under the sun. It drew up at the platform with a grinding of brakes, and a sudden, violent jet of vapor enveloped the end of the station, hiding the waiting crowd from view.

Hallowes felt a strange, sharp contraction of his heart. He had expected a profound, perhaps solemn, emotion at this moment—the triumph of his will made visible. Instead, he felt only a creeping, inexplicable chill. The train, his train, seemed to have arrived with an air of furtive, menacing haste.

The vapor drifted aside, dissolving in the heat, and the cars stood revealed. They were not the open freight wagons he had been led to expect for the transport of copper ore, nor were they the modest passenger coaches that might have carried the first hopeful merchants to the plateau. They were closed, windowless vans of heavy steel, painted a dull, funereal gray. And from these vans, even before the engine had quite ceased its gasping, there poured out a silent, swift, and ordered stream of men.
