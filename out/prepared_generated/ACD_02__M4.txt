It was upon a raw November afternoon, when the lamps were already lit against a premature dusk, that I received a hasty note from my friend Mr. Alistair Vance, whose rooms in Harley Street were for so long the last word in surgical opinion. The note was brief and peremptory: “Come at once. There has been an ugly business here, and I desire a second pair of eyes before the police arrive to muddy the waters.”

I found him in the hallway of his consulting house, a tall, spare figure in a frock-coat, his usually impassive face drawn with an anxiety he made no effort to conceal. “Thank you for coming, Doctor,” said he, taking my arm and drawing me aside from the knot of servants who stood whispering at the foot of the stairs. “The facts are these, and I give them to you without embellishment, for embellishment is the one thing we can afford least. Mr. Josiah Crane, my partner of eleven years, was discovered an hour ago in his consulting room, insensible, with a contusion upon the temple. The door was locked upon the inside. His instrument case stood open upon the table, and one scalpel is missing from its place. Nothing else, so far as I can determine, has been disturbed.”

“And Mr. Crane himself?”

“He has recovered consciousness, but his memory of the affair is a blank from the moment he sat down to write his notes. He complains of a singing in the ears and a confusion of thought. I have put him to bed under the care of his wife, and I have forbidden all conversation upon the subject until he is calmer.”

“You suspect foul play, then?”

Vance’s grey eyes met mine with a look of grave perplexity. “I suspect everything and everyone,” said he, “which is why I sent for you. There is one circumstance more, and it is the one which troubles me most. The missing scalpel is not the only thing that has been taken from that room. There is a question of a patient’s record—a small matter, perhaps, but in a practice such as ours, a matter of the gravest import.”

He led me up the stairs to the consulting room, a lofty apartment with a single tall window giving upon the street, and a fire burning low in the grate. The instrument case lay open as he had described, its velvet-lined compartments displaying a neat array of steel, with one empty slot yawning like a missing tooth. The writing-table was in some disorder, a sheet of paper half-covered with a prescription, the pen dropped from the writer’s hand. But what drew my attention was the window. It was secured by a heavy sash-lock of the old pattern, and the shutters were folded back against the wall. I examined the lock closely. It was fast.

“You are certain the door was locked upon the inside?” I asked.

“As certain as I am of my own name. The maid, Betsy, heard the fall and tried the handle. It would not yield. I was obliged to force the lock with a chisel from the workshop below. The marks are there upon the jamb.”

“And the window?”

“Has not been opened, to my knowledge, for a fortnight. The lock is stiff with paint, as you see.”

I stood for a moment in the centre of the room, considering the matter. A locked room, a stunned man, a missing blade. It had the air of a melodrama, and yet there was about it a quality of the commonplace which I have learned to distrust more than any theatrical villainy. I turned to Vance.

“You spoke of a patient’s record. Be good enough to tell me precisely what you mean.”

He hesitated, and I saw his long fingers close upon the edge of the table. “It concerns a young assistant of ours, a Mr. Percival Rennick, who has been with us these eight months. He came to me not half an hour ago, in a state of considerable agitation, and informed me that the record of a certain patient—a lady of good family, whom I shall not name—had been altered in a particular which he was not at liberty to disclose. He would say only that the alteration was in Mr. Crane’s own hand, and that it had been made within the last day or two. He implored me to speak to Mr. Crane upon the subject, and then, as you may imagine, this catastrophe intervened.”

“And what is your own opinion of Mr. Rennick?”

Vance frowned. “He is clever, industrious, and, I had thought, honest. But he is young, and he has been pressing me of late for a share in the practice which I am not prepared to grant. I would not have him think that I doubt his word, and yet—”

“And yet you do doubt it.”

“I doubt everything,” he repeated, with a thin smile. “You see my difficulty. If I believe Rennick, then my partner of eleven years has been tampering with a record—for what purpose I cannot conceive—and has been struck down, perhaps by some person who surprised him at it. If I believe Crane, then Rennick is a liar, and his story is a fabrication designed to—to what end? To blacken the character of the man whose position he covets? It is a tangle, Doctor, and I had rather have your cool head upon it than my own.”

I asked leave to speak with the assistant, and he was brought to me in the library below—a pale, earnest young man with a nervous habit of clasping and unclasping his hands. He repeated his story with a dogged insistence which carried, to my mind, the ring of truth, though I was careful to give no sign of my impression.

“You are aware,” said I, “that Mr. Crane denies all knowledge of any alteration?”
