In the parish of St. Giles-the-Grinder, which lay like a knuckle of grime upon the fist of London, there was no man more respected for his parsimony, nor more disliked for its consequences, than Mr. Ebenezer Grimes, the parish clerk. Mr. Grimes was a walking ledger, a human colander through which the smallest coppers of the parish poor-rates were strained and saved. He wore a coat of such a severe and ancient black that it seemed to have been cut from the very shadow of the workhouse, and his face was a testament to the principle that a shilling saved is a shilling earned, being pinched at the nose, tight at the lips, and generally so drawn up against the world that it was a marvel he could see out of his own eyes at all. His great boast, which he repeated to the Vestry, to the churchwardens, and indeed to the very gas-lamps as he passed them in the flickering fog, was that he had reduced the parish expenditure on candles by a full ten per cent, and that he had done so without, as he put it, ‘diminishing the intellectual candle-power of the establishment.’

It was, therefore, with a sense of professional triumph and moral rectitude that Mr. Grimes undertook the distribution of coal to the shivering poor during that terrible winter of 1837, when the Thames itself seemed to hold its breath and turn to stone. The Vestry, a body of men who preferred their charity to be conducted at a distance and in a warm room, had entrusted to him the oversight of the ‘Parish Fuel Fund,’ a sum of money so carefully husbanded by Mr. Grimes that it was more of a theoretical concept than a practical supply. He had a list, a long and doleful scroll of names, written in a hand as cramped and joyless as his own heart, and he approached the task of its fulfilment with the zeal of a miser cataloguing his treasures.

The coal-yard, a desolate square of black dust and frozen puddles, was the stage for his great performance. Here, the poor of St. Giles, wrapped in every conceivable article of tattered cloth, from sacking to faded shawls, stood in a long, shivering line, their breath pluming in the frigid air like the ghosts of their own starvation. Mr. Grimes, mounted upon a high stool with his ledger open on his knee, dispensed the precious black diamonds not with a liberal hand, but with the cautious precision of a chemist weighing poison. To one, a measure that would barely warm a teacup; to another, a slightly larger heap, contingent upon a promise of attendance at the Sunday school; and to a third, a stern lecture on the improvidence of large families, accompanied by a scoop of coal so small it seemed a moral judgement rather than a fuel.

He was in the midst of this solemn ceremony, his voice cutting through the wind like a rusty saw, when the beadle, Mr. Pumblechook, a man whose belly was a monument to the parish dinners he had attended, came puffing towards him. ‘A word, Mr. Grimes,’ he wheezed, his face the colour of a boiled beetroot. ‘A word from the overseer.’

‘Speak, Mr. Pumblechook, but be brief,’ replied Grimes, not looking up from his ledger. ‘Time is coal, and coal is money, and money is the lifeblood of this parish.’

‘It’s the Widow Gable, sir,’ the beadle panted. ‘The overseer says her name is to be struck from the list. No proof of settlement, you see. Lived here but six months. Says she’s from the country. Not our responsibility, he says.’

Mr. Grimes made a small, satisfied sound in his throat, a sound like a key turning in a well-oiled lock. ‘Very proper,’ he said, dipping his pen. ‘The parish must be protected. The Widow Gable is to be removed. Note it, Pumblechook. Economy, sir, is the highest form of patriotism.’

As he spoke, a figure detached itself from the end of the line and shuffled towards him. It was an old woman, bent nearly double by a combination of age and infirmity, and wrapped in a cloak so threadbare it was more a memory of a cloak than a garment. Her face was a web of wrinkles, her eyes watery and dim, and she clutched an empty coal-scuttle to her chest as if it were a beloved child. She did not look at Mr. Grimes directly, but at a point somewhere near his ledger.

‘Please, sir,’ she began, her voice a dry rustle of leaves. ‘I am on the list. Gable is my name. They call me the Widow Gable.’

Mr. Grimes did not look at her. His eyes remained fixed upon the name he had just struck through with a single, decisive stroke of his pen. ‘The name has been removed,’ he said, his tone as flat and final as a tombstone. ‘No proof of settlement. You are not a charge upon this parish.’

‘But I have lived here, sir, near on seven years,’ the old woman pleaded, her voice gaining a tremor of desperation. ‘In Pinch-and-Prowl Alley. I was born in this parish, sir. My husband, Thomas Gable, he was a waterman on the river. He is dead these twenty years.’

‘That is a matter for the Vestry, not for me,’ said Grimes, still refusing to lift his gaze. ‘My duty is to distribute the coal to those who are legally entitled to it. You are not. Next!’

The old woman did not move. She took a half-step closer, her scuttle clattering faintly against the stool. ‘Sir,’ she whispered, and now her voice was different, softer, and it carried a note that made the hairs on Mr. Grimes’s neck prickle. ‘Sir, do you not know me?’
