The envelope was the colour of bone, and it had been slit open with a paperknife that was not hers. Clara Denville set down her teacup and regarded the violation with a certain cool detachment. It was the third time that fortnight that Alistair had taken it upon himself to prescreen the morning post, though he never mentioned having done so and she, out of a long-married instinct for the preservation of quiet, never taxed him with it. She picked up the letter, which lay beside a bill from her modiste and an invitation to the Hartwell musical evening, and read it standing, the pale March light falling across her shoulders through the tall window of the morning room.

The hand was young and unsteady. She could see the pressure of the pen where the writer had hesitated, the ink pooling at the turns of certain letters as though he had stopped to think and had not lifted the nib. It was from Cecil Maren. She knew him as a slight, pale young man with excellent cheekbones and an unfortunate tendency to appear at the edges of ballrooms, staring at her with an intensity that she found more tiresome than flattering. He had been introduced to her at the Pemberton affair in November, had danced with her once, and had since then written her three letters, each more fervid than the last, to all of which she had replied with nothing. She had mentioned him to her friend Margot as a curiosity, the way one might mention a strange dog that had followed one home.

The letter was brief. He wrote that he could not live without seeing her, that he had endured the silence for as long as his nature permitted, that he would call at her house on Thursday at four o'clock, and that if she refused to receive him he would, that very evening, put an end to a existence which had become insupportable. He underlined the word *insupportable*. Clara read the passage twice, not because the meaning was obscure but because she was trying to determine whether the theatricality of it was genuine or merely immature. She concluded it was the latter. Young men of two-and-twenty were always threatening to die for one reason or another. It was a condition of the age, like rheumatism or socialism.

She folded the letter and placed it back inside the envelope and was about to set it aside when she became aware that Alistair was standing in the doorway. He was already dressed for the day in a dark coat of excellent cut, and he held a copy of *The Times* folded beneath one arm. He looked at her with an expression she could not immediately read.

"Ah," he said. "You've found it."

There was no point in pretending. "I have."

Alistair crossed the room and took the letter from the table before she could object. He read it with the same thoroughness he applied to the columns of his newspaper, his lips moving almost imperceptibly over the final clause. When he finished he set it down with a care that struck her as deliberate, the way one sets down a piece of evidence.

"You will see him, of course," he said.

Clara laughed, a short, musical sound that she had cultivated for precisely such occasions. "I will do nothing of the kind. The boy is being ridiculous."

"He is twenty-three. He is the nephew of Sir Horace Maren, who sits on the board of the Consolidated Indian Railways, with whom I have been negotiating for six months. If the young man blows his brains out over a married woman and that woman is my wife, I do not think I need to explain to you what becomes of the negotiations."

Clara looked at her husband. He was a tall man, fastidiously groomed, with a face that had once been handsome and was now merely correct. He had the sort of composure that less disciplined people mistook for strength, but which she knew, after eleven years of marriage, to be a form of vanity. He could not bear disorder, and the letter was disorder. It had arrived uninvited into his household and it threatened to make a mess there, and that, she understood, was the sum of his concern. He was not afraid for Cecil Maren. He was afraid of Cecil Maren.

"The negotiations are your affair," she said. "The young man is mine. I have no intention of encouraging him by granting him an audience. He will forget the whole thing by Friday."

"He says he will kill himself."

"And young men say a great many things. I have it on good authority that Lord Ashby's son wrote a very similar letter to Constance Peele last season, and he is now engaged to a girl from Yorkshire and entirely devoted to foxhunting."

Alistair's jaw tightened. He was not a man who enjoyed being contradicted, and he enjoyed anecdotes that made light of his concerns even less. "You do not know what this young man is capable of."

"Nor do you. Nor, I suspect, does he."

They looked at each other across the breakfast table, with its silver urn and its arrangement of white camellias in a cut-glass bowl, and Clara understood that the conversation was not, in fact, about Cecil Maren at all. It was about the letter that had arrived on Tuesday from a man named Dominic Faye, which Alistair had also opened, and which had contained nothing whatsoever of a romantic nature but which had been written in a hand that was not hers and which had begun, simply, *Clara*. She had not mentioned the letter. Alistair had not mentioned it either. But it lay between them now like a stain on cloth, and Cecil Maren's theatrical little note was the instrument by which Alistair intended to address it.
