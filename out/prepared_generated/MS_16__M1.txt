I was alone in the world; that thought, which in earlier years had visited me as a transient shadow, had now fixed itself upon my soul as a permanent and settled truth. My father, Colonel Avelard, a man whose spirit was as honourable and as proud as the ancient oaks of our Dorset estate, was gone; and the hand that had sent him to his grave walked abroad in the sunshine of the world’s esteem, a decorated servant of the King, a respected Member of Parliament, and—oh, cruellest mockery of Providence!—the chosen husband of my dearest friend. The letter, which I held in my trembling hand, announced the betrothal of my beloved Emmeline to Sir Julian Thorne. The paper seemed to burn my fingers, and the elegant, joyous characters of her handwriting blurred before my eyes as a mist rises from a cold sea.

It was not enough that he had killed my father in that fatal dawn upon the heath, a duel fought over some trifling insult to honour that a gentleman of lesser pride might have dismissed with a smile; it was not enough that he had escaped the justice of man through the connivance of his seconds and the silence of the law; but that he should now possess the one solace remaining to me in this desolate world, and that she, the pure and innocent Emmeline, should be linked to a man whose soul was stained with the blood of my kin! I rose from my chair and paced the length of my drawing-room, where the rain lashed against the panes of glass, and the wind howled around the chimney-pots of London with a voice that seemed to echo the anguish of my heart. The city, usually so indifferent and vast, felt to me a prison, and the storm that raged without was but a feeble image of the tempest within.

I possessed the proof; the written challenge in my father’s own hand, and the reply in the scrawling, arrogant script of Sir Julian, which I had found hidden in the lining of his old writing-desk after the funeral. It was a weapon, sharp and terrible, which I held over the head of this man who had robbed me of a father’s love and guidance. Yet, as I looked upon it, I felt not the satisfaction of vengeance, but the heavy weight of a destiny I dared not fulfil. For if I spoke, I should ruin him; I should tear away the mask of respectability and expose the murderer beneath. But in so doing, should I not also shatter the happiness of my Emmeline? Should I not crush her gentle heart, which trusted him with such absolute devotion? To save her from a life of misery with a villain, I must plunge her into a misery far more acute and immediate—the knowledge that the man she loved was a destroyer of life.

"He is a monster!" I cried aloud to the empty room, my voice rising in a phrensy of despair. "He is a serpent who has crept into the garden of her innocence! Shall I stand by and see her perish?" But then a colder, more dreadful thought assailed me. If I remained silent, I became the accomplice of his crime; I partook of the guilt that clung to his name like a shroud. The spirit of my father, whom I had seen fall with a groan upon the wet grass, seemed to rise before me, his eyes reproachful, his hand pointing to the paper that would vindicate his honour. "Speak!" a voice seemed to whisper in the howling of the wind. "Speak, and let justice be done, though the heavens fall!"

I sank upon the floor, burying my face in my hands, overcome by the terrible conflict of my duties. I thought of Emmeline as she had been but yesterday, her eyes shining with the light of a new-found joy, speaking of Thorne with a rapture that made my blood run cold. How could I dash that cup from her lips? How could I be the angel of destruction to the only friend I had left? Yet, to permit her to bind herself for ever to a man without a soul, to the incarnate Belial who had murdered my parent, was a wickedness and a cruelty beyond my acting. I was torn asunder by these contrary influences; the love of my friend and the duty to my father warred within me until my frame was uneasy and restless, and I felt that my reason was tottering on its throne.

I looked out at the darkening sky; the rack in the upper heavens sped along with fearful impetuosity, driven by a furious wind which had not yet descended to the earth. The sun, a lurid and angry red, was setting behind the jagged silhouette of the city, staining the clouds with the colour of blood. It was a scene of sublime and terrible beauty, fitting for the decision I was compelled to make. I knew that I could not remain in this state of hesitation; the moment of execution was at hand. To speak was to destroy the peace of her I loved; to be silent was to betray the memory of him who gave me life. There is no greater enemy to virtue than that want of self-command which cannot force itself to do the thing it hates, nor persuade itself to relinquish the thing it desires. I was the slave of passion and pity.

With a sudden, stony resolution, I took the packet of letters and held it near the candle. The flame leaped up, eager to consume the evidence of the crime. But my hand froze. I could not do it. To burn the proof was to deny the truth of my own suffering. I turned away, the paper still clenched in my grasp. I would go to her; I would see her face to face; and perhaps, in the light of her presence, the path of duty would become clear.
