Mr. Wenlock was a man of very considerable learning, and a proportionate want of every other accomplishment. His daughter, Sophia, who had long superintended the practical concerns of his parsonage in the village of Stanton, had grown so thoroughly accustomed to his total abstraction from all earthly matters that she could receive his most startling communications with a composure which, in a less disciplined mind, would have given way to absolute despair.

It was upon a Tuesday, late in the autumn of the year eighteen hundred and two, that Mr. Wenlock walked into the small back-parlour, where Sophia was employed in mending a piece of household linen, and, with the air of a man who has discharged a duty of the highest magnitude, informed her that he had invited Mr. Crawley to dine with them that very day.

"Mr. Crawley!" repeated Sophia, letting her needle rest, though her voice retained all its steadiness. "The new gentleman from Bramley Park?"

"The very same. He called to inquire concerning my opinion on the Punic Wars, and finding me in the study, we fell into a conversation of so interesting a nature that I thought it only civil to ask him to stay. A young man of very promising understanding, Sophia; and he takes a view of Hannibal’s passage of the Alps which does him infinite credit."

"I am glad to hear it, sir," said Sophia; "but as it is now within two hours of dinner, and my mother is gone to visit Mrs. Fortescue, I wish you had thought a little of the Alps of our own kitchen. We had nothing intended but a cold joint, and Mrs. Hodges has already taken her departure for the day."

Mr. Wenlock looked a little blank at this intelligence. He had not, perhaps, considered the alliance between ancient history and modern cookery. "I dare say," said he, after a pause, "that you can make it do. You are a notable girl, Sophia, and can manage any thing."

With this assurance, he returned to his study, perfectly satisfied that he had done all that the occasion required, and leaving Sophia to the agreeable task of contriving a dinner for a gentleman of fortune, out of materials that would have scarcely satisfied a curate.

Mrs. Wenlock, had she been at home, would have been thrown into an agony of civility, which, in persons who have no real claim to gentility, is always the surest mark of it. She was a good woman, who thought it impossible to bestow too much attention upon a guest, and would undoubtedly have ordered a second joint, sent for a fresh supply of wine, and made herself and every servant in the house completely miserable, in order to prove that she was happy. Sophia, who had more sense and less feeling, determined that Mr. Crawley should be made comfortable, and that the means by which this was effected should be entirely concealed from his observation.

She immediately sought out the cook, a woman of more honesty than genius, who was at that moment contemplating a pair of fowls, which, having been intended for the family’s own dinner on the morrow, were in a state of perfect readiness for the spit, but wholly insufficient for a party of four. Sophia, with a quickness of decision which would have done credit to a general, directed that the cold joint should be hashed, the fowls roasted, and a pudding made from the remains of a rice pudding, enlarged by the addition of eggs and milk from the dairy. She then dispatched a boy to the village shop for a few additional necessaries, and, having done all that ingenuity could suggest, returned to the parlour to change her dress, reflecting, as she went, upon the singular disproportion between a father’s intellectual attainments and his utter uselessness in a domestic emergency.

Mr. Crawley arrived at the appointed hour. He was a young man, not more than eight-and-twenty, of a plain, sensible countenance, and manners which had nothing of the polish of high life, but a great deal of its ease. He had lately come into possession of a very pretty property in the neighbourhood, and was looking about him with that cautious eye which men of small experience are apt to cast upon new acquaintances. He had been told that the Wenlocks were respectable, but poor; and he had a very proper degree of dread of being bored by the father, and of being starved by the mother.

The dinner, when it appeared, was not such as to remove his apprehensions. The hashed joint was respectable, the fowls were tender, and the pudding was, if not elegant, at least substantial. But there was a certain want of variety in the repast, and an absence of wine, except of the home-brewed sort, which might have betrayed the secret to a more minute observer. Mr. Crawley, however, was not a minute observer. He ate with a good appetite, praised the fowls, and entered into a discussion with Mr. Wenlock on the character of Fabius, which lasted till the ladies withdrew.

In the drawing-room, Sophia found an opportunity for that quiet observation of her guest, which, in the country, supplies the place of more various amusement. Mr. Crawley, when released from the labours of the Punic Wars, proved to be a very agreeable companion. He had seen a good deal of the world, though he did not obtrude his experience upon the company; and he had that happy turn of mind which finds its entertainment in the occurrences of common life, without requiring to be stimulated by any thing extraordinary. He talked of the neighbourhood, of the roads, of the crops, and of the new barouche which Lady Bramley had just imported, with an interest which showed that he was not above his situation, and a good sense which showed that he was not below it.
