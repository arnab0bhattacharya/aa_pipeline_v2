There are few things in London so absolutely conclusive as a scandal that has been well-dressed and served at luncheon, and the case of Sir Vivian Cheyne was discussed that afternoon at Lady Althea’s table in Curzon Street with the gusto usually reserved for a new French farce or a particularly outrageous hat. Sir Vivian, whose sole distinction lay in a perfect coat and a perfect idleness, had been informed by his solicitor, Mr. Pettifer, that the estate of Cheyne was entangled in a web of conditions so ancient and so vindictive that it demanded he prove his capacity for industry—a condition Sir Vivian considered as vulgar as a hole in a carpet.

“A year of labour, Vivian?” cried Lady Althea, tapping her fan against a silver salver. “It is too deliciously medieval. It is like being asked to wear hair-shirts because one’s grandfather was a saint. What on earth will you do? You cannot dig, and you are too charming to steal.”

“I have considered the Bar,” said Sir Vivian, sipping his hock with the air of a man discussing a distant climate. “But the wig is unbecoming. Then I thought of the Church, but the early hours are fatal to one’s complexion. Finally, I have accepted a position of the utmost gravity. I am to be the private secretary to the Duchess of Dartmoor.”

The silence that followed was so profound that one could hear the clatter of a hansom cab in the street below. The Duchess of Dartmoor was a woman of terrifying intellect and polkas, a creature who believed that the human soul was merely a rough draft of a parliamentary bill.

“The Duchess!” exclaimed Lord Fitzroy, dropping a forkful of asparagus. “But she writes letters, Vivian! She organizes committees! She reads blue-books! She will expect you to—how shall I say it?—*work*.”

“So I am told,” replied Sir Vivian, examining his fingernails. “But I have a theory that if one performs any action with sufficient grace and a sufficiently bored expression, it ceases to be labour and becomes a form of high art. Besides, the Duchess has a marvellous collection of Sèvres porcelain. I shall regard my employment as a duty to the china.”

It was thus, amidst the laughter of his friends, who agreed that he would last a week before fleeing to the Riviera, that Sir Vivian Cheyne entered the service of the Duchess. The house in Westminster was a gloomy edifice of grey stone, filled with the scent of beeswax and anxiety. The Duchess herself was a tall woman with a nose like a cliff and eyes that saw through the pretensions of the world as easily as glass.

“You are late, Sir Vivian,” she said, looking up from a mountain of correspondence. “Punctuality is the politeness of kings, and the necessity of secretaries.”

“I was delayed by a flower,” said Sir Vivian, sinking into a chair with effortless elegance. “A yellow orchid in a florist’s window. It reminded me of a line of Persian poetry, and I was compelled to memorize it.”

“You are here to memorize my appointments, not poetry,” said the Duchess sharply. “Here is the schedule for the week. You will draft replies to these letters regarding the condition of the working classes. You will find my views are radical but practical. And you will ensure that my speech for the Institute of Practical Morality is typed by Thursday. It is a discourse on the virtues of thrift.”

“The virtues of thrift,” repeated Sir Vivian, as if tasting a bad wine. “How very sad. I have always found thrift to be the enemy of beauty. It is the rich who have the duty to be extravagant, just as it is the sun’s duty to shine. If we do not spend, how shall the poor live?”

“Do not be absurd,” snapped the Duchess. “Get to work.”

For the first month, the friendship between the idle baronet and the earnest Duchess was a source of great amusement to the town. They were seen driving in the Park, a vision of contrast: he in a grey silk scarf, she in a coat of sensible tweed. Yet, strangely, the work was done. Sir Vivian’s handwriting, once a scrawl of indifference, became a marvel of copperplate elegance. His drafts, which he claimed to write while thinking about the arrangement of roses in a vase, possessed a wit and a clarity that bewildered the civil servants.

“It is a mystery,” the Duchess confessed one evening, as they sat in her library surrounded by piles of blue-books. “You do nothing, Sir Vivian. You lounge. You contemplate. You refuse to touch a pen before noon. And yet, the Foreign Office has written to commend the style of my dispatch on the Bulgarian question. They say it has ‘aesthetic force’.”

“Style is everything, Duchess,” said Sir Vivian, adjusting a lamp. “A fact stated is a fact dead. But a fact embroidered is a truth. I merely dressed your politics in better clothes. It is the only form of charity I possess.”

“But the condition of the poor,” she insisted. “My speech for the Institute. I am told it is dry. It lacks… passion.”

“It lacks paradox,” corrected Sir Vivian. “You wish to tell them to save their pennies. I shall tell them that the only way to appreciate the world is to waste one’s time upon it. I shall tell them that the true miser is the man who hoards his hours.”

He took up the pen, and with a flourish that suggested he was signing a death warrant or a sonnet, he began to rewrite the Duchess’s speech. He wrote of the beauty of the gutter, the splendour of the slum, and the moral necessity of the rose. He inverted every commonplace until the Duchess’s dry lecture became a hymn to the uselessness of utility.
