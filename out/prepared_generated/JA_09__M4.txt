The visit of Mr. Cornelius Ashby to the parsonage at Feldon was an event which had been anticipated with a degree of anxious speculation proportionate to the fortune he was understood to possess, and the nearness of his connexion to the family. As the only son of the late Mrs. Ashby, who had been sister to the present Mrs. Langley, and as a gentleman of independent means, unmarried, and approaching his thirty-second year, he was a personage upon whom the hopes of a household blessed with five daughters might naturally be permitted to rest. Mr. Langley, the vicar, was a man of moderate income and immoderate hospitality, whose chief concern upon the occasion was that the dinner should be adequate; Mrs. Langley, whose concern was of a more complicated nature, had spent the preceding fortnight in a review of her daughters’ accomplishments, their complexions, and their linen, and had arrived at the conclusion, common to mothers, that the eldest had the best understanding, the second the best manners, the third the best figure, and the fourth the best temper; while as to the fifth, she could only hope that she might be persuaded to hold her tongue.

Miss Lucy Langley, the youngest, was fifteen, and possessed in full measure that confidence which is the happy portion of those who have never yet been instructed in their own insignificance. She was not handsome, nor was she plain; she was not accomplished, nor was she dull; but she had a pair of very observing eyes, a memory uncommonly tenacious of absurdity, and a disposition to speak her mind which her sisters regarded as a species of domestic affliction, and her mother as a cross sent for the trial of her patience.

Mr. Ashby arrived upon the Tuesday, in a chaise of his own, with a servant, and a great many boxes; and was received in the drawing-room by the whole family drawn up in a line, like a regiment upon parade. He was a tall gentleman, rather grave in countenance, with the air of one who had seen a great deal of the world and had not been much the better for it; his manners were correct to a degree that approached the chilling, and he spoke with that deliberate precision which seems to promise a great deal and to commit itself to nothing. He complimented Mrs. Langley upon her house, her children, and her shrubbery, in a manner so exactly proportioned to their several merits that the lady was delighted, and the daughters began to despair.

The dinner passed in that sort of conversation which is the peculiar torment of a family upon its good behaviour. Miss Langley, the eldest, discoursed upon the weather and the roads; Miss Maria ventured upon a remark concerning the new novel from the circulating library, which she had not read; Miss Hetty smiled whenever Mr. Ashby looked her way; and Miss Charlotte, the fourth, inquired with great solicitude whether he had found the country agreeable, to which he replied that he had found it very green. Mrs. Langley, meanwhile, watched her youngest daughter with the vigilance of a sentinel, and was rewarded for her pains by observing that Miss Lucy ate her dinner in silence, and regarded the guest with an attention which her mother was pleased to consider as the beginning of deference.

It was not until the cloth was removed, and the ladies were left to their tea, that the true character of the evening declared itself. Mr. Ashby, having condescended to accept a dish, was pleased to observe that he had seldom tasted tea of so fine a flavour, and inquired whether it came from a particular dealer in London.

“It does, sir,” said Mrs. Langley, with satisfaction; “we are very particular in our tea.”

“Particularity in such matters,” returned Mr. Ashby, “is the mark of a well-regulated mind. I have often remarked, Mrs. Langley, that the smaller proprieties of life are the surest indications of the greater. A family which is exact in its tea may be trusted, I believe, in its morals.”

“La, sir,” cried Miss Lucy, setting down her cup, “then I am afraid we must be a very wicked family, for we have had no tea at all these three days, and my father has been obliged to drink the small beer, which he says is very bad for his gout.”

There was a pause of some moments. Mrs. Langley coloured; Miss Langley looked a volume of reproof; and Mr. Ashby turned his eyes upon the offender with an expression which his hostess could not perfectly interpret.

“You have had no tea?” said he.

“None at all, sir. The dealer in London, of whom we are so particular, has not sent it; and my mother has been in a fidget about it ever since Sunday, because she says we cannot offer you small beer, and my father says we cannot afford to offer you wine, so we intended to offer you nothing, and to say that tea was unfashionable in Northamptonshire.”

“Lucy!” said Mrs. Langley, in a voice of agony.

“I beg your pardon, ma’am,” said Lucy, “but it is true, and I thought Mr. Ashby would like to know the reason of it, because he is so fond of particularity.”

Mr. Ashby looked at her for a moment in silence; and then, to the astonishment of the whole room, he laughed. It was not a loud laugh, nor a long one, but it was a laugh of genuine amusement, and it changed his countenance entirely.

“I am obliged to you, Miss Lucy,” said he, “for your explanation. I confess that I have been in the habit of attaching more importance to tea than it deserves; and I am very well content to drink small beer, or to drink nothing, in such agreeable company.”

“Then you are not offended?” said Lucy.

“Not in the least.”
