In the decay of a man’s working life, there is often a point where the tools of his trade become less the instruments of his livelihood and more the silent witnesses to a past self. So it was with Abel Grover, the hand-loom weaver of Middlemarch, whose cottage stood on the edge of the common, a stubborn relic of a slower, more individualized industry. The village of Middlemarch had, until recent months, slumbered through the changing seasons with the steady, muffled beat of Abel’s shuttle, a sound as familiar as the dripping of the eaves after an autumn rain. But a new sound had begun to pierce that quietude: the measured, metallic clinking of the surveyor’s chain, and the alien jargon of engineers plotting the path of the Birmingham and Warwickshire Railway.

The proposed line cut directly through the common, sparing the newly built villas of the more prosperous tenants but bisecting the humble network of cottages that clung to the village’s older, more precarious边缘. The railway company, possessed of that boundless yet pragmatic benevolence which characterizes modern corporate ambition, had offered the cottagers sums that seemed, to their unaccustomed imaginations, almost princely. Abel’s neighbors, the Coopers and the Dimsdales, had accepted their compensation with a gratitude that was perhaps mingled with a relief at being delivered from the uncertainty of their present holdings; they were to be relocated to neat brick houses in the village, with gardens that did not flood and roofs that did not leak. But Abel, when the agent called with his papers and his silver pencil-case, had merely shaken his grey head, his hands folded quietly over the waist of his much-mended apron.

“I dunno what your fine company means by it,” he said, in the broad, unhurried Warwickshire speech that matched the rhythm of his loom, “but this here cottage is my own, and my father’s afore me. The bricks is laid with his sweat, and I’ll not be parted from ’em for no puffing devil.”

The agent had argued, pointing out the undeniable advantages of the new houses and the inevitable encroachment of the railway, which would render Abel’s cottage, if it remained, uninhabitable from the smoke and the vibration. Abel had listened, but with that profound, immovable obstinacy which is less a calculated resistance than a kind of physical instinct, rooted in the very fibers of a man who has lived his life in a single, narrow groove. His cottage was not merely a shelter; it was the external shell of his consciousness, a material extension of his memories. To leave it would be to suffer a dismemberment of the spirit.

The neighbors’ initial sympathy for Abel’s resistance soon soured into resentment. The railway company, they argued, had been fair; if Abel refused to sell, the line might be altered, or delayed, and their own handsome compensations might be withheld pending the resolution of this single, stubborn holdout. Human nature is seldom more intolerant than when its own interests are threatened by another’s conscience. The Dimsdales and the Coopers began to mutter over their ale in the evening, their voices dropping to a sullen, resentful pitch.

“It’s all very fine for him to play the martyr,” said Mrs. Dimsdale, a woman whose sharp features were a faithful index of her practical, unsentimental mind. “He’s got no childer to think of, and his loom’s past its work. But if he baulks the company, and they stops the works, where be our new parlour carpets, I should like to know? It’s downright selfishness, that’s what it is.”

The matter came at last to the Reverend Arthur Teale, the vicar of Middlemarch, a man whose broad, thoughtful forehead and mild, grey eyes bespoke a nature more inclined toward philosophical inquiry than pastoral authority. Mr. Teale was deeply versed in the intricate moral economies of human action; he understood that Abel’s stubbornness was not mere perversity, but a desperate clinging to the only framework of meaning his life possessed. Yet he also felt the weight of the villagers’ practical grievances, and the responsibility of his office to promote harmony and temporal welfare. He walked up the rutted lane to Abel’s cottage on a damp Thursday afternoon, the smell of wet earth and decaying leaves filling the air with a melancholy that seemed to mirror his own internal hesitation.

He found the old weaver sitting at his loom, though the shuttle lay idle in his hand. The cottage was dim, the small diamond-paned window letting in a thin, grey light that fell upon the dusty warp and weft. The walls were bare save for a framed sampler stitched by Abel’s dead wife, and a faint, lingering scent of peppermint and old wool.

“Abel,” said Mr. Teale, seating himself on the edge of the wooden settle, his hat in his hands, “I’ve not come to bully you, my friend. But the village is uneasy. They fear your refusal will hurt them.”

Abel looked up, his face a map of deep lines and faded blue eyes. “I know what they fear, sir. They fear losing their brass. But what’s brass to the crying of a man’s heart? This loom—my Martha sat beside it when she was a girl. The railway will tear it all down, and what will I be then? Just a bit of old rubbish swept into the ditch.”

Mr. Teale felt the force of this appeal, which touched the deepest chords of his own sympathetic nature. He recognized the tragic paradox at the heart of all progress: that the collective good so often demands the sacrifice of the individual’s most sacred, because most personal, attachments. He sat for a long moment, his gaze resting on the intricate, fading threads of the sampler, weighing in his mind the abstract right of a community to its welfare against the concrete, poignant right of a man to his sorrow.
