The wind, which had been rising since noon, met me full in the face as I came through the gate of Ashby Cross, and I was glad of it; for it blew the hot tears back upon my cheeks, and drove the dust of the lane against my lips, so that I was forced to set my teeth and bend my head, and in that struggle with the elements there was a certain bitter comfort. Let them say what they would of me within that house—let Mrs. Harrowby's cold voice ring yet in my ears, pronouncing the word "dismissed" as though it were a verdict of the assizes—the wind at least knew nothing of my guilt or my innocence; it treated me as it treated the thorn-bushes and the stones, and I found a strange fellowship in its indifference.

I had walked, I suppose, a mile, when I turned to look back. The chimneys of Ashby Cross stood up black against a sky of ragged grey, and one window—the window of the schoolroom—caught a dull gleam of the sinking sun, as if some hand within had just set a lamp upon the sill. That was Ellen's doing, I thought; Ellen, the housemaid, who had been kind to me after her fashion, and who would miss me at the tea-hour. The thought of her simple face, and of the little ones who would wonder where Miss Ferrers had gone, brought the tears again; but I would not indulge them. I had cried enough in the housekeeper's parlour, when Mrs. Harrowby had told me that Mr. Edward's letters—three of them, and one of them containing a foolish ring—had been found in my work-basket; and that I, who had never so much as spoken to the young man above a dozen times, and those times in the presence of others, was to be sent away without a character, as a woman of loose principles and a corrupter of youth. I had stood before her, my hands clasped, my heart beating to suffocation, and I had said, "Madam, I am innocent of this; and Mr. Edward knows that I am innocent." And Mrs. Harrowby had answered, with a smile that was worse than any frown, "Mr. Edward has been questioned, and declines to speak. His silence, I fancy, is more eloquent than speech." And then she had added, "You will leave this house before nightfall, Miss Ferrers, and you will not return."

And I had left it. I had gathered my few possessions into a bundle—my mother's Bible, a shawl, a change of linen, the little purse that held four shillings and sixpence—and I had gone down the back stairs, and through the kitchen, where the cook had turned her back on me and the scullion had stared with round eyes, and out into the raw November afternoon. Mr. Edward, I knew, was gone to the covers with his gun; he had not had the courage to face me, and I despised him for it more than I despised his mother. A man may be weak, and yet not base; but to let a woman suffer for his folly, and to hide himself behind the silence that was half a lie—that was cowardice that I could not forgive.

The road to Marston, the nearest town, lay across the wolds, and I had been told it was six miles. Six miles on foot, with the light failing and the wind rising, and four shillings and sixpence in my purse—it was not a cheerful prospect; but I was young, and my limbs were strong, and there was that in my heart which would not let me sit down and weep by the wayside. I had been a governess for five years, and I had learned to bear much; I had learned to hold my tongue when I was unjustly blamed, and to keep a quiet face when my soul was in revolt; and now I summoned all that bitter discipline to my aid. "You are not the first," I said to myself, "who has been turned out of doors for another's fault; and you will not be the last. Set your foot to the road, and let the wind blow."

The wolds were bleak enough in that season. The hedges were bare, and the few trees that stood in them were twisted by years of wind into shapes of torment, their branches all leaning one way, like a congregation of unhappy souls. The fields were brown and sodden; here and there a flock of sheep huddled together under a wall, and a rook or two swung overhead, crying dismally. The sky was a great slab of lead, with a lurid streak in the west where the sun had gone down; and presently the rain came on—not a soft rain, but a sharp, spiteful rain, driven almost horizontally by the wind, that stung the face like small shot. I wrapped my shawl closer, and bent my head, and trudged on.

It was in the third mile, I think, that I came to the bridge over the beck, and there I stopped, for I was weary, and the rain had soaked through my thin boots. I leaned against the parapet and looked down at the water, which was swollen and yellow with the flood, swirling among the stones, and I thought—I am not ashamed to confess it—I thought of the deep pools further down, and of how easy it would be to lie down and let the cold water close over my head, and be done with the world and its injustice for ever. The thought was not a wild one; it came to me soberly, as a temptation comes to a hungry man, and I stood there a long minute, with the rain beating on my shoulders, and the water roaring below.
