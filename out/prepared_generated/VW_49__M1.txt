The train had carried her from the damp cloisters of Cambridge to the smoke-stained air of Richmond, and yet, as the cab rattled over the bridge, the distance seemed not to be a matter of miles but of centuries. The river lay below, a sheet of hammered grey lead, indifferent and heavy, and the trees along the bank were turning, their leaves burnt to the colour of old copper. It was October; the year was closing its eyes, and she, Edith, felt herself wide awake, painfully so, as if the skin had been peeled from her body and the air of Richmond was touching her raw.

The house stood where it had always stood, behind the iron railings and the overgrown laurels, a fortress of red brick and ivy that had withstood the weather of many lifetimes. It had not moved an inch. It had not aged. It seemed to Edith, stepping out of the cab and feeling the gravel crunch under her boots, that the house was waiting for her, not with welcome, but with the smug, unyielding patience of an object that knows it cannot be changed.

“Miss Edith! You’re late. The tea has been standing this half-hour.”

It was Mrs. Prewitt, the housekeeper, in the doorway, her cap starched to a blinding whiteness, her face a map of reproach. But it was not Mrs. Prewitt who held the power; it was the hall itself. The smell of it—beeswax, dried lavender, and the faint, sweet rot of the pot-pourri in the blue china bowl—rushed at her. It was the smell of her childhood, the smell of submission. She felt the old habit of her body return instantly: the slight droop of the shoulders, the lowering of the eyes, the softening of the voice. For four years she had argued about Plato and the nature of the soul in drafty lecture halls; for four years she had been Edith, a mind, a voice, a person of consequence. But here, on the threshold of the drawing-room, she was merely the daughter, returned.

Her mother sat by the window, silhouetted against the fading light. Mrs. Armitage was not looking out at the garden, but at the embroidery frame in her lap, though her needle had long ceased to move. She was a woman of great stillness, a stillness that was not peace but a rigid refusal to be disturbed.

“Well, Edith,” she said, without turning her head. “You have cut your hair.”

“I had to, Mother. It was too heavy for the lectures.” The excuse sounded thin, flimsy, a child’s lie. The truth was that she had wanted to look like the women in London, the women who worked, who smoked cigarettes in tea-rooms and talked of suffrage and wages. She had wanted to sever the connection to the girl who sat in this room.

“It looks severe,” Mrs. Armitage observed. “It makes you look older. Come, sit down. Tell us about the gown for the Vicar’s dinner on Thursday.”

Edith sat. The chair was deep and velvet, and it swallowed her. She looked at her hands—long, ink-stained fingers—and then at her mother’s hands, white and soft, resting on the silk. There was the chasm. Between the two pairs of hands lay the whole of her life. To stay was to take the needle, to take the tea-tray, to take the silence. To leave was to walk out into the grey rain of the world, to find a room of one’s own, to earn her bread with the sweat of her brow, which sounded, in this warm room, like a punishment, but in the lecture hall had sounded like freedom.

“The Vicar’s dinner,” Edith repeated. The words tasted of ash. She saw the long table, the silver gleaming in the candlelight, the faces of the neighbours, the endless, polite, circular conversation that went nowhere. She saw herself there, fifty years hence, a ghost at the feast.

“Your father is expecting you to play for us after dinner,” her mother continued. “He has been complaining of the silence. He says the house feels empty.”

“It is empty,” Edith said, the words slipping out before she could catch them.

Mrs. Armitage looked up then, her eyes sharp, a flash of steel in the soft face. “Empty? There are six of us living here, Edith. It is the opposite of empty. It is full. Too full, perhaps.”

The door opened and her father entered. Mr. Armitage was a man who seemed to be made of the same material as the house—solid, unyielding, slightly dusty. He carried a newspaper, folded with precise edges.

“Ah, the scholar,” he said. He did not kiss her. He looked at her as one looks at a piece of furniture that has been moved from its proper place. “And how is the world of books? Have they taught you how to be a good wife yet?”

The question hung in the air. It was the only question that mattered to them. The degree, the knowledge, the years of study—they were merely ornaments, like the embroidery on the cushions, things to be admired but not used.

“I have been offered a position,” Edith said. Her voice trembled, but she held it steady. “In a school. In Kensington. Teaching literature.”

The silence that followed was absolute. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked, loud and metallic, measuring the seconds of her betrayal. Her mother’s needle, which had been hovering, dropped to the floor with a tiny sound.

“A position,” Mr. Armitage said. He folded the newspaper again, though it was already folded. “Teaching. For money.”

“Yes.”

“And where would you live?”

“In a boarding house.”

“A boarding house,” he repeated, as if she had said a prison. He looked at her mother. “This is what comes of sending girls to university. They get ideas. They forget their duty.”
