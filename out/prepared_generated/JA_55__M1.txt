It is a truth universally acknowledged by the younger portion of society, that a young lady visiting a fashionable watering-place must be in want of amusement. Miss Harriet Vance, who had come to Brighton for the summer with her particular friend Mrs. Emily Grey and the latter’s husband, was no exception to this rule. Her expectations were modest but definite: to see the new circulating library, to walk upon the Steine at the proper hours, to acquire a new hat, and, above all, to observe the society of the place, which was at once more brilliant and more dangerous than the quiet country circles of her home.

It was her friend Mrs. Grey, a lady of a lively and prognosticating temper, who first introduced the subject of Mr. Alistair Blackwood. “Now, Harriet,” she began, as they were taking their breakfast one morning, the sea air lending a keenness to their appetites, “I must give you a warning. There is a gentleman arrived in town, a Mr. Blackwood, who is lately come into a considerable fortune, and who is, I am told, of a most interesting and melancholic disposition.”

Mr. Grey, a man whose opinions were formed for him by his wife and the sporting papers, looked up from his egg. “Blackwood? The fellow who lost a fortune at cards in Bath last winter?”

“The very same,” said Mrs. Grey, with a significant nod. “His reputation is, you see, quite established. He is said to have a dangerously romantic air, and to have broken the heart of a young lady of quality. His very silence is spoken of as a kind of eloquence. I am determined you shall not fall prey to him, my dear, for you have a disposition far too susceptible to such a character.”

Harriet listened with a mixture of apprehension and interest. The picture painted was of a man of profound feeling and dark experience, a creature of storm and shadow. She was sensible enough to know that such a description must be exaggerated, yet she could not help but feel a certain thrill at the prospect of meeting him. The idea of Mr. Blackwood, as formed in her imagination, was of a brooding, handsome figure, whose conversation would be as perilous as his reputation.

The meeting took place at a public breakfast, under the patronage of the formidable Lady Fitzwilliam. Mr. Blackwood was pointed out to her from a distance. He was a gentleman of perhaps seven-and-twenty, with a good figure and a countenance that was certainly handsome, though grave. He stood apart from the general conversation, observing the scene with a quiet intensity that Harriet was prepared to interpret as the mark of a wounded spirit.

Soon after, Mr. Grey, who was a man of great social energy when it did not tax his understanding, brought the gentleman over for an introduction. Mr. Blackwood bowed with perfect correctness. His voice, when he spoke, was low and measured. “Miss Vance. Mrs. Grey has informed me you have not long been in Brighton. I trust the change of air is agreeable to you?”

Harriet, her mind full of broken hearts and dangerous allurements, replied that she found it very agreeable. She waited for the conversation to take a more personal, a more perilous turn. It did not. Mr. Blackwood inquired after the health of her family, the comfort of their lodgings, and the state of the roads from the country. He spoke of the weather with a seriousness that bordered on the philosophical, and offered a well-reasoned opinion on the merits of the new pier.

There was a pause, which Harriet felt it her duty to fill with some topic of consequence. “I understand you have travelled a great deal, Mr. Blackwood.”

“I have had that opportunity,” he conceded. “Though I find that the view from any window is much the same, if one’s mind is sufficiently occupied.”

This was closer to the expected mark, and Harriet felt a small flutter of excitement. “And does your mind find itself much occupied, sir?”

Mr. Blackwood considered the question for a moment, his brow furrowed in thought. “It does,” he said at last. “I have been reading a great deal of late. A treatise on crop rotation, I confess, has engaged my attention more than is perhaps fashionable.”

Harriet could only stare. Crop rotation. The conversation drifted to the subject of turnips, a topic on which Mr. Blackwood was not only knowledgeable but passionately so. He spoke of soil quality and fallow fields with a gravity that would have been better suited to a discussion of national politics. When Mrs. Grey joined them, she found Harriet listening with a look of profound bewilderment to a detailed account of the benefits of marl as a fertiliser.

Later, as they walked back along the seafront, Mrs. Grey could not contain her curiosity. “Well, my dear? And what is your opinion of the infamous Mr. Blackwood? Did he press your hand? Did he speak in tragic tones of his past sorrows?”

“He was very civil,” said Harriet, at a loss for a better word. “He is a great reader.”

“A great reader! That is a good sign. And of what does he read? The poetry of passion, I suppose? The novels of terror?”

“Treatises, I believe,” said Harriet faintly. “On agriculture.”

Mrs. Grey stopped in her tracks. “Agriculture? You must be jesting, Harriet. A man of such dangerous reputation? It is a pose, of course. A clever disguise to throw people off their guard. He is a sly one.”

But Harriet was not convinced. She had observed Mr. Blackwood closely, and she had seen no slyness in him, only a kind of earnest, unrelenting dullness. She had looked for the fires of passion and had found, instead, the quiet, well-ordered fields of a gentleman farmer. The disappointment was acute. To be warned against a man of spirit and feeling, and to be presented with a man whose chief concern was the price of hay, was a disappointment of a peculiar and frustrating kind.
