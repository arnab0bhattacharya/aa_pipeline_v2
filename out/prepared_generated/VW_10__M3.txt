The rock was hot under her thighs; the midges circled and bit, but they were nothing; she was impervious, made of salt and granite and the blinding blue that poured from the Cornish sky into the creased and cupped palms of the sea. She was eleven, and the world was not yet a story but a substance, a matter of light striking the wet blades of bladderwrack, of the suck and gasp of water in the cave behind her, where the green darkness smelled of iron and ancient weather. Her brother Hal was up on the cliff path, a red speck moving against the gorse, and her mother sat further down the beach, a parasol tilted like a pale, shut eye. Eleanor did not look at them. She looked at the rim where the water heaved itself up and shattered, over and over, into a lace of foam that the wind whisked away. It was the repetition that held her, the sense that this had always happened and always would, and that she was not separate from it but made of the same exhausted, renewing matter. She dug her toes into the shingle and felt the small, sharp pains as a kind of confirmation. *I am here*, she thought, though she did not think it in words, but in the pressure of the stones, in the salt drying to a fine crust on her lips, in the sun bearing down on her bare brown shoulders like a hand. And then Hal called, or her mother rustled the newspaper, or a gull cried, and the moment broke, scattering its pieces into the ordinary afternoon, and she stood up, brushing the grit from her skirt, already becoming someone who had been happy and did not yet know it.

The garden in Hampstead smelled of wet earth and the last of the beans. It was September, and the light had thinned to a yellowish grey that made the brick walls seem taller and closer than they were. Arthur stood by the trellis, turning a dead leaf between his fingers, and she knew what was coming because he had arranged his face into an expression of resolute gentleness that was not his own. He spoke. The words were the expected words, a little formal, a little tremulous, offered up like a tray bearing a cup she had not asked for. She heard them and simultaneously heard the neighbour's dog barking, and a train somewhere distant, shunting, and the sound of her own breathing, which seemed unnaturally loud. She felt pity, and a sort of impatience, and then a sudden vertigo, as if the garden had tilted and she were sliding towards him, not by choice but by gravity, by the accumulated weight of all the afternoons she had spent in this house, in his company, reading, or pretending to read, while he watched her over the top of his book. She could refuse. The refusal lay in her like a stone, heavy and perfectly shaped for the purpose. But to refuse would be to stay on the beach alone, with the bladderwrack and the gulls, and she was thirty now, and the sea was very far away, and the rock had grown cold. So she smiled, and the smile surprised her by being warm, a real smile, not a mask, because she was moved, after all, by his awkwardness, by the leaf trembling in his hand, by the fact that he wanted her, specifically her, Eleanor, and not the general, abstract woman that men were supposed to want. She said yes, and the word hung in the wet September air like a bell that had been struck and was still vibrating, and she thought, with a clarity that was almost physical, *Now it begins*, though she did not know what it was that began, and the leaf fell from Arthur's fingers and lay on the path, brown and curling, perfectly insignificant.

The room in the Sussex nursing home was white, or had been white once, and was now the colour of old ivory, of bone, of something that had been cleaned too often and not enough. Her sister Dora lay in the bed with her mouth slightly open, and her breathing was the breathing of a tide going out, each breath a little shallower than the last, a little slower, a long, slow recession from a shore that was barely visible. Eleanor sat in the chair by the window and watched the November light move across the floor, a pale, exhausted light that seemed to have travelled a great distance and arrived with nothing left to give. On the mantelpiece a clock ticked, and each tick was a small, precise incision in the silence. She thought she ought to be feeling something large and definite, grief, or terror, or the grand, dark music that was supposed to accompany death, but instead she felt only a floating attentiveness, a hyper-awareness of the grain in the wood of the chair-arm, of the threadbare patch on the carpet, of the way Dora's hand, flung outside the blanket, looked like a hand made of paper, translucent and creased, a hand that might dissolve if one breathed on it. They had not been close, she and Dora; there had been the usual childhood rivalries, the long adult silences, the occasional letter full of stiff affection. And now Dora was leaving, or had largely left, and what remained was this husk, this breathing apparatus, this machine running down. Eleanor took the paper hand in hers and held it, and it was cold, not with the cold of the living but with a deeper cold, and she thought of the Cornish rock, how it had held the sun's heat for hours after sunset, and how the body, by contrast, let everything go, let it drain away into the sheets, into the air, into the blank white room. A nurse passed in the corridor, her shoes squeaking on the linoleum, a small, absurd sound, and Eleanor almost laughed, because it was absurd, all of it, the squeaking shoes and the ticking clock and the paper hand and the fact that she was here, alive, in this chair, holding her sister's fingers, and did not know why.
