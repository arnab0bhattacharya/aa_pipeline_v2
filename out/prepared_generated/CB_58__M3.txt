The letter lay open on the desk, its foreign franking and precise London hand a stark contrast to the worn oak and the faint smell of damp slate that permeated the schoolroom. Eleanor Marsden read it for the third time, not because its terms were obscure, but because she was compelling her own soul to digest their full, bitter weight. Fifty pounds a year, and a residence in the shires of England. It was a fortune to an orphan dependent on her own exertions; it was deliverance from exile. She had been four years in Brabant, four years of teaching the daughters of Flemish merchants their rudimentary English and imperfect French, of enduring the capricious tyranny of Madame Vliet, and of freezing in winter and sweltering in summer within walls that seemed to sweat a perpetual, heavy gloom. Now, the iron gate was unlocked. She had only to walk through it.

A sudden gust of wind from the window, which she had opened to dispel the afternoon’s torpor, sent the letter fluttering to the floor. She did not stoop to retrieve it. She stood in the shaft of pale, watery sunlight, her hands clasped tightly before her, wrestling with a spirit that was both fiercely rejoicing and unaccountably smitten. She was not deceived by the suddenness of the opportunity; she knew her own worth. She was the best teacher Madame Vliet possessed; she was the only one who could govern the wild, sullen girls of the Rue d’Argent without recourse to the rod. She had earned this deliverance, and she told herself she would take it with a hardened heart.

But a sound from the corridor beneath her window broke upon her rigid resolve. It was not the usual shrill, polyglot chatter of the playground. It was a subdued, collective wail, a sound that seemed to press against the very stones of the courtyard. She stepped to the casement and looked down.

The whole school was gathered there, or so it appeared. Some forty girls, from the tall, awkward daughters of the cloth-merchants down to the tiny, flaxen-haired children of the burgomasters, stood in a disorganized, sorrowful cluster. At their head, with a face as pale and set as marble, stood the eldest pupil, Mathilde. She was looking up at the window, and as she caught Eleanor’s eye, she raised her hand in a gesture that was less a wave than an appeal.

“Mademoiselle!” The cry came up, a chorus of accents thick with the guttural tones of Brabant, yet softened by an emotion that was universally understood. “Mademoiselle, do not go!”

Eleanor drew back as if struck. The letter on the floor seemed to rustle with a malicious life. She had not breathed a word of her intention; she had thought to slip away quietly at the term’s end, leaving behind her a cold, unregretted memory. Yet the secret had oozed through the very stones of the Rue d’Argent. Perhaps Madame Vliet, in a fit of pique, had spoken of it to the servants, and the servants had whispered it to the pupils. Or perhaps the children possessed that instinct of the hunted, which tells them when a protector is about to be torn from them.

She closed the window with a sharp snap, shutting out the sight and the sound. But she could not shut out the truth. She was not merely a teacher to these girls; she was their bulwark against the arbitrary rules, the scanty meals, the heavy, unthinking cruelty of the school’s domestic economy. To leave them was to leave them to the wolves. She knew it. She stood in the middle of the room, her breath coming quickly, her eyes burning. What claim had they upon her? She was young; she was twenty-four; she had her own life to live, her own bread to earn, her own future to secure. She was not their mother, nor their sister. She owed them nothing but the instruction she gave and the salary she drew. It was a hard world; she had learned that lesson early, at the hands of an aunt who had taken her in from charity and taught her that gratitude was a coin quickly worn smooth. Why should she sacrifice a comfortable post in her own country for the sake of a pack of foreign girls who would forget her name in a year?

She stooped and picked up the letter, folding it with deliberate care. She would answer it by the evening post. She would accept.

She turned to leave the schoolroom, and stopped short. In the doorway stood a figure that, in her preoccupation, she had not heard approach. It was Miss Atherlow, the English assistant, who taught drawing and geography and slept in the chamber next to Eleanor’s. She was a woman of thirty, with a plain, sallow face, and dark, steadfast eyes that rarely smiled. She and Eleanor had never been friends. There had been no quarrel, no overt coldness, but a barrier had stood between them from the first day, a barrier of mutual reserve and perhaps of mutual pride. They were of the same country, the same profession, the same lonely condition; yet they had kept apart, each walking her own solitary path, exchanging only the necessary words of the classroom and the dining-table. Eleanor had sometimes thought that Miss Atherlow disliked her; at other times she had suspected that the other woman’s silence was merely the mask of a deep and wounded shyness. But she had never sought to know. She had her own wounds to guard.

Now, Miss Atherlow stood in the doorway, and her eyes, usually so guarded, were fixed on Eleanor with a look that was at once searching and profoundly sad. She did not speak. She held in her hand a book, as if she had been seeking a pretext for her presence. But she did not advance into the room, nor did she offer the book. She merely stood, and looked.
