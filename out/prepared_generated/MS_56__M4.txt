The rain had fallen in ceaseless, sullen torrents for three days, and the whole of London seemed to me but a vast and weeping prison, its chimneys and its spires dissolving into the universal grey. I stood at the window of our house in Bloomsbury, watching the drops trace their weary courses down the glass, and I confess that my heart kept time with their melancholy descent. It was on such an afternoon, when the gloom of the heavens without found a too perfect echo in the desolation of my spirit, that I received the letter which completed my ruin.

I had, in the season of my girlhood, when hope was yet a stranger to disappointment, composed a tale—a wild and fervid offspring of a mind that knew not the world it presumed to depict. I had poured into it all the tumultuous feelings of a heart that had not yet learned to dissemble, all the sublime terrors and exalted sorrows that solitude and the grandeur of nature had awakened within me. I had thought it a thing to be hidden forever in the recesses of my escritoire, a secret communion between my soul and the silent page. But circumstance, that cruel mocker of our intentions, had decreed otherwise. A dear friend, to whom I had confided the manuscript, had, with a zeal more generous than wise, placed it in the hands of a publisher. It appeared; it was read; and, for a brief and intoxicating season, I was celebrated.

Ah, the intoxication of that hour! It was a draught too sweet, too potent for mortal lips. To hear one's own thoughts, the very children of one's lonely musings, echoed back by a thousand voices; to feel that the sentiments which had seemed to me but the idle phantoms of a fevered brain had found a responsive chord in the breasts of others—this was a joy so exquisite, so pure, that I believed it must be allied to virtue. I walked as one in a dream, and the common air of heaven seemed to me transfigured. But the awakening was to be bitter indeed.

The secret of my authorship could not be long preserved. A careless word, a servant's gossip, a resemblance too striking to be overlooked—some trivial circumstance, I know not what, betrayed me. And then, in a moment, the scene was changed. The very persons who had lavished upon me their hollow courtesies now turned from me with averted looks. The ladies who had sought my company, to whose houses I had been welcomed as an ornament, now whispered behind their fans, and their whispers were daggers. I was no longer the gentle, unassuming wife of Mr. Edward Ashcombe, the respected man of science; I was a woman who had dared to think, to feel, to write—a monster of unwomanly ambition, a creature whose mind had burst the sacred bounds of feminine decorum. The world, which had crowned me with its garlands, now spat upon the wreath and trampled it in the dust.

But the keenest arrow in my quiver of sorrows was yet to be loosed. My husband—he whom I had loved with a devotion that knew no bounds, he for whose sake I would have sacrificed every laurel that ever bloomed—my husband, Edward, was ashamed. I saw it in the coldness of his eye, in the studied distance of his manner, in the abrupt silence that fell upon him when I entered the room. He, whose intellect was his pride, whose scientific researches were the talk of the Royal Society, could not endure that his wife should have achieved a fame that rivalled, nay, that eclipsed his own. His pride, that tender plant, was wounded to the quick; and his wounded pride sought its remedy in the assertion of a despotism I had never before known him to exercise.

It was on the evening of that day of rain and weeping, when the letter lay before me on the table—a letter from the publisher, proposing a second edition, and hinting at a new work—that Edward entered my chamber. His countenance was pale and set; his lips were compressed into a line of iron resolution. He held in his hand a copy of my book, and he laid it upon the table with a gesture of such cold contempt that my heart died within me.

"I have read," said he, in a voice that was calm and measured, but which thrilled through me like the knell of doom—"I have read the comments of my friends upon this—this production. I have heard the whispers that are abroad concerning you. I have marked the finger of scorn that is pointed at the wife of Edward Ashcombe. Madam, this must cease. The woman who bears my name must be content to shine in the sphere which Providence has assigned her. She must not seek to dazzle the world with the false glitter of an intellect that is not hers by right. I command you—nay, I entreat you, if any affection for me yet remains in your bosom—to abandon this unhallowed pursuit. Write no more. Let this book be the last. Let the world forget that it ever existed, and let us endeavour to forget it likewise."

I stood before him, mute and motionless, as one who has been struck by lightning. The tears, which had been gathering in my eyes, now flowed in torrents down my cheeks. I cast myself at his feet; I clasped his knees; I implored him, with all the eloquence of a breaking heart, not to demand of me this sacrifice—the sacrifice of my very being. I told him that the power to write was not a thing of my own choosing, but a gift, a fatal gift, that had been woven into the very texture of my soul; that to bid me cease to write was to bid me cease to breathe. I reminded him of the pure and lofty aims that had animated my pen—the desire to elevate, to console, to awaken in other breasts the sublime emotions that had visited my own. I pleaded, I wept, I clung to him with the desperate tenacity of a drowning wretch.
