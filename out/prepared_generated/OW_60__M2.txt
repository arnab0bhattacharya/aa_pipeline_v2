The Royal Lake of Aethelgard was, in the opinion of its most distinguished resident, a masterpiece of nature that suffered from the unfortunate presence of too many spectators. Lady Cygnia, a swan of such immaculate whiteness that she seemed less a bird than a fragment of cloud that had fallen into the water by mistake, held this view with the serene arrogance of one who has never been told otherwise. She drifted through the morning mist with a grace that was, if not entirely natural, certainly expensive. To her, the lake was not a body of water but a stage, and the reeds, the lilies, and the distant hills were merely the set dressing for her solitary performance of dignity.

Below her, clustered near the muddy banks where the water turned brown with sediment, were the ducks. They were a tedious crowd, muddied in their plumage and loud in their quacking. They did not possess the aerodynamic perfection of the swan; they were stout, short-necked, and possessed of an irritating habit of looking at one. Cygnia regarded them with the polite disdain one might show a waiter who has spilled the sauce. She had been invited to share a crust of bread with them by the old Heron, who stood in the shallows like a ragged beggar in a green coat, but she had declined with a flick of her tail that could have severed a ribbon.

“Vulgarity is the only language they speak,” she had told the Heron, her voice a cool whisper that cut through the damp air. “To share is to dilute. I am not here to be diluted.”

The Heron had blinked his yellow eyes, but said nothing. He knew, as one does who has lived a long time in the mud, that pride is a garment that fits well in summer but chafes terribly in winter.

Cygnia spent the autumn in a state of exquisite isolation. She preened her feathers until they shone like polished silver. She watched the leaves fall from the ancient oaks on the shore, admiring their descent as a form of dance that was, admittedly, a little too chaotic for her taste. She ignored the growing chill in the air, for cold was merely a suggestion that the world was becoming less comfortable for those who were not prepared to be comfortable. She ignored the way the other birds began to pack their feathers tighter, for she believed that her own feathers were woven from a different substance, one that impervious to the elements.

Then, one morning, the silence of the lake was broken by a sound that was not the cry of a bird or the rustle of a reed, but a sharp, crystalline crack. It was the sound of the first ice forming. It spread across the surface of the water like a grey lace, thin and fragile at first, but growing thicker and harder by the hour. By noon, the Royal Lake was no longer a mirror, but a sheet of white iron.

Cygnia felt a shudder that had nothing to do with the cold. It was a shudder of confusion. The water, which had been her medium, her stage, her home, was no longer liquid. It was a solid, indifferent surface that offered no buoyancy, no flow, no life. She tried to glide, but her feet slipped on the slick ice. She tried to fly, but the air was so heavy with frost that her wings felt like lead. She was stranded, not on the ice, but in the air, suspended in a moment of terrible stillness.

The ducks, who had been driven from the shallows by the rising ice, huddled together in a clump of bare branches. They were shivering, their feathers ruffled and dirty, but they were alive. They were also, to Cygnia’s horror, smiling. Or rather, they were looking at her with a kind of pitying amusement that was far more stinging than any insult.

The Old Heron, who had somehow managed to find a patch of unfrozen water near the root of a willow, watched the scene with detached interest. He did not offer help. He knew that help, like bread, was only useful if one had the humility to accept it.

Cygnia realized, with a clarity that was painful as a knife, that she had been wrong. Not about the ducks’ vulgarity, for they were still vulgar, still muddy, still loud. But she had been wrong about the world. She had believed that beauty was a shield, that elegance was a substance, that dignity was a wall. But beauty was a performance, and when the stage froze, the actor was left standing in the dark.

The cold bit into her legs. The ice was beautiful, in a terrible, austere way. It was a perfect, unbroken plane of white, reflecting the grey sky with a fidelity that was almost cruel. There was no texture, no depth, no life. It was a perfect mirror, and in it, Cygnia saw not the noble swan, but a white ghost, trapped in a frame of glass.

She looked at the ducks. They were moving. They were sliding across the ice with a clumsy, sliding grace that was not grace at all, but survival. They were heading towards the center of the lake, where the water was deepest and the ice thinnest. They knew where the open water lay. They knew, because they had been there, because they had swum in the depths, because they had not feared the mud.

Cygnia had always stayed near the surface, near the shore, near the safety of the reeds. She had never gone to the center. She had never known the depths. And so, while the ducks knew the way to the open water, she was lost in the perfection of the frozen surface.
