The fog had lifted from the river by the time I reached the narrow stair in Pimlico, but the reek of it—that compound of tar, hemp, and ancient mud—seemed to have followed me inland and to have taken up residence in the hallway of my client’s lodgings. Captain Anselm Rusk was a man whose face had been weathered to the consistency of a hide drum, and whose eyes, though clouded with years, retained the watchful habit of one who has spent his life reading the sky for signs of treachery.

“You are the fellow they call Bellamy,” said he, without rising from his chair. “They tell me you find things that are lost. I have lost a son. I want him found.”

I took the seat he indicated and drew out my notebook. “When did you last see him?”

“Three years ago, come Michaelmas. He was a good lad, but headstrong. He would not go to sea, and he would not take the berth I found him in the counting-house. He said he would make his own way, and he made it to the docks. A letter came at Christmas—a short letter, and not a kind one. Since then, nothing.”

“Have you made inquiries?”

“I have. I am not a man who sits idle. I have walked the length of the Ratcliffe Highway and back again, and I have asked questions in every public-house from Shadwell to Limehouse. I have learned only this: that my son took work at a warehouse near the Hermitage Stairs, and that the man who owns it pays his men in cash and keeps no clerk. The name on the gate is Kettle.”

I wrote the name down. “And the nature of the business?”

“That is the thing that troubles me,” said the captain, and his hand tightened upon the arm of his chair. “They ship crates down the river by night. The crates are heavy when they go out. When they come back—for they do come back, sir, by the same boat and to the same stair—they are light. I have seen it with my own eyes from the wharf opposite. A crate that three men could scarcely lift is carried back by one.”

I closed my notebook. “You have been thorough, Captain Rusk. I will begin tonight.”

---

The Hermitage Stairs are a descent of slimy stone that seem to lead less to the water than to some nether region of the city’s commerce, where the lamps burn oil and the men speak in murmurs. I stationed myself in the shadow of a crane-chain at half-past nine, and I had not waited above an hour when a boat came in upon the flood tide, pulled by two men, with a third at the tiller. She made fast, and the crates were unloaded—four of them, of a size to hold a man, had a man been so inclined to fold himself. They were carried up the stair with grunts and curses, and I marked that the bearers staggered under the weight.

I followed at a distance. The crates were taken into the warehouse, and the door was shut. I waited. At the end of a half-hour, the door opened again, and the same four crates were brought out—but now each was carried by a single man, and carried lightly, as one carries a basket of fish. They were loaded into the boat, and the boat pulled out into the stream and was swallowed by the fog.

I returned the following morning in the guise of a labourer seeking work. The warehouse was a tall, narrow building of blackened brick, with a hoist above the door and a smell about it that I could not immediately place—a sweetish, earthy smell, like a cellar where apples have rotted. I asked for Mr. Kettle and was told he was not to be seen. I asked for work and was told there was none. I loitered at the corner until the men knocked off for dinner, and I fell into step beside a young fellow with a broken nose and the look of one who has been at sea and did not care for it.

“Is it a good berth?” I asked.

“It is a berth,” said he, “and it pays in gold, and it asks no questions. That is all I know, and all I want to know.”

“And the crates?”

He stopped and looked at me sidelong. “You are a curious one. I would advise you to be less curious. There was a lad here—a lad named Rusk—who was curious. He is not here now.”

“Where is he?”

“I do not know,” said the man, and he walked on. But he had not gone ten paces before he turned. “Try the mortuary at Wapping,” said he. “They have a body there that no one has claimed. A young man, drowned. It may be nothing. But it may be him.”

---

I went to Wapping, and I viewed the body. It was a young man of about five-and-twenty, fair-haired, with a mark upon his left forearm—a small anchor, pricked in blue ink. I sent a wire to Captain Rusk, and he came at once. He stood for a long time looking down at the face, and then he said, in a voice that was very quiet, “That is my son. That is the anchor he had done when he was sixteen, against my wishes. He was always against my wishes.”

I left him to his grief and went back to the warehouse. This time I did not loiter. I went to the door and I knocked, and when it was opened I put my foot within the jamb and my card into the hand of the man who opened it.

“I am an investigator,” said I, “and I have come about the body of Thomas Rusk.”
