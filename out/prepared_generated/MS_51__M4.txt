The rain had fallen without ceasing since morning, and the house of Thornfield Rise, wrapped in a shroud of grey mist, seemed to me less a dwelling of joy than a mausoleum of hopes. I stood at the window of the library, watching the water course down the panes in shuddering rivulets, and my heart, which should have been buoyant with the near approach of my nuptials, was oppressed by a vague and nameless dread. In two days I was to become the wife of Edmund Harcourt—the brave, the generous, the idol of my soul; and yet, in the very hour of my felicity, a shadow had fallen upon my spirit, dark and cold as the storm that beat against the casements.

It was on the evening of my arrival that I first beheld the woman who was destined to poison the cup of my happiness. She was seated in the schoolroom, instructing the younger children of the household, and her countenance, though pale and careworn, possessed a melancholy beauty that struck me to the heart. Her name was Miss Ellwood; she had been some months in the family, and was spoken of with that cold respect which the world accords to those who serve. Yet there was in her manner a reserve, a something of suppressed anguish, that awakened my compassion; and when our eyes met, I fancied I read in hers a look of strange and painful interest—a look that haunted me like the memory of a crime.

How shall I describe the gradual revelation that followed? It was on the second evening after my arrival, when the family had retired, that I chanced to linger in the gallery, my mind filled with those sweet and solemn thoughts which occupy the heart of a bride on the eve of her espousals. The door of the schoolroom stood ajar; a light burned within; and, impelled by an irresistible curiosity, I approached, and beheld Miss Ellwood kneeling by the open window, her hands clasped, her face bathed in tears, and her lips moving in the accents of prayer. I would have retired, but my feet were rooted to the spot; and in another moment she arose, and, turning, perceived me. A deep flush overspread her cheeks; she advanced towards me with a trembling step, and, seizing my hand, exclaimed, in a voice that thrilled through my very soul—

"Miss Ashby, forgive me! I know that I am guilty of a strange and unpardonable intrusion; but the sight of you—the sight of one who is about to be united to him—has awakened feelings which I can no longer suppress. Hear me, I implore you, before it is too late!"

I was struck with amazement; I knew not what to answer; but her agitation was so intense, her countenance so full of woe, that I could not refuse to listen. She led me to a seat; she threw herself upon her knees before me; and in broken and impassioned accents, she disclosed to me the dreadful secret which had darkened her life.

"Your betrothed," she said, "is Edmund Harcourt—the same who, five years since, stood in this very county as the challenger in a duel that terminated in the death of my brother. My brother, Arthur Ellwood, was the pride of our house, the hope of our aged parents, the protector of my orphan youth. He had a sister—alas! that sister is before you—whose honour he conceived to be assailed by the insolent addresses of a man whom he deemed a libertine. He demanded satisfaction; they met; and your Edmund Harcourt, with a hand too fatal, sent a ball through the heart of him who was dearer to me than life itself."

I shrieked; I started from my seat; the room swam before my eyes; and for a moment I was insensible to everything but the horror of the disclosure. When I recovered, she was still kneeling before me, her hands clasped, her eyes streaming with tears.

"And you," I cried, "you have come hither—you have taken service in this house—to be near the man who slew your brother?"

"I came," she replied, "impelled by a power I could not resist. I came to see if the murderer of my brother was indeed the monster I believed him; I came to watch the man who was to be happy while I was miserable. I have watched him; I have seen him generous, gentle, and beloved; and my hatred has been mingled with a strange and agonizing admiration. But, oh! Miss Ashby, think not that I have been actuated by a base desire of revenge. I have prayed for him whom I once abhorred; I have wept over the memory of my brother; and I have asked of Heaven but one boon—that the man who robbed me of my all might not be permitted to enjoy a felicity that would mock my sorrow."

I was overwhelmed with a tumult of emotions. I loved Edmund with the ardour of a first and only passion; I had esteemed him as the soul of honour; and now, in a moment, the fabric of my happiness was shattered, and I beheld him in the light of a murderer. I rushed from the room; I sought my chamber; I threw myself upon my bed, and gave myself up to the wildest agony. The storm without raged with redoubled fury; the thunder rolled in awful peals; the lightning flashed upon the walls; and the rain beat against the windows like the knell of my departed joys. I rose; I paced the apartment; I called upon the name of Edmund, and then recoiled with horror from the sound. Was it possible that the hand which had so often pressed mine in tenderness had been stained with the blood of a fellow-creature? Was it possible that the lips which had whispered words of love to me had been the instruments of death to another? The thought was madness; I could not endure it.
