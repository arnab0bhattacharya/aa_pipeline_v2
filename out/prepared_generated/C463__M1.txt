The sea at Scarborough did not roar; it breathed. A long, slow exhalation of salt and damp that crept through the very stones of the lodging house, settling upon Miss Eleanor Vance’s muslin gown like a fine, cold dust. She sat on the shingle, her back straight against a chalk-washed boulder, and watched her three charges, the children of Mr. and Mrs. Albright, as they shrieked and splashed at the water’s edge. Young Master Thomas, a boy of nine with a temperament as changeable as the tide, was currently engaged in burying his younger sister’s doll in the wet sand, while little Mary wailed in protest. Eleanor’s fingers tightened on the book in her lap, a volume of poetry she had not read a single word of all morning. Her duty was not to the verse, but to the children, to the constant, low hum of their needs and misbehaviours.

It was a hum she had grown accustomed to. For five years, her life had been measured in the turning of pages, the mending of pinafores, and the quiet, necessary extinction of her own desires. She was Miss Vance, the governess. A useful, unobtrusive piece of the Albright family’s machinery.

It was then that she saw her. The woman was a vision of fashionable ease, strolling along the promenade above the beach with a parasol of pale pink silk held aloft against the weak afternoon sun. Her dress was a confection of ruched silk and lace, the colour of a spring crocus. Beside her walked a portly, well-dressed man, his waistcoat straining slightly, who patted her gloved hand with proprietary affection. As they drew nearer, Eleanor felt a prickle of recognition, a distant memory stirred to life. The woman turned her head, her eyes scanning the beach, and her gaze fell upon Eleanor. A flicker of confusion, then a brilliant, dazzling smile.

“Eleanor? Eleanor Vance? Is it truly you?”

It was. Mrs. Agnes Croft, née Albright. No, not Albright. Agnes Perriwell. They had shared a dormitory at Miss Ainsworth’s Academy for Young Ladies. Agnes, who had been plump and perpetually cheerful, with a talent for embroidery and a weakness for sweetmeats. Eleanor rose, smoothing her skirts, feeling the rough wool of her travelling dress as a sudden, coarse insult.

“Agnes,” she said, her voice sounding thin and reedy. “What a surprise.”

Agnes descended the few stone steps to the beach with a delicate, mincing gait, her husband trailing behind her like a well-tethered balloon. “I knew it! I said to my dear Mr. Croft, ‘That looks like my old friend Eleanor.’ And it is! Oh, you look… well.”

The pause was a canyon. Eleanor knew what she looked like. Her face, always pale, was pinched from the sea wind. Her hands were chapped, her hair escaping its neat knot in wisps of mouse-brown. She looked like what she was: a woman who worked for her living.

“You are married, I see,” Eleanor said, gesturing vaguely towards the man.

“Indeed! To Mr. Croft. He is in shipping, you know. Quite successful.” Agnes beamed, a picture of triumphant femininity. “And you? You are… visiting?” She glanced at the Albright children, who had paused their squabbling to stare at the lady in the purple dress.

“I am in the employ of the Albright family,” Eleanor said, the words tasting like ash in her mouth. “I am the governess.”

Agnes’s smile faltered, but only for a second. It was replaced by a look of such profound, unadulterated pity that Eleanor felt the blood drain from her face. It was worse than scorn. Scorn could be met with quiet pride. Pity was a soft, suffocating blanket that smothered all hope of defence.

“Oh,” Agnes breathed. “Oh, my dear. A governess.”

Mr. Croft cleared his throat. “Commendable work, I’m sure. Commendable.”

“Of course it is,” Agnes said, patting Eleanor’s arm with a gloved hand. The silk was cool and smooth against Eleanor’s skin. “But it must be so… trying. No home of your own. No prospect. Always at the beck and call of others. My dear, you must be quite worn out.”

Each word was a carefully placed stone in a small, elegant prison. Eleanor felt the need to justify herself, to explain that she was valued, that her position was one of respect. But the words would not come. How could she explain the quiet satisfaction of teaching Mary her letters, or the fierce, protective love she felt for Thomas, even in his worst moments? To Agnes, these were just the duties of a servant.

“We are very comfortable,” Eleanor managed, her voice tight. “The Albrights are kind employers.”

“Kindness is all very well,” Agnes said, her head tilted. “But it is not a life, is it? It is merely… existence. I do so worry for you, Eleanor. You were always the cleverest of us. So much promise.” She sighed, a delicate, musical sound. “To see you brought to this. It is just a little bit sad.”

The pity was a physical force, pressing in on her. Eleanor looked past Agnes, at the vast, grey expanse of the North Sea. She thought of the pin-money she carefully saved, the few books she owned, the small, private world she had carved out in the attic room of the Albright’s townhouse in York. Was it a life? Or was it just existence? Agnes’s question, meant as a soft blow, had struck a nerve she did not know was so raw.

“Look at your hands,” Agnes continued, her voice a confidential whisper. “You must be so cold. You should have gloves.”

“I find they impede my work,” Eleanor said, the words sharper than she intended.

Agnes took a small step back, her expression one of wounded surprise. “I only meant… well. You must take care of yourself. No one else will, you see. That is the tragedy of it.”
