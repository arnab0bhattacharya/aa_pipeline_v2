It was on a raw November afternoon, when the lamps were already lit in the City and the fog hung like a soiled curtain over the river, that I found myself in the consulting-room of my friend Mr. Lucian Vane, watching him read a letter with that singular abstraction which his clients so often mistook for inattention. He had been for some years the last court of appeal in those domestic mysteries which the ordinary police decline and the ordinary family solicitor muddles, and his rooms in Devereux Court were seldom without some anxious visitor.

“A lady,” said he, tossing the letter upon the table. “Mrs. Emmeline Ashcombe, of Fenchurch Terrace. She is persuaded that someone enters her house at night and moves her furniture by the breadth of an inch. Her son writes that she is failing, and begs me to reassure her. The son, I observe, is a man of decided opinions and a heavy hand with a pen; he underlines his words. The mother, on the other hand, writes a small, firm, upright hand, and dates her letters. People who date their letters, Stamford, are seldom quite lost.”

“You will go?”

“I have already been. I spent an hour in the house this morning, and I confess that I am not yet prepared to sign the certificate of lunacy which the excellent son appears to desire. There is a circumstance.” He drew his long fingers together and regarded them. “The house stands at the end of the terrace, and its western wall is the boundary wall of the premises of Messrs. Harker and Pyle, bankers. Their vaults, I learn, have lately been enlarged.”

I confess that I did not at once perceive the connexion, and said as much.

“Neither did I, at first,” he returned, with the shadow of a smile. “But I have a fancy to show you the lady. She expects me at six. You will observe the furniture. I would ask you particularly to observe the furniture.”

Fenchurch Terrace proved to be a row of narrow, respectable houses of the last century, their fronts of dingy brick, their windows filled with the reflected glare of the street. The last house of the row stood a little apart, its flank of blank wall abutting upon the high, soot-blackened masonry of the bank. Mrs. Ashcombe received us in a drawing-room of the first floor, a tall, spare old lady with a back as straight as a ramrod and eyes of a disconcerting clearness. She wore black, and her hands were folded upon the head of an ebony stick.

“You will think me a foolish old woman, Mr. Vane,” said she, when we were seated. “My son thinks so. He has been so good as to say that I am in my dotage. But I am not in my dotage, and I shall tell you what I know. For eleven nights a man has come into this house.”

“You have seen him?”

“I have not seen him. I have heard him. I sleep ill, sir, as old people do, and my room is at the back, above the little yard. At some hour between two and three I am wakened by a sound—a sound I cannot describe to you, save that it is like a heavy thing being set down very softly, and then drawn. It is not the wind. It is not the rats. It is a man’s work, and it is done with care. And in the morning the furniture is moved.”

“Which furniture?”

“The tall press in the back room upon this floor. The bookcase in the dining-room beneath. The chest of drawers in the spare bedroom. Never more than an inch, sir—sometimes less. But I have lived in this house thirty years, and I know the place of every leg upon the carpet. I have marked the places with chalk. The marks do not lie.”

Vane rose and walked slowly to the window. “Your son, I take it, has examined the doors and windows?”

“He has. He says there is no entry, and that I have moved the things myself in my sleep. I do not walk in my sleep, Mr. Vane. I have never walked in my sleep.”

“You have no servant?”

“One woman, Mrs. Gedd, who is sixty and deaf, and sleeps in the attic. She hears nothing. She is a good creature, but she hears nothing.”

Vane asked leave to go over the house, and for half an hour he went from room to room with the minuteness of a man who reads a page letter by letter. He examined the fastenings of the doors, the sashes of the windows, the grates, the skirting-boards. In the back room upon the first floor he halted a long while before the tall press, and then, to my surprise, he dropped upon his knees and laid his ear to the floor. He rose with a curious expression, and beckoned me to the window. Below us lay the little paved yard, and beyond it the blank flank of the bank, rising sheer for thirty feet, its stones grimed with a century of smoke.

“You observe the wall, Stamford. You observe that it is of an older date than the bank itself. It is, in fact, the original boundary wall of the terrace, and the bank has been built up against it. Note the seam of mortar at the level of the first floor. It is newer than the rest—much newer. And now come down.”

In the dining-room he repeated his strange performance, kneeling and listening at the floor, and afterwards at the wall itself, his thin face alight with that cold ardour which I had learned to know. He tapped the panelling with his knuckles, first here, then there, and at a spot behind the bookcase the sound came back to us hollow and resonant.

“Hollow,” said I.
