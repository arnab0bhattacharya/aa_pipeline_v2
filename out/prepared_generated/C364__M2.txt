The porcelain clinked softly in the drawing room of Ashworth Hall, a sound that seemed to echo through the decades as Elara adjusted her hat. It was 1896, and the air smelled of lavender and old wood. Across the table, Margot laughed, a bright, sharp sound that cut through the heavy silence of the country house. "You are too serious, Elara," Margot said, pouring tea with a grace that belied her twenty-two years. Beside her, Clara sat with her hands folded in her lap, her eyes downcast, while Helen stirred her cup with a mechanical precision, her gaze fixed on a spot on the carpet. They were close, these four, bound by the rigid walls of St. Jude’s Academy and the unspoken promise that they would never let the world diminish their bond. But as Elara watched them, she sensed the first hairline fracture in the porcelain of their friendship. Helen’s stirring was a defense; Margot’s laughter was a mask. They were girls pretending to be women, playing at domesticity in a house that was slowly falling apart, much like the empire they all believed would last forever.

Time compressed, folding the intervening years into a blur of correspondence and brief, increasingly awkward visits, until they stood again in the same room, but the atmosphere had shifted from the fragrant safety of youth to the electric tension of 1919. Margot was marrying Julian Vane, a man who smelled of money and indifference. The wedding was held in the churchyard of Ashworth, the oaks towering like silent judges. Elara stood in the front pew, watching Margot walk down the aisle. The bride did not look happy; she looked relieved. Clara stood beside Elara, her face pale, her grip on the bouquet white-knuckled. "She’s making a mistake," Clara whispered, her voice barely audible over the strains of the organ. "Julian is not kind." But Margot did not turn. She had chosen security over truth, or perhaps she had simply run out of time to be anything other than what was expected of her. Helen was not there. She had left a note that morning, stating she could not bear to witness the transaction. The absence was louder than the music. As the vows were exchanged, Elara felt a profound sense of loss, not for the girls they had been, but for the possibility that they had squandered. The tea party had been a promise; the wedding was a surrender.

The next time they gathered, the sky was grey and the air was cold. It was 1924, and they were in a small, unremarkable chapel in Kent. Clara was dead. The cancer had been quick, a thief that stole the quiet strength she had always possessed. Elara stood in the aisle, the only one left from the original four who was not weeping openly. Margot, now Mrs. Vane, stood with her husband, her posture rigid, her face a mask of polite sorrow. Helen, who had finally come back into their lives after years of estrangement, stood near the back, her eyes red-rimmed, her presence a tangible ache in the air.

During the service, as the vicar spoke of peace and rest, Elara looked at Margot. She saw not the vibrant girl from Ashworth, but a woman who had buried her own desires under layers of silk and silence. And then she looked at Helen. Helen met her gaze, and in that brief exchange, Elara saw a terrible recognition. They had all changed, but they had changed in different directions. Margot had become a ghost of duty; Clara had become a martyr of resilience; Helen had become a wanderer of regret. And Elara? Elara was the observer, the one who had stayed, who had watched the pieces fall and refused to pick them up. The funeral was not just an end for Clara; it was an autopsy of their shared history. They were no longer friends. They were survivors of a shared tragedy, bound only by the fact that they had witnessed each other’s lives unravel.

Years passed, and the world outside their small circle continued its violent churn. The war had ended, the peace had arrived, and the interwar period settled in like a damp fog. Then, in 1928, Elara found herself at St. Pancras station, waiting for a train to the coast. The platform was crowded, the noise of departing journeys a roar. She was looking at her ticket, half-heartedly, when a familiar voice called her name.

"Elara."

It was Helen. She stood a few paces away, leaning against a pillar, wearing a coat that had been fashionable three years ago. She looked older, her face lined with the specific weariness of a woman who has lived too long in her own head. Elara moved toward her, the crowd parting around them like water around a stone.

"I didn’t expect to see you here," Elara said, her voice steady.

"No," Helen replied. "I didn’t either."

They stood there, two women in their late thirties, separated by a decade of silence and a death. The tea party at Ashworth seemed like a dream, a time when the future was a blank page. The wedding had been the ink that stained that page, and the funeral had been the fire that burned it. Now, they were just two figures in the grey light of the station, watching the trains come and go.

"Did you ever forgive Margot?" Helen asked, the question hanging in the air, heavy and strange.

Elara thought of Margot, now living in a cold house in Kensington, her laughter replaced by a quiet, brittle silence. "I don’t know if forgiveness is the right word," Elara said. "It’s more like... acceptance. We were children, Helen. We played at being women, but we didn’t know how to be them."

Helen smiled, a sad, fleeting expression. "Clara knew," she said softly. "She was the only one of us who knew what she was. And that’s why she had to die. She was too true for this world."
