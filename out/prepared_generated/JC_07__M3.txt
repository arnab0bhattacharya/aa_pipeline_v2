It is a curious thing, perhaps, to reflect upon the profound silence that can exist between two men when the truth of an action hangs suspended, heavy and unspoken, in the air of a tropical twilight. I relate this here as one who was a witness—not to the mere physical acts, which were ordinary enough, but to the shadow they cast upon a human soul. My information was fragmentary at first, gathered from the engineer of that very coaster and from the half-breeds who loiter on the wharves of Puerto Sombrío; but I have fitted the pieces together, and there is enough of them to make an intelligible picture of the man, Iguara.

He was a Basque, a man of the sea if ever there was one, carrying in his face the weather-beaten gravity of the Bay of Biscay, and in his limbs the stubborn, unyielding strength of those northern mountains. For thirty years he had navigated the fever-ridden, shifting coasts of that turbulent Republic, commanding the *Resolución*—a rusty, paddle-wheeling tramp of a steamer that smelled perpetually of damp sacking and decaying timber. He was a man who had carried every conceivable thing the soil or the greed of that land could yield: bales of rubber, hides reeking of the abattoir, bags of cacao, and, under the official seal of some distant bureaucracy, crates marked with innocent abbreviations. He had never asked what was in a crate. It was a point of honor with him, a blind, fatalistic complicity in the endless, petty revolutions of that country. He was the carrier; the sea and the authorities were the judges.

But this time he knew. The rebel commander, a swarthy, soft-spoken man with the cruel, weary eyes of a disappointed fanatic, had come aboard in the stealth of the night, had laid a heavy, ringless hand on the brass rail of the bridge, and had told him, in a voice of mild, almost sorrowful, compulsion, that the cargo now stowed in the fore-hatch consisted entirely of rifles. The rifles were for the men of the fishing village of San Mateo, a cluster of reed huts on the desolate stretch of the coast, where the breakers roar eternally over a treacherous bar. The commander had added, with a chilling indifference to the moral aspect of the affair, that the government troops were already on the march, and that the rifles were to be landed before dawn.

Iguara had listened, his hands clasped behind his back, his dark eyes fixed upon the shadowy, shapeless coast. He had grunted. What else was there to do? To refuse was to be shot, or perhaps to have his beloved *Resolución* sunk in the harbor. He had accepted the mission with a silence that was less the silence of consent than the silence of a man confronted by an inexorable destiny. He had carried every kind of cargo for thirty years, and he had never asked what was in a crate; but now, as the *Resolución* throbbed and vibrated through her rusty plates, forging her way southward through the heat, he knew, and the knowledge was like a leaden weight in his breast.

The night was close, oppressive, breathing a hot, damp air from the land. The sea was as smooth as a sheet of black glass, reflecting nothing of the starry sky, but glowing faintly, with a phosphorescent, sickly gleam, where the paddles churned up the water. The darkness seemed to press upon the little steamer, to stifle her, to swallow her up in its vast, mysterious silence. Iguara stood on the bridge, motionless, a thick, squat figure against the feeble light of the binnacle. He smoked a cigar, but it tasted of nothing, of ashes and bitterness. He was thinking of the rifles.

He was not a man given to moral refinements. Life had taught him to act, not to reason. But in the solitude of that night, with the monotonous, rhythmic pulsing of the engines in his ears, he felt the whole burden of his complicity. Those rifles were not mere merchandise; they were the seeds of death, of slaughter, of burning. He saw, in the darkness, the little village of San Mateo, a few huts of sun-baked clay and straw, huddled together against the sky; he saw the poor, ignorant fishermen, their swarthy faces lit by the fires they had built on the beach to guide him in; he saw the eager hands that would drag the heavy cases ashore, the clumsy fingers that would unlock the mystery of the cold, oiled steel; and he saw, too, the inevitable consequence—the flash, the smoke, the blood-stained sand, the weeping women, the burning huts. He was bringing them the means to defy the government, yes, but he was also bringing them their doom. He was the blind instrument of a fate he could not control.

The heat increased. The air seemed to thicken, to become a tangible, suffocating veil. The stars were blotted out by a haze that crept up from the horizon, a haze that was not mist, but a kind of luminous, oppressive vapor. The *Resolución* seemed to crawl through a void, a soundless, starless void, in which the only reality was the throb of her engines and the hot breath of the land. Iguara, mopping his face with a huge cotton handkerchief, stared ahead into the darkness, trying to pierce it, to see the coast. But there was nothing to see, only the black, impenetrable wall of the night.

It was past midnight when the dim glow of a fire appeared on the water, a faint, reddish spot, wavering and flickering. Then another, and another. They were the signal fires of San Mateo. Iguara gave the order to slow down. The engines died away to a faint, murmuring throb, and the *Resolución* glided forward, silent and stealthy, like a thief in the night. The air was still. The sea was calm. The only sound was the gentle lapping of the water against the iron sides of the steamer.
