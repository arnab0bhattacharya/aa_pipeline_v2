The memory of that night, when it comes upon me unbidden in the small hours, has less the character of a recollection than of a feverish dream, a thing of shadows and oppressive silences, though the events themselves were of the commonest sort at sea. I was young then, and the *Aline B.*—a barque of some twelve hundred tons, deep-laden with sugar from the Mauritius—was my first ship as second mate, a fact which I felt to be written upon my very countenance, a brand of inexperience visible to every able seaman who shambled past me on the poop. The captain, a man named Tregarthen, was of the old school, a Cornishman with a face like a weathered cliff and eyes that held the grey, unyielding light of the North Atlantic. He spoke seldom, and then in a voice that seemed to carry the echo of distant, colder seas. My own state of mind during those first weeks was one of a strained and silent watchfulness, a perpetual apprehension that some flaw in my seamanship would be discovered and held up to the contempt of the crew, that mixed assemblage of Lascars, Scandinavians, and one or two surly Britons who seemed to regard a young officer as a species of necessary but contemptible vermin.

We were some ten days out from Port Louis, standing to the nor'-west under a sky of brass, when the weather broke. It did not break with the honest fury of a northern gale, but with a sudden, stealthy malice peculiar to those latitudes. The wind, which had been a mere hot breath on our cheeks, died utterly; the sea, which had been heaving in long, sluggish undulations, smoothed itself to a sheet of oiled and darkling glass; and the air became thick, a tangible weight upon the lungs. The silence that fell was more profound, more unnerving, than any tumult. It was a silence that seemed to hold its breath, to listen for the approach of some monstrous and unseen thing. The men, who had been lounging about the deck in the drowsy heat, began to move with a new and furtive alertness, their eyes turning involuntarily to the westward, where a bank of cloud, of a livid, bruised purple, had begun to rise from the horizon with a terrifying rapidity. It was a wall of darkness, advancing with a silent, deliberate purpose that was more dreadful than any charge of screaming wind.

I was on watch, and I felt the prickle of a cold sweat upon my brow. My orders were to call the captain should the weather threaten. But the threat was so sudden, so overwhelming, that I found myself paralysed, not by any conscious decision, but by a kind of mental and physical catalepsy. My hand, I remember, was on the rail, and I felt the grain of the wood with a peculiar and agonising distinctness, as though my entire being had concentrated itself in that single point of contact. The great bank of cloud was swallowing the stars, one by one, and the darkness it cast before it was a palpable thing, a wave of blackness rolling across the face of the deep. I heard the first rustle of the wind in the rigging, a sound like the dry hiss of a serpent, and then the squall struck us.

It came not as a blow, but as a pressure, a vast and invisible hand that laid the ship over until her lee rail was buried in a smother of phosphorescent foam. The world dissolved into a shrieking chaos of wind and water. The masts groaned in their sockets, the canvas cracked like pistol shots, and the sea, no longer a thing of glass, leapt up in a frenzy of white, hissing crests. And in that moment of elemental fury, I stood fast, my mind a perfect blank, my limbs turned to stone. I was not afraid, in the ordinary sense; I was simply annihilated. The officer, the man of responsibility, had been wiped out, and there remained only a young and insignificant creature, pinned to the deck by the sheer, overwhelming violence of the elements.

Then I was aware of a presence beside me, a solid bulk that seemed to have materialised out of the storm itself. It was Tregarthen. He had come up without a word, his heavy sea-boots making no sound on the streaming planks. He did not look at me. His gaze was fixed on the labouring canvas, and his voice, when he spoke, was not a shout but a low, distinct utterance that cut through the howl of the wind with the precision of a knife.

'Clew up the fore-topsail! Hard down your helm there!'

The words acted upon the crew like a galvanic shock. Men who had seemed frozen into inaction sprang into a furious life. The mate, a heavy, red-faced man named Gant, was bellowing orders forward, and the ship, under the steady pressure of the captain's presence, began to answer to her helm, to lift her head from the smothering seas. Tregarthen himself moved to the wheel, his great hands closing over the spokes beside those of the trembling Lascar helmsman, and for the next ten minutes he fought the ship, while I stood by, a useless and superfluous figure, watching the drama as a man might watch a play from the back of a darkened theatre.

When the first fury of the squall had passed, leaving us battered but afloat, with the wind now a steady, sullen roar, the captain gave the deck back to me. He said nothing. He did not even glance in my direction. He simply turned and went below, his oilskins glistening with wet, and I was left with the ship, the night, and the bitter knowledge of my own insufficiency. The rain still lashed down, but the worst was over. Gant came aft, his face streaming, and gave me a look that was both a question and a judgment. I met his eye for a second, then turned away, my cheeks burning. I felt that I had been stripped naked before them all.
