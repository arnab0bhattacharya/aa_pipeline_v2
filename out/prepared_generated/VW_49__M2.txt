The train had left the river behind, and the world, which had been rushing past in a blur of grey mist and green undergrowth, had suddenly stilled into the rigid, painted stillness of the suburban street. Elsie stood on the platform, holding her suitcase, a small, battered thing of leather that felt heavier than it ought to, as if it contained not just her few clothes but the entire weight of the last three years. The air here was different; it was thick with the scent of damp earth and cut grass, a scent that seemed to cling to the skin, to the hair, to the very fabric of the clothes she had worn since the lecture at Oxford. It was the smell of the house. It was the smell of waiting.

She took a cab, not because she could not walk, but because the walking would have been an act of exposure, a slow procession through the eyes of neighbors who would see not Elsie, the student, the woman who had argued with the Dean about the rights of women in the workplace, but only the daughter, the child who had gone away to be educated and now returned, perhaps to marry, perhaps to die, perhaps simply to sit in the garden and watch the ivy climb the walls. The cab driver, a man with a face like a crumpled map, did not speak. He only looked at her in the mirror, and in his eyes she saw a reflection that was not quite her own; it was a reflection of someone older, tired, and profoundly alone.

The house stood at the end of the lane, a large, symmetrical thing of red brick and white stone, looking out over the garden with a benign, unblinking stare. It had not changed. That was the first shock. The paint on the porch was the same shade of peacock blue, faded now to a dusty lavender. The rose bushes, which had been pruned into perfect spheres last spring, were still there, though their shape had softened slightly, become more organic, more like the wild things that grew in the ditch. The window over the front door was still frosted with a pattern of ferns, a pattern that Elsie had loved when she was seven and hated when she was fourteen, and now, at twenty-four, she could not say whether she loved it or not. It was simply there.

She rang the bell. The sound was a sharp, metallic cry in the quiet afternoon. For a moment, nothing happened. Then, the sound of footsteps, hurried and light, and the door opened. Her mother stood there.

"Elise," she said, and the name hung in the air, a word that had not been used in months. It sounded foreign to Elsie, as if it belonged to a different person. Her mother’s face was pale, the lines around her mouth deeper than they had been in the last photograph Elsie had seen. She wore a dress of blue serge, simple and severe, and her hands, hanging at her sides, were still.

"Mum," Elsie said. The word came out thin, stripped of the warmth she had intended to put into it.

"Come in," her mother said. "Come in. Your father is in the study."

They walked into the hall. The floorboards creaked under their feet, a sound Elsie had forgotten. It was a familiar sound, yet it sounded like a warning. The hall was cool, smelling of beeswax and old paper. On the side table, a clock ticked with a steady, indifferent rhythm. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* Each beat seemed to mark the passing of a second that could not be retrieved. Elsie looked at the clock and felt a sudden, irrational panic. The time was 2:15. In London, it was 2:15. But here, in Richmond, the time had a different quality. It was slower, more viscous, like honey.

Her mother moved to the stairs, her steps silent on the carpet. Elsie followed, her suitcase wheel clicking on the stone steps. The room was exactly as she had left it. The same heavy drapes, the same chaise lounge by the window, the same table covered in a cloth of white lace. And there, sitting in the chaise lounge, was her father.

He did not look up. He was reading a newspaper, his glasses perched on the end of his nose, his hands holding the paper with a grip that was both careful and rigid. He was a small man, with a face that seemed to be made of angles and shadows. When he heard Elsie’s voice, he lowered the paper slowly, as if it were a shield.

"So," he said. "You are back."

It was not a question. It was a statement of fact, cold and hard as stone.

"Yes, Dad," Elsie said. "I am back."

She looked at him, waiting for something. A hug? A smile? A question about her term? But he only looked at her, his eyes behind the glasses, clear and unreadable. He saw her, yes. He saw the suitcase. He saw the dust on her shoes. But he did not see the woman who had changed. He saw the girl who had gone away to be made into something useful, and now had returned, perhaps broken, perhaps finished.

"Put your things in your room," he said. "Your mother will bring you a cup of tea."

Elsie looked at the chaise lounge, at the way the light from the window fell across the man’s face, illuminating the dust motes that danced in the air. She felt a surge of anger, hot and bright, but it was quickly swallowed by a deeper, more pervasive sadness. She realized then that there was no going back. The Elsie who had left this house three years ago was dead. She had died in the lecture halls, in the pubs, in the arguments with friends who believed that the world could be changed by words. The Elsie who stood in this room was a stranger to her father, to her mother, to the house itself.
