The sandwich was of thin bread and butter, with a slice of ham that had been folded twice, and it was this folding, the neat economy of it, that occupied her while the clock somewhere above and behind her—she never turned to look at it, it was a fact of the place, like the pigeons—struck the half hour and sent its note out over the stones. One o’clock. The clerks came in a little flood through the gate then, and spread, and settled, each to his chosen slab or bench, unfolding their own paper packets with a crackle that sounded, in the enclosed quiet, like a small fire of twigs. She had her place on the low wall by the plane tree, where the bark came away in great scabs of green and cream, and where the shadow, at this hour, lay exactly along the coping, so that her left hand was cold and her right hand warm, and she ate with the one and held her bag against her knee with the other, and thought—or rather the thought went on in her, without her permission, the way the water goes on in a tap that will not quite close—about Mr. Alderney.

Not Mr. Alderney. Edward. For he had said, only this morning, in the passage by the filing cabinets, where the gas-jet hissed and the smell of the ink was strongest: “Miss Pell, if you’ve nothing better to do on Thursday, there’s a thing at the Lyceum—not the Lyceum—the one in—” and had named a theatre she had never entered, so that the name, spoken, had seemed to open a door in the air and show her, for an instant, a red plush darkness, a lit stage, a great gilded mouth of a proscenium, and herself, small and miraculous, seated in it. Thursday. He had a way of standing with his weight on one foot, so, and of putting his hand to his collar as though the collar were the thing that troubled him and not the asking. She had said she would see. She had said it lightly, with a little laugh that cost her something; and all the morning, over the letters, the invoices, the long columns of figures that must come out right, she had felt the words in her chest like a swallowed stone.

For the question was not the theatre. The question was the other thing, the thing that lay under the theatre the way the vaults lay under these stones, and that no one spoke of. He was a nice young man. He was steady. He had a mother in Streatham and a bicycle and a way of whistling the second bar of things. And if she went on Thursday, then Thursday would be a door, and beyond Thursday another door, and beyond that—she saw it quite clearly, sitting on the warm stone with the ham between her fingers—beyond that a small house with a privet hedge, and washing on a line, and herself grown thick about the waist, and Edward, older, in slippers, reading the paper; and the whole of her life, which was now a thing that stretched and widened and might go anywhere, would be folded up, twice, neatly, like the ham in the bread.

The pigeons came for the crumbs. They came with that forward-and-back motion of the head, that little mechanical bow, as though they were being polite to her and to the stones at once. She threw a piece, and another pigeon, and another, and the flock of them made a grey shimmer, a moving brightness, at her feet; and she thought, They are so sure. They are so sure of the crumbs and the sun and the next moment. A pigeon rose and clattered up past the plane tree and turned, and its breast caught the light and was the colour of the inside of a shell, and was gone over the wall into the roar of the street, which came over the wall too, muffled, continuous, like the sea in a dream. And she thought, I am twenty-two. The thought had no weight in it at all; it was a bright, empty thing, like a bubble; and yet it was everything.

The old men were on the bench against the railing, three of them, with their coats buttoned to the throat though the day was mild, and their hands on their knees, and their faces turned up to the sun with the eyes closed, so that they looked like men who had fallen asleep in the middle of a sentence. What did they think about, she wondered—not now, but then, when they were the age of Edward, when the doors were all still shut and the Thursday still to come? One of them had a paper on his knee, unread; one moved his lips as though he were counting, or praying, or remembering a tune. The stones around them were full of the dead; the grass grew up between the slabs; the inscriptions were worn to a soft grey blur, and here and there a name came through, a “Thomas,” a “Ann,” with the dates gone and the text gone, and only the name left, like a hand held up out of the water. She had never thought of the dead before, not really; but now it seemed to her that they were not far off at all, that they were the crowd in the next room, listening, waiting for their turn to be spoken to; and that they had all, every one of them, stood once at the foot of a stair or in a passage by a filing cabinet, and heard a young man stumble through an invitation, and had said, lightly, that they would see.
