It was upon a raw, foggy afternoon in November of '94 that the door of our consulting-room was thrown open and a lady was announced. She swept in with an air of refinement and obvious agitation, her dark dress of severe cut marking her as a professional woman of standing.

"You are Mr. Martin Hewitt?" she asked, glancing from my friend to me.

"I am," said Hewitt, rising and placing a chair for her. "This is my friend and colleague, Mr. Brett. Pray be seated, madam, and tell us how we may serve you."

Our visitor was a woman of middle age, handsome, and possessed of that quiet dignity which comes of long dealing with high society. Her hands were well-shaped but bore the faint ink-stains of a desk-worker.

"My name is Mrs. Venable," she said. "I keep the dressmaking establishment on the corner of Regent Street and Conduit Street. You may have heard of it."

Hewitt nodded. "I have seen your work, madam. It is of the highest order."

"Which makes my present situation all the more intolerable," she continued. "I have built up a clientele among the very first families. Lady Marsham, the Countess of Dalmeny, Mrs. Alderman Phipps—these are my patrons. And now my business, my good name, everything is threatened."

She paused and pressed a gloved hand to her brow.

"Tell us the facts from the beginning," said Hewitt, in his soothing way.

"It began six weeks ago. I discovered that my private measurement-books had been tampered with. These books contain not only the figures for each lady's dress—bust, waist, length of sleeve, train, and so forth—but also the detailed specifications for each gown: the fabric, the cut, and most importantly, any special fittings such as concealed pockets, inner linings, or hooks for the attachment of jewels. You see, Mr. Hewitt, many of my clients wear their most valuable ornaments fastened directly into the gown itself. It is my task to provide the means."

"An unusual but practical arrangement," said Hewitt.

"It is the fashion. Well, six weeks ago I found that the lock on my office desk had been forced. Nothing was taken—no money, no bills—only the measurement-books had been disturbed. I thought little of it at the time, perhaps an oversight on my part. But then the first robbery occurred."

"Where?"

"At the Hunt Ball at Hanover Square. Lady Marsham had worn a gown of white satin with a stomacher of diamonds. The stomacher was fastened by means of three small gold hooks sewn into the bodice—hooks which I had placed there myself according to my own design. During the course of the evening the diamonds vanished. Lady Marsham was distraught. She was convinced the stones had been pulled from her person in the crush of the ballroom."

"And the hooks themselves?" asked Hewitt.

"I examined the gown afterwards. The hooks were still in place, but the small loops of thread which secured the stones had been cut. Not torn, Mr. Hewitt. Cut. With a blade so fine that the damage was invisible until one looked closely."

Hewitt's eyes gleamed. "And the second robbery?"

"A fortnight later. The Countess of Dalmeny wore one of my creations to the Foreign Office reception. A gown of emerald velvet with a diamond spray arranged upon the shoulder. The same method. The stones were removed during a dance, cut cleanly away from their mountings. The Countess had felt nothing."

"And since then?"

"Three more robberies, all following the same pattern. All at private balls, all involving jewels fastened to gowns made in my establishment. In every case the thief knew exactly where to find the stones. There was no fumbling, no clumsy tearing. It was as though he had the plans of the gown before him."

"Or the measurements," said Hewitt.

"Precisely," said Mrs. Venable. "I am now in terror that my clients will connect these robberies and conclude that my workroom is the source of the information. One whisper of such a thing and my business is ruined. I have come to you because the police would only make the matter public."

"You did wisely," said Hewitt. "Now, tell me about your establishment. How many persons work there?"

"Fourteen in all. Twelve seamstresses, my head fitter Miss Craddock, and a boy, Jimmy, who runs errands and sweeps up."

"Who has access to your office and the measurement-books?"

"Only myself and Miss Craddock. The books are kept in a locked desk in my private office."

"Then we must look at those who can reach the desk. Tell me about Miss Craddock."

"She has been with me nine years. She is honest, sober, and devoted to the work. I trust her as I trust myself."

"In my experience, madam," said Hewitt, with a thin smile, "that is precisely the degree of trust which most often invites betrayal. However, we shall see. Who else enters the office?"

"The delivery men, of course. And there is Mr. Bellamy, my ledger-clerk, who comes twice a week to balance the accounts. He is a quiet, middle-aged man, very methodical."

"Does he have access to the desk?"

"The desk must be unlocked for him. I or Miss Craddock usually do so."

Hewitt was silent for a moment, his fingers drumming upon the arm of his chair. "One more question, Mrs. Venable. These five robberies—at how many different balls did they occur?"

"Three balls. At two of them, two of my clients were robbed."

"And at each ball, were there other ladies wearing gowns not made by your establishment?"

"Of course. The majority."

"Yet only your clients were targeted. That is very suggestive. Now, madam, I shall need to see your office, your measurement-books, and your staff. Can you receive us tomorrow at ten?"

Mrs. Venable agreed and departed, leaving Hewitt to his reflections.

"Well, Brett," said he, when the door had closed, "what do you make of it?"

"The thief has access to the books," I said.
