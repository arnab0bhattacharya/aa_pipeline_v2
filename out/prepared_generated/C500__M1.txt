The rain fell in a relentless, grey sheet, turning the gaslight on the station platform outside into a series of hazy, golden blossoms. From his chair in the private parlour of The Albion Hotel, Detective Inspector Silas Croft watched the water run down the windowpanes, each rivulet tracing a path through the grime. It was a night for quiet contemplation, or perhaps a good book, but the London, Brighton & South Coast Railway was rarely so accommodating. The rhythmic clatter of an arriving train was a constant reminder of the endless parade of souls passing through, each with a story, most of them mundane. A sharp, hesitant knock at the door broke his reverie.

“Enter,” Croft called, his voice a low rumble.

The door opened to reveal a young porter, his brass-buttoned uniform still damp from a recent dash across the forecourt. He clutched a single, rather large leather valise to his chest as if it were a live, and possibly malevolent, creature. “Beg pardon for the intrusion, Inspector Croft. Mr. Finch from the front desk, he said you ought to have a look at this.”

Croft gestured for the porter to place the bag on the low table before the fire. “What is it, Thomas? A forgotten trunk from a lord on his way to his country estate?”

The young man, Thomas, shook his head, his eyes wide. “No, sir. It’s from the gentleman in room 212. A Mr. Abernathy. He checked out in a terrible hurry this morning. Paid his bill in coin, didn’t want any change, and practically ran for the first train to Brighton. Said he’d send for his luggage. Well, he didn’t, and the room is let again for tonight. Mr. Finch said it’s your business now, on account of the… well, on account of the smell of gunpowder.”

Silas Croft’s interest, which had been a languid thing, sharpened to a point. He leaned forward, his gaze fixed on the valise. It was of good quality, but scuffed at the corners, the leather creased and tired. “Gunpowder, you say? And a hasty departure. Thank you, Thomas. That will be all.”

The porter, clearly relieved to be rid of the burden, nodded and scurried from the room, closing the door softly behind him. Croft waited a moment, listening to the man’s footsteps fade down the carpeted corridor. Then, with a deliberation that belied his eagerness, he unfastened the two brass clasps of the valise. They snapped open with a sound like tiny pistol shots in the quiet room.

The first thing he saw was a pair of lady’s gloves. They were of fine kidskin, a delicate shade of dove grey, and lay folded neatly on top of the other contents. He lifted one. It was soft and supple, the fingers slightly curled as if still encasing a hand. There was no scent of perfume, only the faint, clean smell of the leather itself. They were an oddity, a soft and feminine thing in a man’s luggage. Croft set them aside.

Beneath the gloves lay the source of the porter’s concern. It was a Webley revolver, its dark, blued steel glinting in the firelight. It was a serviceable weapon, not an ornate gentleman’s piece. Croft picked it up, the familiar weight settling into his palm. He was no stranger to firearms, and he immediately checked the cylinder. Five chambers were loaded with brass cartridges. The sixth was empty, its interior stained with the black residue of burnt powder. A faint, acrid scent, the ghost of a discharge, still clung to the metal. The weapon was clean, well-maintained. It had not been fired in a panic. It had been fired with purpose.

He placed the revolver on the table and turned his attention to the rest of the bag’s contents. There was a change of linen, a toothbrush, a shaving kit, and a leather-bound copy of Tennyson’s poems, its pages well-thumbed. Everything was in order, the possessions of a man who travelled with a quiet sense of routine. Except for one last item, tucked into a side pocket.

It was a railway timetable, the kind sold for a penny at any station kiosk. Croft unfolded the thin paper. It was a timetable for the very line whose trains he could hear rumbling outside. His eyes scanned the columns of destinations and departure times. Most were unmarked, but one line was circled twice with a sharp, dark pencil. The ink had bitten into the paper. It was the 9:15 p.m. service to Portsmouth Harbour, departing from Victoria Station.

The pieces began to arrange themselves in Silas Croft’s mind, forming a grim and incomplete mosaic. A man, ‘Mr. Abernathy’, had left in a rush. A lady’s gloves were in his possession. A revolver had been fired. And he was meant to be on a train to Portsmouth, a port town, a place of departure, tonight. The timetable was not a record of a journey taken, but of one intended. A plan.

Croft walked to the bell-pull and gave it a sharp tug. He returned to the table and picked up the valise again, searching the lining more thoroughly this time. His fingers found a small, hard lump near the base. He worked at the seam with his penknife until he had opened a tiny, concealed pocket. Inside was a single, folded piece of hotel stationery. He smoothed it out.

The address was written in a bold, confident script: Mr. A. Abernathy, 14 Red Lion Square, London. It was a respectable address, a place for a solicitor or a man of business. But a flicker of memory, a note from a constable’s report he had read that morning, snagged in his mind. A missing person. A young woman, the daughter of a wealthy industrialist, who had vanished from her home in Kensington two days prior. The address of the Kensington house was 14 Red Lion Square. The same number, the same name for the square. A clever, arrogant bluff. A false address, chosen to be plausible, yet one that would lead a casual investigator on a wild goose chase, for the real Red Lion Square was nowhere near Kensington.
