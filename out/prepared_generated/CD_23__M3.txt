If any one street in the whole of London could be said to have been set apart for the express purpose of receiving the sighs, the whispers, and the occasional sneezes of the aristocracy, it was undoubtedly Queen’s Crescent Mews in the fashionable district of Mayfair. Here, the hansoms rolled by with a lulling, rhythmic crunch, as if the very gravel were performing a minuet, and the very air seemed to smell of expensive powder, lavender water, and the quiet, unspoken assumption that nobody in the immediate vicinity had ever done a hard day’s work in their lives.

It was at Number Seven, between a shop that sold only the most supercilious French gloves and another that dealt exclusively in fans of a decidedly haughty disposition, that Monsieur Aristide Pouponnet conducted his flourishing hairdressing establishment. Monsieur Pouponnet was a man who had risen in the world entirely by the irresistible force of his own flattery. He was a small, plump, beautifully pomaded man, who bowed with a mechanical elegance that would have put the most accomplished automaton to shame. He possessed a looking-glass which reflected not merely the face of the customer in the chair, but the exact degree of that customer’s vanity; and he could dress a head of hair—be it a Duke’s or a Baronet’s—with a tenderness that was almost maternal.

“Ah, Milor’,” Monsieur Pouponnet would say, sweeping a fine Indian shell comb through the perfumed locks of his noble client, “it is of no use for the republicans of Paris to talk of equality! They may cry ‘Liberty’ until they are quite hoarse in the throat, but where is the hair? Where, I ask you, is the elevation? You cannot build a nation upon a flat coiffure, Milor’. It is in the head itself that the true nobility resides!”

Monsieur Pouponnet always pronounced ‘nobility’ with a superfluous and lingering ‘e’, as if the extra letter were a tiny jewel he was inserting into the crown of the English language. His customers loved him. They tipped him handsomely. They allowed him to heat his irons and to crimp their noble brows, and they went out into the world feeling that if the French were cutting off heads across the channel, they were, at the very least, preserving their own heads in the most exquisite of curls.

But in the year of our Lord 1792, when the spring was reluctantly yielding to a damp and rather theatrical summer, a shadow fell across the looking-glasses of Number Seven. It was not the shadow of a passing cloud, nor the shadow of Monsieur Pouponnet’s own highly developed shadow, but a shadow of a distinctly Gallic and revolutionary complexion.

It began with the newspapers. The Morning Post and the Daily Advertiser were suddenly full of the most alarming reports from France. There were disturbances in the provinces; there were riots in towns whose names the English aristocracy could not pronounce; and at the very head of one such disturbance, in the obscure but fiery little town of Crêpe-sur-Marne, was a name that made Monsieur Pouponnet’s scissors hesitate in mid-air.

The name was Marat. Not the famous Marat, mind you, who was busy with his own inflammatory journalism in Paris, but a provincial Marat—Citizen Marat, the leader of the Crêpe-sur-Marne Fraternal Club of the Unshorn. And this Citizen Marat, the papers noted in passing, was by birth a Pouponnet. He was, in fact, Monsieur Aristide Pouponnet’s own elder brother, Gaspard.

Monsieur Pouponnet read this item in the paper while sipping his morning chocolate, and he choked so violently upon a small piece of sugar that he nearly dislocated his own cravat. Gaspard! Gaspard, who had always been so dull, so heavy, so entirely incapable of dressing even the simplest queue! Gaspard, who had refused to learn the art of the curling-tongs, preferring instead to read pestilential pamphlets and argue with the local cheese-mongers about the rights of man! Gaspard, who had remained in Crêpe-sur-Marne while Aristide had come to London to make his fortune among the great! And now Gaspard was a leader of men, a orator, a revolutionary, and Aristide was standing in his shop in Mayfair, holding a pair of scissors and looking, for the first time in his life, distinctly pale.

The effect upon his customers was immediate and profound. The English aristocracy, which is always ready to suspect a foreigner of the most bloodthirsty intentions, began to look at Monsieur Pouponnet with a new and searching expression. The Duchess of Ditherington, who came in every Tuesday for a modest dressing, sat in the chair and fixed him with a stare that was perfectly terrible in its quiet intensity.

“You—you have family in France, Monsieur Pouponnet?” said the Duchess, in a voice that suggested she was inquiring whether he had a pet crocodile concealed about his person.

“Ah, Madame la Duchesse,” said Monsieur Pouponnet, executing a bow that was, perhaps, a trifle too elaborate to be entirely natural, “I have a brother in France, it is true. But he is—he is—”

He searched for a word that would adequately describe Gaspard without revealing the terrible truth. He was a man, he was a Frenchman, he was a Pouponnet; but above all, he was a fool.

“He is a farmer, Madame,” said Monsieur Pouponnet, with a desperate and brilliant mendacity.

“A farmer,” said the Duchess, coldly. “Indeed. And does he farm the land, Monsieur Pouponnet, or does he farm the mob?”

Monsieur Pouponnet’s scissors gave a convulsive snip at the empty air, as if they were trying to cut off the Duchess’s question before it could do any further damage. “He farms the land, Madame! Nothing but the land! Wheat, Madame! Barley, Madame! Perhaps a few chickens! He is entirely devoted to his chickens!”

The Duchess said nothing further, but she left the shop with an air of having been deeply and irrevocably offended, and she did not return for three weeks. When she did return, it was only to ask, with a terrible politeness, whether Monsieur Pouponnet had received any news of his “chickens.”
