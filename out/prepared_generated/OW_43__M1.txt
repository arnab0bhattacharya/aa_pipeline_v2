It was, of course, the most ridiculous hour of the afternoon, that hour when conversation has exhausted its vitality and only the slow, hypnotic drip of tea and scandal can revive the drawing-room. Lady Beatrice Ravensworth, who had long ago concluded that philanthropy was merely a vice that had been blessed by the clergy, was regarding her husband’s latest act of charity with the weary disdain you would reserve for a bad habit.

“To give away half one’s fortune,” she murmured to the Vicar of St. Jude’s, who was busily consuming a macaroon, “is not virtue, my dear Vicar. It is a species of indigestion. One consumes too much wealth and is forced to vomit it upon the deserving poor.”

The Vicar choked slightly, but recovered. “Lady Beatrice, your wit is, as ever, sharper than a sermon.”

“It is sharper because it is truer,” she replied, arranging the silk of her gown, a fabric of such exquisite violet that it seemed to weep for the lost money. “My husband, Sir Alistair, has just endowed a home for indigent spinsters. He calls it the ‘Ravensworth Refuge for Unwanted Hearts.’ I call it a monument to his own vanity. He loves humanity, which is a dreadful thing to do, for humanity is entirely unlovable.”

At that moment, the heavy velvet curtains parted and Sir Alistair entered. He looked, Beatrice thought, like a man who had been spending too much time with the moral law and not enough with his tailor. He was pale, his eyes had the slightly feverish gleam of the enthusiast, and he was clutching a copy of *The Morning Beacon* as if it were a snake.

“Beatrice,” he said, his voice trembling with a righteous indignation that was quite new to him. “Have you seen this?”

“Seen what, my dear? I have seen the tea, and I have seen the Vicar eat three macaroons. Anything further is a revelation.”

Alistair thrust the paper forward. The headline screamed in black ink, a vulgar shout in the hushed room: **THE PURCHASED SAINT: HOW SIR A. RAVENSWORTH BUYS HIS REPUTATION.**

Beatrice read it. It was written by a journalist named Craddock, a man she vaguely remembered as having a face like a dissipated ferret and opinions like a sewer. He accused Alistair of not giving from love, but from a calculated desire to be named in the papers, to secure a baronetcy, to buy the silence of the poor so they would not rise against the rich.

“It is infamous!” cried Alistair. “I gave that money to the Spinsters’ Home because—because the heart demands it! Because I looked into the eyes of poverty and saw my own reflection!”

“You looked into the eyes of poverty,” Beatrice said coolly, “and saw an opportunity to be ostentatious. That is why I have always found your charity tiresome, Alistair. It is so theatrical. If you must be good, at least be dull about it.”

But as she spoke, something strange happened. She looked at her husband’s face, usually so composed, now contorted by the injustice of the smear. She saw the genuine hurt there, the hurt of a man who believes he has done a beautiful thing and has been told it is ugly. And then she looked at the paper again.

The article mocked the ‘Ravensworth Refuge for Unwanted Hearts.’ It called the name sentimental, the architecture garish, and the founder a hypocrite.

A strange fire, cold and hard like a diamond, ignited in Beatrice’s breast. She had always thought the Refuge a folly, a vanity project. But she found she could not bear that *this* man, this crude Mr. Craddock with his ink-stained fingers and his cynical soul, should be the one to judge it. To dislike one’s husband is a domestic privilege; to have him insulted by a tradesman is a public outrage.

She stood up. She seemed to grow taller, her violet gown rustling like armor.

“Alistair,” she said, her voice losing its drawl and acquiring a tone of steel. “You will go and change your coat. You look like a martyr, and it does not suit your complexion.”

“What are you going to do?” asked the Vicar, dropping his fourth macaroon.

“I am going to call upon Mr. Craddock,” Beatrice said. “I am going to *The Morning Beacon*.”

“But, my dear,” Alistair stammered. “It is not done. A lady does not visit a newspaper office. The smell of ink... the lack of chaperones...”

“My dear Alistair,” she said, sweeping past him, “I am not going to defend your virtue. I am going to attack his bad taste. It is the only crime for which there is no forgiveness.”

An hour later, Lady Beatrice Ravensworth swept into the chaotic, dusty sanctum of *The Morning Beacon*. It smelled of glue, stale tobacco, and the frantic desperation of men who write for a living. Mr. Craddock was sitting at a desk piled high with papers, a cigarette hanging from his lip. He looked up and saw a vision of silk and fury descending upon him.

“Mr. Craddock,” Beatrice said, not waiting to be announced. “I have read your article. It is the most grammatically sound piece of filth I have encountered this season.”

Craddock grinned. He had the hide of a rhinoceros. “Lady Ravensworth! I am honored. Do sit on that pile of rejected manuscripts. They are softer than the truth.”

“I prefer to stand,” she said, looking down at him as if he were a stain on the carpet. “You have accused my husband of buying his reputation. You have suggested that his charity is a sham, a currency exchanged for social applause.”

“I have suggested,” Craddock said, blowing a smoke ring, “that rich men do nothing for nothing. It is the first law of economics.”

“And you, Mr. Craddock? You write this filth. Why? For money, I suppose. Or for the pleasure of dragging a good man down to your own level. You speak of purchased reputations. You, sir, have a purchased soul. You have sold your capacity for wonder, your ability to believe in anything nobler than a bank balance, to the highest bidder, who happens to be your own spite.”
