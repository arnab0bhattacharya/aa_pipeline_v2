It is a well-known fact that a man may be poor, but he must never be shabby; for poverty is merely a lack of money, whereas shabbiness is a lack of soul, and the latter is far more fatal to the spirit. It was with this philosophy that Parnell O’Malley, a poet of considerable ambition and negligible resources, alighted in London. He possessed one coat, which was of a dark green velvet that had once been fashionable and was now merely old-fashioned, and a letter of introduction to the Duchess of St. Ives. The letter had been written by his uncle, a man who believed that flattery was the only currency that did not depreciate, and had signed it with a flourish that suggested he expected the Duchess to open her doors and her purse with equal eagerness.

The Duchess, however, was a woman of such precise and polished emptiness that she had forgotten not only Parnell’s family but also the concept of family itself. When Parnell presented himself at her gate, wearing his only good coat and a hat that sat slightly askew, he did not ask for her aid. To ask is to admit need, and need is the vulgar cousin of want. Instead, he told the butler that he had come to return a volume of poetry that he had lent to the Duchess, a volume that did not exist, but which he described with such vivid and erroneous detail that the butler, a man of limited imagination but vast deference, assumed it must be a work of profound and perhaps dangerous obscurity.

When the Duchess received him in her drawing room, a space so immaculately scrubbed that one could hear the echo of one’s own conscience, she did not recognize him. This was a blessing. Recognition implies memory, and memory is the jailer of the past. Parnell introduced himself not as her kinsman, but as a critic who had been commissioned by the *Times* to write an obituary for a style of dress that had died in 1880. He spoke of the moral decay of the corset and the spiritual bankruptcy of the bustle with such icy severity that the Duchess, who had spent the morning worrying over the width of her waist, felt a sudden and profound respect for his intellect.

Within a week, Parnell was invited to every dinner in Mayfair. He became the favorite of the season, not because he was wealthy, for he was not, but because he was dangerous. He possessed the rare gift of saying the most outrageous things with the most perfect composure. At a dinner hosted by Lord Avenell, a man who collected butterflies and opinions, Parnell remarked that the only thing more tragic than a butterfly trapped in a glass case was a man trapped in a society that mistook manners for morality. The room fell silent. Lord Avenell, who had never been contradicted, smiled with the satisfaction of a man who has just discovered a new species. He invited Parnell to his estate the following week.

Yet, as is often the case with success, it brought with it a single, glaring absence. Parnell was everywhere, except at the house of the Marquess of Galloway. The Marquess was a man of immense wealth and considerable dullness, who believed that art was a form of decoration, no different from a floral arrangement or a piece of furniture. He had heard of Parnell, of course, for in London, silence is the only thing that is truly expensive, and Parnell’s silence was too loud to ignore. But the Marquess had decided, with the infallibility of the mediocre, that Parnell was a fraud. He reasoned that no true artist could be so charming, for charm is the mask that the mediocre wear to hide their lack of substance.

Parnell, who had heard nothing of this reasoning but felt the weight of it in the air, like the scent of ozone before a storm, decided to seek an audience. He knew that to enter the Marquess’s house, he would need more than a coat; he would need a paradox. He wrote a letter to the Marquess, enclosing a small, unmarked box. The letter was brief: “You will find in this box a rose that blooms only in the dark. It is the only flower that tells the truth. I await your judgment.”

The Marquess, who had never received a letter that did not ask for money, was intrigued. He opened the box. Inside lay a single, black glass rose, its petals cut with such precision that they caught the light like a spider’s web. On the base of the stem was engraved a single line of verse: *Beauty is but a mask that lies.*

The Marquess, who had spent his life collecting things that were beautiful but false, was horrified. He invited Parnell to his home, not to praise him, but to confront him. It was the only invitation Parnell had refused for a month, and now, having accepted it, he found himself standing in the Marquess’s library, a room filled with books that had never been read and mirrors that reflected nothing but vanity.

The Marquess was a tall man, with the face of a marble statue that had been left out in the rain. He looked at Parnell with the cold assessment of a jeweler examining a diamond for flaws.

“You are a clever man, Mr. O’Malley,” the Marquess said. “But cleverness is a cheap commodity. It is found in street-corner magicians and cheap parlors. What is your substance?”

Parnell smiled. It was a smile that did not reach his eyes, for his eyes were busy observing the dust motes dancing in the shafts of sunlight. “Substance, my lord, is merely the shadow of the soul. And the soul is a bird that cannot be caged. I have no substance, for I am all spirit. And spirit, unlike matter, cannot be weighed, but only measured by the depth of the silence it creates.”
