The thing was a curiosity, a botanical absurdity, and it arrived from the Amazon in a Wardian case that wept condensation onto the hall tiles of my house in Hampstead. It was the gift of my former pupil, Jack Ellery, who had returned from his travels not merely browned and bearded, but positively crackling with a new, feverish energy. He had brought it to me, he said, because I was the only man in England who would not laugh at it.

“Look at the leaves, Pemberton!” he cried, stamping the mud of the tropics from his boots onto my Persian rug. “*Nepenthes*? Bah! This is something else entirely. The natives call it the ‘Soul-Strangler.’ It doesn’t just catch a fly; it hunts. Watch.”

I watched. The plant, which stood some three feet high on a central stalk, possessed leaves of a deep, leathery green, fringed with stiff, reddish bristles. As Jack waved a handkerchief near a particularly large specimen, the leaf did not merely close; it snapped shut with a sound like a dry twig breaking. It was a violent, predatory action, utterly alien to the vegetable kingdom as I understood it.

“It is a remarkable specimen, Jack,” I said, with the cautious enthusiasm of a man who has spent forty years cataloging ferns. “A carnivorous habit, clearly. But the kinetic energy—”

“It’s not the energy, it’s the appetite,” he interrupted. “You have to feed it. Properly. Not just sugar water. It wants meat. Fresh meat.”

He left me the plant with a bottle of some foul-smelling, dark liquid he called ‘nutrient solution,’ and instructions to keep the soil damp and the room humid. For the first month, the thing was a source of immense academic pleasure. I placed it in my study, the morning sun striking its leaves, and I spent hours sketching the venation and the structure of the trap. It was a masterpiece of natural selection, a testament to the ruthless efficiency of the Creator’s design. I fed it flies, then beetles, then, on a whim of scientific inquiry, a large bluebottle which it dispatched with terrifying speed.

My housekeeper, Mrs. Pringle, was less enamoured. She was a woman of solid, provincial stock, and she regarded the plant with the deep, instinctive suspicion usually reserved for foreign religions and unswept chimneys.

“It gives me the shivers, Dr. Pemberton,” she announced one morning, standing in the doorway with the dustpan, eyeing the plant as if it might leap at her throat. “It’s the way it looks at you. Plants don’t have eyes, but that one does. And it’s getting fat.”

“Nonsense, Mrs. Pringle,” I said, adjusting my spectacles. “It is merely turgid. The cellular structure is engorged with fluid. It is a healthy specimen.”

“It’s engorged with something,” she muttered, and swept out.

But as the weeks turned into the grey damp of an English autumn, I began to notice changes that my scientific training struggled to rationalize. The plant grew with an alarming rapidity. The stalk thickened until it was as thick as my wrist, and the leaves, once a deep green, took on a flush of dark, arterial red. More disturbingly, its appetites escalated.

The flies were no longer sufficient. One morning, I found it had snapped up a bluebottle and was still working on it, the leaf pulsing rhythmically, like a chewing jaw. I found I had to feed it twice, then three times a day. I began to raid the larder for scraps—bits of bacon rind, a chicken bone—and the plant devoured them with a wet, sucking sound that made the hair on my neck prickle.

I began to feel a strange, proprietary devotion to the thing. It was my burden, my discovery. I spoke to it while I worked, explaining the theories of Darwin and Lamarck, and I fancied that the leaves turned towards me when I entered the room, not with the phototropism of a plant, but with the eager recognition of a dog.

Then came the incident with the sparrow. It was a frosty morning, and a dazed sparrow had flown into the study window and fluttered on the rug. Before I could stoop to catch it, the plant moved. It was not a snap this time; it was a lunge. The stalk bent with a groan of wood, and a leaf enveloped the bird. There was a frantic, muffled fluttering, a squeak that was abruptly cut off, and then silence.

I stood paralyzed. I should have been horrified. I should have chopped the thing down and burned it. Instead, I felt a thrill of scientific triumph. The predation was active! It was not a passive trap; it was a hunter.

I did not tell Mrs. Pringle. But she knew. She stopped cleaning the study, leaving the dust to gather in grey drifts on the floorboards. She began leaving my meals on a tray outside the door.

“The smell, sir,” she told me one afternoon, her face pale and set. “It smells like a butcher’s yard in there. And I heard it. Last night. It was making noises. Not rustling. *Breathing*.”

“Plants respire, Mrs. Pringle,” I said sharply, though my own heart was hammering against my ribs. “It is a gas exchange.”

“It’s not gas,” she whispered. “It’s hungry.”

That night, the temperature dropped below freezing. I banked the fire high in the study to keep the tropical beast warm. I sat by the hearth, a glass of sherry in my hand, watching it. The room was dim, lit only by the flickering coals. The plant loomed in the shadows, a grotesque silhouette of stems and fronds. It had grown so large now that it occupied the entire bay of the window. The nutrient solution Jack had left was long gone, and I had been substituting a mixture of raw beef and blood, which I prepared in the kitchen when Mrs. Pringle was out.

I must have dozed off. I was woken by a sensation of cold. The fire had died down to embers. The room was silent, save for a faint, rhythmic sound. *Hhh-hhh. Hhh-hhh.*
