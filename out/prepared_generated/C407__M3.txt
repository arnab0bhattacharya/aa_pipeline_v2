The rain had a way of finding the seams in a man’s coat, and Silas Vane’s coat had many. He stood beneath the awning of Kroeger’s Tobacconist on Litchfield Street, watching the gutters of Soho carry the evening’s filth toward the Thames. He was sixty-three years old, possessed of a pension of two pounds and ten shillings a month, remitted in brown envelopes from an office in Whitehall that did not advertise its business on its doorplate. The pension was the price of thirty years of listening through pub walls and remembering faces and writing down names in a small, careful hand. It was not a fortune, but it kept him in a single room above the chandler’s shop on Wardour Street, and it kept him in gin, and it kept him, most importantly, alive—which was more than some of the men he had named could say.

He had been retired for four years now. Four years of walking the pavements of a city that had forgotten him, which was exactly the condition he had bargained for. He was a thin man, thinner now than when he had worked, with a long, deeply lined face and the kind of restless, watery eyes that never settled on any one thing for long. He wore his hat low. He kept his collar up. He had grown the beard late in life, partly for warmth and partly because it changed the shape of his jaw, and a changed jaw was, in his former trade, a kind of armour.

The gas lamps along the street had just been lit, and their light turned the rain to a faint yellow haze. He was about to step out from beneath the awning and make his way home through the back routes he always took—the alleys and cut-throughs that no cab driver bothered with—when he heard his name.

“Mr. Vane?”

The voice was deep, unhurried, and warm with something that sounded, absurdly, like pleasure. Silas felt the sound of it enter his chest before his mind had finished identifying the tone. He turned.

The man standing before him was broad and tall, dressed in a dark suit that was new but not expensive, the kind of suit a man buys upon regaining his liberty because he has been wearing the same clothes for a very long time and wishes to feel like a person again. His face was heavy-jawed and clean-shaven, and his eyes were dark and very steady. He held a black umbrella, tilted slightly so that the rain ran off it away from both of them, and he was smiling.

Silas searched the face. He had a memory for faces that was near to infallible, a faculty that had been his primary asset and his chief sin, and within three seconds he had the man. The jaw was heavier now, the cheeks fuller, the hairline retreated, but the eyes were the same. They had stood across from one another in a parlour off Berwick Street, in the summer of 1882, and the man had been frightened then, and Silas had been kind to him, gently attentive, and the man had talked, and Silas had written it all down, and the man had gone to Pentonville for nine years.

“Cormac,” Silas said. He did not say the surname. He did not need to.

“Cormac Hoy,” the man said, and extended his hand.

Silas looked at the hand. It was a large hand, pink and clean, the nails trimmed. It was not the hand of a man who wished another man harm. But Silas had spent his life reading hands, reading the set of shoulders, reading the angle at which a man held his body, and he could read nothing in this one that made sense. The hand waited, open and patient.

Silas took it. The grip was firm but not punishing. Hoy pumped his arm twice, as though they were old friends who had happened upon one another after a long and blameless separation.

“I thought it was you,” Hoy said. “I wasn’t certain at first. The beard threw me. But the walk—I’d know your walk anywhere. You have a way of moving, Mr. Vane, like a man who’s late for something but won’t admit it.”

Silas said nothing. He was calculating. Nine years in Pentonville would have ended in 1891, which was three years ago. The man had been out for three years and had not, apparently, spent those years searching for Silas Vane with a blade in his pocket. Or perhaps he had, and the finding had taken three years, and this was the moment before the blade. But the eyes held no violence. They held something else, something Silas could not name and did not trust, because he had never encountered it in this particular context and had no framework for it.

“You’re looking well,” Silas said, because it was the emptiest thing he could say and he needed to say something while his mind worked.

“I’m looking fed,” Hoy said, and laughed. “Which is not the same thing, but it’ll do. I’ve been running a kitchen. A chop house on Greek Street. The Bellamy. Perhaps you know it.”

Silas did not know it. He had narrowed his movements in retirement to a small, familiar circuit, and Greek Street was outside it. He said he did not.

“You should come,” Hoy said. “I mean it. Come tonight. I’ll cook for you myself. I still cook, you know. It was the one thing inside that was worth doing. They let me work the kitchens, and I learned more in there than I ever learned out here, which is a thing I say to myself sometimes and find both funny and sad.” He paused and looked at Silas with an expression that was open and curious and entirely without the thing Silas was looking for. “Have you eaten?”

“I was going home.”

“Home can wait. Come and eat. I’m asking you as a guest.”
