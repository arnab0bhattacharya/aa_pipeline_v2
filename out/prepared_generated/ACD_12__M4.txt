The club was called the Athenaeum Chess Rooms, though it had neither the scale nor the dignity of the great institution whose name it borrowed. It occupied the first floor of a narrow house in a court off the Strand, and consisted of three rooms—a smoking-room, a tournament hall, and a small committee chamber—furnished with a shabby, comfortable respectability that suited its members well enough. There were forty-two of them, mostly professional men of moderate means, and they played their games with a seriousness that bordered upon the sacerdotal.

It was to this establishment, upon a wet November evening, that I was summoned by a note from the secretary, Mr. Lionel Hartree, whose acquaintance I had made some years before in connexion with a trifling matter of a forged subscription list. The note was brief and somewhat agitated in its phrasing, and it requested that I should attend the following Thursday without ceremony and observe the play of a member named Corvin—one Edgar Corvin, a solicitor's clerk of no particular distinction, who had, according to the secretary, won every game he had contested for a period of five weeks, and that against opponents of considerable strength, by moves which no one could have foreseen.

"His play is not merely good," Hartree had written. "It is uncanny. He sacrifices material upon principles that appear to have no foundation in theory, and yet the combinations mature with a precision that is almost mechanical. I confess that I am at a loss. There is talk among the members of something not quite right, though no one can say what. And now poor Ashworth has collapsed at the board after drinking his tea, and the committee is in a ferment. I beg that you will come."

I arrived at half-past seven, and was shown into the committee chamber, where Hartree awaited me with an expression of considerable relief. He was a thin, precise man of fifty, with the manner of one who has spent his life among figures and has learned to distrust anything that cannot be tabulated.

"You are very good to come," said he. "I hardly know how to put the matter without appearing absurd. But I shall state the facts plainly and let you judge. Edgar Corvin joined us in the spring—a quiet, unobtrusive fellow, neatly dressed, with a habit of sitting with his hands folded upon the table between moves. His play at first was mediocre—sound enough, but without imagination. Then, about five weeks ago, he began to win. He won against Hargreaves, who is our champion. He won against old Pemberton, who has forgotten more chess than most men ever learn. He won against Dr. Thurlow, against Masefield, against young Ellery, who is reckoned the most promising player in the club. And in every case the victories were achieved by methods that none of us could follow. It was as though he saw something on the board that was invisible to the rest of us."

"Has he been examined?" I asked.

"Examined?"

"I mean, has anyone looked at his hands, his eyes, his manner of sitting? Has anyone observed whether he is in the habit of touching his face, or of holding his fingers in any particular position?"

Hartree stared at me. "I cannot say that we have. We are chess players, sir, not detectives."

"Precisely. That is why I am here. And the collapse of this Mr. Ashworth—what were the circumstances?"

"Ashworth was playing Corvin last Tuesday. He had been drinking tea—we always have tea at the interval—and about twenty minutes after the resumption of the game he complained of a swimming in the head. He rose from his chair, took a step or two, and fell. He was insensible for some minutes. Dr. Thurlow attended him and pronounced it a transient faint, but Ashworth has been in a low nervous condition ever since, and he declares that he saw something in Corvin's eyes that frightened him."

"What sort of something?"

"He cannot describe it. He says only that it was 'not right.'"

I asked to be shown into the tournament hall, and Hartree conducted me thither. It was a long, low room, lit by gas-jets in frosted globes, with six tables arranged at intervals and a fire burning briskly at the far end. The members were already assembling—elderly men in broadcloth, younger men in tweeds, all with the slightly preoccupied air of persons who are about to engage in a matter of grave importance. At the second table from the window sat Edgar Corvin.

I observed him closely. He was a man of perhaps thirty-five, with a pale, narrow face and hair of a sandy hue that he wore brushed straight back from a high forehead. His hands lay folded upon the table in the manner Hartree had described. His eyes were fixed upon the board with an intensity that was almost painful. There was nothing in his appearance to suggest charlatanism—no rings, no jewellery, no eccentricity of dress. He might have been a bank clerk or a schoolmaster.

The games began. I took a chair in a corner from which I could command a view of Corvin's table without being myself conspicuous. His opponent was a stout, red-faced man named Calloway, who played with a heavy deliberateness, as though each move were a matter of life and death.

For the first twenty minutes Corvin played in a manner that seemed to me entirely orthodox. He developed his pieces, contested the centre, and made no move that would have surprised a player of moderate skill. Then, at a point where the position had become somewhat complicated, he did a very curious thing. He raised his right hand and passed it slowly across his brow, as though wiping away perspiration. It was a gesture that any man might make in a warm room. But as he did so, I saw his lips move. They formed no sound that I could hear, but they moved—and they moved in a rhythm that was not the rhythm of speech.
