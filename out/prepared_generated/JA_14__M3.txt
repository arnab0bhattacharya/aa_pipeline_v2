Mrs. Norcott had resided at Pendarren Park for the better part of seventeen years, occupying the very apartments that had been set apart for her use upon her late husband’s demise. The arrangement had been made with the utmost goodwill by her son, Mr. Edward Norcott, a man of easy fortune and easier temper, who saw no impropriety in his mother’s continued enjoyment of a home she had graced since her marriage. The Park was large, the income ample, and the presence of a quiet, respectable widow in the east wing could be no inconvenience to anyone.

This comfortable reasoning had sustained Mrs. Norcott through years of tranquil widowhood, undisturbed by any apprehension of change, until the spring of the year in which Mr. Norcott brought home a wife. The lady was Miss Clarinda Ferrier, the daughter of a Wiltshire gentleman of some property but less consequence, and she entered Pendarren Park with the calm assurance of a person who has known what it is to manage a household of her own. She was handsome, composed, and possessed of a mind that delighted in books, in music, and in the quiet possession of her own apartments—tastes which, however estimable in themselves, were precisely those least calculated to recommend her to her new mother-in-law.

Mrs. Norcott had formed her notion of a daughter-in-law upon a model which bore no resemblance to Mrs. Clarinda Norcott. She had pictured a young woman eager for guidance, gratefully dependent upon her counsel, and above all warmly sensible of the honour of being admitted into a family of such long standing in Shropshire. What she received was a young woman who expressed herself civilly, accepted every attention with uniform propriety, and betrayed not the smallest wish to be directed in any particular whatsoever.

This independence of sentiment, so foreign to Mrs. Norcott’s expectations, was interpreted by that lady in a manner the most unfavourable to her own peace. She saw in Mrs. Clarinda’s tranquillity a settled purpose of sovereignty, and in her preference for the library over the drawing-room, a design of seclusion that might, in its fullness, extend to the banishment of the elder Mrs. Norcott from the house altogether. The fear took root with astonishing speed, and within a fortnight of the bride’s arrival, Mrs. Norcott had resolved upon a course of action which she believed to be prudence, but which was, in truth, the most certain method of wearying a young woman who only wished to be left in peace.

She began by attending upon Mrs. Clarinda at all hours. If the morning was fine, Mrs. Norcott would propose a walk in the gardens, dwelling upon the beneficial effects of fresh air upon a constitution recently subjected to the fatigue of travel and ceremony. If the morning proved wet, she would suggest an examination of the household accounts, representing such an employment as both useful and becoming in a young matron. When Mrs. Clarinda was discovered with a book, Mrs. Norcott would enter the room with an air of anxious tenderness, inquire after her comfort, adjust the curtains, remark upon the light, and contrive to sit down in such a way as to make the continuation of reading a thing very nearly impossible.

Mrs. Clarinda bore these attentions with a patience that was, in its own way, quite as remarkable as Mrs. Norcott’s exertions. She answered every inquiry with perfect civility, declined every proposal with just sufficient warmth to avoid the appearance of coldness, and received every adjustment of her curtains with a smile of such uniform good humour that Mrs. Norcott could not but feel herself defeated without being able to name the precise moment of her defeat. That the young woman should be so consistently pleasant was, in itself, a source of perplexity. A display of irritation would have been far more manageable, for it would have confirmed Mrs. Norcott in her opinion that her daughter-in-law was a woman of unfeeling disposition, and would have justified, in her own mind, the extraordinary measures she was adopting to secure her position at Pendarren.

Mr. Norcott observed the increasing attentions of his mother with the sort of mild uneasiness which he was accustomed to feel whenever the happiness of his household appeared to require any exertion on his part. He was a man who desired, above all things, that everyone should be comfortable, and who believed, with a simplicity that did him credit, that comfort might be secured by the mere wish for it. He remarked to his mother one morning, as she was hastening toward the library with a shawl she had selected for Mrs. Clarinda’s use, that Clarinda was particularly fond of reading and perhaps might prefer to be left to herself now and then.

“Left to herself!” repeated Mrs. Norcott, pausing with the shawl suspended between her finger and thumb. “Yes, Edward, I have no doubt she would prefer it. Young women often prefer what is not good for them. But I assure you, I know the sex too well to leave a new bride to mope over books in a great empty house. She will think us neglectful, and the servants will remark upon it. No, no—I shall carry her this shawl at any rate, for the library is always cool of a morning, and a chill is easily caught and not easily cured.”

She proceeded with her errand, and Mr. Norcott, having no talent for persuasion where his mother was concerned, returned to his study with the comfortable reflection that he had at least said what was proper.

Mrs. Clarinda, meanwhile, was sitting in the library with a volume of Miss Edgeworth’s tales, in that state of agreeable absorption which a good book and a quiet room are alone sufficient to produce. She had been left in peace for nearly an hour, a circumstance so unusual that she had almost begun to fear Mrs. Norcott was unwell, when the door opened and that lady entered, radiant with purpose, bearing the shawl and an expression of solicitous affection that would have done honour to a sick nurse.
