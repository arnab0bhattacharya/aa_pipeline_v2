I have often remarked—and I trust the observation carries no undue weight of self-regard—that the practice of medicine in a quarter such as ours does not so much cultivate the faculty of judgement as it does the habit of suspending it. One learns to see men as the bearers of symptoms, and symptoms as the bearers of no moral quality whatsoever. A fever is a fever, whether it burns in the blood of a saint or a murderer; a wound demands its dressing with the same cold insistence under the hand of a philosopher or a fool. And yet there are nights—nights such as the one I am about to set down—when this professional equanimity, which is at once our pride and our defence, appears less a virtue than a kind of cowardice, a refusal to look upon the whole man for fear of what the whole man might require of us.

It was late in November, and the fog had come up from the river to lie upon the streets of Whitechapel like a soiled and heavy blanket, muffling the cries of the costermongers and lending to the very gas-lamps a look of sullen, suffocated flame. My rooms in —— Street were never cheerful, but on such an evening they took on an aspect of positive desolation, the bare boards and the worn drugget, the row of bottles catching the light in their bellies, the cold hearth—for I had let the fire die, being too weary to mend it. I had been abroad since morning among the courts and alleys, and had come home with the mud of the district upon my boots and, I confess, a certain muddiness of the spirit also, which the contemplation of so much preventable misery is apt to leave behind it. I was on the point of seeking my bed when there came a knock—not the timid, shuffling knock of the poor, nor yet the authoritative rat-tat of the constable, but something between the two, a firm and secretive signal, as though the hand that dealt it wished to be heard by me and by no other.

I opened the door upon a man of middle height, closely wrapped in a dark coat, with his hat pulled low and his collar turned up, so that I could see little of his face but the pale gleam of a cheek and the glitter of an eye. He asked, in a voice that was carefully levelled, whether I was the doctor, and whether I would come at once to a case of injury in a house not a quarter of a mile distant. There was about him an air I could not immediately name—a tension, a watchfulness, as of one who stands upon the edge of a precipice and affects to admire the view. I took my bag and followed him into the fog.

He led me by a labyrinth of ways I knew well enough by day but which the darkness and the vapour had transformed into a region of the fantastic and the ominous. We spoke little. Once, as we turned a corner, he said, over his shoulder, in a tone of studied indifference: “The gentleman is a foreigner. He speaks our language but indifferently. You will find him—excitable.” And again, after a pause: “He is anxious that no—that no unnecessary persons should be informed of his condition.” I made no reply. The words had about them a quality that I did not like, a suggestion of things left unsaid which is the peculiar property of the conspirator and the criminal.

The house was one of those tall, decayed edifices that stand in certain streets of the East End like the last survivors of a better age, their windows dark, their doorsteps worn into hollows by the feet of generations. We ascended a narrow stair, and my conductor, unlocking a door upon the second landing, stood aside for me to enter. The room was meanly furnished, but there burned upon the table a lamp with a green shade, and by its light I saw a man lying upon a couch, his coat and waistcoat removed, his shirt open at the breast, and his right hand and forearm swathed in a rough and blood-stained bandage which had been applied with more haste than skill.

He was, I judged, some five-and-thirty years of age, of a spare and nervous habit, with a face that would have been handsome but for the pinched lines about the mouth and the feverish brilliance of the eyes. He turned his head as I entered, and regarded me with a look of mingled appeal and defiance which I have seen in the faces of men who expect neither mercy nor justice, and have schooled themselves to expect the worst.

“You are the doctor,” he said, in a voice that betrayed the foreigner in its clipped vowels and its careful, bookish precision. “You will pardon the inconvenience. It is necessary that I should be—that I should have the use of my hand. There was an accident. A chemical—a preparation—it exploded.”

I said nothing, but set down my bag and approached him. He submitted to my examination with a stoical patience that I could not but admire, though his breath came quick and short between his teeth as I removed the bandage and disclosed the wound beneath. It was a ragged and ugly laceration of the palm and the inner surface of the fingers, with the flesh torn and blackened in a manner that spoke not of any ordinary accident, but of the violent disruption of some confined and volatile substance. There were grains of a dark powder in the wound, and a faint, acrid odour, which I did not then identify, but which I was afterwards to remember with a shudder.

“You have been fortunate,” I observed, in the neutral tone which long practice had made habitual. “The tendons are not severed. With care and cleanliness, the hand may be saved.”
