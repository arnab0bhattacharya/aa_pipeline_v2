The ceiling fan turned with the slow, deliberate menace of a blade on a grinding wheel, and Captain Elias Rusk watched it as though it might, at any moment, descend. The air in the marine court was thick with the smell of damp wool, sweat, and the particular mustiness of old paper, a scent that clung to the walls of the Singapore courthouse like mildew to a ship’s hold. He sat stiffly in the gallery, his uniform tunic buttoned to the throat despite the oppressive heat, a vain attempt to hold himself together. His hands, resting on his knees, were the hands of a man who had spent his life wrestling with canvas and wheel, but now they were still, palms clammy, fingers laced together so tightly the knuckles were white.

The inquiry had been going on for two days. Two days of testimony, of charts pored over, of the harbourmaster’s calm, damning voice describing the position of the *Ariadne’s Spindle* when she had struck the reef off Pedra Branca. The facts were not in dispute. The ship, a fine three-masted barque laden with tin and rubber, was a total loss. The reef was well-charted. The night had been clear. The error, they all knew, was his. A miscalculation of the current, a stubborn refusal to heed the lead line, a moment of hubris that had ground his command into the coral.

He had given his evidence that morning. He had been calm, precise, and had accepted the blame with a captain’s stoicism. He had not offered excuses. He had not mentioned the argument on the bridge. He had not mentioned his mate.

Now, they were calling him. “Mr. Thomas Avery,” the clerk announced, his voice a thin, reedy sound that cut through the murmuring of the gallery.

Rusk’s gaze shifted from the fan to the witness box. Avery rose from his seat and walked to the stand with the steady, rolling gait of a man who had not yet found his land legs. He was a lean, weathered man, ten years Rusk’s junior, with a face that was all sharp angles and sun-cracked skin. He looked neither to the left nor the right, his eyes fixed on some point beyond the judge’s bench. He was sworn in, his hand large and brown against the small, black book.

Rusk’s heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic, trapped bird. This was the moment. He had lain awake in his stifling hotel room the past two nights, staring at the ceiling, replaying that final hour on the bridge. The memory was a splinter in his mind, sharp and painful.

*“Two points to port, Captain. The lead’s showing shoaling.” Avery’s voice, quiet but firm in the darkness.*

*“Nonsense, Mr. Avery. The current’s setting us fair. I’ve run this passage a dozen times. Hold your course.”*

*“But the water—”*

*“Hold your course, I said.”*

The words hung in the courtroom’s stale air, though no one else could hear them. Rusk could feel the weight of them, the sheer, pig-headed arrogance of his own voice. He had been tired, anxious to make port before the monsoon broke, and he had mistaken his mate’s caution for timidity. He had been the captain. He had been sure.

The inquiry’s president, a portly man with a florid face and a voice like gravel, leaned forward. “Mr. Avery, you were the officer of the watch on the night of the incident, is that correct?”

“It is, sir,” Avery replied. His voice was low and even, without a tremor.

“Will you tell the court, in your own words, the sequence of events leading up to the grounding?”

Rusk watched Avery’s jaw tighten. The man’s eyes flickered, just for a second, in his direction. It was a ghost of a glance, a fleeting acknowledgment of their shared past, of long watches and shared dangers, of a bond forged by salt water and silence. In that instant, Rusk saw a flicker of something else: the memory of the *Ariadne’s Spindle* groaning, the terrible, grinding shriek of wood on coral, the chaos of the next morning as they abandoned ship. He saw the loyalty that had kept Avery from speaking up during the captain’s own testimony, a silence that Rusk had not asked for but had desperately needed.

Now, Avery was under oath. The truth was the only thing that could save him from a charge of negligence, or worse. And the truth would damn Rusk utterly.

“We were making good progress, sir, with a fair wind from the sou’-west,” Avery began, his voice steady. “The night was clear. I had the watch. At approximately two bells in the middle watch, I took a sounding.”

He paused, and the silence in the room was absolute. Rusk could hear his own breathing, shallow and fast. He felt a bead of sweat trace a slow, cold path down his temple. He wanted to look away, to study the grain of the wooden bench, but he could not. His eyes were locked on Avery, a prisoner awaiting a verdict.

“The lead showed a marked decrease in depth,” Avery continued, his gaze fixed on the president. “Less than ten fathoms, and shoaling rapidly. The current was setting us closer to the reef than the charts indicated for that hour.”

“And what did you do?” the president prompted.

“I informed the captain, sir.”

“And what was his response?”

Rusk’s breath caught in his throat. This was it. The question that would break him or, against all odds, spare him. He saw Avery’s knuckles whiten where he gripped the rail of the witness box. The man’s loyalty was a palpable thing, a heavy coat he was being asked to shed in front of everyone. Rusk knew that if Avery told the simple truth—that his captain had ignored a direct warning—the court would have its scapegoat. The blame would be Rusk’s, and Rusk’s alone. He would be ruined, his master’s certificate revoked, his name a byword for folly from the Cape to the China Sea. He would have nothing. He deserved nothing.
