The rain in Camden Town did not merely fall; it assaulted the cobblestones with a persistent, muddy indignation, as if the very heavens were offended by the soot that choked the air and the dampness that rotted the timbers of the old houses. It was a Tuesday, a day of no particular distinction in the calendar of the world, but in the ledger of Mrs. Pumblechook’s establishment, *The Gilded Sash*, it was a day of profound and shattering catastrophe.

Eliza Thistlewood, or "Lizzie" as the servants called her, or "That girl" as Mrs. Pumblechook addressed her, stood in the center of the dining room, holding a tray of untouched teacups. She was a creature of sharp angles and pale skin, possessed of a face that had not yet decided whether it would be pretty or merely plain, and eyes that had learned to look everywhere except directly at the source of her misery. For the past six hours, she had been the invisible engine of the house, scrubbing the stairs until her fingers were white and swollen, dusting the chandeliers until her lungs felt like they were filled with cotton, and serving breakfast, lunch, and a mid-morning supper to a roomful of guests who spoke of her as one speaks of a particularly annoying fly.

But now, the fly was gone. Or rather, the fly had been promoted to a butterfly, and the transformation was so sudden, so grotesque, and so loud that it shook the very plaster of the ceiling.

"Look at her! Look at the wretch!" Mrs. Pumblechook shrieked, pointing a finger that trembled with a mixture of rage and a strange, suppressed envy. She was a woman of considerable girth and even greater volume, her voice carrying the authority of a foghorn in a dense mist. "Look at her airs and graces! As if she had not been born in the dirt and raised on boiled turnips! As if she had not spent the last five years picking lice off the backs of our lodgers!"

Lizzie said nothing. She could not. Her mouth was a dry, locked gate. She looked down at her hands. They were no longer the hands of the drudge. They were soft, manicured, and adorned with a ring of gold that caught the grey light from the window. On her back hung a dress of deep emerald velvet, embroidered with silver thread in patterns that looked like ferns frozen in time. Her hair, usually tied back in a severe and practical knot, was arranged in a cascade of curls that smelled of lavender and expensively imported perfume.

She had been adopted. Not by a kind aunt, nor by a benevolent uncle, but by Miss Agatha Vane, a spinster of such terrifying eccentricity that she had been known to feed her cats on oyster sauce and to converse exclusively with her mirrors in the dead of night. Miss Vane had arrived at *The Gilded Sash* three days prior, a storm of purple feathers and diamond studs, and had swept into the back kitchen where Lizzie was on her knees scrubbing the floor.

" *You* ," Miss Vane had said, her voice like the cracking of ice on a frozen lake. "You have the eyes of a tragic heroine in a novel I read in 1812. I shall take you. You will be my niece. My heir. My ornament. I am bored of the company of men who smell of tobacco and women who smell of lavender water. You smell of soap and sorrow, and I find that refreshing."

And so, Lizzie had been whisked away to a townhouse in Mayfair, where she had been dressed in silk, fed on truffles, and taught the names of the stars by an astronomer hired for the occasion. For three days, she had been the jewel of the house. But Miss Vane’s affections were as fickle as the weather in Camden. On the fourth morning, Miss Vane had looked at Lizzie, yawned, and said, "I have decided that I prefer the company of my cats. They are less demanding, and they do not ask questions. You may return to your boarding house. Tell Mrs. Pumblechook I have changed my mind. Tell her I am a woman of my word, and my word was that you would be my niece. But I am no longer your aunt. I am merely a woman who has had a brief and unpleasant experience."

And so, Lizzie was back. Back in the dining room of *The Gilded Sash*, holding the tray of teacups, wearing the dress of a lady, and feeling like a ghost haunting her own life.

Mrs. Pumblechook was still screaming. "You think you can come in here, dressed like a harlot from a play, and expect us to bow to you? You think your little fancy dress makes you superior to us? I tell you, girl, you are still the same mud that you were yesterday! You are still the same thief of our time and energy!"

The guests, a collection of retired officers, nervous merchants, and vain ladies, had fallen silent. They watched the scene with the rapt attention of an audience at a pantomime, waiting for the punchline, or the tragedy. One of the retired officers, a man with a moustache that curled upward like a question mark, leaned over to his neighbor and whispered, "Is she going to faint? Or is she going to strike her?"

Lizzie looked at Mrs. Pumblechook. She looked at the tray in her hands. She looked at the velvet dress that felt like a shroud. And then, she did something that no one in the room expected. She smiled. It was not a happy smile, nor a angry one. It was a smile of terrible, quiet clarity.
