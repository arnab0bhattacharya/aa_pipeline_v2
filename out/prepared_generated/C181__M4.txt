The morning of his annual holiday dawned so bright and blue that Albert Finch, a clerk of the most precise and cheerful disposition, felt his heart swell to the size of a cricket ball. He had oiled his bicycle chain the night before, packed a luncheon of bread and cheese and a bottle of ginger beer in his satchel, and now he pedalled out of the sleepy town of Wherwell, his spirits rising with every turn of the wheel. The Hampshire lanes, hedged high with hawthorn and dog rose, unspooled before him like a ribbon of pale earth, and he whistled a music-hall tune, entirely at peace with the world.

It was some five miles on, near a ford where the willows dipped their fingers in the water, that he saw her. A young woman, no more than nineteen, sat upon a milestone with the air of one who had been sitting there for a considerable time and had found no answers in the moss. Her dress was good but dusty, and she held a small valise upon her lap as though it were a sleeping child. When she heard the soft whirr of his approach, she looked up, and Albert saw that her eyes were red with weeping.

He dismounted at once, doffing his cap. “Good morning, miss. I hope I don’t intrude. Are you quite well?”

She regarded him with a wary sort of hope. “I am not lost,” she said, with a defiance that suggested she was. “I am going to my aunt’s.”

“Then you are very nearly there, I should think,” said Albert, who had no idea where she was going but believed in the tonic properties of agreement. “May I be of service? Finch is my name. Albert Finch. I am a clerk at the Wherwell and Deverel Bank, and I am on my holiday, so you may be sure I have nothing better to do than to help a person in need.”

The girl’s name was Clara Pemberton, and her story, told in fits and starts beside the ford, was this: her guardian, a Mr. Josiah Crumm of the Grange, was a man of rigid and uncharitable temper, who had decreed that she should marry a cousin of forty-five for the sake of a business arrangement. When she had refused, he had locked her in her room. She had climbed out of the window at dawn and walked until her shoes were grey with dust. Her aunt, a Mrs. Hollis, lived at a farm called Oakhurst, some eight miles further on, and she had not the first idea of the way.

Albert Finch was a clerk, and clerks are not, as a rule, given to romance. But he was also a young man of twenty-four, and the sight of Clara Pemberton’s trembling lip did something to his chest that no ledger had ever accomplished. “Miss Pemberton,” he said, with a bow that nearly upset his bicycle, “it would be my honour to escort you to Oakhurst. The lanes are quiet, and my machine is strong. You may sit upon the crossbar, and I shall walk beside you.”

She looked at him for a long moment, and then she laughed—a small, surprised laugh, like a bird taking flight. “You are very kind, Mr. Finch. I should be glad of the company.”

And so they set off, Albert pushing the bicycle with Clara perched upon the bar, her valise balanced before her. He told her about the bank, and the peculiar habits of Mr. Deverel, who insisted on counting the silver twice; she told him about the Grange, and the dreadful cousin, and the way the ivy on the east wall had a face in it if you looked at it long enough. The sun climbed higher, the larks sang, and Albert felt that he had never spent a better holiday in his life.

They had gone perhaps three miles, and were passing through a deep cutting where the hedges met overhead, when a sound came to them from behind: the furious clatter of a horse and trap, driven hard. Clara turned white. “It is he,” she whispered. “It is Mr. Crumm.”

The trap came into view, and in it stood a large, red-faced man in a black coat, his hat askew, his whip raised. “You there!” he bellowed. “Stop! Stop, I say! You villain, you scoundrel, you—you abductor!”

Albert, whose experience of villains was limited to the pages of the Police Gazette, drew himself up. “Sir,” he said, “I am no such thing. Miss Pemberton has asked for my assistance, and I am escorting her to her aunt’s house.”

“Her aunt’s house!” Mr. Crumm’s face grew redder still. “A likely story! You have enticed her away—you have plotted this—I see it all! A pretty scheme, to steal a girl from her lawful guardian and make off with her! I shall have the law on you, I shall!”

Clara clung to Albert’s arm. “It is not true, Mr. Crumm! I ran away because you would have me marry that horrid cousin of yours, and I would rather die!”

“Ungrateful girl!” roared Crumm. “Get down from that machine this instant!”

Albert Finch was not a brave man. He had never struck a blow in anger, and his chief ambition was to be made head clerk before he was thirty. But he was, at bottom, a decent one, and he had a bicycle, which is a machine of considerable strategic value. “Miss Pemberton,” he said quietly, “hold fast to the handlebars.” And then, with a suddenness that surprised even himself, he leapt onto the saddle, pushed off with all his might, and sent them flying down the lane.

The trap gave chase, but the lane narrowed and twisted, and the horse, no doubt tired from its earlier gallop, began to labour. Albert pedalled as he had never pedalled before, his heart pounding, Clara’s hair blowing against his cheek, and behind them the bellowing of Mr. Crumm grew fainter and fainter until it was lost in the hum of the hedgerows.
