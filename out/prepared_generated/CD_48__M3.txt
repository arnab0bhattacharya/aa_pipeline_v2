If any pair of young lovers in the whole history of improvident matrimony ever set sail upon the ocean of housekeeping with a more entire absence of ballast, and a more serene conviction of fine weather ahead, than Mr. and Mrs. Felix Blithe, of Laburnum Villa, Kentish Town, then History is a fabulist, and Human Nature itself is a stranger to its own delightful absurdities. Felix was a clerk of the most cheerful and unbusiness-like description, possessing a countenance beaming with perpetual sunshine, a waistcoat of astonishing patterns, and an income which, if it had been a physical substance, might have been comfortably contained within a very moderate-sized thimble. His bride, Mrs. Felix, was a young lady of corresponding gayety, who regarded a bill of account as a mere figure of speech, and a tradesman’s ledger as a volume of romantic fiction, possessing the rare charm of never coming to a tragic conclusion.

They took the villa upon the credit of their smiles, and they furnished it upon the credit of their characters. It is astonishing what a capital of good-humour will do in the way of housekeeping. The cheerful Blithes smiled upon the upholsterer, and the upholsterer smiled upon the cheerful Blithes; and the result was a magnificent suite of rosewood furniture that would not have disgraced a ducal mansion. They jested with the wine-merchant, and the wine-merchant sent them hampers of sparkling Moselle. They nodded to the hosier, and the hosier sent them stockings enough to fit a centipede. Every tradesman in the high street of Kentish Town was captivated by the pleasant Mr. Blithe and his charming wife. They were so frank, so genial, so utterly and transparently void of any present intention to pay, that to refuse them credit seemed a positive affront to the social virtues.

The villa, accordingly, bloomed into a perfect paradise of upholstery. There were Brussels carpets in which the foot sank as it sank, seemed to the depth of two inches at least, and the pattern of which represented convolvulus and roses, so thickly strewn that the very floor appeared a garden. There were curtains of heavy damask that swept the ground with a grand theatrical flourish; there were ormolu clocks that ticked with a deliberate, conscious dignity, as if they were counting the seconds of an estate rather than the hours of a clerkship; and there were china ornaments on the chimney-piece, of such fanciful and impossible shapes that they looked like the disordered dreams of a pastry-cook. The Blithes were perfectly happy, and the bills were perfectly accumulating.

But there arrives a moment in the life of every debtor when the long-suffering earth, having turned upon its axis a certain number of times, brings round a day of reckoning. It was on a bleak Tuesday in November, when the damp fog crept up from the fields of Kentish Town, and hung like a wet blanket upon the laburnum-trees in the little front-garden, that the crash came. The postman, a man of gloomy aspect and heavy tread, knocked at the door with an ominous double-rap, as if he were the very executioner of the Post Office, and deposited a heap of missives upon the hall-table. They were not love-letters, those envelopes; they were not invitations to tea and muffins; they were bills, and bills, and bills—long, narrow, uncompromising bills, enclosing little squares of paper with terrifying additions of pounds, shillings, and pence. The butcher’s bill, a bill of fare so extensive that it seemed to recapitulate the entire animal kingdom; the baker’s bill, a calendar of daily bread that stretched back into a remote antiquity; the upholsterer’s bill, a poem in rosewood and horsehair; and the wine-merchant’s bill, a record of festivities so prolonged that it read like a chronicle of the Crusades.

Felix sat in his rosewood parlour, gazing at the heap of documents with a countenance from which the habitual sunshine had faded, leaving it as blank and as grey as the fog without. Mrs. Felix sat opposite to him, her hands clasped in her lap, her usual sparkling dialogue quite dried up. They looked at the bills, and they looked at each other, and they looked at the ormolu clock, which ticked on with a maddening indifference, as though it were saying, “I told you so,” though it had done nothing of the sort.

It was in this hour of their desolation that a hansom cab, an article of equipage they had fondly imagined was sent for their especial delight, drew up before the villa. It was followed by a portmanteau of such formidable dimensions, that it seemed to have been constructed in the dark ages, and to have survived them by main force. Out of the cab stepped a lady of a certain age, and of a most uncompromising aspect. She was dressed in a bonnet of a fashion so ancient and so rigid, that it seemed to have been built rather than made; and she wore a shawl of a pattern so entirely removed from the existing modes, that it might have been woven by the hands of a former generation. This was Miss Belinda Blatherwick, the maiden aunt of Felix; a lady who had been for years a standing menace to his imagination, a personage whom he had always carefully avoided, and of whom he had spoken in society as “my aunt in the country,” though she lived but ten miles from London Bridge, and was as much in town as any one.

Miss Blatherwick was not a lady to be trifled with. She had a nose that was a sort of moral compass, pointing always towards the right and the disagreeable; and a pair of eyes that seemed to see through the pretences of human nature as if they were made of glass. She had heard of the villa, she had heard of the furniture, she had heard, perhaps, of the bills; and she had come, as she expressed it, to “take charge.”
