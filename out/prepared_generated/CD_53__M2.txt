The mist had hung over the village of Little Fenwick for three days, and on the fourth, it seemed to congeal into a substance so thick and grey that the very cobblestones appeared to be submerged in a cold, sludge-like ocean. It was a fitting atmosphere for the return of one Josiah Pumble, a man who had departed those mud-choked lanes twenty years prior with nothing but a pair of boots and a dream, and who now returned with a carriage that shook like a broken tooth and a pocket full of sovereigns that jingled with a loudness he found entirely indecent. Josiah had made his fortune in the gold fields of Australia, where he had learned to value silence and to distrust the idle chatter of men who had never seen the sun rise over a mine. But here, in the damp heart of Suffolk, the silence was impossible; the world was a clamorous machine of gossip, and he was merely another cog, albeit one that had been polished by distance and grease.

As the hired carriage, a battered affair that smelled of tobacco and old leather, rumbled into the village square, Josiah sat upright, his heart beating with a strange, arrhythmic thud. He looked out at the shopfronts, the thatched roofs, the church spire that had always seemed to point accusingly at the clouds. He had expected, in the feverish dreams of his youth, that the villagers would rush to greet him with open arms, to hail him as a hero, a savior, a man who had beaten the odds and returned with the keys to the kingdom. But as the carriage stopped before the old inn, the *Golden Stag*, the reality set in with the sudden, crushing weight of a wet wool blanket. The people who stood on the pavement did not cheer. They did not smile. They stood with their hands tucked into their coats or their arms crossed over their chests, staring at him with a mixture of curiosity, suspicion, and a profound, petty shame. They were the same faces, or rather, the same types of faces, grown larger and more distorted by the lens of time and resentment.

He alighted, the mud splashing against his polished boots, and felt the eyes of the village bore into his back. He recognized Mr. Gristle, the butcher, who had once called him a "useless little rat." Gristle stood by his cart, holding a cleaver, and instead of extending a hand, he turned his face away, as if Josiah’s very presence were a contagion. He saw old Mrs. Hobbs, who had once threatened to flog him for stealing apples, and she crossed herself with a finger stained with pipe tobacco, muttering a prayer that was clearly directed at his absence rather than his return. The shame that burned in Josiah’s cheeks was not for himself, but for them. He felt a sudden, violent anger, a desire to shake them by the shoulders and demand to know why they could not look him in the eye, why they could not speak a word of welcome to a man who had suffered and triumphed in lands where the air was dry and the ground was gold. He had become a stranger to his own home, a foreigner in the land of his birth, and the realization struck him with the force of a physical blow.

He walked away from the inn, his step heavy, his mind a turmoil of conflicting emotions. He did not know where to go. The houses that had been his childhood playground now looked like dark, sullen sentinels, guarding secrets he no longer cared to know. He passed the bakery, where the smell of burnt crusts usually brought him comfort; now it smelled only of decay. He passed the schoolhouse, a small, stone building that looked as though it were slowly sinking into the earth, its windows dark and shut. And there, in the shadow of the porch, stood a figure that seemed to belong to a different era, a different world. It was Miss Prudence Thistle, the schoolmistress who had taught him to read, who had once told him that his mind was a "bright, if untamed, candle." She was an old woman now, her hair white as the frost that lay on the ground, her spine bent under the weight of years and duty. But her eyes, when she looked up, were sharp and clear, devoid of the malice that had greeted him elsewhere.

"Josiah," she said, her voice thin but steady, like the sound of a dry leaf scraping across stone. "You have come back."

It was not a question. It was an observation, a fact as immutable as the laws of physics. Josiah felt his throat tighten. He opened his mouth to speak, to make an excuse, to offer a grand explanation of his wealth and his travels, but the words died on his tongue. Instead, he simply nodded, a small, defeated movement.

Miss Thistle stepped forward, and for the first time since his arrival, he felt the tension in his shoulders begin to ease. She did not reach out to shake his hand, as the men had not, but she placed a small, cold hand on his arm. "You look tired, Josiah," she said. "And you look rich. That is a strange combination for a man of your birth."

He laughed, a short, harsh sound that echoed in the empty street. "I am neither, Miss Thistle. I am only a man who has been away a long time."

"Then you are welcome," she said simply. "The world has changed, Josiah. People have forgotten how to be kind. But I have not. Come inside. The fire is lit, and I have tea. It is not the finest tea in the world, but it is hot, and it is for you."
