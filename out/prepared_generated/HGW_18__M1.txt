I had been employed at Pettigrew’s for something under three months when the floor of the establishment decided to leave the foundations. It was a Tuesday, and a dull one at that, the sort of afternoon in Kent when the light comes through the shop windows like weak tea and there is nothing to do but rearrange the tins of biscuits for the third time. Miss Eleanora Vance was seated at the small table by the window, as she was every Tuesday at four, taking a cup of the China blend and a slice of seed cake. She was the daughter of the Mayor, a young lady of considerable local consequence and practically no experience of the world beyond the end of her own nose.

“It is quite impossible, Mr. Gable,” she was saying, dabbing her lips with a napkin, “that the jam should be served on the same plate as the cake. It spoils the aesthetic entirely. My father, when he entertains at the Town Hall, insists on separate services for every condiment.”

“Yes, Miss Vance,” I said. I was polishing the counter, watching the dust motes dance in the slats of sunlight. “I shall speak to the manageress. Though I fancy Mrs. Higgins believes the aesthetic is improved by saving the washing up.”

Miss Vance looked at me sharply, uncertain whether I was being impertinent or merely factual. She was about to decide on the former when the universe intervened.

There was no thunder, no wind. There was simply a low, grinding groan, like the sound of a giant taking a heavy chest of drawers across a stone floor. Then the floor of the tea room buckled. It did not merely shake; it seemed to swell upward and then drop away, throwing me against the counter and sending the display of shortbread shattering onto the floor. The ceiling, a heavy affair of plaster and lath, came down with a roar that drowned out Miss Vance’s scream. The world became a tumbling, roaring confusion of darkness, the smell of plaster dust, and the sickening crash of breaking glass.

When the noise ceased, the silence was absolute and terrifying. I found myself pressed against the side of the counter, my face buried in a pile of broken crockery and flour. The air was thick with a choking white mist.

“Miss Vance?” I called out, my voice sounding thin and reedy. “Miss Vance, are you injured?”

There was a coughing fit in the darkness to my left. “Mr. Gable? Is that you? I cannot see. I believe I am blind.”

“You are not blind, Miss,” I said, coughing myself. “It is the dust. And the dark.”

“I am covered in something,” she said, her voice rising in panic. “It is wet. Is it blood? Oh, do tell me it isn’t blood.”

“It is likely the raspberry jam, Miss Vance. And perhaps a good deal of tea.”

I began to move, testing my limbs. My leg was bruised but sound. I reached out a hand and felt the underside of the counter, which was now resting at a drunken angle. Above us, I could hear the groaning of timber. We were in a pocket, a small void left by the collapse of the upper floor and the roof, but it felt as though the weight of the entire town of Ashford was resting just above our heads.

“We must dig,” I said, the practical necessity of the thing cutting through the shock. “If we stay here, the rest of the building will settle and crush us.”

“Dig?” Miss Vance’s voice was incredulous. “With what? My hands? I am not a navvy, Mr. Gable.”

“You have hands, Miss Vance, and I have hands. That will have to do.”

I crawled toward the sound of her voice and found her huddled against the leg of her table. She was trembling violently. In the gloom, I could just make out the pale oval of her face. She was remarkably composed for a woman who had just survived an earthquake, but there was a brittleness to her.

“I cannot do it,” she whispered. “It is too terrible. My dress is ruined. It is silk.”

“With respect, Miss Vance,” I said, groping for a loose board, “if we do not get out, your dress will be your shroud, and silk rots quickly in the earth. Now, take this.”

I thrust a piece of lath into her hand. It was rough and jagged.

“What is it?”

“It is a tool. Now, push the rubble toward me. We must clear a way to the street door. It was only ten yards away.”

“You are ordering me about,” she said, but she sounded too weary to argue.

“I am trying to keep us alive, Miss. Now, push.”

We began to work. It was a nightmare of claustrophobia and exertion. The dust made our throats raw, and every movement sent showers of plaster down our necks. I dug with a piece of the floorboards, levering up chunks of masonry and dragging them behind us, while Miss Vance, to her credit, began to move the smaller debris. She complained constantly—at first about the dirt, then about the heat, then about the indignity of it all—but she did not stop.

“My father will have the Town Hall rebuilt by Friday,” she said breathlessly as she shoved a brick toward me. “He is very efficient.”

“I fear, Miss Vance, that this may be a larger problem than the Town Hall,” I said, heaving a heavy slab of concrete. “The whole of Kent seems to have gone down.”

“Don’t be absurd. It is merely a subsidence. Perhaps the gas mains.”

“The gas mains do not move the earth like a wave, Miss. I felt the ground roll. We are lucky we are not at the bottom of the sea.”

I stopped for a moment, listening. There was a faint rumbling from outside, the sound of falling masonry and distant shouting. Then, a new sound—a high-pitched whistling. Steam. The boiler in the basement had likely burst.
