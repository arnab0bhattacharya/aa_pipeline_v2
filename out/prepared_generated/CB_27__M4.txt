The rain had fallen without ceasing since the Tuesday, and the town of Marrowfield—if town it might be called, being rather a swelling congeries of mills, chimneys, and mean dwellings, wedged into a green valley that the smoke had long since killed—lay under a pall of yellow vapour that stank of soot and wet wool. I had come to it three months before, a stranger with a licence and a leather bag, and had taken the two rooms above the apothecary’s shop in Cinder Lane, where the sign of the mortar and pestle creaked all night in the wind, and where the rats were bolder than the patients.

My trade was the setting of bones and the dressing of wounds; but in Marrowfield the bones were chiefly the bones of children, and the wounds were chiefly the wounds of the mill. It was on the Thursday, at dusk, that they brought me the girl called Nanny Shaw, and she was nine years old, though she looked six, and her arm had been caught in the gearing of a carding-engine at Fielder’s Mill. The overlooker who carried her—a great brute with a face like a boiled pudding—set her down on my table and said, “She’ll do nowt more wi’ yon hand, Doctor; reckon thou’lt have to take it off.” The child did not cry. She looked at me with eyes that had grown old in the mill, and she said, “Please, sir, will it mend by Monday? I durstn’t lose the wage.”

I will not dwell upon the surgery. I will say only that I saved the hand, though the fingers would never straighten, and that when it was done and the child lay white and sleeping on the couch, I went out into the wet street and stood in the rain to cool the fire that was in my blood. For it was not the first. It was the thirty-first since the spring—I had the number in my ledger—the thirty-first child maimed at Fielder’s or at Hargreaves’s or at the great mill of the Ashworths down by the water; and the owners, when I waited upon them with my book and my plea, had told me plainly that I was a young man and did not understand the trade, and that if I did not like the trade I might carry my trade elsewhere.

It was on the Friday that Miss Ashworth came to my door.

I knew her by sight, for she was often to be seen in the town—a slight, straight figure in grey, with a face that was not beautiful but was better than beautiful, being honest, and with a brow that had thought in it and a mouth that had resolution. She was the only daughter of the great Ashworth, the master of the Water Mill, and she was said to be proud, and to have refused two curates and a Manchester merchant. She came into my little surgery with the rain on her bonnet and her gloves in her hand, and she stood before me and looked at the couch where the child Nanny still slept, and her lips were white.

“You are the doctor,” she said. “I have heard of you. You are the man who wishes my father to shorten the hours.”

“I am,” I said. “And your father wishes me to mind my trade.”

“He is wrong,” she said, in a low voice, “and he knows it. He is not a cruel man, Doctor, but he is a frightened man, and fear makes men hard. He fears the other masters; he fears the foreign competition; he fears to be thought soft. And so the little ones go into the mill at six and come out at eight, and the machinery does not care, and the overlookers do not care, and—” She stopped, and her hands closed upon her gloves. “I have no right to speak so,” she said. “I am his daughter.”

“You have every right,” I said. “It is the right of a woman who sees a wrong and has the courage to name it.”

She looked at me then, full in the face, and I felt the blood rise in my own, for her eyes were of that clear grey that seems to see through a man and to judge him and to be neither ashamed nor afraid.

“I have money,” she said, and her voice was steady now, though it was very low. “I have my mother’s money, which is mine, and which my father does not touch. It is not much—fifty pounds—but it is enough to begin. I want you to take it, Doctor, and to open a dispensary in the lower part of the town, where the mill-hands live, and to give your medicines and your skill to the children for nothing, or for what they can pay, which is nothing. I want you to do it secretly. If my father knew, he would forbid it, and I do not choose to be forbidden in a matter of conscience. Will you take it?”

I was a proud man then, and I am not humble now; and the offer of money from a woman’s hand is a thing that a man may take in two ways. But I looked at the child on the couch, and I looked at the rain on the window, and I thought of the thirty-one names in my ledger, and I said, “I will take it, Miss Ashworth, and I will thank you for it as I have never thanked any human creature in my life.”

She flushed then—a sudden, beautiful flush, that made her for a moment almost fair—and she said, “You must not thank me. I am only ashamed that I have not done it before. I have thought of it for a year, and I have been a coward. It is easy to be a coward when one is comfortable.”

“It is not easy to be brave when one is comfortable,” I said. “And you are brave.”
