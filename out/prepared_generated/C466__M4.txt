The trouble with Mr. Ambrose Veale was not that he lacked generosity, but that he could not bear to be invisible while being generous. When he resolved to replace the dilapidated row of labourers' cottages on the southern edge of his Buckinghamshire estate—a muddy, thatch-sagging terrace known locally as Bedlam Row—he did so with the air of a man presenting a gift to the county, and he expected the county to look.

He engaged Mr. Silas Finch, a young architect of advanced ideas, lately down from London, who had published a pamphlet on "Sanitary Dwellings for the Rural Poor" and wore his hair in a manner that suggested he had argued with a wind. Mr. Veale drove him about the estate in a dog-cart, gesturing at hedgerows and prospects, and said, "Light, Finch. Air. Drainage. I want cottages a man may live in without shame."

"Indeed, sir," said Finch, whose mind was already full of octagonal porches.

Mrs. Veale watched the enterprise from the morning-room window with the expression of a woman who has seen her husband buy a telescope. "He means to be in the newspaper," she said to her sister, who was visiting. "He means to be engraved. 'Philanthropy of a Buckinghamshire Squire.' I know him. He would rather be good in the Illustrated London News than good in private."

"Perhaps he is both," said her sister, who was charitable.

"Perhaps," said Mrs. Veale, "but he will make the poor man's comfort a monument to himself, and the poor man will have to live in the monument."

The designs were handsome. Finch had drawn six cottages in warm brick with steep gables, each with a scullery, a copper for washing, a pump within doors, and windows of a size that made Mr. Veale's agent, Mr. Rooke, whistle through his teeth and speak of glass duties. There were allotment gardens at the rear, and a pump-house, and a schoolroom at the end of the terrace, and over the central arch a stone tablet, already commissioned, bearing the words: ERECTED BY AMBROSE VEALE, ESQ., FOR THE COMFORT OF HIS LABOURERS, 1864.

The labourers themselves were less enthusiastic. Finch, walking the site with his measuring rod, encountered an old man named Thomas Hedger, who had lived in Bedlam Row for forty-one years and was regarded as its chief authority.

"You'll be pulling it down, then," said Hedger, not as a question.

"You will have a new house, Mr. Hedger," said Finch. "With a copper. And a pump inside."

"Old Poll has the pump," said Hedger, indicating a woman at the end of the row. "And the copper's at the wash-house, and the wash-house is where the talk is. Where will the talk be, sir, in your new houses, with your pumps inside and no wash-house?"

Finch explained that each cottage would have its own copper, which was an improvement.

"Improvement," said Hedger, "is when the water comes to you and the talk goes away."

He showed Finch the things he valued: the deep eaves where the swallows nested and the thatch kept the sun off in summer; the wide hearth where a man could sit on both sides of the fire; the bit of ground where his hens went, which was not on Finch's plan; the ancient damson tree at the corner, which Finch's plan required to be felled for the sake of the drainage. "My father planted that," said Hedger. "Well, he didn't plant it. But he ate from it."

Finch reported to Mr. Veale that the tenants were attached to their old dwellings.

"Attached!" cried Mr. Veale. "Attached to a leaking roof and a floor of beaten earth? Nonsense. They don't know what's good for them. Build the cottages, Finch. They will thank me when they are in them."

Mrs. Veale, encountering Finch in the hall one afternoon, was more direct. "You are building my husband a statue," she said, "and calling it a cottage. I do not blame you; you are paid. But I hope you will not let him put that tablet over the arch. It is the tablet of a man who wants to be thanked in stone."

"I had thought the inscription a little—prominent," Finch admitted.

"It is the inscription of a man who has never had to live in a house he built for someone else," said Mrs. Veale, and went out to the garden.

Finch went back to his drawings and was troubled. He was young, and he wished to be known, and he had already written to his mother that his cottages at Veale Hall would be noticed. But he had also, in his pamphlet, written that a dwelling must fit the life of the dweller, and he began to suspect that he had designed for the Illustrated London News and not for Thomas Hedger.

He rode over to Bedlam Row in the rain and stood a long while under the eaves, watching the water run off the thatch in a clean sheet and the smoke go sideways from the chimneys and the men come in at dusk and sit on the benches by their doors, and he saw that the row, for all its sag, had a shape that people had made by living in it. He took out his notebook and drew, not the cottages, but the life: the bench, the damson tree, the wash-house, the shared pump, the eaves.

Then he went to Mr. Veale and asked for leave to alter the plans.

"Alter them how?" said Mr. Veale, who was fond of his gables.

"Keep the new drainage and the light and the sculleries," said Finch. "But build a wash-house at the end of the row, with the copper and the pump in it, as they have now. Leave the damson tree. Give each cottage a bench under the eaves, and make the eaves deep. Put the tablet—if there must be a tablet—small, over the wash-house door, where the women will see it every day and think nothing of it."
