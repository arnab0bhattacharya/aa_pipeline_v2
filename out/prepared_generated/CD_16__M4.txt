In the narrow and not over-airy thoroughfare of Princes-street, Soho, where the houses stand shoulder to shoulder like a row of elderly spinsters at a tea-party, each endeavouring to peep over the bonnet of her neighbour, there dwelt at the period of which I write—namely, in the year of our Lord one thousand seven hundred and ninety-three—a certain Monsieur Achille Dufresne, professor of the Terpsichorean art, a gentleman whose heels had once been the pride of the Faubourg Saint-Germain, and whose fortunes, like the heels aforesaid, had been considerably worn down by the march of events.

He occupied the first floor of the house of Mrs. Tabitha Pumble, a widow of portly presence and resolute opinions, who let her apartments to foreigners of quality with a grim satisfaction, as one who should say, “Let them be as French as they please; they shall pay their rent in English gold, or I shall know the reason why.” Her parlour was a museum of small china shepherdesses and memorial teapots, and her conversation was a running commentary upon the weather, the price of mutton, and the improvidence of the French nation.

On a certain foggy afternoon in November, when the lamps were already struggling with the darkness at four o’clock, and the mud in the street was of that peculiar consistency which seems to aspire to the condition of mortar, there came a knock at Mrs. Pumble’s door—a knock so brisk and foreign in its character that the good lady, who was toasting a muffin at the kitchen fire, set down the fork with a clatter and observed aloud, “That’s none of your English raps, I’ll be bound; that’s a Frenchman’s knock, and a Frenchman’s knock never brought good news to anybody.”

The visitor proved to be a small, spare man, dressed in a snuff-coloured coat, much frayed at the cuffs, and a hat of such ancient date that it might have been worn at the storming of the Bastille, or even earlier. His face was pale and pointed, his eyes were quick and restless, and his manner was that of a man who has learned to bow to everybody and to trust nobody. He announced himself as Monsieur Hyacinthe Barbet, of the village of Saint-Loup-sur-Dives, in Normandy, and desired to see his compatriot, Monsieur Dufresne, upon a matter of the most pressing and private nature.

Mrs. Pumble, who had a great genius for detecting a rogue, and who had never in her life been deceived by any man except her late husband, who had been a rogue of the first water, looked at him from head to foot with an expression of profound distrust, and said, “You’ll excuse me, sir, but I don’t like your looks, and I don’t like your hat, and I don’t like the way you’ve got of twisting your hands together as if you were wringing out a pocket-handkerchief. But you can come in, and I’ll fetch him down, and if you’ve got anything to say, you’d better say it before me, for I’m a widow, and widows see further into a bargain than other folks.”

Monsieur Dufresne presently descended, a thin, elegant, melancholy man, with a face like a faded miniature, and a manner compounded of the court and the dancing-school. He had not seen his native village for five years; he had not seen his brother, the unfortunate Étienne, for six; and when he heard the name of Saint-Loup, his countenance underwent a change so sudden and so touching that even Mrs. Pumble’s stony heart was moved to a momentary softening.

“My brother!” he cried. “You have news of my brother Étienne? He is alive? He is well? He is—ah, mon Dieu!—he is still in the prison of Caen?”

Monsieur Barbet bowed, and twisted his hands, and looked at the ceiling, and at the floor, and at the china shepherdesses, and finally at Mrs. Pumble, as if he would have preferred to address that lady, whose countenance was not encouraging.

“Monsieur,” said he, in French, which Mrs. Pumble did not understand, but which she listened to with the air of a magistrate taking down a deposition, “the news is both good and bad. Your brother lives. He is confined in the citadel of Caen, a prisoner of the Republic, accused of correspondence with the émigrés. But—and here is the matter of my visit—there is a certain official, a man of consideration in the town, who, for a consideration of a pecuniary nature, is willing to procure his release. The sum is not large—five hundred livres—but it must be paid promptly, and it must be paid to me, for I am the agent of this official, and I am obliged to return to Normandy without delay.”

Monsieur Dufresne’s eyes filled with tears. He clasped his hands together. He saw his brother already at liberty, already seated by his hearth, already embracing him. He was about to speak, when Mrs. Pumble, who had been listening with the air of a woman who does not understand a word and yet comprehends the whole, interposed in her own peculiar English, which was as broad as a barn-door and as blunt as a poker.

“Monsieur Dufresne,” said she, “I don’t know what this fellow’s been saying to you in his gibberish, but I know a swindler when I see one, and I see one now. Five hundred livres, indeed! And to be paid to him, and he obliged to return to Normandy without delay! Why, the man’s hat’s not worth five sous, and his coat’s not worth five more, and if he’s got a brother in a prison, I’ll be bound he’s the one that put him there.”

Monsieur Barbet, who understood English better than he chose to admit, turned pale, and then red, and then pale again, and protested with many gestures that he was an honest man, the father of a family, a good citizen, and a victim of calumny.
