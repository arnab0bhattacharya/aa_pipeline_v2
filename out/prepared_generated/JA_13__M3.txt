Miss Clara Ashworth had long enjoyed the satisfaction of supposing herself entirely superior to vanity. She was not, like her younger sister Lucy, open to every flutter of admiration, nor was she forever adjusting her bonnet in the reflection of a windowpane; she prided herself on a steady mind, a rational taste, and a calm indifference to the opinions of fashionable young men. It was therefore with a composure that approached the philosophical that she received the intelligence, conveyed by her aunt at tea, that a Mr. Frederick Vance of London had taken Hartfield Lodge for the summer.

Miss Ashworth listened to the news with an expression of mild civility, observing only that the neighbourhood was very pleasant and that she hoped he would find it agreeable. Lucy, by contrast, was all animation. She demanded to know whether Mr. Vance was handsome, whether he kept a carriage, and whether he was thought to be in possession of a fortune—all questions which their aunt, Mrs. Evelyn, was happy to answer in the affirmative, having already collected every particular from the housekeeper at Hartfield Lodge, who was her particular friend.

Mr. Vance was not merely handsome; he was fashionable in the extreme, possessed of an easy address and a ready wit that made him agreeable in any company. Within a fortnight of his arrival he had been introduced to the principal families of the neighbourhood, and had singled out Miss Ashworth for attentions that could not be mistaken. He walked with her at the Tuesday assembly, inquired after her reading with a solicitude that implied a genuine interest in her improvement, and once, in the course of a morning call, pronounced her conversation superior to anything he had met with in town.

This last compliment Clara received with a modest bow of the head, and afterwards, in the solitude of her own chamber, reflected upon it with considerable pleasure. She did not, she told herself, value his praise; she merely observed, as a matter of rational assessment, that Mr. Vance was a man of more discernment than the generality of his sex. If she thought of him more frequently than before, this was only because his remarks furnished her with subjects for useful contemplation. She was not vain; she was merely alive to the pleasure of being understood.

The neighbourhood, however, was less scrupulous in its interpretations. Mrs. Evelyn, who delighted in a matrimonial prospect as keenly as any woman of her years, began to speak of Mr. Vance as though his intentions were already declared. Lucy looked upon her sister with something between envy and admiration. Even the Miss Webbs, who had never before shown Clara anything but a cool civility, now paid her marked attention, in the hope, perhaps, of being introduced to Mr. Vance's London acquaintance.

Clara observed these changes with a quiet amusement that she did not altogether examine. It was agreeable to be sought after, and she could not but feel that the general improvement in her social consequence was a just recognition of her merits rather than the reflection of a gentleman's partiality. This distinction was important to her, and she returned to it often, as though the repetition of it might lend it a solidity it did not quite possess.

It was at the Elliotts' evening party, some three weeks into the acquaintance, that the affair reached its crisis. Mr. Vance was in high spirits, attentive as always, and Clara found herself laughing at his remarks with an ease that surprised her. She was beginning to feel that she understood him—that beneath his fashionable exterior there lay a warmth of character which the world had not yet discovered—when the younger Mr. Elliott, a youth of no great discretion, drew her aside with an air of great importance.

"Miss Ashworth," said he, in a voice lowered to what he supposed was a whisper, "do you know that Vance has laid a wager on you?"

Clara looked at him with a steadiness that concealed the sudden contraction of her heart. "I beg your pardon?"

"A wager. With Captain Bertram. Five guineas that he could secure your declared affection within a month of his arrival. I had it from Bertram himself, who thought it an excellent joke."

She said nothing for a moment. Her eye travelled across the room to where Mr. Vance stood, engaged in lively conversation with Mrs. Evelyn, his countenance wearing that expression of agreeable earnestness which she had lately found so affecting. She observed it now with the cold clarity of a woman who has been shown a picture from its reverse.

"I am obliged to you for the intelligence, Mr. Elliott," she said, with a composure that astonished him. "You can hardly suppose it of any importance to me."

She turned away and took up a position near the window, where she might be less exposed to notice. Her thoughts, however, were in considerable disorder. The wound was not merely to her affection, for she was not yet prepared to acknowledge that her affection had been seriously engaged; it was to her judgement. She had believed herself proof against flattery, had fancied that her admiration of Mr. Vance rested upon a rational estimation of his character, and now she discovered that she had been, all along, the object of a sport—a wager to be won, a name to be laid before a Captain Bertram for the price of five guineas.

The mortification was acute, but it was not the greater part of her suffering. The greater part was the recognition that she had deceived herself with a thoroughness no other person could have achieved. She had called her vanity discernment, her appetite for praise a rational taste for intelligent society, and her growing attachment a calm appreciation of merit. Every shift of language by which she had concealed her motives from herself now stood revealed in its full poverty, and she could not but feel that she deserved, in some measure, the very treatment she had received.
