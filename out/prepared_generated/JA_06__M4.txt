The morning room at Ashcombe Priory had, for three weeks together, presented the appearance of a fortress under a mild siege; and the besieging party, if one might judge by the frequency with which his card was left and his gig observed upon the lane, was Captain Thomas Ashby, late of His Majesty’s naval hospital at Portsmouth. Miss Harriet Ellwood, the heiress in question, had received these attentions with a composure which her friends called coldness and her enemies duplicity, but which was in truth the habitual guard of a woman who had been disappointed once and had resolved never to be surprised again.

She was five-and-twenty, and mistress of Ashcombe since her brother’s death at Trafalgar, a loss which had left her rich, solitary, and inclined to distrust the world’s compliments. She had refused three baronets and a clergyman, not from any settled aversion to matrimony, but because each proposal had seemed to her a bargain rather than an attachment; and the neighbourhood, having no other means of accounting for conduct so eccentric, had long since agreed that Miss Ellwood’s heart was buried with her brother, or that she had none at all.

Her guardian, Mr. Rowland Ellwood, a cousin of her father’s and a man of much property and little imagination, had viewed the captain’s advances with an alarm which he did not scruple to express.

“A surgeon!” said he, when the subject was first mentioned to him. “My dear Harriet, the man’s father was a chandler at Plymouth, and the man himself has been in the cockpit of a line-of-battle ship, sawing off legs. I grant you he has seen the world—but it is not the world we know. No family, no estate, nothing but his pay and his profession. It is not to be thought of.”

“I do not think of it,” said Harriet, calmly.

“You do not think of it—but you receive him.”

“He was my brother’s dearest friend, sir, and has been so these ten years. When William lay dying at Gibraltar, it was Captain Ashby who sat by him. I cannot shut my door against the one man who was kind to my brother at the last.”

Mr. Ellwood could not answer this, but he could and did mutter that gratitude was a very proper sentiment and had carried many a sensible woman further than she meant to go; and he took care to be present at every interview thereafter, and to speak of the captain’s prospects in a tone which was meant to be discouraging and which succeeded in being rude.

Captain Ashby, however, was not a man to be discouraged by a guardian. He was five-and-thirty, brown from long service in southern latitudes, and possessed of a manner at once so plain and so decided that it had the effect of making every other person in the room appear to be talking at random. He had no arts, but he had a certain blunt sincerity which Miss Ellwood had found refreshing in a county where every compliment was weighed and every civility measured; and if she did not encourage him, neither did she repulse him, and the neighbourhood, which had long since decided that she was incapable of feeling, now decided with equal confidence that she was encouraging a fortune-hunter.

“You are very good to sit with me,” said he one afternoon, when Mr. Ellwood had been called away upon business of the estate, and they were left alone for the first time in three weeks. “I know what your cousin thinks of me. I am not offended. If I had a daughter with a fine house and a good fortune, I should want something better for her than a man who has spent his life in a hospital.”

“You do yourself injustice,” said Harriet, with a composure she was far from feeling. “My brother thought there was no better man living.”

“Your brother was my friend. He saw me at my best—that is, he saw me at my work. It is easy to be thought well of when one is doing what one loves.” He paused, and looked at her with a directness which made her drop her eyes to her work. “I am not a young man, Miss Ellwood, and I have no pretty speeches. I came here to ask you to marry me, and I have been coming these three weeks because you have not said no. If it is your cousin’s opposition that keeps you silent, tell me so, and I will trouble you no more. If it is anything else—if it is that you do not like me—say it, and I will go. I am used to being told no. I have told a great many men no, in my time, and some of them died of it. I would rather have it plainly.”

Harriet laid down her needle. The room was very quiet; the clock upon the mantelpiece ticked with an emphasis which seemed to her quite personal. She had been asked this question before, in smoother phrases and with more elegant hesitation, and had answered it with an ease which had surprised herself. She found now that she could not answer it at all.

“You do me honour,” she said at last, “and I am not insensible of it. But I am not a young woman, Captain Ashby, and I have been used to order my own affairs. I do not know whether I could submit to be ordered by another.”

“I do not want to order you,” said he. “I want to sit in the same room with you of an evening, and hear what you have to say about the estate, and tell you about my patients, and know that when I come home there is someone there who is glad of it. I have been alone a great while, Miss Ellwood. I am not afraid of your fortune, and I am not ashamed of my poverty. I am only afraid of spending the rest of my life without you.”
