The request came to me on a Tuesday, in the interval between the geography lesson and the midday meal, and it was delivered by Madame Vervloet herself, who did not rise from her chair nor invite me to sit.

“Mademoiselle Ainsworth,” said she, “you are aware that my daughter Héloïse is of an age to require direction in her studies. I have observed your manner with the elder girls. You are firm. You are also—I speak frankly—without encumbrances. You have no family in the city, no suitors, no distractions. It is precisely this singleness of purpose that I require.”

I might have said that singleness of purpose was a virtue I had purchased at no small cost, and that she praised in me the very isolation she would have pitied in another; but I held my tongue. I had been three years in this school, in this low grey city of canals and bells, and I had learned that Madame Vervloet’s compliments were of the kind that come with a key, and the key fits a lock, and the lock is upon a door you had rather have left shut.

Héloïse Vervloet was fifteen, and she was clever in the way that a knife is clever. She had her mother’s pale, close-lidded eyes and a mouth that arranged itself into sweetness the instant a superior entered the room. On the first afternoon of our instruction she presented herself with her books in a strap, curtsied, and said, “I have heard you are very strict, mademoiselle. I hope I shall not disappoint you.”

“I hope you shall not,” said I. “Sit down. We begin with the verbs.”

For a fortnight she was faultless. Her translations were neat, her recitations musical, her silence in the hours between lessons so complete that I began to think I had been given a lamb instead of a wolf. But I have lived too long among wolves to be comforted by wool. I watched her. And on the fifteenth day I saw it: she had written in the margin of her exercise a small, precise line of Flemish, and beneath it, in English, its translation, and the translation was a mockery of my accent, my gown, my northern manner of pronouncing the letter *r*. She had not meant me to see it. Or she had meant me to see it and to say nothing—to swallow it, and so be her creature.

I did not swallow it. I kept her after the class and set the paper before her.

“You have a talent for satire, mademoiselle,” I said. “It is a pity to waste it upon a subject so dull as myself. Rewrite the passage in French, and let it be the praise of a person you sincerely admire. Your mother, perhaps.”

Her face did not change. “I did not write it to wound you,” she said. “It was a joke among the girls.”

“It was written in a lesson, in a book of grammar, and it was written of your instructress. That is not a joke; that is a small treachery, and treachery is a habit, and habits are not broken by indulgence. You will rewrite it.”

She rewrote it. But that evening, at the supper table, Madame Vervloet remarked, with a smile like the sheen on still water, that Héloïse had told her I had been “severe” that afternoon, and that she trusted severity would not harden into harshness, for her daughter was of a sensitive constitution. I saw the girl’s eyes flick to me and away, and I understood the game entire: she would be the martyr, and I the tyrant, and every just correction I made would be carried to her mother and there transmuted into evidence of my cruelty.

I confess that for some days I was at a loss. To be lenient was to be ruled; to be rigorous was to be accused. There is a species of warfare in which the enemy holds the ground of affection, and you fight uphill in the dark. I thought of my father’s house, of the cold hearth and the harsh voices, and of how I had learned there that the only armour against a liar is a truth so plainly spoken that it needs no second telling. So I resolved upon a course that had in it something of the gambler: I would be neither cruel nor yielding, but I would be *open*, and I would make her open herself.

The occasion came with a theft—a small one, a silver thimble belonging to a little Belgian girl named Cécile, which vanished from a work-basket and was found, three days after, in the lining of Héloïse’s reticule. She denied it with tears. She said the child had put it there from spite. The child cried; the school hummed; Madame Vervloet sent for me.

“You see how it is,” said she. “My daughter is persecuted. You, who are set over her, must protect her.”

“Madame,” said I, “I will do what I can to find the truth, and I will not pretend to find it before I have it. But I will not protect her from the truth, and neither, I think, would you, if you saw it plain.”

Her eyes narrowed. “You are bold, mademoiselle.”

“I am honest,” said I, “and honesty is often mistaken for boldness by those who have not the habit of it.”

I took Héloïse into the empty refectory and shut the door. The long windows showed the canal, grey and slow, and the rain making its small rings upon it. She stood with her hands folded, her face a mask of injured innocence.

“Héloïse,” said I, “look at me. Not at the floor. At me. I am going to tell you what I think, and you are going to answer me, and we shall not part until we have done.”

“You have always hated me,” she said.
