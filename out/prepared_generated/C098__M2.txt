The rain in Lausanne did not fall so much as it hovered, a persistent, grey mist that clung to the windowpanes of the Pension des Cygnes and turned the lake into a sheet of hammered lead. Countess Irina Volkonskaya sat by the fire, her hands wrapped around a cup of tea that had long since stopped steaming. She watched the droplets race down the glass, tracing the outlines of the mountains that should have been visible beyond the water but were now swallowed by the damp. It was a quiet life, a deliberate withdrawal from the noise of St. Petersburg, where the air tasted of coal smoke and ambition. Here, the air tasted of wet wool and old stone.

A knock at the door was not the tentative tap of a housekeeper, nor the brisk rap of a porter. It was a sharp, rhythmic pounding that seemed to vibrate through the floorboards. Irina looked up, her heart giving a singular, uncomfortable thump against her ribs. She knew that knock. It was the cadence of urgency, of a man who had learned to be efficient in a world that rewarded speed over grace.

When she opened the door, the smell of wet wool and tobacco drifted in with the man standing on the threshold. It was Alexei. He looked thinner than she remembered, his face hollowed out beneath the heavy, dark overcoat that was soaked through at the shoulders. His eyes, once warm and steady, were now bright with a feverish intensity that made Irina flinch.

"Alexei," she said, her voice barely above a whisper. She stepped back to let him in, closing the door firmly against the chill.

He did not sit. He stood in the center of the small parlor, his hands clasped behind his back, looking at her with an expression that was half apology, half demand. "Irina, forgive me for the intrusion. I could not wait for the morning."

"You look unwell," she said, reaching for the bell to summon a glass of brandy. "Sit, please. The fire is dying, but I can stoke it."

"No need for ceremony, Irina. We have little time." He glanced toward the window, where the mist was thickening. "The situation in the capital has changed. The strikes have turned into riots. The Tsar’s guard is failing. We are on the brink."

Irina lowered the bell. She looked at him, really looked at him, and saw the tremor in his hands. It was not fear, she realized, but adrenaline. It was the look of a man who had found a purpose so consuming that it had erased the rest of the world. She felt a profound sense of pity, followed closely by a cold, sharp fear. She had fled Russia to escape the shadows of her husband’s political machinations, to find peace in this neutral, wealthy enclave. She had no desire to be drawn back into the machinery of revolution, no matter how romantic it might sound in the salons of Paris.

"I don’t understand," she said, settling back into her chair. "You were his secretary, Alexei. You managed his correspondence, his schedules. You were the pillar of order in that chaotic household. Why are you here? And why do you speak of riots?"

"Because order is a lie, Irina. It is a mask worn by the privileged to hide the rot beneath." He took a step closer, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "We have men who are ready. Soldiers, workers, students. But we lack the means. We need funds to move the pamphlets, to bribe the officers, to secure the weapons. We need a patron who cares about the future of Russia more than the preservation of the old dynasty."

Irina felt a wave of nausea. She looked at the comfortable armchair she occupied, the crystal decanter on the side table, the soft rug under her feet. These were not symbols of rot, to her. They were symbols of safety. She had spent years navigating the treacherous waters of high society, smiling through insults, enduring the cold indifference of her husband, Maxim, who had treated her as another asset to be managed. And now, this man, whom she had once trusted to guard their privacy, stood there demanding her fortune for a cause she did not believe in.

"I have no money," she lied. It was a small, pathetic lie, but it was all she had. "My allowance is modest. I live on a pension."

Alexei’s face hardened. "Irina, do not insult me. You know the Volkonskaya accounts. You know what is held in the Zurich bank. You know the value of the art in the St. Petersburg mansion. This is not about your personal comfort. It is about history. We are the vanguard of a new age. If you refuse, you are choosing the side of the tyrants."

"I am choosing survival," she said, her voice trembling. "I am choosing to stay alive. I have seen what happens to those who oppose the current, Alexei. I have seen the fear in my husband’s eyes. I have seen the way the secret police watched us. I am tired of fear."

"Then you are a coward," he said, the words slicing through the air.

The silence that followed was heavy. Irina looked at him and felt a strange, detached sadness. She remembered the day they had first met, when he was a young man with ink-stained fingers and a shy smile. He had been kind to her when she was lonely, bringing her books, listening to her complaints about Maxim. She had loved him, in a quiet, unspoken way that had never been allowed to bloom. Now, that love felt like a ghost, haunting the room, tangled up with this demand.

"I will think about it," she said.

"There is no time to think. The window is closing. If we do not act within the week, the movement will stall. The Tsar will crush us." He turned to leave, pausing at the door. "Remember, Irina. When the smoke clears, there will be those who remember who stood with them, and those who stood aside. Choose carefully."
