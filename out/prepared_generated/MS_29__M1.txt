It was in the autumn of the year 183— that I first beheld the face of Alice, the daughter of my tenant, Elias Thorne. I had grown old in a solitude so profound that the very silence of my halls had become to me a living companion, and the decay of my house was but the outward and visible sign of the ruin within my own breast. The ancient manor of Thornwood, with its ivy-choked battlements and its windows like vacant eyes, stood as a monument to a life that had been, and was no more. I had outlived all whom I had loved; wife, children, friends, had passed before me into the great darkness, leaving me the sole and wretched remnant of a forgotten world. My heart was a sepulchre, and I had long ceased to mourn the dead that lay within it.

She came one evening, when the wind moaned about the gables and the last light of day bled across the fells, bringing a basket of provisions her father had sent. She stood in the great, shadowy hall, her simple dress and her young, earnest face a startling intrusion of life into my mausoleum. There was a purity in her gaze, a gentle and fearless compassion, that pierced the armour of my long-nurtured misanthropy. She did not shrink from my gaunt figure or the melancholy that I knew must surround me like a shroud. She spoke of the coming winter, of the state of the roads, of simple, mortal things, and her voice was like a forgotten melody, sweet and full of a life I had thought extinguished upon the earth.

She began to visit me, at first with the pretext of some small errand, and then, as a strange and unlooked-for intimacy grew between us, without pretext at all. She would sit by the great hearth in the library, where the fire threw long, dancing shadows upon the towering shelves of books, and she would listen as I spoke. At first my discourse was but of trifles—the history of the house, the names of the hills, the lore of the countryside—but soon I found myself drawn, by the very magnetism of her sympathy, into the deeper and more dreadful chronicles of my own existence. It was a relief I had never imagined, this pouring forth of a story that had been locked within me for half a century, a tale of such misery that I had never dared to breathe it to another living soul.

“It is a long and mournful history, my child,” I said to her one evening, as the rain lashed against the leaded panes. “A history of a happiness so divine, that its destruction was all the more absolute and terrible. I was not always this withered creature, this ruin you see before you. There was a time when my heart was full of a love so boundless, so all-consuming, that I believed it a portion of the eternal and the sublime. I loved a being, Alice, who was to me as the sun to the day, as the very air to my lungs. Her name was Isabella, and her beauty was but the faintest reflection of the celestial spirit that dwelt within her. We were young, and the world was ours; its mountains were our kingdom, its starry heavens our canopy. Every moment was a draught of enchantment, and I, in my foolish arrogance, believed that such felicity was my birthright, a state from which I could never fall.”

I paused, my old voice trembling with the force of these long-buried memories. Alice looked at me, her eyes wide and luminous in the firelight, and in that moment she seemed to me a spirit sent to bear witness to my suffering. I felt the chains of my solitude, which had for so long felt as heavy and natural as my own bones, begin to loosen. I was no longer alone; my grief had an auditor, and in the telling of it, I felt a strange and painful exaltation.

“Alas!” I continued, my voice sinking to a whisper of anguish. “I knew not then the fragility of all earthly joy, the bitter dregs that lie at the bottom of the sweetest cup. A son was born to us, a boy in whom I saw the renewal of my own life and the perfection of his mother’s virtues. He was the very embodiment of our love, a living bond between us, and for a time our happiness was so complete that it seemed to mock the very notion of sorrow. But a shadow fell upon that bright morning of my life. A fever, swift and merciless, descended upon our home. It took the boy first, my beautiful Edward, my hope, my future—and then, in her frantic grief, it laid its cold hand upon his mother. I was left in the space of a single, dreadful week, the sole inhabitant of a world that had become a desert. The sun still shone, the mountains still stood in their sublime grandeur, but they were now but the cold and indifferent witnesses to a desolation that no tongue could tell, no mind conceive.”

The words tore from my breast as if they were living things, and I felt the old agony rise within me, as fresh and terrible as on the day it was born. I looked at the young girl before me, and I saw a single tear trace a path down her cheek. In that silent offering of her pity, I felt a communion more profound than any I had known since the days of my lost happiness. The story was not yet ended, for there were further depths of woe to plumb, further betrayals to relate, but in that moment, by the warmth of that fire and the gentle presence of my young listener, I felt that the long, dark winter of my soul had, at last, found a single, flickering candle.
