The sound began with the gas lamps. As the last of the day’s commerce faded from the pavements of Hatton Garden and the great iron grilles were drawn across the shopfronts, a different kind of industry commenced. From the floor of Elara Vance’s small room above Blackwood & Son, Fine Jewellers, came the hammering. It was not the robust, declarative clang of a blacksmith, but a patient, insistent tapping, the sound of metal being persuaded, not beaten. Night after night, it was the metronome to her studies, a sound as much a part of the London gloom as the rattle of the milk cart at dawn or the low moan of the foghorns on the Thames.

Elara, a student at the Royal Academy with a penchant for capturing the play of light on tarnished silver, found the noise strangely soothing. She would set her easel by the window, the gaslight sputtering on the sill, and try to paint the rhythm of it. The *tink-tink-tink* was a sound of creation, of tiny, precise forces shaping something new. She imagined the jeweller, a man named Alistair Blackwood, bent over his workbench, a loupe screwed into one eye, coaxing a forgotten pocket watch back to life. He was a quiet, slender man with fingers long and pale as a pianist’s, and when she paid her rent to him each month, he would nod his thanks with a gravity that made her feel she was commissioning a portrait, not simply paying for a room.

One evening, the rain was coming down in sheets, blurring the gaslights into watery stars. Elara needed more turpentine for her oils and descended the narrow, creaking stairs to the shop. The bell above the door gave a faint, apologetic jingle. The air inside was thick with the scent of metal polish and old wood, a library of silent, gleaming treasures. Mr. Blackwood was behind the counter, polishing a silver snuffbox with a soft cloth.

“Mr. Blackwood,” she began, her voice a little hesitant. “I was wondering… the noise. The hammering, at night. I don’t mean to complain, it’s just… I was curious.”

He stopped his polishing, his gaze lifting to meet hers. His eyes were a pale, washed-out grey. “The clocks, Miss Vance,” he said, his voice soft as felt. “The great and small mechanics of time. They are temperamental things. They require a delicate touch, and often, a patient ear, long after the world has gone to sleep.” He smiled, a thin, fleeting expression that did not quite reach his eyes. “I find the silence of the night is their most honest confidante.”

She accepted this, of course. It was a romantic, fitting explanation for a man who lived amongst such intricate beauty. She bought her turpentine and returned to her room, the image of him tending to the wounded heart of a longcase clock fixed in her mind. The hammering that night seemed to have a new resonance, the sound not just of repair, but of resurrection.

For a week, all was as it should be. Then, one Tuesday, the silence came. Elara was sketching the intricate shadows cast by a candelabra when she realised the floor beneath her was still. The absence of the sound was more jarring than its presence had ever been. It was a hole in the fabric of the night. She waited, her charcoal poised over the paper. Ten minutes passed, then twenty. Nothing. The quiet was absolute, profound, and deeply unsettling.

The next morning, the shop was closed. The sign that usually read ‘Open’ hung askew, and the great iron grille remained drawn. There was no sign of Mr. Blackwood. The day after, it was the same. And the day after that. A fine layer of dust began to settle on the brass letterbox. The silence from the floor below was no longer peaceful; it was a void.

On the third day of the silence, Elara went to the local constabulary. She was directed to a small, stuffy office where a man with a weary face and ink-stained cuffs introduced himself as Inspector Davies. He listened to her story with a patience that seemed worn thin by a thousand such tales.

“So, the jeweller is gone,” he said, not as a question.

“And the hammering has stopped,” Elara added, feeling foolish as the words left her mouth. “It’s just… it was so constant. So reliable. This silence feels… wrong.”

Davies sighed, rubbing his temples. “Madam, a man in Hatton Garden closing up shop and taking a sudden holiday is not, in itself, a crime. He is a clock-repairer, you say? A man of delicate parts. Perhaps he required a rest cure.”

But Elara felt a prickle of unease, an artist’s intuition for a composition that was fundamentally flawed. “His tools are still there, aren’t they? In the workshop? I could hear them. He wouldn’t leave his tools.”

Davies’s interest flickered. He agreed to accompany her. He had a key from the landlord, and the lock turned with a loud, disconsolate groan. The shop was as she remembered, but colder, the air stale. Davies moved behind the counter and pushed open a small door she had never noticed. It led to a narrow back room, a workshop. And here, the neat order of the shop gave way to chaos.

There were no clocks. Not a single one. There were no delicate little cogs and springs laid out on velvet. Instead, the room was filled with the tools of a far heavier trade. A small anvil, scarred and blackened, sat in the centre of the room. Heavy hammers, tongs, and files were scattered across a workbench stained with dark, greasy smudges. The floor was littered with a fine, glittering dust and a scattering of what looked like lead filings. The air smelled not of brass and oil, but of hot metal and something acrid, like scorched stone.
