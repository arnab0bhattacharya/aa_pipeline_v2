Captain Elias Thorne stood on the bridge of the *Aurelia*, and his soul recoiled. The brass telegraph, the polished wheel, the very air of the enclosed space—it was all an affront. He was a man of canvas and hemp, of the wind’s raw whisper in the rigging and the honest groan of oak in a heavy sea. This vessel, with its iron bones and its coal-breathing heart, was a monstrosity. Below decks, in the fiery pit of the engine room, men like Chief Engineer Mallory tended a furnace that Thorne considered a necessary evil at best, a pact with the devil at worst. He trusted the wind. He did not trust a boiler.

The Bay of Biscay, however, was in a foul mood, and it cared nothing for a captain’s prejudices. The storm had come upon them with a sudden, vicious fury, a shrieking demon of wind and water that turned the world to a churning grey chaos. The *Aurelia* pitched and rolled, her steel hull groaning in a way that was alien to Thorne’s ears. It was a sound of distress, not the sturdy complaint of a wooden ship. On the bridge, the helmsman fought the wheel, his knuckles white. Thorne, his old oilskin slick with spray, peered into the maelstrom, his gut a knot of cold dread. He felt blind, deaf, and helpless, relying on the thrum of the engines beneath his feet to push them through the monstrous seas.

And then, the thrumming stopped.

It was not a gradual fade but an abrupt, shocking silence, as if the ship’s heart had simply given out. The *Aurelia* shuddered, yawed violently to port, and lay dead in the water, at the mercy of the towering waves. For a terrifying moment, there was only the roar of the wind and the crash of the sea. Thorne’s prejudice curdled into a cold, hard fury.

“Mr. Evans!” he bellowed, his voice barely cutting through the gale. The young third officer appeared at his side, his face pale. “My compliments to Mr. Mallory. Tell him the engines have stopped. Tell him I want them started again, now!”

The minutes that followed were an eternity. The ship was being driven sideways toward the jagged teeth of the Biscay coast, a lee shore that had swallowed a thousand ships. Each wave that broke over the side sent a shudder through the vessel, and Thorne could feel the sea’s malevolent intent. He was a master of sail, but here, with no canvas to set, he was a useless figurehead. The power lay below, in the hands of the man he had treated with barely concealed contempt.

Evans returned, his oilskin dripping. “Captain, Mr. Mallory says… he says the main steam pipe has fractured. It’s a major failure. He’s got his men down there now, but it’s a mess.”

“A mess?” Thorne snarled. “We’re in a mess, Mr. Evans! We’re drifting onto the Merrow Rocks! Did you tell him that?” He didn’t wait for an answer. “I’ll see to it myself.”

He stormed from the bridge, descending the iron ladders into the ship’s belly. The heat and noise of the engine room, even in its silence, were overwhelming. The air was thick with the smell of hot metal, oil, and sweat. The great pistons were still, the massive machinery a dead, dark beast. In the dim light, he saw Mallory, a stocky, grey-bearded man in a stained undershirt, directing a team of stokers and mechanics. They were wrestling with a massive, lagged pipe, their bodies slick with grime and perspiration.

Mallory looked up, his face streaked with soot and exhaustion. There was no deference in his eyes, only a grim focus. “Captain. You shouldn’t be down here.”

“I’ll go where I please on my own ship, Mr. Mallory. What is the delay? I need power!”

“And you’ll get it when it’s safe to give it,” Mallory shot back, his voice flat and hard. “That pipe’s carrying steam at two hundred pounds. It’s split wide open. If we try to force it, it’ll blow and scald every man in this room to death. We’re cutting out the bad section and fitting a clamp. It’s delicate work in this roll.”

Thorne felt a hot surge of impotent rage. “Safe? We’ll be safe when we’re dashed against the rocks! Your precious engines have failed us, Mallory. This is what comes of trusting machinery over the wind and the skill of seamen.”

Mallory straightened up, wiping his hands on a rag. He was a full head shorter than Thorne, but he met the captain’s gaze without flinching. “The sea doesn’t care for your wind or my engines, Captain. It just wants to kill us. And right now, the only thing that can save us is this machinery. So unless you can whistle up a fair breeze from the nor’west, I suggest you let me and my men do our job.”

The words hung in the stifling air, a direct challenge. For a moment, Thorne wanted to strike him. But he saw the truth in the engineer’s eyes. He saw the raw competence, the desperate focus of the men around him. They were not cowards or fools; they were fighting a battle he did not understand, in a language he could not speak. He was the master of the ship, but they were the masters of its heart. The sea crashed against the hull, a stark reminder of their peril.

Shame, cold and unwelcome, washed over him. He had spent his life commanding men by the force of his will and his knowledge of the sea. Here, his knowledge was useless. His authority was a hollow shell. He was a stranger in his own command.

He took a breath, the air hot and metallic in his lungs. “What do you need?” he asked, his voice quieter now, the anger drained away.

Mallory blinked, surprised. “Sir?”

“What do you need, Mr. Mallory? More men? Light?”
