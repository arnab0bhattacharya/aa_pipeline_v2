It was a source of no small satisfaction to Arthur Pemberton that he should arrive in Hollingford upon the very day of his father’s interment, for the journey from London had occupied him a full two days and a night, and he had begun to fear that the last offices of filial duty would be denied him. The satisfaction, however, was of that purely intellectual kind which a man takes in the successful execution of a plan, and it sat strangely upon him as he descended from the coach, his London boots already gathering the damp mud of the Trent-side town. He was conscious of a profound hollowness beneath this sense of fitness; the grief he felt for his father was a real thing, but it was an old, familiar shape of sorrow, worn smooth by the long expectation of the news. His father, stout Thomas Pemberton, had been dying for the better part of two years, and his death was less a sudden blow than the final, inevitable chapter of a story they had all been reading with heavy hearts.

The real shock, the true source of the ague that seemed to have taken possession of his limbs, was not the death, but the life that was left. He had pictured his mother in the old tannery house, a widow indeed, but still mistress of the yard and the dwelling, with the familiar smells of oak-bark and hides a comfort to her in her sorrow. Instead, he found her not in her own parlour, but in a small, upstairs room at the house of her cousin, a Mr. Silas Croft, a man of rigid principles and narrow sympathies who dealt in seed-corn and scripture with an equal lack of imagination. The tannery yard, with its vats and its drying-lofts and its very bricks, had been sold over his father’s head six months before to a rival firm from Nottingham. The furniture had followed. His mother, who had been the mistress of a thriving business, was now a pensioner on the grudging charity of a man who never let her forget it.

As he sat opposite her in the little parlour, while Mr. Croft’s clock ticked away the seconds of her dependency, Arthur felt the full weight of his situation descend upon him. His mother, a woman whose placid composure had been the bedrock of his childhood, now possessed a fragile, hunted look. She spoke of his father’s illness and the funeral arrangements with a quiet dignity that was more moving than any display of tears, but her eyes, when they met his, held a desperate question she could not bring herself to ask. She was a woman of the old school, taught to endure and to defer; the catastrophe that had befallen her was, to her mind, a visitation of Providence, to be borne with patience and a clean apron.

It was Mr. Croft who supplied the answer to her unspoken question. A man whose face seemed to have been assembled from spare parts of an unyielding machine, he explained the situation to Arthur after the meagre supper, his voice as dry as the seed-corn in his warehouse. “Your father, God rest his soul, was a good man, but he was a man of impulse. He over-extended himself. The yard is gone, the house is gone. Your mother has nothing but the clothes she stands up in and the small sum I have been able to set aside for her. She is welcome here, of course; she is my wife’s cousin. But a man must look to his own first. And you, young sir, with your fine London wage… you are her natural provider now.”

The words fell upon Arthur with the force of a physical blow. He looked at his small salary—not so small for a single man in London, enough for his lodgings, his books, and the slow, careful accumulation of the knowledge that was to be his fortune—as a captain looks at a sinking vessel. It was to be the foundation of a new life, a life of the mind, far from the provincial world of tanning and trade. He had ambitions, not of wealth, but of consequence; he meant to make his name in the world of letters, perhaps as a critic, perhaps as a scholar. His salary was the seed of that future. To use it to support his mother would be to water a barren patch of earth with it, to see it absorbed and vanish without trace.

He walked out into the town that evening, the decision an impossible weight in his breast. The market square was quiet, the stalls put away. The river Trent, a sluggish, brown artery, flowed past the stone bridge, indifferent to the human dramas enacted upon its banks. He saw his two futures as clearly as if they were two roads diverging before him. One led back to London, to his books and his ambitions, leaving his mother to the cold charity of Silas Croft, a slow erosion of her spirit that he could already foresee. The other led nowhere but to this town, to a clerkship perhaps, to a life of pinched economies and thwarted aspirations, all in the service of a duty that felt both sacred and ruinous. It was a terrible calculus, in which filial love and personal ambition were set against one another like opposing forces in a physical equation, and he, the soul caught between them, felt himself being torn asunder.

He thought of his father, a man of great warmth and little foresight, whose love for him had been as simple and abundant as the water in the tanning pits. His father had encouraged his bookishness, had paid for his apprenticeship with a pride that was almost painful to witness. “Get on, my boy, get on,” he would say. “Be more than a tanner. Use your head.” And now, his head, the very instrument his father had so prized, was to be used for nothing more than the dull arithmetic of survival.
