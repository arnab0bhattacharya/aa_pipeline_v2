The note was a slip of cheap, grey paper, the kind used for fish-and-chip wrappings, yet the hand that had written upon it was a surprisingly elegant copperplate. Mr. Alistair Finch, manager of the Imperial Lyceum, held it at arm's length, as if the ink itself might be infectious. The message was stark: *Do not let the Nightingale sing on opening night. Her song will be cut short.*

He had found it tucked into the morning post, amidst invoices and fawning letters from admirers of his leading lady, Miss Eleonora Vance. The "Nightingale," as the critics had dubbed her. Finch crumpled the paper and tossed it into the wastebasket. It was the work of some jealous rival, no doubt. Perhaps Mrs. Gable, whose own star had waned so spectacularly since Eleonora’s arrival in the West End two seasons ago. Spite was a currency in this business, traded as freely as applause. He dismissed the thought and turned his mind to the pressing matter of the third-act costume, which was proving to be a nightmare of feathers and corsetry.

Later that afternoon, as a grey London drizzle slicked the cobbles of Drury Lane, Finch descended to the stage door for a word with the doorman. Barnaby was a relic from a previous era of theatre, a man whose face was a roadmap of forgotten plays and whose loyalty to the Lyceum was absolute. He sat on his stool, a cup of tea steaming in his hands, his eyes missing nothing.

“Barnaby,” Finch began, adjusting his waistcoat. “Anything out of the ordinary?”

Barnaby took a slow sip of his tea. “There’s always something out of the ordinary in a theatre, sir. But there was a man. Yesterday afternoon and again this morning. Just stood across the lane, by the tobacconist’s. Watching.”

“Watching the theatre? Probably a hopeful wanting an audition.”

“No, sir,” Barnaby said, setting his cup down with a definitive click. “He wasn't looking at the posters or the doors. He was looking at the building. Studying the old wall where the new fly system is being installed. And his eyes kept going down, to the cellar grating. Where the trapdoor machinery is.”

A cold finger, unrelated to the damp, traced a line down Finch’s spine. He thought again of the note. *Her song will be cut short.* The stage of the Lyceum was a creature of wood and iron, a complex beast with a heart of ropes, pulleys, and counterweights. And at its centre, for the climax of *The Crimson Waltz*, was a large, hydraulically operated trapdoor, set to swallow the villain and, in the final moments, to rise again, bearing Eleonora aloft in a triumphant apotheosis. A failure of that mechanism at the wrong moment would be… catastrophic.

“What did he look like?” Finch asked, his voice tighter than he intended.

“Middle height. Dark coat, bowler hat. Kept his collar up. Couldn’t see his face proper. But he had a stillness about him. Not like a workman or a gawker. Like a man measuring for a coffin.”

That evening, Finch found himself unable to settle. The chatter and clatter of the dress rehearsal were a familiar comfort, but the note and Barnaby’s report had woven a thread of dread through the fabric of the familiar. He needed a professional eye, a mind untainted by theatrical hysteria. He sent a boy with a terse message to a discreet address in Bloomsbury.

The man who arrived an hour later was utterly incongruous with the world of greasepaint and gaslight. He was tall and thin, dressed in a slightly frayed tweed suit that spoke of quiet country walks, not London intrigue. His name was Mr. Silas Croft, an inquiry agent Finch had used once before to trace a thieving set painter. Croft’s most notable feature was his complete lack of one; he was a man designed to be overlooked.

“Mr. Finch,” he said, his voice a low murmur. “You have a problem of a… theatrical nature?”

Finch explained the situation, producing the crumpled note. Croft examined it with the detached interest of a geologist studying a rock. “Cheap paper. Good penmanship. The threat is poetic, which suggests either a flair for the dramatic or a genuine connection to the victim’s world.” He looked up. “I will require a ticket for the dress rehearsal. A seat in the upper circle, I think. One can see the stage, but also the wings, the fly-loft, and the audience. The entire ecosystem.”

Finch provided him with a ticket and a seat in the shadows. Croft, a phantom in the gloom, watched the rehearsal unfold. He ignored the soaring notes of Miss Vance, the thunderous declamations of Mr. Sterling as the villain, and the desperate romance of the plot. His eyes, sharp and patient, scanned not the performance, but the machinery of it. He watched the stagehands, their movements economical and practiced. He noted the burly man, Kavanagh, who operated the complex levers for the trapdoor from a sunken alcove stage-left. He saw Kavanagh’s thick hands move with a surprising deftness, but also noted the sweat on his brow and the nervous glances he cast towards the wings.

As the third act began, Croft’s gaze sharpened. He watched the villain’s dramatic demise, the trapdoor opening with a smooth, silent efficiency. Then, the finale. The music swelled. Eleonora Vance, a vision in white silk and feathers, moved to the centre of the stage, directly over the sealed trap. Her voice, clear and pure as a bell, filled the vast space.

Croft did not watch her. He watched Kavanagh. He saw the man’s knuckles turn white on the main release lever. He saw his eyes dart not to the stage, but to the rafters above. And in that moment, Croft saw it. A faint, almost imperceptible glint of metal high in the flies, where a sandbag should have been. It was not a sandbag. It was a counterweight, and it was rigged with a secondary line, a tripwire, that ran down to a hook near Kavanagh’s station.
