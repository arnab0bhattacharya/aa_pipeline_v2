The house had given her the room at the top of the east wing, where the light came in sideways through a tall window and lay across the floor in the morning like a bolt of silk unrolled, and it was here, among the last of the books—the ones no one had thought worth carrying down to the library proper—that Clara found the first of them. A volume of Cowper, its spine cracked and softened with handling, and on the flyleaf, in a hand so young it seemed to lean forward with eagerness, the words: *E. M. from her father, Christmas, 1861.* And then, beneath, in pencil, fainter, the same hand grown a little steadier: *The poplars by the water are all bare and I am very dull. I have read the Task four times and I think the sofa is the only honest thing in it.*

Clara sat down on the window-seat with the book in her lap and looked out at the poplars, which were indeed bare, and at the water, which lay flat and grey beneath a sky of the same colour, and it seemed to her that she had been admitted to something—a confidence, a small warm conspiracy—and that the girl who had written those words (Ellen, she would learn; Ellen Mary, the eldest) was not dead at all but merely waiting, in the manner of the pencilled and the marginal, for someone patient enough to hear her.

The house was called Ashby Ferrers and it had come, in the spring, to a Mr. Roland Ferrers, who was something in the City and who came down only at weekends, bringing with him a smell of trains and cigars and a brisk impatience with the past. It was he who had engaged her—engaged her, that is, to catalogue the library, to make an inventory of the books, and he had said, standing in the great hall with his hat still in his hand as though he might at any moment be called away, that he didn't suppose there was anything in it worth the trouble, but his solicitors had advised it, and one must do things properly. He had a way of looking at the portraits on the stairs as if they were tenants who had failed to pay their rent. Clara had thought, but had not said, that the house did not much like him.

But the house liked her, or so she fancied, and the books liked her better still. They gave up their secrets to her one by one, in the long afternoons, with the rain on the windows and the fire (for the spring was cold) spitting in the grate. In a volume of sermons—the Reverend Somebody, 1858—she found a soldier's notes, written in a cramped and hurried hand upon the endpapers: *Lt. J. Ferrers. Aldershot. The 14th of June. If I am killed let this be sent to my mother and tell her I was not afraid, only sorry.* And then, in the same hand, a month later, a year later, she could not tell: *I was not killed. I am not sorry. I am afraid.* She sat with that for a long while. The rain came down. Somewhere in the back of the house a door opened and closed, and a servant's voice went lilting down a corridor, and the fire made its small collapsing sound, and Clara thought: he was here, in this room, with this book, and he was afraid, and no one knew.

The mother's book was different. It was a Bible, a great heavy thing with a brass clasp, and inside it, pressed flat, were the records of a household: *Alice, teething, June 1872. Ned, the croup, November. Alice, the fever, and Dr. Merton very kind. Ned better. Alice better. Baby Charles, who was not strong, taken to the sea at Easter and came back brown as a berry.* The entries stopped in 1879. Clara turned the pages and found, at the back, in a hand that had grown thin and careful, a single line: *He is gone and I cannot write any more.* She closed the Bible and held it against her breast as though it were a child, and the light through the window had moved round to the other wall, and the room was full of a great tenderness and a great silence, and she thought: I know you. I know you all. I know you better than he does, who has your name and your money and does not know that Ned was afraid or that Alice had the fever or that Ellen was dull among the poplars.

Mr. Ferrers came down at the weekend and asked her, over tea in the morning-room, whether she had found anything of interest. She said that the library was very complete. She said that there were some family papers among the books. He said, "Oh, yes?" and looked at his watch, and then he said that his great-grandfather had been a great one for books, and that his grandfather had let the place go to rack and ruin, and that his father had never come near it, and that he himself—here he laughed, a short laugh, and set down his cup—he himself had not the time to read, and never had had. "But it's a fine house," he said, and looked about the room as if he were appraising it. "It will do very well."

She wanted to say: your great-grandfather's daughter was dull and wrote verses in her Cowper. Your great-uncle was afraid and no one knew. Your great-grandmother could not write any more. But she said nothing, and he said that he supposed she would be finished by the end of the month, and she said that she supposed she would, and he took up his hat and his stick and went out into the hall, and she heard him say to the butler something about the drains.
