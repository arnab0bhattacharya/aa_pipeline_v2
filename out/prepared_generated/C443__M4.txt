The billet was a room above the saddler’s, and its single window looked down the length of Mill Street, where the smoke from the mill chimneys hung low and yellow against a sky the colour of wet slate. Captain Thomas Ashby had been in Marrowfield a fortnight, and in that time he had come to know the rhythm of the place: the clatter of the looms starting before dawn, the bell that called the hands, the shuffle of clogs on cobbles at dusk. He had also come to know the unease that ran beneath it all, a thin current of grievance that could, if the wind turned, become something worse.

He was a militia officer, not a regular soldier, and he felt the difference keenly. His commission had come through the influence of his father, a linen merchant in Leeds, and he wore his scarlet coat with a self-consciousness that had not yet worn off. The men under his command were local volunteers, shopkeepers and farmhands who drilled on Thursdays and returned to their trades on Fridays. They were not, he suspected, much use in a riot, but they were what the magistrates had given him, and he was determined to do his duty.

The order came on a Tuesday morning, delivered by a servant from the magistrate’s house at the top of the hill. It was a single sheet of paper, folded and sealed, and it named one Josiah Fenton, weaver, of Back Lane, as a person suspected of seditious assembly and the distribution of inflammatory pamphlets. Captain Ashby was to arrest him and bring him before the magistrate by noon.

Ashby read the order twice, then folded it and put it in his pocket. He had heard the name Fenton before, in the way that one hears names in a small place: a murmur in the taproom, a reference in a sermon. Fenton was a weaver of some standing, a man who had once been a foreman at the mill and had left, or been dismissed, under circumstances that no one seemed able to agree upon. He was said to be a Methodist, or a Baptist, or something of that sort, and to have a daughter who taught at the Sunday school.

It was this last detail that gave Ashby pause. His own sister, Lucy, was thirteen and had been sent to Marrowfield for her health, to stay with their aunt, and she attended the Sunday school at the chapel on Well Lane. She had spoken of it often in her letters, which arrived weekly and were full of the small dramas of childhood: a girl who had been given a prize for recitation, a boy who had fallen into the millpond and been rescued by a passing carter. And she had mentioned, more than once, a teacher she admired, a Miss Fenton, who was kind and patient and who had taught her to write her name in a fair round hand.

Ashby had not connected the name until now. He stood at the window and looked down at the street, where a cart was being loaded with sacks of flour and a dog was nosing at the gutter. He thought of Lucy, with her ribbons and her chatter, and he thought of the weaver in Back Lane, and he felt the first cold touch of a dilemma that was not, strictly speaking, his to resolve.

He took two men with him, as the order required, and they walked down Mill Street and turned into Back Lane, which was narrow and steep and smelled of coal smoke and wet wool. The Fenton house was at the end, a low stone building with a loom-house attached, its windows small and grimy. Ashby knocked, and after a moment the door was opened by a girl of perhaps sixteen, with dark hair pulled back under a plain cap and a face that was serious and composed.

“Miss Fenton?” he said.

She looked at his uniform, then at the men behind him, and her composure did not falter. “You’ll be wanting my father,” she said.

“I am.”

“He’s in the loom-house.” She stepped aside to let them pass, and Ashby followed her through a dark parlour, where a fire burned low in the grate and a Bible lay open on the table, into the room beyond. The loom-house was cold and close, and the loom itself took up most of the space, its beams and treadles and heddles a complex architecture of wood and thread. Josiah Fenton sat at the loom, his hands moving with the steady rhythm of long practice, and he did not stop when Ashby entered.

“Mr. Fenton,” Ashby said, “I have an order for your arrest.”

The weaver’s hands paused, and he looked up. He was a man of fifty or so, with a lean face and grey hair and eyes that were remarkably clear. “On what charge?”

“Sedition. The distribution of inflammatory pamphlets.”

Fenton was silent for a moment. Then he said, “I have distributed no pamphlets. I have spoken at meetings, as is my right, and I have prayed with my neighbours, as is my duty. If that is sedition, then I am guilty.”

“I am not the judge of that,” Ashby said. “I am only ordered to bring you before the magistrate.”

Fenton looked at him for a long moment, and Ashby felt the weight of that gaze, which was not hostile but assessing, as if the weaver were trying to determine what manner of man he was. Then Fenton rose from the loom and said, “I will come quietly. I would not have my daughter see me dragged through the street.”

The daughter had followed them into the loom-house and stood now by the door, her hands clasped in front of her. She did not speak, but her eyes went from her father to Ashby and back again, and Ashby saw in them a fear that she was trying hard to master.
