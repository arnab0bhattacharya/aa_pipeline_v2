The house of my uncle, Mr. Fenwick, stood upon a promontory of the Caernarvonshire coast, where the mountains of Eryri, descending in dark and shattered steps, cast their shadows upon a sea for ever vexed by the western gales. It was a dwelling of grey stone, built in a former age for strength rather than for beauty, and the winds that rushed down from the hills in winter, shrieking among the chimneys, seemed to me, in the solitude of my girlhood, the voices of the drowned mariners whose bones lay in the caverns beneath our windows. Thither had my uncle retired from the world before my birth, and there had I been nurtured in a seclusion so complete, that the great drama of human life was known to me only as a tale recited by the firelight, and the passions that agitate mankind appeared to me rather the phantoms of a poet’s brain than the true and terrible inheritance of our nature.

I was accustomed to roam at will among the hills, and to sit for hours upon the edge of the cliffs, watching the vast Atlantic, and listening to the solemn monotone of the breakers, which, in their eternal recurrence, seemed to me the very pulse of the great heart of the universe. My uncle loved me, I believe, with an affection deep and watchful, yet ever shaded by a melancholy that I could not penetrate. He never spoke of his past; of my parents he had told me only that they were dead, and that I was committed to his guardianship by the last injunctions of a dying sister. His silence on all that concerned my origin I had long accepted as one of those mysteries which surround the lives of our elders, and which youth, in its reverence, forbears to question.

It chanced, upon a day in the early autumn of my eighteenth year, that, seeking in the old library for a volume of the poets, I took down from a high shelf a casket of dark carved wood, which, yielding to my hand, disclosed within its silken lining a miniature portrait set in gold. My heart stood still within me as I gazed upon it; for the face that looked forth from the ivory was, as in a glass, my own. The same brow, the same dark and melancholy eyes, the same slight and girlish figure, the same expression of mingled gentleness and sorrow, which I had seen reflected in the mirror of my chamber, were there before me, fixed in the imperishable colours of art. The dress was of a fashion older than my birth; the hair was gathered in ringlets upon the temples; and upon the countenance there rested a shade of pensive sweetness, as of one who had known grief early, and had learned to wear it as a garment.

I carried the portrait to my uncle, and demanded of him, with an earnestness that I could not repress, whose features these were that so strangely mocked my own. He started as I presented it, and a deep and painful flush overspread his countenance; then, recovering himself by a strong effort, he took the miniature from my hand, and regarded it long and fixedly in silence.

“It is the portrait of my sister,” said he at length, in a voice that trembled, “thy mother, Ellen; and thou dost well to resemble her, for she was the most lovely and the most unhappy of women.”

He said no more; and though I questioned him with all the ardour of a daughter’s curiosity, he put me aside with a gravity that bordered upon severity, and forbade me to renew the subject. Yet from that hour a change came over me. The image of that mournful and beautiful countenance haunted my dreams; and I began to weave a thousand fancies concerning the mother whom I had never known, and the mysterious sorrow that had clouded her brief existence. My uncle became more reserved and abstracted than before; he would sit for hours gazing upon the sea, and at such times I have seen tears, which he strove in vain to conceal, rolling down his furrowed cheeks.

It was in the November of that same year that the Reverend Mr. Penrhyn, the vicar of the neighbouring parish, came to pass an evening at the house. He was a man advanced in years, of a mild and venerable aspect, who had known my uncle in the days of his youth, and who, upon the departure of that gentleman from the room, addressed me with a kindness that emboldened me to speak of the portrait I had found, and of the little I had learned concerning her whom it represented.

The good vicar listened with attention; and when I had ceased, he remained for a space silent, as one who debates within himself whether he shall speak or be silent. At length, laying his hand upon my arm, and regarding me with an expression of the deepest commiseration—

“My child,” said he, “I know not whether it be my duty to unveil to thee a secret which thy guardian has thought fit to conceal; yet, since thou hast questioned me, and since the truth may one day be forced upon thee by a ruder hand, I will not suffer thee to remain in ignorance. Know, then, that the lady whose portrait thou hast found was indeed thy mother; but he who calls himself thy uncle is in truth thy father.”

I was struck dumb with astonishment and horror. The vicar continued:—

“In his youth, Mr. Fenwick was a man of fierce and uncontrollable passions, and of a pride that brooked no restraint. He loved a lady of noble birth and great beauty, and she returned his affection with an ardour equal to his own. But their union was opposed by her family, and they fled together in secret, and were married in a distant county, without the knowledge of her kindred.
