Mr. Theophilus Pell, bookseller of Paternoster Row, had never in the whole course of his sixty-three years been called upon to do a brave thing, and he was not at all certain that he possessed the organs requisite for the operation. He was a small, dry, circumspect man, who walked as though he carried a dozen fragile volumes balanced upon his head, and who spoke in a voice so carefully modulated that it seemed perpetually to be tiptoeing past a sick-room. His face was the colour of old parchment, and about as expressive; his spectacles were forever sliding down a nose that seemed constructed for the express purpose of defeating them; and his coat, of a sober snuff-colour, was buttoned to the throat against a world he regarded as drafty, improvident, and altogether too loud.

It was this same Mr. Pell who, in the autumn of the year seventeen hundred and ninety-two, found himself rattling over the Channel in a packet-boat that smelt of tar and cabbage, bound for Calais upon a matter of business. A Parisian printer, one Monsieur Vaubert, stood indebted to him in the sum of four hundred and twelve livres, and Mr. Pell, who could no more have forgiven a debt than he could have flown, had determined to collect it in person, the correspondence upon the subject having grown, on the Frenchman’s side, so florid and so evasive that it seemed to the bookseller’s orderly mind a positive duty to present himself and demand satisfaction.

But Paris, when he reached it, was not the Paris of his reckoning. The streets were a boiling cauldron of noise and colour and frantic motion; men ran hither and thither with pikes upon their shoulders; drums beat; crowds surged; and the very air seemed charged with a species of electric fury that made Mr. Pell’s knees knock together beneath his breeches. He had expected a city of shops and ledgers; he found a city of barricades and orators. He inquired, in his careful French, for the Rue des Trois Pigeons, and was directed, with a great deal of gesticulation and many oaths, to a quarter where the houses had been daubed with chalk and the gutters ran with something he preferred not to examine.

The establishment of Monsieur Vaubert was a tall, narrow house, squeezed between a wine-shop and a smithy, with a sign above the door that had been half defaced by a stone. Mr. Pell knocked, and was admitted by a young man of perhaps nineteen years, thin as a lath and pale as the paper he handled, who introduced himself as Hippolyte, the printer’s apprentice, and who conducted the bookseller up a winding stair into a workshop where the presses stood silent and the type lay scattered in the dust.

“Monsieur Vaubert,” said the apprentice, in a low voice, with a glance toward the shuttered window, “is not here. He is—he is at the Hôtel de Ville. He is arrested.”

Mr. Pell sat down very suddenly upon a stool that creaked beneath him. “Arrested!” he repeated. “Upon what charge?”

The apprentice made a gesture with his hands that seemed to take in the whole city. “He printed a pamphlet. It was not a wicked pamphlet, monsieur—it was only an argument, a little argument concerning the rights of man—but it was not the sort of argument that is permitted now. They came for him at dawn. They took him away with two others. I do not know where they have taken him.”

Mr. Pell was much affected. He was a man of peace and of punctual payment, and the notion of a printer being carried off for the printing of a pamphlet struck him as a monstrous irregularity. He said as much, in his cautious way, and the apprentice, who had been watching him with an expression of desperate calculation, suddenly seized him by the sleeve.

“Monsieur is an Englishman,” said Hippolyte, eagerly. “Monsieur is going back to England. Monsieur will carry something for me.”

“My young friend,” said Mr. Pell, disengaging his sleeve with as much dignity as he could muster, “I am a bookseller, not a courier. I have no desire to be implicated in—”

“It is not for me!” cried the apprentice. “It is for him—for my master. They will search the house; they will burn the papers; and if they find them, they will find also the names of those who wrote them, and those men will go to the guillotine. Monsieur, I beg of you.”

And here the boy fell upon his knees, and clasped the bookseller’s hand, and looked up at him with eyes that were bright with a terror and a resolution that Mr. Pell had never seen in all his days of ledgers and invoices.

“I have never done a brave thing in my life,” said Mr. Pell, faintly.

“Nor have I,” said the apprentice. “But I am doing one now.”

Mr. Pell looked at the boy, and then at the presses, and then at the shuttered window, through the chinks of which came the distant sound of singing and the tramp of feet. He thought of his shop in Paternoster Row, of his cat, of his tea, of his warm fire and his quiet evenings. He thought of the four hundred and twelve livres, which he now perceived he should never see. And then he thought—as men sometimes do, in the strangest and most unaccountable manner—of a sentence he had read in a book, a sentence that had lain dormant in his memory for forty years, and which now rose up and confronted him: that the only thing necessary for the triumph of evil is for good men to do nothing.

“Where is the parcel?” said Mr. Pell.

The apprentice rose, went to a press, and drew from behind it a package wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string. It was not large, but it was heavy; and when Mr. Pell took it in his hands, he felt as though he were taking up a stone.
