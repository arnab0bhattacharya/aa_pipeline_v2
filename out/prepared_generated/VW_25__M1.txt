The lamp was already lit; the window square of sky above the gasworks had turned a bruised violet, and inside, the paper on the walls, patterned with small blue flowers, seemed to contract, to gather itself against the coming dark. Edmund stood before the glass, buttoning his collar, and the face that looked back was a stranger’s, a little pale, a little set, the mouth already forming the shape of an argument he had not yet made. He was going to the lecture. He had been going to lectures all winter, in the belief that they would fill him, that the words of clever men would seep through the cracks in his own thinking and make it whole. But they did not. He left them feeling thinner, more transparent, as if he had been polished down by the friction of other minds. Still, he went. It was a habit now, like the evening walk, like the tea at half-past four, like the writing of the verses that he folded and put away in the drawer with the spare buttons and the old theatre programmes. The verses were for himself; the lectures were for the man he hoped to become.

The hall was warm and smelled of damp wool and floor polish. The rows of faces, pale ovals under the electric lights, turned towards the platform where a man with a long grey beard was speaking of the function of the state. Edmund listened, or tried to listen, but his mind, that unruly tenant, kept wandering to the window, to the shapes of the clouds moving past the dome of St. Paul’s; and then to the woman in the row in front. She was not beautiful, not in the way of the women in magazines, but she had a quality of attention that was startling. Her head was bent, not in reverence, but in concentration, as if she were weighing each word the speaker offered, testing it for truth. Her gloves were grey, her collar high and plain. She was, he decided, a person of some seriousness.

When the lecture was over, there was the usual small stir, the gathering of coats, the murmured exchanges. Edmund found himself beside her in the crush by the door. And then, because the silence between strangers in a crowd is a kind of pressure, he spoke. “It seems to me,” he said, and his voice sounded thin and foolish, “that the man mistakes the map for the territory.”

She turned. Her eyes were grey, the same shade as her gloves, and they were very clear. “The map is all we have,” she said. “To pretend otherwise is a form of sentimentality.”

And so it began. Not a conversation, but a collision. They argued there, in the draughty hall, while the last of the audience filtered out into the wet street. They argued about the poor, about poetry, about the nature of duty. Her name was Clara. She worked for a society that gave coal to the destitute. His work was in a government office, filing papers that recorded the movements of ships and the price of grain. His poetry, he did not mention. It seemed a frivolous thing, a private indulgence, in the face of her coal and her certainty.

“We must meet again,” he said, as they parted at the corner of Fleet Street, the rain beginning to speck the pavement. It was not a question.

“Next Tuesday,” she said. “The same time. The library at the British Museum is quiet.”

And so they met. Every week, in the great reading room under its dome of glass and iron, they would find a table, and the argument would resume, as if it had been waiting for them, a living thing that had fed on the silence of the week. They spoke of God, of marriage, of the purpose of art. Edmund, who in his office was a man of quiet routine, of careful handwriting and ordered files, found himself becoming fierce, passionate, his hands cutting the air. Clara, who in her charity work dealt with grimy facts and hungry children, spoke of ideals with a cold, hard light in her eyes. They were two solitudes, shouting across a narrow chasm.

From his window, later, he would watch the lights of the city, the great beast breathing in its sleep, and think of her. The verses he wrote grew sharper, less about the sunset and more about the hard edges of things: a brick, a coin, a word. He felt her presence in the room with him, a kind of pressure, a challenge. The argument was not about the things they said; it was about the things they were. It was a way of being together without the terrifying, silent intimacy of love.

One Tuesday, she was late. The seat opposite him was empty. The silence of the library pressed in, the rustle of pages, the distant cough, the scratching of a hundred pens. He felt a panic rise in him, a sense of loss so acute it was physical. What if she did not come? What if the argument was over? The empty chair was a void, a question he could not answer. Then he saw her, hurrying through the great doorway, her cheeks flushed, a few drops of rain on her hat. She sat down, breathless.

“My apologies,” she said. “A case. A family in Southwark. The father has lost his position.”

And he felt a pang of shame. His verses, his grand theories, were nothing. They were dust. But then she looked at him, and the old fire was in her eyes. “And you will say,” she began, “that the state has no business interfering in the contracts between men.”

“No,” he said, and his voice was quiet. “I will say that a man’s dignity is not a matter for contract.”
