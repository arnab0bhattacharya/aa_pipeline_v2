The town of Wetherby had not, for many years, known such a source of animation as the quartering of the ——shire Militia within its limits. To the families of the surrounding gentry, the arrival of the officers was an event of considerable consequence, for it promised to enliven a society where the winter months had been accustomed to pass in a quiet, if respectable, uniformity. Every drawing-room in the neighbourhood became a theatre of hope and calculation, and the mothers of unmarried daughters found their minds wonderfully concentrated on the single object of securing the attentions of a man in a red coat.

Among these officers, Captain Fortescue was universally acknowledged to be the most eligible. He possessed a handsome person, an easy address, and a fortune which, though not great, was quite sufficient to make him an object of interest to any family whose daughters were not entirely without prospects. His manners were of that open and engaging kind which is designed to please, and which he had found, in general, exceedingly successful. He was accustomed to being admired, and had long since ceased to reflect upon the cause of his popularity, taking it as a natural and proper tribute to his merits. His own family seat was in the north, but he spoke of it with a casual grandeur that suggested a superior station, and he carried himself with an air of good breeding that satisfied all but the most fastidious observers.

His arrival at the Assembly Rooms in Wetherby was consequently an affair of great expectation. Mrs. Elmsworth, with her two daughters, Maria and Charlotte, had taken a place of particular advantage for the purpose of observation. Maria, a young woman of sixteen, possessed of a fine complexion and an ardent desire to be noticed, felt her heart beat with a vivacity that was almost painful. Her sister, Charlotte, who was two years her senior and of a more thoughtful and less demonstrative character, appeared to be occupied solely with the arrangement of her fan.

Captain Fortescue, on entering the room, seemed to understand at once the part he was required to play. He bowed, he smiled, and he danced with an air of gallantry that left few hearts untouched. He was particularly attentive to the Elmsworth family, and it was with a graceful condescension that he solicited the hand of Miss Maria Elmsworth for the first two dances. Maria, in an ecstasy of triumph, could scarcely command her feet to perform their duty, and her conversation was a series of nervous pleasantries which the Captain received with an approving smile.

When the set was concluded, he turned his attention to the elder sister. “And Miss Charlotte,” he said, with a look of easy confidence, “I hope I may not be denied the pleasure of your company for the next?”

Charlotte raised her eyes from her fan with a calmness that was, to a man of Captain Fortescue’s experience, rather surprising. “You are very good, sir,” she replied, “but I am afraid I must decline. I have a slight headache, and do not think I shall dance again this evening.”

The Captain was not accustomed to such a refusal. He was not accustomed, indeed, to any refusal at all, and he attributed it at first to a playful coquetry. “Come, come,” he insisted, with a charming smile, “a headache is a poor excuse on a night of such festivity. Surely the pleasure of the dance is a sovereign remedy for such an ailment?”

“I assure you, sir, my disinclination is quite real,” Charlotte said, her tone even and untroubled. “I am very well content to observe.”

This was a new experience. The Captain, finding his charm had met with its first check, felt a sensation of pique that was not unmixed with curiosity. He bowed and withdrew, but his eye was drawn to her for the remainder of the evening. He saw her engaged in quiet conversation with a friend, her manner composed and her countenance betraying no interest whatsoever in the lively scene before her. This indifference, which he could not comprehend, began to occupy his thoughts. He had been courted, flattered, and sought after by every mother and daughter in the room; yet here was a young woman, of no extraordinary beauty or fortune, who seemed to regard him with as little concern as she would a piece of furniture.

In the days that followed, Captain Fortescue found himself in frequent company with the Elmsworths. Mrs. Elmsworth, a woman of some shrewdness in the art of matchmaking, made it her business to throw her daughters in his way. He was invited to take tea, to walk in the shrubbery, and to join their small evening parties. On these occasions, his attentions were invariably divided. To Maria, he was all gallantry and admiration, for she received his compliments with a blush and a smile that afforded him the gratification he expected. But it was to Charlotte that he now directed his most studied efforts. He sought her opinion on books, on music, on the events of the day, hoping to penetrate the reserve that so baffled him.

Charlotte, for her part, answered his questions with civility, but with no desire to prolong the conversation. Her calmness was not, as he supposed, a calculated strategy. It proceeded from a very different source. She had observed the Captain’s attentions to the whole neighbourhood, and had formed an opinion of his character which was not to be shaken by a few personal compliments. She saw in him a man whose good opinion was too easily won, and whose affections were of that shallow quality which is bestowed upon the whole sex rather than upon any single individual. She had witnessed his flirtation with the Misses Corbin, and had heard him praise the singing of Miss Pratt with the same earnestness he now employed in questioning her own taste. To her mind, such indiscriminate admiration was a mark of a deficiency in true feeling, and she resolved to protect her own tranquillity from its influence.
