The rain had not ceased for three-and-forty days, and the earth, which we had thought the most firm and beneficent of mothers, revealed herself as a drowned and hostile stepdame. I had watched the ruin from the high windows of Marchmont House, and had felt it but as a sombre pageant, a thing of clouds and water, remote from the warm precincts of my own existence. I was young, I was nobly born, and I had been bred in the persuasion that the affairs of state were a wearisome and sordid traffic, fit only for men of coarse ambition and narrow souls; and I had wrapped myself in my books, my music, and my dreams, and had thanked Heaven that no voice of the multitude should ever clamour at my gates. Alas! how little do we know the destinies that are forging for us in the silent hours, or by what stern and sorrowful paths the soul is led from its luxurious slumbers to the bitter knowledge of its fellowship with all that suffers!

The storm had beaten down the harvest in the fields, and the waters had risen over the lowlands, and the granaries stood empty, and the price of bread had grown to a thing that the poor man could not compass; and there had gone up from the great cities a cry of anguish and of wrath, and the people had turned their eyes upon the Government, and had said, “Ye have taken no thought for us in the day of our calamity; ye have let the fat years pass in your pleasures, and now in the lean years ye bid us starve in patience.” And there had been tumults and the breaking of windows, and the burning of a factory or two, and the soldiery had been called out, and blood had been spilt upon the stones of London; and still the rain fell, and still the price of bread rose, and the temper of the people grew more dark and desperate.

I had a cousin, a man of earnest and impassioned temper, by name Edmund Ashby, who had long been a member of the Lower House, and who had striven, with an eloquence that I had been wont to call fanatical, to awaken the Government to the perils that were gathering. He came to me one evening, his face pale and haggard with watching, and his voice tremulous with a deep and suppressed emotion. “Almeric,” said he, “the hour is come that I have long foreseen. The ministers are bewildered; they have no counsel; they have no sympathy with the people, and the people have no trust in them. There is but one man in England who might yet stand between the throne and the abyss, and that man is yourself.”

I started, and would have interrupted him, but he continued with an ardour that would not be stayed. “Hear me, cousin! You are of the great house of Marchmont; your name is dear to the people, for your father was beloved, and your mother was a saint upon earth. You have no share in the corruptions of the court, no stain of party upon your honour. You are young, you are eloquent, and you have a heart that can feel for the wretched. The people will listen to you as they will listen to no other. I have spoken of you to the leaders of the popular assembly, and they have consented to receive you. Come with me to-morrow to the House, and speak to them as a man and a brother.”

I turned from him with a cold and haughty displeasure. “You mock me, Edmund,” I said. “I am no politician; I have no gift for the wrangling of parties; I have no desire to mingle in the coarse strife of the world. I have my books, my fields, my dreams—let me be.”

He rose, and his eyes flashed with a sorrowful indignation. “And is this your answer? You, who have been nurtured in the lap of luxury, who have never known the gnawing of hunger or the chill of despair, you will wrap yourself in your selfish tranquillity, and leave the poor to perish! You will dream of the sublime and the beautiful while your fellow-creatures are dying for lack of bread! Oh, shame! shame upon such philosophy! The spirit of the age calls to you, and you will not hear; the voice of humanity implores you, and you stop your ears. But I tell you, Almeric, that the day will come when you will remember these words with a bitterness that no repentance can efface.”

He left me, and I remained in a tumult of conflicting thoughts. I was stung by his reproaches, and yet I was too proud to yield; but in the silence of the night, when the wind howled around the house, and the rain beat against the casements, there came to me a vision of the suffering that I had never seen, and I could not shut it out. I saw the pale faces of the hungry, the despair of the widow, the cry of the little children; and I felt that I was indeed guilty of a base and cowardly indifference. And there arose within me a strange and unwonted yearning, a desire to speak for those who had no voice, to be the champion of the oppressed; and I resolved, though with a trembling heart, that I would go with Edmund on the morrow.

The House was crowded when we entered, and the air was heavy with the breath of many agitated men. The minister had spoken, and his words had been cold and vague, and the murmurs of the assembly had grown louder and more menacing. Then Edmund rose, and in a strain of passionate and pathetic eloquence he painted the miseries of the people, and implored the Government to take some measure of relief; but he was interrupted by the cries of the angry and the scoffing, and he sat down with a countenance of despair. And then I felt a hand upon my arm, and Edmund whispered, “Now, cousin, now is the moment—speak, and God be with you!”
