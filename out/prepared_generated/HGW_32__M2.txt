The first symptom was a shiver, and the second was the terrible, alien sensation of thirst.

Arthur Pym, a junior archivist in the Ministry of Continuity in New London, sat at his desk in the sterile, white-walled office on the forty-second floor of the Spire. Outside, the sky was a perfect, unblemished azure, scrubbed clean by the atmospheric processors. Inside, the air was regulated to a precise 20 degrees Celsius, with humidity held at a constant 45 percent. It was the ideal environment for the human organism, as defined by the Medical Code of 2140. And yet, Arthur felt a heat rising in his chest that had nothing to do with the climate control.

He looked at his hand. It was trembling.

This was absurd. Absurd in the way that only things could be absurd in a world where error had been engineered out of existence. Since the Great Correction, since the gene-locks had been standardized and the viral libraries purged from the atmosphere, no one had been sick. The word *sick* was a historical curiosity, found only in the old libraries, associated with the muddy, fever-ridden streets of the nineteenth century. Illness was a concept, not a reality. It was like gravity on the moon.

Arthur pressed his hand to his forehead. It was hot. Dry.

"Computer," he said, his voice sounding thin and strange in the silence of the open-plan office. "Query: Physiological anomaly. Temperature discrepancy."

The overhead speaker crackled, a sound like static from a dead century. "Please specify parameters, Arthur Pym. Current vitals are within normal parameters. Heart rate: 72. Blood pressure: 110/70. Why do you query?"

"I am... uncomfortable," Arthur said. The words felt clumsy, like a stone in his shoe. "I feel hot. I feel weak."

"Comfort is a subjective variable," the computer replied, with its usual polite, empty tone. "If you wish to adjust your local environment, please state your preference. Would you like the ambient temperature lowered?"

"No," Arthur said. He stood up, and his knees buckled. He caught himself on the edge of the desk, the plastic cool against his palm. "No. I need... water."

"Hydration protocols are automatic. Your internal reserves are sufficient for another six hours."

Arthur looked around. The office was a hive of quiet, efficient activity. To his left, Elara was typing, her face pale and focused, the blue light of her screen reflecting in her eyes. Across the aisle, Mr. Henderson was reviewing a data file, his posture rigid and perfect. They did not look at him. They could not look at him. To look at him would be to acknowledge the deviation, and deviation was the only true crime left in a society of perfection.

"I need to leave," Arthur said.

He walked toward the door. His legs felt heavy, leaden. Each step was a labor. The air, usually so crisp, felt thick, cloying. He reached the door and pulled it open. The corridor was white, silent, endless. He walked into the lift, his hand shaking so badly he could not press the button for the lobby. The doors closed.

As the lift descended, Arthur felt the heat spreading. It was not just in his head; it was in his blood. A prickling, a burning. He was sweating. The sight of his own perspiration on his shirt collar was shocking, a betrayal of his body’s design. He was leaking.

When the doors opened on the ground floor, the lobby was empty. The security droids stood in their alcoves, their metal faces blank and impassive. Arthur staggered toward the exit. He needed air. Real air, not the recycled, filtered breath of the Spire.

He pushed through the glass doors and out into the street.

New London was a city of glass and steel, gleaming under the sun. The streets were wide and clean, devoid of litter, devoid of noise. People moved in their sleek, silent vehicles, or walked with the smooth, purposeful gait of the healthy. No one coughed. No one wiped their nose. No one looked tired.

Arthur leaned against a pillar of polished obsidian. He felt dizzy. The world was tilting. He closed his eyes, and when he opened them, he saw a woman.

She was walking past, dressed in the standard grey tunic of the civic service. She stopped. Her eyes, wide and dark, fixed on him. She did not speak. She did not offer help. She stepped back, then further back, her face a mask of sudden, visceral horror.

Arthur tried to call out, but his throat was dry. A coughing fit seized him. It was a ragged, ugly sound, a wet rattle that tore through the pristine silence of the street.

The woman screamed.

It was a thin, sharp cry, not of pain, but of terror. She ran.

Others stopped. Heads turned. The crowd did not gather; it scattered. People pressed themselves against the walls, behind the pillars, into the shadows. They watched him with the same look one might give a leper in the old days, or a monster from a forgotten myth. They were afraid of him. Not of his malice, for he had none, but of his *contagion*. They believed, in their deep, instinctive, unspoken fear, that if he was sick, then sickness was real. That the wall of safety had broken.

Arthur slid down the pillar until he was sitting on the clean, cold pavement. He felt a drop of something warm and red slide down his nose. Blood. He touched it with his finger, staring at the red smear on his fingertip. It was so vivid, so violently red, that it seemed impossible.

A drone hovered above him. It was small, a silver disc, silent. It scanned him, its red light blinking rapidly.

"Anomaly detected," a voice said, mechanical and cold. "Quarantine protocol initiated. Do not move."

Arthur tried to stand, but his legs would not obey. He was weak, so terribly weak. He felt a wave of nausea, and he turned his head away, expecting the worst. But nothing came. Just a hollow, aching emptiness.
