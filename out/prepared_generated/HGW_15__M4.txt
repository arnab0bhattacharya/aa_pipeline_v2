I do not know what it is about the English countryside that breeds such a profound and invincible contempt for any sort of knowledge that cannot be weighed out in pecks or entered in a ledger, but the phenomenon was never more forcibly borne in upon me than during the affair of the great storm at Little Wimpling. I was, at that time, a bachelor of some five-and-forty years, retired from the india-rubber trade with a modest competence and a passion for the new science of meteorology, and I had taken the White Cottage at the end of the village for the express purpose of pursuing my studies in peace. My instruments were of my own devising—a mercury barometer of rather delicate construction, a siphon rain-gauge, and a most ingenious anemometer which I had built out of a bicycle wheel and some old clockwork—and I flatter myself that they were as sensitive as anything at Kew.

For a week in late August the mercury had been sinking with a steadiness that I found profoundly disquieting. The air was still and heavy, the sky a brazen, cloudless blue by day and a curious coppery green at sunset, and the swallows flew low and in silence. On the Tuesday I observed a peculiar flickering in the needle of my anemometer, though not a leaf stirred in the garden, and I knew that somewhere to the south-west a great disturbance was brewing. By the Thursday morning my barometer stood at twenty-eight inches and a fraction, and I went down to the village to give warning.

The parish council was sitting in the schoolroom, and I was received by Mr. Habgood, the grocer and churchwarden, a portly man with a great deal of gold watch-chain and a manner of genial condescension that I had always found particularly galling. With him were Mr. Penge, the builder, a lean and saturnine fellow; the Reverend Mr. Cattermole, whose views on the weather were chiefly theological; and old Mrs. Blunt, who kept the post-office and was reputed to know more of the village than the rector himself.

“Gentlemen,” said I, “and madam, I have come to warn you. My instruments indicate that a storm of unprecedented violence will break upon this district within the next twenty-four hours. I advise that the lower parts of the village be evacuated, that the brook be cleared of obstructions, and that all able-bodied persons be prepared to take refuge on the higher ground behind the church.”

Mr. Habgood regarded me over his spectacles with the air of a man who is being made the victim of a practical joke.

“And what,” said he, “might your instruments be, Mr. ———?” (I suppress my name, for reasons that will become apparent.) “Glass bottles and old iron, I’ve heard.”

“They are scientific apparatus,” I replied, with what dignity I could muster, “and they have never failed me yet.”

“Scientific!” cried Mr. Penge, with a short and scornful laugh. “Scientific! And what does the glass bottle say, then?”

“It says,” said I, “that the pressure is falling more rapidly than at any time in the last fifty years. It says that we may expect a wind of hurricane force, accompanied by rain in torrents. It says, in short, that unless precautions are taken, this village will be half-drowned and half-blown away before Saturday morning.”

There was a moment of silence, and then Mrs. Blunt gave a little cackle of laughter.

“Lor’ bless the man,” said she, “he’s been readin’ them penny dreadfuls. Hurricanes in Surrey! Whatever next?”

“My dear sir,” said the Reverend Mr. Cattermole, in his most unctuous tones, “I think you allow your enthusiasm for your—ah—hobby to run away with you. The Almighty, we may be sure, does not send hurricanes to quiet English villages. It would be—if I may say so—unseemly.”

“Unseemly or not,” I retorted, for my temper was rising, “it is coming. And I would remind you that the Almighty has been known to send floods and tempests before now, and that He does not always consult the convenience of parish councils.”

At this there was a general murmur of disapprobation. Mr. Habgood rose to his feet.

“I think, sir,” said he, “that we have heard quite enough. You are a newcomer here, and you do not understand our ways. We are plain people, and we do not hold with new-fangled notions and foreign instruments. If you wish to make yourself ridiculous, that is your affair, but I will thank you not to disturb the peace of this village with your alarmist talk. Good morning to you.”

And so I was dismissed, with the laughter of the council ringing in my ears. I went home in a state of considerable indignation, but I was not idle. I spent the afternoon in making my cellar as watertight as I could, in bringing down from the upper rooms such provisions as I possessed, and in securing the shutters. My housekeeper, a worthy woman named Mrs. Prewett, regarded me with the resigned air of one who has long since given up expecting sanity from her employer, but she helped me as best she could, and I sent her off to her sister’s cottage on the hill before nightfall.

The storm broke at about eleven o’clock that night. I was sitting in my study, watching the barometer, when I heard a low, continuous roar that I took at first for the passing of a heavy cart. But it grew and grew until it filled the whole night, and then the rain came—not in drops, but in solid sheets, as though some great reservoir in the sky had been overturned. The wind followed, and within an hour my windows were rattling as though they would burst from their frames, and the old elms in the garden were groaning and cracking like ships in a gale.
