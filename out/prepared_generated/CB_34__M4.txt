The pensionnat of Madame Vérin had, at the hour of dusk, a quality of silence that was not peace. It was the silence of a house holding its breath; of tall windows shuttered against the foreign street; of corridors where the echo of a footstep died too quickly, swallowed by the heavy drapes and the darker doors. I had come to this place, as I had come to so many, with my trunk of plain clothes and my little store of accomplishments, to teach French and English to the daughters of merchants and minor officials who wished their girls to acquire the polish of a gentility they could not quite afford. I was neither a lady nor a servant, but something vexingly between—a creature of the schoolroom, permitted to instruct, forbidden to presume.

The girl’s name was Anneke Claes. She was fifteen, the eldest daughter of a widowed linen-draper, and she had about her a stillness that I recognized. It was not the stillness of a dullard, nor yet the sullenness of a rebel; it was the guarded quiet of one who has learned early that her feelings are an inconvenience to others. She was not beautiful—her face was too thin, her hair too defiantly straight—but there was a light in her grey eyes that I had seen in my own mirror, in the days when I was a girl of her age and the world had seemed to me a vast, cold room in which I was locked.

I discovered the letter by accident, or perhaps by that instinct which teaches a governess where to look. It was a Thursday evening, and I had returned to the schoolroom to fetch a book I had left behind. The room was empty, the lamps not yet lit, and the last grey light of the Flemish sky fell slantwise through the windows. Anneke stood by the tall press in the corner, her back to me, her shoulders bent as if she were praying. But she was not praying. She was reading, and her hands trembled so that the paper rustled like a dry leaf.

She did not hear me enter. I stood for a moment in the doorway, and I saw what she held—a letter, closely written, the ink blurred in places as if the writer’s hand had been unsteady. I knew the look of it. I had held such letters myself, once, in a garden in Brussels, when the world had seemed to promise something other than this long, grey procession of days.

“Anneke,” I said.

She started as if I had struck her. The letter fluttered to the floor, and she turned to face me with a white face and eyes that were not ashamed but desperate. She did not speak. She did not stoop to retrieve the paper. She stood there, her hands clenched at her sides, and waited for me to pronounce her doom.

I bent and picked up the letter. It was addressed to her in a hand I did not recognize—a young man’s hand, I judged, bold and a little careless. I did not read it. I folded it and held it in my own hand, and I looked at her.

“You know the rule,” I said. “No correspondence with persons outside the school. No letters received or sent without Madame Vérin’s inspection. You know the penalty.”

“Yes, mademoiselle,” she said. Her voice was low and steady, but I saw the pulse beating in her throat.

“Who is he?”

She hesitated. Then: “His name is Pieter. He is a clerk in my father’s warehouse. He is—he is nothing, mademoiselle. He is nobody. But he is kind to me.”

“Kind to you,” I repeated. The words sounded foolish in my own ears, and yet I understood them perfectly. I had known kindness so rarely in my youth that I had once believed it to be a species of miracle, a thing that happened to other people, in other countries, under other skies.

“If I report this,” I said, “you will be expelled. Madame Vérin will write to your father. You will be sent home in disgrace, and you will not be permitted to return. Do you understand that?”

“Yes, mademoiselle.”

“And what will become of you then?”

She did not answer. She looked at the floor, and her thin shoulders rose and fell with a breath she did not quite permit herself to take. I saw the tears gather in her eyes and I saw her force them back. She had learned, as I had learned, that tears are a currency that buys nothing.

I stood there in the grey light, and I thought of myself at fifteen. I thought of the cold schoolroom in the north of England, and the letter I had once tried to hide in the lining of my workbasket—a letter from a young man who had promised me nothing and meant it. I thought of the mistress who had found it, and of the shame that had followed, and of how I had learned, in that moment, that the world was divided into those who held power and those who were crushed by it, and that I had been born into the latter class.

I looked at Anneke Claes, and I saw myself.

“Sit down,” I said.

She looked up, startled. “Mademoiselle?”

“Sit down. There is a chair by the window. Sit in it.”

She obeyed me, slowly, as if she feared a trap. I crossed the room and drew the curtain a little, so that the last light fell upon her face. Then I stood before her, and I held the letter in my hand, and I spoke to her as I would have spoken to myself, if I could have gone back through the years and found that girl in the cold schoolroom, with her hidden letter and her broken heart.
