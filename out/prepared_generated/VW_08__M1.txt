The light on the terrace had the quality of thin milk, diluted and weak, yet it possessed a certain persistence, clinging to the white stone as if it feared the encroaching grey of the Atlantic. Mrs. Pemberton adjusted the rug across her husband’s knees—a gesture so habitual that her hands performed it without consulting her mind—and watched the steam rise from his tea. Edward coughed, a dry, rhythmic sound that seemed to punctuate the silence of the morning, and she felt the familiar, heavy weight of the season settle upon her. They were wintering in Funchal, as they did every year since the doctor had prescribed the mild air, and the hotel, with its potted palms and its echoing corridors, had become a second skin, a shell enclosing their small, measured existence.

Then, the lace girl entered the courtyard.

She did not walk so much as flow, a dark, moving shape against the pale stucco, carrying her basket of white work. Mrs. Pemberton felt a shift in the atmosphere, a stirring of the air that had been so stagnant a moment before. The girl—Isabel, she was called, a name Mrs. Pemberton had learned from the porter—paused to speak to the gardener, her voice a low, musical murmur that did not reach the terrace but rippled through the visual field, disturbing the stillness. There was a vitality about her, a restless energy in the way she pushed a stray curl from her forehead, that seemed at odds with the languid heat of the day.

"Edward," Mrs. Pemberton said, though she did not turn her eyes from the girl. "The lace."

Edward grunted, adjusting his spectacles. "Too expensive. The same pattern as last year."

But Mrs. Pemberton had already risen, drawn by a curiosity she could not quite name, or perhaps by a desire to touch that vibrant, moving thing. She descended the three stone steps into the courtyard, her skirts brushing the gravel. The girl looked up, and her face broke into a smile that was startlingly bright, like a shutter thrown open to the sun.

"Madam," Isabel said. "The sun is good for the senhor today?"

"He is comfortable," Mrs. Pemberton replied, her voice sounding stiff and formal in the open air. She reached out to touch the lace in the basket. It was intricate, a web of white thread that seemed to hold the light within its loops. "This is beautiful work."

"My mother, she taught me," Isabel said, her hands flying to arrange the pieces. "And my grandmother. It is the old way. But see, this one, I have changed the edge. A little more... how do you say... open?"

Mrs. Pemberton looked at the girl’s hands—brown, capable, stained faintly with dye or earth—and then at her own, pale and soft, resting useless against the white linen. "You are clever," she said. "It must take a great deal of patience."

Isabel laughed, a sound that was quick and sharp. "Patience, yes. But there is much to think of while the hands work. My brother, he is in Lisbon now. He writes to me. He says the city is full of noise, and the trams shake the houses. But he is learning the trade of the engineer. He will make a bridge, he says. A bridge over the river." She paused, her eyes drifting toward the horizon where the sea met the sky in a hazy blur. "I think of the bridge when I make the loops. It is strong, like the thread."

Mrs. Pemberton felt a sudden, inexplicable constriction in her throat. A bridge. The word seemed to carry a weight that the girl did not intend, a suggestion of connection, of spanning great distances, of things being built and changed. She thought of the years spent in this hotel, the years spent in the hotel in Torquay before it, the endless, circular routine of Edward’s health, the seasons that turned like the pages of a book read too many times. She saw her life suddenly as a small, enclosed room, neat and comfortable, with the windows shut against the draft.

"And your family?" Mrs. Pemberton asked, her voice sounding thin. "They are well?"

"Ah, my father," Isabel said, shaking her head with a fond exasperation. "He is a fisherman. He is out at sea now, perhaps near the Desertas. He is brave, but the sea, it is a hungry thing. My mother, she worries. She sits by the window and watches the water. But I tell her, we must live. We must eat. And the lace, it brings the bread." She held up a piece of needlework, a complex flower blooming in white. "See? This is for a bride in the town. She is marrying the baker's son. They are very much in love. It is a happy lace."

Mrs. Pemberton looked at the lace, at the intricate, interlocking threads that formed the flower. She saw the girl’s father in his boat, tossing on the grey water; she saw the mother watching the horizon; she saw the bride and the baker’s son in a small, noisy kitchen. It was a life of struggle, of danger, of loud voices and raw edges, and yet it possessed a wholeness, a terrifying reality that made her own existence seem like a shadow play. She had loved Edward, she supposed, in a quiet, undemanding way, but she had never sat by a window in terror of the sea. She had never made something with her hands that would hold a bride’s happiness within it.

"It is very fine," Mrs. Pemberton whispered.

"You like it?" Isabel beamed. "For you, I make a special price. Because you are kind to look."

Mrs. Pemberton fumbled in her reticule for the coins. Her fingers felt clumsy, disconnected. She wanted to ask the girl if she were happy, if she felt the vastness of the ocean pressing in on her, if she feared the silence of the night. But the questions died on her lips. How could she ask? She, who had never known anything but the safety of the shore.
