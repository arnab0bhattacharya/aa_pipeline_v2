He had come in from the street, where the October rain was falling with a persistent, grey insistence, and the fire in the grate was burning with that hollow, red glow which seems to mock the desolation of a wet afternoon. Eleanor sat by it, turning the pages of a review, her face lit up, then shadowed, then lit again, as the flames rose and fell. She was plain; there was no denying it; her features were too large, her nose too decided, her eyes of that unremarkable hazel which takes on no particular lustre in the sun. Yet there she sat, possessed of a kind of mastery, as if the fire were her own invention, and the very rain on the windows a chorus she had summoned up to amuse her. Henry, standing in the doorway with the damp still clinging to his coat, felt the absurdity of his position. He was engaged to Clara. Clara, who was at this very moment in the next room, pouring tea with that exquisite regularity which made of every social duty a small, flawless poem. Clara’s chin was a marvel of delicate modelling; her voice fell like a silver thread into the hum of conversation. He was to marry Clara. He had the ring in his waistcoat pocket, a hard, circular fact. And yet he stood here, dripping on the rug, unable to take his eyes from Eleanor’s large, competent hands as they turned the pages.

She looked up. She did not smile; she looked at him with that direct, unpitying gaze which stripped the social graces from a man and left him standing in his naked, foolish self.

"You are wet," she said.

He made no answer. The words seemed to him not a statement about the weather, but a judgment upon his soul. He was wet; he was ridiculous; he was a man who had bought a ring for a woman with a silver voice, and now stood watching a woman who worked for her bread and found the world, by and large, a matter for mirth.

From the next room came the faint, agreeable clatter of china—the small, domestic music of Clara’s perfection. She was pouring tea; she was handing cake; she was saying things that were at once correct and endearing. He could see her without looking: the turn of her wrist, the pale, flawless curve of her neck. She was the picture of what a young woman should be, hung in the gallery of his mind, gilt-framed, serene. And then there was Eleanor, who had put down her review and was looking at him as if he were a specimen under glass.

"You had better go and change," she said. "Or do you mean to stand there, breeding damp, until you turn into a mushroom?"

He laughed, a short, uneasy laugh. She mocked him; she always mocked him. When he spoke of politics, she asked if he had read the bill. When he spoke of duty, she asked after his boots. She worked in an office in Chancery Lane; she came home on the omnibus with her gloves stained with ink; she had no small, soft ways, no art of pausing in a doorway to let the light fall upon her hair. She was entirely without art. And yet, when she spoke, the world he inhabited—that well-ordered, heavily upholstered world of Clara’s drawing-rooms and his mother’s expectations—seemed to lose its solidity. It wavered, like a reflection in the river, and he felt himself drawn towards something bare, difficult, and coldly alive.

"I was going," he said, and did not move.

"Then go," she said, and turned back to her review.

But he did not go. He advanced into the room and stood by the mantelpiece, looking down at the ornaments—some little brass figures she had brought from Italy, a clock that ticked with a harsh, domestic urgency. The fire spat. A log fell. He was conscious of a profound disorder in his being, as if some interior furniture had been pushed aside to make room for a vast, rushing wind.

"Why do you always laugh at me?" he asked suddenly.

She did not look up. "Because you are laughable," she said. "You come in here, dripping, and stand staring at the fire as if it were an oracle. You are engaged to a beautiful girl who will arrange your life with the precision of a Swiss clock. You have only to wind it up and it will tick for fifty years. What more do you want?"

He felt the ring in his pocket, a dead weight. He thought of the country house in Hampshire, of the lawns stretching away to the elms, of Clara walking across the grass in white, her parasol tilted against the sun. It was all so clear, so inevitable, so arranged. He saw himself handing her into the carriage; he saw himself growing grey in that ordered service; he saw the years stretching out, immaculate, unbroken, like a corridor of polished floors. And into this vision came Eleanor’s laugh—a short, clear sound that broke the surface and let the cold air in.

"I don’t want a clock," he said, and his voice surprised him by its roughness.

She looked up then, quickly, and for a moment the mockery was gone. She saw him, perhaps, as he was: a man suddenly afraid of his own life, standing on the edge of something he could not name. The firelight caught her plain face and gave it a strange, momentary beauty, the beauty of a thing seen clearly and without illusion. Her eyes, so ordinary in daylight, held a dark, discerning light. She did not speak. The silence between them was full of a thousand things unsaid, pressing against the walls, demanding utterance.

Then the door opened, and Clara came in.

She was all in blue, with a little lace at her throat, and she carried an air of fragrance and composure. She moved like a wave that flows over rough stones and leaves them smooth.
