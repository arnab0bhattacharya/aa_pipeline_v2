The first to go mad was a dray horse on the Strand, but no one paid it much heed until the second, and the third, and the tenth. By the time the sun had set on that particular Tuesday in late October, the streets of London were not merely impassable; they were besieged. It was not a violent madness, at least not at first. It was a terrible, high-pitched, vibrating frenzy. The animals would stop dead, their nostrils flaring, their eyes rolling white, and then, with a speed that defied their bulk, they would charge. They did not charge at men or dogs or children; they charged at the very concept of forward motion. They ran in circles, they reared, they bit the air. The police horses, the cab horses, the private carriages, all succumbed to the same invisible contagion.

It was a phenomenon that the newspapers, in their usual panic, began to call the "Equine Plague," though it was no plague of the body. It was a plague of the nerve, a sudden, collective snapping of the tether that bound the beast to the will of the rider.

Arthur Pym, a cabman of thirty years’ standing, stood in the middle of a junction in Whitechapel, his hands still on the reins, though the reins were useless. His horse, a bay mare named Bess, was not running. She was trembling. Her head was down, her ears pinned back flat against her neck, and a low, rhythmic moan was issuing from her throat. It was a sound Arthur had never heard before, a sound of profound and animal distress. Around them, the chaos was mounting. A carriage had overturned, its occupants screaming. A police constable lay on the cobblestones, his leg crushed by a thrashing pony, while the pony itself galloped in tight, screaming circles, kicking up dust and debris.

"Let her go, Pym!" shouted a voice from the crowd, though it might have been a shout of terror or command, the distinction was lost in the din. "She’s next! Look at her!"

Arthur looked at Bess. He saw the fear in her dark, intelligent eyes. It was not the blind panic of the others. It was recognition. She knew what was coming. She knew the world had gone wrong, and she was waiting for him to do something.

"She’s not mad," Arthur said, his voice surprisingly steady. "She’s just afraid. They’re all afraid. But she knows I’m here."

The instinct of the crowd was to shoot. It was the only solution the civilized world had for problems it did not understand. Men with revolvers were emerging from doorways. The police were trying to form a line, but their own mounts were bucking and rearing, making them as vulnerable as the civilians. The air was thick with the smell of horse sweat, coal smoke, and the metallic tang of fear.

Arthur knew he had only seconds. If he released the reins, Bess would join the stampede. If he tried to lead her away, the sheer force of her terror would drag him down. He looked around. To his left was the entrance to a damp, cellar-like bar that had been closed for the evening. The door was heavy oak, but the handle was brass, and it looked old.

"Stay here!" he told Bess, though he knew she would not understand the words, only the tone. He stepped out of the cab, his boots slipping on the wet stones. He grabbed the nearest available object—a heavy iron bar from a fence that had been torn loose in the panic—and hurled it through the window of the bar. The glass shattered with a crash that was almost lost in the screaming of the horses.

He was in the bar in two strides. It was dark and smelled of stale beer and damp wood. The landlord, a stout man with a face like a prune, was hiding behind the counter, clutching a bottle of gin.

"Out!" the man shrieked. "Get out before the things get in!"

"I need the cellar," Arthur said, not stopping. "The door is on the left. Open it."

"Madman!" the landlord yelled. "You’re a madman!"

Arthur did not argue. He took the iron bar, now slightly bent, and struck the lock of the cellar door. It was a simple mechanism, old and brittle. One strike, and it gave way. The door swung open, revealing a staircase that descended into absolute darkness.

"Go on, Bess," Arthur whispered. He was not in the cab. He had left the reins. He had to go with her. He could not leave her alone in the dark.

He climbed down the stairs, the iron bar still in his hand. The air grew colder and damper. At the bottom, he could see the faint outline of the mare. She had followed him. Her breathing was a loud, wet sound in the silence.

Arthur lit a match. The flare of light revealed a narrow space, just large enough for a horse to stand. It was a storage room, filled with old crates and broken bottles. It was not a place for a horse, but it was a place where the madness could not reach. The chaos above was muffled now, a dull roar like distant thunder.

He placed a bucket of water from the bar’s tap, which he had filled on his way down, and a piece of hay he had torn from the cab’s seat. Bess ate it with a trembling head.

Arthur sat on a crate, his back against the wall. He closed his eyes. He could hear the sound of the city above. The screaming had stopped, replaced by a different sound. It was the sound of hooves on pavement, a rhythmic, endless galloping. It was the sound of a world that had lost its mind, running in circles, forever and ever.
