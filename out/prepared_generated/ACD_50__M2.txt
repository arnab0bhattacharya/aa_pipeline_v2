The rain lashed against the leaded windows of the study in a steady, rhythmic hiss, blurring the grey afternoon into a watercolor of slate and charcoal. I stood by the mantelpiece, my pipe unlit in my hand, while my client, Mrs. Armitage, paced the length of the room with the agitated, quick-footed energy of a caged bird. Her husband, Mr. Julian Armitage, lay upon the chaise longue by the fire, a figure of terrible stillness. His face, usually flushed with the ruddy health of a man who had spent twenty years in the high sierras of Mexico, was now the color of old parchment. His eyes were open, fixed upon the ceiling, but they held no light; the spark of intellect that had once been so sharp and commanding had been extinguished, leaving behind only the blank stare of a vessel with its bottom broken.

“He has not moved since the nurse found him,” Mrs. Armitage said, her voice trembling not with grief, but with a frantic, intellectual curiosity. “The doctor says it is a stroke. A sudden paralysis of the motor nerves. But that is not the matter, Mr. Doyle. The matter is the letter.”

She stopped her pacing and pointed a gloved finger toward the desk. Upon the mahogany surface, amidst a scattering of mining reports and half-filled ledgers, lay a single sheet of heavy, cream-colored paper. Beside it rested a small, folded piece of card, upon which was sketched a crude but distinct image of a church façade, its bell tower leaning slightly to the left.

“Pick it up,” I said, moving from the mantelpiece. “Do not touch it with your bare fingers. The oils of your skin might obscure the impression of another’s.”

I took a pair of tweezers from my pocket and lifted the paper. It was a letter written in Spanish, the ink a faded violet, the handwriting hurried and cramped. I turned it over, examining the watermark. It was a common Mexican paper, from a mill in Puebla, dated three years prior. I read the text, my lips moving slightly as I parsed the archaic grammar. It was a confession of a sort, or rather, a warning. It spoke of a debt of honor, of a betrayal at a mine site called *La Cumbre*, and of a secret buried beneath the altar of the Church of San Miguel.

“‘The gold is not in the veins of the earth,’ I translated, my voice low. “‘It lies in the shadow of the cross. And I, who held the key, am the one who must seal the door. If the sun sets on the tenth of November without the key being returned, the debt is paid in blood.’”

Mrs. Armitage gasped. “November the tenth was today, Mr. Doyle. He has been waiting for this letter for three years. It arrived this morning by the post from Madrid. I saw the envelope. It was addressed to him in a hand I did not recognize. I told him to open it, but he merely held it in his hand, staring out the window. Then he collapsed.”

I looked at the sketch of the church again. The leaning tower was a specific detail, a flaw that no generic artist would have included. I turned to Mrs. Armitage. “Where did your husband acquire this sketch? Did he draw it himself?”

“No,” she said. “He found it in a book. A journal of a missionary who visited *La Cumbre* in 1860. He was obsessed with that place, Mr. Doyle. He spent ten years digging there. He left it five years ago, broke, and returned to England. He never spoke of why he left. He only said he had found something that was not gold.”

I walked over to the desk and examined the edge of the sketch. There was a small smudge of blue dust near the bottom corner. I knelt and looked at the floor beneath the desk. There were no footprints, but the rug was worn thin in a specific pattern, suggesting heavy traffic. I looked at Mr. Armitage again. His left hand was resting on the chaise, fingers curled slightly. On the ring finger, there was a faint, reddish abrasion, as if a ring had been removed in haste or force.

“Mrs. Armitage,” I said, rising to my feet. “Did your husband have a ring? A signet ring, perhaps?”

She looked at me, her eyes widening. “He did. It was his father’s. It had a seal carved with a cross and a pickaxe. I wore it myself for a week while he was in Mexico, to show I was with him. But I gave it back to him a month ago. I thought it was in his safe.”

“Is the safe open?”

“No. It is locked in the library.”

“Then we must go to the library.”

We crossed the hall, the sound of our footsteps muffled by the thick carpets. The library was a warm, dust-mote-filled sanctuary, lined with books on geology, Spanish history, and the lives of saints. The safe stood in the corner, a heavy iron box bolted to the floor. I examined the lock. It was a simple tumbler mechanism, but the keyhole was jammed with a fine powder.

“Gauze,” I muttered. “Or perhaps flour. It has been stuffed to prevent the key from turning. Someone knew the code, or had the key, and wanted to ensure it could not be opened.”

Mrs. Armitage shivered. “Who would do such a thing? My husband had no enemies here in Richmond. He was a quiet man, reclusive, but kind.”

“Quiet men often have the loudest secrets,” I said. I took a small brush from my pocket and gently swept the powder from the keyhole. A thin stream of white dust fell to the floor. I caught it in a glass vial. “This is not flour. It is too coarse. It is chalk, mixed with something else. Perhaps a residue from the mines.”
