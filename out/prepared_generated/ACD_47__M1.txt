The bell above the door of my establishment in Croydon gave its usual cheerful tinkle, a sound I had come to associate with the steady prosperity I had earned since leaving the service. It was a wet Tuesday afternoon in late October, the kind of grey, dripping day that sends the respectable folk of the town hurrying to their firesides. My shop, which dealt in fine groceries and imported goods, was quiet, the air thick with the scent of coffee, cinnamon, and damp wool.

The man who entered brought the chill of the day with him. He was tall, with the stoop of a scholar, and wore a heavy Ulster coat of excellent quality, though it was splashed with mud at the hem. He removed his hat—a fine Homburg—and revealed a high, pale forehead and thinning hair of a sandy colour. His face was long and sallow, his eyes a washed-out blue that seemed to take in everything and commit nothing to memory. He asked for a tin of Turkish tobacco and a box of lucifers, his voice quiet, precise, and utterly devoid of any regional accent.

It was as he stood at the counter, his long, white hands resting upon the polished wood, that the recognition struck me with the force of a physical blow. It was not the face, which had aged and thinned, but the hands. I had seen those hands before, and the memory they unlocked was one I had spent ten years trying to bury. It was a sun-bleached cantonment outside Peshawar, the air shimmering with heat, the air thick with the smell of dust and jasmine. And there, in the shadow of a banyan tree, those same long fingers had held not a box of matches, but a length of knotted cord.

My own hands, I noted with a detached sort of horror, were trembling as I made change. The man—this gentleman, this pillar of suburban respectability—paid me with a sovereign, gave a curt nod, and departed without a second glance. He did not know me. To him, I was merely a shopkeeper, a purveyor of tobacco, another cog in the machinery of his comfortable life. But I knew him. I knew him as Major Armitage knew the man who had strangled the subaltern’s wife in the heat of an Indian afternoon, a crime for which a poor, terrified syce had been hanged.

I did not sleep that night. Every creak of the floorboards, every rattle of the window sash against the wind, sent a jolt through my nerves. I had been a good soldier, a steady man, but this discovery had unsettled the very foundations of my world. I considered going to the police, but what could I tell them? That a respectable customer reminded me of a murder from a decade ago, a murder for which another man had been convicted? They would think me mad.

The following morning, the matter was taken out of my hands. I arrived at the shop to find the back door splintered and hanging from its hinges. Inside, a scene of calculated violation met my eyes. The petty cash box was gone, and a small amount of stock had been disturbed. But the burglar had been no common thief. He had passed over my ledger and the silver-topped inkwells on my desk. He had gone directly to a locked cabinet and forced it with a pry-bar. And from the drawer, he had taken only one thing: an old, leather-bound photograph album. Specifically, the photograph of my old regiment, the 27th Foot, taken at Rawalpindi in 1884.

The meaning was horribly clear. He had not recognised my face, but something—my manner, my bearing, a turn of phrase—had troubled him. He had come back, not for money, but for information. He had sought a record of the faces from that time, to see if mine was among them. The theft was a confirmation of his guilt, and of my peril.

It was then that I made my decision. I closed the shop, pinned a note to the door, and took the first train to Victoria. I needed a man who dealt not in the law, but in the truth. I needed an investigator.

His chambers were in a narrow street off the Strand, up a flight of worn stone stairs. The name on the glass read "J. B. Carrick, Confidential Enquiries." The room I entered was a testament to an orderly mind confronting a disorderly world. Books were stacked in neat, labelled piles; a map of the Home Counties, pricked with coloured pins, adorned one wall; the air smelled of pipe tobacco and old paper. Mr. Carrick himself was a compact man in his late fifties, with a trim grey beard and eyes that seemed to miss nothing. He wore a tweed jacket and listened to my story with the unnerving stillness of a hunting dog on a scent.

When I had finished, he steepled his fingers and regarded me across his desk. "A Peshawar cantonment, you say? 1884? And the man's name?"

"I never knew it," I said. "He was a visitor, an officer from another corps, I think. But I would never forget those hands."

"And this photograph," Carrick said, his voice crisp. "It is the only record of your presence there at that time?"

"It is. The others are with my family in the country. He has destroyed the proof of my connection to the place, and perhaps he believes he has silenced me."

Carrick rose and walked to the window, gazing down at the bustling street below. "He has not silenced you, Major. He has confirmed your suspicion. The theft of the photograph is the most telling detail of all. It is the act of a man who is afraid. A common burglar takes cash. A frightened man takes away a threat." He turned back to me, his expression now one of keen interest. "You say he bought Turkish tobacco?"

"A tin of it. And lucifers."
