The November fog had laid a thick, yellow-grey shroud over London, and the traffic of the Strand moved with the cautious deliberation of ships in an unfamiliar channel. It was in this dismal light that my companion, Mr. Julian Thorne, turned from the window of our sitting-room in Jermyn Street with a look of mild annoyance.

“A cab, I think,” said he. “And it has stopped at our door. The fellow is in some distress, by the sound of his horses.”

I had heard nothing beyond the usual rumble and clatter of the street, but I had long since ceased to be surprised by Thorne’s faculty for observation. A moment later, Mrs. Beeton’s heavy tread was heard upon the stair, and she announced a Mr. Silas Croft, a cabman.

He was a man of perhaps fifty, with a face the colour of a ripe plum from long exposure to the elements and, I suspected, to the public-house. His greatcoat was damp and steaming, and he twisted a battered bowler hat in his large, chapped hands. He was, in short, a perfect specimen of his class, yet there was a hunted look in his small, shrewd eyes that spoke of a trouble far beyond a broken fare or a constable’s summons.

“Sit down, Mr. Croft,” said Thorne, indicating a straight-backed chair. “You will forgive the observation, but you have the air of a man who has been driving for some hours without a rest.”

“Aye, sir, that I have,” the cabman rasped, his voice hoarse. “Since eight o’clock this morning. But it’s not the work, sir. It’s the business of last night that’s got me in a coil. The police have been at me already this morning, and the look in that Inspector’s eye… well, sir, I’ve seen kinder looks on a butcher’s dog.”

Thorne steepled his fingers. “Let us have it from the beginning.”

Croft took a deep breath. “It was last night, near ten o’clock. The fog was a proper pea-souper, worse than today. I was on the stand at Ludgate Circus when a gentleman hailed me. A tall, thin cove in a long black coat and a silk hat. He was in a terrible hurry, and his voice was sharp, like a man used to giving orders. He told me to take him to Number 14, Carthusian Street, in Clerkenwell.”

“And you drove him there?”

“Aye, sir. As quick as the fog would let me. He paid me the fare, a shilling and threepence, and got down at the door. I saw him go up the steps and use a key. Then I drove off.”

Thorne’s keen grey eyes were fixed upon the man’s face. “And this is the sum of your trouble? A passenger who was in a hurry?”

“No, sir! That’s just the start of it. This morning, the coppers came round to the cab-yard. They had a witness, they said—a night-watchman on the next street—who swore he saw me set a man down not at Carthusian Street, but at the other end of London, at a house in St. James’s Place. And they say a gentleman was found dead in his library at that St. James’s address this morning, and the time of his death is put at half-past ten last night. They say I’m lying about the Clerkenwell fare to cover my tracks. They say I was the last man to see him alive.”

A lesser man might have dismissed the tale as a drunken fancy, but I saw at once that it presented a neat and baffling paradox. How could one man be set down at two opposite ends of the metropolis at the same hour? And how could a cabman be implicated in a death at an address he swore he never visited?

Thorne rose and began to pace the room. “The police are interested in the passenger,” he murmured. “I, for the moment, am interested in the cab. Where is your vehicle, Croft?”

“Outside, sir. At the door.”

“Excellent. Watson, your coat, if you please. We shall take a look at the engine of this little mystery.”

We descended to the street. The cab was a four-wheeler of the Clarence type, its black panels beaded with moisture. Thorne walked around it once, slowly, his eyes missing nothing. He ran a gloved finger along the running board, then bent to peer at the wheels.

“The mud is consistent with a journey to Clerkenwell,” he remarked, half to himself. “The clay of the back streets, you see, Watson, quite different from the gravel of the West End parks. But that is not conclusive. A man might drive anywhere. Let us look within.”

He swung open the door and leaned inside. I watched as his long, nervous fingers explored the worn upholstery of the seat. He tapped the floor, then moved to the small, folding glass partition that separated the driver from his fare.

“Observe, Watson,” he said, his voice suddenly sharp. “Here is a point of some interest.”

I looked. On the lower edge of the glass partition, just where a passenger’s hand might rest to steady himself, was a smear of something dark.

“Is it blood?” I asked.

Thorne shook his head. He touched it, then brought his fingers to his nose. “No. Grease. And a particular kind of grease. It is not the common axle-grease of the cab itself. It is finer, more pungent. It smells faintly of tallow and… yes, of peppermint. An odd combination.” He peered closer. “And see, it has left a partial impression. A thumb, and the tip of a forefinger. The man who left this mark wore no gloves.”

He sat back on his heels, his brow furrowed in concentration. “Now, Croft, you say your passenger was a tall, thin gentleman in a long coat and silk hat. Did you notice his hands?”

The cabman thought for a moment. “His gloves, sir. He wore grey kid gloves. I remember, because he took one off to pay me, and the other he was pulling at as he got out. A nervous habit, like.”
