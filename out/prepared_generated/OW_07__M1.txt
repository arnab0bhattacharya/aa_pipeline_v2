It is always a mistake to arrive in London with a good conscience and a bad coat; but to arrive with a good coat and a conscience that is merely indifferent is to have understood the city before one has left the station. Mr. Cormac O’Malley, having disembarked from the Irish Mail with a valise containing three shirts, a volume of Villon, and a letter of introduction to the Duchess of Belgrave, possessed both the coat and the indifference. The coat was of Irish frieze, cut by a tailor in Galway who believed that a garment should outlast the wearer’s reputation, and it fitted Cormac with a severity that suggested he had been poured into it. As for the letter, it was written in a violet ink on paper that smelled faintly of regret.

He presented himself at the house in Belgrave Square on a Tuesday, which was the day the Duchess received those she did not know well enough to avoid. The butler, a man whose face seemed to have been carved out of a grey afternoon, looked at the letter and then at Cormac’s coat with equal suspicion.

“Her Grace,” said the butler, “is indisposed.”

“Naturally,” said Cormac, stepping past him with the grace of a cat that has stolen the cream. “Were she disposed, she would not be a Duchess. Indisposition is the only industry that flourishes in Mayfair.”

He walked into the drawing room. It was a chamber of gold and white, like the inside of a lily that had been gilded. The Duchess sat upon a divan that looked uncomfortable enough to be fashionable. She was a woman of fifty, who had preserved her beauty by the simple expedient of never having an emotion that would crease it.

“Who are you?” asked the Duchess, looking at him as if he were a stain on the Aubusson carpet.

“I am Cormac O’Malley,” he said, bowing. “I have a letter from my aunt, the Dowager of Sligo. She writes that you were kind to her in Paris in ’72.”

The Duchess took the letter. “I have never been to Paris,” she said, after a glance. “And I do not know a Dowager of Sligo.”

“Then,” said Cormac, with a smile that lit up the room more effectively than the gasoliers, “you have done me a great service. For if you had known her, you would have been obliged to be kind to me out of duty. As it is, you are free to be kind to me out of curiosity. And curiosity, Your Grace, is the only form of immortality that is available to the living.”

The Duchess looked at him. He was very handsome, in a wild, uncivilized way that was entirely new to her. He had the eyes of a saint and the mouth of a cynic.

“You are Irish,” she said.

“It is my only misfortune,” said Cormac. “For it forces me to be sincere, and sincerity is a bore in London. However, I shall endeavour to lie as much as possible while I am here.”

“Sit down,” said the Duchess. “But do not sit on the Louis Quinze. It is older than your family.”

“And perhaps less fragile,” murmured Cormac, sitting on a chair of spindly gold.

He stayed for an hour. He talked of art, which he said was the only reality; of politics, which he said was the only fiction; and of the Duchess’s diamonds, which he said were the only truth. When he left, he was invited to dinner on Thursday.

Within a month, Cormac O’Malley was the sensation of the season. He was invited everywhere. He was invited to the opera, where he sat in the box of a millionaire and told the millionaire that music was a mistake because it made one feel things one did not wish to feel. He was invited to country houses, where he shot pheasants and told the hostess that the birds had the better of it, for they died beautifully and were not expected to make conversation afterwards. He was invited to the studio of a painter who was painting the naked Truth, and Cormac told the painter that Truth should be kept in the dark, as she was far too plain for the light.

He was witty, he was wicked, he was penniless, and he was adored.

Yet, there was one house he did not enter.

It was the house of Lady Isolde, the widow of Sir Reginald, who had died of a sudden chill after forty years of a warm marriage. Lady Isolde lived in a small house in Curzon Street, surrounded by white flowers and silence. She was the only woman in London who had not asked for Cormac’s autograph.

He saw her first at a tea-party given by a poet who wrote about the sea but had never been in it. She was standing by a window, wearing a dress of black velvet that seemed to drink the light. She had hair the colour of autumn leaves and eyes that were like the sea before a storm.

Cormac approached her. He felt, for the first time in his life, a desire to be good.

“You are Lady Isolde,” he said.

“I am,” she said. Her voice was low and sad.

“I have heard that you do not like society,” said Cormac.

“I like it very much,” she said. “That is why I avoid it. One should never ruin a beautiful illusion by the vulgar contact of reality.”

“That is a paradox,” said Cormac. “I adore paradoxes. They are the only things that are true.”

“You are Mr. O’Malley,” she said. “They say you are very clever.”

“They say wrong,” said Cormac. “I am merely observant. Cleverness is a form of indigestion; it is a struggle to get rid of what one has taken in. Observation is a form of worship.”

She looked at him then, and he felt as though he had been struck. There was a pity in her eyes that he found more intoxicating than the flattery of the Duchess.
