The November dusk gathered early over the yard of Mrs. Thorne’s establishment in the market town of Whetwick. I stood at the casement, watching the soot-stained snow fall on the cobbles. The wind made a forlorn, pious moan, sweeping in from the moors, and its chill crept through the warped wood of the window frame to strike at my ankles. In the dim parlor behind me, the fire was expiring. Mrs. Thorne sat at her table, her thimble glittering faintly in the dusk, her pale eyes fixed upon a length of unfinished muslin. I turned from the glass, feeling the familiar, gnawing pinch of the cold, and took up my own needle. I was glad of the darkness; it hid the sudden, stinging tears that had sprung to my eyes, though I ground my teeth together in fierce anger at my own weakness. Why weep? Tears were a luxury, a self-indulgence I had long since forsworn.

I, Clara Leigh, had known little in my nineteen years but the grey, stone-walled charity of the Greenlow Asylum. There, I had been taught that my lot was to be meek, to be useful, and to ask for nothing. When the time came for me to leave that asylum, I had been sent to Mrs. Thorne, a widow of gentle temper but straitened means, to learn the trade of a seamstress. Mrs. Thorne was kind; but her kindness was salted with the bitter, pinching realities of poverty. We worked long into the evenings, our hands stiff, our eyes aching in the smoky flare of the tallow candle. We were sinking, both of us, in a quiet, desperate struggle against the rising tide of debt. I did not rebel against the labor—labor was my birthright, the sole inheritance of the orphan—but there were moments when the spirit within me chafed violently against the narrow, unending round of hemming and seaming.

A sharp knock at the outer door broke the quiet of the room. Mrs. Thorne started, dropping her needle. It was an unwonted hour for a visitor in our decrepit lane. I went to the door and opened it upon the biting wind. A tall figure stood on the threshold, wrapped in a dark cloak; behind her, a hired carriage waited, its lamps casting a lurid glare through the snow. It was Mrs. Valmont, a lady of considerable estate, whose condescension had occasionally visited Mrs. Thorne’s shop.

"Step within, madam," I said, curtseying, though I knew not why my heart beat so suddenly, and so fast, against my ribs. I felt a presentiment, sudden and dark, as if a change were coming.

Mrs. Valmont swept into the parlor, bringing with her a scent of heavy perfume, a foreign scent that seemed to choke the small, chill room. She was a handsome woman, with a clear, pale skin, dark, observant eyes, and a mouth whose line was firm to the point of cruelty. She looked at Mrs. Thorne, who had risen in flustered greeting, then her gaze turned to me, standing silent by the door. She appraised me slowly, from my coarse gown to my plain, pale face, and something in her look made the blood mount to my cheek. It was the look one bends upon a beast of burden, or a slave put up for market.

"You are Miss Leigh, I presume," she said. Her voice was low, deliberate, and chillingly sweet. "I have heard of your diligence from the good mistress of Greenlow. You are clever with your needle, they say, and possess a certain—aptitude. I am in want of a young person to attend upon my daughter, to make her dresses, to read to her, and to be, in short, a constant companion. I offer you the position."

The words fell upon the air with the weight of a sentence. Mrs. Thorne looked at me, a sudden, wistful entreaty in her eyes. I knew, without her speaking, that she could not, in conscience, bid me stay. The rent was unpaid; the winter promised to be cruel. Yet I knew, too, that she loved me, and that she dreaded the power of this imperious stranger. I looked from Mrs. Thorne’s thin, anxious face to Mrs. Valmont’s commanding one.

"Madam," I said, and my voice surprised me, for it was steady, though a fire burned beneath my ribs, "I am grateful for your offer. But I am Mrs. Thorne’s apprentice. My duty is here."

"Duty is a fine word," Mrs. Valmont returned, her lip curling slightly, "but it will not feed you, nor will it pay your mistress’s debts. I know the state of this house. You will be turned into the streets before the spring thaws. Come, girl, I offer you a roof, comforts, a wage. Do not be a fool."

I felt a sudden, sharp anger, a fierce, uprising sense of injustice. I knew she spoke truth; but the manner of her speaking goaded me. I was not a dog to be whistled for, nor a chattel to be bought. I was a human soul, with all the rights of a human soul, though I stood in a coarse apron. I drew myself up.

"I may be poor, madam," I said, "and I may be an orphan; but I have a will of my own, and a spirit that is not for sale. I will not be spoken to as if I were without understanding."

Mrs. Valmont’s eyes flashed. She was not accustomed to contradiction. For a moment, I thought she would strike me, or turn and leave the house; but instead, a strange, cold smile crossed her face.

"Upon my word," she said, "you have spirit. It is a rare quality in one of your station. I like spirit. I assure you, Miss Leigh, my motives are not unkind. I offer you a life beyond this dingy room. I offer you education, society, the chance to be something more than a drudge."
