Mrs. Eversley had been a wife for three years, and in that time she had come to understand that her life was a thing of surfaces. There was the polished mahogany of the dining table, the smooth lawn of the garden at Surbiton, the unblemished porcelain of her husband’s character. Arthur Eversley was a man of surfaces himself, an estate agent of considerable local success, who saw the world as a series of properties to be valued, sold, and tidied up for the next owner. He was kind, in his way; he brought her chocolates on Fridays and spoke of ‘the little woman’ with a proprietary affection that left no room for doubt as to who owned whom.

It was Mrs. Pankhurst—not *the* Mrs. Pankhurst, but a fierce, bird-like creature of the same name—who was the cause of the disturbance. She ran the Surbiton League for Progressive Thought from her cluttered drawing-room, a place that smelled of damp wool and revolutionary fervour. Here, the comfortable certainties of the suburbs were subjected to a rigorous and often bewildering examination. The talk was of municipal ownership, the irrationality of the Anglican creed, and the biological determinism of the sexes. Mrs. Eversley, who had hitherto considered her greatest intellectual achievement the correct ordering of a dinner party, found herself electrified. The world, which had seemed a fixed and solid thing, was suddenly revealed to be a provisional arrangement, full of hidden mechanisms and unjust assumptions.

Arthur, at first, was amused. “Another meeting of the blue-stockings, my dear?” he would ask, pecking her on the cheek. “Did you learn how to vote in a parish election?” He saw it as a harmless hobby, a feminine eccentricity, like collecting china dogs. But the change in his wife was not so easily dismissed. She began to talk about ‘the economic basis of the family’ and to question the very nature of property. One evening, as he was detailing the sale of a semi-detached villa in Ewell, she interrupted him.

“Arthur, does it not strike you as absurd that a person’s entire security should depend upon a pile of bricks and mortar? We are all, in a sense, tenants of the earth.”

Arthur put down his fork. “I beg your pardon?”

“Ownership,” she said, her eyes bright with a new and dangerous light, “is a legal fiction. A man owns his house, but he does not own the air that passes through it, or the sunlight that falls upon it. He is merely a steward.”

“I am a steward who sells stewardships for a commission of two and a half per cent,” Arthur said, with a pained smile. “And a very good living it makes, too. I think you have been listening to some very muddled talk, my dear.”

The muddled talk, however, continued to ferment in Margaret Eversley’s mind. The League’s discussions on the ‘Woman Question’ were particularly potent. They spoke of the economic dependence of wives, of the marriage contract as a form of indentured servitude. It was all very well to be a decorative object in a man’s home, but what if one wished to be a functional citizen? What if one wished, simply, to be a person?

The crisis arrived on a Tuesday. Arthur had just concluded a particularly lucrative deal involving a property with a desirable south-facing aspect and a modernised scullery. He was in a mood of expansive satisfaction.

“It’s a question of seeing the potential, Margaret,” he was saying, brandishing the evening paper. “The right presentation, a lick of paint, a fair price… it’s all a matter of judgement.”

“I want to earn my own money,” Margaret said.

Arthur’s smile faltered. He looked at her as if she had suggested he sell the house for scrap. “Earn your own money? What on earth for? You have everything you need.”

“I have everything you have decided I need,” she corrected him, her voice steady. “I want my own income. My own capital. It is a matter of principle. A person who is not economically independent is not truly free.”

Arthur stared at her. The word ‘free’ hung in the air between them, an alien and unsettling concept. He saw not his quiet, biddable wife, but a stranger, an agitator from one of those dreadful meetings. He saw his home, his ordered life, threatened by an incomprehensible and irrational force. He was a practical man; he dealt in facts, in deeds and surveys. This was not a fact he could survey. It was, he concluded with a cold certainty, a problem.

And so, the following Saturday, he took the train to Blackheath to consult his father-in-law, Mr. Abernathy.

Mr. Abernathy was a retired provision merchant, a man whose entire philosophy was built upon the soundness of British goods and the folly of new-fangled ideas. He listened to Arthur’s troubled recital with a face of grim foreboding, occasionally shaking his head and making small, disapproving noises in his throat.

“She used the word ‘free’?” he asked, when Arthur had finished.

“Free,” Arthur confirmed, as if reporting a case of measles. “She wants to set up some sort of… enterprise. She wouldn’t say what. She spoke of ‘utilising her faculties’.”

Mr. Abernathy grunted. “It’s these societies. Filling women’s heads with nonsense. A woman’s place is in the home, looking after her husband and children. It’s the natural order. My dear wife, God rest her, never once questioned the price of a leg of mutton, let alone the foundations of society.”

“What am I to do?” Arthur asked, feeling helpless. “Reason is quite useless. She quotes books at me. She talks of ‘the proletariat of the home’.”

Mr. Abernathy was silent for a long moment, staring into the embers of his fire. “There is only one thing for it,” he said at last. “We must present a united front. You must be firm. You must remind her of her duties. If she persists, you must cut off the allowance. These ideas are like weeds; they cannot flourish in poor soil. She has too much time and comfort on her hands. A little hardship will soon bring her back to her senses.”
