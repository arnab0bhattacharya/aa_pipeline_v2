The fog was a living thing in Whitehall, a beast of soot and damp that coiled around the iron railings of Scotland Yard and seeped through the seams of a person’s clothing. Elara Vance felt its chill in her bones as she stood for the first time on the worn stone steps, her gloved hands clutching her reticule so tightly the knuckles were white. She had rehearsed her speech on the omnibus, the words a neat, logical chain of reason. Thomas was not a bomb-thrower. He was a bookbinder. He loved Keats and had a weakness for treacle tart. He was, above all, her brother.

Inside, the air was thick with the scent of boiled wool, floor polish, and the faint, acidic tang of official ink. She was shown into a small, tidy office where a man sat behind a heavy oak desk. He was perhaps fifty, with a neatly trimmed moustache the colour of dried sand and eyes of a pale, washed-out blue that seemed to see everything and give nothing away. A brass plaque on his desk identified him as Inspector Alistair Croft.

“Miss Vance,” he said, his voice a low, courteous rumble. He did not rise, but gestured to the chair opposite his desk. “Please. Sit.”

Elara sat, the dampness of her skirts spreading a dark patch on the worn leather. She began her speech, the words tumbling out, polished and perfect. She spoke of Thomas’s gentle nature, his quiet passions, the impossibility of him being involved in the seditious pamphlets and secret meetings the police alleged. She painted a portrait of a dreamer, a man of ink and paper, not of fire and dynamite.

Inspector Croft listened with an unnerving stillness. When she finished, her voice catching on a final, desperate plea, he gave a small, almost imperceptible nod.

“Your concern for your brother is admirable, Miss Vance,” he said, his tone even and devoid of pity. “And I have made a note of your testimony. However, the evidence against Mr. Vance is not a matter of opinion. It is a matter of record. He was apprehended at a known anarchist meeting place. Literature of a highly inflammatory nature was found in his possession.”

“But a book can’t hurt anyone!” she insisted, leaning forward. “He collects pamphlets, he reads everything! It’s the nature of his work, his curiosity…”

“Curiosity is a fine thing in a scientist, Miss Vance,” Croft replied, his pale eyes fixed on hers. “In a man associated with a plot to disrupt the Queen’s Jubilee procession, it is a liability. The law is not concerned with his character, only his actions and associations. The matter is now for the courts. There is nothing I can do.”

His politeness was a wall, smoother and higher than any brick. He was not cruel; he was simply immovable. He was a functionary of the state, and she was a variable he had already calculated and dismissed. She left his office feeling not angry, but hollowed out, as if her carefully constructed arguments had been turned to dust in her mouth.

The next day, the fog was thicker. Elara returned to Scotland Yard. She was shown to the same office, to the same man. She did not repeat her speech. Instead, she spoke of Thomas’s childhood, of the time he’d tried to build a flying machine from a parasol and fallen out of the apple tree. She spoke of their mother, who had died when they were young, and the promise Elara had made to look after him. She was no longer appealing to the law; she was appealing to the man.

Inspector Croft listened again. His expression did not change. “Miss Vance,” he said when she was done, “the past is a foreign country. We are concerned with the present. Your brother’s actions in the present are what have brought him here. My sympathy is genuine, but it has no currency in this room.”

On the third day, she brought a book. It was a slim volume of poems by Christina Rossetti, the pages soft with handling. “He was reading this the night before they took him,” she said, placing it on the corner of his immaculate desk. “He told me it was the most beautiful thing he’d ever read. Does a man who loves this kind of beauty truly wish to see the world torn apart?”

Inspector Croft looked at the book, then at her. For the first time, she saw a flicker of something in his eyes—not sympathy, but a kind of deep, weary confusion. He did not touch the book. “Poetry is a luxury, Miss Vance. Your brother is accused of conspiring to deny others the luxury of safety. The two things do not compute.”

By the fourth day, her arrival was anticipated. The constable at the door gave her a look that was half-pity, half-annoyance, but he led her to Inspector Croft without comment. The room felt smaller now, the air heavier. The inspector looked tired. The neat lines of his desk seemed to mock her own chaotic desperation.

“Miss Vance,” he began, before she could speak. “I must ask you to stop this.”

“Stop what?” she asked, her voice quiet. “Loving my brother? Hoping for his release? Is that a crime now, too?”

“It is a torment,” he said, the words slipping out before he could stop them. He immediately looked away, towards the rain-streaked window. “To the process of the law. It is… inefficient.”

“Inefficient,” she repeated, the word tasting like ash. “You speak of my brother’s life as if it were a faulty piece of machinery.”

He turned back to her, his face a mask of official composure once more, but his hands, resting on the desk, were clenched. “The law is a machine, Miss Vance. It must be. If it were to pause for every tearful sister, for every grieving mother, it would grind to a halt. It would cease to be just. It processes facts. It delivers verdicts. Your presence here changes nothing. It only prolongs the agony.”
