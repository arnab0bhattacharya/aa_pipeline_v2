The sun, as it does in these latitudes, did not set so much as it was extinguished, leaving the sky a bruised purple and the lagoon a mirror of absolute black. I sat upon the driftwood, my field notebook open on my knee, watching the tide pull back to reveal the skeletal fingers of the reef. Beside me, the ship’s mate, a man named Grubb whose intellect seemed to evaporate in the heat, was busy with his net and a canvas sack that smelled faintly of brine and mildew.

"I’ve got another dozen," Grubb said, his voice thick with the satisfaction of a man who believes he is making a fortune. "You can’t sell these in London, Professor. But in the pet shops, the curiosity corners? They’ll pay a shilling a head. Maybe two. That’s a week’s wages for me, just for sitting in this godforsaken place."

I looked at the sack. Inside, the shapes writhed with a peculiar, synchronized rhythm. They were not the common shore crabs one finds in the tidal pools of the Channel Islands. These creatures, which I had tentatively named *Brachypoda obscura*, were small, no larger than a man’s thumb, with a carapace of deep, iridescent blue that seemed to absorb the moonlight rather than reflect it. Their legs were long and jointed in a way that suggested a complexity of movement I had never seen in the arthropods of the Pacific.

"Grubb," I said, trying to keep the tremor out of my voice, "leave the sack open. I wish to observe them before we board the *H.M.S. Endeavour* in the morning."

Grubb grunted. "Observation don’t fill the belly, sir. And it don’t fill the purse either. I’m keeping them."

He closed the sack and tied it with a rough knot. I watched him do it with a sense of profound unease. It was not the greed that troubled me, though Grubb’s avarice was as constant and reliable as the tides. It was the behavior of the crabs themselves. I had spent three days on this atoll, a place of such desolation that even the birds seemed to avoid its low, sandy ridges. Yet these creatures were everywhere. They did not scuttle in the frantic, erratic panic of the hunted. They moved in clusters, in lines, in patterns that defied the simple stimulus-response model we applied to invertebrates.

I returned to my tent, a flimsy affair of oiled canvas, and sat by my kerosene lamp. The wind was rising, whistling through the palm fronds. I took out my sketchbook. I had drawn them a dozen times, but the drawings always looked wrong. They looked too deliberate. I recalled the incident from that afternoon. I had dropped a piece of bread crust near the waterline. I expected them to swarm, to tear it apart with the mindless hunger of nature. Instead, a single crab emerged from the sand. It approached the bread, stopped, and waited. It did not eat it. It watched me. When I picked up my net, it retreated. When I sat down, it advanced. I had assumed it was a coincidence, a quirk of the species. But now, sitting in the dark, the memory felt less like a quirk and more like an experiment.

I went outside. The moon was full, casting a silver light over the lagoon. I crept back to the spot where I had seen them. The sand was still. I sat down, mimicking my posture from the afternoon. I waited.

Five minutes passed. Ten. The heat was oppressive, but I dared not move. Then, from the shadow of a rock, a blue shape emerged. It moved with a smooth, liquid grace, unlike the jerky motion of any crab I had known. It stopped three feet from me. It raised its front claws, not in a threat, but in a way that suggested... attention.

I held my breath. My heart hammered against my ribs. I was a scientist, a man of logic and classification, yet I felt a primal fear, the fear of being observed by something that understood the observer. The crab tilted its body. It did not blink, for crabs do not blink, but there was a shift in the angle of its eyes, a dark, glassy stare that seemed to pierce through the flesh and look at the machinery within.

I raised a hand, slowly. The crab mirrored the movement. I lowered my hand. The crab lowered its claws.

It was not mimicry. Mimicry is a defense mechanism, a blind repetition. This was communication.

I felt a cold sweat break out on my forehead. I thought of the stories in the newspapers about the natives of the South Sea, how they spoke of spirits in the water, of eyes in the deep. I had always dismissed such tales as the ramblings of superstitious savages. But here, in the moonlight, with this small, blue creature before me, the line between the savage and the civilized, the animal and the human, began to blur.

I stood up. The crab did not retreat. It watched me. I walked away, my legs feeling weak. I did not look back, but I knew I was being watched. I knew, with a certainty that defied all my training, that I had not discovered a new species of crab. I had made contact.

The next day, the *H.M.S. Endeavour* arrived. Grubb was in high spirits. He had filled three sacks with the creatures. "Look at that," he said, holding up a sack. "Shining blue. They’re alive and kicking. I’ll have a crate ready for the captain’s wife. She’s always after oddities for the conservatory."

I looked at the sacks. Inside, the crabs were still. They lay on their backs, legs twitching in unison, a subtle, rhythmic pulsing that looked less like life and more like a signal.

"Grubb," I said, my voice hollow. "We must leave them. We must bury the sacks. We must burn the tents."

Grubb laughed. "You’re not right in the head, Professor. The heat’s got you."
