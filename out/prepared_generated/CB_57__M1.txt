It was in the third week of the Michaelmas term that Mrs. Frobisher of Ashworth Grange sent for me to her private sitting-room. The wind that day had a voice in it—a long, thin, complaining whistle that found out every crack in the ancient window-frames. I found her standing before the fire, her tall figure rigid, her face a pale mask of some emotion she was determined to keep in check.

“Miss Vance,” she began, without preamble, “I wish you to keep a journal of my daughter’s progress. A daily record of her studies, her temper, her health. I wish to be apprised of all.”

This was a reasonable request, and I bowed my assent. A governess’s duty is to report, and I had always prided myself on the clarity of my weekly accounts.

“Very good,” she said, her voice hard as flint. “But understand me. I want the truth. Not a pretty picture painted for my benefit. I want to know if the child is listless, if she is stubborn, if she is unhappy. And I want to know why. I want to know of all that passes in this house when I am not present to see it.”

There was a strange, feverish intensity in her eyes. She was not, I perceived, a woman consumed by a mother’s ordinary anxieties. This was something sharper, more desperate. I left her presence with the leather-bound book she had given me feeling like a weight of iron in my hands.

For several days, I wrote as I had been bidden. I recorded that Elspeth had mastered her declensions in Latin, but had wept over her needlework; that her appetite was good, but her sleep was disturbed by dreams of the sea. My entries were plain and factual. I wrote for one reader, and I wrote honestly.

It was on the following Sunday, after the household had retired, that I was summoned again to the drawing-room. Mrs. Frobisher was not there, but the fire had been built up, and on the small table beside her favourite chair lay my journal. It was not where I had left it, which was on the escritoire in the schoolroom. A cold dread, sharp and sudden as a winter draught, stole into my heart. I opened the book. My own words looked back at me, but on the margin of a page describing Elspeth’s afternoon walk, a faint, spidery pencil mark was visible. It was a small, neat cross. It was placed beside my sentence: ‘Elspeth and I walked as far as the beech copse; her father’s horse was not in the stables, and the groundskeeper said he had ridden out towards the moor.’

The truth struck me with the force of a physical blow. She was not reading for her daughter’s sake. She was using me—using the child’s life as a key to unlock the secrets of her husband’s. She was a spy in her own home, and I was to be her instrument. A hot flush of shame and anger mounted to my face. To be made a party to such a sordid watchfulness was an indignity I could not passively bear. My spirit, which had been trained to submission, rose in revolt.

From that day, my journal had two readers. For the first, Mrs. Frobisher, I wrote a simple, domestic chronicle. Elspeth’s moods, her lessons, the state of the garden. I described the drawing-room fire burning low and the master’s chair standing empty. I wrote of the silence in the house, a silence that seemed to deepen with every day of his absence. I laid the bait in plain, unadorned sentences, knowing she would read between them, that her hungry eyes would seek the one name, the one fact, that mattered to her.

For the second reader—the one I imagined in my own mind, a stern and righteous judge—I wrote another story entirely. In the same hand, with the same ink, I began to record my own thoughts. I wrote of the injustice of her position, a wife made a warden. I wrote of the chill that entered the room with her, a chill that had nothing to do with the Yorkshire wind. I described the look in her eyes—not of love, not of grief, but of a fierce, consuming possession. And of him, the master, Mr. Frobisher, I wrote only what I saw: a man with a restless spirit, who looked at his wife as if she were a fortress he longed to escape, and at his daughter as if she were a fragile, beloved ghost.

The two accounts lived side-by-side on the same pages. One was a surface, placid and clear; the other was the dark, churning water beneath. One day, Elspeth was fractious and would not attend to her geography. I wrote: ‘The child was out of sorts today, and cried for her father.’ This was for Mrs. Frobisher. Then, my pen moved on, and for my secret, righteous reader, I added: ‘She cried for him because she feels the emptiness he has left. She is a sensitive barometer of the storms in this house, and she registers a tempest that her mother mistakes for a simple absence.’

I knew Mrs. Frobisher was reading. I would enter the schoolroom to find the journal moved, its ribbon marker altered. A faint scent of her violet perfume sometimes lingered on its pages. She never spoke of it, and I never accused her. Our battle was fought in silence, across the written word. She sought her evidence, and I gave it to her, twisted about with a truth she could not afford to see. I was no longer merely a governess; I was a chronicler of a domestic tragedy, a moralist passing judgment in the only court that would have me.
