Mrs. Eliza Prendergast, wife to the rector of Little Chalford, was possessed of a benevolence so active as to be nearly indistinguishable from tyranny. It was not in her nature to leave a situation to its own quiet development; she saw the world as a garden of human beings, and considered it her duty to prune, to weed, and to espalier the more promising shoots into pleasing forms. Her latest project was the joining of two lives which had hitherto shown no inclination towards conjunction.

The first was that of Dr. Alistair Finch, a man of some thirty years, who had inherited his practice from his father and ran it with a quiet efficiency that left him ample time for his books and his solitary walks. He was possessed of a good understanding and a dry, ironic wit, which he usually reserved for his own amusement. The second was Miss Harriet Bellweather, the niece and companion of the squire, Mr. Fitzwilliam. Miss Bellweather was a young woman of a gentle disposition and a keen, if private, intelligence. She managed her uncle’s household with a discretion so complete that he was seldom aware of her efforts, and she found a profound satisfaction in the order she created, a satisfaction she had no wish to share with a husband.

To Mrs. Prendergast, the logic of the match was as plain as the parish church on a clear day. Here was a man of sense and a woman of sense; both were of an amiable temper, both were settled in the same village, and both were, in her opinion, lamentably solitary. She could not conceive of a happiness more complete than the one she was prepared to bestow upon them, and she set about her work with the cheerful energy of a general planning a decisive campaign.

Her first move was to contrive an encounter at the cottage of an ailing parishioner, old Mrs. Gable. Dr. Finch was there on his professional rounds, and Miss Bellweather had come with a basket of nourishing broth and a new novel for the invalid’s entertainment. Mrs. Prendergast arrived shortly after, her own basket of sympathy and advice considerably larger than either.

“My dear Miss Bellweather!” she exclaimed, seizing her by the hand. “How good of you to come! And Dr. Finch, I see you are here in your official capacity. What a happy coincidence! You are both so attentive to the poor. Your characters are quite alike in their goodness.”

Dr. Finch, who had been listening to Mrs. Gable’s breathing with his stethoscope, straightened up and gave a slight bow. “I believe Mrs. Gable’s health is of more immediate concern than the similarities of our characters, Madam.”

“Indeed, indeed,” Mrs. Prendergast agreed, waving a dismissive hand. “But it is a comfort to see such virtue united. Miss Bellweather, you must ask the Doctor for his opinion on that book you have brought. He is a great reader, you know. A mind like yours would surely be a balm to him.”

Miss Bellweather, her cheeks colouring slightly, murmured, “I am sure Dr. Finch has no interest in novels of the sentimental kind.”

“On the contrary,” said the Doctor, with a perfectly straight face. “I find them an excellent study in the disorders of the imagination. Though I confess I am more often called upon to treat the disorders of the body.”

The conversation was thus brought to a stand, and Mrs. Prendergast, finding her chief actors unwilling to perform, soon announced her departure. As she left, she imparted a final, significant glance at them both, which was intended to convey a world of encouragement.

For the next week, the village of Little Chalford seemed to shrink in size. Dr. Finch and Miss Bellweather found themselves meeting at the post office, at the baker’s, and once, most awkwardly, by the stile on the path to the common. Their exchanges were perfectly civil, but brief, each sentence delivered with the haste of one wishing to conclude an interview. They were polite to one another, and united in a silent, fervent hope that they would not be forced to speak for long.

Mrs. Prendergast, however, was not so easily discouraged. She perceived their shyness not as a lack of inclination, but as a modesty that required her firmer guidance. She resolved on a more direct assault. She would invite them both to the rectory for tea, under the guise of consulting Dr. Finch on the parish’s new drainage scheme and asking Miss Bellweather’s advice on the choice of wallpaper for the guest chamber.

The invitations were sent and accepted, for to refuse Mrs. Prendergast was to court a week of pointed lectures on Christian fellowship. Dr. Finch arrived first, armed with a set of rather abstract notes on sanitation. Miss Bellweather followed shortly after, carrying a sample of chintz.

“Ah, here you both are!” cried their hostess, beaming. “Mr. Prendergast is sadly detained in his study with a theological point, but I knew I could rely on you both to entertain me. Now, you must sit together and examine these samples. Dr. Finch, you have a man’s eye for utility; Miss Bellweather, you have a woman’s eye for beauty. Together, you cannot fail.”

They were seated side-by-side on a small sofa. For a moment, an oppressive silence fell, broken only by the ticking of the mantel clock. Miss Bellweather stared at the floral pattern in her lap as if it held the secrets of the universe. Dr. Finch examined his cuffs with an intensity he usually reserved for a puzzling symptom.

It was Miss Bellweather who, with a sudden burst of desperate courage, spoke first. “Dr. Finch,” she said, her voice a little too bright. “I find myself in a puzzle. My uncle’s spaniel, Pompey, has developed a most peculiar habit of barking at the moon. Do you suppose it is a nervous complaint?”
