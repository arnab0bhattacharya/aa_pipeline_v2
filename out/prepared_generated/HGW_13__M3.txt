It was upon the 14th of October that the first undeniable anomalies appeared in the weekly returns of the General Board of Health, and it is a curious reflection upon the collective mentality of our official class that it required the plain, blunt insistence of a mere second-class clerk to force the matter upon the attention of a reluctant public. I will not disguise from the reader that I was that clerk, and that the business, from its very inception, struck me as being compounded in equal parts of medical mystery and administrative imbecility.

The facts of the case were simple enough, though alarmingly cumulative. I had been engaged, in my capacity at Whitehall, in collating the mortality statistics forwarded by the various district registrars. It was a dull, mechanical business, this tabulation of human wreckage, a sort of bookkeeping of the grave, and I had grown tolerably familiar with the ordinary fluctuations of phthisis and enteric fever. But about the middle of October, the figures for the eastern and southern districts of London began to display a most extraordinary and unaccountable leap. In Poplar, Stepney, and Deptford, the death rate had nearly trebled in the space of a fortnight.

The symptoms, as reported in the margins by puzzled local practitioners, were of a baffling, not to say terrifying, character. The onset was sudden, a profound and unaccountable lethargy, accompanied by a bluish discoloration of the fingernails and a rapid, feeble pulse. The patient, often a robust working-man or a healthy servant-girl, would simply sit down, or lie down, and decline to move. There was no rash, no fever, no inflammation of the throat. They merely seemed merely to be running down, like a faulty clockwork mechanism, and within forty-eight hours, a surprising number of them had stopped entirely.

It was this peculiar passivity that distinguished the new visitation from any ordinary influenza. There was no groaning, no thrashing in delirium; only a quiet, half-sleeping resignation to an overpowering exhaustion. The local doctors were frankly at sea.

I took my abstracts to Mr. Voules. Mr. Voules was our departmental head, a large, heavy man with a grey beard and a singularly complete ignorance of everything beyond the routine of his own office. He wore thick gold spectacles and had a way of breathing heavily through his nose when any new proposition was made to him.

“The figures, Mr. Voules,” I said, pointing with my pen to the red-inked totals, “are quite unprecedented. We are losing nearly three hundred souls a week in the riverside districts alone, and the thing is spreading westward. I have here returns from Camberwell and Lambeth showing a marked increase in the last four days.”

Mr. Voules stared at me for a moment, took off his spectacles, wiped them very slowly with a silk handkerchief, and put them on again.

“Nonsense, Bickerton,” he said, with heavy severity. “Absolute nonsense. It is merely a seasonal variation. A slight excess of chest affections, due to the damp weather. We have had such fluctuations before.”

“But, sir,” I urged, “the symptoms are quite unlike anything in our records. The local men are calling it a sleeping sickness, or a blood-failure. They are entirely bewildered.”

Mr. Voules drew himself up with a ponderous dignity. “The local men, Bickerton,” he said, “are notoriously prone to panic. It is the business of this department to steady the public mind, not to encourage the sensationalism of the halfpenny press. The mortality is somewhat elevated, I grant you, but it is quite within the limits of ordinary fluctuation. You may file the returns.”

I argued the point for half an hour, but it was like throwing peas at a brick wall. He sat there, breathing heavily, reiterating the phrases “seasonal variation” and “public mind” until I was fain to retire, baffled and exceedingly indignant, to my own desk.

It was quite clear that nothing would be done by the authorities. The official mind, when confronted with an unpleasant fact, has a remarkable faculty for looking the other way. But the figures grew. The next day’s returns showed a further advance, and a sudden, disquieting jump in the registers of Marylebone and Westminster. The thing was crossing the river. It was marching westward, along the tramway routes and the lines of the underground railway, as methodically as an invading army.

I sat at my desk, staring at the totals, and a very uncomfortable sense of responsibility took hold of me. Here was I, a clerk with forty shillings a week and a profound respect for my own position, in possession of facts that meant, if I read them aright, a devastating pestilence. And there was Mr. Voules, serenely oblivious, and the newspapers, which I scanned eagerly every morning in the train, either absolutely silent or printing absurd and contradictory rumours.

The _Daily Chronicle_ had a paragraph about a “mild epidemic of influenza in the eastern suburbs.” The _Evening News_ was more imaginative, headlining the affair as “The Sleeping Terror,” and inventing wild stories of whole streets being stricken in a single night. This sort of thing was bad enough, but the official silence was worse. The public did not know what to believe, and ignorance, in such matters, is the very handmaid of panic.

It was on the Thursday evening, as I sat in my lodging in Bloomsbury, turning the thing over in my mind, that I determined to take the matter into my own hands. I had a slight acquaintance with a journalist, a man named Pendock, who worked for the _Morning Observer_. He was a clever, energetic fellow, with a sharp nose for a scandal and a wholesome contempt for official obstruction. I had met him once at a scientific club in Soho, and we had shared a mutual dislike of red tape.

I found Pendock in a small, smoke-filled office in Fleet Street, surrounded by a litter of proofs and telegrams. He was a thin, dark man, with a quick, nervous manner and an unlighted cigarette perpetually between his lips.
