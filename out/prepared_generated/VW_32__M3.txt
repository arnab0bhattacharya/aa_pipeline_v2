The heat pressed against the glass like a living thing, breathing its heavy, white breath upon the panes, so that Eleanor, standing in the long, cool room that smelled of dried rosemary and old stone, felt the very air outside was a solid mass, a yellow, pulsing jelly into which one must presently plunge. She held a little book of phrases in her hand, but her eyes had left the page; they had wandered out, through the window, over the blistered tiles of the verandah, to where the mountains stood, intensely blue, as if cut out of cardboard and placed there to keep the sky from falling. And all the while the water in the earthenware jar by the door went drip, drip, drip, making a little pool of sound in the vast silence.

She was the only one who knew. That was the strange thing; the thing that made her feel, as she stood there in her grey cotton dress, a different being from the Eleanor who had walked in Kensington Gardens with the children only two months ago, carrying their mackintoshes and smelling the damp London spring. Here she was necessary. Here she was the voice. When Mrs. Villiers had wanted the shutters closed at noon, it had been Eleanor who had leaned over the balcony and spoken to the old woman with the black shawl, and the old woman had looked up, her face a web of brown lines, and had nodded, and had shuffled to draw the iron bolts. And the children, Cecily and Tom, pulling at her skirt, had said, "Say it again, Eleanor, say the funny words," and she had felt a little leap in her blood, a ridiculous pride, as if she had performed a feat of magic.

The magic was in the sounds. The words fell from her tongue differently here; they had a different weight. When she said *mañana*, it was not merely tomorrow; it was a whole philosophy, a shrugging of shoulders, a lifting of the chin, a slow turning away from the urgency of things. And the villagers had begun to trust her. The man who brought the goats in the morning, the one with the cracked leather sandals and the face like a sun-baked wall, he touched his cap to her now, and called her *señorita*, and sometimes gave the children a little twisted stick of liquorice root, refusing money with a wave of his brown hand and a torrent of words she only half understood, but whose warmth she felt as one feels the sun through a shawl.

She thought of that other life—the life of the nursery, of the damp front steps in Ealing, of the clergyman's voice on Sundays droning through the Litany as though he were reading a railway timetable. How flat it had been; how without savour. She had been a shadow there, a person who carried things, who wiped noses, who said, "Don't touch, dear," and "Come along now." Here, under this tremendous sky, she walked differently; she held her head up, because the houses were white and demanded a certain uprightness, and the geraniums blazed in their pots like little fires, and the women at the fountain, their dark hair drawn back from their faces, their earthenware pitchers balanced on their hips, looked at her with recognition now, and called out a greeting that she could answer, and did answer, her voice surprising her with its ease, as if some other woman spoke through her mouth.

And Mr. Villiers—she thought of him, standing in the hall yesterday, his red face perspiring, his collar soft and limp, holding a telegram and looking helplessly at the housekeeper, who knew no English, and the housekeeper looking helplessly back, and between them that gulf, that terrible gulf of incomprehension, wider than the sea they had crossed, wider than all the miles of plain and mountain. And Eleanor had stepped forward, and the words had come, and the housekeeper's face had cleared, and Mr. Villiers had said, "Good girl, Eleanor," and turned away, and she had been left with the afterglow of that small usefulness, that momentary bridging of the chasm, glowing in her like the ember of a cigarette.

But it was more than usefulness. It was as if a door had opened in a wall she had always thought blank. She belonged. Not to the family—they were kind enough, in their vague, preoccupied way, but they would have been equally kind to any well-recommend governess; she was interchangeable there, a unit of service—but to the place itself. To the stones of the villa, which were old and warm and seemed to remember centuries of living. To the narrow street that wound up from the village, with its donkeys and its blue shadows and its smell of cooking oil and dust. To the old priest who sat in the doorway of the church, his hands folded on his stomach, his eyes half closed, but who opened them when she passed and smiled, as if he had been waiting for her, as if her passing were a small event in his day, a tick of the clock that he noted and was glad of.

She thought of the evening before, when she had sat on the verandah after the children were in bed, and the darkness had come on, not suddenly, but by degrees, as if someone were slowly turning down a lamp, and the frogs had begun their rasping chorus in the irrigation ditch, and the stars had appeared, enormous and trembling, so close she felt she might brush them with her hair if she stood on tiptoe. And the sound of a guitar had come from somewhere down in the village, a thread of sound, thin and sweet, winding up through the olives, and a voice singing, a man's voice, singing something she could not catch but that seemed to contain all the sadness and all the beauty of the world, all the longing that she had ever felt, in her grey, unnoticed life, folded up and put away like winter clothes.
