The fog had not lifted from the river all that week, and it hung about the narrow streets of Saint Saviour’s parish like a soiled fleece, so that the gas-lamps burned yellow at noon and the gutters ran with a greasy film that would not be washed away. It was upon the Tuesday, in the third week of November, that a young woman presented herself at our lodgings in Baker Street, having telegraphed beforehand in terms so urgent that Holmes had abandoned his monograph upon the ashes of tobacco to receive her.

She was perhaps three-and-twenty, plainly but neatly dressed in black, with the pale, clear complexion of one who has lived much indoors and the manner of a person accustomed to command herself. Yet her hands, as she laid them upon the arms of the chair, trembled visibly, and it was some moments before she could bring her errand to speech.

“You must forgive me, Mr. Holmes,” said she at last. “I have come upon a matter which I can scarcely put into words without seeming to betray my own father. My name is Edith Marchmont. My father is the Reverend Alured Marchmont, vicar of Saint Anne’s, in the parish of Saint Saviour’s, south of the river.”

Holmes inclined his head. “I know the district by reputation. It is not a wealthy living.”

“It is a very poor one,” she answered, “and my father has served it these two-and-twenty years. He is not a man of learning, sir, nor of any great gifts in the pulpit, but he has been faithful in his fashion, and he has visited every sick-bed and every hovel in his cure, and I believe there is not a soul in those courts who would not speak him kindly. Until a fortnight ago I would have said that he was the happiest man in Southwark.”

“And what occurred a fortnight ago?”

“He went out upon the afternoon of Tuesday the fifth, to visit a woman named Garrity, in Rope-walk—a widow, bedridden these three years, whom he has never failed to see each week. He was to have been home by six. He came in at half-past eight, and I heard his step upon the flags and knew at once that something was amiss. He is a heavy man, Mr. Holmes, and he walks heavily, but that night he came in like a man who does not know where he is going. His face was the colour of clay, and there was a smear of wet upon his sleeve, as though he had leaned against a wall. I asked him if Mrs. Garrity were worse. He looked at me as though I had spoken in a foreign tongue, and then he said, ‘I have been to Rope-walk, and I have come away from it.’ With that he went to his study and shut the door, and he has not been the same man since.”

“He has not preached?”

“He has not entered the church. On the Sunday following he sent word to the curate that he was indisposed, and he has not crossed the threshold since. He sits in his study from morning till night, and at night—Mr. Holmes, it is this that has brought me to you—at night he sits at his table and writes. He writes for hours, with the lamp before him, upon a single sheet of paper, and when the sky begins to grey he takes it to the grate and burns it, and watches it until there is nothing but ash. I have crept down twice and listened at the door, and I have heard him weeping.”

Holmes had listened with an attention that never faltered. “You have not asked him what he writes?”

“I have asked him nothing. He was ever a gentle man, but there is something in his eye now that forbids question. And yet, sir, I have one clue, and it is for the sake of it that I have come. On the morning after that first night I found, in the scuttle among the ashes, a fragment of his letter which the fire had not wholly consumed. It was no more than a corner, but there were three words upon it in his hand, and they were these: *the fourth of them.*”

“The fourth of them,” Holmes repeated softly. “And you preserved the fragment?”

“I have it here.” She drew from her reticule a scrap of charred paper, which Holmes took to the window and examined long and earnestly through his lens.

“The paper is of the commonest kind, such as is sold in penny packets at any stationer’s,” said he. “The ink is the vicar’s own, and the hand is agitated—you will observe that the up-strokes are broken and the down-strokes driven deep, as by a man who writes with a trembling pressure. There is nothing more to be gleaned from it. Tell me, Miss Marchmont, did your father speak at all of his walk, or of any person he had met upon the way?”

“Only one thing, and that I had from our servant, who heard him muttering to himself as he came up the steps. He said, over and over, ‘Four of them, and I am the fourth.’ Then he said a name, or what sounded like a name, and Martha could not be certain of it. It was something like *Carker*, or *Corbet*—she could not tell.”

“Carker,” said Holmes, rising and taking down his index-book from the shelf. “There was a Carker in the Borough some years ago—a money-lender, I fancy, and not a reputable one. But we shall do better than guesswork. Watson, have you your coat? Miss Marchmont, I must ask you to return home and to say nothing of this visit. If your father should ask whether you have been abroad, tell him you have been to see a friend in the City. And should he go out again to Rope-walk, send word to me at once by the nearest telegraph-office. I shall be there before him.”
