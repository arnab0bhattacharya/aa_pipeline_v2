It is a truth not universally acknowledged, that a young lady who has read a great deal of philosophy may still be as vain as her neighbour who has read none at all, and that the difference between them lies chiefly in the vocabulary of their self-deception. Miss Eleanor Whitfield, of Ashcombe Parva, was a case very much in point. She had returned from a winter in Bath persuaded of her own superior discernment; she spoke of flattery with contempt, of fashion with a gentle pity, and of the young men of the neighbourhood with an indulgence so patronising that her mother had been heard to observe, that it was a mercy the poor creatures did not know how little she thought of them. Mrs. Whitfield, a widow of moderate fortune and immoderate hopefulness, considered her daughter's fastidiousness a symptom of excellent understanding, and was therefore the more disposed to triumph when Mr. Charles Delafield came into Hampshire.

Mr. Delafield was the nephew of Sir John Delafield of the Priory, a young gentleman of elegant person, fashionable dress, and that easy assurance which is the natural produce of London and a good tailor. He came to the country, he said, for the shooting; but as the season advanced and the birds grew scarcer, he was observed to be remarkably constant in his attendance at the evening parties of the neighbourhood, and particularly at those where Miss Whitfield was expected. He had a way of singling her out which was extremely flattering, though so managed as to seem almost accidental; he would seat himself near her with a sigh of relief from the fatigues of general conversation, and address to her those remarks upon music and painting which she was too well read to find profound, but which were delivered with a look that made them appear original. When she hazarded an opinion, he listened as though it were an oracle; when she was silent, he watched her as though her silence were the most eloquent thing in the room. Eleanor, who had often declared that she could never be pleased with a man who did not respect her understanding, found herself entirely at her ease with Mr. Delafield, because he respected it so very much.

That he was idle, she called a graceful leisure; that he was expensive, she called a liberal spirit; that he had no settled plan of life, she called the mark of a mind above the vulgar. Her friend Miss Harriett Cole, who had known her from childhood and loved her too well to flatter her, ventured one afternoon to suggest that Mr. Delafield was perhaps a little too much the gentleman of fashion to be depended upon. "I am sure," said Eleanor, with a smile of superior tolerance, "I am the last person to be dazzled by mere fashion. If I admire him, it is because he has sense enough to value a woman for her mind, and does not, like the squires of this county, talk of nothing but horses and the price of wheat. He told me only yesterday that he had never met a woman who thought so much and said so little."

"Did he indeed!" cried Miss Cole. "And did he tell you how many women he had said it to before?"

Eleanor was not angry, because she considered anger a weakness, but she was cold; and she resolved to think no more of Miss Cole's impertinence. It is remarkable how easily a resolution of that kind may be kept, when the person who has given the offence lives three miles off, and the person who has given the pleasure calls every second evening.

The acquaintance ripened with a rapidity very natural in a village where there is nothing else to do. Mr. Delafield began to speak of a cottage near the Priory which he had it in his mind to take; he consulted her upon the planting of a shrubbery; he hinted at a wish that his aunt, Lady Delafield, should know her. Eleanor, who had always said that she would never marry a man whom she could not esteem, began to esteem Mr. Delafield very highly indeed, and to find his conversation daily more improving. She took to reading the books he admired, and discovered, to her own surprise, that she had always thought as he did upon the subject of Rousseau.

It was at the very height of this agreeable intercourse that there arrived at the Priory a Mr. Edward Ferrars—not of Norland, but a cousin of the Delafields, a plain-spoken young man of small fortune and no pretensions, who had lately come from a regiment in the north, and who had the misfortune to be entirely without that polish which Eleanor had learned to consider the first of virtues. He was no respecter of persons; he said what he thought, and he thought that his cousin Charles was the idlest fellow in England. Eleanor disliked him at once, and took some pains to show him, by a cold civility, that she was not to be imposed upon by a man who had neither elegance of manner nor a proper deference for her opinions.

It happened one evening, at a card-party at the Priory, that Eleanor, having gone into the library to fetch a fan, heard voices in the adjoining apartment, and recognised them for those of Mr. Delafield and Mr. Ferrars. She was not a listener by principle; but the door was ajar, her own name was spoken, and she stood still—not, as she afterwards told herself, from curiosity, but because to have moved would have been to betray her situation.

"Come, Charles," Mr. Ferrars was saying, "you have played the lover long enough. When is the farce to end?"
