The fog had curled up from the river in the early evening and now hung about the narrow streets of Shadwell like a soiled fleece, so that the lamps of the Alhambra Music Hall burned with a dull, jaundiced glare. Within, however, all was warmth and gaslight and the odour of orange-peel and cheap tobacco. I had come at the urgent request of Inspector Lestrade, who met me in the vestibule with a face of profound dissatisfaction.

“It is a pretty business, Doctor,” said he, as he led me down the aisle. “Her Grace the Duchess of Carmarthen had her diamond bracelet spirited from her very wrist upon the stage, and though the fellow made a great show of returning it, it is now found to be paste, and the real article is gone. The conjuror, a man named Bellamy, swears he is innocent, and the manager is in a fine taking, for her Grace is a patroness and will have the place shut up.”

The house was half empty now, the audience having been detained by the police. Upon the stage stood a small table draped in black velvet, and beside it a tall, lean man in evening dress, his face pale and his hands, I observed, trembling as he clasped them before him. This was Bellamy, the prestidigitator. A constable stood near him, and in the wings a woman in a sequined bodice was weeping quietly into a handkerchief.

“You have examined the bracelet?” I asked.

“Paste,” said Lestrade, “and a poor imitation at that. The Duchess is in a rare passion. She says the weight was wrong the instant he handed it back, but she held her tongue until the interval.”

I approached the table. Upon it lay the false bracelet, a trumpery thing of glass and tinsel, and beside it a small morocco case, open and empty. Bellamy turned to me with the air of a man who has told his tale too often.

“I have performed the Vanishing Loan a hundred times, sir,” said he. “I borrow the article, I wrap it in a handkerchief, I command it to depart, and lo! it is gone from the handkerchief and found in the box. But the box was locked, sir, and the key in her Grace’s own hand. I never touched the bracelet but to wrap it. When I unwrapped it, it was the one she gave me.”

“You are certain?”

“As certain as I stand here. A conjuror knows his props, sir, as a jockey knows his horse.”

I looked about the stage. There was a trap-door in the boards, a property table with a false top, and a cabinet of oriental design at the back. “You use an assistant?” I asked.

“Miss Lila, sir. She hands me the handkerchief and the box.”

The woman in the wings was summoned. She was a slight, dark creature, with eyes that were red from weeping but which met mine with a certain steadiness.

“You handled the bracelet at any time?” I asked.

“Only the handkerchief, sir. I never touched the bracelet itself.”

“And the box?”

“I brought it on and gave it to her Grace to lock. The key never left her hand.”

Lestrade snorted. “So says everyone. Yet the diamond is gone and the paste is here. It is plain enough. Bellamy palmed it and substituted the false one.”

I asked to see the handkerchief. It was a large square of white silk, and upon close inspection I perceived a faint greasy stain near the centre. “You use this for other tricks?” I asked.

“For the egg and the orange, sir, and the billiard ball.”

“And the bracelet was wrapped in it thus?” I took the silk and folded it as he demonstrated, the bracelet within.

“Then you commanded it to vanish, and when you opened the handkerchief it was gone?”

“Gone, sir, and the box produced and unlocked, and there was the bracelet within.”

“But it was not the same bracelet.”

“It was not.”

I stood for a moment in thought. “You have a dressing-room?”

“Below, sir.”

“Let us go there.”

The dressing-room was a mean apartment, lit by a single jet of gas, with a mirror, a shelf of pots and papers, and a large wicker basket for soiled linen. I examined the basket. At the bottom, beneath a heap of collars, I found a small twist of tissue-paper, and within it a pinch of white powder. I touched it to my tongue. It was bicarbonate of soda, such as a man might use for his digestion, or a conjuror for the production of a puff of smoke.

“You use this in your act?”

“A little, sir, for the fire trick.”

I turned to Miss Lila, who had followed us. “You have been with Mr. Bellamy how long?”

“Two years, sir.”

“And before that?”

She hesitated. “I was in service, sir. In the household of her Grace.”

Lestrade’s eyes narrowed. “In service? In what capacity?”

“As lady’s-maid, sir.”

There was a silence. I saw Bellamy’s face change, and the girl’s chin lift a little.

“You were dismissed?” I asked.

“I left, sir. Her Grace was—” she stopped. “I left.”

“You knew the bracelet well, then. You knew its weight, its clasp, the very feel of it.”

“I dressed her Grace’s hands many a time, sir.”

I turned to Lestrade. “The substitution was not made upon the stage,” I said. “It was made in the handkerchief. The bracelet was wrapped, and in the act of wrapping, the false one was already within the folds, sewn into a little pocket in the silk, which I perceive has been unpicked and re-sewn.” I held the handkerchief to the light. “You see the stitches? Fresh, and not matching the original. When Bellamy wrapped the bracelet, he wrapped the false one, which lay concealed. The real one remained in the hand of the person who had placed it there.”

“But her Grace held the key,” said Lestrade.
