If any weary traveller, plodding through the mire of Great Whisker Lane on a November evening, had sought a cheerful refuge from the fog and the soot, he would have found no prospect more disheartening than the cookshop of Mrs. Salome Topples. The very fowls in the window, suspended by their meagre legs from a great brass wire, appeared to be engaged in a melancholy dance, twirling slowly in the damp draught, as though executing a final, desperate waltz before the oven. The joints of beef, rubbed into a state of high polish, lay in their tin trays with the resigned air of deceased dignitaries; while a pyramid of pork-pies, stacked beneath a glass dome, seemed to regard the passer-by with a stony, unblinking crust, daring him to part with his pence.

Behind the counter, and surrounded by this mortuary of victuals, presided Mrs. Topples herself: a comfortable, commodious widow of fifty, whose black silk gown creaked with the substantial friction of concealed bank-notes, and whose countenance beamed with the cheerful anticipation of a second matrimonial venture. For Mrs. Topples had made up her mind to marry Mr. Petulengro Pinch, the parish constable; and Mr. Petulengro Pinch, walking with a heavy, official tread upon the opposite pavement, had made up his mind to marry Mrs. Topples.

Mr. Pinch was a man of a most commanding and swelling presence. He wore a greatcoat of so blue a hue that it seemed to rival the very heavens in its depth, and he carried a staff of office so knobbed, so heavy, and so terrifyingly official, that the mere sight of it was enough to make every vagrant boy in the parish vanish into the nearest alleyway. He had a broad, purplish face, a double chin that nodded over his cravat with an air of perpetual reproof, and a voice which, when he chose to exert it, resembled the grating of a rusty key in a ponderous lock. He was, in his own estimation, the Law; and he looked upon the inhabitants of the parish as so many offenders who had not yet been found out.

It was upon the morning of his nuptial day that Mr. Pinch sat in the little back-parlour behind the cookshop, consuming a plate of cold collared beef, and casting his eye—in a manner at once affectionate and architectural—upon the shop itself. He knew, with a certainty that warmed the cockles of his heart, that Mrs. Topples possessed four hundred pounds in the savings bank. He had seen her deposit-book. He had kissed it. He intended to convert those four hundred pounds into a detached brick villa in the neighbourhood of Clapham, and to install therein a red-faced cook, a boy in buttons, and a mahogany sideboard of the most expensive description.

‘A noble woman!’ murmured Mr. Pinch, applying a fork to a gherkin. ‘A woman of substance. A woman whose worldly gear is as solid as her joints of meat!’

At that very moment, Mrs. Topples was engaged in the back-kitchen, drawing a glass of hot elder wine, and her reflections were of a nature equally calculating, though of a different complexion. She had no desire for a villa in Clapham. What she desired was Respectability. She was weary of weighing out suet and chopping pigs’-feet; she was weary of being called “Ma’am” by errand-boys; she panted for the social elevation which the title of “Mrs. Pinch, the constable’s lady” would confer upon her. She pictured herself walking to church on a Sunday, with a reticule, and a hymn-book, and the consciousness that her husband could, if he chose, take up any man in the street. She looked upon his staff as a sceptre, and upon his greatcoat as a robe of state.

‘A grand man!’ sighed Mrs. Topples, stirring the wine with a cinnamon stick. ‘A man of weight and warrant. A man as can walk into the beadle’s room without knocking!’

The hour of eleven arrived, and with it, the Reverend Mr. Grume, a pale curate, shivering in the vestry. Mr. Pinch, having donned a pair of spotless white gloves, which gave his hands the appearance of two large stuffed puddings, stood before the altar, swelling with importance. Mrs. Topples entered, attired in a brown silk, which rustled like a thousand dry leaves, and accompanied by her shopman, a lean youth named Thomas, who carried the ring with an air of intense terror.

The ceremony commenced. Mr. Grume droned through the service with the mechanical rapidity of a barrel-organ. Presently he arrived at that passage which requires the parties to declare if they know any impediment why they should not be joined together.

Here, an extraordinary thing occurred. Mr. Pinch, feeling in the pocket of his greatcoat for his handkerchief, drew out, instead, a crumpled piece of paper. It was a memorandum, written in his own hand, of the very bank in which Mrs. Topples kept her account, and the very sum she possessed, with a marginal note of the interest, and a rough sketch of the Clapham villa. He had, in a moment of forgetful rapture, left it in the pocket when he gave the coat to Thomas to brush.

Thomas, in his terror, had found it, and—being unable to read—had shown it to Mrs. Topples, who had instantly deciphered it, and who had spent the night in a state of furious wakefulness.

‘Stop!’ cried Mrs. Topples, in a voice that made the very gas-jets quiver. ‘Stop! I will not proceed! I see how it is. It’s my money he’s after, and not my person!’

Mr. Pinch, thus abruptly accused, and seeing his Clapham villa crumble to dust before his eyes, turned first purple, then pale, and then burst forth.

‘And if it is!’ he roared, shaking his staff, which he had imprudently brought into the church. ‘What then? What is your person, Mrs. Topples, without your money? A cookshop! A mere repository of cold beef! Do you suppose I would marry a woman who smells of dough, if it were not for her deposits? I am the Law! I cannot live upon pork-pies!’
