The invitation had been conveyed upon a sheet of paper so heavily scented that it seemed to Reginald Vane less a summons than a symptom, and the hand that penned it had pressed so languidly upon the pen that the letters reclined upon the page like invalids upon a chaise-longue. Lady Featherstonehaugh—for such was the name of his correspondent—had written of his little volume of verses as though it were a moral triumph, which is the highest praise a poet can receive from a woman who has never read him. She begged, she said, that he would honour their house at Cottesmore Wold with his presence for a weekend in October; the partridges, she added, in a postscript of devastating candour, were plentiful, and her husband would be delighted.

The house, when Vane arrived, proved to be a vast grey edifice of the late Georgian persuasion, situated in a hollow of the Leicestershire wolds, and surrounded by lawns so immaculate that they seemed to have been swept rather than mown. Lady Featherstonehaugh received him in a drawing-room of pale yellow silk, and was herself a composition in amber and ivory, being thin, tall, and dressed in a gown that suggested a tea-rose on the point of expiring from its own refinement. She spoke in a low, exhausted voice, as though conversation were an exertion she could ill afford, and she looked at Vane with the rapt, proprietary gaze of a woman who has discovered a genius and intends to see that he is properly fed.

“You must not mind my husband,” she murmured, as she poured out tea from a pot of old Worcester. “He is a sportsman. It is a disease, like any other, and one must be charitable. He has shot three thousand pheasants this season, and he speaks of them as one speaks of a debt of honour. He will not understand your verses, but he will respect you for having written them, which is more than most of my friends do for anything I admire.”

Colonel Featherstonehaugh proved to be a man of a red, honest, and entirely unilluminated countenance, who greeted Vane with the bluff cordiality of one who has been assured by his wife that the guest is a good sort, though admittedly a poet. He was dressed in a shooting-suit of such uncompromising tweed that it seemed to have been cut from the very hedgerows, and he carried about him an atmosphere of gunpowder and good cigars.

“So you write poetry, eh?” said the Colonel, at dinner, fixing Vane with an eye that had looked down many a barrel. “My wife tells me it’s very fine. I don’t pretend to understand it myself. I like a song with a chorus, and a bit of a tune to it. But I dare say there’s money in it, what?”

“There is very little money in it,” said Vane, with a smile of exquisite resignation, “and even less tune. But then, Colonel, the pursuit of beauty is notoriously unremunerative. One cannot put a sonnet on the market as one puts a bullock.”

The Colonel stared at him for a moment, and then burst into a roar of laughter. “By Jove, that’s good! A bullock! I must remember that. Beauty, eh? Well, I dare say you know your own business best. But you must come out with the guns tomorrow. Nothing like a day’s shooting to put a man in spirits.”

The morrow dawned with that particular clarity which the English autumn reserves for those who intend to slaughter small birds, and Vane, who had passed a sleepless night in a chamber hung with the trophies of the Colonel’s Indian career, descended to breakfast in a costume of his own devising, which he fondly imagined to be appropriate to the occasion. It consisted of a velvet shooting-jacket of a delicate sage-green, knee-breeches of a most unseasonable cut, and a pair of silk stockings that had been designed for a drawing-room rather than a turnip-field. The Colonel, encountering him in the hall, surveyed him from head to foot with an expression of profound and speechless wonder.

“My dear fellow,” he said at last, “you can’t go out like that. You’ll catch your death. And the beaters will think you’re a—well, they’ll think something, I don’t know what.”

“I have always held,” said Vane, adjusting a buttonhole of pale orchids, “that the true sportsman is known by the elegance of his attire rather than the accuracy of his aim. It is the spirit of the thing that matters, Colonel—the poetry of the chase, the—if I may say so—the aesthetic of the slaughter.”

The Colonel, who was not accustomed to having his sport described in such terms, opened his mouth to reply, found no words adequate to the occasion, and contented himself with thrusting a gun into Vane’s hands and leading him out to the butts. The day that followed was one of unrelieved and exquisite torture. Vane, who had never fired a gun in his life, discharged it at irregular intervals in directions that owed nothing to the position of the birds and everything to a desire to appear occupied. The recoil, on the first occasion, flung him backwards into a hedge of considerable thorniness, from which he emerged with a torn jacket and a philosophic smile. The beaters, a rustic and unlettered race, regarded him with a mixture of awe and derision, and the Colonel’s loader, a man of few words, confined himself to muttering “Lord help us” at intervals throughout the afternoon.

It was at the dinner that evening that the crisis arrived. The Colonel, flushed with the day’s bag and a considerable quantity of claret, fixed Vane with a gaze that had in it something of the sportsman’s appraisal of a doubtful shot.

“I’ve been thinking,” he announced, “about that poetry of yours. My wife read me a bit of it after dinner last night. I didn’t understand a word. Now, I’m a plain man, and I say what I think. What’s the good of it? What does it do?”
