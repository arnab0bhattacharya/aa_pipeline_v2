The dining-room of the pension, with its smell of beeswax and boiled coffee, its twelve chairs of crimson plush, its wallpaper whereon enormous ferns ascended in pale green spirals towards a ceiling that had known better days, received them each morning at the half-hour after eight; and Mrs. Fennimore, who had been a governess in the house of a bishop and could never quite relinquish the habit of authority, was invariably first, her napkin already unfolded, her spectacle-case set square beside the salt. She had a way of surveying the table as though it were a class of inattentive girls, and of saying, when Mr. Pargeter came in with his slow, careful step—for his illness had left him with the air of a man walking on ice—"You are late, Mr. Pargeter," which was not true, but established, somehow, the order of things.

He would smile, and take his seat, and unfold his own napkin with fingers that were still a little uncertain of themselves, and the light from the long window—a light that in this country seemed to have been strained through gauze, so soft it was, so without edge—fell across the white cloth and made of the silver a series of small, cold flames. And Mrs. Fennimore, watching him over the rim of her cup, would think: he is better; he is certainly better; the colour has come back into the hollows of his cheeks; and then, with a little pang she did not care to examine, that when he was quite well he would go, and there would be no one to say good-morning to in that particular way, with that particular lifting of the eyebrows which suggested that the two of them, after all these weeks, had come to an understanding about the world.

The widow—Mrs. Cholmondeley—sat at the head of the table, not because she claimed it, but because she had a way of arranging herself wherever she was that made other arrangements seem provisional. Her daughter, Edith, sat beside her, and it was Edith who poured the coffee, Edith who rang the little bell for the maid, Edith who said, "I think there is a little more butter, mamma," in a voice so carefully modulated that it had ceased, long ago, to have any colour of its own. She was thirty-four, and she had a face like a cameo, and she wore, always, a small brooch of seed-pearls at her throat; and no one at the pension had ever heard her say a single thing that was not useful, or kind, or both, and yet Mr. Pargeter, who had once sat beside her for an hour while the rain fell, had come away with the curious impression of having been in the presence of a very great, a very silent, unhappiness—like a room in which someone has died and the furniture has been left exactly as it was.

And then there was Mr. Ludlow, the painter, who came in last of all, with his paint-stained fingers and his air of a man who has been up all night wrestling with an angel, though in truth he had only been up in his room, staring at a canvas that would not come right, and drinking a little too much of the thin red wine of the country. He was failing—he knew it, and the others knew it, and it hung about him like the smell of turpentine—but he talked, when he talked, with a kind of desperate gaiety, as though the words themselves were a reprieve. "The light," he would say, "the light here—it is not like any other light. It is the light of the early Italians. One could paint the Annunciation in that light, if one had the genius. But one has not the genius, and so one paints—" and he would gesture with his fork, and the gesture would end in a little helpless flop, and Edith would look down at her plate, and Mrs. Cholmondeley would say, "You are too modest, Mr. Ludlow," in her comfortable voice.

The days passed. They passed in walks along the Corniche, where the sea lay below like a great bolt of blue silk, and the mountains behind were the colour of smoke, and the little villas among the olive-trees had roofs of faded rose. They passed in the garden of the pension, where a palm-tree rattled its dry fronds, and a cat slept on the warm stone, and the shadows of the leaves moved across the gravel with the slow deliberation of the hours themselves. Mrs. Fennimore took her constitutional at eleven, and Mr. Pargeter, if he was well enough, took his at the same hour, and they would meet, as if by accident, at the bend where the path turned towards the sea, and walk together, and speak of—what? Of the bishop, and his lordship's gout; of the novels of Mr. Meredith, which Mrs. Fennimore found difficult and Mr. Pargeter found delightful; of the curious fact that the oranges on the tree by the gate were green, and yet the oranges in the market were yellow, and how did they do it? And Mrs. Fennimore, who had spent her life in the service of other people's children, and had never had a child of her own, found herself telling him—this young man with the thin face and the patient eyes—about a girl she had taught in the bishop's household, a girl called Lucy, who had had a laugh like a bell, and who had died of a fever at seventeen; and she had not spoken of Lucy for twenty years, and she did not know why she spoke of her now, except that the sea was very blue and the air was very soft and he was walking beside her with his stick, and did not seem to find it strange.
