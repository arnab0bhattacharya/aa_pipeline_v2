He had left them with me, those boxes—a dozen or more of heavy deal, hooped with iron, corded in a manner that suggested the haste of a man pursued by more than the mere departure of a packet boat. Fenney was his name, the English Consul; a man who had spent twenty years in the Republic, and who in the end had the look of one who had been slowly drowned in the thick air of the place, his eyes staring out from a face the color of damp clay, gasping, as it were, for the breath of another world. He was going home. He said it with a strange, breathless emphasis, as though the word "home" were a magical formula to ward off the evil of the harbor, the mud, the vultures, the squalid heat of Higueras. And he left the archives of the consulate in my warehouse, amongst the rope and chain-cable, the tarred canvas and the brass fittings, because, he said, there was no room aboard the _Remigio_ for the papers, and he would send for them. He would send for them. He said it twice, looking at me with that gasping, clay-colored face, and I nodded. What else could one do? He was a fellow countryman, of a sort, and there was the bond of the sea and the port between us, that fellowship of men who dwell in the shadow of ships, in the sordid margin where the ocean meets the land and all its disorders.

I remember the evening well. The harbor was like a copper plate, suffused with a glare that seemed to press upon the water, flattening it, making of it a mirror for a sky that was too vast and too empty to be anything but menacing. The _Remigio_ had slipped her moorings, a dark speck crawling out upon that glittering surface, and I stood upon my doorstep, watching her diminish, with a sense of having been entrusted with something more than boxes of paper. There was in those archives, I knew, the history of the consulate's dealings with the port—of wrecks and seamen left destitute, of debts and disputes, of the slow, grinding friction between the laws of a distant island and the anarchy of this coast. It was the sediment of twenty years, and it had been left to me, a ship-chandler, as though I were a rock in the stream of events, immovable, reliable, safe. And I had felt a certain pride in that, a foolish pride, such as a man feels when he is given a trust that flatters his sense of his own consequence.

It was three days later that Colonel Villegas came.

The revolution had been simmering in the interior for months, a vague disturbance of which we in the port saw little except an occasional troop of ragged cavalry riding in to forage, their officers wearing gold lace and their men going barefoot. But now the fires had crept closer, and there was talk that the Government troops had been broken on the plain of San Mateo, and that the revolutionary chief, a man named Guzman, was marching upon Higueras. The town was in a ferment, such as it was—a wretched place of adobe walls and tiled roofs, with a plaza where the sun beat down upon the bare earth and the vultures sat in rows upon the church tower, waiting with a patience that was more obscene than any human cruelty. The harbor was empty of shipping. The foreign merchants had sent their families aboard the coasting steamers, and the native population had retreated into a brooding silence, that silence of the tropics which is not peace but the hush of things gathering toward an explosion.

Villegas arrived at dusk, with a dozen men at his back, their horses steaming in the cool of the evening. He was a tall man, very lean, with a face that was like a blade—narrow, gleaming, with eyes set too close together beneath a forehead scarred by a glancing bullet. He wore a tunic of brown linen, stained with sweat and dust, and his boots were cracked, and his spurs jingled as he walked. He came into the warehouse as though he owned it, his men remaining outside in the fading light, squatting upon the quay with their rifles across their knees, chewing tobacco and spitting into the water with an air of men who had all the time in the world and the world itself for their taking.

He asked for the archives. He did not demand them; he asked, in a voice that was soft, almost caressing, with an accent that was not of the coast but of the interior, where the Spanish is spoken with a slurring, musical intonation that makes of every sentence a kind of song. He knew they were here. Fenney had told him, it appeared—or rather, Fenney had mentioned it in a letter to someone, and the letter had been intercepted. The details were obscure. What was clear was that Villegas wanted the papers, wanted them with an intensity that was out of all proportion to their apparent value, and that he had come to get them.

"You are an Englishman, Señor Decrow," he said, standing in the middle of the warehouse, among the coils of rope and the casks of tar, his shadow thrown long and thin by the lamp I had lit. "You know what it is to have a trust. I also have a trust. The papers contain—how shall I say?—certain correspondence. Between the Consul and the Government in Santa Cruz. Reports upon our affairs. It is not meet that such things should be in the hands of—a neutral party."

He paused, looking at me with those close-set eyes, and I understood that the pause was for me to fill, and that what I said in it would determine the course of the next few minutes of my life, and perhaps of all the minutes that remained to me. I could feel the heat of the warehouse, the smell of tar and hemp and iron, pressing upon me like a weight. My mouth was dry.
