The steamer, the *Stjerne*, chugged its slow way up the Fjord, and the water, which had been a sheet of hammered silver in the morning, was now a deep, still green, as if all the light of the sky had been poured into it and held there. The mountains rose on either hand, their lower slopes dark with pine, their upper reaches bare rock, scored and gashed by the passage of ice. The air, cool and smelling of wet stone and something sharp and clean, carried the faint, rhythmic thud of the engine and, from the foredeck, the sound of voices.

Clare, who had been watching a single white bird trace its way across the immense face of a cliff, turned her head. The quarrel, if that was what it was, had a texture of its own; it was not loud, but it had a persistent, grating quality, like a stone being ground in the cogs of a machine. It was young Arthur Penhaligon, his fair hair ruffled by the wind, his face earnest and flushed, and young Cecily Vance, who was all sharp angles and dark, impatient eyes.

“But you cannot separate the rhythm from the sense!” Cecily was saying, her voice cutting through the calm. “It is a single organism. To feel the meter and not the meaning is to hear the notes of a tune and call it music.”

“Nonsense,” Arthur replied, leaning against the rail. “There is a pure music in words, a pattern, a design. One can feel the surge and fall of the hexameter without a word of Greek. It is a physical sensation, like the movement of this boat.” He gestured vaguely at the water. “Tennyson felt it. It is a kind of architecture for the ear.”

“Architecture for the ear is a dead thing,” Cecily retorted, turning away to look at the fjord, her profile sharp against the dark water. “Poetry is blood and breath. It is life.”

And so it went, the words of the young flickering against the vast, silent permanence of the stone. Clare felt the familiar weariness of such arguments, the sense of two bright, hard objects striking each other and making only noise. She looked across the deck to where Mr. Finch sat alone. He was a man of perhaps fifty, his face lined by weather and, she suspected, by other things, his suit of a heavy, dark wool that seemed too formal for this wild place. He was a widower; that fact had been imparted by Mrs. Anstruther with the relish of one distributing sweetmeats. He was watching the young people, not with annoyance, but with a kind of remote, melancholy curiosity, as one might watch the play of light on a disturbed pool.

As if feeling her gaze, he turned his head and their eyes met. There was no smile, only a brief, acknowledging flicker. Then he looked down at his hands, which were resting on his knees. Clare found her own thoughts drifting. She had never married. It was not a decision, not a thing she had chosen so much as a state that had simply solidified around her, year by year, like the rings of a tree. Now, at forty, it was her form, her substance. She was a spinster. The word was as plain and unadorned as a piece of driftwood. Sometimes, in moments like this, surrounded by the chatter of the young and the solid, settled presence of married folk like the Anstruthers, she felt a pang of… not loneliness, exactly, but of a profound and unbridgeable distance. She was an observer. A spectator at the theatre of life.

The engine’s note changed, deepening. The *Stjerne* was slowing, preparing to make its final approach to the glacier’s snout, which was now visible at the head of the fjord, a great, fractured wall of ancient ice, glowing with an unearthly blue. The young people had fallen silent, awed into submission by the sight. Mr. Finch rose from his seat and, with a slight hesitation, came and stood at the rail a little way from Clare.

“It is… formidable,” he said, his voice quiet, almost lost in the sound of the engine.

“It is,” Clare agreed. “It makes one feel very small. And very brief.”

“Yes,” he said. “That is it. Brief.” He was silent for a moment, his gaze fixed on the glacier. “My wife, Eleanor, she disliked things that made her feel brief. She preferred… rooms. Gardens. Things with a human scale. She would have found all this rather grim.” He said it without sadness, as a simple statement of fact.

Clare felt a strange sense of intimacy, of being admitted to a small, quiet chamber of his memory. “And you?” she asked, the words coming out before she had fully considered them. “Do you find it grim?”

He turned to her then, his eyes, a pale, washed-out grey, seeming to see her properly for the first time. “No,” he said slowly. “I find it… honest. There is no pretence here. The ice is ice. The rock is rock. It does not pretend to last forever. It simply is, and then it is not.” He paused, and a faint smile touched his lips. “Forgive me. I am being morbid.”

“Not at all,” Clare said. “I think I understand.”

And she did. It was the quality of things that she had always been drawn to, the unvarnished truth of a dead leaf, the stark honesty of a winter branch. She had lived her life among the human scale of things, yet her soul, she sometimes felt, belonged out here, in the vast, indifferent spaces where time was measured in ice and stone. She had never said this to anyone. It would have sounded absurd. But here, with this quiet man and the wall of blue ice before them, it did not seem absurd at all.
