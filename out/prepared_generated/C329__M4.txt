The bell above the shop door in Mill Street had a thin, apologetic ring, as though it knew it had no business disturbing anyone. Edmund Tallow was counting the last of the day’s coppers into the till when he heard it, and he looked up to see Miss Clara Winterbourne standing on the threshold, her bonnet dripping with the fine grey rain that had been falling since noon.

He knew her at once, of course. Everyone in Ashcombe knew the manager of the Ashcombe and Vale Bank, and everyone knew his daughter, who drove in the pony chaise on Thursdays and sat in the second pew at St. Botolph’s with her hands folded just so. She was not the sort of person who came into a shop that sold candles and cheese and penny ink.

“Mr. Tallow,” she said, and smiled. “I have come for a box of your best letter paper. Papa says I must write to my aunt in Bath, and I cannot abide the paper from the stationer’s by the bridge. It bleeds.”

Edmund fetched the paper from the shelf behind him, his ears burning. He was twenty-three years old, three years a clerk at the bank, and he had never in his life exchanged more than a dozen words with Clara Winterbourne. Those words had been “Good morning, miss,” and “Mind the step, miss,” and once, memorably, “The ledger is with Mr. Pugh, miss.”

“Shall I wrap it, miss?” he asked.

“If you please.” She leaned upon the counter, and the rain beaded on the shoulders of her mantle. “You are very quick with figures, Mr. Tallow. Papa says so. He says you have a head for the work that is uncommon in a young man.”

Edmund’s hands fumbled with the string. “Your father is very kind, miss.”

“He is not kind at all,” said Clara Winterbourne, quite calmly. “But he is accurate.”

After she had gone, Edmund stood for a long moment looking at the door, and the bell swinging gently on its hook, and the wet print of her boots on the flags. His mother came through from the back room with a basin of potatoes in her arms and asked him what had kept him so long, and he said nothing, but he was smiling, and she saw it.

The courtship, if it could be called that, proceeded by small and careful degrees. Miss Winterbourne discovered a need for letter paper every week, and then a need for sealing wax, and then a need for a particular sort of ribbon that Mrs. Tallow happened to keep in a drawer, though she sold perhaps two yards of it a year. Edmund began to walk home by the long way, past the bank, and it was astonishing how often Miss Winterbourne happened to be standing at the window of the counting-house at the hour he passed. Once she let him carry her basket to the chaise. Once she gave him a note, folded small, which said only that she had read a book he had once mentioned and found it dull, and that she thought he was probably dull too, and that this was a compliment, though she could not explain why.

He kept the note in his waistcoat pocket and read it so often that the crease wore through.

“You are being courted,” said his mother one evening, when he had come in late and would not say where he had been. She was sitting by the fire with her mending, and she did not look up. “By the Winterbourne girl.”

“She is not a girl,” said Edmund. “She is one-and-twenty.”

“That is what I said.” Mrs. Tallow bit through her thread. “She is the manager’s daughter, and you are a clerk with a mother who keeps a shop. Do you imagine she has looked at you and seen a man?”

“She has seen something,” said Edmund, stiffly.

“She has seen a door,” said his mother. “And she means to go through it. Her father is a hard man, Edmund. I have known him these thirty years, since before you were born, and he is hard as a millstone and twice as grinding. She will be married to whatever he chooses, or she will be kept in that house until she is grey, and she has chosen you because you are the one thing in this town he cannot easily crush. A clerk of his own bank. A humble, grateful, clever young man who would be so flattered to be lifted up that he would never once ask why.”

Edmund said that she was unjust, and that Miss Winterbourne was not like that, and that she had spoken to him of books and of Bath and of the dullness of Ashcombe, and that she had a wit that nobody in this house could appreciate. His mother said nothing more. She only sewed, and the fire spat, and the rain came down on Mill Street.

The invitation came in March, in a hand he knew. Mr. Winterbourne requested the pleasure of Mr. Edmund Tallow’s company at dinner on the Friday following, at the manager’s house in Prospect Row. Edmund read it three times before he could believe it, and then he ran all the way to the shop to tell his mother, who was weighing out sugar for the Widow Pike and did not stop weighing.

The dinner was a stiff affair of many courses and little talk. Mr. Winterbourne spoke of the bank, of the new notes in circulation, of the unreliability of the Welsh drapery trade. He asked Edmund his opinion of the ledger system, and listened to the answer with his head tilted, like a man appraising a horse. Clara sat at the other end of the table in a gown of pale green and said almost nothing, but once, when her father was occupied with the wine, she looked at Edmund and put her finger to her lips, and then smiled, and his heart turned over like a fish on a slab.
