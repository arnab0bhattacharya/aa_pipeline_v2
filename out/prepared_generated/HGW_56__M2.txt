Mrs. Agnes Thorne kept a boarding house at number 14, Elm Grove, Clapham Common, with the rigid efficiency of a clerk herself. It was a respectable establishment, smelling perpetually of boiled cabbage, strong tea, and the faint, dusty tang of gas mantles. The clientele was exclusively male, predominantly junior clerks from the various insurance and accounting firms scattered across the South London suburbs. They were quiet, sedentary men, bound by waistcoats and a certain dullness of purpose, and they found in Mrs. Thorne’s house a sanctuary from the chaotic, noisy world outside. They paid their four shillings and sixpence weekly, ate their mutton, and read their papers. They did not, as a rule, speak much.

Until the arrival of Miss Elara Vance.

Miss Vance was a typist, engaged by the firm of Halloway & Sons, where three of Mrs. Thorne’s lodgers—Arthur Pennington, J. C. Stubb, and Mr. Silas Vane—spent their days. She was a tall woman, with a sharp, angular face and an energy that seemed to rattle the doorframes. She did not stay for the meals, for her work was done in the morning, but she had a room at number 14, which Mrs. Thorne had let to her on the understanding that she would keep the house tidy in return for the reduced rent. It was a practical arrangement. Mrs. Thorne was a practical woman.

The trouble began, as it often does, with a small, insignificant thing. It was a Tuesday in November, and the sky was the color of wet slate. Arthur Pennington, a young man of twenty-two with a tendency to stammer when nervous, was seen walking Miss Vance to the tram stop. They were not holding hands, but they were walking close, their shoulders nearly touching. Arthur returned to the house pale, his tie askew, and he ate his dinner in a state of such distracted misery that he dropped his spoon twice.

Mrs. Thorne watched him from the kitchen doorway. She had eyes like a hawk and a mind like a ledger. She noted the drop of the spoon, the flush in Arthur’s ears, and the way he looked at the empty chair at the head of the table, where Miss Vance usually sat during her brief visits.

"Arthur," Mrs. Thorne said, her voice dry as paper. "You are not feeling well."

"I am quite well, Mrs. Thorne," Arthur said, and his voice cracked. "It is the damp. It settles in the bones."

"Indeed," said Mrs. Thorne. "It does settle in the bones. But I notice J. C. Stubb has taken to polishing his boots until midnight. He was never a man who cared for his boots before. And Mr. Vane has not been seen in the common room for three days. He sits in his room, writing letters. I can hear the scratching of the pen."

Arthur looked up, his eyes wide. "You see everything, Mrs. Thorne."

"I see what is before me," she replied. "And what is before me is a house in disorder. You three boys are acting like children. Miss Vance is a fine girl, but she is not a prize to be fought over. And if you think to disrupt the peace of this house with your romances, you will find I am not a woman to be trifled with."

Arthur had the grace to look ashamed. He apologized, mumbled something about the pressure of work, and left the table. But Mrs. Thorne knew that apologies from young men in love were like smoke; they dispersed quickly and left nothing behind.

The second crisis arrived not with a whisper, but with the clatter of police boots on the gravel path. It was a Thursday afternoon. Two constables, looking ill at ease, stood in the doorway of the common room. Mr. Silas Vane was sitting in his usual corner, a book of poetry open on his lap. He looked up, and Mrs. Thorne saw the blood drain from his face so completely that he looked like a waxwork.

"Mr. Silas Vane?" the senior constable asked. "I have a warrant. Allegations of embezzlement from the firm of Halloway & Sons."

The room went silent. The other clerks, who had been dozing over their tea, sat bolt upright. Arthur Pennington, who had been present for the sake of a game of checkers, looked at Vane with a mixture of horror and pity. J. C. Stubb, who had been cleaning his nails, stopped and stared.

Mrs. Thorne stepped forward. She did not scream. She did not fawn. She simply said, "You will take him, or you will wait? If you take him, you will need to search the premises. If you wait, I shall make tea."

The constable blinked. "We are under instruction to arrest him immediately, Mrs. Thorne."

"Then take him," she said. "But do not let him leave the house until I have spoken to him. He is a lodger. I am responsible for the conduct of my household. If he is to be removed, I wish to know why, so that I may inform the others without rumor taking over."

The constable, perhaps taken aback by her cold, administrative tone, agreed to wait in the kitchen. Mrs. Thorne led Vane to her office. She closed the door.

"Silas," she said, using his first name, a breach of protocol that marked the gravity of the moment. "What have you done?"

Vane was shaking. He was a thin man, with a nervous habit of twisting his wedding band, though he was unmarried. "I don't know what to say, Mrs. Thorne. The books... they didn't balance. I thought if I moved a few sums here and there, it would balance out by month's end. I didn't mean to take the money. I just... I needed to fix the discrepancy. It was a mistake. A terrible mistake."
