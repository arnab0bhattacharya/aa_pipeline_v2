The Thames was the colour of a bruise, and it smelt like one too, of something ruptured and old. Eleanor Vance stood on the deck of the *Southern Cross* and watched the tugs coax the vessel toward the damp stone of Gallows Quay. She had forgotten how grey England was. For ten years she had known only the blinding blue of the Canterbury skies and the dun-coloured expanses of her father’s high-country sheep station, a place called Wharekahu, where the wind never stopped singing through the tussock. Now, the soot-stained warehouses of the docklands closed in around her like the walls of a prison cell. She pulled her heavy wool coat tighter, bracing herself against the October chill that seemed to seep directly into her bones.

She had come back to claim an inheritance. Her father’s elder brother, a bachelor who had died in a house in Bloomsbury, had left her his entire estate. It was a quirk of fate that had brought her back to the city of her birth; she had been sent to Wharekahu at sixteen to care for an ailing mother, and after her mother's passing, she had stayed to manage the station for her father. Now her father was gone too, buried in the red earth beneath the Southern Alps, and Eleanor was an heiress. She scanned the throng of faces waiting at the quayside, looking for the solicitor her father’s letters had mentioned, a man by the name of Higgins.

"Miss Vance?" a voice asked from the foot of the gangplank.

She looked down. A man stood there, holding a black umbrella. He was tall and remarkably thin, dressed in a dark suit that hung off his frame like a shroud. His face was narrow, with cheekbones that cast shadows in the weak afternoon light, and his eyes were a pale, watery blue. He smiled, but the expression did not reach his eyes.

"I am Mr. Alistair Crane," he said, extending a gloved hand. "Of Crane, Thorne, and Thorne. Mr. Higgins sends his profound apologies. He was taken ill in the night—a chill on the lung, I’m afraid. He asked me to attend to you personally."

Eleanor shook his hand. His grip was limp, his leather glove damp. "I see," she said. "Thank you for coming, Mr. Crane."

"Think nothing of it," he replied, his smile fixed and glassy. "Let us get you out of this dreadful damp. I have a hansom cab waiting."

Within three days, Eleanor began to suspect that Mr. Crane was not a solicitor at all. He had installed her in a gloomy, half-furnished boarding house on a quiet street in Bloomsbury, claiming that her uncle's house was currently undergoing essential structural repairs and was unfit for habitation. He had not taken her to an office. There were no ledgers, no stacks of legal parchments bearing the stamp of Crane, Thorne, and Thorne. He visited her every morning at precisely nine o'clock, bringing her cups of bitter tea and asking her, in his soft, oily voice, to sign various documents.

On the third morning, as he bent over the parlour table to dip his pen in the inkwell, she noticed a detail that made her stomach clench. His cuffs were frayed, the white linen yellowed and threadbare, but the suit he wore over them was of a heavy, expensive wool. It did not fit him. The sleeves were too short, and the collar gaped around his thin neck. It was not his suit. It was a dead man's suit.

Furthermore, he asked too many questions about the station. He did not ask about the legalities of her father's estate in New Zealand, nor about the transfer of funds from the Bank of New Zealand. Instead, he wanted to know about the physical layout of Wharekahu. He asked how many men worked the station, how far the nearest constabulary post was, and whether the boundaries were fenced. He asked if her father had kept a strongbox of sovereigns, or if he had trusted the colonial banks.

"Why do you ask about the station, Mr. Crane?" Eleanor had asked that morning, keeping her voice carefully neutral.

Crane had blinked, his watery eyes widening for a fraction of a second. "Merely procedural, Miss Vance," he had said smoothly. "We must establish the full extent of your trans-Tasman assets to satisfy the British probate courts."

It was a lie. Eleanor had spent ten years managing a sprawling sheep station in the remote backblocks of the South Island. She had dealt with stock agents, unscrupulous shearers, and men who thought a woman had no head for business. She knew when a man was trying to fleece her. Crane was not interested in probate. He was trying to ascertain if she had any allies left in the world, or if she was entirely alone and vulnerable.

That night, after the landlady had brought her a supper of cold mutton and stale bread, Eleanor sat at the small writing desk in her room. She opened her travelling trunk and retrieved a sheet of heavy, cream-coloured stationery, the paper she had brought from Wharekahu. She uncapped her inkwell and wrote quickly, her handwriting a sharp, slanted script born of years of writing dispatches in the dust.

*Dear Inspector,*

*I write to you in the utmost urgency and secrecy. My name is Eleanor Vance, lately of Wharekahu Station, New Zealand, and currently residing at 14 Caradoc Street, Bloomsbury. I have recently returned to England to claim the estate of my late uncle, and have been placed under the charge of a man calling himself Mr. Alistair Crane, a solicitor. I have cause to believe this man is an imposter. He exhibits no knowledge of the law, possesses no offices, and wears a suit that does not belong to him. More alarmingly, he is making pointed inquiries regarding the isolated nature of my former home and my late father’s financial holdings. I fear he intends to do me harm, or to defraud me of my inheritance. I dare not go to the police, for he watches my movements. I am told you are a man who can be trusted with delicate matters. I beg you to call upon me tomorrow.*
