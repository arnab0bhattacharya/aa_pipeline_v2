It is a common error in the arithmetic of the nursery, where affection is necessarily doled out in proportion to the visible capital of sweetness or pudding, to suppose that the world at large will continue to hold a corresponding balance in one’s favor when the said capital has suffered a catastrophic diminution. But human nature, considered as a financial instrument, is but a slender security against the hard logic of ledgers; and the discovery that one’s power to charm was intricately interwoven with the precise sum standing to one’s credit at the bank, is a lesson not learned without a certain spiritual contortion.

Miss Clara Vane, only daughter of the late Mr. Vane of Lockley Grange, had been raised in the comfortable persuasion that her thirty thousand pounds were not merely a fact in the world’s political economy, but a sort of moral halo, investing her caprices with gravity and her defects with amiability. She had been accustomed to seeing young men of good county families—Mr. Fothergill of the Red Lion blood, young Captain Ashby, and others of less determinate pursuit—hover about her at the local balls and archery meetings, with an assiduity that she was pleased to attribute to her personal fascination, rather than to the more ponderable fact of her expectations. It is true that Clara had a fine dark eye and a commanding profile, features that might have held their ground without the reinforcement of five per cent; but the five per cent gave them a force of fascination which, like the force of gravitation, requires a central mass to make itself felt. And when, in the autumn of 1864, the highly respectable banking-house of Phipps, Gorringe, and Phipps collapsed with a sickening suddenness that seemed to shake the very foundations of provincial confidence, Clara found that her eye and her profile, unsupported by any solid monetary mass, exerted a pull that was, to say the least, surprisingly feeble.

The catastrophe was complete. Mrs. Vane, a woman of that gentle and dependent type whose intellect moves in a perpetual orbit around the nearest male authority, sat in her morning-room at Lockley Grange, alternately wringing her hands over the disaster and looking to Clara for some indication of what they were to do next, in a manner that suggested she had mistaken her daughter for the managing director of the ruined bank. Clara, for her part, felt the walls of her world closing in upon her with a velocity that left her breathless. The suitors who had formerly thronged the drawing-room at Lockley now contented themselves with formal notes of inquiry, couched in a language of distant sympathy that was the very reverse of that warm and particular interest they had formerly displayed. Mr. Fothergill, indeed, was shortly afterward observed by a mutual friend to be paying very marked attentions to Miss Bellingham, a young woman whose chief recommendation was a property in Staffordshire and a face that might charitably be described as amiable; while Captain Ashby, who had once declared that he could not exist without hearing Clara sing “The Last Rose of Summer,” was discovered to have existed quite comfortably for three weeks in London without once approaching the neighborhood of Lockley.

It was in this period of humiliating destitution, when the very furniture of the Grange seemed to reproach them with an insolence of comfort that was no longer theirs, that Mrs. Vane’s cousin, Mrs. Leach—a lady whose name was not without significance in relation to the sustenance she drew from others—kindly communicated to them the intelligence that Lady Hawthorn of Waveney Park was in want of a governess for her two younger daughters. Mrs. Leach, who had a knack for presenting disagreeable facts with an air of philanthropic effort, as though she had personally extracted them from the stubborn earth for the recipient’s benefit, dwelt upon the excellence of the situation: the salary was forty pounds a year, the work not unreasonable, and Lady Hawthorn, though somewhat exacting, was a woman who knew how to maintain the distinctions that ought to exist between those who pay and those who are paid.

Clara’s first impulse was to reject the proposition with the scorn that a sovereign might be expected to show toward a proposal that she should turn street-sweeper. The transition from being an heiress, courted and deferred to, to being a dependent servant in a household where she had once been visited as an equal, was a descent that her pride refused to contemplate without a rebellion of the whole system. She said as much to her mother, with a bitterness that was perhaps the natural overflow of a heart that feels itself ill-used by the universe, and which, in the absence of any specific culprit to punish, wreaks its vengeance on those nearest to it.

“And what, I suppose, are we to live upon?” she added, with that irony which is the refuge of the helpless. “Upon the recollection of Mr. Fothergill’s devotion, or upon Captain Ashby’s assurance that he could not exist without my singing?”

Mrs. Vane, who had no answer to this, wept; and her tears, as is often the case with tears shed by a parent in the presence of a child whose distress is already acute, had the effect of making Clara feel that she was being both cruel and unreasonable, without in the least diminishing her conviction that she was right. But the days that followed brought with them a slow and inevitable pressure of circumstances—the unpaid bills, the lingering looks of the servants, the necessity of quitting the Grange before the quarter-day—and with this pressure came a gradual transformation in Clara’s view of the governess position. It was not that her pride abated; rather, it changed its direction. To accept the position was humiliating; but to refuse it, and sink into the sort of shabby gentility that hangs about the skirts of relatives and lives upon the remnants of their tolerance, was a humiliation of a still more insidious kind.
