The newspapers lay in a heap upon the table, smelling of ink and damp wool, and Major-General Pemberton, having settled himself into the leather wing-chair with the familiarity of long habit, drew the *Times* towards him. It was a Tuesday. The fog had come down overnight, pressing against the windows of the Bellingham Club in St James’s with a soft, grey insistence, so that the streetlamps were nothing but pale, drowned moons. It was a day for the indoors; a day for the smell of beeswax and the quiet, rhythmic ticking of the great clock in the hall, which marked the seconds with the indifference of eternity.

He turned the page. There was a famine in Russia; a new bridge in Canada; the price of wheat was fluctuating. He read the words, but they did not settle in him. They slid off the surface of his mind like rain off oilcloth. His attention was caught, instead, by the dust motes dancing in the shaft of grey light that pierced the gloom—little frantic things, alive for a moment, then gone. He was aware of the weight of his own body, the stiffness in the knee that had troubled him since Amritsar, and the particular silence of the club, which was not a silence of absence but a silence of many men holding their breath.

Across the room, old Hallowes was asleep, his mouth open, his newspaper draped over his chest like a shroud. The fire hissed in the grate, a low, consoling sound. Pemberton looked at the headlines again. *Election Prospects. The Situation in the East.* It was all noise, noise and clutter, and he was glad to be out of it, glad to be retired, glad to be here where the only thing demanded of him was that he should exist.

At half-past twelve, the gong sounded—a deep, resonant note that seemed to vibrate in the marrow. Pemberton folded his paper with precision, aligning the edges, and rose. The dining room was a cavern of white linen and silver. The light here was warmer, ambered by the chandeliers, but the fog still pressed at the high windows. He took his usual table in the corner. The waiter, a man named Stevens with a face like a dried apple, knew his preferences without being told. A slice of roast beef, slightly underdone; potatoes roasted in the fat; a glass of claret. No vegetables, save for a whisper of cabbage.

He ate slowly. The taste of the beef was familiar, solid, a fact in a world of fluctuating opinions. He chewed methodically, watching the other diners. There was young Trench, laughing too loudly at something a companion had said; his laughter was bright and brittle, shattering the peace. There was the Vicar, eating a sole with delicate, mincing movements. They were all strangers, really. They nodded to one another in the morning room, they passed the salt at dinner, but their interiors were locked fast against him. And he, he realized, was locked fast against them. He did not mind. It was a comfortable prison.

The claret was good. It warmed the stomach and loosened the thoughts, though the thoughts were not of much consequence. He thought of the garden in Hampshire, which would be turning now, the leaves curling brown at the edges. He thought of the dog, Rover, dead these ten years. He thought of nothing at all, and let the claret do the work of thinking for him.

When he returned to the smoking room, the light had failed entirely. The lamps had been lit, casting pools of yellow warmth that left the corners in shadow. He ordered a coffee and a brandy. The telegram boy came in just then. He was a small, red-headed lad, out of breath, his uniform damp with the mist. He looked about him with the terrified eyes of the uninitiated, clutching a brown envelope.

“Major-General Pemberton?” the boy squeaked.

The silence of the room deepened, became acute. Hallowes woke up with a snort. The Vicar looked over his spectacles. Pemberton held out his hand. The envelope was cool, damp. He fumbled for a shilling, and the boy snatched it and was gone, his boots clattering on the floorboards.

Pemberton held the envelope for a moment. He did not want to open it. It was an intrusion, a jagged thing in the smooth texture of the afternoon. He slit it with a silver knife kept for the purpose.

*Reginald getting married St Margaret Westminster Saturday next. Please attend. Love. Mother.*

He read it twice. The words were stark, black against the flimsy paper. *Reginald getting married.* The boy. His son. A strange, possessive heat rose in him, followed immediately by a cold draught of confusion. Married. To whom? He did not know. He had not been told. He looked at the name, *Mother*, and felt a surge of irritation. His wife, Eleanor, had sent this like a command, a summons. But the bride—the name was missing. Who was she? A girl from the county? A shopkeeper’s daughter? A stranger with a smile and a way of talking he would not understand?

He folded the paper and put it in his pocket. He looked at the fire. The flames were yellow and orange, consuming the coal, turning it to ash. *Reginald getting married.* The boy was twenty-two. It seemed only a moment ago he had been in the nursery, a small, red-faced creature with lungs like bellows. Now he was a man, a stranger, standing at an altar with another stranger.

Pemberton felt the room shift. The solid furniture—the heavy sideboard, the leather chairs, the portraits of dead generals—seemed to waver, to lose their substance. The silence of the club was no longer a comfort; it was an emptiness. He was alone in it, and the telegram was a bridge to a world he did not inhabit. He did not know the bride. He would stand in the church, and a girl would come down the aisle in white, and he would not know the colour of her eyes or the sound of her voice until she spoke the vows.
