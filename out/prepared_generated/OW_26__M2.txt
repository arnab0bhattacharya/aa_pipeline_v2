The catastrophe, which was to be the defining event of the morning, occurred at precisely half-past nine, as Reginald Vane stood before the full-length mirror in his dressing room, adjusting the knot of a cravat of such delicate silk that it seemed less like fabric and more like a whisper frozen in time. He was a man who understood that life is a series of carefully curated illusions, and that the buttonhole is not merely an adornment but a vital organ of the social body. To appear without one is not simply to look incomplete; it is to admit, in the most vulgar of terms, that one has no soul to conceal.

Reginald looked at the empty lapel of his charcoal suit. The space where a carnation should have bloomed was a void, a terrible absence that mocked the perfection of the ensemble. He had lost the flower. It had slipped from its velvet pocket, likely during a moment of excessive gaiety at the rehearsal dinner, and had been trampled into the dust of the ballroom by some unfortunate guest’s heel. Reginald did not care for the flower itself; indeed, he had never cared for the flower. He cared for the *idea* of the flower, the green stem, the red petal, the symbol of passion that allowed him to remain detached from the emotion it purported to represent.

He rang the bell with a finger that trembled slightly, not from fear, but from the profound irritation of having his aesthetic order disrupted.

"Mr. Pym," he called out, his voice carrying the cool, clipped precision of a man who had never once in his life been out of breath. "Bring me a carnation. A single one. It must be red, but not the red of a brick, nor the red of a warning, but the red of a secret that has been told too often."

Pym, a man of such unremarkable features that one might suspect he had been designed by a committee of accountants, entered the room with a look of profound distress. "Sir, it is Sunday. The florists are closed. The whole of London is closed. We are in a state of general observance."

"London is never closed to the man who knows where to look," Reginald said, turning to face the mirror again. "It is only closed to the mediocre. Go. You will find a flower. If you do not, I shall have to wear a button of thread, and I cannot bear the thought of being perceived as so frugal."

Pym left, his footsteps sounding like the dull thud of reality striking against the polished floor. Reginald dressed himself with the methodical care of a surgeon preparing for an operation. He knew that the wedding of Lord Ashworth to the Duchess of Halloway was the event of the season. To miss it would be a social suicide; to attend it without the carnation would be an aesthetic murder. He had no choice but to go himself. The pursuit of the flower was no longer a task for a servant; it was a quest for the hero.

He stepped out into the London morning, which was grey and misty, the kind of weather that seems designed to dampen the spirits of the optimistic and the ambitions of the dull. The city was still, the cabs silent, the shops shuttered. Reginald walked with a long, languid stride, his hat tilted slightly over his eyes, giving him the appearance of a man who had all the time in the world and intended to waste it.

He began in Mayfair, where the air was thick with the scent of coffee and the promise of wealth. But the florists here were shut, their doors locked against the incursions of the common day. He moved on to Piccadilly, where the grandeur of the architecture offered a stark contrast to the mundane reality of the closed businesses. He entered a small shop near the Ritz, run by a woman with eyes like flint and a manner that suggested she had seen enough of men to despise them.

"Is there a carnation?" he asked, not looking at her, but at a vase of dried lavender on the shelf.

"Sold out," she said, without raising her head. "Last one went to a boy for his mother. A poor boy. He looked as if he had eaten his breakfast, which is a rarity in these parts."

"Can you sell me the memory of it?" Reginald asked. "I am prepared to pay a premium for nostalgia."

The woman looked up. "I sell flowers, sir, not ghosts. If you want a flower, go to Covent Garden. But I warn you, it is a long walk, and the flowers there are not of the first quality. They are for the market, not for the drawing room."

Reginald thanked her with a nod that was more a dismissal than a courtesy and stepped back into the street. The walk to Covent Garden was a journey through the layers of the city. He passed through the wealthy squares where the horses stood idle, their breath visible in the cold air, and into the narrower streets where the smell of coal smoke and wet pavement became more pungent. He saw a beggar huddled under a portico, and Reginald paused. He did not give the man money, for money is too direct, too vulgar. Instead, he looked at the man with a pity that was as cold as the stone beneath his feet. The beggar had no buttonhole, Reginald thought, and therefore had no need for a flower. It was a mercy, in a way.

By the time he reached Covent Garden, the mist had lifted, revealing a sky of pale, washed-out blue. The market was empty, the stalls covered with canvas, the air still holding the ghostly scent of lilies and roses. He found a young man, a porter perhaps, sitting on a crate of empty crates, eating an apple.
