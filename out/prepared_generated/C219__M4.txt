Mrs. Honoria Pemberton believed that Providence had furnished her with a parish for the express purpose of arranging it. As the wife of the Reverend Mr. Pemberton, rector of St. Botolph’s in the village of Ashcombe St. Mary, she considered herself the appointed agent of order in a world that would otherwise lapse into a regrettable and untidy chaos. It was she who decided which of the village maids ought to be sent to service in Reading and which were better kept at home to mind their younger brothers; she who prescribed the exact shade of green for the altar cloth and the precise number of currant buns for the harvest tea. Her husband, a gentle man with a fine collection of sermons and a horror of contention, had long since learned to nod at the appropriate moments and to keep his own counsel on the subject of currant buns.

It was in the autumn of the year that the new physician arrived in Ashcombe that Mrs. Pemberton discovered her most challenging and therefore most enticing project. Dr. Edward Ashby was a man of perhaps thirty years, with a grave, pleasant countenance and a manner so quietly agreeable that it seemed almost a form of retreat. He had taken the house on the corner of Mill Lane, hung out his brass plate, and proceeded to attend to the coughs and fevers of the parish with a diligence that Mrs. Pemberton found admirable but incomplete. A man in his position, she declared to her husband over breakfast, required a wife. The rector murmured something about the doctor’s own opinion on the matter, which his wife chose to interpret as wholehearted agreement.

The squire’s niece, Miss Louisa Vance, had come to Ashcombe in the spring to keep house for her uncle, a widower of bluff and ruddy habits who preferred dogs to company and company to solitude only when the company was willing to shoot. Miss Vance was five-and-twenty, possessed of a modest competence, a clear eye, and a temperament that Mrs. Pemberton described as “reserved” and that Louisa herself would have described as “private.” She was perfectly polite, perfectly capable, and perfectly content to pass her days in the quiet administration of the squire’s household and the reading of novels from the circulating library in Newbury. She had no more desire to be married than she had to be hanged, but she was too well-bred to say so, and so she smiled when Mrs. Pemberton smiled and said “Indeed?” when Mrs. Pemberton said anything at all.

The first intimation of the scheme came at the Michaelmas tea, when Mrs. Pemberton contrived to seat Dr. Ashby and Miss Vance together at a small table by the window, under the pretext that the light was better there for the doctor’s eyes, which were, she claimed to have noticed, somewhat strained by his long hours of reading by candlelight. The doctor, who had never complained of his eyes in his life, found himself seated beside a young woman who looked at him with a frankness that was almost disconcerting.

“I am told you have a great fondness for chess, Dr. Ashby,” said Louisa, after a silence of some moments.

“I play a little,” he replied. “Badly, I fear.”

“That is a relief. I am surrounded by people who play well and insist upon it.”

He glanced at her, and for the first time that afternoon something like genuine amusement crossed his face. “I shall endeavour to play worse than you, then, so that you may have the pleasure of winning.”

“That is very gallant, but I warn you, I shall not be grateful for it.”

“I should not dream of expecting gratitude.”

They spoke no more of consequence, but Mrs. Pemberton, observing from across the room, noted the doctor’s slight smile and Miss Vance’s unguarded laugh and went to bed that night with the satisfied air of a general who has seen his troops take the first hill.

What followed was a campaign conducted with the utmost delicacy and persistence. Mrs. Pemberton discovered a sudden need for the doctor’s opinion on the health of the squire’s household, which required him to call at the Hall on precisely the afternoons when Miss Vance was at home. She arranged for Miss Vance to be invited to the rectory for the purpose of advising on the decoration of the parish schoolroom, and contrived that the doctor should happen to call at the same hour to examine the rector’s troublesome knee. She spoke of each to the other with an enthusiasm that bordered on the lyrical. Dr. Ashby was “a man of such steady principle”; Miss Vance was “a young woman of uncommon good sense.” She invited them both to dinner on three separate occasions, and on each occasion seated them together, and on each occasion watched them with the hopeful intensity of a hen regarding her chicks.

The difficulty—which Mrs. Pemberton, in her benevolence, could not perceive—was that the doctor and Miss Vance had each independently concluded that the other was a person of considerable sense and no small charm, and had each independently decided that this was precisely the sort of person with whom one might enjoy a pleasant acquaintance, and that any further development would be an unmitigated nuisance. They had no wish to marry. The doctor was wedded to his work and to the quiet of his own fireside; Miss Vance was wedded to her independence and to the comfortable certainty that she need not consult anyone’s preferences but her own. And so they found themselves, by the middle of November, united in a single, unspoken purpose: to escape Mrs. Pemberton’s kindness without giving offence.

The alliance was formed on a wet Tuesday afternoon in the doctor’s consulting room, where Miss Vance had come to fetch a bottle of cough mixture for the squire’s housekeeper. Dr. Ashby, handing her the bottle, remarked that he had heard the rectory was to hold a card party on Friday next.
