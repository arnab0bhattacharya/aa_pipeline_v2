My situation at Thornleigh Hall was one of comfort, if not of happiness. I had been engaged as governess to the two daughters of Sir Arthur Pemberton, and found in them docile, if not brilliant, pupils. The younger, Miss Helen, was a merry child of twelve, her heart as open as the Cheshire sky in summer. The elder, Miss Isabel, was another matter entirely. At eighteen, she was possessed of a beauty so profound and still that it seemed less an attribute than a soul made visible. Her hair was the colour of dark honey, her eyes the grey of a winter sea, and a gentle gravity sat upon her features, lending her a loveliness that was at once captivating and remote. I admired her, but I could not claim to know her. She moved through the grand rooms of Thornleigh like a quiet spirit, her presence a silent rebuke to the noisy world.

The storm, when it broke, did so with the sudden, shocking violence of a squall upon the moors. It was an afternoon in late October; the wind had been rising all day, rattling the casements and driving sheets of rain against the stone walls of the Hall. I had dismissed my pupils and was attempting to read in the schoolroom, but the shriek of the gale made concentration impossible. The fire spat and hissed in the grate, a frantic, orange heart in the gloom. It was then that the door opened, and Isabel stood there, not with her usual composure, but with a wildness in her eyes that mirrored the tempest outside. Her face was as white as the lace at her throat.

“Miss Grey,” she began, her voice a thread of sound against the wind. “May I speak with you? I cannot… I cannot be alone.”

I rose at once, my own heart quickening. “My dear Miss Pemberton, you are distressed. Come, sit by the fire.”

She obeyed, but did not sit. She paced the small room, her hands twisting in her skirts. “He has asked,” she said, the words falling like stones into the silence between the gusts. “Mr. Morley has formally proposed, and my father has given his consent. Nay, more—he has commanded my own.”

I knew Mr. Morley by sight and reputation. He was a man of some fifty years, a manufacturer from Manchester whose wealth was rumoured to be vast and whose face was as hard and unyielding as the iron he traded in. He had been a frequent guest at Thornleigh for the past six months, his dark, possessive eyes following Isabel’s every movement. I had felt a chill in his presence, a sense of a predator sizing up its quarry.

“Commanded you?” I repeated, the word tasting of injustice. “Surely, Miss Pemberton, in this day and age, a daughter’s consent is not a thing to be wrung from her like a debt.”

A bitter smile touched her lips. “You do not know my father, Miss Grey. To him, I am not a daughter but an asset. Thornleigh is mortgaged to the very lintels. Mr. Morley’s fortune is the only remedy. My father sees it as a holy alliance, a salvation. He calls my reluctance a wilful, girlish fancy.” She turned to me, and in her eyes I saw a desperate, searching plea that pierced me to the quick. “He is old, Miss Grey. His hands are cold and his heart is colder still. He speaks of my beauty as a man speaks of a fine horse or a well-tuned piano. He wishes to acquire me.”

The injustice of it, the sheer, brutal commerce of the thing, ignited a fire in my own breast. I felt a surge of fury on her behalf. “And what is to be done? Is there no one to speak for you? No relation, no friend?”

“Who would dare oppose my father in his own house?” she cried, her voice rising. “My mother is gone; she would have wept, but she would not have fought. The world would say I was a fool to refuse such a match. They would see the fortune, not the man. They would not feel the cage as I do.” She came and knelt before me, taking my hands in hers. They were as cold as if she had been standing in the rain. “You are the only one, Miss Grey. You are the only one who looks at me and sees a person, not a prize. Tell me what to do. Tell me there is hope.”

Her appeal broke my heart. For what hope could I offer? I was a governess, a dependent in this house, my own security as fragile as a spider’s web in the gale. To interfere in the affairs of my employer would be madness; it would mean my dismissal without a character, my ruin. The world is a hard place for a woman without means, and I had learned its lessons well. My power was a nullity. My right to interfere was less than nothing.

Yet, to look into that face, ravaged by despair, and speak of prudence and my own precarious position felt like a betrayal of all that is right and holy. Scripture speaks of the bond between sisters, of bearing one another’s burdens; is the bond between the helpless and the just any less sacred?

I rose and went to the window, pressing my forehead against the cold, streaming glass. The wind howled like a lost soul. Below, the great oaks of the park thrashed in torment, their branches groaning as if in mortal agony. I saw my own reflection, a pale, anxious woman, superimposed upon the chaos. I was powerless. To speak was to lose my place, my livelihood, my future. To be silent was to be a coward, to stand by while this bright, gentle spirit was sold into a living death.
