The train jolted, and the rain, which had been merely a grey mist over London, now streaked the glass with thick, heavy drops that raced each other into oblivion. Eleanor watched them, feeling the chill of the October afternoon seep through the window frame. She was travelling north; she was travelling to Darrowby, where the black crepe would already be hanging in the hall, and her sister Margaret’s voice, which had always been so commanding, so quick to reprove, would be utterly silenced. It was a monstrous thought, a thought that ballooned and pressed against the ribs, making breathing a conscious, mechanical effort. She looked at her gloves, pulling at the fingers, seeing not the black kid leather but Margaret’s hands, flashing with rings at the piano, striking chords that seemed to contain all the tragedy of the German masters. Now there was only the rattle of the carriage, a hollow, metallic drumming that sounded like a pulse beating in an empty room.

In the opposite corner sat a young man. He wore the khaki of the new army, a rough, almost greenish fabric that seemed to absorb the grey light and hold it captive. He was looking out of the window, but not, she thought, at the passing fields. His eyes were fixed, unblinking, as if he were watching a picture unroll that was entirely his own. His face was very pale, with a sort of chalky pallor, and beneath his eyes there were bruised hollows that gave him the look of a boy who has been awake too long, or who has seen things that forbid sleep. He held his cap on his knee, turning it slowly, ceaselessly, between his fingers, the brass badge catching a dull, occasional gleam. He was going home, Eleanor supposed. On leave. The word “leave” hung in the air between them, taking on a curious double meaning, as if he were being permitted to leave something, or something was being taken from him. He had a small scar on his jaw, a white nick that stood out against the pallor.

“The rain is very persistent,” she said, suddenly, surprising herself. She had not meant to speak; but the silence had become a third presence in the carriage, too heavy to bear, demanding to be broken by something, anything, however trivial.

He turned, startled, as if he had forgotten she was there. His eyes, which were a pale, watery blue, met hers for an instant, then fell. “Yes,” he said. “Very.” His voice was low, and it had a slight hoarseness, as if it were not used much. “It has been raining for three days, I think. In London.”

“Yes, in London,” she agreed. “And before that, the fog. One feels it will never be quite clean again.” She looked out at the Midlands, unrolling under the sky. The fields were sodden, a dull, heavy brown, broken by the black skeletons of trees that had not yet entirely lost their leaves. A few yellow leaves clung on, like flags of a defeated army. “The country looks very desolate,” she said. “Though I suppose it always does, at this time of year.”

He followed her gaze. “It does,” he said. He paused, then added, as if the words were drawn from him by some compulsion: “But it is very green, still, in places. There was a field, just past Rugby, that was almost emerald.” He spoke with a curious intensity, as if the greenness of that field were a fact of immense, almost sacred importance.

“Yes,” said Eleanor. “I saw it. It was like a jewel, in all that mud.” And she thought: he is looking at green things because where he has been, there is no green. There is only the brown, the grey, the black; the churned earth, the shattered trees. She did not know where he had been; she did not ask. It was not a question that could be asked. But she knew, with a certainty that went beyond words, that he had been in a place where the earth was torn and rent, where the sky was not a canopy but a menace, and where the colour green had become a memory, something that belonged to another world, a world of peace and summer and ordinary, unremarkable afternoons. She thought of Margaret, who had loved green things, who had filled the house at Darrowby with ferns and azaleas, and who would never see them again.

“Are you going far?” he asked, politely, as if he felt he must make some return for her observation.

“To Darrowby,” she said. “It is another two hours yet.”

“Darrowby,” he repeated. “I don’t know it. I am going to Thornton Lacey. It is a little place, in the hills.” He turned his cap again, and the brass badge flashed. “My mother will be at the station,” he said, and then stopped, as if he had said too much.

“She will be glad to see you,” Eleanor said, gently.

“Yes,” he said. “Yes, I suppose she will.” But his voice was doubtful, and he looked out of the window again, at the flying wires and telegraph poles, the endless, unspooling wire that seemed to connect nothing to nothing. Eleanor felt a sudden, sharp pang of understanding. He was afraid. Not of going back—he did not seem to be thinking of going back—but of arriving. Of stepping onto the platform, of seeing the familiar face, of being expected to be someone he no longer was. The boy who had left Thornton Lacey was not the man who was returning; and the mother who waited would look for the boy, and would not find him, and there would be, in that first glance, a moment of bewilderment, of loss, that would be more terrible than any shell or gunfire. She knew that bewilderment. She knew it because she was travelling to a house where she would be expected to be the sister, the elder sister, the capable one, and she was no longer capable; she was hollowed out, like a tree struck by lightning, and she did not know what shape she would present to the mourners, what face she would wear.
