If any one street in the sprawling parish of Lambeth could be said to possess a more abundant crop of mud, fog, and miserable children than its neighbours, that street was unquestionably Gravel Lane. It was a place where the houses leaned so affectionately towards one another across the narrow kennel that they seemed engaged in perpetual whispering, exchanging the damp secrets of their crumbling brickwork. The gutters gurgled with a thick, dark fluid that bore no resemblance to water, and the lamps, when they were lit at all, merely served to make the surrounding gloom visibly thicker.

In the most deeply shadowed recess of this thoroughfare, directly beneath the swaying, creaking sign of The Drowsy Sailor—a public house of a decidedly vinous and bilious complexion—stood Mr. Ebenezer Grout, a blind fiddler, and his granddaughter, Tilly. Mr. Grout was a man of great age and immense whiskers. His face was a map of wrinkles, drawn by the unkindly fingers of the London weather, and his eyes were closed forever beneath two heavy, grizzled arches. He possessed, however, a smile of such surpassing sweetness, and a manner of bobbing his head to and fro in time with his music, that he inspired in the charitable passer-by a feeling of profound and melancholy respect. He was wonderfully assisted in his endeavours by a small, terrier-like dog named Nip, whose principal duty was to hold the wooden collection cup by the handle, and to gaze reproachfully at any gentleman who attempted to enter the tavern without contributing a copper.

"Aha, Tilly!" Mr. Grout would cry, tucking his fiddle beneath a chin that had not known a razor for a fortnight, "they are merry within tonight! The very beams of the Drowsy Sailor do dance a hornpipe! Another tune, my little pearl, another tune to set the wet pavement tapping its toes!"

Tilly, who was a slight, pale child of twelve, with eyes of a very deep and searching blue, would dutifully turn the handle of her small hurdy-gurdy, or strike a triangle with a little stick, and the two would make what Mr. Grout was pleased to call "a very superior sort of harmony." It was, in truth, a sound that might have awakened a defunct pendulum; but to the frequenters of The Drowsy Sailor, freshly emerged into the biting air with their brains pleasantly fuddled by gin, it sounded like the very pinnacle of celestial orchestration.

This harmonious existence, such as it was, suffered a severe and sudden interruption. Mr. Grout, who had for several days been complaining of a dampness in his marrow, was seized one freezing November evening by a fit of shivering so violent that his fiddle bow scraped a series of agonizing discords across the strings, sounding for all the world like the shrieks of a tortured cat. He was conveyed to his damp lodgings in a wheelbarrow, attended by a sympathetic but largely useless crowd, and deposited in a bed that was as damp as the street outside.

The medical advice of the neighbourhood was prompt, and entirely free of charge. Mrs. Gamp, the landlady of the lodgings, a lady of ample proportions and a perpetual cold in the head, pronounced his condition to be a "galloping rheumatics," and prescribed hot gin, which she generously offered to administer herself, at the patient's expense.

"He is gone, my dear," whispered Mrs. Gamp to Tilly, with a sniff of profound meaning. "His toes is turning up to the frost, and his breath is like the draught from a keyhole. You must not look to see him fiddle no more. The Drowsy Sailor will have to do without its music."

Tilly, with a heavy and terrified heart, found herself compelled to face the world alone. On the following evening, she took her grandfather's fiddle, which was almost as large as herself, and stood beneath the swinging sign of the tavern. The wind blew her thin shawl about her face, and the fog crept into her throat, and when she drew the bow across the strings, the sound was so feeble and so tremulous that Nip, the little terrier, set up a melancholy howl in deep sympathy. The gentlemen emerging from the tavern heard the wailing, and feeling perhaps that they had paid quite enough for their own internal music, passed her by without a glance. The collection cup remained emptier than a miser’s promise.

It was on the third evening of her solitary and disheartening vigil—a night when the fog was so thick that one might have cut it with a knife and served it for pudding—that a gentleman approached her from the shadows. He was a man of tall and exceedingly upright figure, enveloped in a capacious coat of a violently checkered pattern. He wore a broad-brimmed hat, set at a rakish angle, and carried a heavy cane with a gold head, which he employed in gesturing to the unseen multitudes that he seemed to imagine surrounded him.

"Upon my soul and honour!" cried this gentleman, halting before the child and raising his gold-headed cane in an attitude of profound theatrical astonishment. "What do I behold? A sylph! A fairy of the gutter! A nightingale perched upon the very doorstep of Bacchus! And does this ethereal creature produce melody? Does she draw from the enchanted strings the very tears of the angels?"

Tilly, greatly alarmed by this torrent of words, drew back against the damp wall, clasping the fiddle to her chest.

"Come, come, my little maestro!" said the gentleman, doffing his hat with a sweeping bow that nearly knocked off the bonnet of a passing charwoman. "Do not be timid. I am a man of art, a patron of the muses. They call me, my dear, the Great Ptolomy. Perhaps you have heard of Ptolomy’s Unparalleled Travelling Exhibition of Human Curiosities and Mechanical Marvels? No? The loss is entirely the world's! I am collecting, my tiny virtuoso, the rarest flowers of talent. I have a pig that can count to forty, and a man with a wooden leg who can recite the poetry of Lord Byron. But I lack—I profoundly lack—a female fiddler of tender years. You shall be the Jewel of Lambeth! You shall be the Infant Strozzi!"
