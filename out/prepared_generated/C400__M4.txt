The steamer *Solveig* moved up the fjord with the steady, deliberate throb of an old heart, and the water it parted was so still and so dark that it seemed less like water than a polished floor of some vast, sunken hall. On either side, the mountains rose sheer from the shoreline, their granite faces streaked with the white of ancient ice and the green of clinging birch. The air was cool and had the thin, metallic taste of snow about it.

On the after-deck, clustered under the awning like a little flock of English sparrows in a land of eagles, the party from the touring company was taking tea. Mr. Haversham, a widower of some fifteen years, sat slightly apart from the others, his cup balanced on his knee, his gaze fixed on the distant, pale-blue shape of the glacier that was their destination. He was a man of quiet habits and a certain settled melancholy, and the grandeur of the scenery served only to deepen his sense of his own smallness, a feeling he did not altogether dislike.

Opposite him, at a small wicker table, sat a woman he had noticed but not yet spoken to. She was not young, perhaps forty, with a sensible tweed skirt and a face that was not beautiful but was, in its way, more interesting than beauty. Her eyes were of a keen, observing grey, and she held her teacup with a firmness that suggested a person accustomed to managing her own affairs. Her name, he had learned from the passenger list, was Miss Finch.

A sudden gust of wind, cold off the water, snatched at the hat of a young lady at the rail, and in the flurry of laughter and grabbing hands, Mr. Haversham found himself looking directly at Miss Finch. She met his eye and smiled, a small, wry, understanding smile. It was an invitation.

“You are not tempted to join the poetry debate, I see,” he said, nodding towards the group at the rail, where a young man named Lacy, red-faced with earnestness, was declaiming something to a Miss Tremayne, who was shaking her head with equal fervour.

“I am not,” Miss Finch replied, her voice low and clear. “I find that a glacier is a more honest thing than a sonnet. It has no opinions.”

Mr. Haversham was surprised into a short laugh. “An excellent observation. Though I confess I have a fondness for a well-turned line. It is a failing of my generation, perhaps.”

“Or a virtue,” she said. “It depends on the line.” She gestured with her cup towards the young people. “They are quarrelling about Mr. Browning. Mr. Lacy believes he is the poet of the age. Miss Tremayne finds him obscure and prefers the simple, honest sentiment of Mr. Tennyson. She has just accused Mr. Lacy of being a ‘muddle-headed optimist,’ and he has retaliated by calling her a ‘sentimental reactionary.’ I fear the peace of the afternoon is shattered.”

“A serious matter,” Mr. Haversham agreed. “To lose a friend over a poet.”

“They are young,” Miss Finch said, with a slight shrug. “They have the energy for it. When one is older, one learns that most disagreements are not about the thing itself, but about the person one wishes to be seen as. Mr. Lacy wishes to be seen as profound. Miss Tremayne wishes to be seen as sincere.”

Mr. Haversham looked at her with renewed interest. “You have a very analytical mind, Miss Finch.”

“I have had a great deal of time to observe,” she said, and there was no self-pity in the statement, only a simple fact. “I have been a governess, a companion, and a librarian. All professions which require one to be a student of human nature, and to keep one’s own counsel.”

The steamer’s whistle gave a short, sharp blast, and they both turned to see the ship was navigating a narrow strait between two towering cliffs. The water here was a deep, opaque emerald, and the silence was profound, broken only by the thrumming of the engines and the occasional, startling cry of a gull.

“It is a strange thing,” Mr. Haversham said, after a moment, “to be in a place that is so utterly indifferent to us. It makes one feel… small.”

“I find it comforting,” Miss Finch countered. “There is a great peace in being reminded that the world does not revolve around our particular hopes and disappointments. The glacier has been there for ten thousand years. It will be there for ten thousand more. Our little dramas are but a summer’s day to it.”

He considered this. “My wife would have agreed with you. She had a great love for mountains. She used to say that a mountain was the only thing that could make her feel properly humble without feeling humiliated.” He had not spoken of his wife so easily in years. The words had simply come, drawn out by the conversation and the landscape.

Miss Finch did not offer the usual platitudes. She simply nodded, her grey eyes thoughtful. “She sounds like a woman who understood the difference. It is a fine distinction.”

“It is,” he said, his voice softer. “I have often thought so.” He paused, then, feeling emboldened by her quiet understanding, he added, “I have a daughter. She is married now, with a child of her own. She writes to me of the price of coal and the latest fashions, and I find I have nothing to say in reply. I feel as though I have been left behind on a station platform while the train of the world has pulled out.”

“I have no children,” Miss Finch said, and for the first time a flicker of something—regret, perhaps, or a long-ago sorrow—passed over her face. “I have a great many books, and a cat who is a tyrant, and a small garden in which I grow herbs. It is a modest life. But I have never felt left behind. I have simply chosen a slower train.”
