The letter from Messrs. Fitch and Dorley, solicitors, of Lincoln’s Inn Fields, had lain for three days upon the marble washstand in her room before she opened it; and even then, having opened it, she did not at once read the figure, but stood at the window watching the rain make its slow snail’s tracks down the glass, thinking of nothing at all, or rather of the smell of the camphor and the sick-room, which still seemed to cling to her cuffs and to the folds of her skirt. Four hundred and twenty pounds, thirteen shillings and fourpence. The words, when she came to them, produced no more commotion in her mind than the ticking of the clock on the stairs; she folded the paper, put it back in its envelope, and went down to her dinner, where her aunt observed that she was late, and her uncle observed that the weather was seasonable for the time of year.

It was in the second week of October that she told them. She had chosen the moment after the cloth was drawn, when the port stood between her uncle and the fire, and the aunts had settled into that particular silence which is the prelude to digestion; and she said it quietly, in the voice she had learned in the sick-room, the voice that neither asks nor expects, that a woman might use to say that the fire wanted another coal. I have taken a passage to Palermo. I shall go on Thursday. Her aunt Letitia set down her glass with great deliberation, as though it contained something explosive, and said that she had never in all her life heard anything so extraordinary; and her uncle, after a pause in which he turned the decanter slowly by its neck, remarked that he had always understood that the money was to be put by, that there were responsibilities, that young women of twenty-six did not—but here he lost the thread of his objection and took refuge in the general proposition that the world was going to the devil, which was received, as it always was, with a murmur of mournful assent.

They did not know, none of them knew, what she knew: the long afternoons when the light thickened in the curtains and the old woman’s breathing filled the room like the sound of the sea in a shell; the way the hand had lain in hers, weightless at the last, a bird’s bone wrapped in paper; the hours upon hours of the small, necessary, tender offices that leave no trace in any record and are the whole of a life while they last. She had done it because it was to be done, and she had not known, until the doing was over, how the doing had eaten her. And now this money—this money which was not a reward, for there are no rewards, but a residue, a something left over, like the coins found in a pocket of a coat long out of fashion—this money should be, she had decided in the moment of deciding, a door. She would go through it alone, and she would speak to no one, and she would be, for a little while, no one’s daughter, no one’s niece, no one’s nurse.

The crossing was grey and the crossing was green, and she was ill for a day and a night, and did not care. She lay in her berth and listened to the engines, and the engines said, in their patient, regular, unarguable way, that the world was large, that the world was very large, and that she had not known it. When she came on deck the light was of a kind she had never seen, a light that seemed to have been strained through some finer substance than air, so that the sea was not one blue but a thousand blues, and the sky, which at home would have been a single flat and respectable grey, was here a vastness, a profundity, in which the clouds stood like distant mountains and the sun went down as though it were a thing that had never happened before and would never happen again.

Palermo. The name had been a word in a geography book; now it was a smell, a noise, a blaze. She took a room in a pension in a side street off the Via Maqueda, a tall, shabby, sun-struck house with a courtyard where a single lemon tree grew in a cracked pot and the concierge’s cat lay all day on the warm stone as though it had been poured there. Her room had a balcony, and from the balcony she could see, between the roofs, a wedge of the sea, and the sea was not the sea she had crossed but a different thing, a blue so intense that it seemed to have been painted on the air and to be about to peel away. She did nothing. That was the wonder of it. She did nothing, and the doing of nothing filled her to the brim. She woke early and lay listening to the bells, the cries, the grinding of cart-wheels, the strange, fluid, musical speech that rose and fell beneath her window like water over stones; and she did not understand a word of it, and the not understanding was a kind of peace. She ate strange things in little dark shops, bread and oil and tomatoes and small silver fish, and the tastes were sharp and new, and she was hungry as she had not been hungry for a year. She walked. She walked until her feet ached and the sun made her dizzy, and she sat on the steps of a church and watched the people go by, and no one spoke to her and she spoke to no one, and it was as though she had become invisible, and the invisibility was not a loneliness but a release, a taking off of a garment that had grown too tight.
