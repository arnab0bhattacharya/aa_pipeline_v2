When Miss Agnes Reave was told that she must go to keep her uncle’s house in the cathedral town of Marchester, she received the news with the silent, swelling resentment of sixteen, which feels a sentence of banishment to be a peculiar cruelty reserved for itself alone. Her father, a corn-factor whose affairs had lately gone the way of chaff before the wind, had spoken of it between the cheese and the walnuts with a forced cheerfulness that sat upon him like a borrowed coat. “Your uncle Theophilus,” said he, “has lived alone these thirty years and more, and wants a woman’s hand about the place. He is a dry stick, but a just one, and you will want for nothing.” Agnes, who had visions of a parsonage with a garden and a pony, and perhaps a young curate with a taste for poetry, heard the words “dry stick” and “lawyer” and felt the whole colour of life turn to a dun brown, like the fog which she was sure must hang perpetually over a town of blackened stone.

The house in the Close was indeed of a piece with her forebodings. It stood in a corner where the minster cast its shadow early, a tall, narrow house of dark brick, with windows that seemed to Agnes to have the prim, tight-lipped expression of their master. Mr. Theophilus Reave was a solicitor of the old school, who had drawn wills and settled conveyances until his very speech had come to sound like a clause in a deed—qualified, provisoed, and incapable of a superfluous adjective. He received his niece with a bow that was civil and a scrutiny that was not unkind, and having shown her the room she was to occupy, the press for her gowns, and the little parlour where she would sit of an evening, he retired to his office, and Agnes was left to the society of a deaf old housekeeper and the minster bells.

For the first weeks she was as wretched as she had promised herself to be. The town was full of clergymen’s widows and retired half-pay officers, who called and looked at her as if she were an article of furniture that had been unexpectedly introduced into a room already sufficiently furnished. The rain fell, the bells rang, and her uncle’s conversation, when he vouchsafed any, was chiefly concerned with the price of coals and the proper method of keeping the cellar dry. She wrote to her mother long letters of lamentation, in which the minster figured chiefly as a large black object that shut out the sun, and her uncle as a machine that ate and signed papers.

It was in the third week of her stay that she made her discovery. Her uncle had gone out upon some business connected with a mortgage, and Agnes, having exhausted the resources of the housekeeper’s gossip, had wandered into the little back study where he kept his books. It was a room she had been told not to enter, not from any severity, but because, as the housekeeper said, “the master cannot abide a thing to be moved.” The books were not many, and they were not, she thought, of a sort to tempt a girl. There were volumes of statutes in calf, a folio of Coke upon Littleton, and divers dry reports. But behind these, in a little shelf that was almost hidden by the curtain, she found a small octavo, bound in worn blue morocco, and, opening it, saw that it was a copy of the poems of Mr. William Cowper. It fell open at a page which had been much read, and there, in the margin, in a fine, cramped hand that she knew at once for her uncle’s, were written these words: “I was twenty-six when I first read this. The garden at B——. She was wearing a white gown. I have not seen her these twelve years.”

Agnes stood with the book in her hand, and felt the whole house about her change its aspect, as a landscape changes when the sun breaks through a cloud. It was as if she had heard a voice speaking in a room that she had thought empty. Her uncle, the dry stick, the machine that signed papers, had been twenty-six once, and had loved a woman in a white gown, and had kept the book for thirty years. She put it back in its place with a reverence that was new to her, and went about the house that day with a strange, tender curiosity, looking at the old man with different eyes. She noticed that his hand trembled when he poured out his tea, and that he sat sometimes for a long while with his pen in the air, looking at the fire, as if he were not in the room at all.

She said nothing, but she began to lay little snares for his confidence. She would speak of the minster, and of the music, and of the poetry that she had learned at school; and once she ventured to quote a line from Cowper, watching him the while under her eyelashes. He started, and looked at her with a quick, strange glance, and then said, “You have a good memory, child,” and fell to his bread and butter again. But after that evening he talked to her more. He told her of the town in the old days, and of the famous bell-ringer who had been hanged for sheep-stealing, and of the great frost when the river had borne a fair upon its back. And once, when the wind was howling in the chimneys, he said, quite suddenly, “I had a friend once who loved the poetry of Mr. Cowper. She is dead now.” And then he was silent, and Agnes felt that she had been admitted to a chamber in his heart which no one else had entered.
