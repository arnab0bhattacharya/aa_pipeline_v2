The business of the morning at Ashby Lacy was ordinarily concluded by noon, but on the day in question, the breakfast-parlour remained occupied until nearly two, and the cold meats grew cold in earnest. Mr. Henry Ashby, who had for thirty years believed himself a man of easy temper, sat at the head of the table with a letter before him which he had read four times, and which he did not intend to read a fifth. His lady, at the other end, was employed in the delicate operation of appearing not to look at him, an exercise which she performed with a zeal that defeated its own object; and in the window-seat, with a newspaper he had no intention of reading, sat his son and heir, Mr. Edmund Ashby, lately of Trinity College, Cambridge, and at present of no fixed address whatever.

The letter was from a tradesman in Lincoln, and it was not the first of its kind. It requested, in terms of the most respectful urgency, the settlement of a sum which Mr. Ashby the elder had already settled once, and which had been settled again, in a manner of speaking, by the young gentleman’s assurances that it should never be incurred a third time. The amount was not ruinous. That was the particular cruelty of it; it was not ruinous, only ruinous enough. It was the sort of sum which a man of estate might pay, and feel the better for having paid, if it were the last; and it was the sort of sum which, being paid, invited its fellows.

“I have been thinking,” said Mr. Ashby at length, in the tone of a man who has been thinking of nothing else for an hour and a half, “that the oak in Rook’s Copse is fit to come down.”

Lady Ashby looked up with the air of one who has been waiting for a more promising opening, and found it wanting.

“The oak in Rook’s Copse,” she repeated, “is the finest timber in the county, and was standing when your father came into the world. I have heard you say so yourself, Henry, and with some feeling.”

“I have said so,” he replied, “and I do not unsay it. But timber is a commodity, my dear, and a commodity is worth what it will fetch. It will fetch a great deal at present. There is a man at Grantham who would take it off my hands before the month is out.”

“And when it is down,” said Lady Ashby, “what is to become of the copse? It will be a field like any other, and Rook’s Copse will be a name without a meaning, which is the worst kind of poverty, for it cannot be mended by economy.”

Mr. Ashby was not insensible to the force of this; he was only insensible to the possibility of acting upon it. He looked down the table at his son, who had turned a page of the newspaper with a deliberation that argued a total absence of interest in its contents.

“Edmund,” said he, “you are concerned in this.”

“I am concerned in it,” said Edmund, “and I am sorry for it. But I hope, sir, you will not cut the oak on my account. I could wish the debt were paid, but I could wish it paid some other way.”

“There is no other way,” said his father, “unless your mother has one in reserve.”

Lady Ashby laid down her work. She had, in fact, a way in reserve, and had had it in reserve for a fortnight, and had been waiting for precisely this moment to produce it, which she did with the composure of a woman who has rehearsed her composure.

“There is Miss Winterbourne,” said she.

Edmund folded his newspaper with great care, as though it were a document of state.

“Miss Winterbourne,” he said, “is at Bath.”

“Miss Winterbourne,” replied his mother, “is to be at her uncle’s at Stamford in a fortnight, and her uncle’s house is nine miles from this door. She has thirty thousand pounds, and her father’s name is not unknown in the city. She is not ill-looking; she is not ill-natured; and she has, I am told, a very pretty way of speaking her mind, which I should think you would admire, since you have always admired it in yourself.”

“I have never spoken to her above six times,” said Edmund.

“Six times is enough to begin with,” said Lady Ashby. “It is not as though you were asked to love her at sight, which is a thing that happens only in novels, and there only to people who have nothing else to do. You are asked to be civil to a young woman of fortune, and to consider, while you are being civil, whether you could bear to be civil to her for forty years. I do not think it an unreasonable request.”

Edmund rose and went to the window, and looked out upon the park with the expression of a man who finds the view less consoling than he had hoped. The park was very handsome; it had been handsomer before the present incumbent, but it was still a park to be proud of, and it was, in a manner of speaking, the whole of the argument. He was the only son. He had been bred to expect it. He had also been bred, though his father had not intended it and his mother had not perceived it, to expect that the expecting would be easy.

“I have no objection to Miss Winterbourne,” he said at last. “I have no objection to any one. But I cannot marry a woman because there is an oak in Rook’s Copse.”

“You cannot marry her because there is an oak in Rook’s Copse?” said his mother. “I do not follow you.”
