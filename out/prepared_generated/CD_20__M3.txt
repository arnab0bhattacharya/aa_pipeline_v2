If any young man of an ardent temperament and a head completely full of unborn epics had ever taken it into his mind to wait for inspiration in a peculiarly damp and gloomy slice of the Borough, that young man was Mr. Felix Pimplebrook. He had engaged his apartment—comprising a sleeping-closet that might have been designed for a particularly melancholy tomcat, and a sitting-room that commanded a majestic prospect of a brick wall and a network of soot-encrusted chimneypots—from a Mrs. Muggridge, a widow of such uncompromising sternness of aspect that the very door-knocker seemed to cower beneath her shadow. It was a lodging-house that had a damp smell of its own, a smell as of ancient cabbage and unfulfilled promises, and the wallpaper hung in pendulous strips, swaying to and fro in the draughts like the tattered banners of a defeated army.

Felix was convinced, with a conviction that no quantity of cold mutton or lack of coals could extinguish, that he was destined to be a Great Poet. He was merely waiting for Inspiration to strike. He sat himself down every morning in his one rickety chair, with a sheet of foolscap before him and a pen that spluttered ink like a miniature geyser, and waited. He waited with his eyes fixed upon the ceiling, where a brown patch of dampness spread itself out in a shape remarkably like a map of England, and he waited upon the arrival of a majestic thought. But the majestic thought, as if detained by some invisible toll-gate upon the road, tarried most exasperatingly, and the foolscap remained blank.

His landlady, Mrs. Muggridge, was a woman who kept her keys and grudges in equal abundance. She wore a bombazine gown that creaked with every movement, as if it were audibly protesting against the meagreness of her lodger’s diet, and she had a habit of looking at him over the tops of her spectacles with an expression that mingled maternal despair with the unyielding severity of a metropolitan magistrate.

‘It’s a wery fine morning, Mr. Pimplebrook,’ Mrs. Muggridge would say, casting her eyes upon the empty page. ‘A wery fine morning for a young man to be a-scribbling, I dare say.’

‘A morning designed by the heavens themselves for the gestation of a sonnet, Mrs. Muggridge,’ Felix would reply, looking ethereal, and clasping his ink-stained fingers together. ‘The Muse is upon me; I feel her gentle breath upon my brow.’

‘Then it’s to be hoped, sir,’ retorted Mrs. Muggridge, ‘that the Muse will leave a trifle of silver behind her, for the baker’s boy has been here twice this morning, and his patience, unlike your poetry, is of a very limited description. I tell you, Mr. Pimplebrook, I can’t keep the house going upon breath and iambics. I’m a-living upon my own fat, I am, and it’s getting wery thin company.’

‘Patience, my dear Mrs. Muggridge!’ cried Felix, waving his hand with an air of grandiloquent tolerance. ‘When the world reads what I am about to write, the name of Pimplebrook will be upon every tongue! You shall ride in your carriage yet!’

‘I don’t want no carriage, sir,’ returned Mrs. Muggridge, with a sniff that agitated the very foundations of her cap. ‘I want three weeks’ rent. And if you was to write a poem about it, it wouldn’t be no more nourishing than a dish of cold water. You’re a-wasting your substance, sir, and my patience, and the baker’s patience, and everybody’s patience but your own!’

But Felix, sighing deeply, would turn his gaze back to the map of England upon his ceiling, and Mrs. Muggridge, with a final creak of her bombazine, would march away to her own domains, murmuring that she had never known such a thing in all her born days.

If Mrs. Muggridge viewed his talents through the spectacles of arithmetic, his other neighbours were equally unappreciative. There was, across the street, a small and wonderfully bright shop, the very essence of snugness, kept by a Miss Prudence Tattle. She sold gloves; kid gloves, and woollen gloves, and mittens of the most approved construction; and she was, in her way, as great a philosopher as Felix fancied himself. She had a bright, sensible face, a pair of honest grey eyes, and a manner of threading her needle with a quiet decision that seemed to say, ‘I know what I am about, and I wish I could say the same for you.’ She had somehow acquired the habit of nodding to Felix through the window, and Felix, in his grandest moods, would sometimes cross the street to converse with her, as a matter of condescension.

‘And how does the poem go to-day, Mr. Pimplebrook?’ Prudence would ask, with a little smile that played about the corners of her mouth in a most provoking manner.

‘It goes but slowly, Miss Tattle,’ Felix would reply, with a melancholy sigh. ‘The world is so terribly prosaic. The clatter of the carts, the cries of the street-sellers, the very vulgar aspect of the cobblestones—they jar upon the finer chords of my spirit.’

‘Dear me!’ said Prudence, smoothing out a pair of gloves. ‘I should have thought the carts and the people would be just the things to make poetry of. I’m sure there’s noise enough and life enough in the Borough to furnish out a dozen poems, if one only knew how to set it down. But I suppose it must be very different when one has a genius.’

‘Infinitely different, Miss Tattle,’ returned Felix, with a compassionate smile. ‘The true poet does not vulgarize his art by descending to the commonplaces of the street. He waits for the afflatus.’

‘The what?’ inquired Prudence.

‘The afflatus—the divine breathing, the celestial inspiration,’ explained Felix, folding his arms.
