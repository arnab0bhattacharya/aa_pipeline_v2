The ink was still wet upon the page when I heard the floorboards creak above me, a slow, deliberate tremor that traversed the ceiling like a spider testing the limits of its web. I did not look up. I had learned, in these six months at Thornfield Hall—or rather, in the shadow of it, in the quiet, damp house on the edge of the moors where we dwelt, to read the architecture of sound as fluently as one reads scripture. It was Mrs. Ellery. She was not sleeping. She was pacing.

My name is Eliza Vane, and I am the governess to little Arthur, a boy of eight who possesses the eyes of a hawk and the silence of a stone. His mother, Mrs. Ellery, is a woman of delicate constitution and brittle nerves, a creature who has been polished by sorrow into a smooth, cold pearl. She had engaged me not merely to instruct Arthur in French and geography, but to record his progress in a daily journal. “A testament to his growth,” she had said, her voice thin as wire, “so that I may look back upon these years and see that I have not failed him.”

But I had failed to see, at first, that I was writing for two readers.

It began with a missing entry. One evening, I found the leather-bound book open on the desk, the pages ruffled as if a gust of wind had passed through the room, though the window was barred shut against the November sleet. The entry for the afternoon was gone, torn out with such violence that the ragged edges of the paper still clung to the binding. I felt a cold prickle run down my spine, a sensation akin to the chill of a graveyard in early morning. I had written of Arthur’s anger that day, how he had struck the dog, how his face had flushed with a rage that was not his own, how he had looked at me with a suspicion that chilled my blood. I had written of the house itself, of the heavy curtains that hung like funeral veils, and of the sound of Mr. Ellery’s carriage wheels on the gravel, turning away at dusk, leaving us in a silence that felt less like peace and more like a held breath.

The next night, I wrote nothing of the carriage. I wrote only of the arithmetic lesson, of the multiplication tables, of the dry, mechanical click of the slate. I watched Mrs. Ellery from the corner of my eye as she sat by the fire, her face buried in a shawl, her fingers twisting the clasp of her bodice until the metal bit into her skin. She was waiting. She was watching.

I realized then that the diary was not a record for the boy, but a map for the mother. She was hunting for clues, for the faintest indication of her husband’s movements, his moods, his absences. Mr. Ellery was a man of few words and many shadows, a merchant whose wealth was built on the labor of others and whose home was a fortress of secrets. He came and went like a storm, bringing with him a pressure in the air that made it difficult to breathe. Mrs. Ellery did not know where he went. She did not know who he met. And so, she turned to me, the outsider, the observer, the one who was bound to stay, to see, to record.

I must confess that a strange, fierce power entered into me. I was no longer the meek, subservient tutor who bowed her head and kept her counsel. I was the keeper of the key. I could give her what she wanted, or I could withhold it. I could lie, or I could tell the truth. And in that choice, I found a dignity I had never possessed before.

I began to write for two.

For Arthur, I wrote the simple truth: the lessons, the games, the small joys of childhood. *He learned the names of the stars today. He built a bridge of cards that stood for an hour. He smiled, for the first time in weeks, at the sight of a robin on the windowsill.* These were facts, bright and clean, meant to soothe the child’s soul and to offer the mother a vision of normalcy.

But for Mrs. Ellery, I wrote in a different hand, a hidden code of omissions and emphases. I would describe the weather with a ferocity that mirrored the tension in the house. *The wind howled like a banshee tonight, rattling the panes until I feared they would shatter. The fire burned low, and the shadows in the corner seemed to lengthen and reach toward me. I heard the door open at midnight, and close again. I did not see who entered, but the scent of tobacco lingered in the air, thick and acrid.*

I watched her reaction to these entries. She would read them by candlelight, her face pale and drawn, her lips moving silently as if in prayer. I saw the fear in her eyes, but also a grim, determined hope. She was looking for proof. Proof that he was unfaithful, or that he was dangerous, or that he was simply gone, forever. I did not give her what she wanted, not directly. I gave her the atmosphere, the mood, the sense of a house on the edge of a cliff, teetering in a gale.

One night, the storm was particularly vicious. Rain lashed the windows, and the thunder shook the foundations of the house. I wrote of Arthur’s fear, how he had clung to my arm, how he had whispered that he thought the house was sinking into the earth. I wrote of Mrs. Ellery’s silence, how she had stood by the window, watching the dark, her face a mask of stone. I wrote of the empty chair by the fire, reserved for a master who did not come.
