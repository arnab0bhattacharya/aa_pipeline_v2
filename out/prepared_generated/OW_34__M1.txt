There is no more tiresome inheritance than a handsome face. From his earliest years, Everard Vane had been told that he was his father’s portrait, a statement which he accepted with the grave pleasure of a boy who is given a good horse, and which he had long since ceased to examine. His father, the late Sir Gideon Vane, had been a baronet of impeccable lineage and appalling dullness, a man whose opinions were as well-polished and as empty as his silver salvers. To be told he resembled such a monument was, Everard felt, a compliment of the most dubious sort.

His mother, the Dowager Lady Vane, a woman whose beauty was a legend and whose conversation was a weapon, was the chief purveyor of this likeness. “You have his very eyes, Everard,” she would say, her own gaze, the colour of a winter sky, resting upon him with a strange, analytical calm. “And that Vane mouth. It is a great comfort to me to see him in you.”

The comfort, Everard suspected, was of a purely aesthetic nature. Lady Vane had never been a woman to be comforted by mere men; she required, rather, to be entertained by them. And Sir Gideon, by all accounts, had been anything but entertaining.

The illusion held until Everard’s twenty-third year, when a cousin of his father’s, a dry little solicitor named Mr. Penrose, came up to London for the season. Over a dinner of roast duck at the Reform Club, the solicitor, fortified by a third glass of port, had looked at Everard and shaken his head. “It’s a strange thing, this heredity,” he mused. “You’ve the Vane chin, I grant you. But those eyes… they are not Gideon’s. Gideon’s were the colour of weak tea and just as lacking in substance. Yours… yours are a different matter altogether.”

Everard felt a chill that had nothing to do with the club’s draughty corridors. “I have always been told I am my father’s son.”

“Oh, you are, you are,” said Mr. Penrose hastily, seeing the look on the young man’s face. “Legally, of course. But the eye of the painter is a fickle thing. Nature has her little jests.”

It was not a jest, Everard felt, but a secret. And he determined to discover it.

The first confrontation was a masterpiece of maternal evasion. They were in the drawing-room of their house in Belgrave Square, a room of pale blue silk and exquisite, uncomfortable chairs. Lady Vane was arranging a spray of white orchids in a crystal vase.

“Mother,” Everard began, “Mr. Penrose said something curious. He said my eyes are not my father’s.”

Lady Vane did not turn. Her fingers, long and pale as the orchid petals, adjusted a single stem. “Mr. Penrose is a solicitor, Everard, not a connoisseur. He deals in facts, not in the subtleties of resemblance. The mind is a crude instrument; it seizes upon a single feature and ignores the whole. You are a Vane. That is the only fact of any importance.”

“But what colour were his eyes, exactly?”

“An insipid grey,” she replied, her voice perfectly level. “Now, do you think this arrangement is too stark? Perhaps a touch of asparagus fern.”

The subject was closed. But Everard, who had inherited more than his father’s chin, now found himself possessed of a new and un-Vane-like tenacity. His search led him, as such searches must, through the hothouse of London society. He began to study the faces in drawing-rooms and at the opera, not for their beauty or their titles, but for a pair of eyes that matched his own in the looking-glass.

It was a fruitless and rather morbid pastime. He saw eyes of every shade—of sapphire and slate, of brown and green—but none that held the particular, arresting quality of his own, which were a deep, stormy grey, flecked with chips of silver. He felt like a collector seeking a single, lost jewel in a mountain of paste.

His quest became a topic of quiet amusement. “Looking for a family resemblance, Vane?” drawled Lord Highbury, a man whose face was a testament to a century of careful breeding and whose conversation a testament to its limitations. “I’m afraid you’ll find we’re all frightfully alike. It’s the result of marrying our cousins, you know. Keeps the bloodlines pure and the conversation dull.”

It was Lady Anstruther, a widow with a reputation for knowing everything and forgiving nothing, who provided the first real clue. She cornered him at a ball, her fan fluttering like a nervous moth. “My dear boy,” she whispered, her breath smelling of sherry and secrets, “you are hunting in the wrong forest. You look at the oaks, but you should be looking at the lightning that struck them.”

“I don’t understand, Lady Anstruther.”

“Your mother,” she said, her eyes glittering, “was the most beautiful woman in London. And where there is great beauty, there are always great artists. Think of the painters, Everard. Not the fashionable portraitists who flatter the rich, but the ones who capture the soul.”

The name came to him a week later, spoken in hushed tones at a private view at the Grosvenor Gallery. It was a name he had never heard: Julian Corvo. A painter of some brilliance who had taken London by storm in the seventies and then vanished just as suddenly. His work was known for its dramatic chiaroscuro and its profound psychological penetration. There were whispers of a scandal, of a great lady, of a portrait that had been destroyed.

Everard found a single, small oil sketch by Corvo in a dusty shop in St. James’s. It was not a portrait, but a study of a man’s hands, resting on a velvet-bound book. The hands were long, sensitive, and powerful. They were, he saw with a jolt, his own hands.
