The train from Rouen had deposited them at the Gare Saint-Lazare in a cloud of steam that smelled of coal and wet wool, and for a moment Elise felt the sheer weight of the city press against her, all noise and hurry and the clang of tram bells. Her brother, Théodore, had taken her arm with the absent habit of a man steering a cart horse, and she had let herself be steered, her new hat—a modest confection of grey felt with a single pheasant feather—held fast against the wind.

The congress was held in a long, echoing hall near the Palais-Royal, and the first evening there was a dinner at which the seating had been arranged by a harassed secretary with a list of names and no knowledge of the people behind them. Elise found herself placed between a physician from Lyon named Dr. Bertillon, who had a red face and a great deal to say about the hygiene of abattoirs, and a quiet man from Bordeaux who ate his soup without looking up. Théodore was somewhere at the far end of the table, already deep in conversation with a specialist in diseases of the lung.

“And your husband,” Dr. Bertillon said, turning to her with the hearty assumption of a man who has decided to be pleasant, “he is in general practice, you said?”

“My brother,” Elise corrected. “I keep his house. In Vireloup.”

“Ah, a sister.” He said it as though sisters were a category of furniture, and then, because he was not unkind, merely thoughtless, he added, “And you find the life agreeable? The country is very quiet, I imagine.”

She might have said what she always said at home, which was that the country was quiet and that she was busy enough with the house and the garden and the preserving and the parish. But here, in this room of white linen and gilt mirrors, with the murmur of a hundred conversations around her, the old answer seemed to shrink. “It is quiet,” she said. “But there is a great deal of illness in quiet places. Théodore is out at all hours. Last winter there was an outbreak of diphtheria in the hamlet of Sainte-Croix, and I helped him with the children. We turned the schoolroom into a ward.”

Dr. Bertillon’s eyebrows went up. “You nursed them yourself?”

“I kept the records and the accounts of the antitoxin, and I sat with the worst cases at night when he could not. There was a boy, the son of a farmer, who would not swallow. I found that if I sang to him he would open his mouth.” She had never told anyone that. It had seemed too small a thing, too domestic, to mention. But Bertillon leaned forward, his soup forgotten.

“And he recovered?”

“He is back at school. He brought me a hen in the spring.”

The doctor laughed, a great rolling sound, and said she must tell him more, and she did, and by the time the dessert came she had described the whole of the winter, the cold in the schoolroom, the way the frost made patterns on the windows, the fear in the mothers’ faces, and the strange, fierce happiness of the morning when the last child’s fever broke. Bertillon listened as though she were reading from a learned journal. He asked questions. He wrote something on the back of his menu card.

At the lectures the next day, she sat at the back with the other wives and sisters, but during the interval a woman in a severe black dress approached her. She was Madame Rouvier, the wife of a Paris surgeon, and she had heard from Bertillon—who had heard it from someone else—that the sister of Dr. Lemaire had managed a diphtheria ward.

“I am organizing a committee,” Madame Rouvier said, “for the instruction of mothers in the poorer quarters. We have doctors who will lecture, but we need someone who has actually done the work, who can tell them what it is like in a small room with a sick child. Would you speak to them?”

Elise looked across the room at Théodore, who was gesturing with a fork at a diagram of a lung. He had never asked her to speak. He assumed, as she had assumed, that her place was behind him, holding the lamp.

“I have never spoken in public,” she said.

“You are speaking to me now,” Madame Rouvier said.

That evening there was another dinner, and this time she was placed beside a man from the Ministry of Health, a thin, precise person named Delacourt, who had the weary air of one who has heard every possible argument. He asked her, in the way of polite conversation, whether she enjoyed Paris. She said that she had not seen much of it, but that she had noticed the price of milk was higher than at home. He blinked.

“And what do you conclude from that?”

“That the children of the eighth arrondissement are probably drinking less of it,” she said. “Théodore—my brother—says that in his district the farmers’ wives water it down when they can, but at least there is a cow. Here there is no cow. Only the shop, and the shop is dear.”

Delacourt put down his glass. “You have thought about this.”

“I have thought about it because I do the accounts,” she said. “You cannot buy antitoxin and milk both if the milk is dear. Something must give.”

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he did something no one at home had ever done: he took out a small notebook and wrote down what she had said. “I should like to hear more,” he said. “There is a commission next month. Would your brother permit you to come and give evidence?”

She felt the floor tilt, very slightly, as though the train were still moving under her. “I am not a doctor,” she said.
