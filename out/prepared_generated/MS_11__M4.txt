The train from the west drew into the capital at dusk, and I alighted into a city that seemed to hold its breath. Vienna, which in the happier years of this century had been a very emblem of concord—its palaces and gardens thronged with the citizens of every nation, its very air, one might fancy, softened by the mingled tongues of a peaceful world—now wore the aspect of a widow who conceals her grief beneath a decent composure. The chestnut-trees along the Ring were already yellowing before their season; the fountains played, but the promenades were thin, and upon the faces of the few who lingered I read that anxious vigilance which betrays a mind divided between present duty and a dread of what must follow. A dry summer, a harvest blighted in the ear, granaries emptied by the improvidence of years that had believed abundance everlasting—these were the tidings that had drawn us hither; and I, Edmund Ashworth, envoy of England, felt the weight of my commission settle upon my spirit like a pall, as the carriage bore me through the silent streets toward the Hofburg.

I had been chosen, I believe, because I was thought to possess a temper not easily inflamed and a tongue not easily silenced; yet in truth I came to that congress with a heart divided against itself, for the instructions I bore were not the instructions of my soul. My countrymen—I say it with sorrow—my countrymen, secure in their island granaries and in the memory of old supremacy, had risen in a fierce clamour of possession. “Let the Continent shift for itself,” was the cry in the streets of London; “our stores are ours; the stranger starve, so that the Englishman be fed.” I had heard that cry echo in the very halls of council, and I had seen the minister who uttered it applauded as a patriot. To such a people I was to speak of sharing; from such a people I was to carry to the assembled nations a promise of succour. It was a bitter embassy, and I entered upon it as a man enters a field of battle, not knowing whether he shall fall by the enemy’s hand or by the treachery of his own.

The congress sat in a great chamber whose gilded ceiling had looked down upon the treaties of a hundred years. There were gathered the delegates of France and of Germany, of Italy and of the northern crowns, of Russia and of the reawakened East; and I thought, as I surveyed that assembly, that never had the world’s peace seemed at once so precious and so frail. The Frenchman, Delacroix, spoke first, and spoke with the ardour of his nation, painting the misery of his southern provinces in colours that made the hearers blench; the German, Von Halden, cold and methodical, set forth the figures of his need with a precision more terrible than eloquence. Then all eyes turned upon me. I rose, and for a moment the words died upon my lips, for I felt upon me the gaze of a hundred strangers who asked of England the bread of life, and I knew that the answer I must give would be weighed against the hunger of millions.

“Gentlemen,” I began, “I will not deceive you with fair speeches. The harvest of my country also has been scant; there are those at home who say we have not enough for ourselves, and who bid me refuse you. But I say to you, and I would say it to my own people were they here to hear me, that no nation liveth to itself alone. If famine ravage the Continent and England sit by her full barns, the plague of want will cross the narrow seas, and the misery we refused to pity will knock at last at our own doors. Let us, then, deal with one another as brothers, not as traders chaffering in a market; let each contribute according to his store, and let no man’s pride, and no man’s fear, stand between the living and the dead.”

A murmur ran through the chamber—of hope in some faces, of doubt in others, of anger in a few; and I saw the Russian, Orloff, knit his brow, and the Spaniard, Alvarez, whisper behind his hand. But it was not until the morrow, when the terms of division came to be debated, that the storm which I had dreaded burst upon me. A courier from London had arrived in the night, bringing me letters: one from the minister, cold and guarded, counselling me to promise little; and one from my own county, from the woman who had nursed me at her breast, who wrote, in a hand trembling with age and fear, of the empty cradles and the silent mills, and besought me, by the memory of her son, not to give away the bread of English children. I read that letter by the grey light of dawn, and I confess that my resolution shook; for it is one thing to speak of brotherhood in a gilded chamber, and another to look into the eyes of one who has suckled you, and to tell her that her grandchildren must go hungry that strangers may be fed.

Yet when I entered the council I had conquered myself, and I pleaded with an earnestness that I had not known I possessed. I painted the desolation of the villages I had seen in my journey—the silent hearths, the fields untilled, the children with the wan faces of old age; I appealed to the common faith of nations, to the memory of the peace that had blessed the century, to the awful alternative of war and rapine which must follow upon despair.
