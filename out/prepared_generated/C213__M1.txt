The rain fell in a steady, silver sheet against the windows of Mrs. Eleanor Vance’s small parlour, blurring the view of the neat Hertfordshire high street. It was the sort of day that made a small jointure feel even smaller, the damp seeping into the old floorboards and the chill into the bones. Eleanor was mending one of her youngest daughter’s stockings, her needle flying with a practised, weary rhythm. The fire in the hearth was a miserly thing, and she had just decided to sacrifice the last of the coal from the scuttle when a carriage, far grander than any that usually stopped at their gate, drew to a halt outside.

Her three daughters looked up from their books and embroidery in unison.

“Oh, Mama,” breathed Clara, the eldest, her sensible face alight with a flicker of hope. “Is that Mr. Croft?”

“Hush, Clara,” said Jane, the middle sister, her eyes sharp and curious. “It is far too grand for him. Perhaps it is Mrs. Gable from the Hall, come to pay a call.”

But Eleanor knew the carriage. It was a deep, lacquered green, with a crest of interlocking ships and anchors that the local wags had already dubbed ‘The Merchant’s Mark.’ Only one man in Oakbridge would arrive in such a vehicle. A moment later, a footman in plain livery held an umbrella over a figure who, despite the inclement weather, seemed to move with an untroubled energy. Mr. Silas Croft.

He was not a handsome man in the way of the portraits in the parish church. His face was too broad, his nose too strong, and there were laugh lines etched deep around his eyes. He was a man of forty-five who had earned his fortune not in the drawing rooms of London, but in the salt-stung air of the Bristol docks, bartering for spices and silks. He was, to the neighbourhood of Oakbridge, a fascinating vulgarity.

He entered the parlour shedding drops of rain and apologies. “Mrs. Vance, my apologies for the intrusion on such a day. I was in town on business and could not pass by without seeing how you and your charming daughters fare.”

He bowed to the girls, who curtseyed, and then his gaze settled on Eleanor. His eyes, the colour of warm sherry, were not appraising her as a commodity, as her late husband’s solicitor had, nor were they judging her reduced circumstances, as her neighbours did. They were simply… pleased to see her.

“Mr. Croft, you are most welcome,” she said, setting aside her mending. “Though I fear we have little to offer but a weak fire and weaker tea.”

“Nonsense,” he boomed, clapping his hands together. “A warm welcome is worth more than a hundred luxuries. And as for the fire,” he said, striding over to the hearth and giving the grate a vigorous poke, “it merely needs encouragement. Like a shy debutante.”

He coaxed a flame from the embers with a surprising deftness. As he did so, a large, damp, and rather smelly object tumbled from his greatcoat pocket onto the rug. It was a lobster, its claws bound with twine, its black eyes staring blankly at the ceiling.

The girls gasped. Eleanor stared. Mr. Croft simply laughed, a rich, booming sound that seemed to shake the very dust from the rafters. “Good heavens, so that is where he got to! I had him sent from Billingsgate. I thought to surprise you with a lobster supper. It appears he wished to see the parlour first.”

He scooped up the creature, holding it at arm’s length. “A rather forward fellow, is he not? A great deal of bluster and a hard shell, but with a tender heart… or at least, a tender tail, if one knows how to cook him.”

And then, for the first time since her husband’s coffin had been lowered into the cold earth two years ago, Eleanor Vance laughed. It was not a polite titter or a sigh of amusement. It was a real, unburdened laugh that started in her belly and erupted from her lips. She saw the absurdity of it all: the wealthy merchant, the bound crustacean, the three wide-eyed girls, the meagre fire now roaring to life. She laughed until her eyes watered, and Mr. Croft laughed with her, his sherry-coloured eyes crinkling at the corners.

The girls, after a moment of stunned silence, joined in, until the small, damp parlour was filled with a warmth that had nothing to do with the coal.

Later, after he had taken his leave, promising to send his cook to deal with the lobster, the conversation began.

“He is a good man, Mama,” said Clara, her tone practical. “He has a fine house and a carriage. He can provide for us. It is the sensible course.”

“Sensible?” Jane scoffed, though her eyes were still bright. “The whole town would talk. ‘Widow Vance, who married for a pittance, now married to a man who smells of the sea.’ They would say you have debased yourself.”

Letitia, the youngest, looked up from the now-forgotten stocking. “But he made you laugh, Mama.”

The words hung in the air, simple and profound. Clara and Jane looked at their mother, their arguments suddenly feeling thin and colourless.

The following Sunday at church, Eleanor felt the weight of Oakbridge’s judgment like a physical pressure. Mrs. Gable, a woman whose family had owned the same patch of land for three generations, gave her a tight-lipped nod that was more of a warning than a greeting. Mrs. Albright, who had once been a dear friend, seemed to find the hymn book endlessly fascinating and could not meet her eye. They saw Silas Croft and thought of ledgers and cargo holds and the grubby business of making money. They saw Eleanor and thought of her late husband, a man of good family but poor fortune, and saw her as a relic, meant to be preserved in genteel poverty.
