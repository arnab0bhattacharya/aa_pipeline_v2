The lecture had ended, and the great hall of the Künstlerverein was emptying into the gaslit street. I lingered in the vestibule, drawing on my gloves, feeling the chill of the marble floor even through the soles of my boots. The orator, a man of fiery eloquence and liberal convictions, had spoken of the rights of man and the tyranny of kings with a passion that had set my own blood moving faster than its wont. For two hours I had sat rapt, a solitary Englishwoman in a foreign crowd, my soul uplifted by the grandeur of the ideas and the music of the German tongue. Now, as the crowd thinned and the heat of the room dissipated, a cold reality descended. The streets of Altenburg were dark, and the wind, which had been only a murmur against the high windows, now howled in earnest down the narrow lanes.

It was as I stepped through the tall doorway that I saw him. He stood beneath the flaring lamp, his face turned towards me, and for a moment my heart seemed to stop its beating. Eight years. It was eight years since I had last stood before that face, and the interval had wrought a change that was at once startling and complete. The slender, earnest student was gone. In his place stood a man of substance and consequence, his fine wool coat buttoned against the cold, his hat of the latest fashion, a ring glinting on his finger. His features had broadened and settled into an expression of placid success. He was no longer looking at me with the hungry, hopeful gaze of a suitor, but with the calm, slightly surprised recognition of an acquaintance.

“Miss Vance?”

His voice, too, was changed. The old tremor of emotion was smoothed away, replaced by a resonant, assured tone. It was the voice of a man who was accustomed to being listened to.

“Mr. Croft,” I replied, and was vexed to hear my own voice sound thin and strained. I had resolved, in the long years since our parting, that if ever we should meet again, I would be calm, self-possessed, indifferent. I would show him that the refusal which had cost me a struggle had cost me no lasting regret. But now, under the sudden assault of memory and the cold night air, I felt my resolution waver. “I had not expected to find you in Altenburg.”

“Nor I you,” he said, his eyes scanning my face, missing nothing. He noted, I was sure, the plainness of my bonnet, the slight wear of my cloak. I was a governess, an English teacher abroad; my livelihood was written in the modesty of my attire. “I am here on business. My wife is with me. We are lodged at the Hôtel du Nord.”

The words fell between us like stones into a still pool. His wife. Of course. A man of his new-found wealth and position would not be long without a wife. I had pictured him, in my bitterest moments, as lonely, perhaps even as pining for the thing he had lost. This vision of a prosperous, married man was infinitely worse.

“I trust Mrs. Croft is well,” I said, the words feeling like ashes in my mouth.

“Quite well, thank you. She found the lecture a little… abstract. She is more at home with the opera.” He smiled, a placid, proprietorial smile. “And you, Miss Vance? You are still at your teaching?”

“I am.”

“It is a noble calling,” he said, and his tone was so perfectly, so terribly condescending that I felt a hot flush of anger rise in my throat. He meant to be kind, I knew. He was offering me the small coin of his approval, the charity of a rich man to a poor one. But his kindness was a deeper insult than any scorn.

“It is a necessary one,” I retorted, my voice gaining a sharpness I could not control. “Not all of us have the luxury of choosing our paths, or of finding them paved with gold.”

A flicker of something—annoyance? hurt?—crossed his face before the mask of calm success settled again. “I have been fortunate,” he admitted. “My ventures in the new railways have prospered beyond my hopes. But it has not been without toil, I assure you.”

We stood in silence for a moment, the wind whipping a stray lock of hair across my cheek. The cold was seeping into my bones. I could see the warm glow of the Hôtel du Nord at the end of the street. He was thinking of his wife, of the fire and the comfort awaiting him. I was thinking of my small, cold room, of the stack of exercise books I must correct before morning.

“You have not changed greatly,” he said suddenly, his gaze intense. “You have the same fire in your eyes. I remember it well. It was what I… what I so admired.”

The past tense hung in the air, a ghost of the passion that had once been. He admired it then. What did he admire now? The placid beauty of his wife, the perfect order of his home?

“And you have changed a great deal, Mr. Croft,” I said, my voice low and fierce. “You have become what you wished to be, I see. A man of weight and substance. I suppose I should offer you my congratulations.”

“Do not mock me, Eleanor,” he said, and for a second the old student, the passionate, wounded boy, looked out from the successful man’s eyes. The use of my Christian name was a shock, a violation of the barrier I had so carefully constructed. “It was a hard choice, what you did. For both of us.”
