It was in the narrow, cavernous throat of Frith Street, on a Tuesday evening singularly damp and dark even for the season of November, that the apparition stepped forth to intercept my sluggish progress. The town was enveloped in a yellow, sluggish fog, a pestilent exhalation that seemed to exude from the very stones of the pavement, muffling the clangour of distant cabs and rendering the shapes of passers-by phantom-like and indistinct. I had been walking with my head bent, my umbrella a frail shield against the settling moisture, carrying with me, perhaps, the heavy and inert melancholy of a man whose life has been spent in the secret service of the law, when a hand, heavy and deliberate, was placed upon my shoulder. I stopped. The touch had no uncertain, hesitating quality; it was the grip of a profound and terrifying certitude.

He stood blocking the path, a bulky shadow that detached itself from the general murk, and as the flare of a neighboring gin-palace threw a sudden, smoky shaft of light across his features, I recognized him with a shock that struck at the very roots of my being. It was Henrick. The years had dealt with him violently, yet had failed to destroy the massive, elemental stubbornness of the man. He had been transported across the seas nearly fifteen years ago, upon the evidence which I, in my youthful and misguided zeal for an abstract justice, had gathered and laid before the magistrates—a tissue of truths woven with a cunning that had cost me my peace of mind forever. I had believed him buried in the living death of a penal settlement, swallowed by the immense, sun-scorched wilderness of the antipodes, from which men of his stamp were reputed never to return. Yet here he was, erect and solid, his great, fair beard streaked with grey, his eyes gleaming with an extraordinary, searching lucidity out of a face tanned to a deep, brick-like hue by foreign suns.

He did not seize me by the throat, nor did he utter the imprecations which, in my secret and most terrified meditations, I had always imagined would precede my end at his hands. On the contrary, his heavy features relaxed into a smile of such expansive, such bewildering warmth, that I was struck dumb, possessed by a sudden, irrational conviction that the man’s mind had been unseated by the cruelty of his long exile.

"Mr. Halloway," he said, in a voice that was deep, measured, and vibrating with a strange, resonant gentleness. "It is surely you. I knew you at a hundred paces, though the London fog is thick enough to hide a multitude of sins."

I muttered some inarticulate sound of recognition, my tongue cleaving to the roof of my mouth. He laughed, a low, pleasant sound that seemed to belong to a different man, to a different world. "You stand amazed," he continued, falling into step beside me as if our last parting had been but a casual nod across a crowded thoroughfare. "You did not expect to see me walk the earth again. No. I do not blame you. The world is wide, and the law is long. But I have returned. I have returned, Mr. Halloway, and I find myself desiring nothing more than a quiet meal and a little conversation with an old acquaintance."

The irony of the word "acquaintance" was lost in the sheer, overwhelming banality of his manner. He directed our steps toward a quiet eating-house in a bystreet of Soho, a place of low ceilings and dark, polished wood, where the air hung thick with the odors of boiled beef, stale tobacco, and damp wool. We entered, and he selected a table in a secluded corner, motioning me to a seat with a courtly, almost paternal gesture. I obeyed him as if animated by a will that was no longer my own, my limbs heavy, my heart beating with a muffled, suffocating violence against my ribs.

He ordered the dinner with a meticulous care, consulting the waiter with grave formality, and when the man had departed, he leaned his elbows upon the table and regarded me fixedly. The silence between us was profound, fraught with the ghosts of a past that neither of us seemed willing to resurrect. The gas-jet above our head flickered, casting unsteady shadows upon his face, and in the shifting light I seemed to perceive the immense, brooding patience of a man who had conquered something more terrible than physical suffering.

"You are retired from your official duties, I perceive," he said at last, breaking the silence with a tone of mild, impersonal inquiry. "The years have dealt gently with you, Mr. Halloway. You have the appearance of a man who has found his rest."

I found my voice at last, a cracked and uncertain instrument. "I am retired," I admitted. "I live upon a small competence. And you, Henrick? You have... returned?"

"I have returned," he repeated, slowly, as if tasting the words. "They gave me a ticket-of-leave at last. I worked in the quarries, Mr. Halloway. I broke stones under a sun that beat upon one’s brain like a hammer. For ten years I labored there, in a land of dust and silence, where the very sky seemed to be made of brass. And do you know what sustained me, what kept the breath of life in my body through all those years of toil and solitude?"

I could not answer. I sat staring at him, my hands clasped tightly in my lap, a prey to an agonizing suspense.

"It was the thought," he said, his voice sinking to a low, intense whisper, "that I should one day return to this city. That I should one day sit down to table with you, Mr. Halloway, and break bread with you in peace."
