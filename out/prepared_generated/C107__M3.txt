The north wind off the Morridge cut through the village of Thorsmoor like a chisel seeking flaws in poor-quality stone. Elias Bream stood in the lich-gate of St. Cuthbert’s, his hands cracked and dry beneath a layer of pale dust. He watched the last of the autumn leaves skitter across the churchyard, knowing that winter would soon halt his work on the south transept restoration. The church was his life’s labor, a slow accumulation of patience and geometry, but the commission currently resting on his trestle table inside had turned his blood to ice. He was being paid to immortalize a tyrant.

Elias walked back down the nave, his heavy boots echoing against the medieval flagstones. Tucked beneath the arch of the baptistery, shielded from the weather by a canvas awning, lay a slab of fine-grained white marble. It was a beautiful piece of stone, quarried from the depths of the hills, possessing a faint, translucent glow when the light caught it right. It deserved a noble inscription. Instead, it was destined to bear a lie.

The door of the church creaked open, admitting a gust of cold air and the slight, nervous figure of the Reverend Thomas Vance. The vicar brushed a scattering of rain from his dark woollen coat and approached the mason. In his hand, he carried a folded piece of heavy vellum.

"A bitter morning, Elias," the vicar said, his breath misting in the frigid air. "The frost has claimed the last of the vicarage roses."

"I have brought the brazier over, Reverend," Elias said, gesturing to a cast-iron pot emitting a dull, orange glow near the trestle. "The marble needs warming before I can strike it. Brittle stone shatters in the cold."

"A careful man, as always," Vance said, stepping closer to the slab. He unfolded the vellum and held it out to Elias. "I bring the final draft from Charnwood Hall. Lady Aldercroft has settled upon the exact wording."

Elias took the parchment but did not look at it immediately. His eyes rested on the marble. "Her ladyship is eager to see it finished."

"She is grieving, Elias, and anxious to see her husband honored. The squire was a pillar of this parish. The hall employs half the village."

"The hall employs half the village in squalor, Reverend," Elias said, his voice low and rough. He finally looked down at the elegant, sweeping ink on the parchment. *In Memoriam: Sir Geoffrey Aldercroft, A Benevolent Master, A Gentleman of Unfailing Charity, A Friend to the Poor.*

Elias felt the familiar, bitter taste of bile in the back of his throat. He handed the parchment back to the vicar. "I cannot carve this."

Vance’s shoulders slumped, a heavy sigh escaping his lips. "I feared this might be your disposition, Elias. But you must understand the position. Lady Aldercroft is financing the entire restoration of the transept. Without her patronage, the roof will fall in within a decade. We cannot afford to offend her."

"And what of the truth, Reverend? Can we afford to bury that beneath a ton of Italian marble?"

"The truth?" Vance asked, stepping closer to the brazier to warm his hands. "What truth do you speak of, Elias? The rumors of the village? The alehouse whispers?"

"They are not whispers," Elias said, turning his back on the vicar to face the cold stone. "I was a boy when the Aldercrofts enclosed the common land at Edale. I remember my father weeping in the kitchen. We lost our pasture, our right to gather firewood. When my father went to the hall to beg for a lease on the new plots, Sir Geoffrey had his steward set the dogs on him. My father’s leg was mangled. He never worked again."

Vance closed his eyes, rubbing the bridge of his nose. "That was a long time ago, Elias. The enclosures were a time of great upheaval for everyone."

"Three winters ago," Elias continued, his voice hardening like mortar setting in the frost, "young Thomas Gable stole a sack of grain from the hall’s store. He was starving, his mother was bedridden. Sir Geoffrey did not have him transported; he had him hung in chains on the gibbet at the crossroads. I carved the boy’s headstone from my own pocket. I know what Sir Geoffrey was. He was a cruel landlord, a hard man who bled this valley to line his own coffers. He was no friend to the poor."

"He was a man of his time, Elias," Vance said softly, a note of pleading in his voice. "He kept the peace. He paid his taxes to the crown."

"He paid his taxes with the sweat of men he worked to the bone," Elias snapped. "I will not spend a month of my life striking the words ‘benevolent’ and ‘charitable’ into stone for a man who would gladly have seen us starve. Let someone else take the chisel. Let someone else lie."

"There is no one else, Elias. You are the master mason of Thorsmoor. If you refuse, Lady Aldercroft will withdraw her funding, take her marble, and leave the church to the elements. And she will ruin you. She has the ear of the magistrate."

Elias stared at the blank face of the marble. It stared back, cold and demanding. He thought of his father’s shattered leg, of the weight of the mallet in his hand, of the way a chisel could shape history. To carve this inscription was to betray every broken man in the valley.

"Tell me, Elias," Vance said, stepping right up beside the mason, his voice dropping to a near whisper. "Does truth belong on gravestones?"

The question hung in the damp air between them, mingling with the smoke of the charcoal brazier. Elias looked at the vicar, seeing the profound exhaustion in the man’s eyes. Vance was not a wicked man; he was a shepherd trying to keep his flock out of the rain, even if it meant making a deal with the wolf.
