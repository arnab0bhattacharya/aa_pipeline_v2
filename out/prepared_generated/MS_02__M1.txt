It was in the third autumn of my sojourn at Edinburgh, when the mists lay heavy upon the Firth and the grey light of the north seemed to penetrate the very marrow of the soul, that the fever which had long tormented my thoughts began to fasten its tenacious claws upon my body. I had dedicated the whole of my ardent youth to the medical arts, not merely as a means of worldly subsistence, but as a sacred, high calling to wrest from the grasp of Death those victims whom he claimed with such arbitrary cruelty. The memory of my sister, Eleanor, whose bright eyes were dimmed and whose vital heat was extinguished by a wasting sickness that defied the diagnosis of the most learned physicians of our county, was the burning coal upon the altar of my ambition. I could not forgive the silence of the medical texts; I could not bear the ignorance that left me weeping beside her bed, a useless scholar while the most precious of lives ebbed away.

Thus, when a strange and terrible malady began to infect the crowded closes of the Old Town—a sickness that withered the flesh and clouded the mind—I saw in it the twin of the demon that had stolen Eleanor. I resolved, with a desperation that bordered on madness, to discover its cause. I believed, with the fervor of a zealot, that the origin of the pestilence lay within the humours of the blood itself, a corruption that could be isolated and conquered if only one were willing to look closely enough. But the living patient is a fortress of secrets, guarding its suffering with the impenetrable wall of the skin. To understand the enemy, I reasoned, one must invite it within one’s own citadel.

Therefore, I commenced a series of experiments upon my own person, a course of action which I hid from my colleagues with a cunning born of guilt. In the solitude of my chambers, amidst the smell of ether and the glint of glass retorts, I introduced the very fluids drawn from the fevered into my own veins. I sought to trace the path of the contagion, to observe its effects upon my own constitution, and thus to map the topography of the disease. I told myself that this was a noble sacrifice, a martyrdom for the sake of humanity; yet, as the weeks wore on, a creeping shadow fell upon my health. A pallor stole over my countenance, and a tremor, at first imperceptible, began to shake my hand—the hand that held the lancet.

It was at this juncture that Clara, my betrothed, whose presence was as the sun to my perennial winter, began to perceive the alteration in me. She was a creature of sensibility and keen observation, her soul attuned to the slightest discord in the harmony of those she loved. One evening, as the wind howled around the chimney pots and the rain lashed the panes with the fury of a besieging army, she entered my study unannounced. I was seated at my table, my face illuminated only by the greenish flame of a spirit lamp, my arm bared for the needle.

She stopped, and a low cry escaped her lips, a sound of such piercing anguish that it might have moved the stones to pity. "Julian," she cried, rushing to my side and seizing my trembling hand, "what is this? You are pale as the shroud that covers the dead! Look at your eyes—they burn with a fever that is not of God’s making. You are killing yourself!"

I endeavoured to smile, to reassure her with the false calm of a man whose mind is elsewhere, but the effort was vain. "Dearest Clara," I said, my voice sounding hollow and distant to my own ears, "you must not be alarmed. It is but a necessary trial. I am on the very threshold of a discovery that shall render death powerless. Do you not see? If I can but understand how this poison works, if I can isolate the agent of decay, I shall save thousands—thousands of sisters, Clara, who will not be torn from their brothers as Eleanor was from me."

She recoiled as if I had struck her, her beautiful face contorted with a mixture of horror and pity. "And what of you?" she exclaimed, her voice rising in that impassioned appeal which is the privilege of deep affection. "Shall I be left alone in the world, a widow before my bride, sacrificed upon the altar of your terrible science? You speak of thousands, but you trample upon the one heart that loves you! Oh, Julian, cease this dreadful work! Let the mystery remain unsolved; let the world perish, so that you may live! Is not our happiness, our quiet life, worth more than all the cold laurels of fame?"

Her words struck deep, yet they could not penetrate the armor of my obsession. I saw in her plea only the weakness of the flesh, the fear of the present moment, whereas I beheld the glorious future, radiant with the light of knowledge. "You do not understand," I whispered, turning away from her tearful gaze to look upon the microscope, that instrument of truth. "I am so near. The veil is thinning. I cannot turn back now, when the hand of destiny is upon me."

She wept then, with a bitterness that seemed to tear the air, and left me to the solitude which I had chosen. But as the night deepened, and the fire burned low, casting long, distorted shadows upon the walls, a sudden chill seized me—a coldness that no blanket could dispel. The room seemed to expand and contract, the walls breathing with a malevolent life of their own. I looked at my hand, and it seemed to belong to a stranger, skeletal and ghastly. A great dread fell upon me, not of death, for I had long defied him, but of the void—the silence that follows when the mind is extinguished before its work is done.
