It was a time when the fog lay upon London not as a mist, but as a solid, creeping entity, a grey and gelatinous beast that sucked the life from the gaslights and turned the cobblestones into slick, treacherous slides of mud. It was a winter of such biting, unrelenting severity that the very stones of the city seemed to shiver, and the breath of the poor men and women who crowded the doorways of the parish offices was visible in great, white plumes, as if the soul itself were escaping the body before the body could be warmed. In this climate of despair, there sat Mr. Silas Crumb, Clerk of the Third District, a man of such profound and unshakeable frugality that he might have been mistaken for a miser who had died and returned to life solely to audit the accounts of the living. Mr. Crumb was a small, sharp-featured individual, whose face was perpetually scrunched, as though he were smelling a penny or calculating the cost of a sigh. He wore a coat of such ancient vintage that its buttons had become fossilized with neglect, and his gloves were so thin that one could count the distinct lines of his fingers through the fabric. To Mr. Crumb, economy was not merely a virtue; it was a religion, a gospel, and a shield against the world. He believed that every grain of sand, every drop of water, and every pound of coal was a sacred trust, and that to waste one was to commit a sin against the Almighty and the ratepayers alike.

When the Committee of the Parish, a body of gentlemen who preferred the comfort of their own firesides to the sight of the suffering, appointed Mr. Crumb to the most onerous of winter duties—the distribution of the coal allowance to the indigent—he accepted with a nod that was as dry and brittle as a winter twig. He did not see this as a burden, but as an opportunity to demonstrate his superior stewardship. He hired a cart, though he insisted on using the old one, because the wheels were already paid for, and he set out at the crack of dawn, before the fog had fully consolidated, to begin his circuit of the wretched hovels and damp cellars that made up his district. The list of names was long, a scroll of misery that stretched from the top of the page to the bottom, a testament to the failure of society and the success of the poor. Mr. Crumb carried the ledger under his arm, his eyes scanning the names not with pity, but with a critical eye, checking for duplicates, for fraud, for any opportunity to trim the allowance by a half-pound.

As he moved through the labyrinthine streets of St. Jude’s, the cold bit into his bones, but he did not stop, for to stop was to waste time, and time, as Mr. Crumb often reminded himself, was money. He delivered his sacks of coal with a grim efficiency, weighing them on a scale that he carried with him, ensuring that not a single ounce was given away for nothing. The recipients, shivering in their tatters, watched him with eyes that held a mixture of gratitude and resentment, for they knew that Mr. Crumb gave them exactly what they were owed, and not a grain more. He was a machine of distribution, devoid of sentiment, a cog in the great wheel of parish administration that ground the poor into submission.

It was near the end of his route, in a narrow, soot-blackened alley that opened into a courtyard so dark it seemed to have been forgotten by God and man alike, that Mr. Crumb stopped. The name on the list was Mrs. Eliza Crumb. He read it once, and then again, the ink blurring before his eyes. His hand, which had been steady as a surgeon’s during the distribution of the previous two hundred sacks, began to tremble. He knew the name. He had known it for fifty years. It was the name of his mother, whom he had not seen in a decade, and whom he had cut off from his life because she had the audacity to ask for a new shawl, a request he had deemed an extravagance. The shame of it was a physical blow, a cold that penetrated far deeper than the winter air. He had not visited her. He had not written to her. He had let the years accumulate like dust in a corner, unacknowledged and uncleaned.

He stood before the door, a low, warped thing of wood that was barely held together by rusted hinges. He knocked, a sound that was barely audible over the howling wind. There was no answer. He knocked again, harder, his knuckles turning white. Still, silence. The fog pressed against him, cold and indifferent. He took out his key, a small, iron thing that he had kept in his pocket for years, not knowing if it would still fit, or if he would ever use it. He inserted it into the lock, and for a moment, it stuck, resisting his will. With a grunt of effort, he turned it, and the door creaked open.

Inside, the air was thick with the smell of damp wool and burning tallow. A single candle, sputtering weakly, cast a feeble light over a room that was sparse to the point of desolation. There was a bed, a chair, a table, and nothing else. And there, in the chair, sat Mrs. Eliza Crumb. She was a small, frail figure, wrapped in a shawl that was more patchwork than cloth, its colors faded and frayed. Her face, which Mr. Crumb had last seen in the flush of youth and health, was now a map of wrinkles, etched by the cold and the years. Her eyes, however, were bright, and they fixed upon him with a gaze that was not angry, but sad, and deeply, painfully familiar.
