It was in the autumn of the year two thousand and eighty-four that our forlorn and scattered remnant, dwelling in the shattered ruins of old Trevarnel, beheld the completion of a vessel destined, if Providence so willed it, to bear the solitary torch of human existence across the Atlantic. The sea, which had so long been to us a barrier of desolation, dividing us from our fellow-men as effectually as if the waters had closed over the habitable earth, was now to be the only path by which we might seek that communion with our kind which the heart, even in its darkest agonies, never wholly ceases to desire. We were a people bereft of the inheritance of centuries; the accumulated wisdom, the triumphs, the arts, the very memorials of our race had been swept away, leaving us to grope among the fragments of a world we could but faintly comprehend. Yet the soul of man, that mysterious principle which allies him to the celestial and the eternal, could not submit to extinction without a struggle; and in the hearts of a few, the hope of finding yet living brethren across the waste of waters kindled a flame that no despair could wholly extinguish.

The undertaking was conceived and sustained by the unwavering spirit of Eleanor, a woman whose intellect and courage might have adorned a happier age, but whose virtues, in our fallen state, shone with the more conspicuous lustre of a solitary star in a tempestuous heaven. She had been the companion of my own sorrows, the gentle nurse of my despair; and when she proposed the voyage, it was not with the lightness of romance, but with a grave and melancholy resolution, which had weighed the danger and found it endurable, if only the faintest prospect of relief remained. Her companion in this design was Lionel, a youth of ardent temperament and generous heart, who had loved Eleanor from his earliest years with a devotion that partook of worship; and who, rather than behold her brave the perils of the deep alone, would have cast himself willingly into the jaws of destruction. Between these two there existed that deep, silent, and confiding affection which needs not the utterance of vows to sanction its purity, but rests upon the inward consciousness of mutual worth, and is itself its own sufficient bond.

To these was joined an aged mariner, whose name was Oram, a man who had survived the wreck of all that had made life precious to him, and who now clung to existence with the pertinacity of one who has outlived every tender feeling, and retains only the hardy, unsubdued energy of his nature. His frame was bowed by years, and his face was seamed with the furrows of care and weather; but his eye, though dimmed, yet kindled when he spoke of the ocean, whose moods he understood as a mother understands the varying humours of her child. He had navigated the great waters in the days when navigation was a science, and when the instruments and appliances of human skill had not yet been wholly annihilated; and it was upon his knowledge, thus painfully recalled from the ruins of memory, that we relied to guide our frail bark through the perils of the voyage.

But it was the fourth volunteer who occasioned the deepest emotion among us, and whose determination to depart was fraught with the most painful reflections. This was Idris, a child of scarcely ten years, the orphan of parents who had perished in the great calamity, and who had been reared by Eleanor with a tenderness that made him as her own. When the rolls of the expedition were made out, and his name was omitted, the boy approached Eleanor with a countenance at once so pale, so resolute, and so touching, that all who beheld him felt the irresistible eloquence of unutterable feeling. "I will not remain behind," he said; and his voice, though low, trembled with an earnestness which no argument could shake. "I have no one here—no home, no kindred. You are all to me; and where you go, I will go; or if you refuse me, I shall perish beside the shore, for I will not live alone." He spoke not with the petulance of a child, but with a solemnity and depth of feeling which would have done honour to a matured and suffering spirit; and Eleanor, who had hoped to leave him in safety, wept as she clasped him to her bosom, and felt that she had not the heart to deny him.

The day of departure was one of those autumnal days which, in the western counties of England, sometimes interpose a melancholy and transient beauty between the storms of the declining season. The sun, descending through mists that hung upon the hills, cast a pale and dubious light upon the waters of the haven, and touched the broken piers and ruined walls of Trevarnel with a hue that was at once desolate and sublime. A profound stillness pervaded the scene, broken only by the distant murmur of the sea, which rolled its long waves against the rocks with a sound resembling the sighing of some vast and sorrowful spirit. The few inhabitants of the place—men and women upon whose faces suffering had impressed its deepest characters—were assembled upon the shore to witness the departure; and as the little vessel moved slowly from her moorings, a cry arose from the multitude, not of cheering, but of lamentation, so touching, so full of anguish and of love, that the hardest heart must have been moved to compassion.

I stood upon the ruined pier, and gazed upon the receding bark until she became a speck upon the dim and troubled horizon. The emotions of that moment I shall not attempt to describe; for they were of a character too complex, too overwhelming, to be conveyed by language.
