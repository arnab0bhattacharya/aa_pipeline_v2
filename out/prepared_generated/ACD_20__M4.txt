It was on a raw November afternoon, when the fog had risen from the river and lay so thick against the windows of the Wexford Railway Hotel that the gas-jets burned at midday, that Mr. Lucian Vane was shown into my private sitting-room by the manager, a nervous, obsequious person named Hobb. Vane was a man of about forty, spare and grey, with the worn, watchful air of one who has spent his life in the service of the law, and he carried in his hands a leather valise of modest size, which he set upon the table between us with the care of a man handling something that might bite.

“You are Mr. Vane, of the private inquiry office in Chancery Lane,” said I. “Your note reached me an hour ago. Pray be seated, and tell me this matter from the beginning.”

He sat, but he did not relax. “I am obliged to you, Mr. Quaritch. The facts are few, but they are queer enough to have kept me awake. A guest—a lady, by her dress—engaged a room here on Tuesday last under the name of Mrs. Harrow of Bristol. She gave an address in Clifton which I have since ascertained to be a builder’s yard. She stayed two nights, and on Thursday morning she went out early, leaving this valise behind her. She has not returned, nor sent for it, nor written. The chambermaid says the bed was not slept in on the Wednesday night, though the room was paid to Thursday noon.”

“And the valise?”

“I opened it, sir, in the presence of Hobb, as the law allows a landlord to do with abandoned property. There was nothing of value in it but these.” He undid the straps and laid the contents before me in a row upon the tablecloth: a pair of lady’s gloves, of good quality but much worn, the kid soiled at the fingers; a small revolver of Belgian make, nickel-plated, with one chamber discharged and the others loaded; and a railway timetable, folded and refolded until the creases were nearly worn through.

I took up the revolver first. The barrel was clean; the discharged chamber was the uppermost as it lay, and there was about the muzzle a faint smear of something dark which was not rust. I sniffed at it. “Cordite,” said I. “Recent. Within the week, I should say. And here is a curious thing, Vane—the trigger-guard has been bent outward, as though the weapon had been wrenched from a hand or struck against something hard in falling. You observe the scratch upon the nickel, and the grain of the metal at the bend. This was no deliberate firing at a target.”

Vane nodded. “That was my own thought, sir.”

I laid the revolver down and took up the timetable. It was a Bradshaw of the current month, and one line had been underlined twice in pencil, so heavily that the lead had broken and left a little grey crater in the paper. I read it aloud: “5.40 p.m., Paddington to Whitcombe, calling at Reading, Didcot, Swindon, Bath, and Bristol. The 5.40 is marked as a slow train, and against it, in the margin, in the same pencil, is written a single word—‘last.’”

“Last train of the day to Bristol,” said Vane.

“Or the last train she meant to take,” I returned. “There is a difference, and the difference may be everything. Now the gloves.” I took them up and turned them over. They were a woman’s gloves, size six and a quarter, and upon the inner side of the right glove, at the base of the third finger, there was a small brownish stain which I did not need to examine twice. “Blood,” said I, “and not her own, I think, for the stain is upon the palm-side, as though she had pressed her hand upon a wound—another’s wound. And here, upon the left glove, the tip of the forefinger is worn through, as though she had been in the habit of pulling a trigger. A lady who shoots, Vane, and who has lately pressed her hand into blood, and who leaves her luggage and vanishes.”

Vane’s grey face grew a shade greyer. “You think there has been a crime?”

“I think there has been an event,” said I, “and I think the event is written here if we have the wit to read it. Let us read it. She comes to London—why? The timetable is a Bristol book; she has come from Bristol, or she is going to it. She takes a room under a false name, which means she is hiding, or she is expecting someone whom she does not wish to find her. She stays two nights, and on the second night she does not sleep in her bed. On the third morning she goes out early and does not return. Between the second night and the third morning, then, something happened which made it impossible for her to come back—or unnecessary.”

“She may have gone to Bristol on the 5.40,” said Vane.

“She may. But if she meant to go, why leave the timetable, which is her guide, and the gloves, which are her comfort, and the revolver, which is her protection? A woman does not pack for a journey and leave her protections behind. No, Vane, she did not go to Bristol on the 5.40. She underlined that train because it was the train by which she expected someone else to go—or by which she meant to follow someone else. The word ‘last’ is not a note of departure; it is a note of pursuit. She was waiting for a man, and the man was to take the last train, and she meant to be upon it or behind it.”

I rose and went to the window, and looked down into the fog, where the lamps of cabs went by like blurred moons. “You have had the ports watched?” I asked.
