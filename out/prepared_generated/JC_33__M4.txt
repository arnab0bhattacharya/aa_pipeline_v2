The heat of that sea is a thing one does not forget. It is not the heat of the sun merely, but a ponderous, accumulated oppression that seems to rise from the very depths of the water, a breathless exhalation of the earth’s old, weary core, and to meet the vertical fury of the sky in a shimmering truce of fire. For seven days we had crawled through it, the *Sephora*—an iron steamer of some fifteen hundred tons, deep-laden with coal and general cargo for the Straits—moving with a dogged, mechanical persistence that was the only evidence of life in a world become suddenly and irrevocably dead. The sea was a plate of burnished copper, unruffled, without a whisper, and the sky a vast dome of white-hot brass. The silence was the most terrible thing of all. It was a silence that had a weight, a pressure, that seemed to flatten the very sounds of the ship—the throb of the engines, the slow creak of the hull—into a muffled, insignificant pulse, as if the great, brooding spirit of the place were listening and would tolerate no intrusion.

I was the master. My name is Thorn, and I had commanded the *Sephora* for three years, a ship I had come to know as a man knows his own hand, with all its flaws and its stubborn virtues. My chief officer was a man named Halloran, an Irishman from the south, with a gift for the sea that was almost a poetry in him, and a temper that could be as sudden and as violent as a squall off the Head of Kinsale. He was a man of about forty, with a broad, sun-reddened face, eyes of a startling and innocent blue, and a great, booming laugh that had often enough, in the cool of the evening watch, made the ship seem a less lonely place. I had trusted him. I had trusted him as one trusts the stars in their courses, without thought, as a part of the established order of things.

It was on the seventh day that I found the bottle. It was not a thing I was looking for. The heat had driven me from my cabin, where the air was like the breath of a furnace, and I had gone forward to the chart-room, a small, iron box of a place perched above the bridge, seeking the lesser torment of a draught from the open door. The door was ajar. I pushed it open, and the scene within is one that has since been printed upon my mind with the sharp, corrosive bite of an acid. Halloran was there, his back to me, and he was in the very act of tilting a squat, black bottle to his lips. The movement was furtive, swift, and yet, in its very swiftness, it had a horrible deliberateness. He did not hear me. He lowered the bottle, wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and stood for a moment staring at the chart spread upon the table, his shoulders slumped, a man alone with a secret and a shame.

I did not speak. I could not. A kind of cold horror had taken possession of me, a cold that was more numbing than the heat. I stepped back, and the slight noise of my foot on the iron grating must have reached him. He turned. The blue eyes, for a second, were wide and vacant, like those of a man suddenly awakened. Then a slow, dark flush, the colour of an old wound, mounted from his neck to his hair. He did not attempt to hide the bottle. He set it down upon the table with a quiet, deliberate care that was more terrible than any outburst.

“Captain,” he said. His voice was thick, but steady.

“Mr. Halloran,” I said, and my own voice sounded strange to me, thin and dry, like the rustle of dead leaves.

There fell between us a silence that was more pregnant than any words. The ship’s bell struck the hour, a single, mournful note that seemed to hang in the stifling air and then die away, absorbed by the immense indifference of the sea. I looked from his face to the chart, and a new and more dreadful suspicion began to form in my mind, a suspicion that coiled in my breast like a cold serpent. My eyes fell upon the pencilled reckoning of the last four days, the neat, precise figures in his hand. I had not checked them. Why should I have? He was my chief officer. And yet, now, as I looked, a dreadful, sickening doubt assailed me.

“The reckoning,” I said, and my voice was little more than a whisper. “Let me see the log.”

He did not move. He stood there, a big, powerful man, his hands hanging at his sides, and in his eyes there was a look I had never seen there before—a look of dumb, stricken entreaty, mingled with a strange, sullen defiance.

“It is there,” he said, with a slight gesture of his head towards the log-book.

I opened it. My hands, I confess, were not steady. I went back a week. I compared the dead reckoning with the observations I myself had taken, and which he had entered. The figures swam before my eyes. A week. For a week, a blind, incredible error had been growing, a difference between the true and the reckoned position that was now a matter of some sixty miles. Sixty miles! In these narrow waters, with their hidden reefs and their currents that run like rivers in the deep, it was a margin that might have been our destruction. We had been steering, for a week, by a dead reckoning that was a lie, a lie born of a mind clouded and unstrung. The ship had been going on, blind and deaf, through the silence and the heat, and we, her masters, had been living in a fool’s paradise. The immensity of the danger we had run, and were still running, came over me with a rush of cold, sickening realisation.
