It is a truth generally acknowledged by those who have kept the ledger of a provincial town that the moral character of a household is a public property, and that the private expenditure of its inmates is a matter upon which every neighbour is entitled to pass an opinion. When Mrs. Mary Ann Pettifer, the relict of the late Dr. Pettifer—a man whose skill in the extraction of teeth had been equalled only by his inability to extract a living wage from the inhabitants of Stonebridge—opened her door to a stranger, she little knew that she was opening a Pandora’s box of speculation. The stranger, who announced himself as Monsieur Valerius, was a foreigner; and in a town situated in the Midlands of England in the year 1833, to be a foreigner was to be a phenomenon upon which the microscope of gossip might be brought to bear with unerring precision.

The house stood in Mill Lane, a thoroughfare where the odour of damp flax and the sound of the factory bell were the prevailing influences. Mrs. Pettifer was a woman of that admirable, yet often burdensome, class of widows who regard the maintenance of respectability as a religion requiring constant sacrifice. She was thin, precise, and possessed of a conscience which, like a perpetual motion machine, was always in motion, grinding the grist of her daily duties. The arrival of Monsieur Valerius was a merciful interposition of Providence; his payment of a guinea a week, in advance, was a sum that seemed to cast a golden halo over the grimy cornices of the parlor.

But Providence, in the eyes of the town, is seldom so generous without a hidden sting. Monsieur Valerius was a man of perhaps forty years, with a complexion that spoke of southern suns and a habit of dress that was at once elegant and singular. He kept no regular hours, rising when the sun was high and descending when the moon had claimed the sky, and his room—formerly the study of the late doctor, where he had kept his leeches and his Latin dictionaries—was invariably locked. He spoke English with a precision that was almost aggressive, and he accepted the meat pies and the weak tea of Mrs. Pettifer with a courtly gravity that suggested he was accustomed to fare of a very different order.

It was here that the daughter, Miss Isabella Pettifer, entered the stage of this provincial drama. Isabella was a young woman of twenty, who possessed that dangerous superfluity of energy which, in the absence of a husband or a profession, is apt to turn into a fever of curiosity. She had been educated at the best seminary in the county, where she had learned to play the piano, to paint flowers in water-colours, and to entertain a profound contempt for the intellect of the male sex. With her father dead and her mother occupied with the anxieties of the lodger’s linen, Isabella found herself in possession of a vacuum.

"Mother," said Isabella, as she stood by the window watching the rain streak the pane, "it is very singular that Monsieur Valerius should require a candle at three o'clock in the afternoon."

"He is a foreigner, my dear," replied Mrs. Pettifer, who was darning a stocking with the grim determination of a woman mending the tears in the fabric of her destiny. "Foreigners have different habits. Do not stare, Isabella; it is not ladylike."

"But to pay a guinea a week for a room in Mill Lane? He must be a spy, or perhaps a Jacobite agent," Isabella mused, her imagination, starved of novel-reading, feeding ravenously on the scant facts before her.

The investigation of the lodger became the silent occupation of Isabella’s days. She noted the arrival of heavy crates, stamped with seals she could not read, which were delivered not by the local carrier but by a rough-looking man in a cart who spoke in a dialect so thick that even Mrs. Pettifer, who prided herself on her understanding of the rural tongue, could make nothing of it.

"Tha'll want to keep thine eyes in thine head, young miss," said Betsy, the servant, a girl from the nearby hamlet of Netherby, whose face was a landscape of freckles and whose speech was a rich, unrefined loam of idiom. "Thar's summat about that gentleman as don't smell right. He walks about at night like a cat on hot bricks, he do. And I heard him singing to himself—lal-lal-lal—like a foreign devil, begging your pardon."

Isabella dismissed Betsy’s superstition but clung to the evidence. She began to watch the lodger’s door with the intensity of a naturalist observing a rare insect. One evening, driven by a compulsion she could not resist, she crept to the keyhole of the study. The smell that emanated was not of tobacco, which she expected, but of sulphur and dried herbs. Through the aperture, she saw Monsieur Valerius bending over a table covered with strange instruments—brass spheres, glass retorts, and a book bound in dark leather. He was muttering in a low voice, a language that sounded like Latin but twisted into shapes she did not recognize.

It is the nature of the human mind, when confronted with the inexplicable, to construct a theory that satisfies its own vanity. Isabella decided that Monsieur Valerius was an alchemist, a man attempting to transmute the lead of the provincial world into gold. It was a romantic notion, and it cast the lodger in the light of a tragic hero.

The climax of her investigation arrived on a Tuesday evening. Mrs. Pettifer had gone to a meeting of the Dorcas society, and Isabella was left alone in the house with the mysterious foreigner. A storm was brewing, the wind rattling the sash windows with the violence of a drunken husband. Isabella, armed with a candle and a courage born of total ignorance, ascended the stairs. The door of the study was ajar.
