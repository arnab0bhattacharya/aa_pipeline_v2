The calm had lasted nineteen days, and the sea had gone the colour of a dead man’s eye. The *Aurelia Vance* sat motionless, her canvas hanging in limp, mildewed sheets, her decks so still that the creak of a timber or the clink of a hook against a mast carried the length of the ship like a bell. The sun rose each morning into a sky of hammered brass, crossed it without pity, and sank into a horizon that was a line drawn in tar. The heat was a physical weight. It pressed the men into what little shade the rigging offered and left them there, breathing shallowly, watching the water casks dwindle.

Elias Rook was the ship’s carpenter, a man of forty with hands like knotted oak and a habit of silence that had served him well for twenty years at sea. He was not liked, but he was needed, and he had always understood the difference. He mended the yards, caulked the seams, and fashioned a new leg for the cook’s table with the same patient care. He kept to himself, and the crew, a mixed lot of Liverpudlians and Cape Verdeans and one silent Finn, let him be. Until the calm.

It began with the salt beef. Then a keg of dried apples. Then, more seriously, a small cask of rum that the bosun, a wiry and volatile man named Corrigan, had been saving for the crossing of the line. The stores were kept in the forward hold, under lock and key, but the lock was old and the key was kept on a nail in the mate’s cabin. It was not a difficult thing to imagine a man slipping down there in the hot, dead nights. It was easier still to imagine which man.

“He’s got the run of the ship,” Corrigan said, his voice low and rough in the fo’c’sle. The men were gathered there, stripped to the waist, their skin slick with sweat. “Who else is always poking about with his tools? Who else could make a key to any lock on board?”

“He’s a close one,” said a young able seaman named Pike, who had a face like a frightened girl. “Never speaks. Never drinks with us. How do we know what he does at night?”

The carpenter was not present. He was in his workshop, a cramped space beneath the quarterdeck, shaping a new belaying pin from a block of ash. The accusation, when it reached him, was not a direct one. It came in the form of silence, of eyes that slid away, of a space that opened around him at the water butt. He felt it like a change in the weather. He said nothing. What was there to say? He had not taken their beef or their rum. The truth was a simple, useless thing.

Captain Absalom Strange was a man carved from the same hard, weathered wood as his ship. He had a long face, a grey beard, and eyes that had seen too much of the world’s indifference to be easily swayed by the fears of his crew. He summoned Corrigan and the carpenter to his cabin, a small, airless room that smelled of varnish and old charts.

“You’re accusing my carpenter of theft,” Strange said, his voice flat. He did not look at Rook, but at a point on the bulkhead just above Corrigan’s head.

“The men are sure of it, sir,” Corrigan said, twisting his cap in his hands. “He’s the only one with the skill. And the stores keep vanishing.”

“Are they sure?” the captain asked. “Or are they thirsty and frightened and looking for a reason?”

“They’re sure, sir.”

The captain finally turned his gaze to Rook. “Well, carpenter? Have you anything to say?”

Elias Rook stood with his hands clasped before him. He could feel the sweat running down his spine. He thought of the twenty years of service, the ships he had kept afloat, the storms he had weathered. None of it mattered now. “I did not take the stores, Captain,” he said. His voice was quiet, but steady.

The captain stared at him for a long moment. He saw a man who was tired, who was honest, who was being crushed by the simple weight of circumstance. He believed him. He believed him as he believed in the set of the wind or the feel of a good keel. But a captain commands men, and men are not governed by belief. They are governed by fear and by a need for a culprit.

“The matter is closed,” the captain said finally. “There will be no more talk of it. Ration the water by half. And Corrigan, you will keep the stores key yourself.”

It was a judgement that satisfied no one. It did not clear Rook’s name, and it did not find the thief. The men saw only that their captain had protected his carpenter. The suspicion, which had been a hard thing, now became something else entirely: a poison.

The calm held. The twentieth day. The twenty-first. The water in the casks began to smell of the wood, a faint, foul sweetness. The men’s lips cracked and bled. They moved through their duties like ghosts, their eyes hollow. The carpenter continued his work, but now he worked alone. He would hear the low murmur of voices stop as he approached, then resume, a low, angry hum, when he had passed. He found a dead rat on his workbench one morning, its throat cut. He swept it into the sea without a word.

On the twenty-third day, the Finn, a man named Aapo, was found in the hold, his pockets full of ship’s biscuit. He was not a clever man, and he had been driven to it by a simple, animal hunger. Corrigan dragged him onto the deck by his collar and threw him before the captain. The man babbled in a mix of Finnish and broken English, his eyes wide with terror.
