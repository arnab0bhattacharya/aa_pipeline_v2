The fog in Ashford Mill did not merely settle; it lived. It clung to the iron beams of the spinning room, soaked the wool, and seeped into the bones of every woman who worked the looms. Eleanor Hargrave stood at the window of the master’s house, looking down at the rows of blackened stone cottages that huddled at the mill’s feet like cowering dogs. She had been standing there for an hour, her reflection ghosting in the glass, watching the mist turn the village into a watercolor of grey and soot.

Her husband, Arthur, was in the counting house. He had been there for six hours, arguing with the clerk about the price of cotton and the wages of the operatives. Eleanor had tried to speak to him at breakfast, to mention the coughing that seemed to ripple through the lower cottages, but he had waved a hand, dismissing her concerns as the sentimental drivel of a woman who had never had to balance a ledger. He did not look at her. He rarely did. To Arthur Hargrave, his wife was an extension of the estate, a piece of furniture that absorbed the dust of his ambitions without complaint.

But the workers despised her with a fervor that Arthur never understood. They called her "The Countess," a title she did not hold, and they spat when they saw her carriage. They believed she was the architect of their misery, the woman who kept the looms moving and the mouths empty. Eleanor knew this. She had heard the whispers at the mill gate, seen the eyes that tracked her movements not with fear, but with a cold, calculating hatred. She was the reason their children slept in the same beds as their parents. She was the reason the air tasted of iron and sickness.

It was a Tuesday when the fever broke out in the Weaver’s Row. Three children in one cottage, two mothers in another. The mill doctor, a man named Dr. Pyle who was more interested in his port wine than his patients, had only visited once, prescribing tincture of opium and leaving the families to their own devices. Eleanor felt a sudden, sharp pang in her chest. It was not pity, exactly. It was a rebellion. If she could not change Arthur, if she could not change the mill, perhaps she could change the silence.

She dressed in her plainest black, a dress she had worn to her mother’s funeral three years prior, and walked out the side gate. She did not take the carriage. She wanted to feel the cold mud under her shoes, wanted to be part of the silt, not above it. She carried a basket of broth, bread, and a small pot of laudanum that she had stolen from the dispensary. It was a small theft, but it felt like the first honest thing she had done in a decade.

The cottages were small, single-story boxes of dark stone, with windows that looked like bruised eyes. Eleanor knocked on the first door, the home of Mrs. Gable, whose husband worked the night shift. The door opened a crack, revealing a face gaunt with hunger and fear.

"Go away, lady," the woman hissed, her voice raspy. "We have no use for your charity. It tastes of blood."

"I have brought soup," Eleanor said, her voice trembling. "And medicine."

"We don't want your poison," Mrs. Gable said, her eyes hard as flint. "We want the wages that were promised. We want the hours cut back. We want you to leave. That is all we want."

Eleanor stepped back, the basket heavy in her hands. She moved from house to house, and the rejection was uniform. Doors were barred. Stones were thrown. A man in a flat cap shouted that she should go back to her counting house and let the poor die in peace. She walked until her feet blistered and her hands numbed, the basket of food growing heavier with each refusal.

It was at the end of the row, in a cottage that leaned precariously against the hillside, that she found Mrs. Elspeth Thorne. Elspeth was old, her face a map of wrinkles, her hands twisted by arthritis. She sat by a fire that was little more than a collection of embers, staring into the dark.

Eleanor knocked. No answer. She knocked again. The door creaked open. Elspeth stood there, thin as a reed, wearing a shawl that had been mended so many times it was more thread than wool.

"Mrs. Thorne, I have brought—"

"Sit down," Elspeth said. Her voice was not angry. It was tired. Deeply, profoundly tired. She pointed to a wooden stool. Eleanor sat, the basket on her lap. Elspeth did not ask for the food. She did not ask for the medicine. She simply looked at Eleanor, her eyes clear and sharp.

"Why do you come here?" Elspeth asked.

Eleanor blinked, surprised. "To help. You are sick. The others are sick. I am trying to—"

"You are trying to save yourself," Elspeth interrupted. "Is that not so? You look at us and you see your sin. You see the faces of those you have starved. You come here with your soup and your pity, not to heal us, but to wash your hands. You want to feel that you are a good woman. You want to tell your husband that you care, that you are not a monster. But you do not care for us. You care for your conscience."

The words hit Eleanor like a physical blow. She opened her mouth to argue, to explain, to justify, but the words died in her throat. Elspeth was right. She had not come here because she loved these people. She had come here because they haunted her. She had come here to find a way to stop the guilt from eating her alive. She wanted to be seen as benevolent, not because it was right, but because it was easier than being hated.
