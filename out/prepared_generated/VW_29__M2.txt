The light in the sitting room was not light, but a substance, thick and amber, suspended in the stagnant air of the Chelsea afternoon. It caught the dust motes dancing in the beam that slanted through the window, turning the ordinary particles into a slow, celestial procession. Mrs. Harrowgate sat in the wing chair, her hands folded in her lap like the wings of a bird that had forgotten how to fly, watching the young man sit opposite her. He was so earnest. That was the thing that undid her, his earnestness. It was a kind of violence, this need to capture, to pin down, to reduce the sprawling, chaotic, luminous web of a life into a few neat paragraphs of ink on paper. He had a notebook open on his knee, the leather cover worn soft by use, and a pencil held between his fingers like a sceptre, ready to command the truth to appear.

Her name was Mrs. Harrowgate, though in the circles she had once ruled, they called her simply "The Matriarch," a title that tasted of cold tea and old perfume. Now, in the quiet of this 1910s afternoon, she was only a woman whose bones ached with the damp, whose memory was a sea where objects floated up and down, sometimes sharp as glass, sometimes blurred into a grey mist. The young man, Mr. Ellery, leaned forward. His eyes were wide, hungry. He wanted the story of the "Great Dinner," the one that had launched the careers of three poets and ended a marriage. He wanted the scandal. He wanted the flesh of the past to be served up hot, garnished with the blood of the living.

"Tell me about the night of the storm," he said. His voice was thin, reedy, a wire stretched tight.

Mrs. Harrowgate did not answer at once. She watched a fly buzz against the windowpane, a tiny, frantic sphere of blackness beating against the glass. The storm. Yes, the storm. It had been a Tuesday, or perhaps a Wednesday. The rain had come down in sheets, blinding the streets of Chelsea, turning the cobblestones into a river of mud and reflection. She remembered the smell of the house, the heavy scent of beeswax and lavender, mixed with the metallic tang of the storm. She remembered the faces around the table. They were not the faces she remembered then, not the sharp, angular, hungry faces of ambition, but the smooth, rounded, dead faces of the present.

"It was very cold," she said. Her voice was low, a murmur that seemed to come from the floorboards. "The fire had gone out. And yet, we were warm."

Mr. Ellery wrote. His pencil scratched across the page, a sound like a dry twig snapping. He was listening, but he was not hearing. He was constructing. He was building a bridge between the past and the present, a bridge of fact and figure, and he was laying the bricks with such care. He wanted to know who left. Who was the one who walked out into the rain, leaving the others in the warmth of the room? He wanted to know the name.

"There was a woman," Mrs. Harrowgate said. "She was wearing a dress of blue silk. It was the color of the sea at twilight. She stood up and looked at us. She did not speak. She simply looked. And then she was gone."

"Who was she?" he asked. The question hung in the air, sharp and demanding.

Mrs. Harrowgate smiled. It was a faint, enigmatic curve of the lips, a smile that did not reach her eyes. "Who was she?" she repeated. "Does it matter? We were all there. We were all one. The storm was outside, but inside, we were still. We were listening to the rain, yes, but we were also listening to each other. To the silence between the words."

Mr. Ellery frowned. He was confused. He was looking for a name, a date, a specific incident that could be verified, cross-referenced, published. He was looking for the solid ground of history, and she was offering him the shifting sand of memory. He was a journalist, a man of facts, and she was a woman of impressions. They were speaking different languages, the language of the ledger and the language of the dream.

"The blue dress," he said, pressing on. "Was it Mrs. Vane?"

Mrs. Harrowgate shook her head. The movement was slow, deliberate, like a pendulum. "No. Mrs. Vane wore green. Green like the moss on the old oaks in the garden. She was always so practical. She brought a basket of biscuits. The storm had knocked down the elm tree in the yard. We heard it crash. It was a sound like a gunshot. And yet, we did not run. We stayed. We ate the biscuits. We drank the sherry. We were civilized. We were safe."

It was a lie, of course. A beautiful, intricate lie, woven from the threads of her own desire. Mrs. Vane had not been there. The elm tree had not fallen that night, or perhaps it had, but it had been a different year, a different storm, a different life. The memory was a mosaic, broken and reassembled, the pieces shifted by an inch, a degree, changing the pattern, changing the meaning. She saw Mr. Ellery’s confusion, his frustration. He was trying to fit the pieces together, to make the image whole, to make it true. But the truth was not a fixed point. It was a river, flowing, changing, eroding the banks.

"Did they argue?" he asked. "The poets?"

"There was no argument," she said. "There was only a silence. A great, heavy silence. The kind of silence that follows a great pain, or a great joy. I cannot tell which it was. It was both, perhaps. They looked at each other, and I saw in their eyes a kind of terror. Not of each other, but of the world. Of the rain. Of the fact that they were alive, and that they would die, and that the storm would go on, regardless."
