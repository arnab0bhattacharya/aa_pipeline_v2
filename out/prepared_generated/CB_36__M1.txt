The wind that swept down from the high moor of Blackstone Edge was a raw and searching wind. It found out every crack in the stone lintels of our house, Chillgill Farm, and moaned in the chimney like a soul in torment. It was a wind that stripped the heather to its grey bones and rattled the very panes of glass in my window, as if to remind me that there was no shelter to be had, neither in the world nor in my own heart. For tomorrow I was to be married to Mr. Silas Croft.

My father sat by the hearth, his face, which I had once thought so strong and noble, now a mask of careworn lines. The firelight caught the silver in his hair and the deep furrows of his brow. He had not looked me in the eye all evening. He stared into the peat-flames as if he could read our salvation in their shifting reds and golds. “The Croft carriage will be here at ten, Martha,” he said, his voice low and roughened by the day’s labours and, I suspected, by a heavy heart. “It is a fine match. You will want for nothing.”

Want for nothing! The words echoed in the hollow spaces of my mind. I had a roof, I had food, I had a warm fire; and these were no small mercies, I knew. For a year past, since the bad harvest and the failure of the wool-buyer at Halifax, these things had hung by a thread. The thread was now to be woven into a rope of gold and silver by Mr. Croft’s generosity. He was a merchant from the town, a man of substance, with a good house and a carriage. He was also forty-five years of age, with a placid, bovine face and a mind that turned as slowly and surely as a mill-wheel. He had been kind. He had paid the rent. He had given my mother the shawl she now wore, and my father the seed for the spring sowing. And for this, I was to give him myself.

I turned from the window, where the last light was dying in a bruised purple stain over the hills. “He is a good man, Father.”

“Aye,” my father said, and the single word was laden with a guilt I could not bear to hear. “He is a good man.”

That was the whole of it. Not love, not passion, not the meeting of minds or spirits I had read of in my few precious books. Gratitude. It was a cold coin to pay for a lifetime. I looked at my father, at the worn shoulders and the hands, calloused and cracked, that had held me as a child. I loved him. Was not that love enough to make me do this thing? Was not my own comfort a small price to pay for his peace, for the lifting of that dreadful shadow from his face? The scripture in my head was plain: *Honour thy father and thy mother*. But another voice, fierce and insistent, cried out from the very marrow of my bones: *What of thyself? What of the life God hath given thee?*

I went to my own small room. The wind howled louder here, a wild, untamed thing against which the thin glass was no match. I could feel its chill on my cheek. Mr. Croft’s world was one of counted shillings and predictable seasons. It was a house with thick walls and shuttered windows, where the wind would not be heard. A safe harbour. But I had been born on this wild coast of the soul, and I feared the quiet of that harbour more than any storm. I thought of his hand, when he had taken mine to seal our bargain—it was soft and warm, and had held mine with a gentle, possessive pressure that had made my own flesh recoil. I had felt like a thing being appraised, a heifer at the market.

A soft tap came at the door, and my mother entered. Her face was pale in the candlelight, her eyes filled with a sorrow that mirrored my own. “Martha, my dear child,” she began, her voice trembling. “You are not happy.”

“How can I be happy, Mother, when my heart is a stone in my breast?”

She came and sat on the edge of my bed, taking my cold hands in hers. “It is not a marriage of the heart, I know. But it is a marriage of sense. Mr. Croft will provide for you. He will give you a life of comfort, of security. Is that not a foundation upon which affection may, in time, be built? A good roof keeps out the rain, child, even if the hearth within is not at first ablaze with passion.”

“A foundation of stone is not a home, Mother. It is a tomb. I would rather have a hovel with a fire in it than a palace of ice.” My words were sharp, and I saw her flinch. I was being unjust, and yet I could not soften them. The injustice of it all pressed upon me—the injustice of my family’s poverty, of my father’s silent shame, of my own body and spirit being bartered to settle a debt. I felt a surge of something wild and dark within me, a rage at the narrowness of my fate.

“Do not speak so, Martha. It is a sin to be so proud.”

“Is it a sin to wish to live?” I cried, my voice rising with the wind outside. “To wish to feel, to think, to be more than a kept woman in a merchant’s fine house? He is generous, I grant you. He is kind. But he is dull! His soul is a closed book, and I am to be the clasp that keeps it shut forever. I shall suffocate!”
