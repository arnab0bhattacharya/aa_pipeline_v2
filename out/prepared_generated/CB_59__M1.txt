The wind came up from the moor with a sound like a rising sea, and the old house of Arden Hall shook to its foundations. I stood at the window of the library, watching the rain lash the panes, and felt that familiar, restless stirring in my blood. It is a strange thing to possess all that the world calls desirable—broad acres, a full purse, a name honoured in the Riding—and yet to feel as one imprisoned in a gilded cage. I had refused three suitors in the course of a single season: a baronet with a face like a waxen mask, a colonel whose conversation never rose above the price of horses, and a young squire whose flattery was as thick and cloying as honey. Each had praised my beauty, my wit, my fortune; not one had looked me in the eye and spoken a word of truth.

It was on such an evening as this, when the world outside was a chaos of wind and water, that the servant announced a visitor. I heard the name with a start—Mr. Edmund Fenton. He had been my brother’s tutor, years ago, before the boy went up to Cambridge and before the tutor, as I had heard, had fallen on hard times. I remembered him dimly: a tall, lean man with a severe face and eyes that seemed to see through the pretences of those around him. My brother had feared him; I, I think, had secretly admired him.

He entered the library with a step that was firm but weary, his coat worn at the elbows, his hat dripping with rain. He bowed, and I saw that the years had dealt hardly with him. His hair, once brown, was now touched with grey at the temples, and his cheeks were hollow. But his eyes—those eyes I remembered—were as keen and as piercing as ever.

“Miss Arden,” he said, and his voice was low and steady, “I trust I do not intrude at an unseasonable hour.”

“Not at all, Mr. Fenton,” I replied. “You are welcome. Pray, be seated.”

He took the chair I indicated, and for a moment we sat in silence, the only sound the ticking of the clock and the drumming of the rain. I looked at him, and he looked at me, and I felt, with a thrill that was almost painful, that here at last was a man who would not flatter me.

“You have prospered, I see,” he said at last, gesturing to the room, to the shelves of books, to the fire that roared on the hearth. “Arden Hall is a fine inheritance.”

“It is,” I said. “But an inheritance can be a burden as well as a blessing.”

He raised his eyebrows. “Indeed? I should have thought that wealth brought only ease.”

“Ease, yes. But not happiness. It brings suitors who love the gold, not the woman. It brings a solitude that is all the more bitter for being surrounded by luxury.”

I spoke with more passion than I had intended, and I saw his eyes soften. “I understand,” he said quietly. “I have known poverty, and I can tell you that it has its own solitude. But at least one is not deceived by the motives of those who seek one’s company.”

“No,” I said. “You speak plainly, Mr. Fenton. It is a rare quality.”

“And yet,” he said, leaning forward, “plainness can be mistaken for rudeness. I have been accused of it often enough.”

“I prefer rudeness to falsehood,” I said. “Tell me, how do you fare? I heard that you were schoolmastering in Leeds.”

“I am,” he said. “I keep a school for the sons of tradesmen. It is honest work, though the pay is small. I teach them Latin and mathematics, and try to instil in them some sense of honour and duty. But the work is hard, and the reward is scant.”

I looked at him, at his worn coat, at the lines of care on his face, and I felt a surge of indignation. Here was a man of intellect and principle, condemned to a life of drudgery, while fools and sycophants rolled in wealth.

“It is unjust,” I said fiercely. “That you should labour so, while others—”

He held up his hand. “Miss Arden, I did not come here to complain of my lot. Providence sees fit to try us, and we must submit. But I will confess that I came here today for a purpose. I have a favour to ask of you.”

My heart leaped. “A favour? Name it.”

“My school,” he said. “The building is old and the roof leaks. The boys shiver in winter and swelter in summer. I have tried to raise funds among the townsfolk, but they are poor. I thought—perhaps you, with your interest in education—might be willing to subscribe something towards the repairs.”

I stared at him. He had come to ask for money. Of course. Why else would a poor schoolmaster visit an heiress?

But even as the thought formed, I saw the flush that rose to his cheeks, the pride that stiffened his back. He was not begging. He was making a proposal, as one gentleman to another.

“I will give you a hundred pounds,” I said.

He shook his head. “That is too much. I could not accept such a sum.”

“Then fifty.”

“Still too much. I ask only for what is necessary—twenty pounds would suffice to mend the roof and buy some coal for the stove.”

“Very well,” I said. “You shall have twenty pounds. But I wish it were more.”

“Your generosity is noted,” he said, and a faint smile touched his lips. “But I must tell you that I cannot accept even that, unless I can repay it in some way. I will not be a pensioner.”

“Then teach me,” I said. The words were out before I could stop them. “I have read little of the classics. My education was that of a lady—embroidery, music, and a smattering of French. I wish to learn more. Would you consent to give me lessons?”
