The wind in the village of Blackgate Hollow did not merely blow; it whistled, shrieked, and muttered with a malicious intelligence that seemed to know every crack in the brickwork and every shiver in the bones of the inhabitants. It was a cold, damp, and pervasive cold, the kind that did not stay upon the surface but burrowed deep into the marrow, settling into the joints of the men who had spent their lives beneath the earth and in the lungs of the women who waited for them above. In the corner of a room that was less a home than a damp cellar of misery, huddled over a fire that sputtered and coughed like an asthmatic old man, sat Mrs. Elspeth Hare. Her face was a map of the sorrows that had traversed her life, etched in lines of anxiety and fatigue, her eyes red and swollen as she held the hand of young Thomas, her son, who lay upon a pallet of straw with a leg that had been twisted and broken by a falling rock, or perhaps by the indifferent hand of Providence, which in Blackgate Hollow was often one and the same.

The door, which was more of a barrier against the wind than a door, was thrown open with a violence that scattered the remaining embers across the floor. In stepped Mr. Silas Vane, the agent for the mine, a man of such substantial proportions that he seemed to fill the room with his mere presence, displacing the air and the hope of comfort in equal measure. He wore a coat of a deep, somber brown, buttoned to the throat, and he carried in his hand a leather portfolio that clattered ominously, like the jaws of a legal trap closing. Behind him, trailing a scent of stale tobacco and arrogance, came Mr. Gable, the owner’s man, a figure of such pale, smooth complexion and oily countenance that he appeared to have been polished by the very friction of his own success.

“Mrs. Hare,” said Vane, his voice a rumble that seemed to originate from his belly, “I trust you are better? We have heard of the unfortunate accident. Unfortunate, I say, for it is an accident, not a negligence. There is a distinction, you see, in the law, and in the ledger.”

Mrs. Hare looked up, her eyes darting from the man’s broad face to the portfolio in his hand. “Thomas will not work again, sir. The bone is crushed. He is only fourteen.”

“Aye, aye, a young man,” said Vane, settling himself into the only chair, which groaned under the weight of his conscience and his waistcoat. “But he has a mother, and the mother has a roof over her head, albeit a leaking one. We are a compassionate concern, Mrs. Hare. We do not wish to see the family destitute. So, I bring an offer. Five pounds. A neat sum. It covers the doctor’s fees, the plaster, and perhaps a few weeks of coal. All you need do is sign this paper, acknowledging that the injury was due to the inherent dangers of the trade, and that no claim shall be made against the proprietor.”

Mrs. Hare reached for the paper, her hand trembling, not from the cold, but from the sheer, crushing weight of the choice presented to her. To refuse was to risk the roof; to sign was to sell her son’s future for a pittance. Before her fingers could touch the ink, a shadow fell across the floor. It was not the shadow of Vane, but of a young man who had slipped into the room unnoticed, his face pale with exertion, his black coat buttoned tight against the wind. It was Mr. Arthur Pennington, the young preacher of the local chapel, a man who had not yet learned to temper his indignation with the prudence of age.

“Hold your hand, Mrs. Hare,” said Pennington, his voice thin but sharp as a needle. “Do not sign that paper. It is a shackle, not a promise. It is the signature of a slave, not a citizen. If you sign, you tell the world that a man’s life is worth less than a load of coal, and that the pain of a boy is to be bought off with copper and a smile.”

Vane turned his head slowly, his eyes narrowing. “You are the parson, are you? The one who preaches about charity and ignores the economics of survival?”

“I preach about justice,” said Pennington, stepping forward, though his legs felt as if they were made of water. “I preach that the strong do not have the right to crush the weak merely because the weak have no voice. This paper is a lie. It is a tool of oppression, dressed up in the language of business.”

Gable, who had been leaning against the wall, smiled a smile that did not reach his eyes. “Mr. Pennington, you are a fine fellow. Truly. But you speak of abstractions. We speak of rent. The lease on this cottage is held in trust by the mining company. If Mrs. Hare does not sign, and if she cannot pay the arrears that have accumulated, the law will see to the eviction. We shall have to empty this room, Mr. Preacher. Every box, every rag, every bone. We shall leave you to preach to the four walls and the damp.”

The threat hung in the air, thick and suffocating. Mrs. Hare’s hand hovered over the paper, her face a mask of terror and despair. The fire crackled, a single spark rising and dying in the dark. Vane tapped the paper with a long, manicured fingernail. “It is simple arithmetic, Mrs. Hare. Sign, and you keep your home. Refuse, and you and your son will be out on the moors with the frost. Is your son’s pride worth the warmth of your home?”
