The fog off the river had a way of crawling into the arches, not like weather but like something alive, a grey, wet breath that smelled of rotted rope and coal smoke. It was under the third arch from the tanning yard that the boys had their kingdom, a soot-stained cathedral of brick and rusted iron where the great beams of the viaduct groaned overhead like the bones of a sleeping leviathan. Here, on a bed of old sacks and stolen straw, they kept the girl.

They called her Pip, for the sound she made when she first woke, a tiny, bird-like squeak of confusion. She had arrived in their midst three weeks past, a scrap of a thing in a torn, muddy dress, found wandering by the ditch near the market by young Jem, the quickest-fingered of the crew. She couldn't have been more than five, with eyes the colour of a winter sky and hair so pale it seemed to hold the fog itself. When they asked her name, she only stared, her small face a perfect, blank slate. Her memory was a locked room, and she had lost the key.

So they became her keepers. Cratch, the eldest at twelve and their unspoken leader, decreed it. "She's ours now," he'd said, his voice gruff with a manhood he was still years from possessing. "Found by us, kept by us." The others, a motley collection of thin limbs and sharp elbows named Spud, Finn, and the twins, Abel and Cain, had agreed with the solemnity of a parliament.

Feeding her was a constant, gnawing anxiety. They were beggars and petty thieves, artists of the small-scale deception. Finn, with his angelic face and a cough that could curdle milk, was their prime exhibition, sent to the coaching inns to elicit a penny or a crust of bread from sympathetic travellers. Spud, who was built like a small, sturdy barrel, was their distraction, capable of creating a commotion over a dropped marble that would draw every eye while Jem’s nimble fingers relieved a costermonger of an apple or a pocket of a loaf. What they scavenged, they shared, but Pip was given the first and the best. A stolen bun, still warm, was broken into pieces, the largest going into her small, trusting hands. A half-pint of milk, its cream soured, was carefully poured into a chipped cup for her to drink. She ate with a quiet, methodical intensity, as if each morsel was a word in a language she was only just beginning to learn.

In return, she gave them something they hadn't known they were missing. She was a small, living hearth in their cold, damp world. When Abel had a fever, it was Pip who sat by his head, her tiny hand on his forehead, humming a tuneless, nameless melody that seemed to soothe him more than any of Cratch’s rough ministrations. She had a way of looking at you, direct and unblinking, that made you feel seen, not as a gutter-snipe or a nuisance, but as a person. She would trace the lines of grime on their faces with a gentle finger, and in her silence, there was a profound acceptance that none of them had ever known. She was their secret, their treasure, a flower growing in a dung heap.

The trouble began with the lady.

She was a vision of another world entirely, appearing at the mouth of the alley one Tuesday afternoon. Her dress was a deep, sober blue, the colour of a stormy sea, and her bonnet was trimmed with lace so fine it looked like spun sugar. She carried a small, leather-bound book and a look of purpose that did not belong in Bermondsey. She stopped the baker's boy, Mr. Tibbs, as he hurried past, her voice clear and carrying. "A word, if you please. I am looking for a child. A little girl, perhaps five years of age. Fair hair, blue eyes. She would have been wearing a grey dress, torn at the hem."

The boys, perched on a low wall a dozen yards away, froze. The description was a perfect, chilling portrait of Pip. Cratch felt a cold knot tighten in his stomach. He nudged Jem. "Get her inside. Now." Jem, understanding the urgency in Cratch's voice, slipped away like a shadow, back towards the arches.

The lady, whose name they later learned was Mrs. Ellery, was persistent. She returned the next day, and the next. She was not unkind; she did not look at the urchins with the usual disgust. Instead, she questioned them with a gentle, sorrowful earnestness that was somehow more unnerving. She offered a shilling for any information. The coin glinted in the grey light, a king's ransom. "She is my niece," she explained to a group of washerwomen, her voice thick with a grief that sounded genuine. "My sister's child. There was an accident… a terrible confusion. She wandered off. We have been searching for weeks."

A shilling. The thought of it burned in Spud's mind. It could buy bread for a month, new soles for Finn's worn-out boots, a warm blanket for the coming winter. He looked at Cratch, his eyes wide with a desperate, questioning greed. But Cratch just shook his head, his jaw set in a hard line. "She's ours," he muttered, the words a mantra against the temptation.

But the lady's presence had poisoned their sanctuary. The arches no longer felt like a fortress; they felt like a trap. The groans of the ironwork above seemed to whisper of discovery. The boys grew tense and snappish with one another. Finn's cough worsened, a dry, hacking sound that seemed to echo their collective fear. The decision of what to do hung over them, a heavy, suffocating weight. To give her up was to lose the heart of their strange little family, to return to the cold, brutish existence of before. To keep her was to risk everything, to live in perpetual fear of the law, of the lady, of a world that would surely crush them for their audacity.
