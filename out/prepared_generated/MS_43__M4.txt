The lamps of the Royal Institution burned with a steady, unforgiving brilliance, and their light fell coldly upon the assembled faces of the Fellows, upon the polished mahogany of the lectern, and upon the covered apparatus that stood upon the table like a coffin awaiting its occupant. I stood at the margin of that learned sea, and I confess that my heart, which had so often beat high with the ardour of discovery, now sank within me with a presage of approaching ruin. The years of my life had been devoted to a single, sublime pursuit: the isolation of that mysterious principle which animates the frame of living things—that vital fluid, the fountain of sensation and of motion, which I had long believed might be drawn forth, concentrated, and made manifest to the wondering eye of man. Yet nature, though she had permitted me to gaze upon her secrets, had ever withheld the final revelation; and my experiments, though fraught with promise, had terminated in disappointment and in silence.

It was on the evening of the fourteenth of November, in the year 1817, that I was to be publicly tried. My rival, a young man of arrogant bearing and uncommon powers of persuasion, one Mr. Alistair Finch, had challenged me before the Society to produce the evidence of my theories; and I, stung by his sneers and by the whispers of incredulity that had begun to circulate among my colleagues, had accepted the challenge with a precipitation that I now deplored. He stood at the far end of the hall, his countenance composed into an expression of polite expectation, though I read in the curl of his lip the triumph he already anticipated.

The President, Sir Humphrey Ellsworth, rose and addressed the assembly. "Gentlemen," said he, "we are met to witness the demonstration promised by our esteemed colleague, Dr. Erasmus Vale, whose researches into the nature of the vital principle have long engaged our attention. I trust that the result will prove worthy of the reputation he has so justly acquired."

A murmur of assent ran through the hall. I bowed, and, advancing to the table, withdrew the cloth that concealed my apparatus. It was an elaborate construction of glass and copper, of wires and cylinders, designed to receive and to concentrate the subtle emanation I sought to capture. Upon a smaller table beside it lay the subject of my experiment—a rabbit, confined within a cage of wire, its eyes bright with alarm, its little frame trembling with the terror of its situation. I had chosen it as the most delicate and sensitive of creatures, hoping that the vital fluid, if drawn forth, might be rendered visible in the agitation of its limbs and the lustre of its eyes.

I commenced my address, and, as I proceeded, the old enthusiasm kindled within me, and I forgot for a moment the peril of my situation. I spoke of the phenomena of life—of the warmth that pervades the living body, of the irritability of the fibre, of the electric spark that flashes from the torpedo, of the light that gleams in the eye of the dying. I spoke of the unity of nature, and of the probability that the same principle which manifests itself in the lightning and the magnet might, in a more refined and subtle form, constitute the vital energy of organised beings. My voice rose, and my words flowed with an eloquence that I had not known I possessed; and I saw, or fancied that I saw, the countenances of my auditors relax into sympathy and admiration.

Then I proceeded to the experiment. I adjusted the wires, I charged the cylinders, I placed the animal within the influence of the apparatus. I turned the handle of the machine; the wheels revolved, the glass discs spun, and a faint, crackling sound arose. I watched the rabbit with an intensity that almost deprived me of breath. Its limbs quivered, its ears erected themselves, its eyes dilated—and then, alas! it sank down, motionless and insensible, as it had done a hundred times before, and no spark, no luminous emanation, no visible sign of the vital fluid, rewarded my anxious gaze.

A dead silence ensued. I heard the blood beating in my temples; I felt the cold dew of shame upon my forehead. I turned to my rival, and I beheld him regarding me with a smile of mingled pity and contempt that stung me to the soul. He rose, and, addressing the President, said: "Sir Humphrey, I grieve to witness the failure of my learned friend's experiment; but I trust that the Society will permit me to offer a few observations upon the nature of the vital principle, which, I humbly conceive, has been misapprehended by Dr. Vale."

He spoke at length, and with a fluency and a plausibility that I could not but admire, even while I writhed beneath his words. He argued that the vital principle was not a fluid, not a substance capable of isolation, but a condition—a harmony, a relation of parts—and that the attempt to demonstrate it by mechanical means was as futile as to attempt to weigh the wind or to bottle the sunshine. His discourse was received with applause; and I stood by my useless apparatus, the object of compassion or of ridicule, and felt that the labour of my life was overthrown, and that my name would henceforth be remembered only as a byword for presumption and defeat.

But in that hour of my humiliation, a thought, sudden and terrible, flashed across my mind. What if the failure of my experiment arose not from the fallacy of my theory, but from the imperfection of my method? What if the vital fluid, too subtle for my crude machinery, required a more exquisite and a more powerful agent to draw it forth? I remembered the galvanic battery in the laboratory below—the pile of Volta, with its mysterious and almost miraculous energy.
