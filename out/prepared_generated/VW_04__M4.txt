The bus conductor—his name was Albert Finch and he had a wife and three children in Wandsworth—noticed her because she never rang the bell. Others rang it, or shouted, or leaned forward with their pennies already counted; but this one sat with her hands folded on the handle of a little bag and looked out of the window as though the streets were a book she was reading and had not yet come to the end of. He noticed her particularly on the mornings when it rained, because then the windows streamed and fogged and she would raise one gloved finger and draw a line down the glass, very lightly, as if she were parting curtains. What she saw through the parting he could not imagine; the same grey Kensington he saw, he supposed, the same shops and pillars and women with umbrellas. But she looked at it as if it were a country she had been told about and was trying to recognise. Once, at the corner of Church Street, the bus lurched and her bag slipped and he caught it—a shabby thing, imitation leather, with a clasp that had lost its brightness—and she said “Thank you” in a voice so low that it seemed to come from somewhere behind her, from a room within a room. He thought about her for the rest of the morning, not with any impropriety, but as one thinks of a tune that has been played badly and might be played well. He had been a conductor for eleven years. He could tell at a glance what people were: the ones who would cheat the fare, the ones who would thank you twice, the ones who were somebody and the ones who were not. This one was not; and yet, he thought, clicking his ticket punch, there was a way she had of sitting that made the seat seem a throne and the other passengers her court, though she would have been astonished to hear it.

Mrs. Latimer, who ran the hostel in Bloomsbury and was the wife of a clergyman who had died of a chill caught in a damp vestry, noticed her because she was never late and never early. The other girls clattered down at eight and made a noise with the teapot; this one came down at five minutes past, when the toast was cold, and ate it without complaint, and read a book propped against the sugar basin. Mrs. Latimer, who believed in the character of breakfasts, was troubled by this. She had a theory—she had many theories, all of them kindly—that a girl who eats her toast cold will one day find herself in a cold house, married to a cold man, and she said as much to Miss Pringle, the secretary, who replied that Miss Ashby was only shy. “Shyness,” said Mrs. Latimer, “is a kind of pride, Miss Pringle, and pride is a kind of fear, and fear—” but here she stopped, because she had forgotten what she meant to say, and because at that moment the girl herself passed through the hall with her hat in her hand, and the light from the fanlight fell upon her hair, which was the colour of weak tea, and upon the nape of her neck, which was very white. She was not pretty, Mrs. Latimer decided; or rather, she was pretty in the way that a window is pretty when the room behind it is dark. She wrote letters, too—long letters, posted every Tuesday, addressed in a small upright hand to a village in Yorkshire—and once, when the girl had left the writing-room in a hurry, Mrs. Latimer had seen the sheet still lying on the table, and had read, before she could stop herself, “the lamps here are lit by electricity, which is very bright, and the girls say it is bad for the eyes, but I think it is only that we are not used to so much light.” Mrs. Latimer had put the sheet down as though it had been hot. She thought of it for days. There was something in it—the “we,” perhaps, or the “so much light”—that made her want to take the girl by the shoulders and say, You are young, you are young, it will not always be like this. But she never did; and on Sundays she gave out the hymn numbers and watched the girl's lips move, and wondered whether she was singing or only pretending.

Mr. Craddock, who sat at the next desk in the office of Messrs. Thurlow & Thurlow, Solicitors, of Lincoln's Inn Fields, noticed her because she typed without looking at her hands. This was not in itself remarkable; the other girls did it too, with a great rattling and darting of eyes. But she typed as if the machine were an instrument and the words were music, and when she made an error—which was seldom—she did not tear the sheet out and swear, as Miss Hooper did, but stopped, and looked at the mistake, and then went on, as though the mistake had been a note in the music and must be played through to the end. Mr. Craddock was twenty-six and engaged to be married in the spring. He noticed other things: that she brought her lunch in a paper bag and ate it in the square when it was fine; that she had a way of tilting her head when Mr. Thurlow dictated, as though she were listening to something behind his voice; that once, when the old man had been particularly tedious about a deed of covenant, she had smiled—not at Mr. Craddock, not at anyone—and the smile had gone across her face and vanished like a ripple on water. He thought about this smile at night, in his lodgings in Camberwell, and was rather ashamed of thinking about it, and told himself that it was only that she was a strange girl and one liked to understand people.
