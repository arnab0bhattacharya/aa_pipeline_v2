If any respectable citizen of the metropolis, standing at the corner of a damp and drizzling Kensington lane in the autumn of the year 184-, had cast his eye through the gathering mist of that November afternoon, he might have observed a figure of such profound and manufactured melancholy that he must have supposed it to be a monument to some civic grief, escaped from its pedestal. It was, however, no such thing. It was but Mr. Jonas Mumble, an undertaker’s mute by trade; a gentleman whose profession it was to stand at the doors of the deceased, draped in the deepest and most unimpeachable black, and to look as if the weight of human mortality rested solely, and with crushing pressure, upon his own particular shoulders.

He was hired that day to attend the obsequies of the late Mr. Melchior Gudgeon, a financier of considerable wealth and proportionate unpopularity, whose house, a staring stucco palace of stucco and insolence, frowned down upon the churchyard. Mr. Gudgeon had lived to amass a fortune, and had died in the very act of trying to amass a little more, leaving behind him a family who mourned his departure chiefly because it involved a tedious suspension of their allowance.

Jonas Mumble stood upon the wet gravel sweep, his hat brushed with a funereal nap that seemed to drink the meagre daylight, his face composed into an expression of such exquisite, such complicated and architectural woe, that it was a positive triumph of the muscular art. He had a large, pale face, like a moon which had been bumped against a wall, and a pair of eyes that appeared to be perpetually retreating into the back of his head to escape the sorrow of his own mouth. He was, to use the phrase of his employer, Mr. Scringe, "a mute of the very first water; a man who could look a guinea out of a grieving family, and make a bishop feel like a sinner just by breathing near him."

The funeral guests, heavy with crape and damp with weather, began to assemble under the portico. Among them was Mr. Gideon Vetch, a relation of the deceased by marriage, a man whose face was chiefly composed of a nose, and whose voice was composed of a perpetual wheeze. He surveyed the assembled mourners through his spectacles, and his gaze fell upon Jonas Mumble, who was standing by the door with an air of having invested his entire capital in the bankruptcy of human hopes.

"Ah!" said Mr. Vetch, nudging the gentleman next to him, a fat solicitor with a cold in his head. "There is the poor fellow. There he stands. The image of a broken heart. It does one good to see such genuine feeling in this cold, deceitful world. Who is he?"

The solicitor, who was entirely ignorant of the family tree, and indeed of most things except his own fees, blew his nose and whispered, "The nephew from the country. The one with the weak chest."

Mr. Vetch immediately shed a tear, or rather, he squeezed out a tear, for his eyes were not naturally watery, and advanced upon Jonas Mumble with both hands extended.

"My dear, dear boy!" said Mr. Vetch, seizing the mute’s passive, black-gloved hand and shaking it with a violence that threatened to dislocate the shoulder beneath it. "How does it? Ah! The old, old story. Dust to dust, my dear friend. Dust to dust, and snuff to snuffboxes. We must bear it. We must bear it!"

Jonas Mumble, whose professional duty it was to remain silent, and whose personal terror it was to speak to anyone above the rank of a parish beadle, was seized with a profound and freezing fright. He knew that he was not the nephew from the country. He knew that he was Jonas Mumble, a man who rented a room in a damp cellar and ate cold mutton when he could afford it. But to undeceive Mr. Vetch seemed a task of such social magnitude that his tongue clove to the roof of his mouth. He merely bowed his head, looked more melancholy than ever, and emitted a sound like a damp cork being drawn from a bottle.

"You find speech too much for you," said Mr. Vetch, deeply affected. "Say no more. Silence is the most eloquent of orators. Come, you must sit by me in the carriage. The family will be delighted to see you taking your proper place."

Before Jonas could protest, or even fully comprehend the depth of the pit into which he was falling, he was bundled into a plumed and padded hearse-carriage, seated opposite Mr. Vetch and the snuffling solicitor, and borne away towards the house for the reading of the will.

The carriage jolted over the Kensington stones, and every jolt seemed to jog the unfortunate mute's conscience. He sat with his hands folded on his knee, his eyes fixed on the velvet lining of the carriage, and his heart beating a funeral march against his ribs. He was a mute, and he was mute in the most terrible sense of the word. He dared not speak. He dared not move. He felt like a man who has walked on stage in the middle of a tragedy, only to discover that he has forgotten his part, his trousers, and his name.

They arrived at the house, a mansion of such oppressive respectability that the very bricks seemed to be dressed in mourning. They were ushered into a dining-room of vast proportions, where a fire of such proportions roared in the grate as if it were attempting to burn the house down in revenge for the deceased. The family was already assembled: a collection of aunts, uncles, and cousins, all dressed in the most expensive and ill-fitting black, and all looking as if they had been sucking lemons.
