The wind, which had been rising since dusk, now hurled itself against the windows of the Guildhall with a fury that seemed to me the very voice of the tempest then raging in my breast. Within, the council of our town of Marston Haven sat assembled, and the air was thick with the smoke of tallow candles and the noisome vapour of fear. The rain lashed the ancient panes, and each gust seemed to shake the very foundations of that venerable edifice, as if the elements themselves protested against the deliberation within. I had been summoned to speak as a physician, and I stood near the great oaken table, my hands clasped behind me, while the grave and portly burgesses regarded me with looks in which distrust and apprehension were mingled in equal measure.

The cause of this midnight assembly was a ship, the *Aurora*, which had anchored in the roads at sundown. She was a vessel of mercy, or so her captain declared, bearing some three hundred souls who had fled a continent laid waste by a pestilence so virulent that whole cities were become charnel-houses. They were our fellow creatures, driven by a calamity beyond their power to avert, seeking only a shore where they might breathe free of the contagion that had pursued them. But the report of their coming had flown before them, borne on the wings of rumour, and the good people of Marston Haven had risen in a tumult of alarm. They spoke of the plague-ship, as they termed her, in accents of horror, and declared that to suffer her passengers to land would be to invite the very pestilence into our midst.

I had spent the day in the company of these same townspeople, and had heard, with a sinking of the heart, the arguments by which they sought to justify their cruelty. "Are we to be the ruin of our own families," cried one, "that we may succour strangers? Shall we open our doors to the infected, and see our children perish before our eyes?" I had answered them, with such eloquence as my deep feeling could command, that we knew not that the passengers were infected; that the disease, whatever its nature, might be stayed by proper precautions; that the duty of humanity was paramount, and that to turn away the wretched in their hour of need was to incur a guilt more deadly than any contagion. But my words, though they drew tears from some, and nods of approval from others, had not prevailed against the general panic.

And now, in the solemnity of that midnight hour, the debate was renewed, and I was called upon to give my testimony. I spoke, I know not for how long, with a fervour that seemed to me to be drawn from the very depths of my being. I described the scenes I had witnessed in my youth, when a fever had ravaged the town of my birth, and I had seen the sick tended by those whose love was stronger than their fear. I painted the picture of the *Aurora*'s passengers—the women and children, the aged and the infirm—huddled in the darkness of the hold, with the waves beating against the hull, and the lights of our town shining like a mockery of hope upon their despair. I spoke of the bond of a common humanity, of the precepts of our religion, of the example of our forefathers, who had never turned a deaf ear to the cry of the distressed. And I concluded with an appeal so passionate, so earnest, that I thought the very stones of the Guildhall must melt with pity.

But the fear of the townspeople was not to be so easily overcome. The leader of the opposition, a certain Mr. Josiah Grimes, a man of considerable property and a member of the council, rose to answer me. He was a dry, precise man, with a countenance in which the lines of calculation had long since effaced every trace of feeling. He spoke of the sacred duty of self-preservation, of the welfare of the community, of the danger of a contagion that might sweep through our streets like a fire through a field of stubble. He proposed that a guard of armed men should be stationed at the quay, and that any attempt to land should be met with force. He argued that the passengers should be directed to proceed to some other port, where the authorities might be better prepared to deal with them; and he concluded by observing that the charity which began at home was the only charity that a wise man would permit himself to indulge.

His words were received with murmurs of approbation, and I saw that the tide of feeling was turning against me. In vain I urged that the *Aurora* was in no condition to put to sea again; that her provisions were nearly exhausted, and her passengers already weakened by hunger and exposure; that to send them away was to pronounce upon them a sentence of death. My protestations were met with cold looks and colder silence. The council proceeded to a vote, and the motion of Mr. Grimes was carried by a majority of voices.

I left the Guildhall in a state of mind which I shall not attempt to describe. The storm had abated, and a pale moon was struggling through the clouds, casting a fitful light upon the deserted streets. I walked down to the quay, and stood for a long time gazing out at the *Aurora*, which lay at anchor in the roads, her lights dimly visible against the dark horizon. I thought of the poor creatures within her, who had perhaps seen the lights of our town, and had dared to hope that their trials were at an end. I thought of the cold and heartless words of Mr. Grimes, and of the cowardly fears of the townspeople, and my soul was filled with a bitterness that I had never known before.
