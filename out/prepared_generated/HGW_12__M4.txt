The trouble first came to my notice through a discrepancy in the quarterly audit—a matter of some four thousand seven hundred and eleven ration units, a figure sufficiently large to be interesting and sufficiently small to be overlooked in the ordinary course of affairs. I am a methodical man by temperament, and I confess the thing annoyed me rather more than it alarmed me at the outset. A clerk in the Bureau of Equitable Distribution, Third District, Sub-Section C, does not expect to discover mysteries. He expects to check columns of figures against columns of figures, to initial the appropriate forms, and to go home at the appointed hour to his modest but adequate supper.

My name is Henry Pemberton, and I had held my position for eleven years when this business began. The Bureau occupied the fourth through ninth floors of the Municipal Provisioning Centre in the district of Ravensmoor—a vast, grey, functional structure of the sort that our administrators favour, all clean lines and recessed windows, designed to convey the impression that everything within is proceeding according to plan. I had a small office on the seventh floor, a desk of stamped steel, a calculating engine of the latest model, and a view of the ventilation towers.

The discrepancy appeared in the allocation figures for the Lower Wexford quarter—a district of some ninety thousand souls, chiefly engaged in the manufacture of synthetic textiles and the sorting of reclaimed materials. The official census listed eighty-seven thousand, four hundred and two persons entitled to full rations. Yet the consumption figures, as reported by the distribution depots, indicated a draw of approximately ninety-two thousand units per week. The difference had been absorbed, I observed, by a series of minor adjustments across a dozen categories—a slight reduction here, a rounding-down there—so that no single line appeared irregular. It was only when one totalled the columns that the anomaly emerged.

I reported the matter to my immediate superior, a Mr. Cadwallader, who listened with an air of patient condescension and informed me that such fluctuations were within the accepted margin of error.

"The margin of error," said I, "is three per cent. This is nearly six."

"Pemberton," said Cadwallader, "you have a keen eye for detail. It is a quality I admire. But you must understand that the system is very large, and very complex, and that small irregularities are inevitable. I suggest you apply yourself to the Wexford audit with rather less zeal and rather more discretion."

I might have let the matter rest there. I am not by nature a rebellious man, and I had no desire to imperil a comfortable position. But the thing had taken hold of my imagination. I began to examine the depot reports more closely—the actual signed receipts, not merely the summaries that crossed my desk. And here I discovered something that Cadwallader's reassurances had not prepared me for. The excess consumption was not distributed evenly across the quarter. It was concentrated in a single depot—Number Forty-Seven, on the corner of Gasworks Lane and Pellam Street—a facility that, according to the official records, served a population of six thousand, two hundred persons. Yet its weekly draw of ration units would have sufficed for nearly eleven thousand.

I resolved to visit the place myself. It was not, strictly speaking, within my authority to conduct field inspections, but I found a pretext in the matter of a routine verification survey, and on a grey Tuesday morning in November I presented myself at the depot with my credentials and a small satchel containing sandwiches and a notebook.

The depot was managed by a woman named Mrs. Halloway—a stout, capable person of middle years, with the brisk manner of one who has spent her life in the administration of practical affairs. She received me with courtesy, answered my questions about stock rotation and storage procedures, and showed me the ledgers with every appearance of openness. The figures were impeccable. Every unit received was accounted for; every unit disbursed was signed for by a registered recipient. There was no trace whatever of the anomaly I had detected in the central records.

I was on the point of departing, somewhat crestfallen, when I noticed a door at the rear of the storage hall—a plain door, painted the same grey as the walls, without any marking to indicate its purpose.

"What is that?" I asked.

"Damaged goods," said Mrs. Halloway. "Spoilage. We keep it separate for the inspectors."

"May I see?"

She hesitated—a fraction of a second, no more—and then she smiled and said, "Of course."

The room beyond was not large, and it was empty of goods. There was a table, several chairs, a stove of the old-fashioned kind that burned solid fuel, and upon the walls a number of hand-drawn charts and lists that I did not immediately understand. Mrs. Halloway stood in the doorway behind me, and I was acutely conscious of her silence.

"This is not a storeroom," said I.

"No," said she. "It is not."

I turned to face her. Her expression was neither hostile nor frightened, but simply watchful—the look of a person who has long expected a certain conversation and has prepared herself for it.

"You are feeding people who are not on the register," I said. "The discrepancy in the figures—it is not error. It is design."

"Yes," said Mrs. Halloway.

"The penalty for misappropriation of public provisions," I began, "is—"

"I know the penalty, Mr. Pemberton. I have known it for six years."

I confess I was taken aback. I had expected denial, or at least evasion. Her directness disarmed me.

"Six years," I repeated.

"Six years last March. When the reclassification was implemented. You will recall that the census was revised to exclude persons of irregular status—those without fixed abode, those whose employment records could not be verified, those who had fallen through the cracks of the registration system. The administrators called it a necessary rationalisation. They said it would improve efficiency."
