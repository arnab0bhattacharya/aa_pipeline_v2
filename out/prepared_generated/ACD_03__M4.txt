I had called at the offices of Messrs. Crosbie & Lewin, Private Enquiry Agents, upon a matter of a missing ledger, and was on the point of taking my leave when the senior partner, a lean, grey-whiskered man named Crosbie, stayed me with a lifted hand.

“One moment, Doctor. You are a medical man, and I fancy you may be of service in a small affair that has been laid before me this morning. It concerns a lady of the flower trade, and there is a question of a locked room and a man who is not there. I confess it has the savour of the trifling, but she is a person of some substance in her way, and I should be glad of a second opinion.”

He touched a bell, and there was shown in a woman of middle years, plainly but handsomely dressed in black, with the quick, appraising eye of one who has spent her life in judging stock. She gave her name as Mrs. Hollis, of the Covent Garden flower market, and it was clear that she had come to us in a state of considerable vexation.

“It is not the money, sir,” she began, when Mr. Crosbie had begged her to tell her tale from the beginning. “It is the queer way of it. For three weeks and more I have sent up a single white lily, fresh cut, every morning at seven, to the Albemarle Hotel in the Strand. It is ordered by a gentleman who signs himself Mr. Vernon, and pays in advance with a sovereign each week and never a word of complaint. That is well enough, and I am not the woman to question a customer who pays. But the room he gives is Number 214, and when my boy Tim went up on Tuesday last with the lily, the porter at the door—a new man, mind you, that Tim had never clapped eyes on—took the flower from him at the hall and told him he need not go up. ‘The gentleman is not at home,’ says he, ‘but I will see it placed.’ And Tim, being a sharp lad, lingered at the corner, and presently saw the porter go up the stairs himself with the lily in his hand, and he never came down again for the better part of an hour.”

“And the hotel records?” asked Crosbie.

“That is the marrow of it, sir. I made so bold as to enquire at the office, on the pretext of a bill, and the clerk—a decent young fellow who knows me—told me, confidential like, that Number 214 has been empty these two months. There is no Mr. Vernon on the books. And yet my lily goes up every morning, and my sovereign comes down every week, and I am at my wits’ end to know what it means.”

I confess that my professional interest was now thoroughly aroused, and I asked whether she had ever seen this Mr. Vernon.

“Once only,” said she. “He came to the market himself the first day, a tall, stooping gentleman in a grey ulster, with a soft hat pulled low and a green shade over his eyes—a sickly-looking man, I thought, and his voice was no more than a whisper. He paid me the first week and said the lilies were for a lady who was fond of them. But there is no lady at the Albemarle that I can hear of, and no gentleman either.”

Crosbie looked at me and I at him. “You have done wisely to come,” said he. “Leave the matter with us, and I promise you that by this evening we shall know whether your lily is a ghost or a gentleman.”

When the door had closed behind her, Crosbie turned to me with a dry smile. “Well, Doctor, what do you make of it?”

“I make of it,” said I, “that a man is hiding in that hotel, and that he has bribed or suborned a porter to carry his food and his flowers and to keep his secret. The empty room is a fiction of the books, and the lily is the signal of his existence.”

“Precisely my own reading,” said he. “And since you have the leisure and the curiosity, suppose we walk round to the Strand and see what the Albemarle has to say for itself.”

The Albemarle was one of those great, grim, stucco-faced houses of the second rank which stand in the Strand like retired merchants, respectable and a trifle dull. We entered the hall, and Crosbie, with the easy assurance of a man who has spent his life in the detection of small frauds, asked for the manager. That gentleman, a plump, important person named Mr. Bickersteth, received us with the wary civility of one who scents trouble.

“Number 214?” said he, when Crosbie had put the question. “It is empty, sir—has been empty since the autumn. I cannot account for any lilies.”

“Then perhaps you can account for your porter,” said Crosbie, quietly. “The man who has been in the hall these three weeks—a new man, tall, with a cast in his left eye.”

Mr. Bickersteth’s plump face went a shade paler. “I have no new porter,” said he. “My hall-porter is named Wills, and he has been with me eleven years.”

There was a silence, during which I heard the great clock in the hall tick twice. Then Crosbie said, very softly, “I think, sir, you had better come up with us to Number 214.”

We went up the broad, carpeted stairs, the manager puffing and protesting at our heels, and on the second landing we stopped before a door which bore the number 214 in neat brass figures. Crosbie knocked. There was no answer. He knocked again, and then, with a glance at me, he turned the handle. The door was locked.

“Have you a pass-key?” he asked.
