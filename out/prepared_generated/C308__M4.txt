The premises in Bermondsey were never quiet. Even on a Sunday, when the river fogs rolled up from the Surrey docks and swallowed the chimneys, there was a sense of things simmering, of vats and kettles and the slow chemistry of money being made. Edwin Crayle had built his fortune in those buildings, and his nephew, Arthur, had grown up in their steam.

Arthur was twenty-six now and kept the ledgers. He knew every column of the business: the cost of the bottles, the cost of the labels, the cost of the brass bands that met the trains at provincial stations. He knew, too, the cost of the tonic itself, which was negligible, and the cost of the advertising, which was enormous. Crayle’s Invigorating Cordial was sold in a bottle shaped like a small lighthouse, with a label depicting a man in a frock coat striding up a hill, his cheeks a study in roseate health. The tonic contained a little gentian, a little sherry, a great deal of burnt sugar, and a quantity of something the label called “Crayle’s Concentrated Vital Essence,” which was water.

Arthur had once asked his uncle what the Vital Essence was.

“It’s the idea of it,” Edwin had said, without looking up from his correspondence. “That’s what people buy. The idea. And the idea is worth tenpence a bottle, which is more than most ideas are worth.”

Edwin Crayle was a large man with a large moustache and a voice that had been trained by years of public speaking at trade dinners. He had begun as a chemist’s assistant in Wolverhampton, had compounded his first tonic in a back kitchen, and had sold it from a barrow. Now he had a house in Norwood, a motor-car, and a conviction that the country was going to the dogs. He had decided, at the age of fifty-eight, to stand for Parliament.

The constituency was Middlington, a manufacturing town in the Black Country, where the sky was the colour of a bruised plum and the men voted Liberal if they voted at all. Edwin had chosen it because his name was already known there. The walls of Middlington were plastered with his lighthouse bottles. He had paid for a fountain in the market square, and the fountain bore his name in gilt letters. He had given a scholarship to the grammar school. He had, in short, prepared the ground.

“I shall stand as an Independent,” he announced at dinner one evening in March. “The party men are all the same. They want to tax the manufacturer and mollycoddle the working man. I shall speak for the plain English virtues. Thrift. Industry. A sound constitution.”

Arthur, who was cutting his mutton, said nothing.

“You’ll come down with me,” Edwin went on. “I need a secretary. Someone who can write a letter and keep his mouth shut. You’ve a good head for figures, Arthur. You’ll be my election agent.”

Arthur looked at his uncle. The man was beaming, his waistcoat tight across his chest, his watch-chain glittering. He was, Arthur thought, the most magnificent fraud in London. And yet he was not a fraud. That was the trouble. Edwin Crayle believed in Crayle’s Invigorating Cordial. He drank a bottle every morning. He had convinced himself that the Vital Essence, whatever it was, had saved him from a dozen maladies. He had convinced himself that his fortune was a proof of his virtue, and that his virtue was a proof of his fitness to govern. He was not a hypocrite. He was something stranger: a man who had told a lie so often that it had become his truth.

“I’ll come,” Arthur said.

Middlington was a town of brick and smoke and chapels. The constituency headquarters were in a room above a draper’s shop, with a window that looked out on the fountain. Arthur spent his days writing letters and his evenings at meetings. Edwin spoke at the drill hall, at the Primitive Methodist schoolroom, at the works canteen of a brass foundry. He spoke about the Empire, about the necessity of a strong navy, about the evils of the drink trade—though he was careful to distinguish the Cordial, which was medicinal, from the public-house. He shook hands with men who smelled of sweat and iron. He remembered their names. He was, in his way, magnificent.

Arthur watched him and felt a shame that he could not explain. It was not that the tonic was a fraud. Arthur had known that for years. It was that the tonic was the whole of his uncle. Every speech, every handshake, every promise was a bottle of Crayle’s Cordial, sold with a smile. The man had no other self. The business had eaten him, and he had eaten it, and now there was nothing left but the label.

One evening, a week before the poll, a young man from the Middlington Guardian came to interview Edwin. He was a thin, earnest fellow with spectacles and a notebook. He asked about the tonic. Edwin spoke for ten minutes about the Vital Essence. The young man wrote it down. Then he asked about the fountain.

“A gift to the town,” Edwin said. “I believe in giving back.”

“And the scholarship?”

“Education is the foundation of the nation.”

The young man nodded. He had a way of looking at Edwin that Arthur recognised. It was the look of a man who has seen the label and wants to know what is inside the bottle.

After the interview, Edwin went to bed. Arthur stayed up, reading the proofs of a handbill. At midnight, he went out for a walk. The streets were empty. The fountain was playing, a thin jet of water in the gaslight. Arthur stood and looked at it. The gilt letters of his uncle’s name were bright in the lamplight.
