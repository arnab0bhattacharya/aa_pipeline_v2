The dust motes danced in the slanting shafts of afternoon light, suspended in the still air like the last, exhausted notes of a hymn long finished. Mr. Alistair Cray sat in the front row, his hands folded upon the open book of *Ovid*, though the Latin had blurred into a meaningless hum, a drone of bees trapped in a jar. It was the last day. The last day of the term, the last day of his life at St. Jude’s, the last day before the pension, the last day before the silence. The silence was not empty, he realized, but full; it was a heavy, velvet thing that pressed against his eardrums, muffling the distant clang of the school bell and the muffled thud of cricket bats from the field below.

He looked down at the desk. It was a solid oak slab, scarred and pocked, a palimpsest of generations. And there, etched into the wood with the sharp point of a penknife or a nail, were the names. *J. Halloway. T. P. Vance. G. L. Moore.* They were not signatures, not declarations of ownership, but marks of presence, like the tracks of animals in the snow, leading back into a past that was rapidly, terribly eroding.

Alistair traced the grooves with his fingertip. The wood was cold, smooth with the polish of years, but the incisions were rough, jagged. He remembered Halloway. Or did he? The name stirred something, a vague warmth, a scent of wet wool and ink. Halloway had been a quiet boy, always sitting by the window, watching the ivy climb the brickwork. He had gone to the Somme. He had not come back. Was that why his name was carved so deep? A testament to absence? Or had it been merely a doodle, a waste of time, and now it had become a monument? The distinction had blurred, much like the faces in his memory, which were peeling away like old paint, leaving only the ghostly outlines of where they had been.

The light shifted, a slow, golden sweep across the floorboards, illuminating the dust that swirled in the air. It was a strange light, this late-afternoon sun, turning the empty classroom into a museum of echoes. He could hear them, or perhaps he was imagining them. The scratch of chalk on the blackboard. The rustle of paper. The low, murmuring hum of conversation, that subterranean current of schoolboy life, which flowed beneath the surface of order and discipline. He had tried to dam it, to channel it, to direct it toward the classical languages and the rigid structures of Latin syntax, but it had always broken through, wild and uncontainable.

*“Aestus”* he had said once, *“means heat, or summer.”* And the boys had laughed, not at the word, but at the word, at the way it fell on the air, light and fleeting. They had been so young then. Before the war. Before the world had cracked open and spilled out its terrible contents. Now they were men, some of them dead, some of them alive but distant, like islands seen across a misty sea. He had taught them to read, to think, to feel, but had he taught them how to survive? Had he taught them how to carry the weight of their own existence? He did not know. The question hung in the air, unanswered, suspended like the dust motes.

He looked at another name. *R. F. Callow.* Roderick Callow. He remembered Roderick clearly. He remembered the redness of his cheeks, the way he blushed when he was corrected, the way he held his books with a reverence that bordered on the sacred. Roderick had been a poet, or thought he was. He had written verses about the Kentish marshes, about the herons and the mist, verses that were clumsy, awkward, but sincere. Alistair had given him a high mark, a C-plus, perhaps, for effort. It seemed a small thing now, a triviality. A C-plus for a life that had been cut short in Flanders, or was it France? The geography of the war was a blur, a smear of red on a map.

The clock on the wall ticked. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* A mechanical heartbeat, steady and indifferent. It marked the passage of time, not with urgency, but with a cold precision. Each second was a grain of sand falling through the hourglass, accumulating in the pile of the past, burying the present. Alistair felt a sudden, sharp pang of loss, not for a person, but for the moment itself. This specific quality of light, this specific arrangement of shadows, this specific silence. It would never happen again. When he left this room, it would become a room in a memory, and memories, he knew, are not faithful records; they are constructions, built from fragments, reshaped by the needs of the present.

He stood up. His knees crackled, a dry, brittle sound. He moved to the blackboard. The chalk was dry, the eraser dusty. He looked at the words written there, remnants of the last lesson. *“Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?”* Who will guard the guards themselves? The question seemed absurdly large, too large for the small, quiet room. Who was guarding the past? Who was guarding the memory of the boys who had sat in these very chairs, who had carved their names into the desks, who had whispered and laughed and dreamed and suffered?

Alistair picked up the eraser. He did not wipe the words away. Instead, he wrote his own name, in small, careful letters, at the bottom of the board. *A. Cray.* It was a humble gesture, a recognition of his own smallness, his own mortality. He was just another name, another mark in the wood, another echo in the air. He was part of the pattern, not the designer.
