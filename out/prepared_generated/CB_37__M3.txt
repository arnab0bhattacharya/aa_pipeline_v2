The east wind of Scarborough came not as a breath of air, but as a living, sentient thing, armed with minute sharpnesses of brine and sand that struck the cheek like driven snow. It was a wind that seemed to scourge the spirit as much as the flesh, bidding every latent weakness rise to the surface, and stripping away all comfortable illusion. I had walked out alone, leaving my employer’s family within the heated drawing-room of their lodgings, where the smell of stale wax and strong tea hung heavy over their frivolous discourse. Mrs. Thorne had complained of the draft, and the Misses Thorne had declared the promenade too rough for their delicate constitutions; therefore, I, whose constitution was deemed sufficiently robust to brave any weather, had been left to my own devices. I was glad of the solitude. I had sought the wild, bare cliff-path, where the grey, tumorous rock descended sheer to a churning sea, and where the cry of the gulls sounded, to my ear, like the shrieks of lost mariners.

I had not walked half a mile before I perceived a group advancing towards me from the direction of the Spa. As they drew nearer, the silhouette of the lady in the van became familiar. I recognized, in the erect, somewhat haughty carriage, the bearing of a former schoolfellow. It was Clara Vane—now, as I knew from hearsay, Mrs. Armiger. She had married a man of considerable fortune and still more considerable self-consequence; a widower of forty, whose estates in the West Riding were a byword for their extent and productiveness. She was attended by her husband, a heavy, florid man wrapped in a greatcoat, and by a trailing lady’s-maid who carried a shawl and an elaborate parasol, though the sun was utterly obscured.

We met. She stopped; and in her pale, well-featured face, framed by the dark velvet of her bonnet, I saw recognition dawn, succeeded instantly by a look of profound, unshadowed pity. She extended a gloved hand, but it was with an air of patronage, a gentle, protecting condescension, as if she touched something fragile, soiled, and unfortunate.

“Good heavens, Margaret,” she exclaimed, her voice pitched in that sweet, deliberate tone which women use when they wish to show their superiority to be benign. “Is it indeed you? I was told you were somewhere in the north, a governess, in some dreary manufacturing district. And here I find you, wandering alone on this bleak cliff, exposed to this bitter wind! You look worn to a shadow. How terribly you must be overworked! How deeply you must suffer!”

I felt a sudden, fierce heat rise to my face—a heat that the freezing Scarborough blast might have cooled, had it been a mere physical flush, but which sprang from an inward fire that no sea-breeze could quench. I stood a moment, looking at her; at her sleek, prosperous attire, at the diamonds that sparkled on her ungloved hand, at her comfortable, unlined brow. I looked at her husband, who stood aloof, staring at the sea with an expression of bovine indifference, treating my existence as a mere interruption to his constitutional.

“I am not suffering, Clara,” I said, with a hard-won restraint. “I am earning my bread. The wind is bracing, and I am accustomed to the north. As for being a shadow, I was never made for bulk. My health is as good as yours.”

“But the slavery of it!” she persisted, her eyes softening into a dew of commiseration that I found more intolerable than a direct insult. “The utter, grinding dependence! To be at the beck and call of capricious children, and of parents still more capricious! I remember your spirit at school, Margaret. You were always so proud, so high. I grieve to see you brought to this. It is a degradation of your nature.”

“A degradation!” The word stung me into a sudden, brief loss of self-command. “Do you call honest labour a degradation, Clara? I had thought the Bible, which you are so fond of quoting, held labour in honour. I serve my employers faithfully; I give them my time, my intellect, my care, and in return I receive my maintenance. I am no man’s slave. I am a free woman, earning her livelihood by her own exertions. If there is degradation in that, then degrade me to the dust! I would rather be a governess, earning her solitary crust by the sweat of her brain, than a fine lady sitting in a silken drawing-room, with nothing to do but count her husband’s wealth and condescend to her former equals!”

I had spoken too fiercely; I saw it in the startled widening of her eyes, and in the slight, offended drawing-back of her figure. Mr. Armiger turned his heavy gaze upon me, his lip curling with a contempt that was half-yawning. I felt a reaction—a sharp, inward rebuke. Was I proving my superiority, or merely exposing my rawness? My soul seemed to writhe within me.

“Margaret, you are unjust,” Clara said, with a gentle sadness that was worse than anger. “I do not wish to wound you. I only feel for you. I wish I could help you out of your hard life. If you would but come to us sometimes—if you would but let me be a friend to you—”

“I want no patronage, Clara,” I interrupted, more quietly, mastering the storm within. “I want no pity. I am not a fallen woman. I am a worker. If you wish to be my friend, treat me as an equal, not as a beggar at your gate. Remember that the women who toil in the world are those who keep the world upright. We are the props of your fine houses, the foundations of your idle ease.”
