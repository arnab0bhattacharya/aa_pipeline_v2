The letter came on a Tuesday, when the rain had stopped and the mill yard stood in that hard, rinsed light that made every cobble and puddle-edge too sharp to look at. Hiram Cawdwell read it standing in the counting-house doorway, and when he had finished he folded it small and put it in his waistcoat, and went out into the yard and stood a long while by the wheel-race, watching the brown water go.

The letter was from the solicitor of his cousin, and it said what Hiram had known for three months it must say: that the whole of the Cawdwell mill, the looms, the warehouse, the cottage, and the ground beneath them, would be sold at Michaelmas to satisfy the creditors of the late speculation in Spanish wool. He had been a careful man once. He had been a man who walked to chapel twice on Sundays and kept his ledger in a fair hand and never bought a thing he could not see. Then a man from Halifax had come with figures on paper, and the figures had seemed so plain, and the war had seemed so certain to continue, and he had signed. That was all. A signature, and eighteen years of work gone into a column of red ink.

He did not tell his sister. He told no one. He went to the chapel on the Sunday and sang the hymns and shook the parson’s hand, and on the Monday he put on his good coat and walked four miles to the house of Miss Dorothea Lindley, who was forty-one years old and had the Lindley money and no husband, and who had looked at him across the pews for eleven years with an expression he had never troubled to read.

She received him in the parlour, which was hung with a paper of small green sprigs and smelled of beeswax. She was a plain woman, tall and straight, with grey eyes and a way of holding her head as if she were listening to something a long way off. She offered him tea. He refused it. He had rehearsed a speech on the road, but the road had blown it out of him, and so he said the thing plainly, as a man says a thing when he has nothing left to wrap it in.

“Miss Lindley, I have come to ask you to marry me.”

She set down the teapot. She looked at him for a considerable time, and he bore it, because he had learned in the counting-house that a man who cannot bear being looked at cannot bear being trusted.

“You have not spoken to me above a dozen words in eleven years, Mr. Cawdwell,” she said.

“I am speaking now.”

“So you are.” She poured the tea she had offered and did not press it on him again. “And why now?”

He had prepared for that too, and the preparation was a lie, and he told it. He spoke of long regard, of the esteem in which he had always held her, of a loneliness that had grown past bearing. He heard his own voice doing it, level and reasonable, and he thought that a man could sell anything if he had sold his own mill first. She listened without moving. When he had done she said, “Very well,” and it was his turn to sit still.

They were married at the chapel before Michaelmas, quietly, with his sister weeping in the second pew and two of the Lindley cousins looking as though they had swallowed something sour. The creditors were paid. The mill stayed in the name of Cawdwell, and the men touched their caps to him in the yard, and he walked through the gate of the house on the hill every evening as though it were a thing he had earned.

For six weeks it went on. She was a good wife in the way of good housekeepers, orderly and quiet, and she asked him nothing. He began to think he had got away with it. Then one evening in November, when the fire was lit and the shutters closed, she came into the parlour with a paper in her hand, and he knew the paper before she had unfolded it, because he had written it, in his own fair hand, to the man from Halifax.

“I found it in the bureau,” she said. “I was looking for the lease of the ten-acre field.” She did not sit. “It is dated the third of June. You asked me on the twenty-first of July.”

He said nothing. There was nothing to say that would not be worse than nothing.

“I have thought about it for a fortnight,” she said. “I have thought that perhaps you were ashamed of it and meant to tell me. But you have not told me. So I ask you now, plainly, as you asked me: did you come to me for the money?”

“Yes,” he said. It came out of him like a stone out of a wall.

She folded the paper and put it on the table between them. Her face did not change, and that was the worst of it; he had expected scorn, and scorn he could have taken, because scorn is a kind of heat. What she gave him was the grey weather of herself, level and cold.

“Then I shall not keep you against your will,” she said. “You may go. The settlement can be undone; my solicitor will see to it, and I shall not pursue you for the debts, since they were yours before they were mine. You will leave the mill, of course. But you will leave it a free man, and I shall not speak of this to anyone, because I have no wish to be a story in this town.”
