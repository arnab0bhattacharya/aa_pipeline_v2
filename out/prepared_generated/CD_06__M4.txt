The establishment of Mrs. Cora Pemberton, situated in a narrow and soot-begrimed by-way off the Camden Road, was one of those houses which, having been built for a family of honest pretensions, had descended in the world by sure and melancholy stages until it found its true level as a lodging-house of the second, or perhaps the third, degree of respectability. It was a tall, gaunt, hollow-eyed sort of house, that seemed to look down upon the passing traffic with the air of a decayed gentlewoman who has known better days and does not wish to be reminded of them; its windows were numerous but many of them were blind, and its front door, which was always a little ajar, gave forth upon the least provocation a smell of boiled cabbage and damp umbrellas that was peculiar to the place and seemed to be its very breath.

Here, in a small and dark apartment at the very top of the house, an apartment which was called a room but which was in truth little better than a cupboard with a skylight, there dwelt, or rather there existed, a girl of some fourteen years, by name Polly Skeggs. She was an orphan, and had been deposited at Mrs. Pemberton’s door some seven years before by an aunt who had found her an encumbrance, and who had departed with the comfortable conviction that she had done her duty by the child and that Providence would do the rest. Providence, acting through the medium of Mrs. Pemberton, had done the rest by setting her to scour, and to blacklead, and to run of errands, and to fetch the supper-beer, from the first grey light of morning until the last gas-lamp was extinguished in the street; and Polly, who was of a small, nimble, quiet, observant nature, with a pair of very bright grey eyes and a mouth that might have been pretty if it had not been so often pursed up in the endeavour to whistle at her work, had grown as thin and as sharp and as silent as a little brown mouse in a wainscot.

Now it happened, upon a certain raw and foggy afternoon in November, that a lady of great fortune and of a temperament as eccentric as her fortune was large, one Miss Tabitha Tuzzlewit, of Tuzzlewit Hall, in the county of Devonshire, being in London upon a visit to her solicitor, was seized with a sudden and violent whim to inspect the interior of a Camden Town boarding-house. She had a great opinion of her own penetration, had Miss Tuzzlewit, and a great desire to see how the other half of the world lived, and a great contempt for the ordinary methods of seeing anything; and so it was that she presented herself at Mrs. Pemberton’s door, and, being admitted by Polly, who was then upon her knees upon the passage-floor with a blacklead brush, she stood and surveyed the girl for a full minute with her head upon one side and her eyeglass screwed into her eye, as if she were a picture in an exhibition.

“And who are you, my good child?” said Miss Tuzzlewit, at length.

“Polly, ma’am,” said the girl, getting up and dropping a curtsy that was a model of its kind, for Mrs. Pemberton insisted upon curtsies.

“And what do you do, Polly?”

“I scour, ma’am, and I blacklead, and I fetch the beer, and I run of errands, and I make the beds when the girl is out,” said Polly, in a small, clear, perfectly composed voice.

“And what do you get for it?”

“My board, ma’am, and my lodging, and a penny a week for myself.”

Miss Tuzzlewit looked at the child’s thin hands, and at the great black smudge upon her cheek, and at the patient, steady brightness of her grey eyes; and then she looked round the dark and greasy passage, and sniffed the cabbage, and heard the distant voice of Mrs. Pemberton rating a lodger for the use of the best tea-things; and a strange, fantastic, imperious notion came into her head.

“Polly,” said she, “would you like to be a lady?”

“I don’t know, ma’am,” said Polly.

“Would you like to have fine clothes, and to ride in a carriage, and to have nothing to do but to learn to play upon the piano and to hold up your head?”

“I think I should like to have nothing to do, ma’am,” said Polly, after a moment’s consideration; “but I don’t know as I should know how to hold up my head.”

“Stuff and nonsense!” cried Miss Tuzzlewit. “I’ll teach you. I’ll take you. I’ll make you my companion. I’ll be your benefactress. Go and pack your box.”

And so, before Mrs. Pemberton could be summoned from her tea-things, or could utter half the protestations and the calculations of the value of Polly’s services which rose to her lips, the thing was done; and Polly Skeggs, with her little bundle, was seated beside Miss Tuzzlewit in her carriage, rolling away from Camden Town as fast as the horses could trot, and staring out at the foggy streets as if she were in a dream.

For three months Polly Skeggs was a lady. She was dressed in silks and in muslins; she was taught to speak with a certain roundness of vowel and a certain fineness of phrase; she was set down to a piano and made to practise scales until her fingers ached; she was taken to see the waxworks and the pantomime; and she was exhibited to Miss Tuzzlewit’s friends as a prodigy of native gentility rescued from the gutter. But Miss Tuzzlewit was a lady of whims, and of whims that were as short-lived as they were violent; and by the end of the third month she had grown weary of her plaything.
