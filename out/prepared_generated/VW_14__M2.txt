The train moved with a rhythmic, almost breathing lurch, a great iron lung exhaling the grey morning from London into the stretching green of the countryside. Muriel sat by the window, her hand resting lightly on the cold glass, watching the hedgerows blur into a continuous green ribbon that unspooled and reeled itself back again. It was a strange kind of silence, this silence of a woman going to bury her sister, a silence that had a texture, like velvet rubbed the wrong way, rough and suffocating. She was not crying. She had done that in the cab at the station, where the driver had looked at her with a pity that felt like an intrusion, a rude hand on her shoulder. Here, in the compartment, the grief was quiet, a flat stone dropped into a deep well, settling at the bottom where it could not splash.

Then the door slid open, and a boy came in. He was not young, really, for he had the hollowed look of a man who has been waiting too long for something that might never come, but he wore his uniform with a sort of desperate neatness, the tunic buttoned to the throat, the cap pulled low over eyes that were the color of the river clay in the fields they were passing. He did not look at her. He looked at the floor, then at the luggage rack, then at the wall, as if the world had suddenly become a series of surfaces to be inspected for flaws. He sat opposite her, a small, dark island in the expanse of the red velvet seats.

For a long time, they did not speak. The train chugged over the sleepers, a monotonous *thump-thump, thump-thump*, like the heartbeat of the earth itself. Muriel watched a crow hop along the fence line, black against the pale green of the new shoots. It was a simple bird, but it held her attention with a fierce intensity, as if by focusing on the movement of its wings, she could keep the other movements, the ones in her own chest, at bay. The boy shifted his weight. The leather of his boots creaked, a dry, sharp sound that cut through the hum of the engine.

"It’s going to rain," he said. His voice was low, rough, as if he had not used it in days.

Muriel looked up. The clouds were indeed thickening, turning a bruised purple overhead, pressing down on the fields. "Yes," she said. "It looks like it."

"The soil’s dry," the boy continued, staring out the window. "They say the soil’s dry in the west. But here, it’s always wet. You can’t dig a hole that stays open."

Muriel blinked. It was a strange thing to say, so specific, so grounded in the physical reality of the earth. She had been thinking of lilies, of white cloth, of the smell of the churchyard. "I suppose so," she said softly. "The earth holds everything, doesn’t it? Even when it’s wet."

The boy turned his head. For the first time, he looked directly at her. His eyes were steady, unblinking, and in them she saw a terrible clarity, a stripping away of all the polite fictions of civilization. He did not ask why she was traveling to the north. He did not ask about the black ribbon on her hat, though it was there, small and stark against the grey wool. He simply nodded, as if he had understood something profound, some secret language of the weather and the soil.

"And you?" he asked. It was a question without a verb, a fragment, but it hung in the air between them, heavy with implication. *And you? Where are you going? What do you carry?*

"Home," Muriel said. The word felt inadequate, too small for the distance she was traveling, too small for the void she was filling. "To be with family."

"Family," he repeated. The word sounded foreign in his mouth, or perhaps it sounded like a weapon, something to be handled with care. "I haven’t seen mine in two years. My mother sent me a picture of the garden. The roses are dead. The ivy has taken over the wall." He paused, watching a line of sheep move slowly across a field, white dots against the green. "I keep thinking about the ivy. How it grows in the dark. How it doesn’t need the sun."

Muriel felt a sudden, sharp pang of recognition. It was not pity; it was a kinship of sorts, a connection forged in the fires of loss and waiting. She thought of her sister, Elise, who had always loved the dark, who had preferred the shadows to the light, who had died with her eyes closed, as if she were simply sleeping in a room where the curtains were drawn. "Yes," Muriel said. "The ivy is strong. It holds on."

The train slowed as they approached a station. The noise of the wheels changed, becoming a higher pitch, a whine of metal on metal. People were getting off, stepping out into the grey light, their forms blurred by the mist. The boy stood up. He placed his cap on his head, adjusting it with a precise, military movement.

"I’m getting off here," he said.

Muriel nodded. She did not offer to help him with his bag, which was a simple canvas sack, worn thin at the corners. She watched him walk to the door, then turn back. He stood there for a moment, looking at her, and she felt exposed, as if the glass window had become invisible, as if her very thoughts were laid bare for his inspection.

"Will you be alright?" he asked. It was a simple question, but it contained a weight that made her breath catch. It was not a polite inquiry. It was a plea, a reaching out across the divide of their separate lives, their separate sorrows.

"Yes," Muriel said. And then, because the truth was too heavy to carry alone, she added, "I think so."
