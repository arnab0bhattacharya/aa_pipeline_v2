The snow had ceased falling some hours before, but the wind, which had come up in the night from the direction of the Carpathians, drove it now in fine, stinging particles across the platform of the station at Zalesie, where I had been stationed for three years as superintendent of the line. It was a small station, of no particular consequence, distinguished only by the fact that it lay at the junction of two branches, one of which ran eastward toward the frontier and the other northward into the dreary, pine-covered country that the peasants of the district called simply "the forest." I had come to know the place in all its moods—the summer evenings when the scent of the lime trees in the stationmaster's garden hung heavy on the air, the autumn mornings when the mist rose from the low meadows like the breath of some vast, sleeping creature, and these winter nights, when the lamps guttered in their brackets and the wind made a thin, complaining sound among the telegraph wires. I had been content there, or as nearly content as a man of my temperament could be, having no politics and no ambition beyond the orderly despatch of the trains and the maintenance of the timetable, which I had come to regard as a species of moral law—a thing not to be questioned, but simply obeyed.

It was past eleven o'clock, and I was sitting in my office, a small, close room whose single window looked out upon the platform and the dim, snow-covered tracks beyond, when the door opened and my clerk, a young Pole named Wierzbicki, came in with a telegram in his hand. He was a pale, nervous young man, with the manner of one who expects bad news and is rarely disappointed, and he stood before me twisting the paper between his fingers in a way that I found irritating.

"Well?" I said, perhaps more sharply than I intended.

"It is from the director's office, sir. Marked urgent."

I took the telegram and read it. It was brief, and in the code we used for official communications, but I had no difficulty in deciphering it. A special train, consisting of a single coach and an engine, was even now approaching from the west, carrying a prisoner of importance—a man whose name I did not know, but whose destination was the fortress at K——, some forty versts beyond the frontier. The train was to be held at Zalesie until further orders. That was all. There was no explanation, no indication of how long the delay might last, no signature beyond the initials of the director himself.

I read it twice, and then I looked up at Wierzbicki, who was watching me with an expression of painful anxiety.

"Send word to the signalman at the west box," I said. "The special is to be stopped here. It is to be sidetracked, and the engine is to be kept under steam. But no one is to approach the coach. Do you understand? No one."

He nodded and went out, and I was left alone with the telegram and the wind and the growing sense of something profoundly wrong. For I had recognized the initials at the bottom of the message. They were those of my brother, Konstantin Vassilievitch, who had risen high in the service of the Ministry of the Interior—a man of cold and methodical habits, whose career had been built upon the principle that the ends of order justified any means, however disagreeable. We had not spoken in seven years. Our estrangement had been quiet and complete, arising from no single quarrel, but from a slow accumulation of differences that had made any further intercourse impossible. He had called me a dreamer, a man without convictions; I had called him, in the heat of one unforgettable evening, a butcher of souls. He had not forgiven me, and I had not sought forgiveness. And now his initials were at the bottom of a telegram that bade me hold a train and a prisoner at a wayside station in Poland, with no explanation and no assurance that the order came from any legitimate authority.

I put on my coat and went out onto the platform. The wind struck me like a blow, and the snow, fine and hard as sand, stung my face. The lamps were burning dimly, and beyond them the darkness was absolute, save where the signal lamp at the west box showed a tiny, red point of light. I walked up and down, my hands thrust deep in my pockets, and I tried to think clearly.

The special train arrived at twenty minutes past midnight. It came in slowly, with a great hissing of steam and a grinding of brakes, and the engine—a heavy, old-fashioned locomotive, its boiler wrapped in sacking against the cold—stood panting in the siding like some exhausted beast. The coach behind it was a common third-class carriage, its windows covered with frost, and from within there came no sound, no movement, nothing to suggest that it contained a human being at all. Two gendarmes in gray coats stood on the platform before it, their rifles slung across their backs, and they looked at me with the blank, incurious stare of men who have been told nothing and expect nothing. I asked to see the officer in charge, and after a moment a young lieutenant emerged from the coach, buttoning his tunic, his face flushed with sleep.

"You have orders?" he asked.

"I have orders," I said, "to hold the train here. Beyond that, I know nothing."

He shrugged. "It is the same with me. I was told to deliver the prisoner to the fortress at K——. I was not told to stop at Zalesie."

"Nevertheless," I said, "you will stop."

He looked at me for a moment with a kind of dull resentment, and then he shrugged again and turned away. "It is all one to me," he said. "The prisoner is in the last compartment. He has not spoken since we left Warsaw. He is either very sick or very silent. I do not care which."
