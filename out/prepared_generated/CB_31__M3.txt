The wind from the moors above Gallow’s Hollow came shrieking down into the stone throat of the valley, bringing with it a scouring rain that rattled against the narrow windows of St. Jude’s vestry. I sat in the cold, shadowed alcove behind the heavy oak screen, a forgotten Testament upon my lap. I had come to seek my father an hour ago, bearing the parish register, but he had been occupied, and I had been left to wait in the damp, fireless room, shivering in my merino shawl, listening to the tempest that beat against the ancient leaded glass.

My father, the Reverend Arthur Sterling, stood before the grate, though no fire burned there. His black cassock was buttoned to the throat, his mien severe and unyielding as the grindstone of the mill below. He was a man who carried his righteousness like a lash, and he wielded it with a fervour that left little room for mercy. Opposite him, drenched to the bone, stood a figure of a very different stamp: a weaver from the valley floors, a man whose hands were hardened by the loom, but whose eyes, dark and fierce, held a fire that the damp vestry could not quench. His name was Jonas Thorne.

"You speak of insubordination, Mr. Thorne," my father was saying, his voice a low, controlled rumble that barely concealed the fires of his wrath. "You stir the dross of the valley with the rod of your discontent. I have preached this Sabbath on the divine ordinance of station. The labourer is worthy of his hire, but he is not ordained to question the master who provides it. The Scripture bids us be subject unto the higher powers. What, then, is this doctrine of equity you whisper in the alehouses and at the mill-gates? It is the serpent’s hiss in Eden."

Jonas Thorne did not flinch. He stood straight, though the water pooled at his feet. His face was pale, sharpened by hunger and toil, but there was a fixed, hard endurance in his bearing. "I speak not of insubordination, sir," he replied, his voice rough but steady. "I speak of justice. The masters grind the faces of the poor. They take the bread from the mouths of the widow and the orphan, and they call it commerce. You preach obedience to the magistrate and the mill-owner, but you do not preach obedience to the God who said, 'Woe unto you that are rich, for ye have received your consolation.'"

"Blasphemy!" my father cried, striking the oaken table with a heavy hand. "You wrest the Scriptures to your own damnation! The Apostle Paul wrote of obedience to masters, of servants obeying in all things."

"And Christ himself said, 'Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!'" Jonas returned, his tone suddenly flaring with a white-hot intensity. "You quote the Epistle to Titus, Reverend, but you forget the Sermon on the Mount. You forget the woes. You bind heavy burdens and grievous to be borne, and lay them on men's shoulders; but you yourselves will not touch them with one of your fingers. You take the text of obedience and make a yoke of it, a chain to bind the poor to the wheel of the mill, while the owner drinks his wine in peace. Is that the Gospel?"

The silence that followed was heavy, charged with the crackle of a spiritual tempest. My father's face darkened. He was a man who never forgave a challenge. "You are a dangerous man, Thorne," he said, his voice dropping to a dangerous whisper. "You are a fomenter of sedition. You come into the house of God with your radical cant, learned from pamphlets and seditious rags. You are a self-taught weaver, not a divine. You have no authority to interpret the Word."

"The Word needs no authority but the Spirit," Jonas said. "The Spirit which is given to the poor in spirit, to those who mourn, to those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake. You, sir, have your reward. You have the patronage of the mill-owners; you have your fine rectory and your stipend. But I have read the Book, and I know that the God of the Bible is a God of justice, not of oppression. He hears the cry of the needy, and He will not always be silent."

My father turned his back upon him, a gesture of supreme and withering dismissal. "You are dismissed, Thorne. And let me warn you: if I hear of your preaching at the mill-gates again, I shall not hesitate to invoke the law. The magistrates will not look kindly upon a weaver who sets himself above his betters."

Jonas Thorne stood for a moment, his dark eyes fixed upon my father’s averted countenance. Then he turned and walked out of the vestry, his wet boots leaving dark stains upon the stone floor. The door closed behind him with a hollow echo. My father remained standing at the grate, his shoulders rigid, his hands clenched behind his back.

I sat motionless behind the oak screen, my heart beating wildly against my ribs. I had heard every word. The dialogue had struck upon some deep, untouched chord within my own spirit. I had always known my father to be harsh, exacting, a man of iron rule and cold doctrine. I had felt the weight of his authority in my own life, the crushing pressure of his will upon my own small, struggling identity. But I had never heard it challenged. I had never heard the voice of the oppressed raised in articulate, scriptural protest.

And the words of Jonas Thorne had burned into my mind like a brand. He had quoted Scripture with a directness and a fire that my father’s sermons never possessed. In my father’s mouth, the Word of God was a rod of correction, a rule of law, a cold and terrible edict. In Jonas Thorne’s mouth, it was a living flame, a cry of justice, a promise of deliverance. The difference was as stark as the contrast between the black, smoke-grimed valley and the open, wind-swept moor.
