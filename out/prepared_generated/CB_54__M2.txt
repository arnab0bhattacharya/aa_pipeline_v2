The frost lay thick upon the cobblestones of the Rue de la Paix, a white crust that cracked and splintered under the iron heels of the passersby. I stood in the shadow of the Palais, the breath of the lecture hall still warm and stifling against my face, before the chill of the street seized my throat like a cold hand. Inside, Monsieur Valois had been speaking of liberty, of the soul’s imperative duty to rise above the mundane, his voice a trumpet that shook the very rafters. I had listened, not with the rapturous attention of the students who swelled his robes, but with the sharp, critical scrutiny of one who knows the weight of words and the hollowness of their echo. I was there to teach, not to worship.

It was as I emerged, pushing through the heavy oak doors into the biting air, that I saw him.

He stood by the lamppost, his figure tall and straight, wrapped in a great coat of dark grey wool that seemed to absorb the street’s dim light. He did not turn at first; he merely stood, watching the snow fall with a stillness that was peculiar to him. It was only when he shifted his weight, causing the heavy clasp of his watch-chain to catch the gaslight, that I recognized the set of his shoulders.

Thomas Waverly.

The name struck me with the sudden, violent force of a physical blow. The blood rushed to my cheeks, burning fiercely beneath the cold. Ten years. Ten years since we had stood in the garden of my father’s estate in Yorkshire, the air thick with the scent of dying hawthorns, and I had told him, with a clarity I still found shocking in its cruelty, that I could not marry a man who sought only to preserve, while I sought only to break. He had looked at me then with a mixture of hurt and defiance, his eyes dark and deep as a well with no bottom. I had called him stagnant. I had called him safe. I had called him unworthy of the fire I believed I carried within me.

Now, he stood here, in the heart of a foreign city, and he was not the same. He was not the pale, slight young man who had trembled under my judgment. He was solid. He was prosperous. I saw it in the cut of his coat, in the polished boots, in the easy, commanding way he held his head. And by his side, though he had stepped slightly away from her as I emerged, stood a woman. She was dressed in the severe fashion of the continent, her face hidden by a hood, but her posture was rigid, expectant.

He turned.

Our eyes met across the distance of the street. There was no surprise in his face. There was no joy, nor any shadow of resentment. There was only a cool, steady recognition, like the meeting of two old instruments that have not been played in a long time. He raised his hand, a slight, formal gesture, and then, with a nod to the woman at his side, he approached me.

"Mrs. Sterling," he said. His voice was lower than I remembered, roughened by time and perhaps by the cold, but it held the same quiet authority. "I did not expect to see you in Paris."

"Nor I to see you, Mr. Waverly," I replied. I kept my hands clasped tightly in front of me, my fingers white-knuckled against my own gloves. "You are... well."

"I am well," he said. He did not elaborate. He did not ask how I was. The silence between us was not empty; it was full of the ten years we had not shared. The snow continued to fall, drifting around us in slow, silent spirals, burying the evidence of our footsteps.

"Is she... your wife?" I asked, glancing at the woman who had not moved, who stood like a statue in the background.

"She is," he said, simply. "Eleanor. We have been married four years."

The words hit me with a dull thud. Four years. I had been teaching English in various households, moving from one cold room to another, polishing the minds of others while my own remained in a state of perpetual, feverish unrest. I had told myself I had rejected him because he lacked the spark of genius, because he was too settled, too bound by the conventions of a life I deemed suffocating. But as I looked at him now, I saw not the suffocating prison, but a man who had built a fortress of his own and guarded it well.

"And you?" he asked. The question was sharp, direct. "You are the same. The same fire in your eyes, Mrs. Sterling. The same impatience with the world."

"I am what I am," I said, and the words came out harder than I intended. "I am a teacher. I am alone. I am free."

"Are you?" He asked it softly, but there was a steel in his tone that I had forgotten. "Freedom is a cold word, Eliza. I have seen it in your eyes before. It is not peace. It is only absence."

I felt a surge of indignation, a hot rush of anger that I had not felt in years. "You speak of my life as if you knew it. You did not know it then. You did not know it now. You wanted a wife who would sit by your fire and embroider, who would agree with you when you spoke. I wanted a partner who would challenge me, who would see the world as I saw it, with its jagged edges and its necessary violence. You could not. You could not, and you never would have."
