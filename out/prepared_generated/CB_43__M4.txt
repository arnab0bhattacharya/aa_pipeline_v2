The rain had fallen without ceasing since Saturday noon, and now, upon the Sunday afternoon, it drove against the leaded panes of the schoolroom with a petulance that seemed to mock the weary stillness within. I had kept my class an hour beyond the proper time, for the children were restless and the text would not fix itself in their minds; but at length I dismissed them, and they clattered out into the wet like a flock of starlings, leaving me alone with the smell of damp wool and the dull red glow of the stove. I was gathering my books together when a small figure appeared again at the threshold—it was Jem Sowerby, a boy of nine years, who had been absent from his place that morning, and who now stood twisting his cap in his hands, his face white and his lip quivering.

“Well, Jem?” said I. “You are late to-day.”

He made no answer, but came a step forward into the room, and I saw that he walked stiffly, as one who has been sitting long in one posture, and that his left hand was bound up in a rag of dubious cleanliness. When he lifted his eyes to mine, there was in them that look which I had seen before in the faces of the mill-children—a look not of childish grief, but of a dumb, bewildered shame, as though the wrong done to him were somehow his own disgrace.

“Who has hurt you?” I asked, rising.

“It were the overlooker, miss,” he said at last, in a low voice. “It were for being late o’ Monday. He said I were asleep at t’ frame, and he knocked me down wi’ his hand, and then he set on me wi’ t’ strap. It’s nought, miss—it’s nought.” And he tried to smile, but his chin worked piteously.

I felt the blood come hot into my cheeks. “Your father—does he know?”

“Father says it’s no use, miss. He says if I complain I shall be turned off, and there’s five of us at home, and mother’s bad.”

I said nothing for a moment. I am not a woman of violent temper, but there are times when the sight of injustice makes me feel as though I could take the law into my own hands and smite the oppressor hip and thigh, as the prophets of old did. I laid my hand upon the boy’s shoulder and bade him wait, and then I put on my bonnet and shawl with fingers that trembled with a purpose I scarcely dared to name to myself. The rain was still falling, but I cared nothing for that. I told Jem to go home and tell his mother that I would come to see her on the morrow; and then I set my face towards the mill.

The way was long and miry, and the wind came sweeping down from the moor, driving the rain into my face and making the dark stone walls of the town glisten like polished jet. The mill stood at the bottom of the valley, a great, grim, many-windowed place, with its chimney towering against the leaden sky and its wheels sending up a dull, monotonous thunder that shook the ground beneath my feet. The gates were open, and I passed into the yard, where the water dripped from the slated roofs and the air was heavy with the smell of oil and wool and the warm, greasy steam of the fulling-mills. A man directed me to the counting-house, and there I found a clerk, who, upon hearing my errand, looked at me with a sort of mild astonishment, as though a woman with a complaint were a phenomenon as rare as a snowstorm in June, and then desired me to wait while he inquired whether Mr. Halstead could see me.

I waited a full quarter of an hour, and was beginning to think that I had been forgotten, when the door opened and a young man entered. He was tall and well-made, with a pleasant countenance, and dressed in the fashion of a gentleman, though with a certain careless ease that seemed to say he was not unaccustomed to command. He bowed to me with politeness, and begged that I would be seated.

“I am told, madam, that you wish to speak with me concerning one of our hands,” he said, in a voice that was low and well modulated. “I am sorry that my father is from home, but I shall be glad to hear anything you may have to say.”

I told him my errand plainly, for I am not skilled in the soft phrases of diplomacy, and I felt that the matter was too grave for mincing. I described the boy’s bruised body and his frightened face; I said that such cruelty was a scandal to the town and a sin against God; I asked him whether he knew how his overseer used the little ones who toiled in his mill, and whether he thought that the Master in heaven would hold him guiltless of their blood.

He heard me out with great patience, his eyes fixed upon the fire that burned in the grate, and when I had done he was silent for a moment. Then he said, with a slight smile, “You speak warmly, madam, and I honour your zeal. But you must remember that these people are not as we are. They are ignorant and careless, and if they are not kept to their work with a firm hand they will idle and grow insolent, and then the whole discipline of the mill is ruined. My father has his own way of managing them, and I do not interfere. As for the overseer, I daresay he was hasty; but the boy was probably in fault, and a blow or two does no lasting harm to a lad of that class.”
