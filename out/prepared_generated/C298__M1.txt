Alderman Fitzwilliam Pumble stood before the cheval mirror in his morning room, adjusting the intricate folds of his cravat until the starched linen sat like a perfect, rigid cloud beneath his chin. Outside, the sky over the market town of Oakhaven was the colour of a bruised plum, heavy and swollen with the promise of a late autumn deluge. Fitzwilliam, however, was impervious to the gloom. Today was the culmination of his life’s work. The new Town Hall, a monument to Victorian progress and, more specifically, to the civic ambition of Alderman Pumble, was to be officially opened. He smoothed the lapels of his dove-grey waistcoat, a garment of such exquisite cut that it seemed to defy the very notion of mud and rain. He was a man of substance, a man who built things, and today the town would feast in the hall that bore his spiritual, if not yet official, signature.

He descended the stairs with the measured tread of a man who owned the floorboards. His breakfast of kippers and toast was consumed with relish, each bite a testament to his well-earned success. At half-past nine, he donned his heavy overcoat and top hat and set out for the Town Hall, his heart swelling with a proprietor’s pride. He expected to find the final touches being applied: the scent of beeswax, the bustling of servants laying out the long tables, perhaps even a foreman offering a last, fawning word of praise.

What he did not expect to find was silence. A heavy, wet, and profoundly wrong silence. The great oak doors were closed, but as he pushed one open, a gust of damp, chilly air rushed out to meet him, carrying with it the smell of wet plaster and raw timber. The magnificent entrance hall, which should have been a hive of activity, was empty. In the centre of the floor, a single puddle of rainwater was steadily spreading from a point in the vaulted ceiling.

Fitzwilliam’s first thought was one of simple annoyance. A leak. A minor, rectifiable flaw in an otherwise perfect creation. He strode across the marble floor, his boots clicking sharply in the echoing space. He looked up. And his world, so carefully constructed and so perfectly ordered, began to crumble.

There, directly above him, was not a small, manageable drip, but a gaping wound in the roof. The ornate plasterwork of the ceiling gave way to bare, dark rafters, and beyond them, the swirling grey sky. Rain fell not in a gentle patter but in a steady, insistent sheet, splattering onto the floor below and darkening the polished wood of the dais where he was to stand. The contractor, a Mr. Silas Croft, a man with a plausible smile and a firm handshake, had not been applying finishing touches. He had been applying the finishing touches to his own escape, presumably with the final payment of two hundred pounds in his pocket.

A cold dread, far more chilling than the rain, seeped into Fitzwilliam’s bones. The banquet. Two hundred of Oakhaven’s finest citizens, including the Mayor and the visiting Member of Parliament, were due to arrive in four hours. The food was ordered, the wine was decanted, the speeches were written. To cancel now would be not merely a failure, but a humiliation of epic proportions. It would be an admission that he, Alderman Pumble, had been duped, outmanoeuvred, and left holding a very large, very expensive bag. The thought was intolerable.

Panic, a raw and unfamiliar emotion, clawed at his throat. He paced the length of the hall, his mind racing through a dozen disastrous scenarios. But then, as he stood beneath the deluge, a solution began to form, born of sheer, unadulterated stubbornness. He would not admit defeat. He would not admit anything at all. He would simply… proceed. The event would go on. The rain would be ignored. The hole in the roof would be treated as a minor, almost intentional, architectural feature. He would preside over the greatest civic disaster in Oakhaven’s history with a smile so fixed and a dignity so profound that no one would dare suggest anything was amiss.

He summoned the head caterer, a stout woman named Mrs. Higgins, whose cheerful efficiency was already curdling into alarm. “Mrs. Higgins,” Fitzwilliam announced, his voice booming with a confidence he did not feel. “A slight change of plan. A touch of inclement weather, you see. We shall move the tables to the perimeter of the hall, under the porticos and in the alcoves. It will create a more… intimate atmosphere. A grotto, if you will. A celebration of the English weather!”

Mrs. Higgins stared at him, then at the ceiling, then back at him. “A grotto, sir?”

“Precisely! A triumph of adaptability! Now, see to it. And find me some buckets. Large ones. For… decoration.”

By the time the first guests began to arrive, their umbrellas creating a canopy of black silk and waxed cotton, the hall was a bizarre tableau. Long tables were pushed against the walls, their white cloths stark against the dark wood panelling. In the centre of the room, directly under the skeletal roof, stood a collection of brass spittoons, copper milk pails, and ceramic soup tureens, all silently collecting the steady drip of rainwater. The air was thick with the smell of damp wool and roasting beef.

Fitzwilliam stood by the door, greeting each arrival with a beaming, unshakeable smile. “Alderman Pumble! Dreadful weather!” boomed the Mayor, shaking a cascade of water from his coat.

“Is it, Your Worship?” Fitzwilliam replied, his tone one of mild surprise. “I hadn’t noticed. A fine, bracing autumn day! It reminds us all of the resilience of the English spirit, which this very hall is built to embody!”

He guided the bewildered guests to their damp seats, chattering about civic duty and architectural innovation. When a portly alderman named Griggs pointed a trembling finger at the sky, visible through the hole, Fitzwilliam simply chuckled. “Ah, yes. The Oculus. A modern feature I insisted upon. To connect the citizenry with the heavens. A symbol of transparency in local government!”
