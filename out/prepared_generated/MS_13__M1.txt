It was with a heavy heart that I descended to my parlour in the evening, the day’s labours of a lodging-house keeper being such as to exhaust the body while leaving the mind a prey to its own bitter reflections. The rain, which had fallen without ceasing since the meridian hour, now beat with a mournful constancy against the window-panes of my house in Bath, each drop seeming to weep for the ruin of my former self. I sat by the fire, its cheerful blaze a mockery to the chill that pervaded my soul, and I thought upon the deceitfulness of that peace which I had so painfully constructed for myself. For seven years I had lived as the respectable widow, Mrs. Eleanor Vance, my past a locked and buried casket, the key of which I had cast into the fathomless sea of my own despair. But how vain is the labour of the soul to build a wall against the encroaching tide of memory and consequence! A single knock at the door, sharp and insistent as a summons from the grave, was enough to shatter the fragile edifice.

My servant, honest Martha, announced a gentleman who desired to speak with me upon a matter of business. I bade her show him in, expecting some tradesman or perhaps a new lodger, though the hour was late and the weather unkind. He entered, a figure of such gaunt and spectral aspect that my heart seemed to cease its beating within my breast. He was tall and thin, his face a roadmap of privation and vice, and his eyes, sunk deep within their sockets, possessed a predatory gleam that was horribly familiar. He wore the rough garb of a man who has known hardship, yet he carried himself with a sort of ruinous dignity that was more menacing than any ruffian’s swagger.

“Mrs. Vance,” he said, and his voice, though altered by years of suffering and coarse living, struck a chord of such profound terror in my memory that I felt the blood drain from my face. I knew him. In that dreadful instant, the veil of seven years was rent asunder, and I saw not the stranger before me, but the phantom of my youth, the architect of my misery—Thomas Gable.

“You do not know me, perhaps,” he continued, a cruel smile twisting his lips as he saw my agitation. “Time and misfortune are potent changers of the human countenance. But I think you knew my brother. A man of great talent, and a great misstep. A man you once called husband.”

I could not speak. My tongue was a leaden weight in my mouth; my limbs were frozen. He paced the small room, his wet boots leaving dark prints upon the rug, his presence polluting the sanctity of my hard-won sanctuary. He spoke of the past, of the forged bills and the trial, of the sentence of transportation to that far-off penal colony. He spoke of my own testimony, how I, as his wife, had pleaded for mercy, my tears and my youth availing nothing against the stern face of the law. I had been left with nothing but shame and a ruined name. I had fled, changed my identity, and through years of silent toil and unremitting anxiety, had raised myself to this station of modest security. My life was a testament to endurance, a quiet triumph over a past that should have destroyed me. And now, that past stood before me, clad in rags and demanding its due.

He had escaped, he told me, or rather, had served his term and returned to a world that had forgotten him. He was destitute, his health broken, his spirit soured by years of misery. He knew my secret. He knew that the respectable Mrs. Vance of Bath was once Eleanor Gable, the wife of a forger. “And a wife,” he hissed, leaning close, his breath foul with the smell of stale gin, “is bound to her husband, in life and in this living death. You have prospered, my dear Eleanor, while I have rotted. You have built this house upon the bones of my disgrace. It is time you paid a small measure of your debt.”

He demanded money. Not a pittance, but a sum that would sever the very sinews of my existence—enough to leave this country and begin anew. “Five hundred pounds,” he stated, his voice flat and merciless. “You have it, or can get it. A woman in your position, with such a respectable establishment… you must have saved. Think of what will happen if I am forced to tell your lodgers, your neighbours, the good people of Bath, the story of the forger’s wife. Your reputation, so carefully cultivated, would wither in a day. Your lodgers would flee. You would be cast out, a marked woman, as you were before.”

Oh, the agony of that moment! To have my soul laid bare to this fiend, to see my life’s work, my very identity, held in his grimy hand like a hostage. My first impulse was a wild, desperate fury. I would not yield! I would defy him, expose him as a blackmailer, and trust to Providence to see me through the storm. But as I looked at his face, at the utter ruin of the man before me, I saw the reflection of my own potential ruin. He had nothing to lose; I had everything. My respectable life, the quiet routine, the respect of my servants and lodgers, the peace that, though fragile, was the only peace I knew—was it worth this price? Was this gilded cage of security worth purchasing with the last of my resources, only to live in constant fear of his return?
