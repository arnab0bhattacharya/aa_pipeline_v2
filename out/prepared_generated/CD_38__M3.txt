If any honest chronologer of the human heart were to set down the precise temperature of the establishment of Mr. Vulture on a December morning in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and forty-something, he would be forced to confess that it was considerably colder than the freezing fog settling upon the river Thames. Mr. Vulture was a money-lender and a wringer of rents, a gentleman of so much acidity and so little mercy that the very ink upon his desk seemed to dry up in terror of him, and the shivering clerks in his outer office were wont to keep their pens in their waistcoat pockets to prevent them from catching their deaths. Chief among these clerks was one Eustace Sallow, a thin, pale, threadbare young man of five-and-twenty, who was remarkable for possessing a nose of a very crimson hue, a pair of boots that admitted the wet with an impartial hospitality, and a heart which he had long ago locked up in the iron safe of his employer’s counting-house, alongside the mortgages and the dishonoured bills.

‘Sallow!’ said Mr. Vulture, in a voice that seemed to scrape against the plaster of the ceiling and leave a chill wherever it passed. ‘Take the usual notice. Go up to Saffron Hill. Number fourteen, Beggar’s Court. The Pinch family. Three weeks in arrear. Turn them out. If they will not go, put their chairs and their crockery and their wretched bedding into the street. The law is the law, Sallow, and bricks and mortar cannot be maintained upon empty stomachs and sentimental sighs.’

‘Yes, sir,’ said Eustace Sallow, taking the folded paper, which seemed to shiver in his grasp as if it knew the nature of its errand. He placed it in his hat, buttoned his shabby coat tightly to his chin, and sallied forth into the biting air.

Saffron Hill, in those days, was a very jungle of brick and mud, a dismal, sweltering, and reeking labyrinth, where the houses leaned across the street to scowl at one another, as if conspiring over the miseries of their inmates. The gutters were choked with a rich compost of ashes, cabbage-stalks, and unmentionable filth, through which a troop of ragged children played with a frantic merriment that was half a howl. Pigs rooted in the doorways, and a general odour of ancient smoke, damp plaster, and undefinable decay hung over the whole neighbourhood like a pall.

Number fourteen, Beggar’s Court, was the very centre of this dismal kingdom. It was a house that appeared to have been originally built for a giant of moderate size, and subsequently squeezed by some hydraulic press into a habitation for dwarfs. The staircase, which Eustace Sallow began to ascend, was so treacherously rotten and so steeply inclined that it seemed to invite him to break his neck in the dark. The walls wept with green damp; the banister was sticky with grime; and at every landing, the ghostly apparition of a broken chair or a headless bedpost loomed in the gloom, starting out of the darkness like the skeletons of former evictions.

Mr. Sallow, being a methodical young man, though not a hard-hearted one by nature, proceeded on his mission, ascending flight after flight. His boots creaked dolefully, as if in protest against the errand, and the paper in his hat felt heavier at every step. He repeated to himself, as a sort of charm against the whispers of his own conscience, ‘The law is the law, and bricks and mortar cannot be maintained upon empty stomachs.’ But the charm was a weak one, and the higher he climbed, the weaker it grew.

At the very top of the house, in a sort of garret that smelt strongly of soot and rain-water, he beheld a sight which gave his crimson nose an additional twinge, and made him stop short upon the landing. Seated upon a broken stool, with her hands folded in her lap, was a little girl of perhaps ten years old. She was dressed in a faded cotton gown, which had apparently been made for a person of twice her stature, and was pinned up in various places to keep it from trailing upon the floor. Her face was very pale and sharp, but her eyes were as large and bright as two stars shining through a London fog. Beside her, upon the bare boards, lay a battered tin saucepan, in which reposed a melancholy mixture of water and a few crusts of bread, which seemed to be making a feeble pretence of being soup.

As Mr. Sallow paused, the child looked up at him without a start. She appeared to have expected him; perhaps she had heard the heavy creaking of his boots upon the stairs, and had sat there in the dark, waiting for the executioner.

‘Are you the gentleman come to turn us out?’ she asked, in a voice that was very quiet and very steady, but which had a little tremble in it, like a leaf on a frosty morning.

‘I am Mr. Vulture’s clerk,’ returned Eustace Sallow, feeling as if he ought to apologize for the fact, and looking with some confusion at the tin saucepan. ‘I have a notice. The rent—’

‘Mother is out washing,’ interrupted the child, with a nod of her small head, as if the matter were entirely understood. ‘She was to be paid yesterday, but the lady lost her purse, and so mother came home with nothing but a shilling. She left it with me, in case the landlord’s gentleman should come. She said he would perhaps be satisfied with a shilling, and let us stay till Saturday.’

Eustace Sallow looked at the child, and then he looked at the shilling, which she had drawn from the bosom of her frock, and which she now held out to him in her little dirty hand. It was a very old shilling, worn quite smooth, and it looked as if it had passed through a thousand hands, and done a thousand turns of honest service, and was now, in its old age, called upon to perform the impossible task of stopping the wheels of the law.
