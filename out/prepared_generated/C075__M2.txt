The air in the solicitor’s office smelled of beeswax and stale tobacco, a dry, suffocating scent that tasted like the end of something. Arthur Pendelton sat on a high-backed chair that felt too large for his frame, his hands resting on his knees, fingers intertwined until the knuckles whitened. He was a man built for the reins, for the rhythm of hooves on cobblestone, not for the stillness of a room where silence was expected to do the work of a knife. Outside, the London rain lashed against the windowpane, a gray curtain that separated him from the street and, more importantly, from the past he had spent ten years trying to bury.

The solicitor, a thin man named Mr. Gumble with eyes that seemed to peel back layers of skin to look at the truth beneath, adjusted his spectacles. "Mr. Pendelton, you were the head coachman for the late Comte de Valois in 1789. You drove his carriage from Paris to the countryside estate at Bellevue. Do you recall the incident in question?"

Arthur’s throat tightened. He remembered everything. He remembered the weight of the leather gloves, the damp wool of his coat, the specific creak of the left wheel that had plagued him for months. He remembered the heat of the summer that had broken into the autumn, the way the light had hung in the air, thick and golden, deceptive.

"I recall the date," Arthur said, his voice sounding foreign to his own ears, rough and unused. "October. The leaves were turning."

"And the accident. The child."

Arthur closed his eyes. In the darkness behind his lids, he saw the road. It was a stretch of gravel near the river, bordered by tall poplars that swayed in the wind. The Comte’s carriage was a sleek, black affair, the only respectable conveyance in a sea of mud and horse dung. He had been driving alone that day. The Comte was inside, reading a letter, his face pale and drawn, his fingers trembling slightly. Arthur had not looked back. He never looked back. It was against the rules of the road, a superstition of the trade, but more than that, it was a discipline. A coachman’s eyes were for the path ahead, for the obstacles, for the life of the horses.

But he had heard it. A shriek, thin and high, cut from the background of the wind. Then the thud. A wet, heavy sound, like a sack of grain dropping from a height.

"I was driving," Arthur said, opening his eyes. "I saw the boy."

Mr. Gumble leaned forward. "Did you see him?"

Arthur hesitated. This was where the loyalty fractured. The Comte de Valois had been a kind master, in his way. He gave good wages, decent food, and respect. He was a man of the old world, a man who believed in duty and hierarchy. But he was also a man who had sent his son to fight in the armies that were tearing France apart, a man who had looked at the rising smoke of the revolution and seen only chaos, not change. When the accident happened, the Comte had not screamed. He had not jumped from the carriage. He had simply closed his eyes and turned his face to the window. He had let the driver deal with it.

Arthur had stopped the horses. He had jumped down. The boy was lying in the ditch, a small thing, no older than eight, wearing a blue tunic that was now dark with blood. His eyes were open, staring at the sky, but they were not seeing it. They were seeing nothing. Arthur had knelt, his knees sinking into the cold mud, and he had tried to stop the bleeding. But the blood was warm and endless, and it soaked through his gloves, his hands, his soul.

"He was dead," Arthur whispered. "Or nearly so. I called for help. A farmer came from the field. We took him to the village."

"And the Comte?" Mr. Gumble asked. "What did he do?"

Arthur looked at the solicitor. "He stayed in the carriage."

The room seemed to shrink. The rain outside grew louder, a drumming that matched the pounding in Arthur’s chest.

"Did you tell him what had happened?"

"Yes. I told him the boy was dead."

"And his reaction?"

Arthur thought of the Comte’s face. It had not changed. It had remained calm, a mask of aristocratic composure that felt cruel in its indifference. "He asked if the carriage was damaged. He asked if the horses were frightened. He did not ask about the boy. He did not ask for his name."

Arthur had driven back to Paris that evening. He had not spoken of it to anyone. Not to the other coachmen, not to his wife, not to the priest. He had carried the weight of it in his spine, a physical ache that worsened when it rained, which was often. He had left France a year later, when the violence in the streets made the very air too thick to breathe. He had taken what little money he could save and sailed to England, where the language was familiar and the politics, though grim, were distant.

In London, he worked for a funeral director, driving hearses. It was quiet work. People slept in the back, or they were gone, and there was no one to ask if the carriage was damaged. He had told himself that he had done his duty. He had saved the boy’s life, or at least, he had been there. But the memory was a splinter that worked itself deeper with every passing year.

"Mr. Pendelton," Mr. Gumble said, his voice soft. "The family of the deceased child is seeking compensation. They claim that the driver failed to exercise reasonable care. They claim he was driving too fast."
