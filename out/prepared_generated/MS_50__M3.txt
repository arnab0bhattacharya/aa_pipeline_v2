It is a melancholy truth, engraven upon the very fibres of my heart, that the human soul, when plunged into the abyss of utter solitude, becomes at once its own tormentor and its own only refuge. A year had now elapsed since the great pestilence swept through our peaceful vales, leaving behind it, in its fearful course, the silence of the grave. I, Clara Vale, was the sole survivor in the village of Aynho, situated amidst the gentle eminences of that district once called the Cotswolds. The sweeping sickness had spared neither the young nor the old, and had borne away my father, my sister, and all the familiar faces that composed my world, leaving me to wander among the vacant dwellings like a restless ghost condemned to haunt the ruins of its former happiness.

The season was late in the twenty-first century, yet the face of nature had reverted to a wild and desolate majesty, unmarred by the futile triumphs of that modern science which had so signally failed to arrest the plague. I was standing at the latticed window of my solitary chamber, looking out upon the tangled overgrowth of the garden and the dark, impenetrable woods that encircled our desolate habitation. The evening was drawing near, and the clouds, heavy with the promise of a tempest, were gathering with a sullen and oppressive gloom over the western hills. My heart was a very void, and I gazed upon the approaching storm with a sort of desperate apathy, wishing that its fierce blasts might demolish the frail tenement of my body, and thus terminate a existence which seemed to possess no further end but to chronicle its own misery.

It was at this moment, while the first mutterings of the distant thunder rolled across the heavens, that a sound fell upon my ear—a sound so abrupt, so piercing, so utterly unlooked for, that it froze the very current of my blood. It was the solemn peal of a church bell. It came from the east, borne upon the sighing wind, from the neighbouring valley of Deepwater, where the ancient spire of St. Jude’s rose above the canopy of elms. For a whole year, that bell had hung motionless and mute in its tower, even as the hearts of its ringers had ceased to beat. My frame trembled violently, and I supported myself against the window-frame, lest I should sink to the earth. I listened with breathless agony. Again it tolled—slowly, heavily, as if striking the knell of some vast, unutterable sorrow, yet undeniably real, undeniably the work of human hands.

What conflicting emotions agitated my bosom during the few moments that succeeded this startling summons! A year of unbroken silence had taught me to believe myself the last faint ember of humanity in a world of ashes. To hear that sound was to awaken from a lethargy of despair, only to be plunged into a whirlpool of contending terrors. I was afraid—afraid of what I might find in that distant valley. Were they a band of wretched survivors, driven to madness by their sufferings, seeking whom they might devour in their despair? Or was it some cruel phantom of the storm, an aërial spirit mocking my loneliness? Yet, overpowering this terror was the far more dreadful dread of remaining behind. The thought of turning away, of resigning myself again to the blank, hopeless silence of Aynho, was insupportable. I felt that I must go, though I were going to my destruction; for any fate, even death, was preferable to the lingering torture of a solitude which had well-nigh destroyed my reason.

I hastily gathered around me such garments as the inclemency of the season demanded, and wrapping my cloak closely about my form, I descended into the lower apartment. As I passed the threshold of my father’s door, a pang of acute sorrow shot through my heart. I knelt and pressed my lips to the cold stone, breathing a silent and impassioned prayer to the spirit of him who had been my guide and protector. "Farewell," I murmured, while the tears flowed fast and scalding down my cheeks; "farewell, until that day when the grave shall render up its dead, and we shall meet, never more to part!"

I issued forth into the tempestuous air. The wind swept fiercely through the leafless branches of the elms, and the rain began to descend in heavy and dismal showers. The pathway that led towards the Deepwater valley was already choked with rank weeds and encroaching briars, the tokens of nature’s triumph over the depopulated works of man. As I toiled up the steep ascent which divided the two valleys, the darkness deepened around me, save when the vivid flashes of lightning illuminated the scene with a lurid and terrible splendour. Then might be seen the blasted ruins of ancient farmhouses, their roofs fallen in, their windows staring like hollow eyes from which the vital light had fled. The sublimity of the storm was above me, the desolation of the earth beneath; and I, a solitary worm, crawled amidst the ruins, the sport of the elements and the victim of destiny.

Yet I pressed onward, impelled by a supernatural strength. The bell still tolled at intervals, and its sound was to me as the voice of a guardian angel calling me away from the tomb of my despair. "I come! I come!" I cried aloud, lifting my voice above the howling of the tempest, as if the unknown ringer could hear my accents across the roaring void.

As I attained the summit of the ridge, a crash of thunder, more appalling than any I had yet heard, burst over my head. I clung to the gnarled trunk of a solitary oak, and as the dazzling brilliance of the lightning faded, a profound and solemn stillness seemed to fall upon the air. The rain ceased its pelting, and through the parting clouds a pale and watery moon shed her uncertain light upon the landscape beneath. I looked down into the valley of Deepwater. There, rising from the mist which lay thick upon the lowlands, was the ancient church, its tower pointing upwards to the sky.
