The rain had not ceased for three days. It lashed the grey stone of Thornwood Hall with a fury that seemed almost personal, as if the heavens themselves held a grievance against the ancient house and all who dwelt within its chill, high rooms. I stood at the window of the schoolroom, watching the bleak Warwickshire landscape dissolve into a mist of falling water, and felt the dampness of the old walls creep into my very spirit. I was a prisoner, not merely of the weather, but of my own station—an orphan, a governess, a creature of dependence, set to govern the ungovernable.

My charge, little Miss Cecily, was gone to her mother’s apartments for the afternoon, claimed by Lady Faversham for some feminine domesticity that did not require my presence. I was thus left to my own solitary devices, which in such a house and such a weather meant a profound and aching contemplation of my own insignificance.

I was roused from my reverie by a knock—sharp, imperative, and wholly unexpected. I turned. The door opened to reveal Mr. Leander Vance.

He was a cousin of the Favershams, a visitor of some weeks’ standing, and an artist by profession. The family treated him with that peculiar blend of indulgence and contempt which the English aristocracy reserves for those who, possessing birth, choose to labour. He was tall, dark-haired, with a face that was not handsome in the classical sense, but which drew the eye nonetheless by its sheer, restless intelligence. He had a trick of looking at one as though he were measuring not the face, but the soul behind it—a gaze I found disquieting, and which I had hitherto taken great pains to avoid.

“Miss March,” said he, entering without ceremony, his dark eyes sweeping the room before they came to rest on me. “I have sought you everywhere. The house is a labyrinth of damp corridors and bad temper, and I am in need of a subject.”

I stiffened. “I beg your pardon, Mr. Vance?”

“A subject. For my brush. I am at leisure, the weather confines me, and I find I cannot endure another hour of my cousin Augusta’s conversation. You, I perceive, are similarly marooned. Will you sit for me?”

I regarded him with the suspicion that was my default posture toward any kindness from those above me. “I do not think Lady Faversham would approve of my sitting idle, Mr. Vance.”

“Lady Faversham does not govern my actions, nor, I venture, yours—when you are off duty. Come, Miss March. I have a canvas prepared. I ask but an hour.”

I should have refused. I knew it. The proper response was a polite, firm declination, a retreat into the safe fortress of my own obscurity. But there was something in his manner—not flattery, for he did not flatter, but a kind of direct, commanding appeal—that disarmed me. Perhaps, too, I was simply tired of my own company.

“Very well,” said I, with what I hoped was cool indifference.

He led me to a small parlour on the upper floor, a room I had never entered, filled with the smell of turpentine and oil paint. A canvas sat upon an easel, and the light from a single tall window fell cold and clear across the space. He seated me upon a low chair, turned my face slightly toward the light, and began to mix his colours with a concentration that excluded all conversation.

For a long time, there was no sound but the scratch of his brush and the relentless drumming of rain against the glass. I sat rigidly, my hands folded, my eyes fixed on a point beyond his shoulder. I was acutely aware of his gaze—travelling from my face to the canvas and back again, studying me with an intensity that felt almost invasive.

At last he spoke, without looking up from his work. “You have a remarkable face, Miss March.”

The words fell into the silence like a stone into still water. I felt a flush rise to my cheeks, and I was irritated by it.

“That is not a compliment I am accustomed to receiving, Mr. Vance,” I said, “nor one I am sure I welcome.”

“It is not intended as a compliment. Merely an observation. You have a face that does not lie. The brow is too broad for conventional beauty, the jaw too firm; but the eyes—they are quite extraordinary. They hold a fire that one does not expect to find in a governess.”

I could not tell if he was mocking me. “Perhaps you mistake fatigue for fire,” I said coldly. “Governesses are not generally supposed to possess fire. We are expected to be mild, patient, and invisible.”

He paused, brush suspended, and looked at me fully. “And are you?”

“I am what circumstance has made me.”

“Which is to say, what you have allowed circumstance to make you. There is a difference.”

His presumption was staggering. I felt a surge of anger—hot, sudden, and unwelcome. “You know nothing of my circumstances, Mr. Vance.”

“I know that you are angry,” said he quietly, returning to his canvas. “I know that you feel the injustice of your position as keenly as a blade. I know that you sit in that chair, in that plain dress, with your hands folded, and that beneath that composed exterior there is a will of iron and a temper that would frighten half the household if it were ever let loose.”

I was shaken. No one had ever spoken to me thus—not Lady Faversham, not the servants, not even Cecily, who knew me best. I felt exposed, stripped bare, as though he had reached into my chest and laid hold of my heart. I wanted to rise, to leave, to tell him he had no right; but I sat, pinned by the truth of his words, and by something else—something I would not name.

“You are impertinent,” I whispered.
