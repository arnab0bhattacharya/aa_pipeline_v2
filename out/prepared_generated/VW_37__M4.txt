The attic had been hers by right of conquest—or of silence, which in the Ferrers household amounted to the same thing. She had climbed the stairs one April morning with a duster in her hand and a lie on her lips (that she meant to clear the lumber), and having cleared it, having dragged the horsehair trunk and the three-legged chair and the portrait of Great-aunt Someone-or-other down to the landing, she had simply stayed, and no one had come to fetch her, and the room had become hers the way a shell becomes the hermit crab’s—by occupation, by the slow exhalation of her own breath against its walls.

Clarissa Ferrers was twenty-four, and the family had decided. Not with cruelty—they were not cruel people; her mother wept when she spoke of it, and her father said “my dear” and looked at the fire—but with the weight of a thing long settled, a boulder that has lain so many years in a field that the grass has grown round it and the sheep have worn paths about it, and no one thinks to ask whether it fell there or was placed. Mr. Ashworth, who came on Thursdays and taught her to mix ochres, had been kind too, which was worse. He had taken the brush from her hand—gently, gently—and said: “You have feeling, Miss Ferrers. Feeling is not enough.” And she had watched his grey head go down the stairs and heard the front door close, and had gone up to the attic and stood before the canvas she had been at work on for a month, and had thought: feeling is not enough, and had begun, that very afternoon, the painting of the jars.

For it was jars she painted now. She had discovered them—or they had discovered her—in the second summer, when the light in the attic turned the colour of weak tea at four o’clock and she had set down a blue-and-white jug and a green glass bottle on the windowsill because she had nothing else to look at, and had seen, all at once, that the light did not fall upon them but through them, that the bottle held within its green throat a small green world, and that the shadow of the jug on the sill was not grey but violet, unmistakably violet, and that nobody in the world was watching her see it. That was the thing. Nobody was watching. The house below hummed with its business—the kettle, the callers, the letters on the salver—and here in the roof the dust went up and down in the long bars of sun like a slow breathing, and she was alone with the violet shadow, and she was happy, and she thought, if this is not talent, then talent is a word made up by Mr. Ashworth to keep the world tidy.

She painted the jars, and then the window itself, and then the corner of the room where the light gave out and the shadows went blue and bottomless; and she painted the mornings when the fog came up from London and pressed its grey face to the glass, so that the jug floated in a nothing, a softness, and the whole attic became a place under the sea. She painted the afternoons of a heat that made the leaves hang like washing, and the evenings when the sun went down behind the chimneys of the next house and set the whole sky to a boiling of copper and rose, and the jars stood black against it, patient, substantial, indifferent, having outlasted her father and her father’s father and meaning to outlast her too. She was not deceived. She knew—the way one knows the shape of one’s own hand in the dark—that no one would ever see them. They stood along the wall with their faces to the plaster, or leaned in the corner under the sheet, and grew dusty, and she dusted them, and turned them, and sometimes at night, when the house was asleep, she came up with a candle and looked at them one by one, as a woman might look at her sleeping children, and thought: there, that is the fog as it was on the fourteenth of November, and there is the green bottle with the world in its throat, and nobody knows, and it does not matter that nobody knows, and it matters so much that nobody knows that I could put my fist through the plaster.

The marriage was to be Mr. Ellison, who was in tea, and who had a house at Highgate with a view, and who had said to her, at the piano, while her sister played, that he thought a woman’s true sphere was the home, and had looked so pleased with the thought, so warmed by it, that she had not had the heart—or had she had the courage?—to ask him what he supposed she had been doing all these years in the attic. He would have said, kindly, that she had been amusing herself. And perhaps, she thought, standing at the window with a brush in her hand, perhaps that is all it is, and the violet shadow is only a shadow, and the green world in the bottle is only glass, and Mr. Ashworth was right, and mother is right, and the boulder has lain in the field so long that it is part of the field. And then the light would move, one degree, as it does, and the shadow on the sill would deepen from violet to a blue that had no name, and she would think: no. No. I have seen this. I have seen this and no one else has seen it, and that is a thing that cannot be taken from me, not by Mr. Ellison, not by Highgate, not by the whole warm humming house below with its kettles and its salvers and its view.
