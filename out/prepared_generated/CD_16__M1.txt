In that labyrinthine and much-distrusted quarter of London which is known as Soho, where the French tongue is heard more frequently than the English, and where the very houses seem to huddle together for warmth and mutual consolation against the chill of a foreign sky, there stood a certain house in a certain court, which was known to the postmen by the name of a certain Mrs. Pettigrew. This Mrs. Pettigrew was the landlady, and she was a woman of such profound and comprehensive suspicion that she regarded the very sunshine with distrust, as if it were an untrustworthy agent of some foreign power, and would not be surprised to find a conspiracy brewing in the steam of her own kettle.

In the attics of this house, and in the hearts of its inhabitants, lived Monsieur Armand de Valois, a dancing-master of the old school, who had fled the blood-stained streets of Paris with nothing but his honour, his wardrobe, and a capacity for the minuet which was, in his own estimation, second to none in Europe. Monsieur de Valois was a small man, with a large forehead and a face that was perpetually poised between a smile and a sigh. He wore his hair powdered, though powder was a scarcity, and he moved through the narrow rooms of his lodgings with a grace that made the crooked floorboards seem like the parquet of Versailles. He taught the daughters of the English merchants to glide and to curtsey, and he instructed their sons in the noble art of the bow, though the boys, being English, and being boys, generally preferred to wrestle in the gutters.

It was a wet Tuesday in November—a day when the fog of London had descended upon the city like a wet woollen shroud, and the rain fell not in drops but in a continuous, grey, soul-wearying wash—when the peace of the house was shattered by a knock at the door. It was not the knock of the postman, nor the knock of the baker, nor yet the timid tap of a pupil arriving for a lesson in the gavotte. It was a knock that was at once imperious and furtive, a knock that seemed to say, 'I come from a place of secrets, and I demand entry!'

Mrs. Pettigrew, who was at that moment engaged in the intricate and highly moral task of mending a stocking with a needle that seemed to have a will of its own, paused in her labours. She looked at the door as if it had personally offended her. She rose, smoothed her apron, and advanced upon the portal with the air of a general preparing to repel an invasion.

'Who is it?' she demanded, her voice sharp enough to cut glass. 'We want no hawkers, no peddlers, and no revolutionaries!'

'Madame,' came a voice from the other side, a voice thick with the accent of the Midi and the dust of the road, 'it is I, Gaston. Gaston Dubois. I have news for Monsieur de Valois. News of the heart!'

Mrs. Pettigrew opened the door a crack, and a man squeezed himself through the aperture like a cork from a bottle. He was a man of a singular and unwholesome appearance. His clothes were of a French cut, but they were travel-stained and frayed at the edges. He wore a hat that was too large for him, and his eyes, which were of a pale, watery blue, darted about the hallway with the restless energy of a ferret in a poultry-yard.

'I have come,' said Monsieur Dubois, bowing low and nearly toppling over, 'from our native soil. From the village of Saint-Étienne-du-Grès. I bring the scent of the lavender and the warmth of the sun!'

'We have no lavender here,' said Mrs. Pettigrew, blocking his path with her substantial person. 'And the only warmth is from the coal, which costs money. What is your business with Monsieur de Valois?'

At this moment, the dancing-master himself appeared at the top of the stairs. He had heard the commotion, and he descended with the slow, dignified steps of a man who knows that gravity is a force to be respected.

'Gaston?' he cried, his hands flying to his breast. 'Gaston Dubois? Is it possible? Can it be you?'

'Mon ami!' cried the visitor, rushing forward and embracing the smaller man with a fervour that threatened to crush him. 'Armand! My brother in exile!'

They stood there for a moment, locked in an embrace that was a study in contrasts: the neat, powdered elegance of the master and the rough, desperate energy of the visitor. Mrs. Pettigrew watched them with narrowed eyes, her lips pursed as if she had just tasted a lemon that had gone bad.

'Come, come,' said Monsieur de Valois, recovering his breath and his dignity. 'We must talk. We must have wine. We must have news!'

They ascended to the drawing-room, which was also the dining-room, and the bedroom, and the kitchen, in the way of London lodgings. Mrs. Pettigrew followed them, ostensibly to fetch the wine, but actually to ensure that the visitor did not make off with the silver spoons, which were, in fact, pewter, but which she valued as if they were the crown jewels of France.

'And so,' said Monsieur de Valois, pouring out a meagre glass of claret, 'you have come from the village? And how is my mother? And how is my brother, Philippe? Is he still... is he still in that place?'

The visitor, Monsieur Dubois, looked at the floor. He looked at the ceiling. He looked at the rain streaking down the windowpane. He sighed, a long, theatrical sigh that seemed to come from the very depths of his boots.

'Philippe,' he said, his voice dropping to a whisper, 'is a prisoner. A prisoner of the Republic. They have him in the Conciergerie. He is accused of... of being a friend of the aristocrats.'
