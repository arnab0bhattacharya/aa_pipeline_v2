There are some passages in a boy’s life which stand apart from the common traffic of his days, marked off by a darkness and a solitude all their own; and my first week aboard the collier brig *Sorrowful* was one such passage. I, Timothy Pettigrew, aged twelve years and three months, a boy who had never been further from home than the distance a runaway dog might cover in a morning, found myself a cabin boy upon that floating mountain of coal and misery, with the salt-spray in my eyes and a grief in my heart as black and heavy as the cargo in her hold. My father, a man of the ledgers and the quiet lamplight, had been taken by a fever; my mother, a wisp of a woman rendered yet more insubstantial by her loss, had put me aboard with a shilling and a handkerchief that wept with her, and so the great world of the sea, which I had only ever seen as a grey smudge on the horizon, had swallowed me whole.

The *Sorrowful* was a vessel that seemed to have been built in a bad temper and launched in a worse one. Her timbers groaned as she moved, a constant, querulous complaint against the waves that buffeted her. Her deck was a dark, gritty purgatory of coal-dust and damp, where every rope and pulley seemed to exist for the sole purpose of tripping the unwary and dashing the incompetent. And her crew—oh, her crew! They were a company of men as rough and weather-beaten as the ship herself, men who spoke in grunts and spits, and who looked upon my small, sorrowing person as they might upon a curious barnacle that had attached itself to their hull. The mate, a Mr. Grimshaw, a man with a face like a clenched fist, made it his daily business to see how many knots I could tie in a minute and how many oaths he could tie to my name. The boys of the crew, hardened apprentices of the sea, found in my shyness and my clumsy hands an inexhaustible source of sport, tripping me into scuppers and hiding my tin mug. I was a ghost among them, invisible in my misery, save when I was being made to feel it more keenly.

It was in this floating, salt-sprayed wilderness of misery that I first beheld the cook, Mr. Silas Croft. He was not a man so much as a geographical feature, a mountain of a creature who had to stoop to pass through the galley door. He had the shoulders of a bullock and hands like a pair of weathered hams, and where his left eye should have been, there was not a smooth, sailor’s patch, but a puckered, angry-looking hollow, as if the eye had been dug out with a marlinspike in a fit of pique. His remaining eye, a small, sharp, blue thing, was set deep in a face as craggy as a cliff-face, and it missed nothing. He had a temper that could curdle the milk from a hundred miles away, a roar that could make the very waves flinch, and a tongue that could strip the paint from a barnacle at forty paces. The other sailors gave him the widest of berths; he was a tyrant in his little kingdom of the galley, a despot of the duff and the scouse, and all aboard feared his terrible wrath.

My first real encounter with him was, as might be expected, one of terror. I had been sent below for a bucket of water, and in my haste to avoid the jeering of the deck-hands, I stumbled over the raised coaming and sent the empty bucket clattering across the galley floor. The great figure, who had been stirring a vast cauldron of something brown and bubbling, froze. He turned his head slowly, that single, terrible blue eye fixing upon me with the force of a pistol-shot. I expected a torrent of abuse that would scour the very soul from my body. He took one step towards me, and his shadow fell over me like a night without stars. He bent down—his joints cracking like pistol-shots—and picked up the bucket. Then, in a voice not of thunder, but of a low, grinding rumble, like stones shifting in the deep, he spoke.

“You’ve the look of a half-drowned kitten, boy,” he said. “What’s the matter with you? Speak up, or I’ll shake it out of you.”

I could only stammer, my throat as dry as a biscuit. “N-nothing, sir. I am sorry, sir.”

He squinted his one eye at me, a look of profound dissatisfaction on his face. “Sorry? Sorry’s no good to me. I asked a question. The sea don’t care for your sorries. The wind don’t stop blowing because you’re sorry. I want to know what’s the matter. Is it the food? It’s better than what you’ll get in the workhouse. Is it the work? It’s work, boy, and it’s what keeps you from thinking. So what is it?”

And in that moment, in the steamy, greasy air of that galley, with the ship groaning around me, a strange thing happened. No one had asked me that question since my mother had pressed her handkerchief into my hand. No one on this floating world of indifference had looked at me and seen anything other than a nuisance, a weakling, a boy to be tormented. This giant, this one-eyed ogre with a voice like a landslide, had asked me what was the matter.

And so, with a great, shuddering sob that seemed to tear itself from my very entrails, I told him. I told him of my father, and the quiet house, and the ledgers, and the terrible silence he had left behind. I told him of my mother’s tears and the shilling in my pocket that felt like a stone. I spoke in a torrent of grief and fear, and the words, once they began, would not stop. Mr. Croft stood and listened, his great arms folded across his chest, his face unreadable. When I had finished, he was silent for a long moment, and the only sounds were the bubbling of the pot and the creaking of the ship.
