The season in London, as is its habit, had commenced with a display of brilliance that was both dazzling and distinctly expensive. Mr. Arthur Penrose, a gentleman of comfortable but not ostentatious means, found himself residing in a modest lodgings near the Piazza, owing to the hospitality of his cousin, Mrs. Gwendolen Fane. Mrs. Fane was a woman of considerable consequence in the society of St. James’s, possessing a fortune that allowed her to be indifferent to the price of silk, and a temperament that allowed her to be indifferent to the comfort of her guests.

It was at one of Mrs. Fane’s many gatherings, where the air was thick with the scent of orange blossom and the smoke of fashionable vices, that Mr. Penrose first encountered Miss Eliza Vane. She was seated in a window, engaged in a conversation with a gentleman of no particular distinction, her eyes fixed on the street below with an air of dignified detachment. Mr. Penrose, who had been admiring the arrangement of the room’s chandeliers, felt his attention suddenly diverted. There was something in the composure of her bearing, the quiet grace with which she turned her head when spoken to, that arrested him with a force he had not anticipated.

"She is Miss Vane," Mrs. Fane whispered, appearing at his elbow with a smile that suggested a shared secret. "Her father has recently returned from the East Indies. They speak of a very considerable settlement. I do not think she is quite so handsome as my niece, but she has an air of the most perfect breeding."

Mr. Penrose, who had been considering the prospect of a quiet country retirement in his native Sussex, found that the thought of Miss Vane’s breeding was suddenly of far greater interest than the soil of his ancestral home. He approached her, and after an exchange of pleasantries that were conducted with the mutual politeness of two people who are each trying to determine the other’s worth, they found themselves engaged in a conversation that lasted until the arrival of the quadrille music.

Miss Vane, he noted, possessed a mind that was quick and sensible, though she did not suffer herself to be too readily understood. She spoke of the changes in the taste for drawing rooms, and of the decline in the number of genuine scholars among the younger men. "It is a pity," she said, glancing at him with a slight arch of her eyebrow, "that so many gentlemen seem to value a carriage more highly than a library. One can always purchase a chaise, but wit is not to be bought at the coach-builder’s."

Mr. Penrose, who owned a small but select collection of classical texts, felt a surge of pride. He replied that he valued the company of a sensible mind above any material acquisition, and that he found the pursuits of the town often insufficient to occupy a spirit of any refinement. Miss Vane’s eyes brightened. "You speak like a man of real education," she said, with a warmth that was entirely unexpected. "I had begun to despair of finding any such in this circle. I am told you are of a family of some standing?"

"Standing is a relative term," Mr. Penrose replied, with a smile that he intended to be modest but which he feared might appear evasive. "We are of an old county family, though we have never been particularly noted for our wealth. We have always preferred the simplicity of the country life to the expense of the town."

Miss Vane nodded, her expression one of agreeable surprise. "And yet you are here in the height of the season. I had not imagined that a man of your station would find it necessary to maintain a residence in London. I am told that Mr. Penrose is one of the most eligible bachelors in the county. There is talk of a large inheritance having come to him from an uncle."

Mr. Penrose felt a sudden chill. The rumor of an inheritance was a fabrication that had been circulated by a well-meaning but gossipy aunt, who believed that a little financial puffery would help her nephew in the marriage market. He had not corrected the error, assuming it was harmless. "The accounts are often exaggerated," he said, keeping his voice steady. "My uncle was a kind man, but his estate was encumbered. I have been able to maintain my present comforts, but I am far from being the wealthy figure that the town imagines."

Miss Vane looked at him with an expression of such frank disappointment that he was momentarily stung. "I see," she said softly. "I had hoped... I had believed that your position was more secure. I am afraid that I have been a fool, assuming that a man of your manners and taste would be supported by a fortune. I am myself in a rather precarious situation. My father’s health is failing, and the settlement, which I had thought to be substantial, is actually quite modest. I am not the heiress of which the town speaks. I am merely a girl of small means, trying to make the best of a difficult situation."

The room seemed to spin slightly. Mr. Penrose stood there, the music of the quadrille rising and falling around him, while the truth of their respective situations lay between them like a wall of glass. They had both been mistaken. He had loved the idea of her independence, which he had imagined was backed by a fortune; she had admired his sense, which she had imagined was backed by a state. In reality, they were both young people of moderate means, struggling to navigate a world that valued only gold and status.

For a long moment, neither spoke. The silence was not uncomfortable, but rather a space in which the pretenses of the season fell away. Mrs. Fane, who had been observing them with the sharp eye of a woman who calculated the value of every alliance, saw the change in their demeanor and moved on to another guest, leaving them to their own thoughts.
