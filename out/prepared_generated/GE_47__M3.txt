It is in the nature of youthful sorrow to believe itself unique, and perhaps singularly ill-used, in the vast and indifferent economy of the world; for the young, having but recently emerged from the intensely personal atmosphere of childhood, cannot yet conceive that their particular grief is but a drop in the great, brimming vial of human suffering. So it was with Lettice Faringdon when, in the hard winter of 1824, she was transported from the bustling market-town of her birth to the precincts of Thurwell, a cathedral town in the Midland shires, there to keep house for her bachelor uncle. Her father, a man of speculative habits and insufficient caution, had recently plunged from the precarious heights of mercantile ambition into the quiet, irrevocable depths of insolvency; and the immediate consequence of this submersion was that the family fabric, hitherto held together by a threadbare gentility, was rent entirely asunder. Lettice, being sixteen and possessed of a tidy intelligence, was deemed the proper instrument to be dispatched to Mr. Jeremiah Faringdon, a lawyer of some local repute, whose practice lay chiefly in the drafting of settlements and the untangling of trust properties. Jeremiah was a man whom the world had long agreed to call "dry," a man whose countenance seemed built of the same pale, unyielding stone as the cathedral close, and whose speech was as sparing and measured as if he were billing his auditors for every syllable.

To Lettice, therefore, approaching this new life with the despondency of a prisoner entering a cell, the prospect was one of unmitigated gloom. She had left behind her a mother broken by circumstance, a father shorn of his sanguine pretensions, and the lively chatter of younger siblings, to enter a household where the very ticking of the long-case clock in the hall seemed to emphasize the silent desolation. Her uncle’s house was a tall, narrow structure of dark brick, hemmed in by ancient yews that pressed their funereal branches against the diamond-paned windows. Within, the furniture was of a respectable but somber age, and the air of the parlor smelled faintly of parchment and sealing-wax. Mr. Faringdon, who received her with a brief, mechanical nod, appeared to her as a creature entirely devoid of those tender infirmities which constitute the warmth of human fellowship. He was a man of perhaps five-and-fifty, with gray, closely shaven cheeks, thin lips that seemed habitually closed against any superfluous utterance, and pale blue eyes that looked out from under heavy brows with a cold, penetrating scrutiny. To her youthful imagination, he was less a man than a human legal instrument, a thing of clauses and provisos, entirely fortified against the inroads of sentiment.

The first weeks of her residence were passed in a dreary routine. Jeremiah departed for his chambers at half-past eight, returned to a dinner of boiled mutton at two, and spent his evenings in the parlor, reading the provincial newspaper or poring over briefs with a candle shaded by a green tin reflector. Lettice, sitting opposite him with her needlework, would sometimes venture a timid remark, only to be met with a grave, non-committal assent that froze the conversation at its very birth. She concluded that he was utterly indifferent to her, and that her lot was to be one of perpetual, silent servitude. Yet the slow, insensible growth of acquaintance is a process that often defeats our most confident anticipations; and Lettice, without knowing it, began to observe her uncle with a curiosity that was the first step toward sympathy. She noticed that he had a peculiar habit of rubbing his right thumb against his forefinger when he came to a difficult passage in his newspaper, and that he ate his mutton with a methodical precision which suggested a mind intolerant of disorder. She observed, too, that his eyes, when raised from his papers, sometimes rested on the fire with a look of intense, though repressed, abstraction, as if some inward drama were being enacted behind that stony exterior.

It was on a sharp January evening, when the frost had cast its white net over the close, that the first fissure appeared in her uncle’s defensive armor. Jeremiah had been detained at his chambers longer than usual, and Lettice, having finished her household accounts, entered his study to lay a fresh fire. The room was empty, but the desk lamp threw a broad, yellow glare across the scattered papers, and she perceived that one of the deep drawers, usually locked, had been left slightly ajar. Among the stiff, legal documents that protruded from its edge, a small, shabby volume of poetry lay half-concealed. It was an edition of Wordsworth, worn to a frayed softness at the edges, with a pencil lying between the leaves. Lettice, with the instinctive reverence of a young mind for a secret, did not touch the book, but the revelation that her uncle, the dry and silent lawyer, should read such poetry in secret, struck her with a force that entirely altered her conception of him. It was as if she had unexpectedly seen a wild flower blooming in a desert of petrified clay.

The discovery did not immediately transform their intercourse, for Jeremiah was not a man to be approached with sudden familiarity; but it planted in Lettice’s mind a gentle, enduring patience, a readiness to interpret his silences with a more charitable imagination. She began to leave small offerings on his desk—a sprig of late winter jasmine from the garden, or a newly ironed handkerchief folded with a care that exceeded the demands of mere duty—and though he never spoke of these trifles, she sometimes found them placed with a lingering affection in his private drawer. The winter wore on, and the slow thaw of their mutual reserve was as imperceptible as the melting of the frost. It was not until a Sunday in late February, when the dusk was gathering in the cathedral close and the bells were ringing for evensong, that the final barrier was lifted.
