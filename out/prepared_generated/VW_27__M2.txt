The light was not the light of the office, with its harsh, humming tubes that seemed to dissect the air into sterile slices, but a soft, greyish gold that lay upon the gravestones like a shroud. Margaret sat on the cold stone bench, her knees drawn up, holding the sandwich in her hand as if it were a fragile bird, and felt the weight of the city lift from her shoulders. It was a strange sensation, this sudden silence, broken only by the cooing of the pigeons, a low, rhythmic murmuring that seemed to come from the very earth. She watched them, those iridescent heads turning, the iridescent necks flashing purple and green in the dull afternoon sun. They moved with such purpose, such absolute indifference to the hands that had thrown the crumbs, that Margaret felt a pang of envy. To be a pigeon. To live only in the moment of the grain, the wing, the stone.

She took a bite of the ham, the mustard sharp on her tongue, and chewed slowly. The flavor was simple, unadorned, just as the day had been. She thought of the letters she had typed, the endless columns of figures, the *click-clack* of the keys that had echoed in her mind all morning. And now, here, in the shadow of the church, with its spire piercing the low clouds, the world seemed to expand. The clerks were passing by in their dark suits, their faces set in expressions of grim determination, their feet striking the pavement in a steady, monotonous beat. They were going to their meetings, their dinners, their lives, and Margaret was here, suspended in a pocket of time that belonged to no one but her.

Who was he, this young man, Thomas? She could not quite recall the name. It had come to her in a whisper, amidst the clatter of the typewriter, a voice that seemed to come from a distance, from a dream she had not yet fully woken from. *The theatre, Margaret. I should like you to come to the theatre.* The words had hung in the air, delicate as spiderwebs, and she had accepted, not out of desire, but out of a sudden, inexplicable curiosity. To see him again. To see if he was real. For in the office, he had been a shadow, a blur of grey flannel and quiet eyes, but in the churchyard, with the sun warming her face, he seemed to take on a solidity that was both comforting and terrifying.

She looked up at the sky. It was a pale, washed-out blue, streaked with wisps of cloud that drifted lazily, like thoughts refusing to settle. An old man sat on the bench opposite, his hands folded in his lap, his eyes closed. He looked as if he were waiting for something, or perhaps simply resting, letting the world pass him by. Margaret wondered what he saw in his sleep. Did he see the office, the endless rows of desks? Or did he see the sea, the wide, open sea, where the light was different, where the air tasted of salt and freedom? She, too, had seen the sea, once, in a photograph in a book, and the memory of it was a bright, sharp splinter in her mind, a reminder of a life she had not lived, a life of vastness and silence.

The pigeons began to gather around her feet, their eyes bright and intelligent, watching her every move. She held out a crumb, and one of them, a grey one with a streak of white on its breast, hopped forward and pecked at it. The movement was quick, precise, and Margaret felt a sudden connection, a thread of sympathy that bound her to this small, living creature. They were both trapped, she and the pigeon, in this circle of stone and shadow, waiting for the next moment, the next grain, the next word.

Thomas had said he would pick her up at six. Six o’clock. The time seemed distant, yet immediate, a horizon that was both approaching and receding. She wondered what he would say, what he would do. Would he look at her with those quiet eyes, or would he look through her, as if she were a glass wall, transparent and insubstantial? She had told herself that it did not matter. That it was simply an evening, a change of scene, a break in the monotony. But as she sat there, watching the light change, the shadows lengthen, she felt that it mattered more than she had ever admitted. That in going to the theatre, she was stepping out of her life, into a life that was not yet defined, not yet known.

The old man opened his eyes and looked at her. His gaze was mild, almost apologetic, as if he were surprised to find her there. He nodded, a small, slow movement, and then closed his eyes again. Margaret felt a surge of emotion, a swelling of the heart that was difficult to name. It was not happiness, exactly, nor sadness, but something in between, a recognition of the shared fragility of their existence. They were all, she thought, sitting in this churchyard, waiting for the light to go, waiting for the night to come, waiting for the next thing to happen.

She finished the sandwich, wiping the crumbs from her fingers onto the stone. The taste of the ham lingered, a faint, salty memory. She looked at the clock on the church tower. Five o’clock. An hour. An hour to be alone, to be no one, to be a woman sitting on a bench in a city churchyard, watching the pigeons. It was a strange freedom, this freedom of the in-between, of the pause. It was in these moments, she realized, that she was most herself, most present, most alive. The office, the letters, the clerks, the young man—they were all part of the fabric of her life, but they did not define it. It was the light, the silence, the cooing of the pigeons, the weight of the stone beneath her, that defined her.
