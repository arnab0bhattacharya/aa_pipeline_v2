The rain had fallen for three days without remission, and the whole valley of Millbeck seemed to have taken the colour of the mill-stone itself: a grey, sodden, comfortless hue, in which the roofs of the cottages, the bare boughs of the alders, and the turbid water that hurried down the goit were all confounded together. From the windows of the house I had married into—a house of good ashlar, standing somewhat apart upon the rise, with laurels about it that dripped and glistened—I could see the great mill, and the tall chimney that never ceased to vomit its slow coil of smoke into the low, leaden sky; and I could see, likewise, the figures of the hands going and coming, with shawls drawn over their heads, and clogs that struck a dull, wet sound from the paving.

My husband was at the mill, as he was ever. He had his ledgers, his orders, his calculations of the market; and he had, besides, a manner of late so coldly civil toward me, so measured and so distant, that I had come to feel myself less a wife than a piece of furniture of superior quality, set in the best room and expected to remain there without offence. At table he spoke of cotton and of the war, and of the price of bread; but of me he asked nothing, save whether I had ordered the carriage, or whether the servants were paid. I believe he thought he had done handsomely by me: he had taken me from a small house in a dull town, and given me a large one in a duller valley; he had clothed me richly, and fed me well, and asked in return only that I should be silent and content. But the heart will not be fed with bread alone, and mine had begun to grow fierce within me for want of something to lay hold upon.

It was in this humour that I first went down among the cottages. I told myself I did it from duty; I know now that I did it from hunger. I took with me a basket of such things as I thought might serve—a little wine, some arrowroot, a few warm stuffs cut and sewn by my own hands—and I went from door to door, and was received with curtsies that were too deep, and thanks that were too loud, and eyes that watched me from behind half-closed shutters as I came away. There was no love in it. They took my gifts as they would have taken a tax, and they spoke of me as “the mistress,” with a certain edge upon the word that I could not mistake.

One cottage there was, at the very end of the row, standing where the lane turned and the alders closed in, that I had not yet entered. A woman lay there, I had heard, who was long sick and like to die—a woman named Nelly Hartley, the widow of a weaver who had been killed some years since by the fall of a beam in the mill. Her daughter, a girl of sixteen or thereabouts, worked in the carding-room, and kept her mother as best she could. Thither I went one afternoon, when the rain had thinned to a soft, persistent drizzle, and knocked at the door with a strange beating at my heart, for I had a fancy that this woman would not be like the others.

The door was opened by the girl, who stood in the entry and looked at me without a word. She had her mother’s eyes, I afterward learned, but just then I knew only that they were dark and steady, and that there was no curtsy in her.

“I am come to see your mother,” said I. “I have heard she is ill.”

“She is ill,” said the girl. “But she will not see you.”

“Not see me? Why should she not see me? I bring her no harm.”

“She will not see you,” the girl repeated, and made as if to shut the door.

I put my hand against it—I, who had never in my life forced myself upon any creature—and I felt the colour rise into my face, and a heat at my heart that was half anger and half shame.

“Tell her,” said I, “that I am the mistress of the mill, and that I am come to do her a kindness.”

The girl looked at me a moment longer, and in her look there was something that made me shrink; it was not insolence, it was a kind of pity, as though she saw me more plainly than I saw myself.

“She knows who you are,” said she. “That is why she will not see you.”

Then she shut the door, and I stood in the wet lane with my basket on my arm, and the rain upon my face, and felt as though the whole world had turned against me. I could have wept; I could have struck the door; I could have gone home and sworn never to set foot in the lane again. But I did none of these things. I stood still, and I thought. Why should Nelly Hartley, who lay dying in a poor cottage, refuse the sight of a woman who came only to help her? What had I done to her? What had my husband done? And there came into my mind, like a cold wind, the recollection of the beam that had fallen, and of the jury that had brought in a verdict of accident, and of the few pounds that had been paid to the widow, and of the new machinery that had been put up in the place where the old had been.

I went home, and I asked my husband. I asked him at the supper-table, when the servant had left the room, and he was sitting with his glass of port before him and his eyes upon the fire.
