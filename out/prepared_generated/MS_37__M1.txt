The hour was late, and a chill wind from the river moved through the naked branches of the plane trees, rattling the casement of my study like the importunate hand of a beggar. It was in the winter of the year 1815, when the great world had at last laid down its arms, that I found myself engaged in a war of a different and more terrible nature—a war waged within the citadel of my own mind. For three years I had laboured in solitude, nurturing the fire of inspiration which I believed burned upon my altar, and which I fondly imagined would one day illuminate the world. I had written of the passions of men, of the sublime indifference of nature, and of the agony of the soul stretched upon the rack of existence. Yet, until that evening, I had sought no judgement but my own.

My sister, Clara, whose gentleness was the only balm to my restless spirit, had often urged me to submit my verses to the judgement of the world. "You write as one inspired," she would say, her voice trembling with that earnest sensibility which rendered her the guardian of my hopes; "but the world is blind, and needs the voice of authority to guide it to the light." Thus, with a heart that alternated between the arrogance of youth and the terror of failure, I had procured an audience with Mr. Alistair Vane, that titan of letters whose pen was said to weigh like iron upon the reputations of men, and whose praise was the only currency that could purchase immortality.

I found him in his library, a cavern of books that smelled of dust and dry leather, where the only light came from a dying fire that cast long, distorted shadows against the spines of forgotten authors. Mr. Vane was a man of severe aspect, his face a mask of erudition and indifference. He received my manuscript—a sheaf of papers on which I had expended the very essence of my being—with the air of a physician receiving a plague-ridden garment. He did not read them then; he merely held them, weighed them in his hand as if judging their physical substance rather than their spiritual worth, and bade me return in a sennight.

That sennight was an age of purgatory. I paced the floor of my room until the carpet was worn beneath my feet; I started at every sound, fancying it the footfall of the postman who would bring my doom or my salvation. Clara watched me with silent, terrified eyes, her own happiness entirely subordinated to my fluctuating moods. She would bring me wine, or place a hand upon my arm, and in her silent way, she prayed for that success which I desired with a frantic, devouring hunger. When the appointed day arrived, I returned to the house of the critic.

"Sit, young man," said Mr. Vane, pointing to a chair opposite his own. The fire had burned lower, and the room was colder than before. He did not look at me, but stared into the embers. "I have read your work," he began, his voice dry and devoid of all emotion. "It is the outpouring of a mind that mistakes the heat of the blood for the fire of genius. You have written of storms and ruins, of love and death, yet you possess no experience of these things. You are an echo, sir, not a voice. You mimic the great masters with the fidelity of a parrot, but you add nothing to the sum of human understanding."

I felt the blood drain from my face; the room seemed to swim before my eyes. I opened my mouth to speak, to protest, to defend the sacred truth of my feelings, but he raised a hand to silence me.

"Do not speak," he commanded. "I have seen a thousand such youths as you, burning with a false fever, who consume the paper and the ink of the booksellers to no purpose. You have no merit. Your verses are devoid of structure, devoid of originality, and devoid of soul. I advise you, for the sake of your sanity and the peace of your family, to cast these follies into the fire. Seek a profession; learn a trade; become a man of science, if you must pursue knowledge, for the realm of Poetry is closed to you forever."

He returned the manuscript to me. I took it with a trembling hand, and I fled from that house as from a tomb. The walk home was a blur of agony. The lights of London, which had once seemed to me the jewels of a magnificent stage, now appeared as the mocking eyes of demons. The wind howled in the streets, whipping my face with rain, but I felt nothing save the cold desolation that had settled upon my heart. I had built a temple in the clouds, and the thunder had struck it down, leaving me standing in the ruin of my own ambition.

When I entered our lodgings, the sight of Clara’s anxious face was more than I could bear. She rushed to me, her eyes wide with apprehension. "Brother! Tell me! What says the critic? Do not keep me in suspense, I implore you!"

I threw the manuscript upon the table. "It is finished," I cried, my voice breaking with a passion that bordered on madness. "He has condemned me. He says I have no soul, no genius, no merit. He bids me cast these follies into the fire, and I shall obey him. I am not a poet; I am a fool, a dreamer who has wasted the prime of his youth upon shadows. From this hour, I renounce the Muses. I will become a man of science. I will study the anatomy of the earth, the laws of the stars, the mechanics of matter—anything but this treacherous art that offers hope only to dash it into the dust!"
