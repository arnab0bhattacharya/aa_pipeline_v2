The rain fell in sheets on the slate roofs of Ashgrove, a relentless, icy hammering that turned the village lanes into rivers of mud and shadow. It was a miserable November night in 1812, and the air smelled of coal smoke, wet wool, and the metallic tang of impending conflict. I sat in the corner of the inn’s common room, my boots unlaced, staring at the fire that struggled against the dampness of the hearth. The militia had been billeted here for three weeks, ostensibly to keep the peace, but in truth, we were merely occupying a powder keg. The weavers, stripped of their trade by the new power looms, were a hollow-eyed, furious populace. They did not riot with the wild abandon of London mobs; they seethed with a cold, mechanical precision, their looms silent in the factories, their eyes fixed on us with a hatred that was personal, visceral, and absolute.

"Captain Thorne," a voice rasped from the doorway. It was Major Hargrave, his face grim as a storm cloud. "The Colonel has issued the order. You are to go to the cottage on the hill. Arrest Elias Vane. He is accused of sedition, of plotting to burn the mill."

I looked up, my heart sinking. Elias Vane. I knew the name. He was a man of few words, a weaver whose fingers were calloused to stone, whose back was bent by years of hunching over a shuttle. But I also knew of his daughter, Eliza.

"Is it true?" I asked, my voice low. "Did he speak against the King? Against the law?"

Hargrave’s lip curled. "He spoke against the men who own the mills, Captain. And that is treason in their eyes. You have your orders. Go. Do it quietly if you can, but do it."

I stood, my legs stiff from the damp. "I will go."

The walk to the hill was a torment. The wind howled through the bare branches of the ash trees, sounding like the wailing of the damned. The cottages were dark, the windows shuttered tight against the storm. When I reached the Vane cottage, it was a small, huddled thing, barely visible in the gloom. I raised my fist to knock, but before I could strike the wood, the door opened.

It was not Elias. It was Eliza.

She was standing in the doorway, a candle in her hand, its flame trembling in the draft. She was young, perhaps seventeen, with a face that was pale and sharp, her eyes large and dark, burning with an intelligence that seemed out of place in such humble surroundings. She wore a plain muslin dress, the only luxury she possessed, and her hair was bound back severely.

"Captain Thorne," she said. Her voice was clear, cutting through the wind. "You are expected."

I was taken aback. "Expected? By whom?"

"By me," she replied, stepping aside to let me enter. "And by my father, who is in the back room. He knows you are coming. He has been waiting for you."

I followed her into the house. It was small, but clean, and the air smelled of pine resin and old paper. The walls were lined with books, a shocking sight in a weaver’s home. In the corner, a small table was set with a few cups of tea. Elias sat in a chair, his hands clasped over his knees. He did not look up when I entered.

"Captain Thorne," Elias said, his voice rough. "You have come to take me."

"I have orders, Mr. Vane," I said, forcing my voice to remain steady. "I am sorry."

"Sorry?" Elias finally looked up. His eyes were red, but dry. "Pity is a luxury I cannot afford. You are here to imprison a man for speaking the truth. Is that not what you do? You enforce the will of those who have nothing to lose, against those who have everything to gain?"

"The law is the law," I said, but the words tasted like ash in my mouth.

Eliza stepped forward. She placed a book on the table. It was a Bible, but it was open to a passage I recognized. "My father does not seek to overthrow the government, Captain. He seeks to understand our condition. He teaches the Sunday school in the village. Do you not know that?"

I paused. I did know. I had noticed the quiet, earnest woman who led the children in their hymns. I had assumed she was a respectable, timid creature. I had not suspected the fire that burned within her.

"Your father is accused of plotting to burn the mill," I said.

"He is accused of many things," Eliza said, her voice rising. "But if you wish to know the truth, Captain, look at your own hands. Are they clean? You are a soldier, but you are also a servant of a system that grinds men into dust. My father speaks of justice. You speak of order. Which is more important?"

I looked at Elias, then at Eliza. The candlelight flickered, casting long shadows on the walls. I thought of my sister, who attended the Sunday school in the village church. I thought of the way Eliza spoke to the children, with a patience and a fervor that I had never seen in any other teacher. I thought of the injustice that lay at the heart of this conflict, the way the wealthy men in the town hall sat in their warm parlors while the weavers starved in their cold homes.

"I have my orders," I said, but I did not move to draw my sword. "I must take you, Mr. Vane."

Elias stood up. He was a smaller man than I had imagined, but there was a strength in his posture that was undeniable. "Then take me," he said. "But know this, Captain Thorne: you are not a hero. You are a tool. And tools can be broken."
