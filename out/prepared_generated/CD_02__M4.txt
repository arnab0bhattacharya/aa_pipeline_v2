Mr. Septimus Quill, junior clerk of the firm of Tottlebury, Grimes and Tottlebury, Wharfingers and Shipping Agents of Candlewick Reach, was a young man of so retiring and modest a disposition that the mere circumstance of his having won a prize of fifty pounds in the Great Metropolitan Charitable Lottery (a benevolent institution, as its advertisements declared, for the relief of decayed gentlewomen and the encouragement of the arts) occasioned him more alarm than joy. He had purchased the ticket, indeed, in a moment of unusual daring, moved thereto by the sight of a bill upon a hoarding which represented a family of destitute respectability receiving soup from the hands of an allegorical female in a helmet; and when the numbers were drawn, and his own among them, he sat down upon the edge of his bed in his little chamber in Pump Court, Clerkenwell, and trembled in every limb.

“Fifty pounds!” said Mr. Quill to himself, in a whisper, as though the walls might hear him. “Fifty pounds! It is a fortune. It is a precipice. It is—it is a very dangerous thing.”

For Mr. Quill was blessed—or, as he had sometimes reason to consider it, cursed—with a family of the most affectionate and attentive description, whose affection had this peculiar quality about it, that it invariably expressed itself in the shape of a request for money. There was his aunt, Mrs. Prudence Vellum, widow of a custom-house officer, who lived at Islington, and whose misfortunes were as regular as the quarter-day; there was his cousin, Mr. Orlando Quill, a gentleman of elegant appearance and no visible occupation, who was for ever upon the point of realising a magnificent speculation, and for ever requiring but a trifling loan to carry him over the week; there was his uncle by marriage, Mr. Bartholomew Grubb, a man of a watery eye and a circumstantial manner, who had been unfortunate in the chandler’s business, unfortunate in the cheese trade, unfortunate in the keeping of a small tavern, and was now unfortunate in a manner so complicated and so deeply involving that he could never be brought to explain it in less than two hours; and there were others, a numerous and hungry tribe, who descended upon Pump Court at intervals, like the flies of summer, and departed with whatever small coin could be spared them.

To all of these, Mr. Quill, in a moment of that self-congratulation which follows a stroke of luck, had determined to say nothing. He would put the money in the Savings Bank. He would draw it out by degrees. He would buy a new hat, and perhaps a pair of boots, and the rest should be a secret and a comfort.

Within a week, every soul of them had heard of it.

It is one of the curious properties of a secret in London, that it is the most widely disseminated thing in nature. Mr. Quill had told the news to but one living creature—to his fellow-clerk, Mr. Timothy Tapp, in the privacy of the counting-house, and under the seal of the most solemn confidence—and Mr. Tapp had told it to his landlady, who had told it to her daughter, who had told it to the young man who kept the oil-shop, who had told it to a friend at the corner, who had told it to everybody; so that by the Saturday morning it had travelled the whole length of the Clerkenwell Road, and had even reached Islington, where it was received with a scream of joy by Mrs. Prudence Vellum, and a thoughtful silence by Mr. Orlando Quill.

On the Saturday morning, at about eleven o’clock, as Mr. Quill sat in his little sitting-room over a rasher of bacon and a cup of tea, reflecting with a gentle satisfaction upon the new hat which was to be the first-fruits of his fortune, there came a knock at the door, and Mrs. Vellum entered, in a black bonnet of the most funereal description, and with a pocket-handkerchief already in her hand.

“My dear Septimus,” said Mrs. Vellum, sinking into a chair, and applying the handkerchief to her eyes, “I hope I see you well. I hope I see you prosperous. I hope I see you,” here she sobbed, “happy.”

Mr. Quill, who had risen in confusion, and whose tea was getting cold, begged his aunt to compose herself.

“Compose myself!” cried Mrs. Vellum. “Compose myself, Septimus, when I am a ruined woman! When the broker’s man is in the house at this very moment, and my little bits of furniture—my poor dear Vellum’s furniture, that he brought from the Custom House, where he was so respected—is to be sold on Monday next, unless I can find fifty pounds by Monday morning! Fifty pounds!” and here Mrs. Vellum wept aloud. “Fifty pounds, Septimus! It is a sum that I could never hope to raise. It is a sum that I dare not name to my relations. It is a sum—”

Mr. Quill, who had turned very pale, and who felt that the rasher of bacon was regarding him with a reproachful eye, stammered something about the hardness of the times.

“Hard times!” said Mrs. Vellum, with a sudden accession of energy. “Hard times, Septimus, when a young man wins fifty pounds in a lottery and keeps it from his own aunt! Oh, the ingratitude of the young! Oh, the heartlessness of the age! But I will not reproach you. I will not say a word. I will go home and die.”

She rose, and had actually reached the door, when Mr. Quill, in an agony, implored her to be seated, and promised—with a sinking at the heart—to consider of it. Mrs. Vellum, with the air of a martyr, consented to take a little breakfast.

Scarcely had she swallowed her second cup of tea, when a light, jaunty step was heard upon the stair, and Mr. Orlando Quill entered, in a surtout of the most approved cut, and with a flower in his button-hole.
