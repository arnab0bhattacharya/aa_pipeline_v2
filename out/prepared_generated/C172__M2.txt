The bell above the entrance of Hartley’s & Finch did not ring so much as it sighed, a metallic exhalation that announced the arrival of another soul seeking the comfort of a new hem or the promise of a tailored suit. Elara adjusted her cap, smoothing the brim with fingers that had gone numb from the chill seeping through the wool of her uniform. The air in the main hall was a thick soup of lavender water, stale tobacco, and the dusty sweetness of unworn cotton. It was a sensory landscape she had memorized over three years of service, a labyrinth of mahogany counters and glass display cases that reflected her own face back to her, pale and serious, always watching.

"Excuse me, miss," a voice clipped by impatience cut through the ambient hum. "I need this altered. It’s tight across the shoulders."

Elara took the garment, a sea-green silk blouse that felt cool and heavy in her hands. "Of course, madam. The tailor will have it ready by Friday. Shall I note your preference for a loose fit?"

"No, I want it fitted. But not suffocating." The customer’s eyes, sharp and critical, scanned Elara’s attire as if looking for a flaw. "You know how it is. One must look presentable."

Elara smiled, the corner of her mouth twitching in a practiced, hollow arc. "Indeed, madam. Presentability is essential."

She turned away, tucking the blouse under her arm, and retreated to the tailoring department. The afternoon was a blur of measurements, pins, and the rhythmic clack of hems being turned. She moved through the space with the efficiency of a machine, her mind elsewhere, drifting toward the memory that had anchored itself in her heart the previous week.

It had been the dance at the Victoria Hall. The band had played jazz, a snarling, unpredictable sound that shook the floorboards. She had been standing by the punch bowl, feeling the heat of the crowd, when he had appeared. He was not a man of the department store; he wore a tweed jacket that was slightly too large for his frame, and he had hair that fell over his forehead in a way that suggested he had been careless with his comb, or perhaps with the world. He had asked her for a dance, his voice low and rough, smelling of rain and pipe tobacco.

"May I?" he had asked. It was not a question, but a plea.

They had danced until the music faded, their bodies moving in a syncopated rhythm that felt less like dancing and more like a conversation conducted in a language they both secretly spoke. He had introduced himself as Julian Thorne, a clerk at a bookshop on Charing Cross Road. He had not asked for her address, nor had she given it. When the night ended, they had simply parted at the curb, the city lights blurring into streaks of amber and red. He had looked at her with an intensity that made her skin prickle, a look that said *I will remember you* without saying a word.

For three days, Elara had lived in that look. She had replayed the melody of their steps, the weight of his hand on her waist, the way he had laughed at her clumsy attempt at the Charleston. It was a dangerous thing, to hold onto a stranger in the bustling, indifferent machinery of London. But it was the only thing that made the grey drizzle of the afternoon feel like something other than a dampening of her spirit.

At one o’clock, the whistle blew for lunch. Elara descended to the staff room, a cramped, windowless basement where the air tasted of boiled onions and floor wax. The room was filled with women in various states of uniform, their caps askew, their makeup smudged. They sat on wooden benches, eating tinned soup from paper cups, the metal spoons clattering against the rims.

"You’re far away, Elara," said Mrs. Gable, the head of the shoe department, sitting opposite her. Mrs. Gable was a stout woman with a face like a crumpled map, her eyes always scanning for shoddy work.

"Just tired," Elara lied, stirring her soup.

"Tired is good. Tired keeps you humble. Happy is dangerous." Mrs. Gable took a bite of her bread, chewing slowly. "My son, he thinks he’s happy because he’s courting a girl from the slums. He’s ruined his prospects. You keep your head down, Elara. The world is not made for daydreams."

Elara looked down at the swirl of cream in her soup. She thought of Julian Thorne. She thought of the way the light had caught the dust motes dancing around him in the hall. "Happiness is a matter of perspective, Mrs. Gable," she said softly.

Mrs. Gable snorted. "Perspective is a luxury. We have rent to pay and clothes to sell."

The rest of the shift passed in a haze of bells and fabric. When the clock struck five, Elara felt the release of tension in her shoulders. She changed into her own clothes—a simple grey dress, a coat that had seen better days, and sensible shoes that ached from the day’s walking. She walked out of Hartley’s & Finch, stepping onto the pavement of Oxford Street.

The street was a river of humanity. The year was 1924, and London was shedding the mourning of the war, but the shadows remained. Women in cloche hats and long pearl necklaces hurried past men in flat caps and overcoats. The sound of the street was a roar: the rumble of buses, the hooting of horns, the chatter of a thousand voices. Elara moved with the current, her head down, her hands tucked into her pockets.

She had walked to the corner of Regent Street, intending to turn left toward the Underground. The air was crisp, carrying the scent of coal smoke and wet asphalt. She paused at the crosswalk, waiting for the traffic to clear. Her mind was still drifting, constructing scenarios of how she might find him. Perhaps she would go to Charing Cross Road. Perhaps she would wear the blue dress she had bought with her last savings. Perhaps he would be there, standing in the window, looking for a book on philosophy or poetry.
