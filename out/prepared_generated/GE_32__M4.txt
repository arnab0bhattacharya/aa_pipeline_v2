It was a fact, universally acknowledged in the county, that Miss Honoria Vane possessed a fortune of thirty thousand pounds, and a face and figure which rendered the possession of that fortune a secondary consideration in the eyes of every unmarried gentleman within a day’s drive of Marchmont Priory. The Vanes were not of the oldest blood—old Mr. Vane had made his money in the China trade, and had bought the Priory with the fine indifference of a man who had never known the want of it—but they were rich, and the rich are never without a pedigree, if they care to look for it. Honoria, therefore, had grown up with the conviction, never stated in words but breathed into her from the very atmosphere of her home, that she was a creature of exceptional value, and that her value was not solely, or even chiefly, dependent on the accident of her father’s wealth. She had been praised for her beauty, her wit, her horsemanship, and her taste in music, until she had come to regard admiration as the natural tribute to her merits, and the absence of it as a kind of impertinence which it was her duty to punish with indifference.

The suitors had come, as suitors will come to a beautiful heiress, in a steady and well-regulated procession. There was Lord Algernon Fitzclarence, a younger son with a title and a taste for expensive living; there was Mr. Percival Lydgate, a barrister of some distinction, who had the habit of talking to her as though she were a jury whom it was necessary to convince; and there was Captain the Honourable George Fotheringay, whose conversation was chiefly concerned with the condition of the hunting, and who had once, in a moment of unwonted ardour, compared her eyes to the stars, and then, becoming conscious of the extravagance, had added that they were a good deal brighter than any he had seen that season. To all of these Honoria had listened with a gracious indifference which she mistook for discrimination, and had dismissed them severally with a smile which left them in doubt whether they were accepted or refused, but which left them, in either case, convinced that she was the most charming woman in the world.

It was in the second week of October, when the leaves at Marchmont were turning to a sombre gold, and the pheasants were beginning to show themselves in the stubble, that the news came. The banking-house of Vane, Vane, and Crump, in which the greater part of Mr. Vane’s fortune was invested, had failed. The failure was not a matter of a few thousands, but a matter of everything. Mr. Vane, who had been a man of sanguine temper and large expenditure, had been in the habit of regarding his capital as a kind of inexhaustible reservoir, from which it was the most natural thing in the world that he and his family should draw. He had not been dishonest, but he had been imprudent, and when the crash came, he was left with the Priory, a mortgage upon it, and a small income which was barely sufficient to keep the roof in repair and the garden in order. Honoria, who had never in her life been called upon to consider the cost of anything, found herself suddenly confronted with the necessity of considering the cost of everything.

The change was not long in making itself felt. Lord Algernon Fitzclarence, who had been on the point of proposing, discovered that his mother was in a state of health which required his immediate presence in Scotland. Mr. Percival Lydgate, who had been wont to speak of her as the one woman who could have made him forsake his profession, wrote a letter in which he spoke of the claims of his profession as being paramount, and of the impossibility of his offering to a woman who had been accustomed to a position which he could not hope to give her. Captain Fotheringay, who had never been a man of many words, simply ceased to call. The suitors had fallen away, as suitors do when the fortune that attracted them has taken its departure, and Honoria was left to the society of her father, who had grown silent and aged, and of her aunt, Miss Vane, a maiden lady of fifty, who had always predicted that no good would come of the China trade, and who now found a melancholy satisfaction in the fulfilment of her prophecy.

It was in these altered circumstances that the proposal came which was to decide the future of Honoria Vane. It came not from a suitor, but from Mrs. Delamere, the wife of a country clergyman in the neighbouring parish of Stoke, who had been a friend of Honoria’s mother, and who now wrote a letter of sympathetic condolence, in which she mingled a suggestion that Honoria might find a position as governess in the family of a friend of hers in London—a certain Mrs. Hargreaves, of Portland Place, who had three daughters, and who required a person of good family and accomplishments to superintend their education. The salary was not large, but it was sufficient for a single woman of modest requirements, and Mrs. Delamere was of opinion that Honoria would do well to consider it.

Honoria read the letter twice, and then laid it down upon the table with a smile which had in it something of the old disdain. “A governess!” she said to herself. “I, who have been the mistress of Marchmont, to be a governess in the house of a Mrs. Hargreaves, of whom I have never heard, and who probably made her money in some shop!” The thought was intolerable to her pride. She had been accustomed to command, and she could not conceive of herself in a position of dependence. She had been accustomed to admiration, and she could not conceive of herself as a person whose merits were to be weighed in the balance against her usefulness.
