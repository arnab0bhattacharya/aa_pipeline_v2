The fountain in the courtyard of the palace of King Theodorus was a marvel of pink marble and silver water, and in it there lived a fish of so brilliant a gold that the courtiers, who were themselves rather given to gilding, would often pause beside the basin to admire a splendour they could not hope to emulate. It was a fish of no particular ancestry, having been netted in a muddy river and presented to the king by a hopeful fisherman who was subsequently made a lord, but it carried itself with the serene indifference of an aristocrat, and the king, who was a man of some taste, had named it Aurelius and forbade that it should ever be caught.

For a long while Aurelius said nothing, which the courtiers took as a sign of good breeding, for silence in a palace is generally understood to be either stupidity or disapproval, and both are considered respectable. Then one evening, as the moon laid a thin sheet of silver upon the water, a fairy—who had her reasons, as fairies generally do—leaned over the fountain’s rim and granted the fish the power of human speech. The fairy was a small, bright-eyed creature with a gown the colour of wet violets, and she exacted from Aurelius a promise that he would speak nothing but the truth, which is the sort of promise that fairies find amusing and fishes find inconvenient.

The next morning, when the Lady Eglantine, the queen’s chief lady-in-waiting, came to the fountain to admire her own reflection, which she considered the finest work of the gods, Aurelius rose to the surface and regarded her with a round, unblinking eye.

“Madam,” said he, “your left eyebrow is painted half a finger’s breadth higher than the right, which gives you the appearance of a woman perpetually on the point of asking a question to which she does not wish to hear the answer.”

The Lady Eglantine dropped her fan. She looked about her, but the courtyard was empty save for a gardener’s boy who was pretending to trim the roses and was in fact listening with both ears. She gathered her skirts and went into the palace at a speed that was undignified in a woman of her rank and entirely justified in a woman of her situation.

By noon the fish had spoken to the Lord Chamberlain, a portly man named Brassus who wore a chain of office so heavy that it seemed to be dragging him toward the earth from which he had been made. “My lord,” said the fish, “you have embezzled three thousand crowns from the treasury, and your wife knows it, and your wife’s confessor knows it, and the only person who does not know it is the king, who is too honest to suspect a man with such an excellent tailor.”

Lord Brassus laughed a great, hollow laugh and said, “A fish! A talking fish! How delightful!” and then went away to count his money and found, to his considerable distress, that it was three thousand crowns short.

The fish spoke to the young Count Florimond, who had been sighing at the feet of the Lady Eglantine for a twelvemonth and writing sonnets to her eyebrows, which were, as has been established, unequal. “You do not love her,” said the fish. “You love her dowry, and you love the idea of being in love, which is the most flattering of all ideas, and you love yourself for loving, which is the most common of all loves.” The count turned pale, then red, then a colour for which there is no name in the language of the court, and went to write a sonnet about the cruelty of fishes.

By evening the courtyard had become a place of terror. The courtiers approached the fountain in the way that men approach a mirror in a dark room—with a mixture of longing and dread. Some came to hear their praises sung, and went away wounded. Others came to hear their sins condemned, and went away strangely relieved, for there is a kind of comfort in being told the worst by a creature that cannot repeat it at dinner. The fish, for his part, was inexhaustible. He had lived in a muddy river and then in a marble fountain, and he had observed the human race from below the waterline, which is a vantage point that reveals the truth of things. He told the bishop that his sermons were admired for their length and not their doctrine. He told the duchess that her charity was a form of vanity with a receipt. He told the poet laureate that his verses were the sort of thing that could be written by anyone who had never suffered and had a good dictionary. And to each of them he spoke with the same round-eyed serenity, as though truth were a kind of food that he had been given and was now obliged to share.

The king, who had been hunting in the forest, returned at dusk to find his court in disarray. The Lady Eglantine was in her chambers with a cold compress upon her eyebrow. Lord Brassus was in his counting-house with a ledger and a bottle. The Count Florimond had torn up a hundred sonnets and was composing a satire. The bishop had announced that he would preach a sermon on the sin of idle curiosity, which everyone understood to mean that he intended to denounce the fish. And the poet laureate had gone to bed with a headache, which is the traditional refuge of the man who has been told the truth.

King Theodorus was a man of simple tastes and complicated duties. He liked hunting, he liked his queen, and he liked the idea of being a good king, which he was, though he was not always certain how one went about it. He sat down beside the fountain and looked at the fish, and the fish looked back at him.
