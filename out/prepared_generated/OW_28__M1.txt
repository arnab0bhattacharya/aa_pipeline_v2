Sir Alaric Vane had long ago discovered that the truth was a currency of such vulgar abundance that it was practically worthless. In his drawing-room in Mayfair—a chamber of amber silk, tortoiseshell clocks, and the scent of hothouse gardenias—he treated sincerity as one might treat a rash: an embarrassing affliction best left to the provincial. His great-nephew, Cyril, a young man of that singular beauty which consists entirely of a lack of definition, stood by the mantelpiece, looking as perplexed as a faun that has wandered into a lecture on logic.

“You are quite hopeless, Cyril,” said Sir Alaric, examining a snuff-box of lapis lazuli with the affectionate disdain of a jeweller. “You have actually asked me how to succeed. Success is the one thing in the world that should never be attempted. It is the refuge of the mediocre and the consolation of the ugly. To succeed is to be admitted, and to be admitted is to be bored.”

“But I wish to be a Member of Parliament, Uncle,” said Cyril, his voice trembling with the awful sincerity of youth. “I wish to do some good in the world.”

Sir Alaric shuddered and closed his eyes. “Do not use that word, my boy. ‘Good’ is a word that belongs to the vocabulary of the clergy and the cook. It is a term of digestion, not of intellect. If you wish to enter Parliament, you must learn the art of the inverted truth. You must say the opposite of what you mean, for the world is so constituted that it only understands the language of irony. If you wish to be admired for your honesty, you must never tell the truth. If you wish to be loved for your kindness, you must be ruthlessly cruel. It is the only way to survive the boredom of one’s fellow creatures.”

Cyril looked at the Persian carpet, a riot of peonies and pomegranates, and tried to grasp this philosophy. “So, if I wish to be thought a man of honour, I should be a scoundrel?”

“Precisely,” said Sir Alaric, warming his hands at the fire. “But you must be a scoundrel with such exquisite politeness that people will call it a style. Now, tell me, what is the particular difficulty that has brought you to my sofa today? Is it a woman, or a debt? They are usually the same thing.”

“It is Lady Augusta,” said Cyril, blushing like a white rose. “I love her. I wish to propose to her. But I am afraid she thinks me a frivolous youth.”

Sir Alaric laughed, a sound like the clinking of ice in a crystal glass. “My dear Cyril, if you love her, you must treat her with absolute indifference. You must tell her that her eyes are the colour of boiled cabbage, and that her conversation is a torture to your aesthetic sensibilities. Women cannot resist being told that they are ugly; it is the only compliment they have not learned to distrust. If you wish to make her your wife, tell her you would rather marry her maid.”

“And if I wish to secure a seat in the House?”

“Ah, politics,” said Sir Alaric, picking a piece of invisible lint from his velvet sleeve. “The House is a collection of men who have sacrificed their intellects to their ambitions. To succeed there, you must tell them that the nation is ruined, that the Empire is a sham, and that they are a set of glorious, unthinking fools who will save the world. You must promise them everything and give them nothing. Tell them you will lower their taxes by raising them. Tell them you will bring peace by declaring war on the very concept of diplomacy. They will elect you by acclamation, for the English have a profound love for paradox, provided it is stated loudly enough.”

Cyril nodded, his eyes wide. He was beginning to see the pattern, though it was as distorted as a reflection in a curved mirror. “And if I am asked about my religion?”

“Tell them you are a pagan,” said Sir Alaric. “Tell them you worship the sun because it is the only thing that never tells a lie. Then, immediately, tell them you are an atheist who believes in the Trinity. Confusion is the highest form of authority. A man who is never understood is always respected.”

For the next three months, Sir Alaric watched from the window of his club as Cyril moved through the season. It was a performance of such magnificent cynicism that it brought tears of joy to the old diplomat’s eyes. Cyril insulted Lady Augusta’s bonnet so poetically that she wept and accepted his hand in marriage the same afternoon. He stood before the electors of West Dulwich and denounced the very idea of representation, arguing that the country would be better governed by a committee of blind poets. He promised to abolish the Church of England and replace it with a museum of beautiful lies.

The results were, as Sir Alaric had predicted, astonishing.

Cyril was returned to Parliament with a majority of three thousand. Lady Augusta declared him the only man in London with a mind of his own. The newspapers, unable to comprehend his inverted logic, hailed him as a profound philosopher. He was invited to dinner by the Prime Minister, who wanted to know how Cyril had managed to unite the radicals and the conservatives by telling them both that they were wrong.

One evening, Cyril came to Sir Alaric’s house. He wore a coat of emerald green and looked, Sir Alaric thought, quite detestably successful.

“Well, Uncle,” said Cyril, helping himself to a glass of the old man’s sherry. “I have done exactly as you instructed. I have insulted my betters, flattered my enemies, and promised the moon to the electorate. I am the most popular man in London.”
