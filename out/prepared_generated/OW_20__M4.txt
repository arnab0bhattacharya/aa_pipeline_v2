The tea-things had been cleared away, and the drawing-room of the Delamere house in Kensington assumed that air of solemn vacuity which follows the departure of the muffins. Mrs. Delamere, a lady whose moral sense was as highly polished as her furniture and considerably less comfortable, sat by the fire engaged in the delicate occupation of judging her neighbours. Mr. Delamere, who had made his fortune in something respectable and forgotten it, dozed gently behind the *Pall Mall Gazette*. And Miss Christabel Delamere, their daughter, a young person of nineteen whose education had been expensive and whose opinions were correspondingly few, was reading aloud—for the family permitted itself this one indulgence—from the novel which all London was discussing and no one was admitting to have purchased.

“*And so*, ” read Christabel, in the voice of one reciting a lesson, “*the Duchess drew the emerald from her bosom and pressed it to the lips of the young guardsman, saying, ‘Wear it, my darling, in memory of a woman who has ruined herself for a kiss.’*”

Mrs. Delamere permitted herself a sound between a sigh and a sniff.

“It is the most shocking book of the season,” she observed, with the satisfaction of a connoisseur. “They say the author is a gentleman. I cannot credit it. No gentleman could write such a sentence.”

“No gentleman,” murmured Mr. Delamere from behind his paper, “could write any sentence at all, my dear, without the assistance of his butler.”

“I was not speaking to you, Algernon.”

“I am aware of it. I was speaking to the *Pall Mall*, which is a great deal more sympathetic.”

Christabel read on, her cheeks acquiring a becoming pink. The novel was called *The Purple Sin*, and it had sold, in the space of a fortnight, some eleven thousand copies, all of them, as Mrs. Delamere frequently remarked, purchased by other people. It was a book of jewelled improprieties, of duchesses who sinned in sonnets and guardsmen who perished in paragraphs, and it had the singular quality of making virtue appear not merely dull but positively ill-bred.

“There is a rumour,” said Mrs. Delamere, laying down her knitting as though it were a weapon, “that the author is a person of the most respectable connections. Lady Marchbanks told me at the Cranleighs’ that she had it from her own dressmaker, who had it from a footman, who had it from the publisher’s own lips, that the man is a *servant*.”

“How delicious,” said Mr. Delamere, waking fully. “A servant who writes of duchesses. I daresay he knows them better than we do. One always knows the habits of the people one dresses.”

“Algernon, you are coarse.”

“I am nothing of the kind. I am merely observant. It is the same thing, only better paid.”

At this moment the door opened, and Mr. Pilkington, the butler, entered to remove the tea-equipage. He was a man of fifty, of a dignified and somewhat melancholy aspect, with the hands of a bishop and the eyes of a man who has read everything and forgiven most of it. He moved about the room with that noiseless precision which is the poetry of servitude, and as he passed the table he permitted his gaze to rest, for the briefest instant, upon the yellow-covered volume in Miss Christabel’s hand.

Mrs. Delamere saw the glance. She was a woman who saw everything except the obvious.

“Pilkington,” she said, and her voice had the sharpness of a knife drawn from a drawer, “you may leave the tray. I wish to speak with you.”

“Certainly, madam.”

He stood before her, immaculate, attentive, a monument of respectability in black.

“Pilkington, I have been told—I do not say I believe it—that the author of this—this book which is causing so much scandal is a person in service. A butler, I am informed. In a respectable family.”

If Mr. Pilkington felt anything, his face did not betray it. It was a face which had been trained, through thirty years of other people’s dinners, to express nothing but the anticipation of other people’s needs.

“Indeed, madam,” he said. “The profession has fallen upon strange days.”

“It has fallen upon *writing* days,” said Mr. Delamere, with a chuckle. “Come, Pilkington, you look like a man of letters. Confess. Have you a manuscript in your pantry?”

“I have a *Bradshaw*, sir, and a *Peerage*. They are the only works of fiction I permit myself.”

Mr. Delamere laughed heartily. Mrs. Delamere did not.

“The book,” she continued, “is immoral.”

“It is very popular, madam,” said Pilkington, with the air of one offering a correction.

“Popularity is no defence. The *plague* was popular.”

“The plague, madam, had not the advantage of a good publisher.”

Mrs. Delamere rose. She was a tall woman, and she rose as though she were unfolding a screen.

“Pilkington, I shall be plain with you. If you are the author of this book, I desire you to tell me so at once, that I may dismiss you.”

There was a silence in which the clock upon the mantelpiece could be heard committing the indiscretion of ticking.

“And if I am not the author, madam?”

“Then you will remain.”

“And if I am?”

“Then you will go.”

Pilkington considered this for a moment with the gravity of a judge.

“Madam,” he said at last, “I find myself in a position of some delicacy. If I deny the authorship, I remain in your service, but I remain also under the imputation of having written a book which I have not written—a thing no honest man should endure. If I confess it, I am dismissed for having written a book which eleven thousand persons have read with pleasure and which you yourself, madam, have read with—if I may say so—the most flattering attention.”

Mrs. Delamere coloured. “I have read it,” she said, “as one reads the *Lancet*—for the symptoms.”

“Precisely, madam. And it is a very healthy book. It has made a great many people feel better.”
