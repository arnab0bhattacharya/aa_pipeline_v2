Mr. Arthur Helstone, of Thornfield Park in Derbyshire, was a young man of excellent understanding and considerable address, who found himself, at the age of six-and-twenty, in the unwelcome position of being thoroughly encumbered. His late father had been a man of more taste than economy, and the estate, though imposing in its aspect and respectable in its acreage, was settled so heavily with debts as to render the present owner little more than a tenant at will upon his own property. Mr. Helstone was therefore under the absolute necessity of marrying a fortune, or of selling the land which he could not afford to keep. He was, upon the whole, more inclined to marry than to sell, being naturally attached to his home, and having that preference for the comforts of rank over the exertions of industry which is frequently found in young men of good family and narrow means.

The fortune he sought was, at that moment, residing within five miles of him. Miss Margaret Bexley, the only daughter of a manufacturer from Leeds whose ironworks had yielded a very splendid income, was staying with her aunt at the neighboring village of Haycroft. Miss Bexley was possessed of twenty thousand pounds, a sum which, if applied to the discharge of the most pressing encumbrances upon Thornfield, would enable Mr. Helstone to retain his estate and to consider himself, in the moderate degree which prudence allowed, an independent gentleman. She was not, in the common acceptation of the word, handsome; but she had a look of sense, a degree of composure in her address, and a readiness of observation which Mr. Helstone found, upon acquaintance, to be rather more formidable than agreeable.

He had paid his respects at Haycroft with regularity for nearly a month, and had advanced in his attentions to that point which is generally understood, by those who watch such matters, to imply a serious design. He walked with her in the shrubbery, he rode with her on the common, and he conversed with her on subjects of literature and improvement with a degree of elegance which he flattered himself was not without its effect. Miss Bexley received his attentions with civility, but without any of that flutter of gratified vanity which such attentions are wont to produce. She listened to his remarks on the picturesque with apparent attention, and replied to them with a precision which occasionally made him feel that his taste had been consulted rather than his judgment. He was not, however, a man to be discouraged by a want of enthusiasm where there was no positive indifference, and he continued his pursuit with the steady resolution of a man who knows that his object is necessary to his welfare.

It was on a fine morning in the latter part of their acquaintance that they found themselves walking together in the gardens of Haycroft, the aunt having withdrawn to attend to some matter of household economy, and the younger sister of Miss Bexley having been dispatched in the carriage to call upon a friend. They were, for the first time, wholly without a third party, and Mr. Helstone, who had been meditating an explicit declaration, perceived that the opportunity was one which he could not, with any regard to his own interest, allow to pass.

He began, as men in his situation are apt to begin, with a general observation upon the pleasantness of the scene, the fineness of the weather, and the satisfaction he derived from the society of Haycroft. Miss Bexley heard him with patience, and when he had concluded his preamble, which was rather longer than his own taste would have approved in another, she turned to him with a look of calm intelligence and said, "You are very obliging, Mr. Helstone. But I believe I can spare you the trouble of further circumlocution. You wish to address me, and you wish me to accept you. Am I correct?"

Mr. Helstone, who had not anticipated so direct an approach, felt himself considerably discomposed. He had prepared a speech of some elegance, in which the warmth of his regard was to be tempered by the moderation of his expectations, and in which the subject of money was to be introduced only through the most delicate of implications. He found himself now, however, in the position of a general whose plan of attack has been anticipated by the enemy, and he could only reply, with an air of less composure than he usually wore, that she was, in the main, correct.

"I am glad of it," said Miss Bexley, "for I should be sorry to waste either your time or my own in a discussion which might be conducted with more frankness. You are, I understand, under the necessity of marrying a fortune. Thornfield is encumbered, and you cannot retain it without an addition to your income which your own resources do not provide. I am, as you are no doubt aware, in possession of twenty thousand pounds. You have paid me attentions which, in a man of your station, can have but one meaning, and I have received them with a degree of willingness which, in a woman of my circumstances, can have but one construction. We understand each other, I think, very well."

Mr. Helstone, who had by this time recovered some portion of his self-command, replied that he was happy to find that she had entered so fully into the nature of his situation, and that he could assure her, whatever might be the motives which had first directed his attention to her, his regard was now founded upon a sincere esteem for her understanding and character.

"I do not doubt it," said Miss Bexley, "but I must observe, Mr. Helstone, that esteem, however sincere, is not the only consideration which will determine my answer.
