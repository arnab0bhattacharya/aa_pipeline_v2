The ambition of Mrs. Cheriton was of that active and enterprising cast which, when unhappily directed, is capable of giving pain to all within its sphere, and of procuring for its possessor a degree of mortification which a quieter spirit would have been spared. To have seen her three daughters advantageously established in life was the great object of her existence; and her notions of advantage were of a nature so decided, so little sullied by any alloy of prudence or moderation, that nothing short of a coronet could satisfy them. The Miss Cheritons were, it is true, handsome, accomplished, and agreeable; but as their mother was pleased to observe, with a frequency which betrayed the anxiety of her hopes, beauty and accomplishments were but the setting, and it was the duty of a mother to provide the jewel.

It was upon the strength of a report, brought to her by a lady of her acquaintance whose intelligence was ever in an inverse proportion to its truth, that the young Lord Lavington was to pass the autumn at Ramsden, that Mrs. Cheriton had removed her whole family to that fashionable watering-place with a promptitude which left her husband no leisure for objection, and scarcely for the arrangement of his affairs. The report was of the vaguest; the nobleman in question was described as the only son of a dowager of great expectations; and it was added, in a whisper of peculiar significance, that he was of a shy and retired disposition, and would probably be seen but little in public. To Mrs. Cheriton, this last particular was not discouraging, but the reverse. A young man of rank, who avoided the crowd, must be the more accessible to the approaches of a judicious and well-regulated family; and she had already, during the journey, arranged in her own mind the several parts which her daughters were to perform, with a nicety of distribution which did honour to her maternal solicitude.

The first week at Ramsden, however, produced nothing but the usual occupations and the usual disappointments of a watering-place. The ladies walked upon the pier, and were seen at the assembly; they drank the waters, and they examined the circulating library; and Mrs. Cheriton, in the midst of these employments, preserved an air of mystery and of expectation which her daughters, who knew their mother, were at no loss to interpret. It was not until the ninth day that the intelligence, which was to give life to the whole scheme, was at last confirmed. A carriage and four, with a coronet upon the panel, was observed to draw up at the principal inn; and the servant, who had been dispatched upon the instant to make inquiry, returned with the information that his lordship was arrived, and that he had brought with him a governess.

“A governess!” cried Mrs. Cheriton, with a countenance in which triumph and perplexity were strangely mingled. “A governess, and no mother? Then he is indeed unprotected, and our duty is the more plain. A young man of rank, without a mother, and with a governess, is exactly in the situation to be benefited by the society of a respectable family. Letitia, my love, you will wear your white muslin to-morrow; and, Sophia, you will have the goodness to be indisposed, and to keep your chamber.”

The Miss Cheritons, who had been too long accustomed to their mother's manoeuvres to be much surprised at any of them, submitted with the resignation of well-bred young women; and the following morning beheld Mrs. Cheriton and her two eldest daughters established upon the pier, in the most advantageous position which the place afforded, and in a state of expectation which it would be difficult to describe.

His lordship was at length observed to approach. He came attended by a lady of middle age, of a grave and composed appearance, who walked beside him with the air of one who was accustomed to direct rather than to follow; and between them, or rather before them, in a state of restless activity which seemed to belong to no one in particular, came the young nobleman himself. He was a boy of seventeen, with a countenance in which the awkwardness of his age was rendered still more conspicuous by a habit of continual motion; his hat was in his hand, his gloves were in his pocket, and his eyes were everywhere but upon the persons whom he was approaching. He bowed to Mrs. Cheriton with a haste which seemed to imply that he hoped to have done with the ceremony as speedily as possible; he trod upon the flounce of Letitia's pelisse, and begged her pardon with a confusion which was almost painful to behold; and he then, as if by a sudden effort, fixed his eyes upon the ground, and remained silent.

Mrs. Cheriton, however, was not a woman to be discouraged by trifles. She addressed him with that mixture of affability and respect which she conceived to be due to his rank; she spoke of the fineness of the weather, of the beauties of the coast, and of the pleasure which it must afford to a young man of his lordship's expectations to escape from the fatigues of the metropolis; and she was proceeding to inquire, with an air of the most delicate interest, whether his lordship had yet visited the assembly-rooms, when the governess, who had hitherto remained silent, interposed.

“My lord,” said she, in a tone of calm authority, “you have not yet taken your morning exercise. You know that Dr. Merton was very particular in his directions, and that a walk of at least half an hour before breakfast is indispensable.”

“Yes, ma'am,” said his lordship, with the alacrity of a schoolboy who is glad of any release from an irksome situation; and, turning to Mrs. Cheriton, he made another hasty bow, and was about to depart, when that lady, with great presence of mind, detained him by an inquiry respecting the health of his mother.
