The vestry was cold, for the sexton had let the fire die down to a handful of grey ash, and the rain beat against the small leaded panes with a sound like the rattling of dry bones. Mr. Ambrose Heathcote, rector of St. Michael’s, Fairweather Moor, stood with his back to the empty grate, his hands clasped behind him, and his countenance composed into that expression of patient superiority which he was wont to wear when dealing with the more obstinate of his parishioners. Before him, cap in hand, his rough coat steaming gently from the weather, stood Silas Thwaite, weaver, of the mill cottages down in the hollow.

“I tell thee, parson,” said Thwaite, and his voice, though low, had in it a quality that made the little room seem smaller and the silence of the church beyond more profound, “I tell thee, thou hast preached a doctrine that would make the Almighty a tyrant, and man a worm that doth but crawl to be crushed. ‘Servants, obey your masters,’ saith the Scripture—and thou dost harp on that string till the poor folk think God made them for naught but to fetch and carry at the bidding of them that grind them. But what of the other word—‘Masters, give unto your servants that which is just and equal; knowing that ye also have a Master in heaven’? Hast thou preached that, Mr. Heathcote? Hast thou stood up in yon pulpit and told Sir Ralph Pendlebury to his face that his overlookers cheat the children of their wages, and that the Lord of Hosts hath a controversy with him?”

The rector’s pale cheek flushed. “Thwaite,” he said, and his voice was sharp, “you forget yourself. You are a self-taught man, and self-taught men are apt to fall into the error of private judgment. It is not for you to instruct your betters in the interpretation of Holy Writ. The Church hath her appointed officers——”

“The Church!” cried the weaver, and his eyes, deep-set and burning, flashed with a light that was not of this world’s making. “The Church! And what is thy church but a stool for the rich to set their feet upon? I have read thy books, parson—ay, and I have read others. I have read till my eyes ached and my brain reeled, and I have found no warrant there for the doctrine thou preachest. ‘The rich and poor meet together: the Lord is the maker of them all.’ Hast thou preached that? Hast thou told thy congregation that the soul of the little piecer in the mill is as precious in the sight of God as the soul of my lady in her silks? Nay, thou hast told them to be content in that state of life to which it hath pleased God to call them—as if the state of life of a half-starved weaver were the appointment of Heaven, and not the greed of man!”

Mr. Heathcote’s hands, clasped behind his back, tightened till the knuckles showed white. “You are a dangerous man, Thwaite,” he said, and his voice trembled with a rage he scarce could master. “You speak of things you do not understand. You would set the poor against the rich, and bring down ruin upon us all. I forbid you to speak thus in my vestry. I forbid you to speak thus at all.”

The weaver looked at him for a long moment, and in that look there was neither fear nor anger, but a strange, sorrowful pity. “Thou canst forbid me, parson,” he said, quietly. “Thou canst turn me from thy door. But thou canst not forbid the Word of God to speak in a man’s heart. I have a book at home—thou wouldst call it a wicked book, I doubt not—‘The Rights of Man,’ by one Thomas Paine. I would have thee read it. I would have thee read it, and then read thy Bible again, and see if thou canst still preach that the poor man hath no rights but to obey.”

He turned, and went out into the rain, and the door closed behind him with a dull thud that echoed through the empty church like a knell. Mr. Heathcote stood motionless for a moment, and then he sank into a chair, and passed his hand across his brow.

Neither of them had observed that the door of the vestry had been ajar, and that in the dark passage beyond, half-hidden by the heavy curtain, stood a girl of some seventeen years, her hands pressed tightly together, her face pale as the marble font within the church. This was Mirabel Heathcote, the rector’s only daughter, a creature of quick feeling and a spirit that ill brooked the narrow bounds of her father’s doctrine. She had heard every word, and the words had fallen into her heart like seeds into a furrowed field.

She waited till her father had risen and gone heavily into the house, and then she stole out, her cloak drawn close about her, and went down the hill to the mill cottages. The rain had ceased, and a wild, watery sun was breaking through the clouds, and the moor behind the village was purple with heather and loud with the cry of curlews. She knew where the weaver lived—a low cottage at the end of the row, with a loom visible through the window and a few books upon a shelf. She knocked, and Thwaite himself opened the door.

He looked at her with surprise, and then with something of the old wariness. “Miss Heathcote,” he said, and made as if to bar her way.

“I heard you, Mr. Thwaite,” she said, and her voice, though low, was steady. “I heard what you said to my father. I am not come to reproach you. I am come to ask you for the book.”

He stared at her. “The book?”

“The book you spoke of—the one by Thomas Paine. I would read it.”
