I had come to the city of Vireo by the northern road, and the first thing that struck me was not its towers, which are considerable, nor its gardens, which are famous, but the profound and unbroken silence of its people at labour. There was no chatter among the clerks, no argument among the porters, no singing from the kitchens. Each man and woman moved to his task with the calm, unwondering assurance of a planet in its orbit. It was a city of perfect adjustment, and I confess that after the roar and muddle of my own London, it affected me at first with something like relief. It seemed a solution, and a very tidy one, to the ancient problem of human waste.

My guide was a small, precise official named Ferrers, whose duty it was to conduct strangers through the principal institutions and to answer their questions with a patience that suggested he had answered them all before. He showed me the waterworks, the great electric accumulators, and the public dining halls where the food was apportioned by a chemical calculus to the exact needs of each citizen’s metabolism. But the thing I had come to see was the machine itself, the Assessor, as they call it, to which every child is brought at the age of twelve to have his profession assigned to him for life.

It stood in a vast, cool chamber of white stone, a thing of brass and crystal and coiled silver wire, with a hundred dials that flickered and a low, steady hum that never ceased. One by one the children were brought before it, and one by one it pronounced. “Agricultural chemist,” it would say, in a voice of metallic sweetness, and a small boy would be led away, weeping perhaps, but led away. “Sanitary engineer.” “Draughtsman, third grade.” “Poet.”

“Poet?” I said, for I had not expected that.

“We have a sufficiency of poets,” said Ferrers, with a slight shrug. “The Assessor computes the public need and the individual aptitude together. It is a matter of supply and demand, but elevated to an exact science. We have no bad poets in Vireo, and no superfluous ones. Each fills his niche.”

I might have pursued the matter, but at that moment there was a small disturbance at the end of the queue. A girl of about twelve, with a pale, intense face and hair the colour of weak tea, had broken from her mother’s hand and was standing before the machine, her fists clenched at her sides. The Assessor hummed and flickered, and then it said, “Sweeper, municipal, class two.”

The girl did not move. “I want to be a poet,” she said, in a clear, carrying voice.

There was a murmur among the attendants. Ferrers stepped forward. “My dear child,” he said, “the Assessor has spoken. It is not a matter of wanting.”

“I have written poems,” she said. “I have written a great many. I can say them.”

“That is very creditable,” said Ferrers, “and it will make you a better sweeper. A mind well furnished is an asset in any station. But the Assessor has measured your aptitudes, and it finds you are not suited to the poetic life. It is for your own good. A poet who cannot write poetry is a most unhappy creature. We spare you that.”

She looked at him with an expression I can only describe as one of polite and terrible contempt. “How does it know?” she asked.

“It has been calibrated,” said Ferrers, “over three generations, against the actual performance of every citizen. It is never wrong. Come, now. There is a broom waiting for you, and a very good broom it is.”

I thought of the matter no more for some days, being occupied with the drainage and the electric tramways. But it happened that I was lodged in a house in the eastern quarter of the city, near the great reservoir, and it was there that I encountered the girl again. Her name was Lira, and she was, as the machine had decreed, a sweeper of the second class. She swept the long stone terrace before the reservoir each morning from six until nine, and in the afternoons she swept the market square. She did it well, I observed; her lines of swept stone were as straight as a ruled edge, and she never left a leaf or a scrap of paper behind her. But she carried a small notebook in the pocket of her grey overall, and whenever she paused to rest, which was seldom, she would take it out and write in it with a stub of pencil.

I spoke to her one afternoon in the market square, when the stalls were being taken down and the light was yellow and long. She was sitting on the edge of the fountain, her broom across her knees, writing.

“You are the stranger,” she said, before I could speak. “The one who asked about the poets.”

“I am,” I said. “And you are the poet who was made a sweeper.”

“I am a sweeper,” she said, “who writes poetry. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of my life.”

I asked her to read me something, and she did, without embarrassment, in a low, steady voice that made the verses seem better than perhaps they were. They were about the reservoir at dawn, and the way the mist lies on the water, and the sound of the first tram, and the men who come to fish at the outlet with their long poles. They were not, I thought, the work of a great poet, but they were the work of a real one; there was observation in them, and a kind of stubborn music, and a love of the thing seen for its own sake.

“And they will not let you be a poet,” I said.
