The rain struck the narrow window of my lodging in Deansgate with a lash that seemed almost personal, as if the heavens themselves had conspired with the Wentworths to batter my resolution. I sat mending a gown for Mrs. Poole, the mercer's wife— coarse stuff, worth only fourpence the repair— while little Arthur slept in his crib, his breathing the sole sound in that bleak room that did not fill me with dread or defiance. The gown was dark; the thread was dark; my prospects were dark; and the November evening closing in upon Manchester's soot-stained bricks was darkest of all. Yet I sewed, and the needle went in and out, in and out, and each stitch was a small defiance, a small prayer, a small act of keeping this child to my own breast where he belonged, where God had placed him, and where no scheming, well-funded, scripture-quoting family of his dead father should wrest him.

They had come first in August, while the weather was yet warm and the cholera had not yet carried off the poor souls in Angel Meadow. Mrs. Wentworth herself, vast in black silk, smelling of lavender and the peculiar cold scent of money, had seated herself on my one good chair and surveyed the room with eyes that missed nothing and pitied nothing. She had spoken of duty. She had spoken of the boy's prospects. She had spoken of the advantages of a Wentworth upbringing— the tutors, the London season, the introduction to trade and counting-house that would shape a gentleman of commerce. And when I had refused, gently at first, and then with a firmness that surprised even myself, she had risen, and her face had undergone a change not unlike the shutting of a door. "You are very foolish, Mrs. Ashby," she had said. "The offer will not be repeated." And she had gone, and I had been left trembling, not with uncertainty, but with the sheer violence of the will that had been required to say no to her.

Then came the lawyer. Mr. Garrett. A thin man, neat, with a pale face and hands that were never still, turning always over some paper or the handle of his cane. He had called first in September, and had spoken softly, as one speaks to the bereaved, which I was, though I refused to be treated as a child or a simpleton. He had brought documents. He had brought figures. He had shown me, with a patience that I found more insulting than open harshness, what it would cost to feed and clothe and educate a boy in Manchester on the earnings of a needlewoman. The figures were correct. I knew they were correct, for I lived inside those figures every day, and they were a prison-house, and the walls were hunger, and the floor was cold stone beneath my feet at morning and at night. But I had looked at Mr. Garrett, and I had said, "The boy is mine, sir. I will not give him up."

He had returned in October. And now again, in November, on this night of rain and wind, when the very house seemed to groan beneath the weather, and I could hear the drunkards reeling past in the street below, and the distant clatter of the mills that never ceased, that ground on and on like the machinery of perdition. I expected him. I had been expecting him all day, with a fatalism that sat upon me like a garment. And at half-past seven, when the candle had burned low and I was preparing to undress Arthur for his bed, there came the knock.

I opened the door, and there stood Mr. Garrett, wet through, his hat dripping, his thin face drawn with cold. He stepped in without ceremony— or rather, with that smooth, practiced ceremony which is worse than none, because it presumes upon intimacy where none exists. He looked at the room. He looked at the sleeping child. He looked at me. And in his eyes I saw something I had not seen before— not pity, exactly, for pity from such a man would have been an insult, but something adjacent to it, something that troubled the surface of his professional calm like a stone dropped into still water.

"Mrs. Ashby," he said, "I will not waste your time or mine with repetitions. The family's position is unchanged. They will provide for the boy entirely. They will educate him, establish him, and see him settled in a respectable calling. They ask only that you relinquish the charge of his person and his upbringing. You would be free to—"

"I am free now," I said, though my voice shook, and I hated it for shaking. "I am free in my poverty, Mr. Garrett, as I was never free in my marriage. This child is my own flesh, and I will not barter him for bread."

He was silent a moment. The rain drove against the window. Arthur stirred and murmured in his sleep, and the sound of that small voice, that unconscious, trusting murmur, went through me like a blade.

"You call it bread," said Mr. Garrett, and his voice was lower now, stripped somewhat of its professional varnish. "I call it a future. I have seen, Mrs. Ashby, in my fifteen years of practice, what becomes of the children of the very poor in this city. I have seen them in the courts, in the hospitals, in the workhouse. I have seen them dead of fever in cellars no better than the one below this room. I do not say this to frighten you. I say it because it is true, and because I am weary— yes, I am weary— of bringing offers that are refused by people who have not the means to refuse them."
