The letter lay open on the breakfast table, its black seal broken like a cracked robin’s egg. Mrs. Eleanor Vance had read it three times, and each reading had pressed the air a little more tightly from the room. Outside the rectory window, the November fog had swallowed the spire of St. Cuthbert’s, leaving only a grey suggestion of stone against a greyer sky. Her husband, the Reverend Arthur Vance, sat opposite her, stirring his tea though he had long since ceased to take sugar.

“Mr. Hodge,” he said at last, the name falling from his lips like a dropped spoon. “Mr. Hodge, of all men.”

Eleanor did not reply. She was thinking of Mr. Hodge’s wife, a woman who wore puce to church and spoke of her cousin in the archdeaconry with the reverence other women reserved for the Apostles. She was thinking of Mr. Hodge’s sermons, which were four-and-twenty minutes of moral weather, neither storm nor sunshine, and of the way the parish ladies nodded their bonnets as though he had said something profound. She was thinking, too, of the living of Wexcombe St. Mary, which had been vacant these three months, and of the eight years her husband had spent in this damp and thankless curacy, visiting the sick, catechising the young, and mending the church roof with his own hands when the vestry pleaded poverty.

“He has connections,” she said. “His wife’s cousin. That is the whole of it.”

“He is a good man,” Arthur said, with the gentle stubbornness that had once charmed her and now sometimes made her want to shake him. “He will do the parish no harm.”

“And you would have done it good. You would have preached there as you preach here, and the people would have come to love you as they love you here, and—” She stopped. She had been about to say, and I should have been the rector’s wife, and the ladies of Wexcombe would have had to curtsey to me. The thought was ugly, and she was ashamed of it, and she was ashamed of being ashamed. She had been educated above her station—her father had been a schoolmaster with a taste for Latin and a daughter who could parse a sentence before she could knit—and she had married a curate with a good mind and no fortune, believing that merit would out. It had not out. It had been outmanoeuvred by a puce bonnet and a cousin in the archdeaconry.

She rose and went to the window. The fog was thinning, and she could see the ragged edge of the churchyard, where the poor of the parish had been buried in unmarked rows. She had tried to be kind to the poor. She had taken them broth and blankets and the tracts she thought improving. They had received her with suspicion, as though her charity were a trap, and she had felt their eyes on her back as she walked away, and had known that they called her “the curate’s fine lady” when the door was shut.

“You must not fret,” Arthur said behind her. “The Lord’s will be done.”

“The Lord’s will,” she said, “seems to have a great deal in common with the archdeacon’s.”

Arthur set down his cup. “Eleanor.”

“I am sorry.” She turned. “I am sorry. I know you do not like to hear it. But I have watched you these eight years, and I have watched the Hodges of this world, and I cannot believe that God is so very fond of dullness.”

He looked at her with his mild, tired eyes. “Perhaps God is fonder of dullness than you are.”

She laughed, though it hurt. “Perhaps He is.”

That afternoon she went to the parish school, where she taught the girls their letters and their catechism, and where the vicar’s wife, Mrs. Ashby, was already seated in the corner, her hands folded over her bombazine, her bonnet nodding approval at intervals. Mrs. Ashby had been kind to Eleanor in the beginning, in the way one is kind to a clever dog that has learned to sit. She had lent her books, and corrected her pronunciation of “sacerdotal,” and had once said, in company, that Mrs. Vance was “quite a scholar, you know,” in a tone that made the word a warning.

“I hear,” Mrs. Ashby said now, when the girls were bent over their slates, “that Mr. Hodge is to have Wexcombe.”

Eleanor kept her eyes on the slate before her. “So I understand.”

“Such a comfort,” Mrs. Ashby said, “for the parish. Mrs. Hodge is so very sensible.”

“Indeed.”

“And your dear husband—such a disappointment for him. But he is young yet. And so diligent. I am sure he will find something in time.”

Eleanor looked up. “He has been diligent for eight years, Mrs. Ashby. He has buried your dead and baptised your children and sat up three nights with the Cooper child when the fever was in the town. He has not been idle.”

Mrs. Ashby’s bonnet was still. “I did not say he had been idle, Mrs. Vance.”

“No,” Eleanor said. “You did not say it.”

There was a silence in which the scratch of slates seemed very loud. Then Mrs. Ashby rose, and smoothed her bombazine, and said that she had calls to make, and went out, and Eleanor was left with the girls and the smell of chalk and the knowledge that she had made an enemy of a woman who had only ever been condescending, which was not, in the end, the same as cruel.

That evening she walked down to the river, where the poor of the parish lived in cottages that leaned together like old men in a storm. She had brought a basket of apples from the rectory garden, and she meant to leave them at the door of the Widow Pike, who had a cough and a son in the militia. But as she came near, she heard voices through the thin wall—the widow’s voice, and another woman’s, and her own name.
