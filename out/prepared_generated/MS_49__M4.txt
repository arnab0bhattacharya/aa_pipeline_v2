The packet from the Levant, stained with salt and the finger-marks of many carriers, lay upon the table between us, and for a long while my sister Arabella did not break the seal. She had written to him so often, with such ardent encouragement, such confident prophecies of the noble work that awaited him, that I believe she feared, in that moment, to meet her own words returning to her like accusations. When at last she unfolded the letter, her countenance changed by degrees, as the sky changes when the sun abandons it; and when she had finished, she let the paper fall and sat motionless, her hands clasped in her lap, as one who has received a sentence.

Our brother Edmund—for so I must call him still, though the sea lies between us and I know not whether he lives—had been from his boyhood a creature of that exalted temper which kindles at the recital of wrongs and burns to redress them. At school he was ever the champion of the oppressed, and came home bruised and exulting; at the university he forsook the pleasant fields of learning for the thorny paths of politics, and spoke of liberty with a fervour that made the dullest listener catch fire. When the tidings came of the rising in Greece—of a Christian people lifting their chains against their oppressors, of the graves of Thermopylae and Marathon profaned by the hoof of the barbarian—he could not be restrained. I remember the evening: the rain beat against the windows of our little parlour in the village of Ashcombe, and the wind moaned among the elms, and Edmund stood before us, his face illuminated, his voice trembling with a holy enthusiasm, and declared that he could no longer eat the bread of inglorious ease while the descendants of those who fell at Salamis bled for the faith of their fathers. Arabella, ever the echo of his nobler passions, rose and embraced him, and bade him go, and wept for joy that she had a brother worthy of the age. I confess that I was silent, and my heart misgave me; for I had read the histories with a colder eye, and I knew that the love of liberty is one thing and the love of men another, and that the field of battle is not the field of Marathon, however the poets may paint it.

He sailed in the spring, full of hope, bearing letters to the committees of philhellenes in the cities of the Morea, and for a time his dispatches were all that Arabella could desire. He wrote of the mountains purple in the dawn, of the sea breaking in silver upon the ruins of temples, of the solemn joy of beholding a people rise from the dust of ages. But by degrees the tone of his letters altered, as the clear stream grows turbid when the rains descend. He spoke of delays, of jealousies, of captains who would not obey and soldiers who would not fight; of money that vanished in the hands of the chieftains and never reached the camp; of the burning of villages and the massacre of prisoners, and of deeds done in the name of liberty that made him blush to bear the name of Christian. His last letter, which Arabella now held in her trembling hand, I shall set down as nearly as I can remember it.

“I am sick at heart,” he wrote, “and the enthusiasm which bore me hither has died within me like a flame extinguished by a sudden rain. I have seen the cause I loved divided against itself; I have seen men who prated of the freedom of Hellas refuse to free a single captive; I have seen the spoils of the slain divided among those who came too late to the battle. I have marched with a band of brave men, and I have watched them fall one by one, not in the shock of war, but by the slow fever of the camp, and by the cruelty of those who should have been their leaders. I begin to think that I have been a fool, and that the dreams of a boy are no armour against the realities of life. Yet I do not repent that I came; I only repent that I did not come with a wiser heart. If I fall, let it be remembered that I fell in a cause which, though degraded by those who profess it, is yet the cause of humanity. Farewell—pray for me, and do not grieve.”

Arabella read these words aloud, and when she had finished, she pressed the letter to her lips, and her tears fell fast upon it. “I sent him,” she cried, “I sent him with my own hand, and I rejoiced at his departure as at a festival! I told him that the field of glory awaited him; I bade him remember that the eyes of all England were upon him. And now he is dying in a strange land, and the cause he loved is not worthy of him, and I have murdered him with my praises!” I sought to comfort her, but my words were cold, for I too felt the weight of that which she had done. We had fed his ardour with our admiration, and had never once asked whether the object of it was worthy of the sacrifice.

The winter passed heavily, and no further letter came. Arabella grew pale and silent, and would sit for hours at the window watching the road, as if she expected him to return. At length, in the spring, there arrived a stranger, a Greek merchant, who brought a packet sealed with Edmund’s ring, and a letter written in a hand that faltered at every line. He had been wounded in an obscure skirmish, he said, and had lain for weeks in a fever in a ruined monastery, tended by a poor priest who knew no tongue but his own.
