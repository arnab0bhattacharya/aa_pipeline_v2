The air in the Board of Health offices, usually thick with the scent of stale ink and coal smoke, now carried a new, metallic tang of disinfectant. It was a futile gesture. The smell of fear was stronger. Arthur Finch, a man whose life was a neat ledger of other people’s sickness and death, felt it in the very paper he handled. For a week, the figures had been wrong. Not wrong in their arithmetic, but wrong in their reality. The numbers, stark and black in his meticulous hand, told a story that the world outside was not yet ready to read.

It was called ‘The Gas’ by the street-corner hawkers and ‘London Sickness’ by the more cautious broadsheets. The authorities, embodied by the portly and perpetually perspiring Under-Secretary, Mr. Abernathy, had issued a statement: a bout of seasonal influenza, exacerbated by the autumnal damp. A matter for physicians, not the government. But Arthur’s ledgers, the cold, hard accounting of mortality, told a different tale. The mortality rate from ‘pneumonia’ and ‘respiratory failure’ had tripled in three weeks. The cases were not confined to the tenements of Whitechapel but were now appearing in the fine houses of Kensington. The pattern was a terrifying, upward curve.

Arthur was a man built for quietude. His world was the scratch of his nib on foolscap, the precise alignment of columns, the satisfying finality of a sum balanced. He lived in a small, rented room in Bloomsbury, took his lunch at the same chophouse every day, and read the *London Chronicle* on the omnibus home. He was a creature of order, and the world was becoming dangerously, chaotically untidy. He would see the faces in the street—pale, clutching handkerchiefs to their mouths, their eyes holding a new, hollowed-out look—and then he would return to his desk and write down the cold fact of their neighbours’ demise.

The breaking point came on a Tuesday. A telegram arrived from a parish in Southwark. The vicar, a man named Davies, reported not three, but thirty-seven deaths in his parish over the weekend alone. He wrote of a ‘breathless pestilence’ that stole a person’s life between one heartbeat and the next. Arthur copied the figures into the master ledger, his hand steady, but a tremor began deep inside him. He looked at the neat column: ‘Southwark, St. Jude’s Parish. Week 43. Deaths: 37.’ It was just data. But it was a lie, a terrible, sanitised lie. It was Mrs. Gable from the baker’s, the young lad who ran messages for the telegraph office, old Mr. Hemlock who always nodded to him on his way to the park. They were not data.

He looked across the office at Abernathy, who was dictating a letter to a clerk, his voice a smooth, reassuring baritone. “...and let us assure the public that there is absolutely no cause for alarm. The situation is entirely under control.” Abernathy caught Arthur’s eye and gave a small, tight smile, a gesture of command. *Do your job. File the numbers. Be invisible.*

That evening, Arthur did not go to his chophouse. Instead, he walked. He walked through streets that seemed to hold their breath. The usual clamour of London was muted. Carriages rattled by with their windows shut tight. A child’s cough echoed unnaturally in an alleyway. He walked until he found himself outside the imposing granite edifice of the *London Chronicle*. The gas lamps hissed, casting long, dancing shadows. In the great windows, he could see the frantic energy of the newsroom, the clatter of typewriters like a frantic heartbeat. He had read the *Chronicle*’s front page that morning: ‘MINOR FLU OUTBREAK – AUTHORITIES URGE CALM.’ It was the same lie, dressed in slightly different clothes.

He had the evidence. A small, leather-bound notebook was tucked inside his waistcoat, pressed against his ribs. In it, he had copied the key figures, the parish reports, the stark, undeniable truth. It was a simple thing. Walk in, ask for the editor, and hand it over. The thought was both terrifying and exhilarating. It was the most disordered act of his life.

A man in a heavy overcoat and a bowler hat was leaving the building, pausing to light a pipe. He had the tired, sharp eyes of a journalist. Arthur’s heart hammered against his ribs. This was it. He took a half-step forward, his hand moving to his waistcoat. The journalist looked up, his gaze sweeping over Arthur—his clerical grey suit, his pale, anxious face, his worn shoes. The man’s eyes held a flicker of professional curiosity, but it was quickly extinguished by dismissal. He saw nothing of importance. A clerk, perhaps, lost on his way home. The journalist turned and strode off into the gloom, a plume of smoke trailing behind him.

The moment passed. Arthur stood frozen on the pavement, the cold stone of the building seeming to leech the warmth from his body. The impulse was gone, replaced by a wave of crushing self-awareness. Who was he to challenge Abernathy, the government, the entire weight of official denial? He was Arthur Finch, a clerk. His duty was to the ledger. To accuracy. To order. To take this notebook to a journalist would be an act of chaos. It would create headlines, panic, questions. It would tear a hole in the fabric of the world he knew.

He turned and walked away, the notebook a leaden weight against his heart. The journey back to Bloomsbury was a blur. The city felt like a vast, sleeping beast, its illness a secret fever in its veins. He let himself into his silent room, the familiar scent of old books and floor polish a small comfort. He sat at his small desk, the one where he did his own private accounts, and took out the notebook. He read the figures again, each number a name he could not know, a life extinguished. The vicar’s words echoed in his mind: *a breathless pestilence*.
