The rain had fallen without ceasing for three days over Harrowgate, and the smoke of the mill chimneys, beaten down by the weather, hung in the streets like a sullen fog that would not lift. It was on the fourth morning, when the clouds broke at last and a pale, watery sun laid itself along the wet slates, that I first saw Miss Eleanor Ashcombe descend from her uncle’s carriage at the gate of the Rectory, and I confess my heart hardened within me at the sight.

She was tall, and carried herself with that easy grace which money bestows and labour never learns; her pelisse was of a fine blue merino, cut in the newest fashion, and her hair, dark and abundant, was dressed by hands more skilful than any in our valley could boast. I stood at the parlour window with my aunt’s mending in my lap, and I thought—God forgive me—that I hated her before she had spoken a word; for I was but the rector’s niece, plain Lucy Hargreaves, with a dowry of seventy pounds and a face that the looking-glass told me was honest rather than handsome, and I had already learned that in this world the heiress walks in where the poor relation must knock and wait.

But I wronged her, and I have been glad all my life since that I was given the occasion to know it. She came to the Rectory that afternoon with her uncle, Mr. Justice Ashcombe, who was the principal owner of the Harrowgate mills; and when my uncle presented me, she took my hand in both of hers and said, with a warmth that no art could counterfeit: “I have heard of you from the vicar of Kirklees, Miss Hargreaves. He says you have read more books than any woman in the Riding, and that you can hold your tongue about it, which is rarer still.” And she laughed, and I laughed, and the ice was broken between us.

Thereafter she came often to the Rectory. Her uncle’s house was a great stone mansion on the hill, full of servants and silence, and she told me once, with a candour that startled me, that she would rather sit in our little parlour with the wind in the chimneys than in her own drawing-room with nothing but the clock for company. We read together, and walked together on the moor when the weather permitted, and she would question me about the poor of the parish, and the children in the mill, and the sickness that came with the winter, in a manner that showed her heart was not set upon her own pleasures. I began to love her; and yet I was never quite at ease with her, for there was that in her position which stood between us like a pane of glass—invisible, but cold.

It was in the February of that year that I first perceived how matters stood with regard to Mr. Henry Markland. He was the owner of the Lower Mill, a man of five-and-thirty, grave and spare of speech, with a face that seemed to have been cut out of the very stone of the district—hard, patient, and worn by weather. His father had built the mill, and he had sunk into it every shilling he possessed, and now the times were against him: the war had closed the foreign markets, the machines were old, and the men who had money in the trade were waiting, like hawks, for him to fail. I had known him since I was a girl; he had been my uncle’s churchwarden, and had sat in our parlour of a Sunday evening, and I had thought him the most honourable man in Harrowgate, and—I will not deny it—I had thought of him more warmly than I had any right to think of a man who had never spoken a word of love to me.

But when Eleanor Ashcombe came, I saw that he looked at her as he had never looked at me. It was not the look of a fortune-hunter—I will do him that justice—for he was too proud for such baseness; it was rather the look of a man who sees in another the very thing he lacks: the ease, the freedom, the power to act without fear of ruin. And she, for her part, did not conceal her regard for him. She spoke of him with an admiration that was all the more dangerous because it was sincere. “He is the only man in this valley,” she said to me one day, “who cares for the people he employs as if they were his own children. My uncle calls him a fool for it. I call him a hero.”

I said nothing. I could not say what was in my heart, for it was a poor, mean thing, and I was ashamed of it.

The crisis came in March. Mr. Markland’s chief creditor, a banker in Leeds, called in his loan, and it was known in the town that unless the sum of three thousand pounds could be raised within the month, the Lower Mill would be sold, and the man who had given his life to it would be turned into the street. I heard the news from my uncle at breakfast, and I went out into the garden and stood by the bare apple-tree, and I felt as if the world had grown suddenly dark. Three thousand pounds! It might as well have been three million. I had seventy pounds, and my uncle had little more, and the poor of the parish could not raise a shilling.

That afternoon Eleanor came to the Rectory. She was pale, and her eyes were bright with a purpose that I did not understand. She took me by the arm and drew me into the little summer-house at the end of the garden, and there she said to me, without preface: “Lucy, I have three thousand pounds of my own, settled upon me by my mother’s will. I can save him. But I will not do it unless you tell me that I may.”
