I have been asked, in my time, to account for many things—my presence in that climate, the colour of my skin, the reasons for my silence—but never has an accounting been demanded of me with such polite ferocity as when the General’s daughter was placed upon the threshold of my establishment. It was the dry season in Puerto Azul, that interminable season when the heat does not merely weigh upon the body but seems to penetrate the very marrow, baking the thoughts until they crack like the mud of the riverbeds. The city lay under a white glare, a place of crumbling stucco and rusting iron, inhabited by a people who had learned to speak in whispers and look over their shoulders.

I am an Englishwoman. I state this not as a boast of nationality, but as a fact of geography and temperament, like the existence of a rock in a stream. I had come there with a small inheritance and a foolish notion of civilising the savage heart through the instruction of young girls. I ran the school—St. Theresa’s, I called it, with a touch of irony that was entirely lost upon my patrons—out of a large, high-ceilinged house that had once belonged to a Spanish merchant. It smelled of damp plaster, floor wax, and the drying ink of exercise books. It was a fortress of boredom, and I was its gaoler.

The General, whose name was Valeriano, did not arrive with the usual clamour of his kind. There were no bugles, no clatter of hooves on the cobblestones. He came in the twilight, when the shadows of the palms began to stretch across the veranda like long, grasping fingers. He was a small man, surprisingly so, dressed in a suit of white linen that glowed with a ghostly luminescence in the gathering dark. He smelled of expensive tobacco and something else, perhaps the faint, metallic odour of fear that clings to men who live by the sword.

“Madam,” he said, his voice soft, almost caressing, yet possessing the hard edge of a blade drawn across a whetstone. “I have a daughter. She is... difficult. She requires the English discipline. The English coldness.”

He stood there, twisting a heavy gold ring on his finger, his eyes—dark, liquid, and utterly unreadable—fixed upon my face. He was the leader of the insurgents, the man who held the mountain passes and threatened the capital, a figure of terror to the government and of messianic hope to the peasantry. To me, he was merely a client.

“I teach arithmetic, General. Geography. The history of the British Empire,” I replied, my hands folded in my lap to hide their trembling. “I do not teach politics.”

“Everything is politics, Madam,” he murmured, looking out at the harbour where the lights of a gunboat blinked in the distance, a solitary, rhythmic eye watching the shore. “But I ask only that you teach her to write a good hand. She is wild. Like the country.”

The girl arrived the next morning. Her name was Isabella. She was not wild in the manner of a savage, but wild in the manner of a trapped animal—silent, sullen, and possessed of a vitality that seemed too large for her slight frame. She wore a simple white frock, yet she carried herself with the haughty indifference of a queen. She did not speak to the other girls, the daughters of the local merchants and the minor officials, who eyed her with a mixture of curiosity and revulsion.

To teach Isabella was to engage in a battle of silences. She would sit at the desk, her dark hair braided severely, her eyes fixed on the chalkboard where I wrote the names of rivers and kings. She knew nothing of the Thames or the Danube; her world was the Rio Seco and the blood-red soil of the sierra. When I asked her a question, she would answer in monosyllables, her voice flat, betraying no accent of the mountains, only the clipped, harsh tones of the city’s poor.

“Why do you wish to learn this, Isabella?” I asked her one afternoon, the heat pressing down upon us like a physical weight, the air thick with the drone of flies.

She looked at me then, really looked at me, and for the first time, I saw the shadow of her father in her eyes.

“My father wishes it,” she said. “He says the world is a book, and if we cannot read it, we are blind.”

“And what does he wish you to see?”

“That the strong survive, Madam. And that the weak are merely the soil in which the strong grow.”

It was a chilling sentiment, delivered with the innocence of a child reciting a nursery rhyme. I felt a sudden, sharp premonition of danger, a sense that my school, my quiet refuge of ink and order, had been breached by the chaotic forces of the world outside.

The change in the atmosphere was imperceptible at first, like the drop in temperature before a storm. The mothers of my other pupils began to find excuses. Their daughters were ill; the heat was too much; the journey was too far. The school, once a bustling hive of girlish chatter, became a hollow shell. I saw the way the shopkeepers looked at me when I went to buy provisions—the averted eyes, the sudden silence when I entered a room. I was no longer the harmless English eccentric. I was the governess of the rebel. I was tainted.

One evening, as I sat on the veranda watching the sun sink into the ocean—a violent, bloody spectacle of red and purple—a group of men passed by the gate. They were soldiers of the Republic, the government forces. They were not the polished officers of the capital, but rough men with sweat-stained uniforms and rifles slung carelessly over their shoulders. They stopped. They looked at the house. One of them spat into the dust.
