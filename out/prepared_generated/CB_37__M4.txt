The sea at Scarborough is a different creature from the sea I had known at ——; there it was a grey and sullen thing, forever fretting against a cold shore, and the wind that came off it carried the salt of desolation. Here it stretched out broad and glittering under a September sun, and the waves broke with a white and laughing edge upon the sand, as if the whole creation were at peace and gladness. Yet I stood upon the cliff-path that morning with no eye for its beauty, for my heart was sore within me, and I had been weeping where none could see.

I had come down with the Harcourts—my employer, his lady, and their three children—for a fortnight’s change of air; and the change, for my part, was chiefly a change of servitude. At —— Hall I was governess to the two elder girls, and my life was one of quiet and regular labour, of lessons conned and tasks corrected, of early rising and late watching, with now and then a half-hour of solitude in which I might read and think and be myself. But at the seaside all routine was broken; the children were wild with liberty, and I was expected to be at once their nurse, their playfellow, and their jailer; to keep them from the water’s edge and yet to amuse them; to be cheerful when I was weary, and patient when I was fretted to the soul. Mrs. Harcourt, a kind but indolent woman, left all to me; and Mr. Harcourt, who was of a sanguine and thoughtless temper, would have it that I was the happiest creature in the world, because I had “the blessing of health and the pleasures of the sea.”

It was upon the third morning, as I walked with the children upon the sands, that I saw approaching a party of ladies and gentlemen whose air and dress bespoke the fashion of the place. I would have turned aside, for I was in my plain stuff gown, with my bonnet of the year before, and my hands were full of shells and seaweed; but the children ran forward, and in a moment I heard a voice exclaim, “Why, surely! It is not—it cannot be—Ellen Fairfax!”

I looked up, and my eyes met the face of Amelia Thorne—no, Amelia Beaumont now, for she had married a rich merchant of Leeds, and was become a lady of consequence. She was handsomer than in her school-days, and that is saying much; her complexion had the glow of prosperity, her dress was of the newest mode, and her hand, as she extended it to me, was white and jewelled. She had been my friend once—at least, she had called me so; and I had loved her with the passionate devotion of a girl who has few to love. But there had been a coolness between us at the last; she had laughed at my “romantic notions,” as she termed them, and I had been too proud to explain myself; and so we had parted with a kiss indeed, but with a little reserve on both sides.

“My dear Ellen!” she cried, with an effusion that I felt to be half theatrical. “What an unexpected pleasure! And how do you do? And are these your pupils? Dear little things! And you are at Scarborough—with whom? Not alone, surely?”

I told her I was with the Harcourts, and she nodded and smiled, and said she knew the name—a very respectable family—and then she drew me aside, leaving the children to the care of her maid, and walked with me along the sands, her arm in mine, and her tongue going at a great rate about her husband, her carriage, her house in Leeds, her visit to the Spa, and the fine company she had met there. I listened and said little, for I was conscious of a strange sinking at the heart. She had been my schoolfellow; we had sat on the same bench, conned the same tasks, and shared the same hopes. And now she was a wife and a mistress, and I was a governess, dependent on the will of another for my daily bread.

“But, my dear Ellen,” said she, stopping suddenly, and looking at me with a compassion that made my cheek burn, “you look thin and pale. You are not well? You work too hard, I am afraid. Ah, I often think of you, and of the difference in our lots! You were always the clever one, you know—the one who was to do something great; and here I am, a poor simple body, with nothing but my husband and my home; and you—you are wearing your life out in teaching other people’s children. It is very sad—very sad indeed.”

I thanked her for her solicitude, and said that I was very well, and that my life, though laborious, was not unhappy. But she shook her head with a knowing smile, and went on:

“Ah, you are proud, Ellen—always were. You will not confess. But I can see how it is. You are not fitted for such a life; you have too much sensibility. I remember how you used to cry over your books, and talk of poetry and the like. And now—to be shut up in a schoolroom, with no society, no pleasures, no—no prospects! It is a thousand pities. If I could do anything for you—but, alas! I have no influence in that way. Mr. Beaumont is a good man, but he does not like governesses; he says they are always intriguing. But I might speak to him—I might, indeed, if you would let me.”

I felt the blood rush to my temples. “You are very kind,” I said, “but I have no wish to change my situation. I am content.”
