The house was called Ashbourne, and it stood in a hollow of the Derbyshire hills, where the wind came down from the moors with a bitterness that seemed to have gathered in its passage over miles of stone and heather. It was a handsome house, grey and square and solid, with a portico of weathered columns and windows that looked out upon a lawn which the sun rarely visited. I had been governess there for seven months, and in that time I had learned the geography of its corridors as a prisoner learns the walls of his cell—not from love, but from necessity.

Mrs. Ellwood had engaged me in London, where I had answered her advertisement with a trembling hand and a heart that beat too fast with hope. She had seemed then a kindly woman, ample and fair, with a voice that dripped honey and a manner that promised protection. I had been deceived, as the simple are always deceived, by the appearance of goodness rather than its substance. For Mrs. Ellwood was kind only to those whom she considered her equals, and to a governess she was not kind at all; she was merely not unkind, which is a different thing altogether, and colder.

It was on a Tuesday evening in November that I heard them. I had gone down to the library to fetch a book I had left there—a volume of sermons that I read not from piety but from a certain grim comfort in their certainty—and as I approached the door, which stood ajar, I heard my name spoken in the drawing-room beyond. I should have gone away. I know that now, and I knew it then; but there is in me a strain of that morbid curiosity which makes the wounded creature turn to look upon the knife. I stopped. I listened.

"She is useful enough," Mrs. Ellwood was saying, "in her way. The children are quieter since she came. But she has a look about her—I cannot describe it—as though she were always judging one. It is most disagreeable in a person of her station."

"Then why keep her?" said Mr. Ellwood, in the voice of a man who discusses the weather.

"Because she is cheap," replied his wife, "and because she does not complain. I had a governess once who complained of the cold. Can you imagine? In a house like this. I told her that if she was cold she might walk in the garden for exercise, and that if she was still cold she might consider the state of her soul, which was likely colder still."

Mr. Ellwood laughed—a low, comfortable laugh, like the sound of a fire settling in its grate. "You are too severe, my dear."

"I am practical," she said. "The girl has no money and no family worth mentioning. She is fortunate to have a roof. If she does not like it, she may leave. There are a hundred like her in London, waiting for just such a place."

I stood in the passage with my hand upon the door-frame, and I felt the blood go out of my face and then rush back again with a heat that burned my eyelids. I thought of the long journey from London in the coach, and how I had looked out at the hedgerows and told myself that I was going to a home. I thought of the little room at the top of the stairs where I slept, and how I had arranged my few books upon the shelf and set my mother's miniature upon the table, and called it my own. I thought of the children—Edith and little Thomas—and how they had come to me in the first weeks with their small troubles and their smaller joys, and how I had held them and taught them and loved them, though I had no right to love them, being only their governess.

And then I thought of the words I had just heard, and I saw myself as they saw me: a thing without feeling, a piece of furniture that could be moved from room to room, that could be spoken of in the third person while it stood within hearing, that could be dismissed when it grew shabby or inconvenient. I went back up the stairs without the book, and I sat upon my bed in the dark, and I did not weep. There is a grief that is beyond weeping, a dry and terrible grief that hardens the heart as frost hardens the earth.

That night, at about two in the morning, I was wakened by a sound that brought me out of my bed and to my feet before I was fully conscious. It was a cry—a child's cry, thin and sharp and full of pain—and it came from the nursery at the end of the corridor. I threw my shawl about me and went out. The passage was dark, but I knew it well, and I found the nursery door and pushed it open. The fire had burned low, and by its faint light I saw Edith standing beside her brother's cot, her small face white with terror.

"He will not wake," she whispered. "I have shaken him and shaken him, and he will not wake."

I went to the cot. Thomas lay upon his back, his face flushed scarlet, his breath coming in quick, shallow gasps. I laid my hand upon his forehead, and it was dry and burning as a stone in the sun. I felt his pulse, and it fluttered under my fingers like a trapped bird. I had seen fever before—in the village where I was born, when the sickness came and took the children one by one—and I knew what I saw now. It was the scarlet fever, and it was the kind that kills.
