The rain had stopped—that was the first thing, the cessation of it, the sudden silence in which the gutters still ran and the air, washed and thin, carried the smell of wet stone and something faintly green, a hint of gardens beyond the grey façades. Edwin stood at the window of his rooms in Bloomsbury, his hand upon the curtain, and watched the last drops fall from the sill above, each one catching, as it fell, a particle of the pale afternoon light that had begun to leak through the clouds, so that for a moment the air seemed strung with small descending lamps, each extinguishing itself upon the pavement below with a tiny, unheard report. He had been standing there, he supposed, for some quarter of an hour, though time had taken on that quality—was it a quality?—of stretching and contracting, so that the minutes since Beatrice had left him (she had left him, had she not? had she not said, in her clear, composed voice, that she would not wait, that he must come to her father’s house in Surrey on Thursday, that the train left at four?) seemed to have elapsed in a single beat of the heart, or the heart to have beaten a thousand times in a single minute.

Beatrice. He said the name to himself, and it produced, as always, a sensation of something smooth and cool and perfectly proportioned—a marble hand, perhaps, laid upon his forehead. She was beautiful; everyone said so; her photograph had been in the Illustrated London News, and her father, Sir Henry Ashcombe, had a house in Surrey with a cedar on the lawn and a drive that curved, and the curve was the curve that everyone admired, and Beatrice, standing at the top of the steps in her white dress, was the figure that completed it. He was to marry her in the autumn. It was settled. It had the quality of a thing settled—a house bought, a treaty signed, a boundary line drawn upon a map in ink that would not run.

But the rain had stopped, and he found that he was thinking not of Beatrice but of the way Nellie Marsh had laughed at him on Tuesday. She had laughed—not unkindly, that was the thing, not with any malice, but with a kind of sudden, helpless explosion of merriment, as if the sight of him standing there, his umbrella dripping, his collar wilting, his carefully prepared sentence about the Ruskin exhibition scattered to the winds, had been too much for her; and her laugh had gone up, a sharp, unmusical sound, into the dusty air of the office where she sat at her typewriter, and she had put her hand over her mouth and said, “Oh, Mr. Farriday, I am sorry—I am sorry—but you did look so terribly solemn,” and he had felt, beneath the sting of it, something loosen in his chest, some knot that Beatrice’s perfections had drawn tighter and tighter, and he had laughed too, then, standing there with his dripping umbrella, laughed until the tears came into his eyes.

The office. He had gone there to deliver a message from his uncle—a matter of a contract, a paragraph to be altered—and Nellie Marsh had taken it from him, her fingers stained with ink, her hair coming down, her blouse (he noticed these things, he could not help noticing them) slightly torn at the cuff, and she had read it through, her lips moving, and had said, “Well, that’s clear enough, but it’s wrong, you know. It’s wrong in the third line. Mr. Harbottle won’t sign that.” And he had said, with the assurance of a man who has never in his life been contradicted by a woman he admired, “I think you will find it is correct,” and she had looked up at him, and her eyes—grey, not blue, not the blue of Beatrice’s eyes, which were like the sky in a picture—had held a glint of something that was not quite respect, and she had said, “Shall we ask Mr. Harbottle?” and he had said, “By all means,” and Mr. Harbottle had said that Miss Marsh was right, and Nellie Marsh had not said, “I told you so,” she had simply taken the paper and begun to retype it, and he had stood there, dismissed, and then she had looked up and laughed.

He turned from the window. The room was dim; the fire was low; a letter from Beatrice lay upon the table, and he did not open it. He knew what it would say. It would say that he had been odd on Sunday, that he had seemed preoccupied, that her mother had remarked upon it, that he must remember that they were to dine with the Marchbanks on the fourteenth, and that she sent her love. Her love. He took up the letter and put it down again. The paper was thick, cream-coloured, and it bore the Ashcombe crest, which was a heron, and the heron stood upon one leg in the corner of the envelope and regarded him, he thought, with a cold and ancient eye.

Nellie Marsh lived in a house in Camden Town with her mother and a sister who was a nurse, and she walked to the office every morning, and she had once told him—why had she told him? they had been standing at the door, waiting for the rain to stop, and she had said, “I like the rain, Mr. Farriday. It makes the streets look as if they belonged to somebody else”—she had once told him that she had never been to the theatre, not once, not even to the pantomime, and that she would like, above all things, to see a play by Mr. Shaw, because she had read one, and it had made her laugh, and she thought a play that made you laugh was worth more than a play that made you cry, though she supposed that was very shocking.
