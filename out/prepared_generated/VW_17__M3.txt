The gravel on the path through Lincoln’s Inn was the colour of dampened bone, and Catherine, stepping carefully, felt the sound of her own boots — crunch, crunch — to be the only thing holding the afternoon together. It was October; the air had that peculiar, brittle quality of something about to shatter, and the leaves, turning in on themselves, were the colour of rust, of old leather, of the bindings in her father’s library. Mr. Vance was walking beside her. He was saying something about the Chancery division; about a case, a point of law, a ruling; his voice was like the ticking of a clock in an empty room — steady, earnest, unassailably correct. He admired her, she knew. He admired the way she walked, the way she held her gloves, the way her grey velvet coat absorbed the pale light. He would tell her so later, in the drawing room in Kensington, with a kind of trembling, dog-like sincerity that made her want to cry, or laugh, or simply turn away and look at the fire.

She had said yes. That was the fact of it. She had said yes because there was no reason to say no. He was her father’s most promising pupil; he would be a KC one day; he had a clean, undented forehead and hands that never shook; and when he had knelt — rather clumsily, knocking the edge of the Persian rug with his knee — she had looked down at the parting in his hair and thought, *So this is what happens.* One says yes; one is supposed to say yes; and the machinery begins to turn, the vast, indifferent machinery of dresses and flowers and announcements in *The Times*, and one is carried along on it as one is carried along on a river, past the reeds, past the muddy banks, past all the places where one might have stopped, might have stepped out onto the grass and simply stood still.

But standing still was the one thing, Catherine thought, pausing by the stone balustrade, that one was never permitted to do.

The sky was the colour of a bruised plum over the rooftops. A pigeon strutted on the ledge below, its neck iridescent, catching the light in shades of green and purple, and for a moment she watched it with a fierceness of attention that startled her — as if the bird, with its absurd, mechanical bobbing, contained some answer, some clue to the question she could not quite formulate. What was the question? She did not know. She only knew that something was missing; that there ought to be more; that the world — this world of law courts and chambers and the long, panelled corridors where her father walked with his gown billowing behind him like a sail — was a sketch, a mere outline, and that the real thing, the thing itself, with all its colour and terror and light, lay somewhere just beyond the edge of it.

And then Mr. Vance spoke, and the moment contracted, folded in on itself, became ordinary again.

"The leaves are rather fine," he said, gesturing with his umbrella at the plane trees. His voice was full of a gentle, tentative wonder, as if he had discovered this observation himself and was rather proud of it. "I don’t think I’ve ever noticed them quite so — so — "

"Yellow?" Catherine offered.

"Yellow," he repeated, and smiled, and she saw that he was pleased she had helped him, pleased that they had arrived at the word together, as if this were a small marriage of minds, a foretaste of the decades to come — decades of arriving at words together, of completing each other’s sentences, of sitting across from each other at breakfast with the marmalade between them and the morning paper folded back, and the silence, the enormous silence, filled with nothing but the sound of two people being perfectly, irreproachably content.

She felt a wave of something — not revulsion, not despair, but something colder, more lucid — the recognition that she was standing at the edge of her own life and watching it happen to someone else. As if the woman in the grey velvet coat, with the engagement ring (her mother’s diamond, reset, rather ostentatiously, in a platinum band) catching the thin light, were a stranger; a figure in a painting she had once seen in a gallery, all muted browns and greys, beautifully composed, perfectly still, and utterly, utterly lifeless.

They walked on. The trees in the square were shedding their leaves with a kind of reluctant grace, each one falling as if it were the first to do so, as if the tree were inventing the act of falling, moment by moment, leaf by leaf. She watched one — a single, curled leaf, brown at the edges, still holding a thread of green at its centre — descend in a long, hesitating spiral and come to rest on the path. Mr. Vance’s boot came down on it. She heard the faint, dry crack. He did not notice.

In the house in Kensington the fire was lit. It was always lit. Her mother saw to that. The drawing room was a place of cushions and antimacassars and the faint, persistent smell of lavender and wax, and Catherine, sitting on the sofa with her hands folded in her lap, felt the room close around her like a jewel box. Her mother was pouring tea. Mr. Vance was talking to her father about some matter of conveyancing. The words floated across the room and dissolved against the picture rail. Her father nodded, stroked his beard, looked at Mr. Vance with that expression of calm, proprietary satisfaction which meant: *Yes, this is the one; this is right; this is what I have chosen.*
