My name is Abel Pinchbeck, and at the time this history commences I was a clerk of some two-and-twenty years, seated upon a high stool in the offices of Messrs. Quill, Quagley, and Vellum, solicitors, of Symond’s Inn, in the parish of St. Clement Danes. I was a slender young man with a stoop in the shoulders, a complexion the colour of a candle-end, and a habit of holding my pen as though it were a live thing that might bite me; and I lived, at that period, in a little back room in Lamb’s Conduit Street, where the landlady’s cat and I contended nightly for the warmest corner of the hearth.

One November afternoon, when the fog had got into the office and refused to be got out again, and Mr. Quagley himself had gone home with a cold, and the junior clerk was asleep with his head upon the ledger, old Mr. Vellum—who was the eldest of the firm and wore spectacles so thick that his eyes appeared to swim behind them like oysters in a bottle—summoned me to his desk, and placed in my hands a packet.

It was a small packet, wrapped in brown paper, sealed with three seals, tied about with red tape, and inscribed in a cramped and crabbed hand: *For Mrs. Althea Winterbourne, at Candleford Hall, in the county of Essex. To be delivered into her own hands, and no other.*

‘Pinchbeck,’ said Mr. Vellum, ‘you will go down with this by the four o’clock coach. You will deliver it into the lady’s own hands—into her own hands, mind you, and no other—and you will bring me back her answer, if she gives one, and if she does not, you will bring me back word of her face and her manner of receiving it, for I am too old to travel and too curious to be easy. Take your hat, Pinchbeck, and don’t lose the packet, and don’t lose yourself, and don’t, on any account, lose your head.’

I promised him I would lose nothing, and went out into the fog with the packet buttoned inside my coat, and my heart beating against it as though it were a second packet of greater consequence.

The coach set me down at a turning of the road where a milestone leaned like a drunken man, and a signpost pointed one arm at the sky and the other at the ground, as if it had given up all hope of agreeing with itself. From thence I walked a mile and a half between hedges that dripped and ditches that gurgled, until I came to a pair of iron gates, tall and rusty, with a lodge beside them in which a fire burned and a dog barked and nobody answered. So I opened the gates myself, and went up a long avenue of elms, whose bare boughs creaked and knocked together overhead like the bones of an old skeleton in the wind, and so came at last to Candleford Hall.

It was a great, gaunt, solemn house of red brick, with more windows than there were fires to warm them, and a flight of stone steps before the door as wide as a street. The knocker was a lion’s head, and I knocked with it timidly, and the sound went rolling through the house as if it had fallen down a well. A serving-man in a worn livery admitted me, and took my name, and left me standing in a hall so vast and so cold that I could see my own breath, and so silent that I could hear the clock upon the stairs ticking out the seconds as if it grudged them.

Presently I was shown into a sitting-room, where a lady stood by the window with her back to me, looking out upon the darkening park. She was tall and straight, dressed in black, and when she turned I saw that her face, though handsome, was pale and set, like a face carved in ivory, and that her eyes were of that grey which is nearer to winter than to spring.

‘You are from Messrs. Quill, Quagley, and Vellum,’ she said. It was not a question.

‘I am, ma’am,’ said I, ‘and I have brought a packet, which I am to deliver into your own hands, and no other.’

‘Give it to me,’ she said. She took it, and looked at the writing upon it, and I thought her lip moved, though whether it was a smile or a tremble I could not tell. Then she laid it upon the table, unopened, and put her hand upon it, and stood a moment very still.

‘You will call again,’ she said, ‘next week—upon the same day, at the same hour—and I will give you my answer.’

I said I would, and she rang the bell, and I was shown out, and walked back through the creaking avenue and past the drunken milestone, and so home to London with the fog and the cold, and with nothing in my pocket but the recollection of her face.

The next week I went again, and the next, and the next. Every Wednesday at the same hour I presented myself at Candleford Hall, and every Wednesday the serving-man admitted me, and every Wednesday Mrs. Winterbourne received me in the same room, with the same pale face and the same grey eyes, and the packet lying upon the table, unopened, with the dust gathering upon the seals.

At first she would say only, ‘You will call again.’ By and by she said, ‘You will call again, Mr. Pinchbeck,’ and then she asked me whether the coach was cold, and whether the roads were bad, and whether I had dined. In the spring she had a fire lighted for me in the hall, and in the summer she gave me a glass of wine; and once, when I came in wet through, she sent for a servant to dry my coat at the kitchen fire, and bade me sit nearer the blaze, and said that I was a foolish young man to travel in such weather, and that the packet could wait. And all this time the packet lay upon the table, and never a seal was broken.
