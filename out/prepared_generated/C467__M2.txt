The rain lashed against the leaded windows of the nursery, a rhythmic drumming that usually lulled the children into their deep slumber. I sat by the dying fire, a book of verses open on my lap but unread, my mind drifting to the strange, heavy silence that had settled over Hawthorne Hall in the last fortnight. It was not the peaceful quiet of a resting house, but the taut, vibrating silence of a held breath.

I had come to the East Riding seeking refuge from the suffocating expectations of London society, hoping that the vast, grey moors of Yorkshire would offer a clarity I had never possessed. Instead, I found a labyrinth of secrets.

A soft knock at the door interrupted my thoughts. I had not rung the bell for anyone, and the children were fast asleep. I rose, extinguishing the lamp to save the wick, and crossed the darkened room. The handle turned with a whisper, and the door creaked open to reveal a figure shrouded in a black shawl, her face pale and haggard in the gloom.

It was Lady Elspeth, the sister of my employer, Mr. Arthur Vane. She had been in the house for only a week, a guest who spoke little and observed much. I expected a reprimand for leaving the nursery door unlatched, but she stepped inside, pulling the door shut behind her with a gentle finality.

"Miss Halloway," she whispered, her voice trembling. "You must not be afraid. I come to you because I trust no one else in this house. Not the servants, who have eyes for everything and ears for nothing, and certainly not my brother."

I tried to maintain the composure that my training had instilled in me, though my heart hammered against my ribs. "My lady, it is past midnight. Is something wrong?"

Elspeth moved to the chair opposite mine, her movements stiff and deliberate. "The harmony here is a lie, Miss Halloway. You have seen it, have you not? The way Arthur smiles at the dinner table, while his jaw tightens until the muscles jump. The way Lady Vane watches him with a gaze that could curdle milk. They perform the role of a happy couple for the village, for the clergy, for me. But the mask is cracking, and I am terrified of what will happen when it falls."

I thought of the long, cold dinners where the conversation circled around nothing, the sharp, clipped replies that cut through the air like blades. I had told myself it was merely a difference in temperament, a friction of wills that all marriages endured. But hearing it spoken aloud by a member of the family gave it a weight that was difficult to bear.

"I do not understand why you tell me this," I said softly.

"Because you are the only one who sees the truth without being consumed by it," Elspeth said. "You are an outsider. You care for the children, but you do not care for the bloodline. I need you to carry a message. A letter, specifically."

She reached into the folds of her shawl and produced a small, folded piece of parchment. It was sealed with wax, dark red like dried blood.

"Take this to the post box at the crossroads before dawn," she instructed. "There is a mail coach leaving at six. It must reach London by Thursday. It is a letter to a solicitor in the City. It concerns... a separation. A formal, legal separation."

I stared at the letter, my stomach churning. To interfere in the private affairs of one’s employer was a breach of trust that could end my career, if not my life. If Mr. Vane discovered I had been a party to this, I would be dismissed without reference. But to refuse was to stand by while a woman I had come to admire—despite her sharpness—was trapped in a cage of her own making.

"And if I am seen?" I asked.

"You will not be," Elspeth said firmly. "I have bribed the night porter. He will be asleep in his chair by the kitchen fire. The path through the kitchen garden is overgrown, hidden by the hedgerow. You will go through the scullery door, down the side path, and out to the road. No one will see you."

I looked at the letter in her hand, then at her face. I saw the desperation there, the fear of a collapse that would leave her exposed, vulnerable, and alone. I thought of young Thomas and Emily, who played in the garden below, their laughter bright and innocent, unaware of the storm gathering in the house above them.

"If I do this, and it is discovered, I am ruined," I said.

"You are already living a lie, Miss Halloway," Elspeth replied, her voice hardening. "You are pretending to be content in a house where the air is thick with resentment. Do you want to be a complicit bystander, or do you want to be a witness to justice? I am not asking you to hate Arthur. I am asking you to help me leave before I am forced to do something I will regret."

The fire in the grate crackled, sending sparks into the darkness. I reached out and took the letter. It was light, but it felt heavier than any stone I had ever carried.

"Be careful," I said, my voice barely a breath. "And Elspeth, if you leave, take the children with you. They do not need to be raised in this tension."

Elspeth’s eyes widened, a flicker of surprise crossing her face before she nodded, a single, sharp movement. "I will think of that. But tonight, just the letter."

She stood up, adjusting her shawl. "Thank you, Miss Halloway. Do not speak of this to anyone. Not even to me, if you can help it. Once it is done, we are strangers again."
