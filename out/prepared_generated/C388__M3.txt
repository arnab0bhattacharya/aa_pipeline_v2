By ten o'clock in the morning, Clara Forsythe had already smoothed three separate quarrels. The string quartet from the Royal Lindley Academy had arrived in a state of cold-eyed fury, the first violinist refusing to share a stand with the second, whom he accused of dragging the tempo during the Wednesday rehearsal. Clara stood in the green room of the Harland Institute, a converted lecture hall on Kensington Gore, and listened to them hiss at one another while the wind outside threw sleety rain against the tall windows. She said very little. She poured tea. She moved the second violinist to the opposite side of the room and promised the first that the acoustics would be kinder tonight, that the hall would be warm, that the audience would be generous. She said these things with such calm certainty that even she half believed them, and the quartet unpacked their instruments with sulking, resigned faces.

The Harland Institute was not a concert hall. It was a civic building, bequeathed to the borough in 1883 by a soap manufacturer, and it had the proportions of a chapel and the ventilation of a tomb. Clara had spent four months organising the evening's programme — a benefit for the Kensington Distress Fund, which supported families still adrift in the long, grey aftermath of the war — and every decision had been a small battle. The stage was too shallow for a full orchestra, so she had programmed chamber works. The heating boiler had failed twice in November, and the borough council had dragged its feet over repairs. She had written eleven letters. She had visited the town clerk's office in person and sat in his waiting room until he saw her simply to be rid of her. Now the boiler clanked and sighed through the walls like some living creature, and the hall was merely cold rather than punishingly so, which she counted as a victory.

She spent the afternoon arranging chairs. The Institute's own chairs were a punishment — iron-framed, slatted, designed for civic discipline — so she had hired a hundred and twenty folding chairs from a firm in Hammersmith and had spent a week persuading the caretaker, Mr Pellew, to help her carry them from the delivery lorry through the side entrance. By two o'clock she had them set in curved rows across the hall's wooden floor, angled toward the low platform that would serve as a stage. She walked the aisles twice, checking sightlines, adjusting chairs that sat slightly askew. Mr Pellew watched from the doorway, eating an apple, and offered no help. He had told her, more than once, that the hall was not meant for concerts and that the council would hear about the wear on the floor. Clara smiled at him and said she was sure the council would understand, given the cause.

By four the pianist had arrived — a Miss Edith Lavery, a woman of perhaps thirty with severe dark hair and a manner that suggested she found most human interaction a disappointing formality. She sat at the hired Bechstein and played a few bars of the Debussy, then stopped and looked at Clara with undisguised alarm. The piano's middle register was slightly deadened, the bass uncertain. Clara sat in the third row and listened to her play the passage again, and then a third time, and then she went up to the platform and said, very quietly, that the hall's acoustics were peculiar and that the sound would open up once the room was full of people. She had no idea if this was true. Miss Lavery looked at her with the expression of someone who knows she is being lied to but has decided to permit it, and played through her programme once without further comment.

Clara left the hall at half past four and walked home through the cold, along Kensington Church Street, past the shut-up shops and the houses whose front windows showed the pale, cautious glow of late afternoon lamps. She turned into Edwardes Square and let herself into the narrow front door of number 14. The house smelled faintly of cold ash and furniture polish. In the kitchen she found the remains of Robert's breakfast — a half-eaten piece of toast, a cup of tea gone cold — and she stood for a moment looking at the congealed tea and felt something tighten in her chest, a familiar, sickly apprehension that had been growing for weeks.

Robert had not told her. That was the thing she kept returning to, the fact that sat beneath all the other facts like a stone in a shoe. She had learned it not from him but from a remark dropped by Gerald Ashworth at a dinner party a fortnight before — a casual, wine-loosened comment about the poor performance of the Argentine railway shares, and how several men Robert knew had taken losses. Gerald had glanced at Clara when he said it, a quick, searching look, and she had understood at once that the information was meant for her, that Robert had been keeping it from her, and that Gerald knew he had been keeping it. She had said nothing at the time. She had smiled and changed the subject to the concert. But since that evening she had been carrying the knowledge like a bruise, pressing on it, testing its shape and depth.

She did not know how much he had lost. She did not know whether it was money they could absorb or money that would change the shape of their life. She knew only that he had not told her, and that the not-telling was itself a kind of damage, a hairline fracture running through the ordinary architecture of their marriage. She had thought, at first, that she would confront him. She had rehearsed the conversation in her mind, standing at the kitchen sink or lying awake beside his sleeping body.
