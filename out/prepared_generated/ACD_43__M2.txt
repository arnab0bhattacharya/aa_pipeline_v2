The house on the riverbank in Lambeth stood like a decaying tooth in the jaw of the city, its windows blackened by decades of soot and neglect. When the police arrived, they found the front door ajar and the air inside thick with the scent of damp plaster and old dust. There was no furniture, no sign of habitation, yet in the center of the bare floorboard lay the body of a man. He was dressed in a suit of grey tweed, respectable but worn, and his face, though pale, held a strange, frozen calm. Beside his right hand, resting on the cold floor, lay a folded American newspaper.

Inspector Gilmour of Scotland Yard, a man whose eyebrows were perpetually knit in a state of professional bewilderment, leaned over the corpse. He pulled out his notebook, scribbled a few lines, and looked up at me with an expression of profound irritation.

"Well, Watson," he said, tapping the floorboard with his stick, "this is a nuisance. A man found dead in an empty house. No footprints in the mud outside, no forced entry. The door was simply left ajar. And this—" he gestured vaguely at the paper, "—this American rag. It’s three weeks old. The date is the 14th of October. What on earth does a dead man in a vacant Lambeth house want with a stale penny-paper?"

I glanced down. The newspaper was a broadsheet, the *New York Chronicle*, its yellowed pages brittle with age. Next to the date, in the corner of the front page, a circle had been drawn in thick black ink. The date itself, *Oct. 14*, was circled twice.

"It is a clue, Gilmour," I said softly. "Or a mistake."

"A mistake?" Gilmour snorted. "I have seen many mistakes, but I have never seen a dead man committing one. It suggests premeditation, yet the scene is devoid of any struggle. No weapon, no blood, no motive that jumps out at us. It is a dead end, Watson. A complete and utter dead end."

Just then, the door opened again, and a figure stepped in with the brisk, economical stride of a man who had measured the distance and found it insufficient. Sherlock Holmes entered, his eyes bright with the particular intensity that I had learned to associate with the arrival of a puzzle. He wore a long brown coat, and a hat that had clearly seen better days, but his gaze was fixed not on the corpse, but on the newspaper.

"Ah, Gilmour," Holmes said, his voice light and conversational. "You are looking for a motive where there is only a memory. You see the circle, but you do not see the history."

Gilmour straightened up, adjusting his cap. "Mr. Holmes. I had not expected you. I have taken the liberty of securing the scene, though there is little to secure. A dead man, a newspaper. It is simple, and therefore unexplainable."

"Simple is often the most deceptive of things," Holmes replied, crouching down. He did not touch the body, but he picked up the newspaper with a pair of tweezers he produced from his pocket, as if it were made of explosive glass. "The *New York Chronicle*. October 14th. Let us see what the world was doing on that day."

He unfolded the paper. The headlines were stark. *GREAT BANK ROBBERY IN WALL STREET*. *SIX MEN GONE.* *MILLIONS VANISHED.*

Holmes’s eyes narrowed. He read the article aloud, his voice dropping to a murmur as he scanned the details. "The First National Bank. Six million dollars in gold bullion. Taken in broad daylight. The guards were killed. The robbers vanished into the night, never to be seen again. It was a feat of audacity that even the most cynical American could not fathom. And here, in the corner, the date is circled. Why, Gilmour?"

Gilmour shrugged. "Perhaps he was reading about it when he died. Perhaps it was a coincidence."

"A coincidence?" Holmes stood up, brushing the dust from his knees. "You speak of coincidence as if it were a frequent visitor to the corridors of crime. It is a rare guest. This man was not reading for pleasure. He was marking a milestone. Or a confession. Tell me, Gilmour, what is the name of the man?"

"Thomas Armitage," Gilmour said. "A clerk, I believe. Or a former clerk. He lived in the district, but this house has been empty for years. No one knows how he got in."

"An empty house," Holmes repeated, his eyes drifting to the window. "A house that has seen no one for years. And a man who comes to die in it, leaving a newspaper that marks the anniversary of a crime committed twenty years ago on the other side of the Atlantic."

Holmes walked to the window and looked out at the grey London sky. "Twenty years. A long time to hide. But a short time to regret. I suspect, Gilmour, that this man was not a victim of a random attack. He was a participant. And he came here to make a statement, or perhaps to meet someone who never came."

"Who would he meet?" I asked.

"Someone who shared the burden," Holmes said, turning to face us. "Or someone who was going to collect it. But let us not speculate on the unseen. Let us look at the seen. The circle. It is not just a date. Look at the ink. It is not a pen. It is a stick of blue-black, the kind used by accountants. Precise. Deliberate. And look at the fold. The paper is folded into a small square. It was placed here after he died. Or was it?"

Holmes knelt again, this time closer to the hand. He examined the fingers. They were stiff, but the grip was not that of a man holding a paper in life. The paper had been placed beside the hand, on top of it, likely after the body had cooled.
