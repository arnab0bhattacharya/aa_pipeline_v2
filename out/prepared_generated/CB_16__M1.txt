It was a bitter December morning in Millbridge, and the north wind, which had a way of searching out every crack and crevice in the old town, found its way even into the attic workroom of Mrs. Ashworth. I felt it in my fingers, stiff and awkward as they were around the fine muslin of a wedding gown. The town below was a grey blur, its chimneys sending up plumes of smoke that were torn and scattered by the gale. It was a landscape of hard lines and unforgiving stone, much like my own life.

For ten years I had known the bare boards and whitewashed walls of the Foundling Asylum, and for the last two, the slightly better, but no less confined, space of this attic. Mrs. Ashworth, my mistress, was a good woman—good in the way that a worn, smooth stone is good. She was patient and kind, but she was poor. Poverty was the lodestar of our existence; it dictated the number of candles we burned, the thinness of the broth we supped, and the ache in my back from dawn till dusk. I was her apprentice, and in time, I might hope to be her assistant, and perhaps, if God were very kind and my own health held, a mistress of a small workroom myself. This was the entire horizon of my ambition.

It was a narrow life, I will not deny it. The world of books, of ideas, of any conversation that did not concern stitches and seams, was as distant as the stars. Yet it was a life I understood. My place in it was clear, earned by my own labour. There was a certain dignity in that, a quiet anchor in a world that had given me so little else to hold.

One afternoon, as a weak sun struggled to pierce the cloud, a carriage drew up below. It was a fine carriage, its dark lacquer gleaming wetly, and it seemed an alien thing in our narrow, cobbled lane. A footman in livery descended, and after him, a lady. Her name, we soon learned, was Mrs. Vane of Thornleigh Hall.

She swept into our small parlour, a vision of silks and furs that seemed to suck the very air from the room. She was a handsome woman, with eyes the colour of a winter sky and a smile that did not reach them. She spoke to Mrs. Ashworth of her need for a seamstress for a series of balls, and then her gaze fell upon me. I had been mending a lace cuff, and I rose, my hands clasped before me, feeling the rough wool of my own dress like a brand of my station.

“And this is the girl of whom I have heard,” Mrs. Vane said, her voice smooth as polished mahogany. “The one from the Asylum. They say you have a remarkable talent with a needle.”

I felt a hot flush of shame and pride. “I do my best, madam.”

She came closer, and I smelled a scent of roses and something sharper, like wintergreen. She took my hand, turning it over in her own gloved one. Her touch was cool and impersonal. “A good hand. Strong. Not the hand of a lady, of course, but skilled. Mrs. Ashworth, I wish to engage this girl as my personal attendant. She would live at the Hall. She would have better clothes, better food, and a wage you could never hope to give her.”

The words struck the small room like a thunderclap. Mrs. Ashworth looked as if she had been slapped. “Madam, she is my apprentice. Her term is not served.”

“Terms can be bought,” Mrs. Vane said, with an airy wave of her hand. “I will compensate you handsomely. It is an opportunity for the girl. A great opportunity.”

She looked at me again, and I felt myself being weighed and measured, like a bolt of cloth. “What is your name, child?”

“Edith, madam.”

“Well, Edith,” she said, her smile widening, “what do you say to leaving this attic and all its dust for a life of comfort and elegance? You would see a world you can only dream of.”

That night, sleep would not come. The wind howled around the eaves like a hungry beast. Below me, in her small room, I could hear Mrs. Ashworth’s quiet, persistent cough. She was ailing; the damp and the cold were gnawing at her bones, and her poverty afforded no remedy. I could not leave her. It would be an act of ingratitude, of cruelty. To abandon the woman who had taken me in and taught me a trade for a stranger’s patronage felt like a sin. My conscience, a stern and unyielding companion, told me my duty was here, in this narrow, honest life.

Yet, when I closed my eyes, I saw Mrs. Vane’s carriage, the gleam of her silks. I saw a great house with warm fires and rooms full of books and music. Was it so wrong to want more than this attic? Was it a sin to wish for warmth, for beauty, for a life that was not a constant struggle against cold and want? The feeling was a fierce, rising thing, a rebellion against the fate that had been dealt to me. It felt like a fire kindling in my chest, and I was both terrified and thrilled by its heat.

Mrs. Vane sent for me the next day. I was shown into a parlour at the town’s best inn, where she sat before a roaring fire. The warmth was intoxicating after the chill of my attic.

“Well, Edith?” she began, without preamble. “Have you made your decision?”

“I… I cannot leave Mrs. Ashworth, madam. She is unwell, and she depends upon me.”

Mrs. Vane gave a short, sharp laugh. “Sentiment! It is the luxury of the rich, my dear, and the ruin of the poor. Mrs. Ashworth is a businesswoman. She will take the money I offer and find another girl. It is the way of the world.”
