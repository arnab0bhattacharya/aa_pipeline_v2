In the Kingdom of Amaranthia, where the fountains ran with rose-water and the very pebbles in the garden paths were polished like pearls, there reigned a King whose countenance was as fixed as the marble of his own statues. His name was Lord Valerius, and he had never been known to smile. His face was a mask of beautiful, melancholy ivory, and his eyes, dark as the pools in the Temple of Silence, held a perpetual sorrow that baffled all his courtiers. So great was the gloom that settled upon his spirit that it seemed to infect the very roses, causing them to bloom in shades of mauve and lilac instead of the cheerful crimson that is the proper colour of joy.

Because of this affliction, for a smile is surely an affliction when it is absent, the King issued a proclamation. It was written upon vellum scented with sandalwood and sealed with a drop of molten gold. It declared that to whoever could make him smile, he would grant one half of his kingdom, with all its gardens, its fountains, and its treasuries of lapis lazuli and malachite.

The court became a theatre of desperation. Jesters arrived in motley of every clashing hue, juggling with eggs of ostriches and blowing bubbles from pipes of blown glass, yet the King watched them with the weary eyes of a man observing insects crawling upon a windowpane. Poets recited verses of such heartbreaking beauty that the courtiers wept into their handkerchiefs of Valenciennes lace, but the King remained unmoved, his lips a straight line of exquisite despair. Philosophers arrived to discourse on the nature of mirth, arguing that happiness was merely a lack of pain, or perhaps a surplus of digestion. They spoke of Socrates and the absurdity of the human condition, using words so long and polysyllabic that the listeners grew dizzy, but the King’s brow did not furrow, nor did the corners of his mouth twitch. He sat upon his throne of peacock feathers and gold, a statue of grief amidst a whirlwind of mirth.

In the lower regions of the palace, where the air was thick with the steam of boiling sugar and the scent of baking bread, there worked a kitchen maid named Perpetua. She was a small creature, with flour dusting her hair like a premature snow, and she had never heard of the King’s proclamation. Her world was one of simple duties: to turn the spit, to scour the copper pots until they shone like the sun, and to carry the trays of delicacies up the winding stairs to the banqueting hall.

One afternoon, as the sun was setting and casting long, amber shadows through the stained glass of the corridors, Perpetua was instructed to carry a tray of the King’s favourite tarts. These were tarts of the finest pastry, filled with crushed raspberries and cream that had been whipped with vanilla and a drop of the rarest liqueur. As she ascended the grand staircase, her mind was occupied not with the King’s melancholy, but with the difficulty of keeping her balance on the polished steps. She was thinking of a song the scullery boys sang, a tune of no importance and less melody.

As she entered the Great Hall, the silence of the court was heavy and suffocating. The King was staring at a tapestry depicting the Judgment of Paris, looking as though he would prefer to be judged himself rather than endure another moment of existence. Perpetua, blinded by the glare of the chandeliers and distracted by the rhythm of her own thoughts, caught the heel of her shoe in the hem of her apron. Gravity, that most democratic of forces, asserted its authority. The tray tipped; the porcelain shattered; and the tarts, those monuments of culinary art, flew through the air in a magnificent arc of cream and crimson.

They landed with a wet, unceremonious slap directly upon the King’s velvet robes and the marble floor at his feet. A splash of raspberry cream decorated the royal cheek like a rouge of the wildest fashion. A silence fell upon the hall, so profound that one could hear the beating of the moth’s wings against the candles. The courtiers gasped, expecting the executioner’s axe to fall upon the poor girl. Perpetua stood frozen, her hands empty, her eyes wide, looking at the destruction she had wrought upon majesty.

But the King looked down at his stained robe, then at the cream upon his face, and then at the terrified, flour-dusted girl standing amidst the wreckage of the tarts. He saw the absurdity of it—the great Lord of Amaranthia, the master of gardens and fountains, defeated by a raspberry tart. A strange sensation tickled his throat. A sound escaped him, dry and rusty at first, like a gate long unopened. Then it grew. His shoulders began to shake. His eyes, those pools of sorrow, began to water with a different kind of liquid. He laughed. It was a loud, ringing sound, startling in its joy, and it echoed off the vaulted ceilings and the gilded pillars.

The courtiers looked on in horror and confusion. The King was laughing at the mess, at the absurdity of his own dignity, at the sheer, delightful chaos of the moment.

‘You,’ said the King, wiping a tear of mirth from his eye and pointing a finger at Perpetua. ‘You have done what no philosopher could achieve. You have shown me that dignity is a burden and that a tart in the face is a liberation.’

He stood up, shaking the crumbs from his royal person. ‘According to my proclamation, half of the kingdom is yours.’

Perpetua, who had never desired a kingdom and found the idea of ruling half of one rather exhausting, looked at the King with genuine concern. ‘But, Your Majesty,’ she said, her voice trembling only slightly, ‘the tarts are ruined. I shall have to make more.’
