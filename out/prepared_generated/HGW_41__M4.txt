The thing came up out of the water a little after two in the morning, and I saw it as plainly as I see this inkpot now. I had been keeper at Skerryvore Light for eleven years, and in that time I had seen the sea do a great many queer things, but never anything so queer as that. The night was clear and cold, with a moon in its third quarter, and the water was like oil, so that every ripple showed black against the silver. I was at the gallery window with my pipe, having just trimmed the lamp, when the disturbance began about a hundred yards out from the western rocks. It was not a splash, you understand, but a kind of slow heaving, as though something of considerable bulk were rolling itself up from the bottom. Then a long, glistening back broke the surface, and I saw the moonlight on it, and it was not the wet sleekness of a seal or a porpoise, but something rougher, more like the hide of a rhinoceros, if you can imagine a rhinoceros that had taken to the sea.

I stood there with my pipe halfway to my mouth and watched it for the better part of a minute. It moved with a kind of lumbering purpose, not swimming but wading, if you follow me, and it made a low, grunting noise that carried across the water. Then it sank again, and there was nothing but the moon and the black rocks and the long swell breaking white against the point. I went down and woke my assistant, a lad named Callum Ross, but by the time we got back to the gallery there was nothing to see, and he looked at me as though I had been at the whisky. I was not, and I told him so, and we had words about it. But I wrote it all down in the log, and the next time the supply boat came out from Oban I sent a letter to the Commissioners, and thought no more about it.

The naturalist arrived six weeks later. His name was Mr. Percival Gannet, and he came out on the *Cormorant* with a case of instruments, a camera the size of a small trunk, and a rifle of the kind they use for big game in Africa. He was a thin, eager man of about forty, with spectacles and a way of talking very fast and very precisely, as though he were reading from a book. He had read my letter, he said, and he had come to see the creature for himself. I told him he was welcome to the lighthouse and to my table, but that I doubted he would see anything, for I had not seen it again, and I had been watching every night.

“My dear Mr. MacLeod,” he said, “you have seen something, and that is the main thing. The Outer Hebrides are a great blank in our knowledge of the marine fauna. A creature of that size, if it is what I suspect, would be a discovery of the first importance.”

“And what do you suspect?” I asked.

He tapped his case of instruments. “A species of giant cephalopod,” he said, “or possibly a surviving plesiosaur. The descriptions are not clear. But we shall see.”

We did not see for eleven nights. Mr. Gannet spent his days arranging his apparatus and his nights at the gallery window, and he was very patient and very exact, and I began to like him, though he had a way of talking about the creature as though it were already dead and in a jar. On the twelfth night it came.

It rose again from the same place, and this time it came closer in, and I saw it more clearly. It was perhaps twenty feet long, with a body like a barrel and a long neck that it held out of the water, and it had flippers, or something like flippers, that it used to drag itself along the rocks. Mr. Gannet was at the window with his camera before I could speak, and he had the thing focused and the flash prepared in a matter of seconds. But when the creature turned its head toward the light, and I saw its eyes, I put my hand on his arm.

“Wait,” I said.

“Wait?” he said. “My dear sir, this is the opportunity of a lifetime.”

“It is looking at us,” I said. “It is not afraid. It is looking at us as though it were trying to understand.”

Mr. Gannet laughed. “It is looking at the light,” he said. “It is a phototropic response, nothing more.” And he took his photograph, and the flash went off with a great white blaze, and the creature gave a cry that I can only describe as a kind of hoarse, unhappy bellow, and slid back into the water and was gone.

Mr. Gannet was delighted. He developed his plate that very night, and by morning he had a picture that showed the creature plainly, with its long neck and its barrel body and its great dark eye. He was for sending it to the Royal Society at once, and he talked of nothing but the paper he would write and the name he would give it. But I found that I could not share his enthusiasm. I kept thinking of that cry, and of the way the creature had looked at the light, and I began to feel a kind of responsibility for it, as though I had betrayed a confidence.

The next night it did not come. Nor the next. Mr. Gannet sat at the window with his rifle across his knees and his camera ready, and he grew more and more impatient. “It will return,” he said. “They always return. The curiosity of the lower animals is stronger than their fear.”
