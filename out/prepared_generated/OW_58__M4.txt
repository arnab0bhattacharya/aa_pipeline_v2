The afternoon was one of those perfect compromises which the English climate occasionally offers to those who have ceased to expect anything of it, and Mr. Julian Vane, standing at the window of his club in Pall Mall, was inclined to regard the sunshine as a personal compliment. Three years in the interior of Australia had not diminished his appreciation of London, though it had given him a complexion which his friends were pleased to call interesting and his enemies weather-beaten. He had returned that very morning, without warning, as a man returns when he wishes to surprise others and rather hopes to be surprised himself; and having eaten a chop with the gravity of one who has long eaten worse, he was now surveying the passing world with the pleasant sense of a spectator who has not yet decided whether to re-enter the play.

It was thus that he saw, on the opposite pavement, the Honourable Mrs. Arthur Merivale descending from a hansom with the air of a woman to whom hansoms were a necessity and not an adventure. She was dressed in a gown of dove-coloured silk, and she carried a parasol of the same shade, and she paused for a moment upon the kerb as though the world were a stage and she were waiting for her cue. Mr. Vane, who had once known her as Miss Cecilia Thorne, and had loved her with the ardour of a young man who has not yet learned that ardour is a poor substitute for income, felt his heart perform that curious little antic which is the body's way of reminding us that it has a memory of its own.

He went out to her at once. She received him with a composure that was almost an achievement.

"Julian!" she cried. "How brown you are! And how delightfully unexpected! I thought you were in the bush, or the desert, or wherever it is that young men go to make their fortunes and lose their manners."

"I have made a little money," said Mr. Vane, "and I hope I have not lost the manners. But I see that you have made a marriage. I congratulate you. Merivale is a lucky fellow."

"Arthur is in the country," said Mrs. Merivale, with a smile that was intended to be enigmatic and succeeded in being merely sweet. "He is shooting. He shoots a great deal. It is his way of being serious."

"And you? Do you find marriage serious?"

"I find it excessively pleasant," she said. "One has one's own house, and one's own servants, and one's own opinions. It is like having a kingdom, only the taxes are lower."

They walked together in the direction of the park, and Mr. Vane, who had rehearsed this meeting a thousand times upon the deck of a steamer and in the dusty streets of Ballarat, found that the rehearsal was of no use whatever. She talked of the opera, of a new painter who painted nothing but lilies, of a new poet who wrote nothing but sonnets, and of a new dish which her cook had invented and which she described with a gravity that made him laugh. And all the while he was looking at her hands, and remembering that they had once been given to him, and wondering whether the rings upon them were a sufficient answer to the question he had come three thousand miles to ask.

At last, when they had reached the bridge, and the water was doing that pretty trick of trembling beneath the sunlight which the Thames performs so well and so often, he stopped and said:

"Cecilia, I have something to ask you. I have come back to ask it. When I went away, you promised to wait for me."

She did not start. She did not even colour. She looked at the water with the air of a woman who is considering whether it is worth while to be dramatic.

"I thought you were dead," she said.

"Dead!"

"There was a report. It came from a man who had been in the same country as you. He said you had been drowned in a river, or shot by a native, or something equally disagreeable. I forget which. I cried for a week. Then I married Arthur."

"You cried for a week," repeated Mr. Vane, slowly. "And then you married my best friend."

"Arthur was very kind," she said. "He was always kind. He proposed to me on a Thursday, and on Friday I accepted him, and on Saturday I bought a hat. It was a beautiful hat. I wore it at the wedding. I have it still."

"And you never thought of writing to me? You never thought of making certain?"

"One does not make certain of a dead man," said Mrs. Merivale, with a little shrug. "It would be indelicate. Besides, I was told that you had been buried at a place with a name I could not pronounce. It seemed so final. Death is always final, is it not? It is one of its few advantages over life."

Mr. Vane looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed, and the laugh was not entirely a happy one.

"You are admirably frank," he said. "You might have told me that you had grown tired of waiting. That would have been simpler."

"Would it?" she said. "I wonder. Men are so fond of the truth when it is flattering to them. If I had said I was tired of waiting, you would have thought me heartless. If I say I thought you were dead, you think me——"

"I think you are a very clever woman," said Mr. Vane.

"You are not the first to say so," she returned. "But I am not clever. I am merely truthful. It is a great deal easier than people imagine, if only one is truthful about other people's feelings as well as one's own. I was fond of you, Julian. I was very fond of you. But I was fonder of being married. And Arthur was there. And you were not. And so——"
