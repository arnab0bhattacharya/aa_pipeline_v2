In the most northern reaches of the kingdom of Varn, where the pine-trees grow so tall and thick that they seem to hold up the grey sky like the pillars of a vast, melancholic cathedral, there lived a boy named Ilmar. He was a lame boy, and he lived in a low, thatched cottage at the very edge of the great forest. His left foot dragged behind him with a heavy, rhythmic sorrow whenever he moved, and he bore his misfortune with that beautiful and silent dignity which is the peculiar privilege of those whom Nature has marked out from the common herd. For it is only the quite ordinary who complain; those who are truly afflicted know that suffering is simply a backdrop to give their lives distinction, and they wear their tragedy as a king wears his crown, with a certain bored and weary grace.

Ilmar had no parents, and was looked after by an ancient woman who spent her days spinning flax and her evenings telling him tales of the white stag that roamed the deep woods. She told him that the stag was a portent of great fortune, but Ilmar, who had the wisdom of stillness about him, knew that in this world a portent is merely a promise of a disaster that has not yet had the good manners to arrive.

Every night, when the frost lay upon the heather like a veil of crushed diamonds, the white stag came to the edge of the cottage garden. It stood perfectly still amidst the dark, shivering branches of the juniper bushes, its antlers catching the pale moonlight as though they were carved from milk-white alabaster. It never called to him, for speech is the refuge of those who have nothing to say, and silence is always more eloquent. But Ilmar would rise from his rough bed, wrap his coarse grey cloak about his shoulders, and, taking his wooden crutch, limp out into the night. The stag would turn its beautiful, vacant head, and move with a slow, gliding motion into the depths of the forest, and Ilmar would follow.

The forest by night was a place of wonderful and terrible enchantment. The snow lay upon the ground like a shroud of silver tissue, and the icicles that hung from the branches of the fir-trees flashed with the cold, hard brilliance of emeralds and sapphires. The wind whispered through the needles of the pines with a sound like the murmuring of distant water, or the sighing of those who have been disappointed in love. The boy's crutch made a dull, monotonous thud upon the frozen earth, and the stag moved on before him, a ghostly vision of grace that seemed scarcely to touch the ground.

They travelled thus for what seemed to Ilmar a great while, passing through groves of black yew and over frozen streams that gleamed in the starlight like ribbons of polished steel. At last, they came to a clearing in the forest, and Ilmar saw before him a city.

It was a city of white marble and pale stone, with slender spires that rose into the night like lances of pearl. The gates were of wrought silver, and the streets were paved with a substance that shone like the nacre of a shell. There were no lamps, for the city seemed to glow with a light of its own, a cold, ethereal luminescence that was as beautiful and as lifeless as a painted moon.

As Ilmar passed through the silver gates, the white stag vanished into the shadows of a thicket of yew, leaving the boy to hobble forward alone. The streets were filled with people, and Ilmar, looking upon them, was struck with a profound and terrible wonder. For in this city, there was no one who was lame. There was no one who was old. No withered face, no bent back, no halting step was to be seen. All the men and women who walked the shining streets were possessed of an extraordinary and flawless physical beauty. Their forms were as perfect as the forms of the gods of old Greece, their faces as symmetrical and as cold as the faces of statues. They moved with an effortless, gliding grace that was pleasing to the eye, but strangely unsettling to the soul.

Yet, as Ilmar looked closer, he perceived that upon every face there rested an expression of the most profound and unutterable melancholy. Their eyes, which were large and clear and wonderfully bright, were filled with a dull, vacant despair. They did not speak to one another. They did not smile. They simply walked, back and forth, up and down the shining streets, like prisoners in a beautiful gaol.

Ilmar approached a man who stood by a fountain of clear crystal, watching the water fall with a listless, indifferent gaze. The man was tall and wonderfully handsome, with hair like spun gold and eyes the colour of a winter sky. He wore a tunic of white silk, and upon his breast was a brooch of pale jade.

"I am a stranger here," said Ilmar, in his quiet, gentle voice. "Can you tell me the name of this city?"

The man turned his beautiful, sorrowful eyes upon the boy, and when he saw the crutch and the dragging foot, a look of pity, mixed with a curious envy, passed across his face. "This is the City of Sylvain," he said, in a voice that was as cold and as smooth as the marble of the streets. "And you are a stranger indeed, for you bring your sorrow with you."

"My sorrow is but a lame foot," said Ilmar, with a faint smile. "It is a small thing, and I have learned to bear it."

"In Sylvain, we have no sorrows," said the man. "We have banished age, and disease, and deformity. We are all perfectly beautiful, and we live forever."

"That is a great blessing," said Ilmar, though he felt a strange chill at his heart.
