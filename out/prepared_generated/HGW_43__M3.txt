It was the fourth week of the Great Fog, and London was already sinking into a condition of mediaeval barbarism. The atmospheric disturbance had descended upon the capital with a suddenness that baffled the meteorologists, a yellowish, suffocating vapour that clung to the river, blotted out the sun, and turned the gas-lamps at midday into mere, feeble, reddish smudges. Trade had stopped entirely. The steamers lay moored in the Pool, their foghorns bleating in a dismal, endless chorus. The railway companies had suspended their timetables, and the streets, choked with a greasy, sooty grime, were given over to the footpad and the pickpocket. I had ventured out of my lodgings that afternoon only to seek a tin of condensed milk and a half-pound of bacon, having been reduced to the very edge of starvation by the closure of my usual eating-house.

I must explain that I am a junior clerk in the London and Suburban Bank, a person of the most timid and regular habits, entirely unaccustomed to adventures of the body or the mind. I had been groping my way along the gutters of a narrow street in Eastcheap, keeping one hand upon the damp brickwork, when I turned down a flight of steps, thinking to make a shortcut towards the river. I pushed open a door at the bottom of the steps, which yielded to my touch, and found myself precipitated headlong into a brightly lit cellar.

The sudden transition from the opaque and stifling gloom to the glare of a powerful oil-lamp blinded me for a moment. I was aware of a close, tobacco-laden atmosphere, and of a low murmur of voices. When my vision cleared, I perceived that I was standing in a vaulted underground room, perhaps twenty feet long, in the centre of which stood a rough deal table surrounded by a dozen men. They were a singularly unkempt and earnest-looking group. Two of them wore the greasy caps of mechanics, another had the pallor and stoop of a clerk, and the rest possessed the shabby, aggressive respectability of the suburban socialist.

"Hah!" said the man at the head of the table, a brawny, red-bearded fellow with a terrible squint, whom I afterwards knew as Halkett. "Just in time! Shut the door, brother; we are just coming to the vote."

I was extremely alarmed, but before I could stammer out my explanation and retreat, a little man with spectacles had seized me by the wrist and pulled me into a vacant seat. "Hold your tongue," he whispered, with fierce intensity; "we want no police spies here, and if you are one, you will go out by the way of the coal chute. We are electing a secretary."

I sat down, trembling violently, my condensed milk utterly forgotten. I gathered from the muttered talk around me that this was the East Central Branch of the Federated Workers, and that they were engaged in a debate of the most desperate character. The extraordinary persistence of the fog had convinced them that the entire fabric of commercial society had dissolved. The police, they argued, were demoralised; the government was paralysed; the time for the Revolution had arrived at last.

"The opportunity is unprecedented," Halkett was saying, leaning forward upon his elbows and glaring at the company with his disquieting eyes. "The bourgeoisie are choking in their West End squares. The banks are shut, the telegraph wires are down, and the very bobbies have deserted their beats. I say to you, now is the hour to march upon the Mansion House, to seize the railway termini, and to proclaim the Cooperative Commonwealth. If we wait for the fog to lift, the capitalists will have re-established their grip, and we shall be back to our drudgery."

A murmur of assent went round the table. I was struck by the matter-of-fact tone in which they discussed the overthrow of the British Empire. There was no grandiose rhetoric, no theatrical gesticulation. They talked of insurrection as plainly as a vestry committee might discuss the repair of a gutter. They were ordinary, middle-class and working-class men, a little dirty and very earnest, impelled by a profound conviction that the social system was an illogical and temporary nuisance.

"But what of the military?" objected the little man with spectacles, whose name was Podmore. "The Guards are at the Tower. If we march up Cheapside, they will simply turn a machine-gun upon us, fog or no fog."

"Fiddlesticks!" said Halkett. "In this fog they could not see their own officers. They would be as helpless as beetles in a bucket. We have only to organise a column, arm ourselves with crowbars and a few revolvers, and walk into the Mansion House. The Lord Mayor will surrender his keys as meekly as a Sunday-school superintendent."

I sat listening to this monstrous proposal in an agony of terror. I was a bank clerk, the very embodiment of that capitalist system they proposed to annihilate. I felt that at any moment they might discover my identity, drag me to the front of the cellar, and make a terrible example of the suburban middle classes. I endeavoured to make myself as small as possible upon my chair.

"Then we are agreed," said Halkett. "We strike tonight. But we must have a secretary to take the minutes and draw up the proclamation. Podmore, you write a shocking hand. What about this new brother?"

He pointed his thick finger directly at me. Every eye in the cellar turned upon my pallid and perspiring face.

"I—I am no orator," I managed to say, my voice cracking. "I am merely a clerk. I came here by accident. I was looking for a grocer's."

"A grocer's!" cried Halkett, with a roar of laughter. "Man, this is the grocer's shop! We are going to take all the grocers' shops in London. A clerk is exactly what we need. He knows the routine of commerce, the arithmetic of exploitation. You shall be our secretary. What is your name?"
