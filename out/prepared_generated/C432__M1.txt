The gas lamp in the corner of their Bloomsbury room hissed a soft, steady note, the only sound competing with the muffled rattle of a late omnibus on Gower Street. Outside, the November fog was a thick, wet shroud, muffling the city in a blanket of grey. Inside, the small fire in the grate spat and crackled, casting long, dancing shadows that made the familiar room seem alien. Dmitri sat in the worn armchair, a book of English poetry open on his lap, but the words were meaningless shapes. His gaze was fixed on the windowpane, on the tiny, perfect spheres of condensation that gathered and merged, tracing slow, silent rivers down the glass.

He felt the distance between them like a physical chasm, though they were only a few feet apart. Elara was at the small table, mending a tear in his good coat, her head bent in concentration. The lamplight caught the gold in her hair, turning it to a warm, soft halo. She was humming, a tuneless little melody that was as much a part of this room as the scent of beeswax and tea. It was a sound of peace, of a settled and gentle life he had craved with a desperation that frightened him. And yet, he was already gone.

He had felt the summons arrive that afternoon, not in a letter, but in a pattern. A chalk mark on the pillar by the British Museum, a discarded copy of *The Times* with a specific page corner folded three times. The message was in a book left for him at a second-hand stall on Charing Cross Road, a faded volume of Gogol. Page 143. The words were underlined in faint pencil, but the real message was in the book’s origin: a tiny, almost invisible stamp from a St. Petersburg press that had been shut down by the Okhrana three years ago. It was the signal. *Return. The work is ready.* The work. A sterile, clean word for a messy, bloody act.

“Dmitri?”

He flinched, the name pulling him back across the chasm. Elara had set her sewing aside and was looking at him, her brow furrowed with a gentle concern that was worse than any accusation.

“You’ve been a thousand miles away all evening,” she said, her voice soft. “Is everything alright at the university? You seem so… distant.”

He forced a smile, a brittle, unfamiliar thing on his face. “Just a difficult manuscript, my love. A particularly stubborn translator from the 14th century. It has my mind in knots.”

It was his standard excuse, the careful fiction he had constructed for their life together. Dmitri Volkov, the quiet, brilliant Slavic scholar, a man of letters who had fled the oppressive intellectual climate of the Tsar’s empire for the freedom of London’s libraries. A man who had fallen for a bright, practical English girl who worked in a bookbindery and smelled of paper and glue. It was a good life. A safe life. He had loved it so fiercely he had almost believed it himself.

Elara was not convinced. She rose and came to stand behind his chair, her hands resting on his shoulders. Her touch was a brand. “It’s more than that. It’s as if you’re preparing to leave, even while you’re sitting here.”

Her intuition was a blade, twisting in his gut. She saw too much. She saw the man he was trying to be, and in her loving, perceptive way, she saw the cracks in the facade. He was packing, he realised. Not his clothes, but his soul. He was folding up the Dmitri who loved rainy afternoons in the British Library, who took pleasure in the precise taste of a good pint of bitter, who adored the way Elara’s nose wrinkled when she laughed. He was packing that man away to make room for the other one. The one with no name, only a purpose.

“Elara,” he began, his voice hoarse. He wanted to tell her everything. He wanted to explain the endless, grinding poverty of his childhood in a cramped tenement in Kazan, the rage that had been born there. He wanted to tell her about his brother, Mikhail, shot by Cossacks for daring to organise a strike at the Putilov works. He wanted to describe the grim-faced men and women who had become his family, his cause, his church. He wanted to tell her that the man she loved was a phantom, a carefully constructed mask worn by a revolutionary, a member of a faction of the Socialist Revolutionary Party that believed the only language the autocracy understood was the language of dynamite and the assassin’s bullet.

But he looked at her face, open and trusting in the lamplight, and the words died in his throat. To tell her would be to destroy her. It would drag her beautiful, ordered world into the filth and chaos of his own. It would make her a conspirator, an accessory. It would put her in danger. The silence was the only protection he could offer her.

“It’s just a chill,” he said, covering her hands with his own. “Winter is coming early this year. And… I may have to go away for a short while. For the university. A research trip.”

It was a pathetic lie. He saw the flicker of doubt in her eyes, the quick, sad understanding that he was shutting her out. She squeezed his shoulders and then moved away, returning to her mending. The moment of intimacy was broken. The chasm between them was wider than ever.

The next morning, the fog was still thick. He stood on the platform at Victoria Station, a small valise at his feet. Elara was there, her face pale, a stray curl escaping from under her hat. She had made him a thermos of hot tea and pressed a small, paper-wrapped parcel into his hand. “Some biscuits,” she said. “For the journey.”
