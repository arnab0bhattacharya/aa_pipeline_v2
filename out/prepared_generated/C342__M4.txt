The commission came on a Tuesday, in a letter so heavily sealed and so thick with paper that Elias Thorne half-expected it to contain a writ. Instead it contained an offer: one hundred guineas, a sum that would pay his rent until Michaelmas and silence his landlady’s pointed remarks about the coal bill, to paint Mrs. Celia Vance, wife of the Right Honourable Ambrose Vance, Minister for the Colonies. Thorne, who had spent the better part of a decade painting the ruddy faces of merchants’ wives and the occasional alderman’s spaniel, read the letter twice and then sat down rather heavily on his studio stool. One hundred guineas. He was to present himself at the Vance house in Belgrave Square the following Monday, at ten o’clock precisely.

The house was as he had imagined it and worse: a great cliff of Portland stone with a black door polished like a boot, and a butler who took his hat as though it were a specimen. He was led up a staircase wide enough to drive a carriage through and into a morning room where the light fell in pale, expensive sheets through tall windows. And there, by the fireplace, stood Mrs. Vance.

She was not what he expected. He had painted enough wives of great men to know the type—the careful smile, the hands arranged just so, the eyes that measured a man’s coat and found it wanting. Celia Vance was small and dark, with a face that seemed to have been assembled from contradictions: a heavy jaw and a delicate mouth, brows that would have suited a general, and a restless, intelligent gaze that fixed on him at once and did not let go.

“Mr. Thorne,” she said. “You are not what I expected either. I thought you would be older. Or at least taller.”

He laughed before he could stop himself. “I left my height at home, madam. It tires easily.”

Her mouth twitched. “Good. Sit down. I have been told to be still for an hour, which I shall hate, but I am told also that you are not a fool, which may make it bearable.”

The sittings began. She chose the chair herself—a high-backed thing of faded gold, positioned so that the light from the window caught the side of her face and left the rest in a soft, ambiguous shadow. Thorne set up his easel and began to sketch, and for the first quarter of an hour there was only the scratch of charcoal and the distant sound of a carriage in the square.

Then she said, “You may talk to me, you know. I shan’t dissolve.”

He asked her about the house, and she told him it was her husband’s house, not hers, that she had wanted a smaller place in Kensington but Ambrose had insisted, and that she had learned to keep her opinions to the rooms where no one important could hear them. He asked about her days, and she told him they were a procession of calls and cards and committees for charitable causes she did not believe in, and that she had once suggested to Ambrose that she might take up botany, and he had laughed as though she had proposed to walk to the moon.

“He does not beat me,” she said, on the third sitting, in a tone of mild wonder. “He is not cruel. He is simply certain that I am a smaller thing than he is, and he cannot imagine why I should mind.”

Thorne kept his eyes on the canvas. “And do you mind, Mrs. Vance?”

“I mind,” she said, “that I have not been asked a real question in eleven years. Until you. You ask questions like a man who wants the answer.”

He did want the answer. That was the trouble. By the fifth sitting he had begun to see her—not the statesman’s wife, not the careful arrangement of silk and jet, but the woman underneath, the one who read novels in secret and had opinions about the Reform Bill and laughed at things she was not supposed to find funny. He painted her with her head turned slightly, her lips parted as though about to speak, and in her eyes a flicker of something that was not quite amusement and not quite grief. It was the best work he had ever done. He knew it the way a man knows when a knot has pulled tight.

On the seventh sitting she said, “You will make me too honest. Ambrose will look at it and see a stranger.”

“And what would he see if I painted what he expects?”

“A porcelain figure with a pleasant expression and no thoughts at all.” She was quiet a moment. “He has a picture of me in his study. I sat for it when we were first married. I look like a woman who has been stuffed and mounted. He loves it.”

Thorne set down his brush. “What do you want me to paint?”

She looked at him then, directly, and he felt the full weight of her attention, which was considerable. “I want you to paint what you see. I have spent my life being seen by people who were not looking. I would like, once, to be looked at properly.”

The portrait was finished on a Friday. On Saturday morning Thorne received a note from Ambrose Vance requesting his presence at the house at four o’clock. He went, carrying the canvas wrapped in brown paper, and was shown into the study, where the Minister for the Colonies stood with his back to the fire. Ambrose Vance was a large, handsome man with a ruddy face and the easy confidence of someone who had never in his life been contradicted by anyone he considered his equal. He looked at the painting for a long time.

“It is very well done,” he said at last. “You have a fine hand, Mr. Thorne. But it will not do.”
