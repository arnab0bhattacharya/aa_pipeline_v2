The wind, which had blown fresh and favourable from the shores of England, now veered suddenly to the north-east, and came sweeping down from the vast and trackless regions of polar ice, bearing with it a chill that pierced to the very marrow, and driving before it a succession of those dense and heavy mists which, in those latitudes, seem to annihilate the horizon and to wrap the whole world in a shroud of grey and melancholy vapour. Our little vessel, the *Aurora*, strained and shuddered at every plunge, and the sails, stiffened with frost, rattled like sheets of iron against the masts. I stood upon the deck, wrapped in my cloak, and gazing upon the sublime and desolate scene, when my wife, who had quitted the cabin notwithstanding my entreaties that she would remain below, approached and laid her hand upon my arm.

“Edwin,” said she, “you are gazing at the sea-birds; but I think it is not they which occupy your thoughts. You are thinking of the paper you are to read before the Royal Society, and of the observations you have made upon the volcanoes of Iceland.”

I turned to her with a smile, for she had ever the art of reading my heart with a penetration that was almost painful. “You are right, my Clara,” I replied; “my thoughts were indeed busied with that subject. The world of science expects much from me; and I confess that I am not without anxiety as to the reception which my theories may meet with. I have laboured, as you know, with no common assiduity; I have braved the perils of the deep, the rigours of an arctic climate, and the dangers of descending into the very craters of Hekla, to obtain a faithful record of facts which may serve to illuminate the dark and hidden operations of nature. But I am aware that my conclusions are bold, and that the learned are not always disposed to receive with favour doctrines which contradict their preconceived opinions.”

“And yet,” said Clara, “you have not been alone in your labours. Have you forgotten that I have been your companion in all your wanderings? Have I not stood beside you upon the brink of the burning gulf, and noted with my own eyes the phenomena which you have described? Have I not held the chronometer while you observed the oscillations of the mercury, and written down, at your dictation, the results of your experiments? And was it not I who first remarked the peculiar configuration of the lava which you afterwards found to be so important?”

I was silent, for I knew that she spoke the truth. Clara had indeed been my constant and indefatigable assistant. Her mind, naturally quick and observant, had been cultivated by a liberal education, and she possessed a delicacy of perception which often enabled her to detect minutiæ that had escaped my own more comprehensive but less patient scrutiny. She had been the first to notice the singular arrangement of the basaltic columns in the ravine of Almannagjá, and the remarkable difference in the colour of the sulphur deposits upon the eastern and western slopes of the crater. She had kept a minute journal of the weather, and had recorded the variations of the magnetic needle with a fidelity which I could not but admire. In truth, I had found her presence not only a solace in the hours of solitude and weariness, but a positive advantage to the prosecution of my inquiries.

“You are right, Clara,” I said at length; “you have been my fellow-labourer, and I should be unjust if I did not acknowledge it. But the paper which I am to read is a formal communication to a learned body, and it is the custom to mention only those who have been engaged in the actual observations. I fear that the world would scarcely be prepared to receive with gravity the name of a woman in connexion with a scientific memoir.”

“And why not?” she replied, with a gentle but earnest warmth. “Is truth less truth because it is uttered by a woman’s lips? Is the observation less accurate because it was made by a woman’s eye? You speak of the world, Edwin; but the world is not always just. It is the business of those who love truth to enlarge the boundaries of knowledge, and not to consult the prejudices of the ignorant. If I have deserved well of science, let my name be inscribed with yours; if I have not, let it be omitted. But do not, I beseech you, sacrifice the claims of justice to the fear of ridicule.”

Her words sank deep into my mind, and I revolved them in my heart during the remainder of our voyage. The storm continued to increase, and the night was one of considerable danger. The waves rose like mountains, and the vessel, labouring heavily, threatened every moment to be overwhelmed. Clara, who had never before been at sea in a gale, displayed a courage and composure which astonished me. While I was agitated by fears for her safety, she remained calm and collected, and even assisted the sailors in their efforts to secure the rigging. I remember that, as we were standing together near the helm, she pointed out to me a peculiar appearance in the clouds which she had observed on the preceding evening, and which she believed to be a certain indication of a change of wind. Her prediction was verified; the gale moderated, and by morning we were enabled to proceed on our course.

When at length we reached the coast of Iceland, and I began to arrange my materials for the paper which I was to present to the Society, I found myself again embarrassed by the question which Clara had raised. I could not deny that she had rendered me essential service; and yet I shrunk from the idea of associating her name with my own in a public communication.
