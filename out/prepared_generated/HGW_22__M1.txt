When Arthur Pemberton opened his eyes, the first thing he noticed was the air. It had a curious, sterilised quality, like the atmosphere of a very large and very clean greenhouse. He was lying on a low, cushioned bench of some resilient, silvery material, in a room whose walls were a single, unbroken curve of pale grey. There were no pictures, no ornaments, no furniture in the conventional sense, only the bench and a few other moulded shapes that might have been chairs or tables. The light, which emanated from the ceiling itself, was shadowless and even.

He sat up. His tweed suit felt absurdly heavy and hot, his stiff collar a garrotte. He had been walking on the South Downs, he remembered, a sudden, violent thunderstorm had broken, a flash of lightning of impossible, sheeting brilliance, and then… this. He was a man of methodical habits, an engineer by trade, and his first impulse was not to panic but to assess. The room was sealed; there was no door he could see. The air tasted faintly of ozone and damp earth.

“Hello?” he called, and his voice sounded thin and flat in the immense quiet.

A section of the wall opposite him dissolved, not opening, but simply fading into transparency, and a woman stood there. She was tall and slender, with a face of such serene and placid beauty that it seemed less a human countenance than a carefully composed work of art. Her hair was a cloud of pure white, but her skin was smooth and unwrinkled, and her eyes, a startlingly clear blue, held an expression of calm, detached curiosity. She wore a simple, flowing garment of a soft, dove-grey fabric.

“Good morning,” she said. Her voice was melodious and clear. “You have been in temporal stasis. The adjustment period can be disorienting. Please, be calm.”

“Temporal stasis?” Pemberton swung his legs to the floor. “Madam, I can assure you I have been caught in a thunderstorm. I demand to know where I am and what is the meaning of this… this theatricality.”

The woman smiled, a gentle, pitying expression. “You are in what was once called England. The storm you experienced was likely a chronometric discharge, a rare but not unheard-of phenomenon. You have been displaced. By your dress and speech, I would estimate several centuries.”

Pemberton stared at her. The calm, the certainty in her tone was more unnerving than any threat. “Several centuries? That is preposterous.”

“Is it?” she asked, with a scientist’s mild interest. “Come. Let me show you.”

She led him through the dissolving wall into a vast, open space. It was a city, but unlike any he could have conceived. Great, graceful structures of white and silver soared into a hazy sky, connected by shimmering walkways. There were parks of astonishingly green and perfect grass, and trees of strange, geometric beauty. And everywhere, there were people. They strolled in pairs or alone, their faces bearing the same placid, untroubled expression as the woman’s. They were all, without exception, old. Their hair was silver or white, their bodies lean and graceful with an age that seemed to confer elegance rather than decay.

“But… where are the children?” Pemberton asked, his engineer’s mind searching for the flaw in this perfect design. “The young people?”

“There are no young people,” the woman said simply. “There are no children. There have been none for over two hundred years.”

He stopped walking and turned to face her, his mind reeling. “No children? But… that’s impossible. The race would die out.”

“It is dying out,” she said, with no more emotion than if she were stating the barometric pressure. “We are serene. We are content. We have conquered disease, want, and passion. We live our two centuries in peace and contemplation. But the biological imperative has… faded. The desire for procreation was the last of the great, messy urges to be mastered. And so, we are the last generation. A long, quiet, and dignified twilight.”

Pemberton felt a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature. It was the horror of a perfect, sterile, and utterly final system. “You’re mad,” he whispered. “The whole world is mad.”

“The world is sane for the first time,” she countered gently. “But you are an anomaly. A living fossil. You are a man of energy, of… messiness. You cannot understand our peace. Perhaps you should meet Elara. She is an anomaly, too, in her own way.”

She led him to a dwelling on the edge of the great city, a place where the perfect geometry of the future gave way to a more untidy, sprawling garden. Here, the plants grew with a riotous, almost aggressive vitality. A woman was kneeling in the soil, her hands black with earth. She was perhaps thirty, with a sharp, intelligent face and dark hair cut short. She looked up as they approached, and her eyes, unlike the placid blue of the others, were a restless, stormy grey.

“Elara,” said the white-haired woman. “This is Arthur Pemberton. He is from the past.”

Elara wiped her hands on her trousers and stood up, surveying Pemberton with a frank, almost challenging gaze. “The past,” she said. “The time of noise and babies. I’ve read about it. It sounds exhausting.”

“This is the last child born,” the first woman explained to Pemberton. “Elara. She was a… a statistical error. A final, flickering candle. Now, she is thirty, and she is alone.”

The woman who had guided him bowed her head slightly and glided away, leaving them in the sudden, profound silence of the garden.

“So,” Elara said, her voice sharp and full of a life he had not heard since his arrival. “You’re the man who fell out of a thunderstorm. Do you miss it? All that… striving?”

“I miss common sense,” Pemberton retorted, stung by her mockery. “I miss a world that has a future. This place… it’s a beautiful tomb.”
