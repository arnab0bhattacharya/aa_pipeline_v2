The royal gardens of Maharajah Aravind of Serampore were laid out with that exquisite sense of colour and absolute disregard for nature that marks the true landscape artist. Nothing so vulgar as a weed was permitted to intrude upon the geometric perfection of the parterres, and the flowers were trained to bloom in strict accordance with the aesthetic theories of the Court. There were orchids that looked as though they had been carved out of violet jade, and roses of a crimson so profound they seemed to drip with the blood of poets. The air was heavy with the scent of frangipani and sandalwood, and the fountains murmured a continuous, silver-tongued complaint about the heat.

In the very centre of this magnificent artifice, upon a pedestal of white marble veined with green, stood Lord Pavo. He was a peacock of quite extraordinary personal beauty, and he was perfectly aware of it. His neck was a polished cylinder of sapphire, his crest a diadem of spun moonlight, and when he spread his train, he displayed a galaxy of emeralds and gold that made the Maharajah’s crown jewels look like mere glass beads in a pawnbroker’s window.

Lord Pavo spent his days in a state of sublime self-contemplation. He had a small, silver-framed mirror placed near his favorite parterre, and he would gaze into it for hours, admiring the way the light caught the iridescent plumage of his breast. He was a great admirer of his own feet, too, which were a marvel of evolutionary sculpture, though he rarely allowed them to touch the earth, preferring to perch upon his marble column, elevated above the common squalor of the garden.

"It is a tragedy, is it not," he remarked one afternoon to the Minister of State, who happened to be strolling past, "that I am forced to share the garden with the begonias?"

The Minister, a wily old civet, bowed low. "Your lordship's plumage is indeed a masterpiece," he replied. "But surely the begonias have their place. They provide a certain… contrast."

"Contrast is merely a polite term for ugliness," said Lord Pavo, adjusting his crest with a languid shake of his head. "The world is divided into two classes: those who are beautiful, and those who are useful. It is a great misfortune to be born into the latter category. They are forced to work, while we are forced to be looked at. I assure you, the strain is quite as great."

The Minister, who had spent forty years in the arduous business of governing Serampore, and whose fur was turning grey with the effort, bowed again and retired, feeling that he had been in the presence of something very rare, and very empty.

It was the following Tuesday that the grey bird arrived.

She was a plain little creature, the colour of London fog, with a pair of bright, terrified eyes and a voice that seemed altogether too large for her body. She had been blown in by a storm, and she took refuge in the branches of a tamarind tree that grew just beneath the Maharajah's window.

She was wet, and she was cold, and she was lonely. And so, as is the custom of her kind when they are afflicted by these miseries, she began to sing.

It was a simple song, without artifice or ornament. It was a song about the wind, and the rain, and the long, grey sea that stretched between the cliffs of her homeland and the gilded cages of Serampore. It had in it the wild, salt sadness of the northern oceans, and the piercing sweetness of a world that exists only in memory.

The Maharajah Aravind, who had been reclining upon a divan of purple silk, attempting to read a Persian manuscript on the cultivation of melons, heard the song and sat upright. He pushed the manuscript aside and went to the window.

"What is that?" he said to the Grand Vizier, who was standing behind him with a plate of candied fruits.

"It appears to be a bird, Your Majesty," said the Grand Vizier, peering down. "A grey bird. A very common grey bird."

"I did not ask for its biography," said the Maharajah, testily. "I asked what it was. It is music. It is the first real music I have heard in Serampore. The court musicians are all technique and no soul. They play the notes, but they leave out the pain. This little bird has put the pain back. Go and fetch her."

The Grand Vizier, who was a man of taste and discernment, and who therefore knew that it was useless to argue with a Maharajah in the grip of an aesthetic revelation, bowed and went.

Lord Pavo, watching from his pedestal, observed the Grand Vizier's approach with a mixture of curiosity and disdain. He had heard the grey bird's song, and he was not impressed. It lacked, he thought, the visual splendour that was the hallmark of true art. It was all very well to make a noise, but to make a noise in grey was, in Lord Pavo's opinion, the height of bad taste.

"A word with you, madam," said the Grand Vizier, gazing up into the tamarind tree. "His Majesty is pleased to invite you to sing for him in the throne room."

The grey bird, whose name was Mira, fluttered down. She was trembling. "I am sorry," she said, in a voice that was like the rustle of dry leaves. "I did not mean to disturb anyone. I was only singing because I was sad."

"That," said the Grand Vizier, "is the only possible excuse for singing. His Majesty awaits you."

Mira was taken into the throne room, which was a vast, gilded cavern of opulence, hung with tapestries of blue and silver. The Maharajah sat upon his throne, a mountain of pearls and purple silk, and he listened to her sing.
