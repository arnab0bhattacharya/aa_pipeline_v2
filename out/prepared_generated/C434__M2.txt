The rain in Caerdydd did not fall; it hung in the air, a heavy, grey curtain that turned the harbor into a soup of rust and rot. I stood in the parlor of the house on Llanberis Road, my hands trembling not from the chill, but from the scent of lavender and old grief that seemed to cling to the wallpaper. Before me sat Elara Vane, the widow of Captain Josiah Thorne. She was a small woman, wrapped in black silk that had begun to fray at the cuffs, her eyes red-rimmed but dry, as if the tears had been burned away by the shock of the wreck three days prior.

She had sent for me specifically. Not the other survivors, not the dockhands who had hauled my body from the wreckage of the *Mistral’s Wake*, but me. I was the ship’s boy, the one who had been lashed to the mast when the hull tore open, the one who had watched everything.

"Tell me how he went," she said. Her voice was quiet, devoid of the hysterics I had expected. It was the stillness of a held breath, the silence before a storm breaks. "The papers say he went down with the ship. That he stayed at his post until the end. I need to know if that is true. I need to know if he was the man I married."

I looked at her hands, resting on the velvet arm of her chair. They were steady. I looked at the portrait on the mantelpiece, the oil painting of Josiah Thorne. He looked noble there, his jaw set, his eyes looking out toward a horizon that no longer existed. In that portrait, he was a hero. In the water, he had been a rat.

I remembered the moment the *Mistral’s Wake* hit the rocks off Penrhyn Bay. The sound had been like a giant bone snapping, a shriek of tearing metal that silenced the wind. The water had come in not as a wave, but as a wall, black and freezing. I had been thrown against the railing, the rope biting into my wrists. I had looked up, expecting to see Thorne barking orders, his face a mask of command, directing the crew to the lifeboats.

Instead, I had seen him on the quarterdeck, his face pale as the moon that had been hidden by the storm clouds. He had not been shouting. He had been holding the rail, his knuckles white, his eyes wide with a terror so pure it was almost beautiful in its ugliness. He had looked at the lifeboat being lowered, then at me, and then at the sea. He had made a choice. It was not a choice of duty. It was a choice of self-preservation.

He had scrambled into the boat before the order was given. He had pulled himself up over the gunwale, leaving the rest of us to the mercy of the tide. I had seen his face as the boat drifted away, just for a second. It was not the face of a man accepting his fate. It was the face of a man relieved.

I cleared my throat. The sound was harsh in the quiet room. Elara looked at me, her eyes searching for the truth in my face. She wanted the lie. She wanted the story that would allow her to bury him with honors, to tell her children that their father had died a gentleman. I could give it to her. It would be easy. I would say he held the wheel until the water swallowed him. I would say he saved a cabin boy. I would say he was brave.

But then I thought of the other men. I thought of young Davy, who had frozen to death on the deck, waiting for orders that never came. I thought of the way the sea had taken them, one by one, while Thorne sat safe in the bottom of that little dinghy, watching the *Mistral’s Wake* break apart.

"He was brave, Mrs. Vane," I said. The words felt like ash in my mouth.

Elara let out a breath she seemed to have been holding for days. A small, trembling smile touched her lips. "Thank you, boy. Thank you."

I watched the relief wash over her, the way her shoulders dropped, the way the tension left her jaw. It was a mercy, I realized. A cruel, necessary mercy. If I told her the truth, I would not be giving her justice. I would be giving her a wound that would never heal. She would look at her husband’s grave and see a coward. She would hear his name spoken and feel disgust. The story of his death would become a stain on her life, a secret she would carry to her own grave.

But there was something else. A knot in my stomach that tightened with every second of my silence. I was a liar. I was a boy of sixteen, and I had just committed an act of deception that felt heavier than the ship itself. I had stolen his death, and in doing so, I had stolen her right to know the man she had loved. Was that love real, if it was built on a foundation of lies? Or was the lie a kindness?

I looked at the portrait again. Thorne’s painted eyes seemed to mock me. He had been a good man, in his way. He loved his family. He paid his debts. He treated the crew fairly, mostly. But he was human. And in the face of the abyss, he had chosen himself. That is what humans do. We choose ourselves.

"Did he speak?" Elara asked, her voice softer now, intimate. "Did he say anything to you, in the end?"

I hesitated. The rain tapped against the windowpane, a rhythmic ticking that matched the beating of my heart. I could say he screamed my name. I could say he apologized. I could say he promised to come back.
