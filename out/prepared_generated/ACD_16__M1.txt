It was in the late autumn of 1892 that a certain strangeness began to pervade the personal column of *The London Courier*, a paper of some standing but modest circulation, which I then edited from a set of rooms just off Fleet Street. The advertisements, those curious little communications which serve as the public’s confessional and marketplace, had taken on a peculiar and uniform character. Where once there had been the usual fare of lost lap-dogs and rooms to let, there now appeared a series of notices composed almost entirely of numerals.

“One Three Seven. Two Four. Nine Nine Nine,” read one. Another, under the heading ‘Private,’ offered simply: “Seven Eight. Fourteen. One. Sixty.” For a week, these cryptic messages appeared daily, always in the same type, always placed by the same anonymous hand and paid for in advance with a single, crisp sovereign. My foreman, a man named Peters, a veteran of the composing room with ink-stained fingers and a printer’s stoop, merely grunted that it was a new sort of madness. But it troubled me. A newspaper column is a public trust, and I felt a vague unease at being the unwitting conduit for such nonsense.

The explanation, when it came, was delivered not by a professional but by a retired schoolmaster from Clapham, a Mr. Abernathy, whose letter was as precise as his penmanship. He had been a reader for forty years, he wrote, and had a keen interest in ciphers. The numbers, he posited, were not random but a simple substitution code. Each digit corresponded to a letter of the alphabet. He had decoded several and found them to be terse, practical messages: ‘MEET THE QUAY’, ‘BRING THE GOODS’, ‘ONE MAN ONLY’. He concluded, with the sober certainty of a man who has solved a puzzle, that we were being used as a communication channel for a gang of thieves.

His theory was sound, but it remained a theory until the following Tuesday morning. Peters did not arrive for his shift. An hour later, a constable from the City of London Police was at my door, his helmet in his hand and a grim look on his face. Peters had been found in a narrow alley off Shoe Lane, unconscious and brutally beaten. His pockets had been turned out, but the few shillings and the cheap watch he carried were left untouched. This was no common footpad’s work. The motive, the constable suggested, was not robbery but a warning, or perhaps the extraction of information.

A chill ran through me. The abstract puzzle had suddenly acquired a violent and corporeal reality. Peters, in his simple curiosity, must have seen something, or perhaps he had tried to decipher the messages himself and had come too close to the truth. The police, while sympathetic, were methodical and slow. They saw a case of grievous bodily harm; I saw the direct consequence of the columns I printed. The responsibility was mine, and I knew I could not wait upon the ponderous machinery of the law. I needed a mind that could move with the speed and ingenuity of the criminals themselves.

I had heard of such a man. His name was Alistair Finch, and he was said to operate from a set of chambers near the Temple, taking only those cases which presented a unique intellectual challenge. The following afternoon, I found myself climbing a narrow, dusty staircase to a door marked with nothing more than a brass plate bearing his name. The man who admitted me was tall and spare, with a high, pale forehead and eyes of a startling grey that seemed to take in every detail of my person and my purpose in a single, sweeping glance. His room was a chaos of books, papers, and strange chemical apparatus, yet there was an underlying order to the disorder, as if every object had its precise and necessary place.

“Mr. Hargrove, of *The London Courier*,” he said, without a question in his tone. He gestured to a worn leather armchair. “You are here about the numerals in your personal column. And, I perceive, about something more. You have the look of a man who has received a shock. A cup of tea? No, you would prefer something stronger. The sherry is on the sideboard.”

I was taken aback by his immediate grasp of the situation. “You know of the matter?”

“I make it my business to know of such things,” he replied, his long fingers steepled before him. “A novel cipher, a public forum, a sudden act of violence. The elements are suggestive. Pray, tell me the facts, from the first advertisement to your visit to the police this morning. Omit nothing, however trivial it may seem.”

I did as he asked, my account punctuated by his sharp, incisive questions. He asked for the exact wording of Mr. Abernathy’s letter, the precise location of the alley where Peters was found, the nature of his injuries, and the make of his watch. When I had finished, he rose and paced the room, his shadow long and thin in the gaslight.

“A warning, then,” he murmured, more to himself than to me. “They wished to silence the typesetter. Why? Because he was the one man, besides yourself, who had a legitimate reason to handle the original copy. They feared he had noticed a discrepancy. A smudge of ink, a particular quality of paper, a scent… something that would betray the identity of the man who placed the advertisements.” He stopped and turned to me, his eyes alight with a fierce intelligence. “The police are looking for a common assailant. We must look for a ghost. But every ghost, Mr. Hargrove, leaves a trace. Bring me the original copy for the last month’s advertisements. Every scrap of paper. And tell me the name of the clerk who takes the payments for the column.”
