The rain in Calder Valley does not fall; it strikes. It hammers against the slate roofs of the curate’s lodging with a persistence that felt, to me, like the judgment of God made manifest in the physical world. I sat by the cold grate, watching the embers die, and I felt the same coldness settling into my bones. I had been in Ellington for three weeks, and in three weeks, I had learned nothing of comfort, nothing of welcome. I was the shadow of the mill-owner, a priest without a flock, a man of the cloth who smelled of coal dust and silence.

My name is Arthur Penhaligon, and I am a curate, though the title seems a mockery now. Mr. Hargreaves, the master of the spinning mills, had placed me here not out of charity, but out of convenience. He needed a chaplain to keep the workers from rioting, to whisper into their ears the patience of Job and the submission of the slave. He did not need a conscience; he needed a mute. And so I sat, listening to the rain lash the windowpane, and I hated him. I hated him with a ferocity that made my hands shake, and I feared it, for it was a fire that consumed the very reason I claimed to serve.

The door opened with a creak that sounded like a bone breaking. I looked up, expecting the face of Mr. Hargreaves, or perhaps one of his spies, but it was not. It was Mrs. Elspeth Thorne. She was the schoolmistress of the parish, a widow of fifty years, with a face like a crumpled map of endurance and eyes that burned with a cold, sharp intelligence. She did not bow; she did not smile. She stepped inside, shaking the water from her cloak, and closed the door with a decisive click that severed the howling wind outside.

"You look like a ghost, Mr. Penhaligon," she said. Her voice was low, devoid of the honeyed deference the workers used to address me, and devoid of the polite indifference the masters showed. It was plain, and therefore, it cut deep.

"I am merely resting, Mrs. Thorne."

"Resting?" She moved to the hearth, not to warm herself, for the fire was dead, but to inspect the grate with a critical eye. "You are brooding. I can see it in the set of your jaw. You are turning the stones over in your mind, looking for a crack in the foundation. Do not trouble yourself. The foundation is solid. The mill is solid. The master is solid. You are the only thing in Ellington that seems ready to collapse."

I turned to face her, my anger flaring up like a spark in dry tinder. "I am a clergyman, madam. I am here to offer solace, not to be inspected for structural integrity."

"Solace," she repeated, the word tasting of ash in her mouth. "You offer them the solace of starvation? You tell the weavers that their hunger is a cross they must bear? You tell the mothers that their children’s coughs are the will of Providence?"

She stepped closer, and for the first time, I saw the storm in her eyes. It was not a storm of rage, but of a fierce, righteous grief. "I have watched you, Mr. Penhaligon. I have watched you walk past the huts where the children lie sick with the fever, and you do not look down. You look straight ahead, at the horizon, as if the suffering of these people is merely a landscape you are passing through. You are cold. You are distant. And you are wrong."

The accusation hit me with the force of a physical blow. I wanted to deny it, to plead my ignorance, to cite the orders of my patron. But the words died in my throat. For she was right. I had been so busy preserving my dignity, so terrified of offending the master, that I had allowed my soul to freeze over. I had become a mirror, reflecting back the cruelty of Hargreaves, and calling it duty.

"I did not know," I whispered, and the shame of it was a hot, prickling sensation on my skin.

"Ignorance is no excuse," she said, her voice softening, yet retaining its steel. "But it is a beginning. Come. There is a child in the cottage on the hill. Little Thomas. He is dying. The doctor says it is consumption. The father cannot pay the fee. I have come to ask if you, the man of God, will come and speak to him. Not to speak of patience. Not to speak of submission. But to speak of life. To tell him that his life matters, that his suffering is not a penance, but an injustice."

I looked at her, really looked at her, and I saw in her face the image of the saint I had forgotten. She was not beautiful; she was plain, and her hair was grey, and her clothes were worn. But there was a dignity in her bearing that shamed my own pretensions. She was the only person in Ellington who did not flatter me, who did not fear me, and who did not despise me. She saw me, and she demanded that I see myself.

I stood up. My legs felt weak, but my mind was clear. The rain outside seemed less a judgment now, and more a cleansing. I reached for my hat.

"I will go," I said.

Mrs. Thorne nodded, a single, sharp movement. "Then go. And Mr. Penhaligon?"

I paused, my hand on the door. "Yes?"

"Do not return with your eyes fixed on the horizon again. Look at the ground. Look at the people. That is where the truth is. That is where the fire burns."
