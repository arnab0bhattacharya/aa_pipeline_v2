I will not attempt to recount the particular incidents of my early life, nor to lay before the reader the gradual and insidious steps by which my ruin was accomplished; for the memory of that which was once my glory is now become the source of my most unutterable suffering, and to dwell upon it is to plunge afresh into the burning lake of my own despair. Let it suffice that I was once the wife of Mr. Alistair Blackwood, a man of whose reputation in the world of science and philosophy it would ill become me here to speak, save only to confess that I loved him with a fervour, an idolatry, that was the very religion of my soul. And it was this love, this fatal and absorbing devotion, that first tempted me to a transgression of those silent laws which govern my sex, and to lift my voice in the great assembly of mankind.

For I had written a book. In the solitude of our chambers in Wimpole Street, while my husband pursued his profound and inscrutable researches into the hidden workings of the physical universe, I had given birth to a creation of my own. It was a story of passion and of woe, of a soul struggling against the dark decrees of fate; and in the writing of it, I had poured out all the secret sympathies, the wild imaginings, the sublime and terrible emotions that agitated my own heart. When it was finished, I sent it forth into the world under a name that was not my own—a mere phantom of the brain, a shadow to conceal my trembling hand. And lo! this phantom was crowned with laurels. The world, which had been to me a vast and indifferent wilderness, suddenly rang with the praises of my work; my secret child was caressed and cherished by strangers, while I, its true mother, remained unknown, a silent and happy priestess at the shrine of my own hidden divinity.

But no secret can be long concealed beneath the all-seeing eye of the world. A base and envious spirit, some reptile of the press, traced the phantom to its source; and one morning, the dreadful truth was blazoned in the public journals. I remember the hour—the very slant of the sunbeams through the window, the dust motes dancing in the light, the cheerful cries of the street-sellers below—as if it were the last moment of a tranquil dream before the onset of a nightmare. My husband read the intelligence in silence, a silence more dreadful than any thunder; he laid the paper down, and turned upon me a look in which I read my sentence. It was not anger, but a cold, profound, and annihilating shame. The woman he had chosen for his quiet companion, the gentle minister to his domestic comforts, had unsexed herself; she had become a public spectacle, a creature of notoriety, her name coupled with the coarse plaudits of the multitude.

From that moment, a gulf, dark, wide, and impassable, yawned between us. The world, which had so lately applauded me, now shunned me as a thing of monstrous and unfeminine ambition. The ladies who had courted my acquaintance now passed me with averted eyes; the men regarded me with a curiosity that was worse than scorn. I was an anomaly, a violation of the natural order, and was made to feel the full weight of that social anathema which is reserved for such as I. But the coldness of the world was as a summer breeze to the frozen atmosphere of my own home. My husband, in the pride of his masculine intellect, could not endure that the wife of his bosom should be the author of a popular romance. He forbade me, in a voice of stern and final authority, ever to touch the pen again.

“Your province, Elara,” he said, and his words were as drops of vitriol upon my soul, “is here, in the quiet performance of your duties. Leave the struggles of the imagination to those whose minds are fitted for such follies. You have brought ridicule upon my name, and disgrace upon yourself. Let this be the end of it.”

And so my prison was built around me. The very instrument of my joy became the emblem of my bondage. I would look upon my little writing-desk, the silent confidante of all my hopes and fears, and my heart would swell with a passion too mighty for utterance. The world of my imagination, which had been a glorious and peopled universe, now rose before me as a paradise from which I was for ever exiled. The characters I had created, my spiritual offspring, seemed to cry out to me from the silent pages of their existence, and I was powerless to aid them. To write was a crime; to remain silent was a living death. My mind, denied its natural and necessary sustenance, began to prey upon itself. A profound melancholy, a settled and inconsolable wo, took possession of my spirit.

One evening, when the autumnal winds were howling without, and the rain lashed against the window-panes like the tears of some despairing spirit, I was sitting in the darkened parlour, my hands clasped in my lap, my gaze fixed upon the dying embers in the hearth. The storm was but a faint echo of the tempest that raged within my own breast. My husband sat opposite me, engrossed in a scientific treatise, his brow calm, his countenance unmoved by the elemental war, or by the silent agony of the woman before him. In that moment, a thought, dark and terrible as the wing of a destroying angel, descended upon me. The gulf between us was not of his making alone; it was the work of a cruel and unjust society, a society that would chain the soaring eagle to the earth, and bid it be content with the dust.
