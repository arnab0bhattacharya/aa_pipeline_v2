The rain had fallen for three days without ceasing, and the valley of Blackstone was drowned in it. From the parlour window of the mill-owner’s house I could see the great grey face of the factory, its rows of windows streaming, and the river swollen and brown, churning against the weir. The sound of the water was a constant roar beneath the clatter of the looms, and the smoke from the chimneys lay upon the hillside like a shroud. My father sat at the head of the table, his broad back to the fire, his newspaper spread before him. He was a man of iron constitution and iron opinions, and the failing of the season’s orders had hardened him into a sort of relentless tranquillity.

“It is a pity,” he said, looking up at last, “that the weather should turn so foul just as we are compelled to thin the hands.”

I said nothing. I had learned, in the twenty-five years of my life, that silence was often the only shield against his logic. He saw the world as a ledger; profit and loss, credit and debt. He did not see the hollow cheeks of the children who gathered at the mill gate, or the way the women wrapped their shawls tighter against a hunger that the fire could not warm.

“The orders from Liverpool are cut by a third,” he continued, folding the paper with a decisive crack. “I have no choice. The wages must come down. The people must understand that we cannot pay for work that is not there. It is the law of the market.”

“It is a law that starves them, sir,” I said, my voice trembling despite my efforts to steady it. “I walked through the lower lane yesterday. The widow Hemmings has not a crust in the house. If you reduce the rate further, they will die.”

My father looked at me with a kind of pitying contempt. “You are too soft, Eleanor. You have been reading too many of those radical pamphlets. The poor are always with us. If they cannot endure a little hardship now, how will they ever learn thrift? It is for their own good.”

He rose and left the room, his heavy boots ringing on the flags, and I was left alone with the fire and the rain. The injustice of it burned in my throat like acid. I had no money of my own—my pin-money was a pittance, barely enough for ribbons and books. I had no power to open the granary or to sign a check. I was a woman, a daughter, a dependent creature in a house where the master’s word was scripture. I went to the window and pressed my forehead against the cold glass. The valley lay grey and wretched before me, and my heart swelled with a fierce, impotent anger.

Later that afternoon, Mr. Vane and Captain Harwood came to call. They were my father’s chosen suitors for me—men of property and standing, with smooth hands and smooth consciences. They sat in the drawing-room, drinking my father’s claret and warming their shins at the fire, while the wind howled outside like a hungry beast.

“Damnable weather, Miss Thorne,” said Captain Harwood, a florid man with a laugh that sounded like a whip-crack. “Ruins the hunting. But I suppose it keeps the looms busy, what?”

“The looms are silent, Captain,” I replied coldly. “The orders have failed.”

“Ah, well, trade is trade,” said Mr. Vane, a thin, precise man who looked at me as if I were a heifer at market. “Your father is a prudent man. He knows how to manage his capital. These people must learn to manage theirs. It is not for us to feed the idle.”

I felt the blood rush to my face. “They are not idle!” I cried, rising from my chair. “They are starving! They have worked for us until their fingers are raw, and now, in their hour of need, you speak of capital and prudence? Have you no bowels of compassion?”

The two men exchanged a look of amused surprise. “Come, come, Miss Thorne,” said Vane, smoothing his waistcoat. “You are letting your feelings run away with you. It is unbecoming in a lady. We cannot alter the economy of the nation by weeping over it. A pretty face should not be clouded by such gloomy thoughts.”

I looked from one to the other—these men who would have me for a wife, who would have me bear their children and preside over their tables—and I saw them clearly. They were as hard as my father, as indifferent as the stone walls of the valley. They saw the poor as a different order of being, a machine to be used and discarded.

“Get out,” I said, my voice low and shaking. “Leave this house.”

“Eleanor!” my father’s voice boomed from the doorway. He had heard the commotion. “What is the meaning of this? Apologize to these gentlemen at once.”

“No,” I said. I stood my ground, my hands clenched at my sides. “I will not apologize for speaking the truth. If they cannot hear the cry of the hungry, they are not fit to sit in a Christian house. Let them go.”

The men rose, looking offended and confused, muttering about hysteria and bad breeding. My father’s face was black with thunder. He ushered them out, offering apologies that tasted like ash in the air. When the door closed, he turned to me, his eyes blazing.

“You have made a fool of yourself, Eleanor. You have ruined your prospects. Vane is a man of five thousand a year! And you send him away because of a few paupers in the lane?”

“I would rather starve in the lane,” I said, my voice ringing out clear and hard, “than feast with men who have no hearts. Let me be. If I cannot help the poor with money, I will at least not insult them with my company. I am going to the widow Hemmings. I have some lace I can sell. It is little, but it is all I have.”
