The light in the room was the colour of thin honey, pouring through the tall, uncurtained window and pooling on the faded blue carpet. It illuminated the grain of the wood, the specks of dust dancing in the shaft, the silver handle of the cane leaning against the writing desk. Mrs. Ailish Coram sat in the centre of this light, entirely still, her hands folded in her lap like two frail, winter birds. She was eighty, or perhaps eighty-five; the year of her birth had become, over time, a matter of artistic debate rather than historical record. The room itself was a museum of her reign: the cluttered desk, the chairs pushed back against the tapestry as if a crowd of invisible guests had only just risen to leave, the smell of dried lavender and old paper.

The young man sitting opposite her was named Philip. He held a notebook, and a pencil hovered between his fingers, poised to strike. He had a smooth, earnest face, the kind that collects certainties. He had come from an office in Fleet Street, bringing with him the chill of the November streets and a desire to pin her down, to enclose her life in a column of newsprint. He thought her a monument; she thought him a mirror, reflecting only what she wished to show.

“And Mr. Vance,” Philip prompted, his pencil making a faint, scratching sound. “You must have known him well. The stories of the two of you in the nineties are legendary.”

“Ah, Julian,” she said, and let the name float into the air, turning it around in her mind as if it were a polished pebble. Julian Vance. He had been a man of violent tempers and exquisite phrases. But the truth of him was a dark, tangled wood, and Philip was looking for a paved path. “Yes, he used to sit exactly where you are sitting now. He would arrive at midnight, soaked to the bone, having walked from Charing Cross simply because he had spent his last shilling on a volume of Baudelaire. He would stand by the fire, dripping onto the rug, and recite the entirety of ‘The Raven’ to the grandfather clock. He said the clock was his only true critic.”

She watched the young man’s face. He wrote this down. He believed it. He did not know that Julian Vance had been terrified of the dark and had always taken a hansom cab, paying for it with money borrowed from his long-suffering landlady. He did not know that Julian had loathed Poe, considering him a vulgar sensationalist. The beauty of the young, Mrs. Coram thought, lies in their hunger for the impossible; they prefer the myth, for the myth has a shape, whereas life is merely a smudge on the glass.

“He threw the manuscript of his first novel into the river,” she continued, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial murmur. “I watched him do it. We stood on Battersea Bridge in the fog. The pages scattered like white birds on the black water. He wept. I held his hand. It was the hand of a genius, you know, quite remarkably small, like a child’s.”

She paused, adjusting the grey shawl around her shoulders. In truth, she had never been on Battersea Bridge with Julian Vance. She had been in bed with a fever, and he had been in a tavern in Wapping, drinking gin with a woman who sold flowers. The manuscript had not been thrown into the river; it had been lost behind a radiator in a lodging house in Pimlico, only to be discovered years later by a charwoman who used it to light the kitchen fire. But the bridge, the fog, the white birds—these were the necessary ornaments of a legend. They gave the dead a dignity they had never possessed in life. And Philip’s pencil moved swiftly, transcribing the fiction, binding it to the page.

The fire shifted, sending a sudden flare of crimson light across the ceiling. The house in Chelsea seemed to contract around them, a shell worn smooth by the tides of conversation. For thirty years, this room had been the centre of the earth. Poets had wept here; critics had sharpened their claws on the legs of this very table; a young Irish playwright had confessed his ambitions to the grandfather clock, which had ticked on, indifferent. The air was thick with the ghosts of unfinished sentences.

“Did he ever speak of the woman in the painting?” Philip asked, glancing up at a portrait on the wall. It was a dim, impressionistic smudge of a face, all blues and greys, the eyes two dark, unfinished strokes.

“Every day of his life,” Mrs. Coram said, though the painting was of her own mother, done by a third-rate artist who had died of consumption before he could finish the left ear. “She was a dancer. She broke her ankle on the opening night of a ballet, and he carried her through the streets of Paris to a hospital, but it was too late. She never danced again. He kept the broken slipper in a glass case. It was lined with red velvet. He said it was the only truly tragic object in the world.”

Philip’s eyes widened. The red velvet, the broken slipper, the streets of Paris—it was a magnificent, devastating tale. He wrote it down with a reverence that was almost religious. Mrs. Coram felt a sudden, sharp pang of something—was it guilt? No, it was closer to pity. He was building a cathedral out of matchsticks. He needed her to be the keeper of the flame, the high priestess of a golden age that had never been quite so golden. If she told him the truth—that the nineties had been largely a matter of unpaid bills, bad teeth, and tedious arguments about the nature of the adjective—he would be profoundly disappointed. The truth would be a draught that extinguished the light in his eyes.
