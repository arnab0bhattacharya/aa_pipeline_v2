If there be any reader of this humble narrative who has ever taken a cold plunge into the murky waters of adversity, let that reader decide whether the sudden shock is more endurable than the slow, creeping freeze of an obligation one cannot repay. For my part, I believe it is the gradual ice, the inch-by-inch encroachment of a debt that freezes the blood far more completely than any sudden calamity; and it was precisely this creeping frost that pinched the heart of Mrs. Salome Tappitt.

Mrs. Tappitt was the relict of the late Mr. Ezekiel Tappitt, a man who had, in his time, lent money upon various and sundry pledges in the neighbourhood of Somers Town. The establishment of the Tappitts was situated in a narrow, ill-ventilated street, going down from the main road towards the railway works, which street shall be called, for the purposes of this history, Pottaker’s Passage. It was a street where the houses seemed to lean against one another for drunken support, where the bricks were stained with the soot of a hundred dingy winters, and where the very gutters appeared to have given up the struggle of life and resigned themselves to a stagnant despair. Over the warped and blistered door of Number Fifteen hung the three brass balls, tarnished to a sickly green, which, in the days of her prosperity, Mrs. Tappitt was wont to regard as the three golden orbs of her particular universe.

Mrs. Tappitt was a woman of a remarkably hard and dry exterior. She possessed a hooked nose, sharp as a hatchet, and two small, unwinking eyes, set close together, which seemed to be perpetually calculating the exact depreciation of a customer’s misfortune. Her voice was high, and cracked, and nasal; and she had a peculiar habit of drawing in her breath with a hiss, as if the very mention of money not immediately forthcoming caused her physical pain. "The interest, if you please," she would say, extending a claw-like hand, "is two-and-sixpence in the pound per month. I make no exceptions, I am not a charity, I am a lone widow, and the world owes me a living, though it is mightily backward in paying it."

Such had been her constant refrain for twenty years. She had lent to the desperate, the improvident, the starving, and she had exacted her ruinous percentages with a regularity that would have done credit to a clockwork automaton. She had never been known to remit a farthing. If a borrower wept, she hissed; if a borrower pleaded illness, she demanded the pledge; if a borrower was carried out dead, she presented her account to the bewildered and sorrowing widow. In all this, she was assisted by a lodger, a Mr. Gudgeon, a lean, cadaverous man with a red nose, who had once been a schoolmaster, but who now devoted his talents to the valuation of second-hand clothing, and who occupied a back room on the second floor, subsisting chiefly on bread, gin, and the contemplation of his own misfortunes.

It was upon a Tuesday in November, in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and forty-something, that the wheel of Mrs. Tappitt’s fortune began to turn, as wheels will turn, with a most unpleasant and lurching suddenness. She had been descending the dark and broken staircase to answer a knock at the street door, when her foot, encased in a stout and heavy boot, caught upon the frayed edge of the oil-cloth. She stumbled, she pitched forward, she made a wild and convulsive clutch at the banister, which gave way with a splintering crash, and she fell, with a heavy and resounding thud, the entire length of the staircase, coming to rest at the bottom in a heap of black bombazine and disordered grey hair.

The fall was of a nature to shatter the strongest constitution. Her left leg was broken in two places, and she had sustained a severe blow to the head, which left her, for the space of a week, in a state of bewildered and rambling insensibility. Mr. Gudgeon, hearing the crash, had descended with an unsteady haste, and had gazed upon the fallen widow with an expression of profound and helpless melancholy. "She is gone," he murmured, shaking his head, "the poor lady is gone, and I shall be a lodger without a landlady, which is a ship without a rudder, and a melancholy predicament for any man." But Mrs. Tappitt was not gone. She was merely broken; and it was her singular fate to be gathered up from the floor of her own passage, and deposited upon her own bed, by the very neighbour whom she had, but three months before, refused a loan of five shillings upon a silver teaspoon.

This neighbour was a Mrs. Penelope Pringle. Mrs. Pringle occupied the house opposite, Number Fourteen, and she was a woman of a very different stamp. She was a stout, comfortable, bustling person, with a broad and placid face, and a manner of cheerful industry that seemed to Mrs. Tappitt to be nothing short of an insult to human suffering. Mrs. Pringle took in washing. Her hands were red, and her arms were stout, and her voice was loud and good-natured. She had a catchphrase, too, which she employed upon all occasions, and which was, to the mind of Mrs. Tappitt, the most maddening combination of syllables in the English tongue. "Never say die!" Mrs. Pringle would exclaim, wringing out a sheet with a twist of her powerful wrists. "Never say die, Mrs. Tappitt! It’s a long lane that has no turning, and the darkest hour is just before the dawn!"
