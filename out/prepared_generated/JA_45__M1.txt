It was a measure of Mrs. Delavere’s regard for her cousin, that she had written to invite him to London at all, and a measure of her superiority of mind, that she should regard the visit as a subject for amusement rather than embarrassment. Mr. Edward Ferrers, the Vicar of a small parish in Somersetshire, was, she informed her circle, a creature of such primitive habits and unpolished understanding, that his arrival in town must necessarily afford a spectacle as novel as it was instructive. She pictured him as a man who would be astonished by a street lamp, and oppressed by the very height of the ceiling in her drawing-room. The reality, when it arrived, was a gentleman of about five-and-thirty, whose chief peculiarity was a profound and settled quietness. He was not handsome, nor was he ill-looking; he was simply unadorned, possessing a countenance which seemed to have been formed for the expression of good sense and good humour, and which had settled into that expression from long and untroubled use. His dress was respectable but unfashionable, and he carried a small, worn leather bag which he refused to entrust to the footman.

Mrs. Delavere, observing him from the window of her fashionable house in Hanover Square, felt a pang of triumph. Here was her rustic relation, delivered from the obscurity of his parsonage into the brilliant light of her world. She resolved to present him to her friends not as an equal, but as a study, a character from a novel brought to life for their edification. Her first step was to introduce him to Lady Anne Wrotham, a lady whose chief accomplishment was an air of perpetual, fashionable boredom. “Cousin Edward,” said Mrs. Delavere, with a smile that was all for her guest, “has come to town on the most serious of errands. He is to petition the Bishop of London for the restoration of his church roof, which has, I believe, the audacity to leak.”

Lady Anne turned a languid gaze upon Mr. Ferrers. “A leaking roof,” she murmured, as if it were a novel form of social affliction. “How very distressing. And you have come all this way, alone, to speak of it?”

“My parishioners could not be spared,” Mr. Ferrers replied, his voice even and calm. “The harvest is nearly in. And it seemed a matter better discussed in person than by letter.”

The simplicity of the answer, so devoid of self-importance, gave Lady Anne a moment’s pause. She had expected a provincial to be either fawning or overwhelmed. Mr. Ferrers was neither. He was merely stating a fact. During dinner, which Mrs. Delavere had arranged with an eye to dazzling him, Mr. Ferrers was more silent than not. He listened to the talk of politics and fashion with the same attentive air he might have given to a sermon. When addressed, he answered with a brevity that bordered on the monosyllabic, yet was never uncivil. A visiting Colonel, a man made up of bluster and self-satisfaction, attempted to draw him out on the subject of country sport.

“I suppose, Vicar, you are a great man for the foxes and the pheasants? Nothing else to do out in the sticks, eh?”

Mr. Ferrers considered this. “I confess, Colonel, that my duties leave me little time for sport. The parish accounts, the visiting of the sick, and the preparation of two sermons a week occupy me sufficiently. But the hounds are a great interest to the farmers, and I am glad for their sakes.”

The Colonel, having expected either a hearty agreement or a tiresome moral objection, found himself with nothing to say. The conversation turned to a new play at Drury Lane, of which Mrs. Delavere had seen the first performance.

“It is all very tragic,” she declared, “and the leading lady has a most affecting way with her tears. She has the entire audience in raptures.”

“I find,” said Mr. Ferrers, after a slight hesitation, “that I am often more moved by the good sense of the minor characters. The hero and heroine are so intent upon their own feelings, they seem to forget they are not the only persons in the world.”

A quiet fell over the table. It was a remark of such unexpected observation that even Lady Anne looked at him with a new interest. Mrs. Delavere felt a momentary flicker of irritation; her quaint cousin was not behaving quite as he ought. He was not meant to have opinions. He was meant to be a curiosity. Yet, the following evening, when she held a small musical soiree, she found that her guests were not laughing at him, but listening to him. A young lady, Miss Amelia Croft, had been playing a complicated sonata with much feeling but little precision.

“It is a very difficult piece,” she sighed, looking for praise.

“Your passion for it is very evident,” said Mr. Ferrers, kindly. “I confess I do not have the ear to follow all the notes, but I could hear the story in the music, and that, I think, is the greater part.”

Miss Amelia, who was accustomed to being told her fingering was exquisite, found the compliment to her soul far more gratifying. Soon, a small group had gathered near the vicar, asking him about his parish, his people, and the simple, orderly life he led. They were people of the world, tired of its artifice and its endless, trivial contests. In Mr. Ferrers’s good sense, they found a restfulness they had not known they craved. He spoke of a farmer’s stubborn cow with the same serious consideration he would give a question of theology, and in doing so, he made both seem matters of quiet, human importance.
