Lord Arthur Pomeroy had dedicated his life to the pursuit of the useless, and had consequently achieved a perfection of manner that made him thoroughly indispensable to society. His volume of verse, "Vellums and Venoms," had been read by all the people who mattered, and universally condemned by those who did not. This, of course, was the supreme triumph of his career. For there is only one thing in the world worse than being talked about by the middle classes, and that is not being talked about by them at all.

It was, therefore, with a sense of profound satisfaction that he accepted an invitation to Brackenbury Park in Leicestershire. The invitation came from Lady Arabella Vance, a woman of impeccable cheekbones and questionable taste, who kept her heart in an ivory casket and her husband in the stables. She had declared Arthur’s verse to be a "delicate poison," a compliment Arthur found infinitely preferable to being called a healthy tonic.

Her husband, Sir Tristram, was a man entirely composed of boots, horses, and a profound contempt for anything that could not be saddled or shot. He possessed a vocabulary of roughly three hundred words, mostly relating to the weather and the habits of partridges, and he deployed them with the relentless confidence of a philosopher. Sir Tristram viewed Arthur as a species of exotic parasite, harmless perhaps, but certainly not something one would wish to see in one's drawing-room.

Arthur arrived on a Friday afternoon, dressed in a velvet coat the precise colour of a bruised violet. The house was a vast pile of grey stone, smelling faintly of damp spaniels and old silver. In the great hall, a fire of colossal proportions roared up a chimney built for giants, making the room unbearably hot and giving the surrounding tapestries the appearance of being slowly baked.

"So glad you've come," said Lady Arabella, gliding towards him with a smile that was as carefully constructed as a Japanese fan. "Tristram is out with the keepers, inspecting the coverts. He is counting the pheasants, a melancholy arithmetic that always puts him in a dreadful temper. You must be exhausted by the journey."

"One is never exhausted by beauty, Lady Arabella," Arthur replied, sinking gracefully onto a sofa of embroidered crimson damask. "One is merely exhausted by the necessity of looking at things that are not beautiful. The English countryside is a terrible trial to the aesthete. It is so aggressively green."

"It is the price we pay for our pheasants," she laughed. "You do shoot, Mr. Pomeroy?"

"I shoot only with the eye," said Arthur, lighting a cigarette in a long silver holder. "The only weapon I trust myself with is a paradox, and even then, I rarely aim at anything so small as a bird."

The weekend’s ordeal commenced the following morning. Arthur was roused at an hour that no civilized person should be asked to confront, and dressed in a suit of coarse tweed that smelled faintly of gunpowder and rubbed his skin in a manner he considered entirely barbaric. He was handed a gun, a heavy, cold mechanism of polished steel and walnut wood that felt utterly alien in his delicate hands.

They stood in a damp field, the air sharp with the smell of wet bracken and the distant barking of dogs. Sir Tristram, magnificently attired in leather gaiters, looked at Arthur with the contemptuous pity one reserves for the hopelessly infirm.

"Keep your barrel up, Pomeroy," Sir Tristram barked, ejecting a spent cartridge. "Can't have you blowing off a beater's head. Bad form, and frightful paperwork."

"I am sure the beaters are far too well-hidden to be in any danger," Arthur murmured, shifting his weight from one aching foot to the other. "Indeed, I should be highly surprised to see a pheasant. They must possess a remarkably refined aesthetic sensibility to remain in such an ugly landscape."

As if in direct defiance of his taste, a magnificent cock pheasant suddenly rose from the ferns with a harsh, whirring cry, a burst of iridescent copper and emerald against the pale sky. Sir Tristram raised his gun to his shoulder with the mechanical precision of a machine. Arthur merely watched the bird, mesmerized by the flash of colour.

"Shoot, man!" Sir Tristram roared.

But Arthur did not. He lowered the muzzle of his gun, gazing after the bird as it disappeared over the brow of the hill.

"Missed it," Arthur said softly. "How exquisite."

Sir Tristram turned a colour that clashed violently with his complexion. "You didn't even fire!"

"True," replied Arthur. "To kill a thing of such brilliant plumage merely to watch it fall into the mud seems to me a terrible error of judgment. One should only shoot things that are badly dressed. If you were to aim at the vicar, for instance, I should be delighted to assist."

Sir Tristram said nothing, but his silence had a density to it that was almost physical. He turned on his heel and strode away, firing twice into the air with an accuracy that was entirely wasted on the sky.

The evening brought the true trial. Dinner at Brackenbury was a lengthy affair, served on heavy gold-edged china that glinted in the candlelight. The long table was adorned with arrangements of late roses, their petals scattered with a calculated carelessness that spoke of an expensive florist. Arthur had changed into evening dress, a white waistcoat heavily embroidered with silver threads, and felt himself restored to a state of grace.

Sir Tristram, however, had not forgotten the incident of the pheasant. He sat at the head of the table, carving a joint of mutton with the grim ferocity of a man dismembering a personal enemy.

"I have been reading your little book, Pomeroy," Sir Tristram said suddenly, fixing Arthur with a steely eye. "Found it in Arabella's boudoir. Dreadful stuff. All about lilies and languor and dead lovers. No healthy sentiment in it at all."
