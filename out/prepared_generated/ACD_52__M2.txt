The sound was not, in the first instance, a noise that one might readily attribute to the mundane mechanics of a trade. It was a rhythmic, heavy thudding, distinct and regular as the beating of a large, slow heart, which rose through the floorboards of my attic room with a persistence that defied the silence of the street. I am, by profession, an artist, and I dwell in the upper story of a narrow building in Hatton Garden, directly above the establishment of Mr. Silas Thorne, a jeweller of some modest reputation. My lodging is small, the walls are thin, and the plaster, though white and clean, offers little resistance to the vibrations of the night.

For three weeks, I had endured this percussion. It began, invariably, at eleven o’clock, when the gaslight in the shop below was extinguished and the iron shutters were drawn across the window. I had assumed, with the charitable credulity of the young, that Mr. Thorne was a man of industry, perhaps a horologist by inclination, who devoted his leisure to the delicate restoration of antique timepieces. A jeweller who repairs clocks is not an incongruous figure, and I had made no attempt to question him. Yet, as the nights wore on, the character of the sound altered. It ceased to be the sharp, metallic tick of a movement or the soft tap of a file on a jewel. It became a dull, heavy blow, followed by a dragging sound, as if some substantial object were being shifted across the stone pavement.

It was on a Tuesday evening, thick with the mist that hangs so tenaciously over the Thames, that the final incident occurred. I was seated at my easel, the smell of turpentine sharp in my nostrils, when the hammering began. It was louder than usual, the vibrations shaking the dust from the ceiling beams. I laid down my brush and listened, a frown of irritation tightening my brow. Suddenly, the rhythm faltered. There was a sharp, metallic clang, distinct from the dull thuds, and then a profound, unnatural silence. It was a silence that seemed to press against the ears, more oppressive than the noise itself.

I waited ten minutes, then twenty. No further sound emerged from the floor below. I rose, pulled on my shawl, and descended the narrow staircase. The air in the shop was cold and smelled faintly of oil and dust. Mr. Thorne was not to be seen. The counter was empty, the display cases locked, and the shop door stood ajar, the iron shutters rolled down halfway, leaving a sliver of the dark, wet street visible.

I stepped out into the lane. The mist was heavy, blotting out the gas lamps until they were mere haloes of pale light in the gloom. I called out to the shop window, but there was no answer. I knocked upon the shutters, the sound echoing sharply in the narrow alley. A cold dread, unreasoned and sudden, seized me. I knew no one else in the building who could account for the sounds, and the absence of the proprietor was a fact that demanded explanation. I returned to my room, sat by the fire, and waited. The silence from below was absolute.

By morning, the mist had lifted, revealing the grey, damp streets of London. I found a cab at the corner and directed the driver to the offices of Mr. Alistair Finch, a man whose name I had seen in the papers in connection with several matters of a delicate and legal nature. I had heard that he possessed a faculty for unraveling the most tangled threads of human conduct.

Mr. Finch received me in a room that smelled of old paper and strong tea. He was a tall man, with a face of such austere plainness that it seemed to lack any feature not strictly necessary for expression. He listened to my account with that intense, unmoving attention which he maintained throughout the narrative. When I had finished, he did not immediately speak. He walked to the window, looked out at the traffic below for a moment, and then turned to me.

"You say the sound was rhythmic," he said.

"Most certainly. And heavy. Like a hammer on stone."

"And you say the shutters were down?"

"Partially. There was a gap of perhaps two inches."

"Did you see the street?"

"I saw the wet cobblestones. I saw the reflection of the gaslight. I saw nothing else."

"Did you notice the condition of the door?"

"It was ajar. The key was not in the lock."

Mr. Finch nodded slowly. He pulled a pair of spectacles from his pocket and put them on, though I had not seen him squint. "Come with me," he said. "We will go to Hatton Garden."

We traveled in a hansom cab, Mr. Finch sitting in a corner, his knees drawn up, his eyes closed. I watched him with a mixture of curiosity and anxiety. He was silent, but I could feel the machinery of his mind working behind that calm facade. We arrived at the shop just as the sun was beginning to break through the clouds. The street was quiet, the shops just opening.

Mr. Finch did not enter the shop. Instead, he stood on the pavement opposite, leaning against the railings of the churchyard. He watched the entrance to Thorne’s shop with an intensity that made me uncomfortable. For ten minutes, nothing happened. Then, a figure emerged from the side door of the building, a man in a dark coat, carrying a heavy leather bag. He did not look at the front window. He walked quickly, his head down, toward the river.

"There," said Mr. Finch, in a low voice. "Follow him. Do not let him see you. I will remain here."
