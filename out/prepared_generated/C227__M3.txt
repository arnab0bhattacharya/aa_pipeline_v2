# The Coming Hunger

The morning light fell grey and uniform across the rooftops of Westminster, where the old palaces had long since been converted into administrative halls for the Republic of Britain. Cassian Vale stood at the window of the National Planning Office, watching the Thames slide by like a slow, patient animal. He was thirty-two years old, the youngest senior minister in the Coalition for Civic Renewal, and he had built his career on a single conviction: that the people of England deserved the truth, even when the truth was inconvenient.

He turned from the window and looked at the report in his hand. The pages were dense with projections, supply chain analyses, and climate yield data from the agricultural colonies in what had once been called the Southern Provinces—now the Associated Territories across the Atlantic. The conclusion was buried on page forty-seven, stated in the flat, clinical language of bureaucracy, but it was no less devastating for its restraint. Blight in the grain belts. Soil exhaustion in the maize provinces. Transport disruptions compounded by fuel rationing. The colonies would fail to meet their export quotas. And Britain, which had restructured its entire domestic agriculture around imported staples, would face critical food shortages within twelve months.

Famine. The word was not used in the report. The authors preferred "sustenance deficit." But Cassian had studied the numbers long enough to know what they meant. People would go hungry. People would die.

He set the report on the table and looked at the three colleagues who had gathered in the room with him. Liora Hale, the Minister of Resource Allocation, sat with her arms folded, her expression carefully neutral. Jonas Thorne, who oversaw the Colonial Liaison Office, was pretending to read something on his tablet, his eyes not moving. And Eliza Marsh, the senior strategist for the Coalition, stood by the door as if she might leave at any moment.

"We have to tell them," Cassian said.

Nobody responded for a moment. Then Liora shifted in her seat and spoke with the measured tone she used when she was about to deliver a disappointment. "Tell them what, exactly? That the colonies are struggling? We already knew that. The quarterly reports have been public for months."

"The quarterly reports say yields are down," Cassian said. "They do not say that the situation is terminal. They do not say that within a year, we will not have enough food to feed the population of this country. That is what the public needs to hear."

Jonas looked up from his tablet. His face had the pallid, slightly swollen look of a man who had not slept well for several nights. "Cassian, I understand your impulse. I even admire it, in principle. But you are asking us to release a projection based on data that is, at best, incomplete. The colonial governors have told us they are implementing new remediation programmes. If those programmes succeed, the situation may stabilise."

"And if they do not?"

Jonas pressed his lips together. "Then we reassess in six months."

"In six months, it will be too late," Cassian said. "The whole point of early warning is to give people time to prepare. If we wait until the shortages are visible, we will have panic. Hoarding. Violence. Everything we have built in the last twenty years—the civic trust, the rationing infrastructure, the community networks—all of it will collapse under the weight of sudden fear."

Eliza Marsh, who had been silent until now, spoke from the doorway. Her voice was low and steady, carrying the authority of someone who had managed three national elections and survived two political crises. "Cassian, I have spent my career studying how populations respond to catastrophic news. The pattern is consistent. When people are told that disaster is coming and they cannot see it, they do not prepare. They deny. They turn on the messengers. They look for someone to blame. And the first person they blame is the one who spoke."

"So we should stay silent?" Cassian asked. "We should let them believe everything is fine while we quietly stockpile reserves they do not know about?"

"I am not saying silent," Eliza replied. "I am saying careful. We release information in stages. We frame it as a challenge, not a catastrophe. We give people actions they can take—community gardens, reduced consumption, voluntary rationing. We let them feel that they are participating in a solution, not awaiting a doom."

Cassian stared at her. He respected Eliza. She was pragmatic where he was idealistic, and she had saved him from his own naivety more than once. But he could feel the weight of the report in his hand, the weight of the numbers, the weight of the empty stomachs that those numbers represented. He thought of his mother, who had raised him alone in a council flat in Battersea after his father died in the infrastructure wars. She had taught him that honesty was not a strategy but an obligation. You told the truth because it was the truth, and you trusted people to meet it.

"The remediation programmes in the colonies will not work," Cassian said. "I have read the implementation plans. They are underfunded, understaffed, and based on soil models that are five years out of date. Jonas knows this. Liora knows this. We all know this."

Liora met his gaze. "Yes," she said quietly. "We know."

"Then how can we justify silence?"

Jonas set his tablet down. "Because the Republic is fragile, Cassian. You know that better than anyone. We are twenty years into an experiment in democratic governance, and half the country still thinks it was a mistake. If we announce a famine, the factionalists will use it as proof that the republican system cannot protect its people. The monarchists will say the old order would have prevented this. And the people—the ordinary people you want to help—will not plant gardens. They will riot."
