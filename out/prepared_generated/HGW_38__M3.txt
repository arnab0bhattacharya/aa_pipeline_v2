Dr. Aldous Vane had taken the rooms above the ironmonger’s in Haversham market square because they were cheap and the south-facing window gave an excellent light by which to examine his specimens. He was a lean, sandy-haired man from the north, with the impatient, speculative eye of a modern biologist forced to make do with rural accommodations. He had come to Norfolk under the auspices of the Haversham Experimental Farm, a small but well-funded venture, to solve a problem that had begun to vex the Empire. The soil across the eastern counties was growing impoverished, the wheat yields declining year upon year with a depressing regularity. Vane’s solution, developed over three years of patient crossbreeding and Mendelian calculation, was a hardy, dwarf variety he had designated *Triticum vigens*—the vigorous wheat. It was engineered, through a process of relentless selection, to draw sustenance from the thinnest, most exhausted chalk and clay, sending down deep, tenacious taproots where lesser grains would merely wither.

The innovation had been a triumph of the laboratory. In the experimental plots, *Triticum vigens* grew with a startling, almost sinister lushness. The ears were heavy and thick, the stalks short and defiant against the wind. Vane had written several enthusiastic papers for the agricultural journals. He anticipated a knighthood, or at the very least a handsome pension, from a grateful legislature. He was, in his quiet, methodical way, an idealist of the first order. He believed in the mastery of the human intellect over the blind, wasteful processes of nature.

It was on a Tuesday morning in late July that the first sign of trouble manifested itself. Vane was shaving, his eyes fixed on his own foamy reflection in the mirror, when he heard the clatter of heavy boots on the uncarpeted stairs outside his door. It was an angry, determined clatter, entirely unlike the cautious tread of his landlady, Mrs. Hibble. He put down his razor and listened. The boots stopped, and a heavy fist struck the wood.

“They’re here, sir,” Mrs. Hibble said, her voice thin with alarm from the landing. “The farmers. I told them you was at your toilet, but they won’t be put off.”

“Very well,” said Vane, wiping his face. He pulled on his jacket, reflecting that an angry farmer was a comic enough figure to a man of science. He opened the door.

There were three of them. The foremost was Mr. Elias Grout, a broad, red-faced man who rented four hundred acres of heavy clay on the eastern side of the valley. Behind him stood a younger man, Thorne, and old Carfax, whose family had tilled the same soil since the days of the Georges. Their faces were set in expressions of profound, unyielding grievance.

“I’m Dr. Vane,” Vane said pleasantly. “What can I do for you, gentlemen?”

Grout stepped forward, his heavy boots creaking on the floorboards. “You can tell me what you mean by it, Doctor. That’s what you can do.”

“Mean by what, precisely?”

“By poisoning my land!” Grout’s voice was a low, trembling growl. He thrust a calloused hand into his coat pocket and produced a fistful of green stalks, which he threw down upon Vane’s reading table. The ears were thick, the stalks jointed and stiff. Vane recognized them instantly. *Triticum vigens*. “I found this sprouting in my lower field on Monday. By Wednesday it had crossed the hedgerow into the barley. Today, it’s choking the roots of my mangels. It’s everywhere. It’s eating the very heart out of my soil.”

Vane stared at the stalks, his mind leaping quickly from surprise to speculation. “That is impossible, Mr. Grout. The experimental plots are fully enclosed. There is a stone wall and a drainage ditch—”

“Your wall don’t stop roots, Doctor,” old Carfax said, his voice a dry rustle. “Nor does it stop the seed blowing in the wind. Whatever devil’s brew you cooked up down at that farm, it’s out. And it’s killing everything else.”

Thorne spoke up, his youthful face pale and earnest. “It’s not like ordinary wheat, sir. It’s got a sort of root that goes deep, and it spreads sideways under the earth. When I tried to hoe it out, it just broke and sprouted twice over from the pieces. It’s smothering my oats.”

Vane picked up a stalk from the table, examining it with a clinical curiosity that entirely missed the fury in the room. He noted the unusual thickness of the rhizome. It was, he supposed, a perfectly predictable biological adaptation. The very trait he had bred for—extreme hardiness and aggressive nutrient uptake—had made it a monster. It was not content with the poor soil of the experimental plot; given the opportunity, it would colonize any soil, draining the moisture and binding the earth in a dense, fibrous mat that no other plant could penetrate.

“It seems,” Vane said slowly, “to have hybridized beyond my initial projections. The lateral root spread is a recessive trait I thought I had bred out.”

Grout stepped forward, his vast frame blocking the light from the landing. “I don’t care a damn for your recessive traits, Doctor. I’ve got a mortgage on that land. If this weed of yours takes hold, I’m ruined. We all are.”

“You must understand, Grout,” Vane said, an impatient note entering his voice, “that the advancement of agricultural science inevitably involves some degree of risk. The potential benefits of a strain like this—”

“Benefits!” Grout spat. “You sit up here in your rooms, playing with your seeds, and you tell me about benefits? My boys have been working since dawn to pull this stuff out, and it’s no use. It just comes back. You’ve loosed a plague on us, and you sit there like a schoolmaster explaining a sum!”

Vane felt a sudden, sharp irritation. These men were stupid, he thought; they could not grasp the larger sweep of progress. “If the strain is proving invasive,” he said, “we shall simply have to devise an eradication protocol. A strong solution of copper sulphate, perhaps, or systematic burning and deep ploughing.”
