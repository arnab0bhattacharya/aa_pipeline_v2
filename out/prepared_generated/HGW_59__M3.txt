It is a curious thing to observe the modern man of science when he is suddenly confronted with a fact that entirely wrecks the neat, comfortable cosmogony he has spent his life constructing. The ordinary mind, when faced with the impossible, merely dismisses it; the scientific mind, on the other hand, is apt to fall into a panic of classification, desperately seeking to force the astounding anomaly into some dusty, pre-existing drawer. It was exactly this sort of panic that I witnessed in my patron, Mr. Bellingham, when I presented to him the facts of the Tsang Basin expedition.

We had been at work for three months in that desolate, sun-baked depression of central Asia, some two hundred miles west of the nominal authority of the local Russian administration. The basin is a vast, dead sea of gravel and bleached clay, rimmed by stark, fantastic hills of red sandstone. It is a place of stupefying silences and blinding light, where the air shimmers like gauze over the bones of the earth. Our camp was a cluster of patched tents, pitched beside a brackish well that tasted strongly of magnesium. Our sole companions were a handful of stolid, unwashed Mongol diggers, who viewed our frantic scratching in the dirt with a placid, unvarying contempt.

Bellingham was a man of considerable wealth, derived from some murky combination of South African mining and London speculation. He was a large, stout person, excessively neat, and possessed of a mind that was impatient of anything that could not be immediately translated into pounds, shillings, or social prestige. He had come out to the desert to hunt for dinosaurs. He wanted a complete skeleton, something gigantic and ferocious, to mount in the entrance hall of his new museum in South Kensington, thereby confounding his rival, Sir William Haversham, who possessed only a fragmentary Iguanodon. Bellingham’s interest in palaeontology was entirely competitive. He cared no more for the remote ancestry of the crocodile than he did for the theology of the Druids, provided it was big enough to make a show.

I was his hired expert, engaged to see that the bones were taken from the rock without fracture. I had been lured by the promise of unrestricted access to a virgin strata, and for six weeks I had been remarkably content. We had uncovered the massive, unmistakable vertebrae of a Ceratosaurus, a beast of the late Jurassic, and Bellingham was already drawing up telegrams of triumph to send to the *Times*. But then, on a Tuesday morning, our Mongol foreman, a man of singularly repulsive features named Urga, brought me a fragment of stone that entirely displaced the Ceratosaurus from my thoughts.

It was a piece of laminated limestone, and embedded in its surface was a length of petrified bone. It was not the bone of a reptile. It was unmistakably mammalian, and, more astonishing still, it was the femur of a biped. I went immediately to the tertiary basin where Urga had found it, leaving the Ceratosaurus to the pickaxes of the uncomprehending diggers.

What I unearthed in the next four days was a thing so bizarre and so fundamentally unsettling to our accepted chronology that I scarcely knew how to record it in my notebook. The creature had stood upright. It was not an ape, nor a sloth, but something entirely other—a bipedal, air-breathing organism that had walked the earth in the late Cretaceous period, a full sixty million years before the accepted dawn of the first primitive lemur. The skull was shattered, but the pelvic structure and the articulated bones of the right leg proved beyond any shadow of a doubt that it had carried itself erect. It was roughly the height of a modern man, though far more heavily built in the trunk.

But it was not the skeleton alone that froze me in my tracks. Beside the bones, arranged with obvious deliberation, lay a collection of tools.

They were fashioned from a dense, greenish flint, chipped and flaked with an undeniably purposeful art. There were heavy, chopper-like implements, and there were long, pointed blades, keen enough to have sliced through the hide of a contemporary dinosaur. The art of their making was not the crude battering of an ape; it was a deliberate, systematic flaking, indicating a mind that understood the cleavage of stone. They had been buried with their maker, or perhaps dropped in some sudden catastrophe and sealed beneath the silt of an ancient river.

I sat on the hot gravel beside this impossible revelation, mopping my brow, and felt the entire fabric of Darwinian progression waver and stretch. We had been accustomed to think of human intelligence as a recent, sudden thing, a brilliant spark struck from the collision of ice and mammalian adversity. But here was evidence of a thinking, contriving creature, walking the earth when the great reptiles still held dominion, making tools and shaping its environment, utterly unconnected by any thread of descent to the humanity that was to follow it millions of years later.

I did not telegraph Bellingham immediately. I was too absorbed in the intellectual shock of the thing. I packed the bones and the flints in raw cotton and carried them myself to his tent.

He was engaged in dictating a monotonous letter to his London secretary when I entered. He looked up with that impatient, proprietary air he habitually wore. "Well, Alders? Have we got the skull out yet? I want to cable Haversham by Friday."

I did not answer immediately. I placed the wooden case on his table and opened it. I took out the greenish flint blade and laid it beside the femur. "We have found something else," I said.

He stared at the flint, then at the bone, with a blank expression. "What is this? Some sort of native rubbish? I told you not to let the men bring in modern refuse."

"It is not modern," I said. "It is Cretaceous."
