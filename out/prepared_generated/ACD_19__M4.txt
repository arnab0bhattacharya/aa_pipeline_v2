The rain had beaten upon the windows of my consulting-room in Marylebone for the greater part of the afternoon, and I had fallen into that state of pleasant torpor which a dull fire and a duller day will engender, when the door opened and my friend Mr. Edgar Vane was shown into the room. He came in with a little rush, as a man does who has been hurrying through weather, and stood upon the hearthrug shaking the drops from his coat, his thin, sensitive hands moving with a nervous quickness which spoke of a temperament ill at ease.

“You will forgive the hour, Doctor,” said he. “I know that I have no claim upon your time, but I have come to you because I am told that you are a man who can see a meaning where others find only confusion, and because I am myself in a confusion which I cannot resolve.”

“Pray sit down,” said I, pushing a chair towards him. “You are a music teacher, if I do not mistake the case which you carry.”

He glanced at the flat leathern portfolio under his arm and smiled, though without much heartiness. “You are right, sir. I teach the pianoforte and the theory of music. My name is Edgar Vane, and I have a small practice among families of the better sort in this quarter of the town. It is upon a matter connected with one of my pupils that I have come to consult you.”

“I am at your service. Tell me the facts without reservation.”

He drew his chair closer and laid the portfolio upon his knee. “For three years,” said he, “I have given lessons twice a week to Miss Clara Ashcombe, the daughter of the late Mr. Josiah Ashcombe, the shipowner. She is seventeen years of age, and since the death of her father, two years ago, she has lived in the house of her guardian, Mr. Thomas Rennick, in Wimpole Street. She is a girl of singular gifts—I say it without flattery—and her progress upon the instrument has been such as I have rarely seen equalled in a pupil of her years. Until three months ago she was the most docile and diligent of students, and her work gave me nothing but satisfaction.”

“Until three months ago,” I repeated. “Something occurred at that time, then?”

“Something occurred, Doctor, which I can describe only as an oddity, and which has since grown into a mystery. She began to play a different tune at every lesson. I do not mean that she played badly, or that she played carelessly, but that she introduced into her exercises—into scales, into studies, into the very pieces which I had set her—a melody which was not in the score, and which she could not, or would not, explain. At first I thought it a trick of memory, some air caught from the street; but when I questioned her she told me, with a gravity which I did not like, that it was a tune which a stranger hummed beneath her window at night.”

I confess that my interest was now thoroughly aroused. “A stranger beneath her window? Did she describe him?”

“She said that she had never seen him, that she had only heard the humming, always in the same low, monotonous key, and always late at night, when the house was quiet. She said that the tune came to her in her sleep and that she found herself playing it in the morning before she was well awake. I spoke to her guardian, Mr. Rennick, upon the subject, and he forbade the lessons at once. He is a hard, practical man of business, and he would hear nothing of humming strangers or of tunes that came in the night. He paid me what was due and dismissed me from the house.”

“And yet you have come to me.”

“Because, Doctor, I cannot put the thing out of my head.” He opened the portfolio and drew from it a sheet of music-paper, closely written. “I have here the notes of the melodies as nearly as I could set them down after each lesson. There are five of them, and they are all different, yet they have about them a certain family likeness which I cannot explain. I am a musician, sir, and I tell you that these are not the vagaries of a schoolgirl. There is a design in them—a design which I am too dull to read, but which I am certain is there. I have brought them to you because I am told that you have a genius for such puzzles, and because I am uneasy about the child.”

I took the paper from his hand and studied it for some minutes. The notation was neat and professional, and the melodies, as I ran my eye over them, had indeed a curious quality of resemblance, though I could not have said in what the resemblance consisted. They were simple airs, such as a man might hum under his breath, and yet there was in them a certain deliberate progression which suggested something more than idle song.

“You have shown these to no one else?” I asked.

“To no one. I thought it best to keep the matter quiet.”

“You have done wisely. And you can tell me nothing of the stranger?”

“Nothing, save what the girl has said. But there is one thing more, Doctor, which I had nearly forgotten, and which may be of importance. Upon the last occasion that I saw Miss Ashcombe—the day before her guardian dismissed me—she told me that the humming had changed. It was no longer a single air, she said, but two airs, one following the other, and the second always the same. She was much agitated when she spoke of it, and she begged me not to repeat what she had said, for fear, I think, that her guardian should be angry.”
