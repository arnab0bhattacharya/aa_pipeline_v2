The house stood in a narrow street that climbed away from the harbour, one of a row of dwellings whose grey stone fronts seemed to have absorbed the perpetual drizzle of that coast until they were indistinguishable from the sky itself. I had been summoned by a letter—a thing of formal, angular characters, in a hand unused to correspondence—and I went because I could not conceive of a reason for refusing, though I had spent the intervening days in a state of considerable uneasiness. The widow of Captain Elias Morgan received me in a parlour that smelled of coal-smoke and the sea, a room whose few ornaments—a shell, a brass telescope, a framed lithograph of some foreign port—spoke of a life conducted elsewhere. She was a small woman, dressed in black that had begun to show the wear of daily use, and her face, which must once have been handsome, had the pinched, watchful quality of one who has learned to expect very little from the world.

“You were with him at the end,” she said. It was not a question. Her voice was low and careful, as though she were handling something fragile.

I said that I had been.

“Sit down,” she said. “I have wanted to speak with someone who was there. They tell you nothing, you know—the Company, the officials. They send a paper with a seal upon it, and the paper says that the vessel was lost with all hands, as if that were an explanation.” She paused, and her hands, which had been folded in her lap, tightened upon each other. “I have imagined it a hundred times,” she went on. “I have lain awake at night and seen it—the darkness, the water, the men crying out. And I have thought of him—of my husband—standing upon the bridge, as he would have stood, giving orders to the last.” She looked at me directly. “I want you to tell me how it was. I want to hear it from one who saw.”

The fire in the grate settled with a small, dry sound. Outside, in the street, a cart went by, its wheels grinding upon the wet cobbles, and the sound receded and was gone, and the silence that followed seemed to me immense and terrible. I was a boy then—seventeen at most—and I had come through that night with the numbness of one who has seen too much, but I had not yet learned the language in which such things may be told. I had not learned, either, the language of evasion. I sat before her with my cap in my hands, turning it round and round, and I felt the weight of what I knew pressing upon me like a physical thing, a stone lodged in the chest.

What could I tell her? That the captain had been drunk? That he had been drunk for three days, and that the ship had been navigated by a mate who was himself hardly fit for command? That when the squall struck—a squall that any seaman worth his salt might have seen coming—the captain had been below, insensible, and had been dragged upon deck by two men who could scarcely stand themselves? That when the water came over the rail and the vessel began to settle, he had clung to the wheel-box and wept, and begged to be saved, and called upon God and upon his mother, who had been dead these thirty years? That he had not gone down with his ship, but had been washed away screaming, like a rat from a sinking basket? That I, a boy, had seen him thus, and had lived, and that the memory of it had made me old before my time?

She was waiting. Her eyes, fixed upon my face, held a terrible patience. I understood in that moment that she had constructed for herself a certain image—the image of a man who had met his end with dignity, who had done his duty as he saw it, who had gone into the darkness with his eyes open and his soul prepared. It was not, perhaps, a true image. But it was the image she required, the image she had earned through years of solitude and anxiety, through the long nights of watching and waiting, through the endless small sacrifices of a woman married to the sea. And I—what was I, to shatter it? What right had I to take from her the one thing that made her grief bearable?

And yet—and this is the thing that troubles me still—was it not her right to know? Was not the truth, however bitter, a kind of debt that I owed her? She had asked me, and I had come, and I sat before her with the words in my mouth, and I did not know whether I was a coward or a gentleman.

“It was dark,” I began, and my voice sounded strange to me, thin and uncertain. “There was no moon. The wind had been rising since the afternoon, and by nightfall the sea was very heavy. The ship laboured. We had had some difficulty with the cargo—it had shifted in the hold—and the captain had been much occupied.”

She nodded. “He was always careful of his cargo,” she said. “He would never trust the stowage to others. He said it was a matter of conscience.”

“Yes,” I said. “He was careful.” And I thought of the empty bottles in his cabin, the smell of spirits that clung to him like a garment, the way his hands had trembled when he took the wheel. “He was very careful.”

I went on. I spoke of the squall, of the suddenness with which it had come upon us, of the confusion that had followed. I said that the captain had been below, attending to his duties, and that he had come upon deck as soon as he was aware of the danger. I said that he had done what he could.
