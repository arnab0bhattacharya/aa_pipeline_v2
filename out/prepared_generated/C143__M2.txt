The bell of the *Hotel Aeternum* did not chime for the morning delivery; it was merely a heavy, wooden thud against the service door at the back of the Strand. Elara Vance, owner of *Vance & Sons Floral Emporium* in Covent Garden, wiped her hands on a burlap apron stained with the green juice of crushed stems. She watched the porter, a man she had never seen before in her six years of supplying the hotel, take the crate from her errand boy, Pip. The porter’s face was pale, obscured by a high-collared coat, and his eyes held a flat, terrifying vacancy. He did not ask for a signature. He did not look at the invoice. He simply took the white lilies—three stems, fresh-cut, wrapped in brown paper—and vanished into the shadowed corridor leading to the guest wing.

Pip returned to the shop, his breath hitching in the cool London air. “He didn’t take the rest, Miss Vance,” he said, his voice trembling. “Just the lilies. And he didn’t look at me. He looked *through* me.”

Elara frowned, checking her ledger. The *Hotel Aeternum* was a grand establishment, a monument to Victorian excess with its gilded balconies and velvet drapes. It was also, notoriously, meticulous about its accounts. The guest requesting the lilies was listed as Room 404. Elara pulled the register from her shelf, flipping the yellowed pages until she found the date. Room 404 was marked ‘Vacant’ in the hotel’s own log, a fact she had verified twice with the night manager. Yet, for the last three weeks, a fresh bouquet had been delivered to that room every morning at precisely eight o’clock. No one else was there. No one, it seemed, could see the recipient.

“Who was he, Pip?” Elara asked, her voice low. “The porter?”

“I don’t know, Miss. He wore a grey coat, but the fabric looked old, frayed at the cuffs. And he smelled of wet earth and iron.”

Elara locked the shop early. The streets of Covent Garden were thick with the murmur of merchants closing up and the clatter of horse hooves on cobblestones. The fog was rolling in from the Thames, a grey shroud that swallowed the gas lamps and made the shadows stretch long and distorted. She needed answers, but the hotel’s management would only offer polite deflections and vague references to private reservations. She needed someone who dealt in things that did not fit neatly into ledgers.

She found Mr. Silas Thorne at his office in a narrow townhouse near Charing Cross. Thorne was a private investigator of quiet reputation, known for cases that the police found too bizarre to pursue and too subtle to ignore. His office was a clutter of maps, photographs, and dusty files, the air thick with the scent of pipe tobacco and old paper.

“Miss Vance,” Thorne said, not looking up from the ledger he was balancing. “You look like a woman who has seen a ghost.”

“I have seen a delivery,” Elara said, sliding a folded note across his desk. It was a scrap of paper she had found tucked inside the lily petals. It bore only a single word, written in elegant, faded ink: *Remember.*

Thorne raised an eyebrow. He picked up the note, turning it over in his fingers. “And the room is empty?”

“According to the hotel, yes. But the flowers are gone every morning. Always the same time. Always the same quantity.”

Thorne set the note down. “I will come with you tonight. But be warned, Miss Vance, some doors are locked for a reason.”

They arrived at the *Hotel Aeternum* just as the fog was at its thickest. The hotel stood like a monolith of dark stone, its windows glowing with a warm, inviting amber light that seemed out of place against the chill of the night. Elara felt a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature. They bypassed the main entrance and went through the service door. The corridor leading to the fourth floor was dimly lit, the gas jets flickering in a draft that seemed to come from nowhere.

The air grew colder as they climbed. The carpet, usually thick and red, seemed to fade to a dusty grey. The wallpaper, a pattern of gold vines, appeared to twist and warp in the low light. When they reached Room 404, the door was slightly ajar.

Thorne stopped. “Do you hear that?”

Elara listened. There was a faint, rhythmic sound. A scratch, like a pen on paper, or perhaps a nail on wood. It was slow, deliberate, and infinitely sad.

“Stay here,” Thorne whispered. “I’ll look.”

He pushed the door open. The room was small, sparsely furnished. A single bed, a wardrobe, and a writing desk. On the desk, a candle burned, though the flame was blue and did not flicker. In the center of the room, on a chair, sat a figure. It was a man, dressed in the style of the 1850s, his back to them. He was holding a quill, writing furiously in a book.

Elara took a step forward, her heart pounding. “Who is he?”

The man turned. His face was gaunt, his eyes hollow, but his expression was one of profound exhaustion. He looked at Thorne, then at Elara. He did not speak, but his hand trembled. He pointed to the desk, where a small vase held a single white lily. The flower was wilting, its petals brown and curling.

Thorne moved closer, his hand resting on the small revolver in his pocket. “What are you?” he asked softly.

The man looked at the lily, then at Elara. “A debt,” he said. His voice was raspy, like dry leaves skittering over stone. “I promised her I would bring her flowers. Every day. Until I remembered her name.”

Elara felt a surge of pity. “Who is she?”

The man closed his eyes. “I do not know. That is the punishment. I must give the flower, but I cannot remember the face. I cannot remember the name. I only know the scent of the lily. And the weight of the silence.”
