The candle had guttered low, and I had drawn my chair close to the dying embers, for the night was bitter and the wind came keening off the wolds with a sound like a woman weeping. The house of Thruscross Grange lay silent around me—that heavy, listening silence which is not peace, but the suspension of breath. I had been three months in that place, and had learned to know the sound of its corridors, the creak of its stairs, the particular hush that fell when the master passed. Mr. Halgrave was a man of stone and courtesy; his wife a creature of pale, obedient smiles. They moved through their days like figures in a tableau, and I had thought it mere coldness of temperament—the reserved manner of the county gentry.

I was wrong.

A knock, so soft I might have mistaken it for the settling of a beam, drew my eyes to the door. It opened before I could speak, and Mrs. Halgrave herself stood upon the threshold, wrapped in a shawl of grey wool, her hair unbound and her face as white as the ashes in the grate. She pressed her finger to her lips and entered, closing the door behind her with a care that spoke of long practice in the art of not being heard.

“Miss Aldworth,” she said, and her voice was a whisper strained thin as wire, “I must speak with you, or I shall go mad. I have watched you these many weeks. You are not like the others—you do not flatter, you do not spy. I have seen you look at my husband with those steady eyes of yours, and I have thought: she sees. She knows.”

I rose and would have fetched her a chair, but she caught my wrist. Her fingers were cold as the rain upon the window, and they trembled with a violence she could not master.

“Do not trouble yourself for my comfort. I have no comfort. I have not known comfort since the day I swore before God to love and obey that man.” She laughed then, a sound with no mirth in it, and released me. “Obey! I have obeyed him in all things. I have smiled when he bade me smile, and held my tongue when he bade me hold it, and borne his rages in silence, and his coldness, and his—his cruelties, which leave no mark that the world may see. And now he would have me consent to a thing so base, so vile—” She broke off, pressing her hand to her mouth as though to hold back a cry.

I confess I was moved, and yet I was wary. I had come to Thruscross to teach the children their letters and their catechism, not to be made the confidante of a marriage I could not mend. But I could not turn her from my door. Whatever her errand, she stood before me a woman in extremity, and I had been bred to believe that extremity claims its own rights.

“Sit, madam,” I said, more firmly than I felt. “Sit, and tell me plainly what you would have of me.”

She sank into the chair I had vacated, and drew the shawl tight about her shoulders as though a great cold had entered the room. “My sister,” she began, “my sister Constance—she lives at Farrowby, five miles hence. She is all the kin I have that cares whether I live or die. My husband has forbidden me her company. He says she is a bad influence, a meddler, a woman of no principle. He has forbidden me to write to her. He has forbidden her to cross this threshold. And I—I have submitted, as I have submitted to everything.” Her eyes, which had been fixed upon the fire, lifted suddenly to mine. “But I have written to her. I have written many letters, and I have hidden them, and now I must get them to her, and I have no means. The servants are his creatures—every one of them. The post is opened and read. I am watched, Miss Aldworth. I am watched as a prisoner is watched, and I am as much a prisoner, though I wear silk and dine at his table.”

I stood very still. The wind buffeted the casement, and the fire leapt and sank. “What would you have me do?” I asked.

“Take them,” she said. “Take the letters. Hide them in your basket when you walk to the village. Give them to no one but Constance herself—she will come to the gate of the churchyard on Thursday at dusk, and she will wear a cloak of green. No one will suspect you. You are the governess. You are beneath notice.” She smiled bitterly. “Even my husband, who suspects everyone, does not suspect the governess.”

I looked at her, at the desperate hope that had kindled in her hollow eyes, and I felt the weight of the thing she asked settle upon me like a hand upon my shoulder. To carry letters in secret—to deceive my employer—to make myself the instrument of a wife against her husband. It was not a small thing. It was not a thing I could do lightly, or without counting the cost.

“Madam,” I said, “I am not insensible of your distress. But I am a stranger here, and I am bound by the terms of my position. If I am discovered—”

“You will not be discovered,” she said swiftly. “I have thought of everything. The letters are few—three only. They are sealed and small. You have but to carry them and speak not a word to any living soul. Is it so much to ask? Is it so great a wickedness to show a little mercy to a woman who has none?”
