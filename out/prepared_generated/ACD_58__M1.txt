It was a gusty afternoon in late October, with a thin, driving rain that turned the gutters of the Caledonian Road into miniature torrents. The weather seemed to have entered my rooms along with my visitor, for he stood dripping upon the mat, a tall, stooping man whose black frock-coat shone with damp and whose face was the colour of unbleached wax. He held his hat in his hands, turning the brim with a nervous, repetitive motion that betrayed an agitation far beyond the ordinary.

“You are Mr. Faversham?” he asked, his voice a dry rustle.

“The same. And you are Mr. Silas Croft, of Croft and Son, Morticians, if I am not mistaken.”

His eyes, pale and watery, widened. “You know me, sir?”

“I know your establishment by reputation, and the buttonhole of your coat bears the faintest ghost of a lily’s pollen, though the flower itself is long since withered. Pray, take a seat and tell me what brings you here in such weather.”

He sank into the armchair with a sigh that seemed to come from the soles of his boots. “It is a matter of a coffin, sir. And a grave. And a family who will not listen to reason.” He paused, gathering himself. “Yesterday, we interred a Mr. Alistair Finch, a retired provision merchant of some means. The family—a widow and her son, a Mr. Edmund Finch—wished the matter concluded with all haste. The service was brief, the mourners few, and the whole affair had a… a hurried air about it.”

“This is not uncommon in cases of sudden bereavement,” I remarked.

“Indeed. But there was one detail that struck me as profoundly wrong. When my men and I lifted the coffin to place it on the bier, the weight of it was… monstrous. Mr. Finch was a man of slight build, no more than nine stone, even allowing for his final illness. This coffin, with the casket, the shroud, and the man himself, weighed nearer to eighteen stone. I have handled a thousand such cases, Mr. Faversham. I know the feel of a man in a box. This was not a man alone.”

I leaned forward, my interest sharpening. “Did you voice your concern?”

“I did. I took the son aside, as delicately as I could. I said, ‘Mr. Finch, sir, forgive me, but the weight is considerable. Are there… mementoes placed within?’ He grew quite white and told me it was none of my affair, that his father was a large man in spirit if not in body. I then offered, as is my custom, to arrange a final viewing for the family before the interment. It is a small courtesy, you understand.”

“And his reply?”

“He refused. Quite vehemently. Said there had been suffering enough, that they wished to remember the deceased as he was in life. The widow, a frail creature in deep black, merely nodded and wrung her hands. They hurried us to the cemetery at St. Jude’s, in Lower Holloway, with a sum of money that was meant, I think, to purchase my silence as much as my services.”

“And the grave?” I asked.

“It is dug, sir, but not yet filled. The gravedigger, a man named Hackett, is an old friend. He knows the feel of earth and the weight of things that go into it. He was to fill it this afternoon, but I have begged him to wait. I told him I had a doubt, a professional doubt. He will hold off until tomorrow morning, no longer.”

It was a curious tale, and my mind began to sift its particulars. The haste, the refusal of a viewing, and above all, the impossible weight. A coffin is a sealed vessel, its contents a mystery to all but those who place them there. What, then, had been added to Mr. Alistair Finch to double the weight of his final journey?

“You did rightly in coming to me, Mr. Croft,” I said, rising. “This is a matter that requires immediate attention. We must go to the cemetery at once.”

The rain had eased to a fine mist by the time our hansom pulled up before the iron gates of St. Jude’s. The cemetery was a place of somber order, its yews dripping and its paths slick with wet leaves. Mr. Croft led me with a certainty of purpose to a fresh mound of earth, stark and raw against the surrounding graves. A simple headstone was already in place: *Alistair Finch, 1832-1894. At Rest.*

A burly man with a shovel leaned against a nearby monument, smoking a short clay pipe. This was Hackett, the gravedigger. He touched his cap to Mr. Croft.

“Kept it open for you, Mr. Croft, as you asked,” he grunted. “Though the superintendent’s been asking questions.”

“We shall not be long,” I assured him. “Mr. Croft, you are certain of the family’s wishes to have no viewing?”

“Certain as I am of my own name, sir.”

“Then our course is clear. We must see for ourselves what weighs so heavily upon the late Mr. Finch.”

Mr. Croft looked aghast. “Sir, I could not! It would be a desecration! A violation of my professional oath!”

“It would be a far greater violation to allow a crime to be concealed within that box,” I said, my voice firm. “If I am wrong, we shall replace the earth, and no one will be the wiser. But if I am right, we may prevent a villain from escaping justice. Hackett, I will double the fee you were promised if you can open that grave and lift the lid of that coffin within the next ten minutes.”

The gravedigger’s eyes gleamed. He knocked out his pipe and set to work with a will. The wet earth came away in heavy clods. Soon, the polished oak of the coffin lid gleamed dully in the fading light. With a crowbar and a combined heave, Hackett and Mr. Croft prised it loose.
