It was a wet afternoon in November when the card of Miss Araminta Vance, of Vance & Crewe, dressmakers of Regent Street, was brought up to our chambers. My friend, Sherlock Holmes, received it with a certain languor, for he was engaged in tracing the history of a Roman coin which had been found in the throat of a fishmonger’s dog; but the name of the firm was known even to me, and he tossed the coin aside with a sigh.

“Vance & Crewe,” he mused, his long fingers steepled beneath his chin. “A house of some reputation, Watson. Their patrons are the pick of the ton. Show the lady in.”

Miss Vance entered with the rustle of silk and the scent of lavender, a woman of perhaps fifty, handsome and composed, though a tremor in her gloved hands betrayed a state of considerable agitation. She was dressed with severe elegance in charcoal grey, and she carried a small morocco-bound book in one hand.

“Mr. Holmes,” she began, her voice low and well-modulated, “I come to you upon a matter which, if it becomes known, will ruin me more surely than a fire or a flood. I am a woman of business, and I have no head for romance or intrigue, but I am forced to believe that I am the victim of a conspiracy.”

Holmes gestured her to a chair. “You have our attention, madam. Pray, be precise.”

“My business is the making of gowns for the highest society in London,” she said. “My clients entrust to me not only their money, but their figures—their most private dimensions. These are recorded in my ledgers, which are kept under lock and key in my office. A week ago, I discovered that my most recent book of measurements had been tampered with. The lock on my cabinet was unforced, but the leaves of the book had been turned and handled by a stranger.”

“A subtle theft,” Holmes remarked, his grey eyes keen with interest. “The book itself was not taken?”

“No, sir. That is what puzzles me. Only the knowledge within it was stolen. And now, the consequence. At the Duchess of Alnwick’s ball on Tuesday, the Countess of Marlbury was robbed of her emerald stomacher. On Thursday, at the Merriweather reception, Mrs. Ferguson-Grant lost a diamond necklace worth some five thousand pounds. In both cases, the jewels were concealed within the bodice of their gowns—in a secret pocket, or stitched behind a panel of lace. The thief knew precisely where to look.”

Holmes sprang to his feet and began to pace the room. “Excellent! The problem is a perfect one. A thief who requires a map of the treasure before he sets out to rob the house. Tell me, Miss Vance, who in your establishment has access to these books?”

“Myself, my head cutter, a Mr. Silas Croft, and my two senior fitters, the Misses Pettigrew. They are all of long service and, I would have sworn, unimpeachable honesty.”

“And the balls themselves? Who attends such functions? It is a closed world.”

“Quite so. Only the guests, their servants, and the hired staff of the house.”

“Then our thief is a guest, or one who passes for one,” said Holmes. “He consults your stolen measurements, notes which lady is to be a walking jewel-box, and then attends the very function where she is to be seen. It is the work of a man of some nerve and education. Watson, my coat! We shall take a cab to Regent Street.”

Vance & Crewe occupied a handsome establishment with a discreet entrance. The showroom was a symphony in rose and cream, but Miss Vance led us through to the cutting-room at the back. It was here that the work was done, a large, airy space smelling of chalk and cloth, with long tables and dressmaker’s dummies standing like a silent, headless chorus.

Holmes moved about the room with that curious, bird-like energy of his, his eyes missing nothing. He ran a finger along a window-sash, examined the lock of the ledger cabinet, and peered at the floor.

“The thief did not force an entry from the street,” he announced. “And he did not come through the shop. The dust in this room is undisturbed, save for the prints of your workers. Tell me, Miss Vance, who cleans this room?”

“A charwoman, Mrs. Higgins. She comes in the mornings.”

“And she is trustworthy?”

“As I am myself.”

Holmes paused by a large basket of fabric scraps. He poked at it with his cane. “A strange place for a cigarette end,” he remarked, plucking a small, flattened stub from beneath a roll of taffeta. He held it up to the light. “Egyptian. A costly brand. And look here, Watson!” He pointed to the floor near the window, where a tiny splinter of wood, no bigger than a matchstick, lay against the skirting board. It was of a dark, polished mahogany.

Before I could reply, a voice spoke from the doorway. It was a man of about forty, with a pale, fleshy face and a neatly trimmed moustache. He was dressed in a well-cut suit of tweeds and held a pair of shears in one hand.

“Is there some trouble, Miss Vance?” he asked, his eyes resting on Holmes with a look of cool appraisal.

“No trouble, Mr. Croft. This is Mr. Sherlock Holmes, the detective. He is consulting with me on a matter of security.”

The man, Silas Croft, inclined his head. “Indeed. A wise precaution. One cannot be too careful with a client list such as ours.” His gaze was steady, but I noticed that the hand holding the shears was quite motionless, as if carved from stone.

“Mr. Croft is my head cutter,” Miss Vance explained. “He has been with me for fifteen years.”

“An eternity,” said Holmes smoothly. “You must know the secrets of every gown that passes through your hands, Mr. Croft. The placement of every seam, every pocket.”
