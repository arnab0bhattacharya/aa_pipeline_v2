My name is Dorcas Pennywhistle, and I was, at the time of which I write, a governess of twenty-three years, of a retiring disposition and a constitution not formed for the endurance of great terrors. I had come to Paris in the service of the family of the Marquis de Hautefeuille, a nobleman of ancient lineage and a temper so exceedingly combustible that it might have been said to have been laid on in the blood, like a family complaint. We were apprehended at the Barrière du Trône, in the very act of flight, bundled into a cart with our few remaining effects, and conveyed to the prison of the Madelonnettes, a place of such gloom and dismal celebrity that the very stones seemed to sweat a cold and guilty perspiration.

The Marquis, his lady, their two daughters, and the ancient Abbé who had been their confessor since the days of their prosperity, were thrust into a single, noisome chamber, while I, as a person of no consequence whatsoever, was permitted a small adjoining closet, which had once been a scullery, and which contained a tap of cold water and a broken stove. Here, then, was the whole world of the Hautefeuille family reduced to a space not much larger than a respectable hen-coop, and here did they sit in a state of petrified grandeur, the Marquis with his chin upon his hand, the Marquise in a swoon that lasted three days, the daughters in a torrent of tears, and the Abbé in a silence so profound and so cadaverous that I half expected to see the moths fly out of him.

For the first day, they spoke of nothing but the indignity of their situation, the perfidy of the Revolution, and the certain interposition of the Emperor of Austria, who, as the Marquis observed with a bitter laugh, was his second cousin by marriage. On the second day, they began to look about them with a dawning and dreadful comprehension, for the bread that was served was of a blackness and a hardness that would have baffled a mason, and the soup was a thin and greasy water in which a few stray vegetables wandered forlornly, like lost souls in a fog.

It was on the third day that the Marquis, fixing his eye upon me with an expression in which hunger and pride struggled for the mastery, addressed me thus:—

“Mademoiselle Pennywhistle, is it within the compass of your English education to prepare a ragout?”

I replied, with a curtsy that was half a shiver, that I had been accustomed to assist in the kitchen of my father, a country curate, and that I believed I could make a tolerable broth.

“Then,” said he, “for the love of Heaven, and for the sake of my poor wife, who is perishing for want of nourishment, make us a broth.”

The materials at my disposal were a few carrots of a wooden consistency, an onion that had seen better days, and a bone that had been stripped of its meat by some previous and more fortunate tenant of the cell. These I set to simmer in the broken stove, with a pinch of salt that I had concealed in my pocket, and a handful of herbs that I gathered from a cranny in the prison wall, where they grew in a sickly and persecuted fashion. The result was a liquid of a pale and unassuming hue, but of a savour that I am not ashamed to say was wholesome and even comforting.

The Marquis tasted it, and his eyes, which had been dull and fish-like, kindled with a spark of their ancient fire. “Mademoiselle,” said he, “you are a genius. You have the soul of a Vatel in the body of a sparrow.”

From that hour, my position in the prison underwent a change that was as complete as it was unexpected. The Marquise, recovering from her swoon, desired to have her hair dressed, and I, who had never dressed a hair in my life but my own, contrived by some miracle of pins and fingers to arrange it in a fashion that she was pleased to call “not utterly barbarous.” The elder daughter, Mademoiselle Henriette, discovered that her petticoat was torn, and I, with a needle that I had begged from a turnkey and a thread that I had unravelled from my own stocking, mended it so neatly that she declared the rent was invisible, and embraced me with a warmth that I had never before experienced from her.

The Abbé, who had been silent for so long that I had begun to think him dead, at last spoke, and his voice was like the rustling of dry leaves. “Mademoiselle,” said he, “I have a great desire for a cup of tea.”

I had no tea, but I had a handful of dried lime-flowers that I had gathered in the prison yard, and these I steeped in boiling water, and served to him in a cracked cup. He drank it with a solemn satisfaction, and pronounced it to be the finest tea he had tasted since the year 1788.

The days passed, and the Hautefeuille family, who had once been so proud that they would not have accepted a glass of water from a servant without a formal introduction, now came to depend upon me for every comfort of their existence. The Marquis, who had been a connoisseur of sauces, would consult me upon the seasoning of our daily soup. The Marquise, who had been a martinet in matters of etiquette, would ask my opinion upon the arrangement of her hair. The daughters, who had been accustomed to the most delicate of pastries, would beg me to make them a little cake of the coarse prison flour, and would eat it with tears of gratitude.
