Lord Arthur Pomeroy had long enjoyed the somewhat melancholy distinction of being the dullest young man in London. This is, in itself, no small achievement. To be universally recognised as tedious in a society that prides itself on talking brilliantly about nothing requires a certain consistency of character, a steadfast refusal to be either surprising or original. Lord Arthur possessed this negative genius to a remarkable degree. His countenance was flawlessly regular, his hair an unexceptionable brown, and his waistcoats of an admirable but entirely unprovocative sobriety. When he entered a drawing-room, the air of intellectual vitality did not precisely die, but it certainly took to its bed with a slight headache. Women forgot their most cherished scandals when he spoke, and men found their finest epigrams evaporating on their lips, replaced by a sudden and profound interest in the weather.

It was generally agreed that if silence is golden, Lord Arthur was an inexhaustible mine of wealth. He had never made a joke, and consequently had never made an enemy. His life was a flawless blank page in a volume of rather sparkling memoirs. He was, on the whole, considered a thoroughly safe man, which is the worst thing one can say about anybody.

It was at the salon of Lady Brilliana Vance that the catastrophe occurred. Lady Brilliana’s drawing-room in Belgrave Square was the true forum of the English aristocracy. It was there that one went to hear the precise measure of one’s own wit echoed back, finely polished, by one’s peers. The room itself was a masterpiece of aesthetic delicacy, hung with dim-coloured tapestries of pomegranates and peacocks, and filled with the heavy scent of dark carnations. On this particular evening, the chief ornament of the gathering, aside from the hostess herself, was Monsieur Aristide Dufour, a French journalist of profound reputation. Monsieur Dufour had come to London to write a series of articles on the English character, a subject upon which he knew absolutely nothing, which is the essential prerequisite for all successful foreign correspondence.

"I find your English aristocracy charming," Monsieur Dufour was saying, his eyes wandering restlessly over the assembled company. "They possess the art of saying nothing with an air of tremendous conviction. But I am told there is one among you who is the absolute master of silence. A Lord Pom-something. They tell me he is the dullest man in London."

A slight, silvery ripple of laughter went around the room, and all eyes turned, with a delightful cruelty, to the doorway, where Lord Arthur Pomeroy had just entered. He stood there, looking mildly apologetic, as if he were an unpaid bill that had unexpectedly arrived during dinner.

Lord Arthur advanced into the room, his face bearing its usual expression of mild, uncomprehending politeness. He had not heard the Frenchman’s remark, but he felt the familiar weight of the room’s collective indifference. He stopped near a table where a magnificent cluster of yellow tea-roses stood in a porcelain vase.

"What a great many petals these roses have," Lord Arthur observed, with an air of profound meditation. "I suppose it is because they have nothing else to do."

He had intended merely a statement of botanical fact. He had once read that plants in idle soil produce more foliage. But to his utter astonishment, Monsieur Dufour leaned forward, his eyes suddenly blazing with interest.

"Ah!" cried the Frenchman, striking his hand lightly upon his knee. "Magnificent! What an exquisite paradox! The idle aristocracy produces idle beauty! You are, then, the famous wit they have hidden from me. You are Lord Pomeroy!"

Lord Arthur blinked twice. "I am Lord Arthur," he said, mildly.

"Of course, of course! The master of the epigram!" Monsieur Dufour turned to Lady Brilliana. "You told me he was dull. You English, you are all ironists. You hide your brightest jewels under the bushel of a perfect nonchalance. 'They have nothing else to do.' It is a satire upon our whole decadent century. It is a phrase of a La Rochefoucauld!"

Lady Brilliana, who would have agreed that the moon was made of green cheese if a sufficiently fashionable Frenchman had suggested it, immediately adopted the new opinion. "Yes," she said, turning her lorgnette upon Arthur. "Arthur is always saying those quaint things. I often tell him he is too dangerous for general society."

Lord Arthur, who had never been told anything of the kind, nevertheless felt a strange, warm glow begin to spread through his chest. It was not the warmth of truth, for he had spoken no truth, but it was the entirely intoxicating warmth of applause. He decided that he rather liked being a wit. If all that was required was to state the obvious, it was surely easier than being thought tedious.

He took a step further into the room. "The lamps are quite bright tonight," he ventured, looking up at the crystal chandelier. "I dare say they are trying to outshine the guests."

It was a remark of pure, unadulterated imbecility. Lord Arthur had merely noticed that the chandelier had six bulbs instead of the usual four. But the room, now primed by Monsieur Dufour’s enthusiastic endorsement, immediately burst into a peal of delighted laughter. A young diplomat, who had been trying to think of something clever to say to a celebrated beauty for the last hour, seized upon the opportunity.

"Outshine the guests! Capital!" he cried. "It is a satire on our vanity, is it not, Lady Brilliana? We come here to be seen, and the lamps upstage us entirely."

"I meant," Lord Arthur began, trying to be honest, "that there are more candles than usual."

But this only provoked further laughter. "More candles than usual! A double meaning! He implies we are waxworks, Lady Brilliana! We are mere tallow before the flame of his intellect!" cried the diplomat, who was determined not to be outdone.
