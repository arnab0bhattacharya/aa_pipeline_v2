It was a dark, foggy, choking afternoon in the month of November, when the sap of the street-lamps was just beginning to ooze, and the very houses seemed to have pulled up their collars and buttoned them tightly about their ears, that Mr. Ebenezer Grout, a gentleman of the whip, sat in the cramped and musty parlour of the Crippled Sexton tavern. Mr. Grout was by profession a coachman; a man who had seen a great deal of the road in his time, and rather more of his late master’s temper than was strictly good for his digestion. He was a man of a tightly-buttoned disposition, having drawn himself in, as it were, at the waistcoat, in the firm belief that the world in general was a large, hungry wolf, and that to expose one’s inner feelings was to offer it a prime cut of mutton.

The parlour of the Crippled Sexton was a highly suitable place for such a man. It was low-ceilinged, and smelt very much of old sherry, damp straw, and the fustian of generations of travellers who had no business being there. The smoke from a dozen pipes had so completely blackened the ceiling that it appeared to be made of polished jet, and the solitary window, which looked out upon a dismal yard, was so encrusted with the soot of London that one might have imagined it a piece of mourning-stationery provided by Nature for the death of the sun.

Here sat Mr. Grout, nursing a pint of stout, and opposite him sat a gentleman of the law, Mr. Pumblechook of Symond’s Inn, who had a moist and clammy hand, an eye that wandered like a lost sheep, and a habit of rubbing his chin as if he were trying to strike a light from it. Mr. Pumblechook was engaged in taking down a deposition, and he held his pen as if it were a dagger, which he was prepared to plunge into the breast of Truth at a moment’s notice.

“Now, Mr. Grout,” said Mr. Pumblechook, dipping his pen with a flourish that splattered the tablecloth, “let us have it again, if you please. The accident in the Rue de la Serviette, in Paris. The child. The carriage. You were on the box, you say?”

“I was on the box,” returned Mr. Grout, speaking with the slow, deliberate weight of a man who is acutely aware that every syllable might cost him a shilling. “I was on the box, and the night was as black as the inside of a chimney. Black as this very pot, sir, if you was to take the pot and turn it over your head, which I don’t recommend, for the stout would be wasted, and good stout is a sin to waste, though I cannot say the same for French wine, which is mostly vinegar and conceit.”

“Never mind the French wine, Mr. Grout,” interposed Mr. Pumblechook, who had no interest in viticulture. “The child. Was the child in the road?”

“That is the question,” said Mr. Grout, wiping his mouth. “Was the child in the road, or was the road in the child? A philosophical point, sir. I have driven many a road, and I have seen many a child, but the two do not naturally consort, like roast beef and mustard. They are separate entities. The Marquis—he was a Marquis, or a Count, or a Something-or-other of great consequence, with a nose like the prow of a ship and a temper like a lit fuse—the Marquis was in a tearing hurry. He says to me, ‘Grout,’ he says—in French, of course, which I render for you in English, having no talent for the Gallic tongue, which always sounds to me like a cat in a fit—‘Grout,’ he says, ‘fly!’ And I flew.”

“But the child?” persisted Mr. Pumblechook, his pen scratching like an industrious mouse.

“The child,” said Mr. Grout, and here he paused, and took a long, reflective pull at his stout, as if he were drawing up the secrets of the universe through a straw. “The child was a small thing. Very small. The size of a half-bushel, or thereabouts. It darted out from a doorway, as children will, having no more sense of self-preservation than a moth in a candle factory. I pulled the leaders, sir. I pulled them till the leather spoke, and the leaders they swerved, and the off-leader he stumbled, and the carriage it gave a lurch like a ship in a storm, and the Marquis he swore a most terrible oath, which I would not repeat for a golden guinea, and then—well, then there was a bump.”

“A bump,” repeated Mr. Pumblechook, writing it down.

“A bump,” said Mr. Grout, nodding sagely. “Not a great bump. Not a bump that would signify if you was driving over a turnip. But a bump it was, and the carriage went on, for the Marquis he would not stop. He says, ‘Drive on!’ he says. ‘It is nothing!’ he says. ‘A dog!’ he says. But I knew it was not a dog, sir, for a dog yelps, and this did not yelp. It was silent, sir, as silent as the grave which I make no doubt it soon occupied.”

“And you are certain,” said Mr. Pumblechook, leaning forward with his chin-rubbing intensifying, “that the carriage of the Marquis de la Tour-Blanche struck this child?”

Mr. Grout looked at his stout, then at the ceiling, then at his boots, which were of a venerable and much-mended character, and then, finally, at Mr. Pumblechook.

“I am certain,” said Mr. Grout, with the air of a man who is about to perform a feat of intellectual gymnastics, “that a carriage struck a child. I am certain that I was driving a carriage. I am certain that the Marquis was in that carriage. But whether it was his carriage that struck the child, or the child that struck his carriage, or whether indeed it was a different carriage, a different child, and a different Marquis altogether, on a different night, in a different country—these are things, sir, which the memory, being a frail and treacherous vessel, does not permit me to swear upon a stack of Bibles as tall as this very room.”
