The cottage clung to the headland like a limpet, its stone walls the colour of a storm at sea. Inside, Elara Vance had made the world small, contained within four whitewashed walls and the ceaseless sigh of the tide below. She had been Elara Thorne for less than a year, a name that felt like a borrowed dress, never quite fitting. Now she was a Vance again, the name of her childhood, a name that tasted of salt and sorrow.

Thomas was in the water. It was a simple, brutal fact that she repeated to herself as she mended a torn nettle-damaged shawl or scrubbed the stone flags of her floor. It was a fact as immutable as the granite of the cliffs. He had gone out on a friend’s fishing boat, a rare and foolish venture for a man who knew more of ledgers than of currents. A squall, sudden and vicious, had turned the bay into a churning cauldron. His body had been found two days later, tangled in kelp, his face pale and strange, as if he were merely sleeping. The sea had taken him, and in return, she had come to the sea. It was not a choice, but a gravity, an instinct to be near the thief, to watch its every mood.

She refused the villagers of Portgarrow, their hesitant knocks and offers of stew or company. She sent them away with a quiet, unbending firmness that soon became legend. The widow of the headland, they called her. The woman who speaks to the waves. Let them talk. Words were just noise, the frantic chatter of gulls against the eternal, honest roar of the ocean. She preferred the roar.

The first time she saw the flowers, she thought the wind had torn them from a cliff face and hurled them at her door. A small, tight bundle of sea-thrift and kidney vetch, tied with a piece of dried grass, lay on the worn step. They were the colours of the shore itself, the dusty pink and pale yellow of sand and shell. Elara looked up and down the empty, winding path that led to her cottage. There was no one. She picked them up, their stems cool and damp, and carried them inside, placing them in a chipped jug of water. They were a small, silent intrusion into her hermetic world, and she did not know what to make of it.

The next week, there was another offering. This time, a spray of bright yellow bird’s-foot trefoil and a single, perfect sprig of heather. Again, the path was deserted. Elara began to watch, not with expectation, but with a kind of grim curiosity. She would sit in her window, the glass warped and wavy, and scan the path, the gorse, the sky. She saw only the wind, bending the grasses in silver waves.

It was on the third occasion that she saw the girl. She was a wisp of a thing, all sharp angles and long, untidy hair the colour of wet sand. She moved with the silence of a plover, her bare feet making no sound on the path. She laid the small bouquet of sea-pinks down with a reverence that was almost painful to watch, then flitted away, melting back into the landscape as if she were a part of it.

Elara knew who she must be. The fisherman’s daughter. Finn’s girl. She had heard the story, a sad echo of her own. Finn’s wife, Morwen, had been gathering shellfish on the rocks below the old watchtower when a rogue wave had claimed her. It had been three years ago. The girl, whose name Elara did not know, had been twelve.

A strange, unwelcome tenderness pricked at Elara’s heart. She did not want it. She did not want connection, or pity, or the quiet, shared language of loss. She wanted only the cold comfort of her solitude. But the flowers kept coming. A handful of wild thyme, its scent a ghost of a summer day. A cluster of scarlet pimpernel, so bright against the grey stone of her doorstep it looked like a spill of blood. Each one was a small, silent message. *I see you. I know.*

One afternoon, a bitter wind was whipping spray from the waves and rattling the windowpanes. Elara was mending a shirt, her needle moving with a steady, mechanical rhythm. She heard a faint scuff of stone on the path and looked up. The girl was there, her thin shawl pulled tight against the wind, her arms full of gorse and a few late-blooming sea-holly. She saw Elara in the window and froze, her eyes wide and startled, like a fawn caught in a hunter’s gaze. For a long moment, they simply looked at each other, the widow and the girl, separated by a pane of glass and a world of unspoken grief.

Elara’s first instinct was to turn away, to pull the curtain and resume her mending. But she did not. Instead, she raised a hand, a slow, hesitant gesture. The girl did not wave back. She simply lowered her eyes, placed the flowers on the step, and fled.

The next day, Elara did something she had not done in months. She unbolted her door and stepped outside. The wind was still sharp, but the sun was out, glinting on the water. She walked to the edge of the path where the girl had disappeared. Tucked into a crevice of the rock wall, she found a small, smooth piece of driftwood. It was pale and weathered, worn to a shape that was almost, but not quite, a heart.

Elara took it inside. She turned it over and over in her hands, its surface cool and silken. It felt like an answer to a question she had not asked. That evening, she found a small, flat piece of slate and, using a shard of flint, scratched a single word onto its surface: *Thank you.*
