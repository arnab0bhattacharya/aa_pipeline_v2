My name is Mrs. Ada Pemberton, and I keep a boarding-house at Number 9, Lavender Gardens, Clapham—a circumstance I mention not from any vulgar pride, but because a boarding-house is a sort of microcosm, and the landlady of one comes to know more of human nature than many a clergyman. I am a widow, my husband having been in the hardware line and leaving me this house and a sufficiency of furniture but very little else. I let rooms to clerks, principally, young men of sober habits and modest salaries, and I have found them, upon the whole, a most agreeable class of person, being neither so flighty as the shop-assistant nor so consequential as the professional man.

It was in the autumn of the year that I found myself the confidante of no fewer than three of my lodgers, and that the whole fabric of Number 9 was very nearly torn asunder by the passions of young people.

The first to open his heart to me was Mr. Henry Tilling, a thin, earnest young man with a pale face and spectacles, who was a clerk at a firm of solicitors in the City. He had been with me two years and was the quietest lodger I ever had, spending his evenings in the sitting-room with a book, and never giving me a moment’s uneasiness except on the subject of his digestion, which was delicate. One evening he lingered at the table after the others had gone, and after some coughing and fidgeting, he said to me:

“Mrs. Pemberton, I am going to ask you a question which may seem impertinent, but I assure you it is not so intended. What is your opinion of Miss Wren?”

Miss Wren was the typist at the office of Messrs. Harbottle and Fitch, where Mr. Tilling was employed, and she had once or twice come to the house to bring him papers, a neat, brisk young woman with a fringe and a way of looking you straight in the eye.

“I think her a very sensible girl,” said I, “and one who knows her own mind.”

“You do not think her flighty?” he asked, anxiously.

“I think her the reverse of flighty,” I replied.

He was much relieved, and then he told me, with a great many hesitations, that he had conceived an attachment for Miss Wren and was considering whether he might presume to address her. I encouraged him, for I thought it a very suitable match, and for some weeks I had the pleasure of watching his courtship proceed by fits and starts, as it were—a bunch of violets on a Friday, a book of poetry on a Tuesday, and once, I believe, a box of chocolates, though he was a long time in deciding whether this was not too forward.

But I had scarcely grown accustomed to this romance when it was complicated by another. Mr. Alfred Perkins, my lodger on the first floor, a stout, cheerful young man in the office of a tea-merchant, came to me one morning with his face very red, and said:

“Mrs. Pemberton, I want your advice. There is a young lady at the office where Tilling works—Miss Wren—and I have met her at the Choral Society. I am very much taken with her, and I want to know whether you think I should have any chance.”

I was quite taken aback. “But Mr. Tilling is also paying attention to Miss Wren,” I said.

Mr. Perkins looked blank. “Tilling!” he said. “Why, he never told me.”

“That is not surprising,” I replied, “for young men do not usually confide such matters to one another.”

He was much disconcerted, and went away, but he returned in the evening to ask whether I thought it would be dishonourable in him to continue his attentions, and I said that I could not undertake to decide questions of honour, but that I thought Miss Wren would be the best judge of her own affairs. He was not satisfied, and for some days the atmosphere at my table was very constrained, the two young men sitting opposite one another and passing the salt with a studied coldness that was painful to witness.

It was at this juncture that the third lodger, Mr. Reginald Crisp, became involved in the affair. Mr. Crisp was a clerk in the employ of a firm of wholesale druggists, a dapper young man with a waxed moustache and a taste for the theatre, who had always struck me as being a little too fine for his position. He had a way of talking about “the guv’nor” and “the governor’s daughter” that I did not altogether like, and I had noticed that he spent more money than his salary could well allow.

One afternoon in November, two men in plain clothes came to the door and asked for Mr. Crisp. I showed them into the sitting-room, and when Mr. Crisp came down from his room, I heard a low murmur of voices, and then a sharp exclamation from him, and then the sound of the front door closing. I went to the window and saw the three of them walking away towards the Clapham Road, Mr. Crisp in the middle, looking very white and small between the two detectives.

The news was all over the house in an hour. Mr. Crisp, it appeared, had been falsifying the accounts of the wholesale druggists for nearly a year, and had got into the hands of a money-lender, and there was talk of a prosecution. My lodgers were much excited. Mr. Perkins said it was a disgrace to the house, and talked of giving notice. Mr. Tilling said that we should not judge a man until we had heard his defence, and Mr. Perkins retorted that there could be no defence for embezzlement, and the two of them were very near a quarrel.
