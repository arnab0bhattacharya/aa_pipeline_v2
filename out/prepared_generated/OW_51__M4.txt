The tower of Master Ambrose Vane stood at the very crown of the town of Hjelmstad, and it was said—chiefly by Master Vane himself—that the tower had been built by a man who wished to be near to Heaven but nearer still to his money. Thither, each evening, when the lamps of the town were being lit below him like a scattering of small yellow sins, the old miser climbed his winding stair with a candle in one hand and a leathern bag in the other, and there, in a chamber hung with faded arras and smelling of dust and winter, he counted out his gold.

He counted it as a priest counts beads, with a slow and reverent finger, and the coins rang softly on the oak as though they were little bells tolling the hours of his contentment. There were florins of the old southern mint, broad and bright as buttercups; there were ducats that had sailed from islands he had never seen and never wished to see; there were thin silver pieces worn smooth by a hundred greasy thumbs, each of which he loved the better for its weariness, since weariness in a coin is the mark of a long and useful service. To Ambrose Vane the world was a vast and rather untidy place, full of people who were perpetually wanting things, and the only honest answer to them all was a locked door. He had locked his door against charity, against friendship, against the importunities of love and the impertinences of religion, and had found the lock to hold remarkably well.

It was on the eve of Saint Nicholas, when the snow lay thick upon the roofs of Hjelmstad and the wind came down from the mountains with the manners of an uninvited guest, that there came a knocking at his door.

Now, a knock at that door was so rare a thing that Ambrose Vane sat quite still for a moment, his hand suspended above a little pile of ducats, as though the sound had been a mouse in the wainscot. Then it came again, small and persistent, the knock of someone who had no great fist to knock with. He rose, and took his candle, and went down the stair, grumbling at every step, and opened the door the width of a prayer.

In the dark of the doorway stood a child—a girl of perhaps eight winters, with a shawl drawn over her head and her small boots sunk in the snow. Her nose was red with cold, and her breath made little ghosts in the candlelight, and she looked up at him with eyes that were neither bold nor frightened, but simply patient, as though she had been taught by experience that doors are slow to open and that waiting is the common lot of the poor.

“Please, sir,” she said, “my mother’s lamp has gone out, and she is ill, and I have no light to light it with. May I take a little flame from yours?”

Master Vane looked at her for a long moment. He was a man who could compute the value of a thing to the last farthing, and he was computing now—not the value of the flame, for a flame is a thing that costs nothing and is worth nothing, but the value of the precedent. Give a child a flame to-night, and to-morrow she would be wanting a loaf; give her a loaf, and by the week’s end she would be wanting a roof. Charity, he had long ago observed, is a beggar that always comes back with a larger hat.

“A flame,” he said at last, “is a very dangerous thing to give away. It might set the whole town ablaze.”

“It is only a little flame, sir,” said the child. “I will carry it very carefully in the lantern.”

He grunted. Then, because the wind was cold and the child was small and the argument, he felt, had been won by him in any case, he turned and went up the stair again, and came down with a taper lit at his own candle, and held it out to her through the crack of the door.

“There,” he said. “Take it. And see that you do not come knocking again at honest men’s doors in the middle of the night.”

“Thank you, sir,” said the child. She did not say it as one who is grateful, but as one who is merely accurate; and she put the taper into her little lantern, and the flame stood up in it like a small gold saint, and she turned and went away down the hill, her boots making a delicate track in the snow.

Ambrose Vane shut the door and locked it, and went up to his chamber, and sat down to his counting. But the coins did not ring as they had rung before. He counted the florins twice and got two different sums, which had never happened to him in forty years; and when he came to the ducats he found himself looking at the face of the child in the flame of his candle, and the flame of his candle was doing what no flame had ever done before—it was reminding him of something.

He put down the last coin and sat back in his chair, and the wind went moaning about the tower like a beggar at a rich man’s gate. He thought of the child going down the hill with her lantern, and of the mother lying ill in a room where the lamp had gone out, and of the small, patient voice saying, “It is only a little flame, sir.” And then, because he was a miser and had a miser’s memory, he thought of another winter, very long ago, when he had been a child himself, and had knocked at a door in this same town, and had been given a flame by a woman who had not asked him for anything in return, and had gone home with it, and had found his own mother sleeping in a room that was very cold and very dark.
