The rain of that late autumn had a quality of persistence, an obstinate, grey slanting that seemed to wash the colour from the very stones of the city, leaving only the soot-blackened surfaces to gleam dully under the gas-lamps. From the window of their lodging in Bloomsbury, one could see the wet flagstones of the square reflecting the yellow, sickly light in long, distorted smears. It was a scene of profound quietude, the quietude of a city worn out by its own vastness, yet for Nikolai Vasilievich, it was a silence that pressed upon the eardrums, a silence pregnant with the noise of a thousand miles of frozen land, of a train’s relentless, metallic rhythm, of a destiny waiting in the dark.

He sat with his back to the room, to the small fire that spat and hissed in the grate, and to the woman who sat near it, her needlework lying idle in her lap. He was a man of medium height, but with a stillness about him that seemed to lend him stature, a coiled and watchful energy that his English wife, Eleanor, had always mistaken for a profound and thoughtful melancholy. To her, he was a scholar, an exile from some vague, intellectual persecution, a man whose mind lived in the abstract realms of philosophy and history. She loved this gravity in him, this air of a world she could not quite enter. She did not know that the world he inhabited was one of ink-stained manifests, of whispered rendezvous in the fog-bound alleys of the East End, of a faith so absolute it had become a form of damnation.

“You are very far away tonight, Nikolai,” she said at last, her voice soft, yet it cut through the oppressive quiet of the room with the startling clarity of a dropped glass.

He did not turn. His gaze remained fixed on the weeping window-pane, on the way the lamplight fractured and reformed upon it. A distant, mechanical sound, the clanging of a bell from a passing tram, reached them as a faint, mournful tremor. He felt the question as a physical touch, a hand upon his shoulder, pulling him back from a precipice to which his soul was already committed.

“Far away?” he repeated, the words foreign and heavy in his mouth. “Is a man ever far from himself, Eleanor?”

It was a deflecting, formal reply, one that he knew would satisfy her mind for a moment, even as it troubled her heart. She was accustomed to his oblique manner of speech, his tendency to answer a simple question with a moral inquiry. But tonight, the evasion felt different. It was not a philosopher’s turn of phrase; it was a shield.

She rose and came to stand behind his chair, her hand resting lightly on its back. He could feel the warmth of her presence, a warmth that seemed to belong to another order of existence altogether—one of domestic peace, of shared books and quiet evenings, of a life built upon the solid ground of affection. His own life, he knew, was built upon shifting sands, upon a foundation of sacrifice and violent necessity.

“It is not your mind that is distant,” she murmured, her fingers tracing the worn fabric of the chair. “It is as if your spirit has already departed. As if this room, and I, are but a memory to you.”

He felt a profound and weary pity for her, and a sudden, sharp hatred for the man he was compelled to be. He longed to turn and take her hands, to tell her of the great, terrible engine of history into which he was but a single, willing cog. He longs to explain that the peace she offered him was a beautiful, fragile lie, and that he must leave it to do a monstrous thing in the name of a future she could never comprehend. But the words would not form. The discipline of years, the oath that was a cold stone in his breast, forbade it. To speak of it would be to break a spell, to invite weakness, to compromise not only his purpose but her safety.

“It is a matter of… of a journey,” he said, the words coming slowly, as if dragged from the depths. “A long journey I must undertake. It has been decided.”

“A journey? But you have said nothing. When? Where to?” Her voice held a tremor of alarm now. The abstract had become concrete.

“It is necessary,” he said, and the two words contained the whole of his explanation. They were a wall. “There are duties… obligations… that call a man back to his origins.”

He felt her stiffen behind him. The unspoken hung between them, vast and terrifying as the sea he would soon cross. She could feel the presence of that other world, the one that claimed him, a world of harsh duties and unspoken allegiances. She had always known he belonged to it, but she had believed, in the quiet vanity of her love, that she had drawn him from its influence, that she had created a small, sheltered harbour for him. Now she saw that the harbour was merely a temporary anchorage.

“Will you come back?” The question was barely a whisper.

He closed his eyes. The image that rose before him was not of her face, but of a man’s face he had never seen, a man who was an abstraction, a name on a slip of paper, a target. He saw the dark street, the glint of steel, the final, shocking moment. He saw, too, the long, cold exile, the Siberian snows, the face of a betrayed comrade. He saw the end of Nikolai Vasilievich. What would come after, he did not know. Perhaps only silence.

“I will do what is required of me,” he said, his voice hollow. It was the only truth he could offer her. It was the truth of a man who had already died to the world, and who was now merely awaiting the funeral.
