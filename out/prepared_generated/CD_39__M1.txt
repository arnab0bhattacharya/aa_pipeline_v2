The town of Calderbridge, which had been a mere village of green fields and sleeping sheep within the memory of living man, was now a roaring, panting, monstrous creature of brick and iron, which had swallowed up the fields and the sheep, and which puffed forth a perpetual cloud of sooty breath that obscured the sun and powdered the windowsills with a fine black snuff. In the very heart of this roaring creature, in a street called Greave’s Row, stood the ironmongery of Mrs. Sarah Pettigrew, a young widow whose face, though framed by the black weeds of her bereavement, possessed a quiet, enduring beauty, like a pale flower that has somehow survived a hard frost. Her shop was a cavern of metallic wonders: a forest of nails and screws, a glittering array of kettles and pans, a silent army of spades and trowels, all of which seemed to listen, with a clinking, rattling attention, to the stories their mistress told them as she polished and arranged them.

Mrs. Pettigrew’s story was a simple one, and one which she kept to herself with a patient dignity. Her husband, John, a man of gentle hands and a constitution as brittle as old glass, had left her the shop and a mountain of debt that loomed over her like the great dark mill at the end of the street. She worked from dawn until the gas-lamps hissed in their brackets, her small hands, chapped and raw, counting out pennies and shillings, her mind a constant ledger of stock and credit, of hope and anxiety. The townsfolk, a hardy and practical people, respected her industry; they said, ‘There is a woman who does not sit down and cry, but stands up and sells.’ And she did stand, a slender, resolute figure amidst the cold iron of her inheritance.

Into this world of honest, hard-won pennies strode Mr. Josiah Croft, the proprietor of the Calderbridge Cotton Mills, a man whose very footsteps seemed to jingle with the sound of coin. Mr. Croft was not a bad man, as men go in the world of profit and loss; he was, in his own estimation, a builder, an architect of prosperity. His face was a broad, ruddy expanse of confidence, and his hands, soft and white, were accustomed to signing large cheques rather than handling small screws. He saw in Mrs. Pettigrew not a woman to be wooed, but a property to be acquired; not a heart to be won, but a sensible asset to be merged with his own considerable estate.

He began his courtship as he began all his business ventures: with a proposal. One Tuesday afternoon, when the shop was quiet and the rain drummed a soft, monotonous tattoo on the roof, he arrived, shaking the water from his fine umbrella.

‘Mrs. Pettigrew,’ he began, his voice a comfortable rumble, ‘a word, if you please. I have observed your enterprise with considerable admiration. It is, if I may say so, a sound concern, though somewhat under-capitalized.’

Sarah, who was polishing a brass candlestick, looked up, her grey eyes wary. ‘You are very kind, Mr. Croft.’

‘Kindness is a luxury, madam. I deal in realities,’ he said, drawing up a stool and sitting with an air of proprietorship. ‘The reality is this: your business, while plucky, is exposed to the fluctuations of the market. A single bad debt, a season of poor trade, and this admirable venture could be brought to a standstill. It is an inefficient arrangement.’

‘It is my husband’s legacy, sir,’ she said, her voice low but firm.

‘Precisely. And a legacy, like any other asset, must be managed for the greatest return. Now, I propose a merger,’ he continued, warming to his subject. ‘A consolidation of interests. My capital, my influence, and my name—Croft is a name of weight in this town, Mrs. Pettigrew, a name that opens doors and silences doubts—would be brought to bear upon your enterprise. In return, your stock, your premises, and, of course, your own admirable qualities of management and… domesticity… would be brought to mine. It is a proposition of mutual advantage. The sum of the parts would be greater than the whole.’

Sarah set down the candlestick. Her heart, which had begun to beat with a foolish, fluttering hope when he first entered, now felt as heavy as one of her own anvils. She had heard of love as a sudden light, a warming fire, a shared glance across a crowded room. She had never heard it described as a consolidation of interests.

‘You speak of marriage, Mr. Croft?’ she asked, her voice barely a whisper.

‘Marriage, madam, is the most sensible and powerful merger of all,’ he declared, beaming. ‘It combines two estates, secures two futures, and provides a stable foundation for the next generation of shareholders. I have made a calculation,’ he added, taking a small notebook from his waistcoat pocket. ‘The initial outlay would be considerable, of course, to clear your outstanding liabilities, but the long-term projections are most favourable. I see a ten per cent increase in turnover within the first two years, simply through economies of scale.’

He went on, talking of balance sheets and profit margins, of synergies and market share, and as he spoke, the warm, hopeful image of a partner, a companion, a helpmate, that had been forming in Sarah’s mind, began to curdle and turn to cold, hard arithmetic. His words were like the relentless clatter of the looms in his mill, a mechanical, soulless sound that drowned out all gentler music. He did not speak of companionship, but of cohabitation; not of affection, but of advantage; not of a shared life, but of a merged enterprise.

When he had finished, he looked at her expectantly, as a man looks at a clerk who has been given a set of figures to tally.
