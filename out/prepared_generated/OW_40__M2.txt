The wager was struck over the second bottle of champagne, in the dim, opium-scented gloom of the Blue Room at the Carlton, where the air hung heavy with the scent of tuberose and the fumes of expensive vanity. Lord Julian Vane, a young man of such exquisite symmetry that one might have mistaken him for a piece of Neoclassical sculpture had he not been wearing a slightly disheveled waistcoat, leaned back in his velvet chair and smiled with the serene detachment of a cat watching a mouse.

“I am bored,” Julian announced, his voice a soft baritone that seemed to vibrate through the silk cushions. “I am bored of the weather, I am bored of the politics, and I am profoundly bored of the fact that I have never been in love. It is the one social obligation I have yet to fulfill, and I suspect I shall find it as tedious as paying one’s taxes.”

His friend, the Honourable Arthur Pendennis, a man of considerable girth and even greater credulity, raised his glass. “You have never been in love, Julian? That is impossible. You are the sort of man who makes women faint with a mere glance. You are too beautiful to be unloved; it is a moral hazard to the public.”

“Beauty is a form of tyranny, Arthur,” Julian replied, “and tyranny is rarely reciprocated. I am merely admired, which is a very safe distance. But I am willing to bet you five hundred pounds that I can make Miss Elara Vance fall in love with me within thirty days.”

Arthur blinked. The room seemed to hold its breath. Miss Elara Vance was known in London society as the Ice Queen of Kensington. She was a woman of severe asceticism, dressed in shades of grey and charcoal, who possessed a mind so sharp it had cut off more than one suitor’s tongue. She was famous for her coldness, her silence, and her habit of looking through people as if they were transparent glass. To make her love him was not just a social conquest; it was a meteorological impossibility.

“Elara Vance?” Arthur squeaked. “Julian, that woman once told a duke he had the emotional range of a frozen pond. You cannot melt ice with a smile. It requires fire, and you, my dear boy, are made of frost.”

“Fire is vulgar,” Julian said, standing up with a fluid grace. “I shall use sympathy. I shall use art. I shall use the one thing that Elara Vance cannot resist: the perfect, detached observation of her own soul. I shall be her mirror. I shall win the bet, Arthur. And when I do, you will buy me a new opera cloak. A very expensive one.”

Arthur, who had never been good at refusing a challenge that involved money he could not afford to lose, agreed.

The thirty days passed with the speed of a well-choreographed dance. Julian did not court Elara; he curated her. He attended her lectures on Byzantine mosaics, not as a student, but as a critic who found her interpretations "aesthetically stimulating but spiritually dry." He sent her no flowers, but rare, extinct books on the history of color. He spoke to her of the tragedy of Sappho, not with passion, but with the dry, academic detachment of a man discussing a particularly difficult tax law.

Elara, who was used to men who wanted her body or her money, found in Julian a strange, refreshing vacuum. He wanted nothing from her but her company, and he treated her company as one treats a piece of fine porcelain: with caution, with admiration, and with the absolute certainty that it might break.

On the thirtieth day, they were walking in the gardens of St. James’s Park. The autumn leaves were turning the color of old gold and dried blood. Elara was wearing a dress of deep emerald velvet, a color that Julian had suggested she wear, claiming it would do justice to the green in her eyes. It was a small, calculated stroke, but it had worked.

“You are strange, Julian,” Elara said. Her voice was no longer the flat, brittle tone of their first meeting. It had softened, gaining a warmth that surprised her.

“I am merely honest,” Julian replied, stopping by the railings that looked out over the lake. “I am honest because I am bored of lies. And I am bored of you, Elara. I have been bored of you for three days. You are predictable. You are safe. You are... boring.”

Elara stopped walking. The wind rustled through the bare branches, a sound like dry paper tearing. She looked at him, and for the first time, the ice in her eyes cracked. “You are joking,” she whispered. “You cannot be bored of me. I have given you everything I have. My time, my mind, my affection. I am in love with you, Julian. I have been in love with you since the day you told me that my poetry was technically proficient but emotionally vacant.”

Julian turned to look at her. He smiled, but it was the smile of a man who has successfully completed a difficult puzzle and is now putting the pieces back in the box. “My dear Elara, I won the bet. I made you fall in love with me. It was a triumph of intellect over instinct. But you are right. I am bored. It was a game, and the game is over.”

He bowed, a gesture of perfect, devastating courtesy, and walked away, leaving Elara standing alone by the lake, her heart aching with a pain that was both exquisite and terrible.

Julian returned to the Blue Room. Arthur was waiting, a glass of brandy in his hand, his face pale with anxiety.

“Well?” Arthur asked. “Did you do it? Is she...?”

“She is in love with me,” Julian said, taking the brandy. “It is a very nice trick, isn’t it? To take a woman who is as cold as a marble statue and make her warm? It is the ultimate aesthetic achievement. To turn the soul into a canvas and paint upon it whatever one pleases.”
