It was with a feeling of strange and painful disquietude that I first beheld the monument of my own humiliation, raised in stone and mortar to the glory of my enemy. The new school of Marthwaite, a grand edifice of imposing Gothic design, stood where the old mill-yard had been, its pointed windows glittering in the grey Lancashire light like so many watchful, accusing eyes. Its patron, Mr. Josiah Croft, had risen from the dust of this very town to become its most celebrated benefactor; and I, Arthur Penhaligon, grandson of the man whose ruin had been the first and firmest foundation of that rise, had been invited to stand before the assembled populace and offer up the inaugural address. The irony was a bitter draught, which I was commanded to swallow with a smile of public gratitude.

As I stood upon the temporary platform, facing the multitude of faces—some ruddy with contentment, others pinched with the familiar poverty of the district—I felt the weight of a history known only to myself. Before me sat the great man himself, Josiah Croft, his countenance a benevolent mask of civic pride, his hands, now soft and uncalloused, resting upon his considerable paunch. He looked upon me with an expression of patronising warmth, as if I were a promising sapling he had graciously agreed to shelter. He could not know, or perhaps he had long since forgotten, that the very ground upon which we stood was hallowed by the memory of my grandfather’s betrayal. It was in that old yard, now paved over and forgotten, that my grandfather, Elias Penhaligon, had taken in a destitute and friendless youth, and that same youth, in the dead of night, had absconded with the patterns for the new steam-loom, the invention that was to make Elias a king of industry, and which instead made Croft a man of fortune.

The applause that greeted my name was a thunderclap that jolted me from my reverie. I stepped forward, the prepared speech, a hollow litany of praise for education and progress, feeling like ashes in my mouth. I began to speak, my voice sounding thin and alien in the vast open air. I spoke of the blessings of knowledge, of the duty of the fortunate to uplift the unfortunate, and with every measured, eloquent phrase, I felt the ghost of my grandfather at my shoulder, his silent, anguished cry echoing in my soul. How could I extol the virtue of a man whose every success was a monument to his own deceit? How could I stand in the very place of the theft and laud the thief?

My eyes met Croft’s. He smiled, a slow, triumphant curve of the lips, and in that moment, I saw not the benevolent philanthropist, but the cunning, desperate boy he had once been. A tempest of emotion, long suppressed, began to rise within my breast. The carefully constructed sentences of my oration faltered; the platitudes died upon my tongue. A greater, more terrible truth was demanding utterance. I looked from Croft’s face to the hopeful, trusting faces of the children who would be educated within those walls, children who were being taught to revere a man who had built his world upon a foundation of stolen honour.

“My friends,” I cried, my voice now ringing with a passion that was not my own, but that of my martyred ancestor, “we are here to celebrate the sowing of a seed—the seed of learning! But let us not forget that a seed, to flourish, must be planted in soil that is honest and true. What of the soil from which this great fortune springs? What of the labour, the genius, the very life’s blood that was torn from another to enrich this one?” A murmur ran through the crowd. I saw Croft’s smile vanish, his face turning a ghastly, mottled white. His eyes widened, not with benevolence, but with a sudden, primal fear. He knew. He knew at last that the grandson of Elias Penhaligon had not come to praise him, but to call his spirit from the grave.

I abandoned my speech entirely. The sublime fury of justice possessed me. I did not speak of facts, of dates or of legalities, but of the sacred bond between a man and his word, between a benefactor and his charge. I painted a picture of a good man, ruined and broken, not by fate, but by the serpent he had warmed in his bosom. I spoke of a legacy of poverty and despair that had flowed down through the generations, a direct consequence of that one, cowardly act. The wind began to rise, whipping my words across the square, and a dark cloud scudded across the sun, casting the grand new school into a sudden, dramatic shadow. It was as if nature itself bore witness to my lament.

When at last I fell silent, my chest heaving, a profound and terrible stillness had fallen upon the assembly. All eyes were fixed upon Josiah Croft. He did not speak. He could not. He merely rose to his feet, his grand frame seeming to shrink and wither before their gaze, and without a word, he turned and fled, pushing his way through the silent, parted crowd like a man pursued by furies. The great benefactor, the pride of Marthwaite, was unmasked; and I, who had come a humble schoolmaster, stood alone upon the platform, the author of his ruin, and the inheritor of my grandfather’s righteous, yet desolate, revenge. The storm broke then, and the cold rain fell upon us all, washing the dust from the new stones, but it could not cleanse the stain of the past that now lay exposed for all the world to see.
