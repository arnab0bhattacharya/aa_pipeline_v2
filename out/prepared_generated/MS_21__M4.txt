The rain, which had fallen without intermission since morning, beat against the windows of my study with a melancholy persistence, and the wind, sighing through the chimney, seemed to voice the disquiet of my soul. I had been sitting for some hours over my ledgers, but the figures swam before my eyes, and my thoughts would not be chained to the columns of profit and loss. It was ever thus upon the anniversary of that day—the day when my testimony, delivered with a firmness that I had mistaken for righteousness, had consigned my partner, the unfortunate Mr. Ellsworth, to a felon's cell. Twenty years had passed, yet the memory of that hour clung to me with the tenacity of a remorse that no prosperity could stifle. I had built my fortune upon the ruin of his, and though the world esteemed me a man of probity and honour, I knew myself to be the architect of a monstrous iniquity. The fraud for which he suffered had been mine; the forged signature, the misappropriated funds—all were the work of my own hand, and the guilt, which should have been mine, I had cast upon him with a lie so plausible that even he, in his bewilderment, had seemed to doubt his own innocence.

A servant entered to announce a visitor, and I started from my reverie with a confusion that I strove in vain to conceal. "A young woman, sir," he said, "who desires to speak with you on a matter of employment. She gave her name as Miss Ellsworth."

The name smote me like a physical blow. Ellsworth! Could it be that the daughter of the man I had destroyed stood even now within my gates, seeking succour at the hands of her father's destroyer? My heart beat violently; a thousand apprehensions crowded upon me. Did she come in ignorance, or in knowledge? Was this the beginning of a retribution long delayed? I commanded the servant to admit her, and in a few moments a young woman entered the room.

She was of a slender figure, clad in mourning garments of a plainness that spoke of narrow means, and her countenance, though pale and touched with sorrow, possessed a beauty that was rather spiritual than earthly. Her eyes, of a deep and pensive blue, seemed to hold within them the shadow of a grief too profound for tears, and her brow, fair and high, was marked with the delicate tracery of care. She stood before me with a modest yet unwavering demeanour, and when she spoke, her voice, though low, was clear and musical.

"Sir," she said, "I crave your pardon for this intrusion. I am informed that you stand in need of a secretary, and I have come to offer my services. My name is Caroline Ellsworth, and I am the daughter of one who was once a merchant in this city."

I strove to compose my features, to still the tempest that raged within my breast. "Miss Ellsworth," I replied, "your name is not unknown to me. I was acquainted, in former years, with your father. I trust he is well?"

A shade of deeper sorrow passed over her face. "My father, sir, is dead. He died in prison, broken in spirit and in health, a few years after his conviction. I was but a child when he was taken from us, yet I remember him well—his kindness, his gentleness, his love for me. He protested his innocence to the last, and I have never doubted him. But the world was cruel, and we were left destitute. My mother did not long survive him, and I have since earned my bread by such means as a woman may—by teaching, by copying, by any honest labour that came to my hand. I have heard that you are a man of business, and that you require assistance in your correspondence. I am proficient in accounts and in the writing of letters, and I would serve you faithfully."

As she spoke, I felt the full weight of my iniquity press upon me with a force that I had never before experienced. Here was the child of my victim, standing before me in her poverty and her orphanhood, offering me her labour, while I, the author of her misfortunes, sat in ease and affluence. The contrast was insupportable. I could not look upon her without a sense of guilt that seemed to scorch my very soul. Yet I dared not confess; I dared not reveal the truth that would have overwhelmed her with horror and loathing. I was a coward, and I knew it.

"You are aware," I said, with a voice that I struggled to keep steady, "that your father's name is associated with a great crime? That he was convicted of fraud, and that his reputation—"

"I am aware," she interrupted, with a dignity that silenced me, "of what the world believes. But I am also aware that my father was a just and honourable man, and that he was the victim of a conspiracy so dark that even he could not penetrate it. He told me, before he died, that he had been betrayed by one whom he trusted as a brother, and that the true author of the fraud was one who walked abroad in honour while he lay in chains. He did not know the name of his betrayer; but he knew that God would one day bring the truth to light."

A cold sweat broke out upon my forehead. I felt as though the very walls of my house were closing in upon me, as though the avenging angel of the Lord stood at my shoulder. I rose from my chair and walked to the window, turning my back upon her, that she might not see the agony that convulsed my features. The rain still beat against the glass, and the wind moaned in the chimney, and I thought of the storm that raged within my own breast—a storm of fear, of shame, of a remorse that was now become a torture.
