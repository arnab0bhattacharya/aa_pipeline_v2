The morning had been oppressively warm and still, one of those close, airless days that seem to press upon the skin like a damp cloth, and the tea room in the High Street of Cranbrook had done a tolerable trade in ices and cool drinks. I was behind the counter, idly polishing a glass and reflecting upon the extraordinary dullness of the hour, when the first tremor came. It was not a violent shock, but a curious, sickening vibration that ran up through the floorboards and set the cups chattering upon their hooks. I had scarcely time to remark upon it to Mrs. Pemberton, who was arranging a tray of seed cake at the far end of the shop, when the second and infinitely more terrible convulsion seized the building.

The sound was like nothing I had ever heard—a deep, groaning roar that seemed to come from the very bowels of the earth, mingled with the shriek of rending timber and the crash of falling masonry. The world tilted. I have a confused memory of the ceiling dissolving into a white cloud of plaster, of the great bow window bulging outward and then collapsing in a glittering cascade, and of being hurled forward with a force that drove the breath from my body. Then something struck me a glancing blow upon the head, and for a space I knew nothing at all.

When I recovered my senses, I found myself lying upon my side in a darkness that was almost absolute, save for a thin, dusty shaft of daylight that pierced the gloom some yards away. The air was thick with plaster dust and the pungent smell of spilled paraffin, and a profound silence reigned, broken only by a faint, persistent trickling of debris. I attempted to move and discovered that my legs were pinned beneath a heavy weight, which upon investigation proved to be a portion of the counter, now sadly dismembered. My head ached abominably, and my left arm was stiff with blood from a cut upon the scalp.

“Is anyone there?” I called out, my voice sounding strange and muffled in that confined space. “Mrs. Pemberton? Are you hurt?”

For a moment there was no reply, and a cold fear gripped my heart. Then, from somewhere to my right, came a small, tremulous sound—half a sob, half a gasp.

“Oh! Oh, merciful heaven! I am killed! I am sure I am killed!”

It was a woman’s voice, high and refined, and it held a note of hysteria that I recognised at once as belonging to Miss Adelaide Frobisher, a young lady of considerable pretensions who had come into the tea room not half an hour before the catastrophe, to wait, as she had informed me with some condescension, for her father’s carriage. She was, I knew, the daughter of a retired solicitor who had made his fortune in the City, and she was accustomed to a degree of attention and comfort that had ill prepared her for the present circumstances.

“Miss Frobisher!” I cried out, with as much cheerfulness as I could muster. “It is I, Thomas Witney. Pray do not distress yourself. We are alive, at any rate, and that is something to be thankful for.”

“Alive!” she repeated, in a voice that suggested she found the condition scarcely preferable to the alternative. “I am pinned down! There is something upon my legs! I cannot move! Oh, this is too dreadful!”

I set myself to extricate my own limbs with what expedition I could manage. The counter, fortunately, had been but lightly constructed, and by dint of much wriggling and no small expenditure of effort, I succeeded in drawing myself free. I stood up, or rather crouched, for the ceiling was now but a few feet above my head, and made my way towards the sound of her voice, my hands outstretched before me in the darkness. I found her presently, half-buried beneath a heap of broken lath and plaster, her face, when I could discern it in the gloom, pale as paper and streaked with tears and dust.

“There now, miss,” I said, adopting the soothing tone I reserved for difficult customers. “We shall have you out in a trice. It is only a bit of the ceiling, and light as a feather.”

I set to work with my hands, tearing away the debris and casting it aside. It was, in truth, a labour of some difficulty, for the space was cramped and the air was foul, but after some minutes I had cleared enough to enable her to draw her legs free. She sat up, trembling violently, and clutched at my arm with a grip that was surprisingly strong.

“We must get out,” she said, her voice now sharp with a new and more practical terror. “We shall be buried alive! We shall suffocate!”

“We shall do nothing of the sort,” I replied, though in truth I was by no means so confident as I sounded. “There is a way out yonder, where the light comes through. It is but a matter of digging.”

The task that confronted us was formidable. The collapse had been thorough, and the pile of rubble that separated us from the street was considerable. I began to work at it with my hands, clawing at the broken bricks and mortar, but I soon saw that my efforts were but a flea’s attack upon a mountain. I cast about for a tool and discovered, by good fortune, a broken leg of the marble table that had once stood in the window. Armed with this, I commenced a more systematic assault upon the barrier, prising away the larger fragments and burrowing like a rabbit.

Miss Frobisher, after a period of stunned inaction, surprised me considerably. She rose to her feet, or as near to her feet as the low ceiling would permit, and began to assist me, dragging away the smaller pieces of rubble and piling them behind us. Her hands, which I had noted earlier as being of a delicate and useless whiteness, were soon grimed with dirt and blood from a dozen small cuts, but she made no complaint.
