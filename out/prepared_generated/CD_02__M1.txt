Mr. Timothy Wrigglesworth was a clerk. He was not a clerk of the brilliant, or the aspiring, or the well-to-do sort; he was a clerk of the steady, the plodding, the seven-and-sixpence-a-week-and-be-thankful-for-it sort. He inhabited a garret in Bumbledom Court, a narrow fissure of a place squeezed between two great commercial houses in the City of London, where the sun, when it condescended to visit at all, did so for a few grudging moments at noon, as if checking its ledgers before moving on to more profitable districts. His world was the world of ledgers and inkwells, of carbon paper and quills, of the great, grimy, thundering machinery of commerce, which he served as a single, insignificant cog. His relations, however, were a machinery of a different kind—a relentless, grasping engine of familial obligation, of which he was the sole and perpetually exhausted fuel.

There was his uncle, Mr. Silas Wrigglesworth, a man whose face seemed permanently compressed in a fiscal vice of his own making; his aunt, Mrs. Prudence Gable, who had been named, with a fine irony, for a virtue she had never possessed; and his cousin, the dashing but perpetually distressed Mr. Barnaby Finch, a young gentleman whose chief occupation was the artful generation of small, pressing debts. This triumvirate of need had, for years, regarded Timothy’s modest stipend as a common purse, to be dipped into at will for the repair of a leaking roof, the settlement of a tailor’s bill, or the urgent purchase of a ticket to Brighton for the recovery of a dubious health. Timothy, a man whose heart was as soft as his head was plain, had never known how to say them nay.

It was on a murky Thursday evening, with the gas-lamps sputtering like tired fireflies in the fog, that the bolt of fortune struck. He had, in a moment of unwonted extravagance, invested the sum of one shilling in the Grand Metropolitan Lottery. And he had won. Not the magnificent, life-altering prize that adorns the headlines and makes men mad, but a sum so modest as to be almost shy: Fifty Pounds. To Timothy, it was a king’s ransom. It was the down-payment on a future free from the gnawing anxiety of his relations’ demands. It was a small, warm, golden sun in the cold firmament of his existence. And a resolution, born of long and bitter experience, crystallised in his mind with the hardness of a diamond: this sun was for him alone. He would keep it secret. He would bank it. He would be silent.

For five days, he was a man transformed. A secret smile played upon his lips as he dipped his pen in the ink. He walked with a lighter step through the bustling, indifferent crowds of Cheapside. He looked upon the world not as a debtor, but as a man with a hidden reserve, a private fortress of solvency. He was, for those five days, the happiest and most discreet young man in all of London. On the sixth day, the fortress was breached.

It began, as such things always do, with a knock at his garret door—a knock of such studied desperation that it could belong to no one but his Aunt Prudence. And there she stood, a large woman rendered small by an immense bonnet and an even more immense affliction. Her pocket-handkerchief was already deployed, her voice already a tremolo of woe.

“Oh, Timothy!” she wailed, sweeping into the room and nearly extinguishing the candle with the agitated flapping of her shawl. “Oh, my dear, dear boy! I am undone! Ruined! The very rafters of my house conspire against me! A leak, Timothy, a most villainous and persistent leak, right through the ceiling of my best parlour, threatening to dissolve my poor dear late husband’s portrait into a watery soup! The man from the board-works has been, and he has looked, and he has shaken his head with a dreadful finality, and he says the repair will cost—oh, it is too terrible to speak!—the repair will cost exactly fifty pounds!”

Timothy’s heart, which had leapt to his throat at her entrance, now sank into his boots. “Fifty pounds, aunt? But I am only a clerk. Where could I possibly—”

“Do not speak of it! I cannot bear it!” she cried, pressing the handkerchief to her eyes. “I know your means are slender. I know I ought not to ask. But I thought, perhaps, in the goodness of your heart… but no! Forget I spoke! I shall go and live in the workhouse, and think of you always!” She made a great show of rising to depart, a performance so theatrical that it required her to stumble over a stool for full effect.

Before he could succumb to the familiar tide of pity and guilt, there was another knock. This one was sharp, peremptory, and rattled the very door in its frame. It was his Uncle Silas. He did not wait for an invitation but strode in, his face a mask of indignant fury.

“Prudence!” he barked, ignoring Timothy entirely. “So you are here before me, are you? Crying your crocodile tears, I’ll be bound! Well, let the boy hear it from both of us! Timothy,” he said, turning his gimlet eye upon his nephew, “a catastrophe has befallen me. A conspiracy of iniquity! I have been presented with a bill—a fraudulent, extortionate, and entirely fabricated bill—for a new suit of clothes I did not order, from a tailor I have never seen, for an occasion I did not attend! The sum demanded is an outrage! It is a scandal! It is… fifty pounds!”

“Uncle, this is impossible,” Timothy stammered, feeling the walls of his small room begin to close in. “First a leak for fifty pounds, and now a bill for fifty pounds?”

“A likely story!” sneered his uncle. “She has invented a leak! A classic feminine wile! My need is the more pressing, for a man’s reputation is at stake! A clerk’s honour is one thing, but a gentleman’s credit is another!”
