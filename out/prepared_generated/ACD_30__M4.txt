It was on a blustery afternoon in the late October of that year, when the smell of damp earth and wet straw was heavy in the air, that Mr. Nathaniel Corbin was shown into our sitting-room in Baker Street. He was a spare, leather-faced man of sixty, with the stoop of one who has spent a lifetime in the saddle, and he turned his cap round and round in his gnarled hands as though it were a wheel he might steer to safety.

“Mr. Holmes,” said he, when he had taken the chair my friend indicated, “I am at my wits’ end, and that is a thing I have never said before in my life. I have trained horses on the Heath for five-and-thirty years, and I know the game as I know my own stables. But there is a thing happening at Ringmere Lodge that beats me altogether, and I would rather have your opinion of it than any man’s in England.”

“You have a horse in trouble,” said Holmes, without hesitation.

Our visitor started. “How did you know that?”

“You have come to me in riding-breeches and gaiters, with a spur still buckled to your heel and a wisp of stable straw caught in the seam of your coat. You are a trainer, and a busy one, or you would not have come to town in your working clothes. A trainer’s troubles are his horses. But you said your lads were loyal and your jockey honest, so it is not a matter of villainy among your own people.”

Corbin stared at him, and then a slow smile broke over his weathered face. “You have it in one, sir. It is the colt Firefly—the best I have had in twenty years, and I have had some good ones. He is by Lamplighter out of a mare of mine, and he can gallop. Mr. Holmes, he can gallop like a ghost. In his trials he has beaten everything I have put against him, and he has done it on the bridle, with his ears pricked, as pleased with himself as a child at a fair. And then he goes to the races and he is a different animal. He sweats before the start, he will not settle, and when the flag falls he runs as though his heart were not in it. He has been beaten four times this season, and each time by horses he has left standing on the trial ground.”

“The jockey?”

“Tom Arkwright—as honest a lad as ever threw a leg over a horse. He is as sick about it as I am. He says the colt is frightened of something, but what it is he cannot tell me.”

“The feed?”

“Tested, every sack of it, and my head lad sleeps in the feed-house.”

“And the stables? Who comes and goes?”

“That is the queer part of it, sir. Nobody. The lads are all old servants of mine. There is not a stranger about the place from one week’s end to the other.”

Holmes sat silent for a moment, his long fingers pressed together, his eyes fixed upon the fire. “You have had the colt a year?”

“Since he was a yearling.”

“And the trouble began when?”

Corbin considered. “At the April meeting at Newmarket. He ran there first, and he ran badly. I put it down to greenness. But he has run badly every time since.”

“You have tried him at home since then?”

“Aye, and he has gone like a champion. Only last Tuesday he gave seven pounds and a beating to my second string, and came home as fresh as a daisy.”

“Then the difference is not in the horse, but in the circumstances,” said Holmes, rising. “I think I must come down to Ringmere Lodge and see for myself. If you will have me, I shall come to-morrow and stay a week. I should like to see the colt, and I should like still more to see what happens to him on the night before a race.”

Corbin’s face showed his relief. “You will be very welcome, sir. And you, Doctor?” he added, turning to me.

“I shall be delighted,” said I, for I had no mind to miss the business.

Ringmere Lodge stood upon the edge of the Heath, a low, red-brick house with stables behind it and a paddock running down to a belt of firs. For four days Holmes said little and saw much. He walked the gallops, he watched the trials, he stood in the stable-yard at all hours with his hands in his pockets and his chin upon his breast. He talked with the lads in their own tongue, and he made a particular friend of an old groom named Dobbins, who had been with Corbin since he was a boy. And every evening, when the lamps were lit, he would sit by the window of the room they had given us and look out across the darkening Heath, saying nothing.

On the fifth night, as we sat at supper, he spoke at last. “Watson, the colt is to run at the Houghton meeting on Thursday. To-morrow night is the night before the race. I fancy we shall see something.”

“You have a theory, then?”

“I have an observation, which is not the same thing. The colt is a nervous, high-spirited creature, and he is frightened in public. But he is not frightened of the crowd, for he has been paraded before crowds at home and taken no notice. He is not frightened of the noise, for he has stood by the starting-gate at Newmarket and never turned a hair. What, then, is different on the racecourse? The other horses, the strange stables, the journey in the box—all these he has known and minded not at all. But there is one thing, Watson, which is present at every racecourse and absent from every trial-ground, and which a horse of his temperament would feel most keenly.”
