It is a truth not sufficiently acknowledged that a mother in possession of a tolerable constitution must be in want of a disorder, provided it may serve to transport her family to a spa town. Mrs. Stanhope had long cultivated a delicacy of health that was more convenient than alarming, and when she proposed removing to Cheltenham for the waters, her daughter Clara could not but feel that the journey was undertaken less for the stomach than for the society. The lodgings in Bath Street were taken for six weeks, the trunks were packed with an attention to evening dress that spoke little of invalidism, and Mrs. Stanhope’s spirits, throughout the fifty miles of road, were so uniformly excellent that Clara began to suspect the entire enterprise of being a comedy performed for an audience she had not yet been permitted to see.

She was not left long in uncertainty. On the very morning after their arrival, while Clara sat at the window attempting to read and attempting still harder not to watch the passing promenade, her mother entered the sitting-room with a letter pressed to her bosom and a countenance coloured by something more than the exertion of the stairs.

"Clara, my dear," said she, "you will never guess whom I have encountered—or rather, whom I am upon the point of encountering, for the meeting is not yet positively assured, though I think we may count it as good as settled."

Clara, who had learnt by long experience that her mother’s disclosures were best hastened by silence, laid down her book and waited.

"Mr. Vivian," pursued Mrs. Stanhope, with an air of studied calm that would have deceived nobody, "Henry Vivian, of Thornbury—though I daresay he does not reside there now—is at present in Cheltenham. He is established in Cambray Place, and I have it on the best authority that he is quite alone, for poor Mrs. Vivian died, it must be two years ago come Michaelmas—a decline, they say, though I never heard that she was particularly delicate before. But there it is. He is a widower, and here, and I confess I cannot think it an accident."

"An accident, Mama?"

"Well, my love, one does hear of these things. I wrote to Mrs. Parmenter, you know, when we were first thinking of Cheltenham, and I mentioned—quite in passing, merely as one does mention the names of old acquaintance—that I understood Mr. Vivian sometimes frequented the town. And Mrs. Parmenter wrote back with a great deal of information, which I am sure she meant kindly, though she does want brevity. And now here we are, and here is he, and I must think the thing was meant to be."

Clara felt herself at once enlightened and bewildered. Mr. Henry Vivian was a name she had heard often in childhood, always uttered by her mother with a particular softness of voice and a slight elevation of brow, which, even at the age of eight, Clara had instinctively associated with matters of the heart. She had gathered, in fragments and by degrees, that Mr. Vivian had once paid her mother very marked attentions, in the days before Mr. Stanhope had appeared upon the scene with his five thousand a year and his decisive manner; and that the affair, though it had ended without any formal declaration, had left upon Mrs. Stanhope’s mind an impression of what might have been, which twenty years of respectable matrimony had not wholly effaced.

"But, Mama," said Clara, recovering herself sufficiently to speak, "you cannot mean that you came to Cheltenham expressly to renew an acquaintance with a gentleman you have not seen in—how many years?"

"Nineteen," said Mrs. Stanhope, with a precision that rather undid her air of indifference. "But you mistake me, my dear. I came for my health. The waters have been strongly recommended. Only think how convenient, then, that Mr. Vivian should also be here. One cannot help meeting people at a spa town; it is what the place is for."

Clara was not satisfied. She was a young woman of good sense and some observation, and she had lived long enough under her mother’s roof to distinguish between those schemes which Mrs. Stanhope acknowledged and those which she merely conducted. The present business bore every mark of the latter species. The choice of Cheltenham over Leamington, where Mrs. Stanhope’s sister resided and where the waters were equally efficacious; the letter to Mrs. Parmenter, with its artful dropping of a name; the rooms in Bath Street, which were not only the most commodious in the town but situated within an easy walk of Cambray Place—all these circumstances, when considered together, formed a pattern so plain that Clara could only marvel at her mother’s expecting it to pass unnoticed.

Yet she was uncertain whether to be shocked or delighted. The propriety of the thing was, on the face of it, unexceptionable. Mrs. Stanhope was a widow; Mr. Vivian was a widower; both were of an age at which the world is inclined to be lenient, and neither was so situated in life as to make an alliance imprudent. Clara’s father had been dead five years, and though Mrs. Stanhope had worn her widow’s weeds with exemplary regularity, Clara had sometimes suspected that the grief beneath them, while genuine enough in its degree, was not of that consuming sort which precludes all future happiness. There had been, moreover, a certain restlessness in her mother’s manner of late, a disposition to linger over old letters and to speak of former times with an emphasis that suggested the present was no longer quite sufficient to her.

What chiefly struck Clara, however, was the question of Mr. Vivian’s intentions. That he had been informed of Mrs. Stanhope’s presence in Cheltenham she could not doubt; Mrs. Parmenter’s industry in the distribution of intelligence was equal to her mother’s appetite for it. But whether he would seek the acquaintance, or let it quietly drop, was a matter upon which Clara’s imagination could fix nothing.
