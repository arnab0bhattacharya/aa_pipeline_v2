Miss Caroline Peele had been for twenty years the senior mistress at Brantwood House, a select establishment for the daughters of professional men in the neighbourhood of Denmark Hill, and it was generally conceded that no one in that quarter of London could manage a roomful of young ladies with quite her combination of firmness and Christian tact. She was a spare, upright woman of five-and-forty, with hair already going grey at the temples, a plain, honest face, and the sort of clear, carrying voice that makes itself heard in a schoolroom without apparently being raised. Her employers, the Misses Tregarthen, who had inherited the school from their father along with a fine stock of genteel prejudices, regarded her as a pillar of the institution; and when, in the Michaelmas term of 1904, she gave in her notice and announced that she intended to open a school of her own in the same suburb, their astonishment was only equalled by their sense of injury.

The news travelled, as such news will, along the respectable tea-tables of the district, and it was presently understood that Miss Peele’s academy was to be conducted on lines that the Misses Tregarthen could only describe, in the privacy of their sitting-room, as “peculiar.” It was not merely that she proposed to take day-girls only, and at fees that made the Tregarthen prospectus look like a mortgage; the extraordinary feature of the thing was that she had announced her intention of receiving the daughters of tradespeople—shopkeepers, in plain English—the very class of girl whom Brantwood House had been at some pains to exclude, or to admit only at a distance and with a certain delicacy of condescension.

“My dear Caroline,” said Miss Amelia Tregarthen, when she had come to reason with her, “you cannot possibly know what you are doing. A school is not a charity. There must be a tone. And the tone of a school is determined—I am sorry to say it, but it is so—by the home. A girl from a shop—from a draper’s, Caroline—brings the shop with her. She cannot help it. She talks of the counter; she has seen her father bowing to customers; she has, in a word, no reserve. And the parents! My dear, have you considered the parents?”

Miss Peele, who was a woman of few words and much obstinacy, replied that she had considered the parents a great deal, and that in her opinion they were, on the whole, rather more anxious about their daughters’ education than some of the professional gentlemen who paid their bills a quarter in arrears. Miss Amelia went away grieved, and the Misses Tregarthen, in conclave, decided that Caroline Peele had been spoiled by success and must be left to learn her lesson.

The school opened in October in a large, ugly, convenient house in Ferndale Road, with a brass plate upon the door and a smell of new paint in the passages. Miss Peele had engaged as her assistant a brisk young woman named Miss Hocking, fresh from a training college, who had theories about the teaching of geography, and a charwoman who came in by the day. There were nine pupils on the opening morning, and by Christmas there were sixteen. They were the daughters of a grocer, of two drapers, of a chemist, of a furniture dealer, of a baker in the Camberwell Road, and of a stationer who also sold the local newspaper; and their ages ranged from eight to fifteen. They were healthy, talkative, rather shy, and exceedingly well provided with boots.

For the first fortnight everything went forward with that pleasant bustle which attends a new enterprise. Miss Peele taught English and arithmetic and Scripture with her old decision; Miss Hocking made the geography lesson a perpetual delight by reason of a home-made relief map of Europe in which the Alps were represented by a ridge of putty; and the girls, who had never before been taught by anyone but a governess or a Sunday-school teacher, took to the whole business with a gusto that was very refreshing. It was in the third week that the trouble began.

It began with a visit from Mrs. Blaycock, the wife of the grocer in Ferndale Road, a large, anxious, handsomely dressed woman who had come, she said, to have a word about her Ethel. She sat on the edge of the chair in Miss Peele’s little study and turned her muff round and round in her hands.

“It’s like this, miss,” she said. “I want Ethel to have a good education, and I don’t mind paying for it, and I’m sure the school’s a credit to you. But there’s a thing that’s been on my mind. Ethel came home last week and she says to me, ‘Mother,’ she says, ‘Miss Peele says we’re to learn to speak properly, and not to say “serviette” for “napkin,” and not to say “chimley” for “chimney.”’ And I says to her, ‘Well, Ethel, and what’s wrong with that?’ And she says, ‘Nothing, Mother, only Miss Peele says it’s common.’ And I thought to myself, miss, that if my girl’s to be told that her mother’s way of speaking is common, why, then, I don’t know as I want her to come to school at all.”

Miss Peele, who had listened to this with a growing discomfort, said that she had meant nothing of the sort, that the object of teaching correct speech was not to shame anyone’s mother, but to give the girls a command of the language that would serve them well in whatever walk of life they might be called to adorn. But Mrs. Blaycock shook her head.

“That’s as may be, miss. But there’s another thing. My husband, he says to me, ‘Maria,’ he says, ‘why is it that Ethel’s always talking about the pictures and the music and the poetry, and never a word about the shop? It’s as if she was ashamed of us.’ And I’m sure, miss, I don’t know what to say to him.”
