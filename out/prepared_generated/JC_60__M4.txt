The sea, in the grey hour before the dawn, wore the aspect of a thing that had been beaten into a sullen and menacing stillness; but it was a deceitful quiet, for the long, oily swells that marched across the Bay of Biscay had a weight in them, a portentous deliberation, that spoke of violence not distant but merely gathering its breath. The *Ariel*—a steamer of some fifteen hundred tons, her black hull streaked with the rust of many voyages—laboured through this uneasy calm with a rhythmic, mechanical throb that seemed to the man upon her bridge a profanation of the silence. Captain Elias Harkness stood with his hands clasped behind his back, his chin sunk upon his breast, and his eyes, which were of that pale, washed blue that one sees in men who have spent their lives under a hard sky, fixed upon the horizon with an expression of profound and inarticulate distrust. He was a man of the old school, bred in sail, and the transition to steam had been for him not a progress but a fall from grace. The engines, those iron hearts that beat below the waterline, were to him an abomination, a noisy, smelly, and unreliable substitute for the clean winds of heaven; and the engineers, the men who tended them, were a race apart, oil-stained and mysterious, whose talk was of pressures and pistons and whose eyes held the knowing look of those who traffic with dark and dangerous powers.

The chief engineer, a squat, grizzled Welshman named Pryce, he regarded with a particular and silent animosity. Pryce was a man of few words, but his words, when they came, were uttered with a kind of patient certainty that Harkness found insufferable. He had, moreover, a habit of wiping his hands upon a piece of cotton waste with a slow, deliberate motion that seemed to the captain a gesture of contempt for the clean order of the deck. Between them there had grown a silence that was not peace but a truce, maintained by the rigid etiquette of the sea and by the fact that each man, in his own domain, was absolute. Yet the captain, pacing his bridge, felt himself to be a man in a house whose foundations he could not see, and he trusted them not at all.

By noon the wind had freshened from the south-west, and the *Ariel* began to pitch with a short, vicious motion that sent the spray flying over her forecastle head. The sky had closed down upon the sea like a lid of lead, and the horizon had contracted to a circle of perhaps two miles in diameter. Harkness, who had been watching the barometer with a gloomy satisfaction, sent word to the engine-room to reduce speed, and the *Ariel* began to labour more heavily, taking the seas over her forward well-deck in great, green, hissing cataracts. It was then, in the deepening gloom of the afternoon, that the trouble began.

It announced itself not with a crash but with a hesitation—a faltering in the rhythmic throb that was the ship's pulse, a stutter, a pause, and then a resumption of the beat, but with a note in it that was new and wrong. Harkness, on the bridge, felt the change through the soles of his feet. He said nothing, but his hands, clasped behind him, tightened their grip. The second mate, a young man named Ellison, glanced at him inquiringly, but the captain's face was a mask of stone. Then it came again, a longer hesitation, a jarring shudder that ran through the entire fabric of the ship, and the engines stopped.

In the sudden silence, the voice of the storm became immense. The wind shrieked in the rigging, the seas hammered at the hull, and the *Ariel*, bereft of her mechanical heart, fell off into the trough and rolled with a sickening, helpless motion that threw the men on the bridge against the rails. Harkness, his face grey, gave his orders in a voice that was steady but strangely thin. The helm was put hard over, the trysail was set to give her some steerage way, and the ship, after a long and agonising moment, came slowly up into the wind, her bowsprit pointing into the eye of the gale, and there she hung, pitching and rolling, a thing of wood and iron at the mercy of the elements.

It was then that Harkness, descending from his bridge, made his way aft to the engine-room hatch. He had not been below in months, and the descent into that iron pit, lit by the dim glow of the engine-room lamps, was for him a journey into a foreign country. The air was thick with the smell of hot oil and steam, and the great engines, now silent, stood like dead beasts, their pistons motionless, their flywheels still. Pryce was there, with his two assistants, their faces streaked with grime, their eyes red-rimmed from the heat and the strain. The chief engineer looked up as the captain's heavy form appeared at the foot of the ladder, and for a moment their eyes met.

"It's the main bearing, sir," said Pryce, in his slow, deliberate voice. "The white metal's run. We'll have to strip it down and pour new metal, and that's a job of some hours, even if the weather holds."

Harkness stared at him. The words were English, but they conveyed to him nothing but a sense of his own helplessness. He was a master mariner, a man who could navigate by the stars and handle a square-rigger in a gale, but this—this was a mystery beyond his ken. He looked at the great, silent engine, and it seemed to him a malevolent thing, a traitor that had failed him in his hour of need.

"How long?" he asked, and his voice was harsh.
