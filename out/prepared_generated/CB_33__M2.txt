The rain lashed the windows of the study with a fury that seemed personal, as if the sky itself were angry at the intrusion of light into this dark, damp corner of Ashcombe Hall. I sat in the chair by the fire, my hands folded tightly in my lap to suppress their trembling, and watched Mr. Halloway work. He was a cousin of the master of the house, a painter who had returned from the Continent with eyes that looked as though they had seen too much and believed too little, and a mouth that smiled only when he intended to wound.

He had been painting for an hour. Not me, strictly speaking. He had painted the room, the shadows, the heavy velvet curtains, the dust motes dancing in the beam of the dying candle. But his gaze, when it strayed from the canvas, was fixed upon me with an intensity that made my skin prickle. It was not the look of a man observing a subject; it was the look of a hunter tracking a hare in the long grass.

“You are staring, sir,” I said, my voice steady, though my heart hammered against my ribs like a trapped bird.

Mr. Halloway did not start. He dipped his brush into a jar of turpentine, the sharp chemical smell cutting through the scent of the rain. “I am studying, Miss Vane. There is a difference. Staring is the act of the ignorant; studying is the duty of the artist. And you, Miss Vane, possess a face that demands study.”

I looked away, toward the fire. The logs crackled and spat, sending up sparks that died quickly in the cold air. I felt a sudden, fierce anger rise in my throat. I was a governess. I was a paid servant, albeit of a higher station than the maids, and I had learned long ago that my face was to be invisible, a blank screen upon which the children of the house might project their whims. To be seen was to be vulnerable. To be flattered by a man who had never lifted a finger to help me was to be insulted.

“Remarkable,” I repeated, the word tasting like ash. “Is that your word? Remarkable. I have been told I have a plain countenance, suitable for a woman who speaks little and thinks less. I prefer it that way. A remarkable face is a face that invites speculation, and speculation, in a house like this, is a dangerous thing.”

Mr. Halloway set down his brush. He turned from the easel, and for the first time, I saw the exhaustion in his eyes, the deep shadows beneath the rims. He walked toward me, his movements slow, deliberate. He did not sit. He remained standing, looming over my small figure, his presence filling the room like a storm front.

“You think this is a trick,” he said. It was not a question.

“I think,” I replied, lifting my chin, “that you intend to paint me. And I think that if you hang that portrait in the gallery, Lady Ashcombe will see it. She will see the light in my eyes, the strength in my jaw, and she will know that I am not the meek, silent creature she believes me to be. She will see that I have a spirit, and she will find it inconvenient. Is that your aim? To provoke her? To use my image as a weapon in some petty domestic war?”

He laughed, a dry, harsh sound that seemed to come from the depths of his chest. “You are sharp, Miss Vane. Sharper than I expected. But you mistake the motive. I am not fighting Lady Ashcombe. I am fighting the silence that has eaten this house alive.”

He leaned forward, his voice dropping to a whisper that I had to strain to hear over the drumming of the rain. “Do you know what this house is? It is a tomb. The master is a ghost, the lady is a statue, and the children are dolls made of wax and wire. And you... you are the only living thing in this place. I see it in the way you stand when you think no one is looking. I see it in the way you correct the boys, not with anger, but with a kind of fierce, cold justice that burns me. I do not paint you to provoke. I paint you because I am afraid that if I do not capture you, you will vanish. You are so quiet, Miss Vane, that I am terrified you will simply disappear into the walls.”

I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the draft from the window. It was the chill of being known. Of having my armor stripped away by a man who had never spoken to me, who had never helped me carry a book or mended a torn sleeve. It was an intimacy that I did not want, and yet, I could not deny the accuracy of his words. I did feel like a ghost in my own life. I did feel the suffocating weight of the Ashcombe name, the gold leaf and the silver spoons, pressing down on my spirit until I could barely breathe.

“You do not know me,” I said, my voice trembling now, the restraint breaking. “You see what you want to see. You see a martyr. You see a victim. And you are gratified by the image. You are an artist, Mr. Halloway. You feed on pain. You take the suffering of others and turn it into something beautiful, something you can hang on a wall and admire. But you do not care for the person behind the paint. You care for the canvas.”

“Perhaps,” he said quietly. “But does that make the pain less real? If I paint the wound, does it cease to bleed?”
