Mr. Fitzwilliam Grey, of Ashby Lodge in Lincolnshire, was a gentleman whose principal misfortune lay in possessing an income sufficient to maintain the appearance of his station, but not the reality of its demands. His estate, though respectable, was entailed upon a distant cousin, and his personal fortune was of that moderate kind which makes the prospect of a sudden expense feel like a personal affront. It was with a countenance of such affront that he entered the drawing-room one October morning, a letter trembling in his hand, and announced that their son, Mr. Arthur Grey, had returned from Oxford not merely with a first in Classics, but with a debt of some three thousand pounds.

Mrs. Grey, a lady whose sensibilities were as finely drawn as her features, received this intelligence with a gasp that was perfectly proportioned to the shock. Her first thought, as was natural, was for the injustice of the thing; her second, for the remedy. “Three thousand pounds, Mr. Grey! It is not to be borne. He must have been imposed upon at every turn. A boy of his age, and with his disposition!”

“His disposition is precisely the difficulty,” replied her husband, pacing before the mantelpiece. “It is a disposition to please and to be pleased, without a thought for the consequences. The university bills, the tailor’s accounts, the stabling of a horse he had no right to keep—these are the monuments of his good nature. And now they must be paid.”

The conversation then turned, as it must, to the source of payment. Mr. Grey, in the manner of a man who has already exhausted all other possibilities in his own mind, laid his proposal before his wife. “The oaks in the north wood,” he said, with the gravity of a judge passing sentence, “are the finest timber in the county. They have stood since my grandfather’s time. But they are worth a considerable sum. I have had an estimate from the timber merchant at Grantham. It would cover the whole of the debt, with a little to spare.”

Mrs. Grey looked at him as if he had proposed to sell a family member. “The oaks! Our oaks! The very trees that give the Lodge its character and dignity! You would fell them to pay for a billiard table and a set of foolish wagers? I would sooner see Arthur in the Fleet Prison. There must be another way.”

It was then that the full force of her ingenuity, which had for some years lain dormant, was brought to bear upon the problem. “There is Miss Eleanor Vance,” she said, her voice taking on the measured tone of a strategist. “She is to arrive this very afternoon for her visit. Her uncle, Mr. Vance, is said to be worth at least fifty thousand pounds, and she is his sole heiress. She is of a good family, and, by all accounts, of a gentle and amiable temper. Arthur is a handsome young man, and when he chooses to be, can be very agreeable. A marriage between them would settle everything. It would not only discharge the debt, but secure the future of the estate.”

Mr. Grey was not without his own romantic notions, but they were of a more practical nature. The idea of sacrificing his trees was a wound to his pride; the idea of securing his son’s future through a wealthy marriage was a balm. “It is a fine scheme, my dear,” he admitted, “provided the young people can be brought to like one another. We cannot force the matter.”

“Like one another?” Mrs. Grey repeated, with a smile of superior understanding. “My dear Mr. Grey, when a young man of twenty-one with debts and no prospects meets a young woman of fortune and good nature, a mutual affection is the most natural consequence in the world. They require only the opportunity.”

The opportunity arrived in the form of Miss Vance’s carriage, and the object of their domestic conspiracy, Mr. Arthur Grey, was nowhere to be found. He was, in fact, at that very moment, in the gun-room, examining the lock of his favourite fowling-piece with an attention he had not recently bestowed upon any of his creditors. He was a young man of considerable personal charm, with an open countenance and an easy manner that made the world seem to like him as much as he liked it. He felt the burden of his father’s disappointment and his mother’s anxiety, but he felt it as a temporary cloud, rather than a permanent condition. The debts were an unpleasantness, certainly, but one to be forgotten in the crisp morning air and the satisfaction of a well-aimed shot.

His father found him there. “Arthur, Miss Vance has arrived. Your mother expects you in the drawing-room.”

Arthur did not look up from the gun. “I shall be there directly, sir. I am just off to the turnip fields. The birds will be thick this morning.”

Mr. Grey’s patience, already worn thin, gave way. “The birds! Good heavens, boy, can you think of nothing but your own amusement? There is a matter of three thousand pounds to be discussed. There is a proposal to be made.”

Arthur finally met his father’s gaze, his own eyes holding a look of guileless incomprehension. “A proposal, sir? To whom? And for what?”

“For your future, for the estate, for everything!” his father exclaimed. “Your mother has a plan. Miss Vance is an heiress. It is a most excellent match.”

A slow understanding dawned on Arthur’s face, not of the gravity of the situation, but of the inconvenience. “You wish me to marry her? But I have only just met the lady. It is a little premature, is it not? Besides,” he added, with a charmingly apologetic smile, “I have a prior engagement. With the partridges.”
