The rain had ceased by the time the hansom drew up at our door in Fetter Lane, though the pavement still shone like a sheet of hammered silver, and the gutters ran with a brown and lively water. I was at my desk, engaged upon the duller sort of ledger-work which is the price one pays for a small private practice, when I heard a timid knock and the sound of a voice in altercation with Mrs. Pemberton, our landlady.

“I tell you I must see him,” the voice was saying. “It is a matter of life and death—my own life, if you will have it plain.”

The door opened, and there entered a stout, red-faced man of some fifty years, clad in a cabman’s cape of faded blue, with a woollen comforter about his neck and his hat crushed in his two hands as though he would wring a confession from it. His boots were wet, his trousers splashed to the knee, and his whole aspect that of a man who has been driving hard through the night and has not slept.

“Mr. Harrowby?” said he. “They told me at the yard you was the man for a puzzle. I am Josiah Tredgold, licensed driver, number four hundred and nine, of the Belvedere rank. And I am in mortal terror, sir, of the police.”

“Sit down,” said I, “and tell your story from the beginning. You have come straight from your work, I think.”

“That I have, sir. I have not been home nor eaten since yesterday noon.”

“Then we will have tea first,” said I, ringing the bell, “and you shall talk while you drink it. But I warn you, Mr. Tredgold, that I am not a magistrate nor a policeman, and if you have done murder I can promise you no protection.”

“Murder!” he cried, starting up. “I have driven a many queer fares in my time, sir, but I never murdered one yet, and I hope I never shall.”

“Very good. Then begin.”

He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and his eyes wandered to the window as though he would rather have been out in the rain than in that warm room.

“It was at the corner of Marchmont Street,” he said, “that I picked up the two gentlemen. That would be about a quarter before eleven. One was a tall, thin, dark-whiskered party in a black coat, and the other was a small man, very spruce, in a grey ulster and a soft felt hat. The tall one gave me the address—Number 14, Cresswell Gardens, out by the Regent’s Canal—and he spoke sharp and short, like a man who has made up his mind and will not be kept waiting. The little man said nothing at all. He got in first, and the other after him, and I noticed that the tall one sat forward on the seat with his hand upon the little man’s arm the whole way, as though he were afraid he might jump out.”

“Did you observe anything else?”

“I observed that the little man’s hands were shaking, sir. I saw them in the light of a lamp as we passed. And I observed that the tall one had a bag—a black leather bag, such as a surgeon carries—upon his knee.”

“Go on.”

“We drove fast, for the streets were empty, and we came into Cresswell Gardens a little after eleven. The tall gentleman paid me at the corner, and gave me a shilling over the fare, which was liberal. Then he and the little man walked up the steps of Number 14 together, and I saw the door open and them go in, and I drove away.”

“And that was the last you saw of them?”

The cabman’s face went a shade paler. “That was the last I saw of the tall one, sir. But the little one—the little one I saw again, and that is what has brought me here.”

He set down his cup with a hand that was not steady.

“I had gone no farther than the bridge over the canal,” he said, “when I was hailed by a constable, who asked me if I had seen a small man in a grey ulster. I said I had, and that I had set him down at Number 14, Cresswell Gardens, not ten minutes before. The constable looked at me very queer, sir, and then he says, ‘You had better come with me to the station, my man, for that small man in the grey ulster was found dead in a doorway in Topham’s Row, on the other side of the water, at the very hour you say you set him down.’”

“And you told the police your story?”

“I told it them, sir, and they would not believe a word of it. They said I had murdered him myself and invented the tall man to save my neck. They have kept me at the station half the night, and only let me go this morning to find a lawyer or a friend. And so I came to you.”

“You have done wisely,” said I, “for if your story is true, there is a very pretty problem in it. But before I hear another word, I must see your cab.”

We went down together into the wet street, where the hansom stood at the kerb with its horse still in the shafts, a patient, drooping beast that looked as tired as its master. I walked round it slowly, examining every part, while the cabman stood by with the air of a man who has put his last penny on a horse.

“You have had a passenger who was not well,” said I, pointing to the floor of the cab. “There is a stain there, and it is not mud.”

“The little man, sir. He coughed a good deal, and once he put his handkerchief to his mouth.”

“Show me the handkerchief.”
