The palace of the President-General Don Álvaro Salcedo stood upon the crest of a hill above the town of Santa Maria de la Ribera, and from its long, shuttered windows one might look down upon the huddled roofs, the single spire of the cathedral, and beyond them, the wide, brown river sliding seaward under a sky of brass. It was a building of the old colonial time, heavy with stone, its whitewash peeling in great leprous patches, and its corridors, floored with worn red tiles, were full of a cool, dank gloom that smelled of earth and forgotten incense. Here, in the great sala, whose ceiling of painted beams had darkened through a century of smoke and neglect, the President-General had caused a long table to be spread with damask and with silver, and had summoned the foreign merchants of the republic to a banquet of reconciliation.

They came, those merchants, in their black broadcloth and their high white collars, with faces carefully composed to an expression of commercial neutrality. There was Herr Baumgarten, of the house of Baumgarten & Co., dealers in indigo and hides, a heavy, slow-moving man with pale eyes that seemed to have been washed too often in the waters of calculation; and M. Perrault, of the French bank, thin and dry and yellow, with a manner of exhausted politeness; and the Englishman, Mr. Wedderburn, who represented certain shipping interests, and who carried his long body with the stiff and wary dignity of a man who has learned to expect nothing and to be surprised by nothing. They stood in little groups upon the tessellated floor, speaking in low voices of the weather, of the river's falling, of the price of coffee, while their eyes, in despite of them, went straying to the tall double doors at the end of the sala, behind which, as they were aware, the President-General was accustomed to sit in the twilight of his own thoughts.

The young lieutenant of the guard, whose name was Ignacio Fuentes, stood at the sideboard, where the wine had been set out in decanters of heavy, dimmed glass. He was very young, with a face that had not yet lost its look of wondering candour, though the eyes were older than the face, and he wore the blue and silver of the presidential guard as though it had been laid upon him against his will. He had come but three months since from the province of the interior, where he had seen certain things which he did not speak of; and now he stood, with the decanter in his hand, and watched the faces of the guests as they turned, all together, at the opening of the doors.

The President-General entered alone. He was a small man, and old, with a face like a withered apple and a thin white beard that came to a point. He wore a plain black coat, and upon his breast a single order, and he walked with a slow, shuffling step, his hands clasped behind him, his head bent a little forward, as though he listened for something at the floor. But when he raised his eyes, they were of a pale and terrible blue, and they went from face to face with a kind of gentle and implacable inquiry, as if he sought to read there the secret of their thoughts, and to forgive them before they had sinned.

"Señores," he said, and his voice was low and courteous, with the rustle of dry leaves in it, "you do me too much honour. I am but a simple soldier, and these are not the days for banquets. But I would have you know that the troubles in the provinces are ended. There is no more fighting. The republic is at peace. I have said it."

He bowed, and motioned them to the table, and there was a scraping of chairs and a settling into places, and the lieutenant Fuentes went round with the wine, beginning with the Englishman, who took it with a nod and a murmured word, and then with the German, who said "Danke," and then with the Frenchman, who said nothing at all.

The dinner was long and heavy. There were courses of fish and of roasted meats, and a sweet made of honey and almonds, and all the while the President-General talked in his low, courteous voice of the prosperity of the country, of the fertility of the soil, of the great future that lay before those who should invest their capital in the peaceful development of its resources. He spoke of the river, and of the new railway that should run beside it, and of the mines in the mountains that waited only for the hand of enterprise. And the merchants listened, and ate, and now and then made answer in the phrases of their trade, while their eyes went, in despite of them, to the tall windows where the light was beginning to fail, and to the two sentries who stood at the doors with their rifles at rest, and to the young lieutenant who went round and round with the wine, and whose face, as the evening wore on, grew paler and more set.

It was Mr. Wedderburn who, in a pause of the talk, asked a question. He asked it in his slow, dry way, with his eyes upon the tablecloth.

"And the prisoners, Excellency? The prisoners in the interior?"

The President-General did not answer at once. He sat quite still, his thin hands resting upon the arms of his chair, and his pale eyes went slowly round the table and rested at last upon the face of the young lieutenant, who stood by the sideboard with the decanter in his hand.

"The prisoners," said the President-General, at length, and his voice was very gentle, "have been released. All of them. I have given the order. There is no more imprisonment. The republic is at peace."
