It was an observation too generally received to be any longer disputed, that Miss Isabella Vane, at five-and-twenty, had conducted her search for a husband with a coldness bordering upon perversity. She possessed a fortune of twenty thousand pounds, a pleasing countenance, and a share of good sense that, in the opinion of her guardian, should have guided her more wisely than to refuse three eligible offers in as many years. Sir Giles Pemberton, whose connexion to the Vane family was one of law rather than affection, held it as a fixed principle that a woman’s duty was to secure her situation, and he could not reconcile himself to the spectacle of his ward’s obstinacy. Her reasons, she alleged, were sufficient; that is to say, she had found in each gentleman a want of that essential congruence of mind which alone could render a state of matrimony endurable. This explanation, while possessing the elegance of a maxim, was of little comfort to Sir Giles, who saw in it only the romantic folly of a young woman who had read too many novels and expected too much of the world.

Her refusal of Mr. Croft, a man of unimpeachable character and six thousand a year, had been the occasion of their last serious disagreement. “A want of congruence, my dear Isabella?” he had said, pacing the length of the drawing-room at Pemberton Hall. “The man is sensible, he is established, he admires you. What more, in God’s name, can a woman require?” Isabella had replied that she required a companion, not merely a provider, and that Mr. Croft’s conversation, though perfectly correct, afforded her mind no more exercise than a well-tended garden path. Sir Giles had scoffed at such distinctions, and the matter had been laid aside, though not, by any means, concluded.

The arrival of Mr. Thomas Croft’s successor in the neighbourhood was therefore an event of some interest. He was a Mr. Robert Ainsworth, a naval surgeon, who had been the closest friend and fellow-officer of Isabella’s late brother, Captain Edward Vane. His coming was announced by a letter, written in a clear, steady hand, requesting permission to pay his respects to the family of his late friend. Sir Giles, who considered the navy a profession for gentlemen’s younger sons and surgeons a class of men altogether below notice, gave a reluctant consent. He could not, in honour, refuse a request prompted by such a connexion; but he resolved to observe the young man with a critical eye, and to take the earliest opportunity of discouraging any pretensions he might form.

Mr. Ainsworth, when he arrived, proved to be a man of about thirty, of a quiet and thoughtful demeanour. His manners were not polished in the London style, but they were marked by a directness and sincerity that spoke of a life spent in more serious pursuits than the drawing-rooms of the fashionable world. He was not handsome, but his countenance was open and intelligent, and he spoke of his profession not as a trade, but as a science and a sacred duty. To Sir Giles’s pointed questions about his family and prospects, he answered with a frankness that bordered upon the blunt. He was the son of a country vicar; he had no fortune; his future depended entirely upon his skill and his continued employment in the King’s service. Sir Giles heard him out with a look of profound disapproval, and afterwards pronounced to Isabella that the man was a very respectable sort of person, no doubt, but that his situation rendered any thought of an intimacy quite out of the question.

Isabella, however, was not so easily persuaded. In Mr. Ainsworth’s company, she found a conversation that did not merely fill the silence, but animated her own thoughts. He spoke of his travels, of the strange maladies he had treated, and of the fortitude he had witnessed in men facing death far from home. He spoke of Edward with a warmth and an understanding that brought her brother vividly before her. He did not offer her the empty flatteries of Mr. Croft, nor the dull recitations of property that had characterised her other suitors. He asked for her opinion on books, on the state of the country, on the very nature of courage and sacrifice. For the first time, Isabella felt herself engaged not as an heiress to be won, but as a mind to be conversed with. She began to suspect that her long-cherished caution, which she had always believed to be the fruit of wisdom, might in truth be a more subtle form of fear—a fear of surrendering her independence to a man who would see her only as an ornament to his establishment.

The crisis arrived on a damp Tuesday afternoon. Sir Giles, having summoned Isabella to the library, announced that Mr. Ainsworth had made him a formal application for her hand. “I have told him, of course, that it is an impossibility,” Sir Giles declared, his face flushed with indignation. “A man without a penny, without family, without any prospect but the sea! It is an insult to your understanding to suppose you would consider it.” He then proceeded to lecture her upon her duty, upon the folly of sentiment, and upon the necessity of securing her fortune against the encroachment of adventurers.

Isabella listened in a silence that her guardian mistook for assent. When he had finished, she spoke, her voice calm and even. “Sir Giles,” she said, “I have always esteemed your counsel and respected your care of me. But in this matter, I must be guided by my own judgement.” She saw his face darken, but she continued. “You speak of Mr. Ainsworth’s want of fortune as an insuperable objection. But I have a fortune sufficient for us both. You speak of his lack of family. But he has a character, which I find far more estimable than a long and idle pedigree. His profession, which you consider beneath me, is one of honour and of essential service to mankind. He has seen the world in its harshest aspects, and it has not hardened him, but refined him.”
