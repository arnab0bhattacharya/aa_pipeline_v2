The letter had come at dusk, brought by a passing trader across the great northern mountains, and it lay now beneath my clasped hands on the narrow desk of my chamber. The wind without made a hollow moaning in the cloisters of the convent-garden; the shutter clashed against the stone wall, and a thin rain beat on the pane. Within, my small fire had sunk to a red, sullen glow, casting flickering shadows on the plaster image of the Virgin in the corner. I had read the letter once, and the words had seemed to enter my brain not as language, but as a sharp, sudden blow, driving the breath from my body. My brother—my only brother, the last tie that bound me to the old English rectory, to the voices and the hearth of my kindred—was dead. A fever, contracted in the marshes of his new parish in the western counties, had carried him off in three days. He was dead. I repeated the words to myself; I strove to force my mind to grasp the dire reality, but it reeled away, stunned and blinded. He, who had been so strong, so full of restless purpose, so fiery in his zeal, so gentle in his affection—he was struck down, and the grave had closed over him, and I was here, an alien in a foreign city, severed from all who could comprehend my grief.

I rose, and paced the narrow room. The floor was of cold, uncarpeted stone; the walls were damp; the air, breathing through the ill-fitted casement, struck to my very marrow. I knelt by the bed, but no prayer would frame itself on my stiffened lips. I could only gaze into the dark abyss of my bereavement, and feel that I was utterly alone. There was no human creature in this great, gloomy establishment who knew my heart. Madame Martigny, the directress, was a woman of correct rules and worldly prudence; she would offer me a half-day of leave, and think she had discharged the utmost debt of sympathy. The other teachers were aliens in language and in spirit; between us lay the barrier of cold customs and foreign ways. I was hemmed in by a hundred pupils, yet I was as solitary as if I stood on a rock in the midst of a raging sea.

The night wore on. I heard the convent clock strike one, strike two, strike three. The fire had died out entirely; the room was black and freezing. Yet I did not feel the cold; a feverish heat throbbed at my temples, and my hands burned. I thought of the clay-cold sod that would lie on his breast, of the darkness of the vault, of the silence that would swallow up his voice forever. I thought of his youth, of his noble aims, of the cruel, sudden arrest of all his hopes; and a cry of rebellion rose in my soul, a fierce, suffocating anguish that seemed to choke the very life in me. Why had this been permitted? Why was he taken, and I left to drag out my days in this dreary exile, severed from all that made existence dear? The question was vain; the heavens gave back no answer but the howling of the wind; and I bowed my head, and wept till my tears were spent, and a dull, hard calm settled on my spirit.

When the early bell rang for the duties of the day, I rose from my knees. My face was pale and lined, my eyes heavy with watching; but I washed away the traces of tears, I arranged my simple dress, and I descended to the schoolroom. I had a class to teach at eight; I had a lesson in English literature to give at ten; there was no remitting the round of toil, no pause in the machine. I must take my place at the desk, and speak of grammar, of syntax, of the forms of sentences, while my heart was riven and bleeding.

The great schoolroom of the Pensionnat Martigny was a long, vaulted apartment, with high windows that looked out on a narrow street, where the foreign sun slanted in and made bars of light on the polished floor. The hum of many voices filled the air; a hundred girls, in their dark uniforms, sat at their desks, and the rustle of papers, the scratch of pens, the murmur of whispered talk, made a confused, mingled sound. I took my seat at the little estrade, and opened my book. The page swam before my eyes; the letters danced and wavered. I fixed my gaze on the blackboard behind me, and strove to collect my scattered faculties.

The class came up—twelve girls, with bright, curious faces, and eyes that glanced at me with a quick, questioning look. They had noted, perhaps, my pallor, the dark circles beneath my eyes; but they were too young, too much engrossed in their own small world, to divine the truth. I began the lesson. My voice, at first, sounded strange and far-off in my ears, as if it came from another person; but I forced it into steadiness, I compelled it to utter the commonplaces of instruction. We read a passage from a book—some old English essay, grave and tranquil in its tone. The words fell from my lips, and each one seemed to carry a separate sting. I read of the vanity of human wishes, of the brevity of life, of the passing away of all earthly things; and the sentences, that had been to me but dull abstractions before, were now illuminated with a fiery meaning. I felt their truth in my very soul; they were written on my heart with a pen of fire.
