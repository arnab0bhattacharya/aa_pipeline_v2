In the kingdom of Valedore, where the fountains were said to weep petals instead of water and the very peacocks walked with the measured dignity of ambassadors, there reigned a king named Aurelian. He was a monarch of impeccable taste and immaculate melancholy. He had a profile that sculptors wept over, a collection of lapis lazuli that would have made a pope envious, and a profound, unassailable sorrow that had settled upon him like a winter frost that never thaws. He had never laughed. Not once, not in the whole of his long and gilded life. He had smiled, yes—a thin, wintry movement of the lips that was less an expression of joy than a concession to the demands of polite society—but the full, unbuttoned music of laughter had never once escaped him.

This absence was the great scandal of his court, the one flaw in an otherwise perfect jewel. His ministers, who were practical men, saw it as a threat to the stability of the realm. A laughing king, they reasoned, was a beloved king; a beloved king was a secure king. So Aurelian, in a moment of rare and royal whimsy, issued a proclamation. Half his kingdom—the half with the better vineyards and the more reliable sunshine—would be given to any soul who could make him laugh.

The news travelled through Valedore like a rumour of spring. From every corner of the land they came: jesters with bells on their caps and bells on their toes, who tumbled and capered and told jokes so old they had been inscribed on the walls of the first palace. Aurelian watched them with the weary courtesy of a man attending a funeral he had already paid for. He did not laugh.

Then came the poets, who composed odes to the absurdity of existence, sonnets to the cosmic joke, and epics on the theme of a flea who believed himself to be a philosopher. Their verses were elegant, their metaphors exquisite, and their understanding of the human heart profound. The king listened, nodded gravely, and commended their metres. He did not laugh.

Finally, the philosophers arrived. They came in robes of sombre grey, with long beards and longer arguments. They spoke of the nature of humour, the mechanics of mirth, and the essential ridiculousness of all things. One, a particularly bold thinker from the northern provinces, argued that laughter was merely a physiological response to a perceived incongruity, a sort of intellectual sneeze. The king found this interesting. He did not laugh.

And so the days passed, and the kingdom grew weary of the king’s unyielding gravity. The fountains seemed to weep more petulantly than before, and the peacocks dragged their tails with a heavier despair. It was in this season of universal gloom that a small kitchen maid named Elara was carrying a silver tray laden with fresh tarts from the palace ovens to the great hall. She was a girl of no particular consequence, with flour on her nose and a mind occupied entirely by the precise temperature at which pastry achieves its most sublime state of flakiness. She had not heard of the king’s proclamation; her world was one of ovens and rolling pins, and the great affairs of state were as remote to her as the moon.

The tarts were of a new and experimental design, filled with a compote of wild cherries and a hint of rosemary, and they were arranged with an artistry that Elara herself had conceived. She was thinking of this arrangement, of the perfect symmetry of the glazed red fruit against the golden crust, when her foot caught upon a slight unevenness in the marble floor.

The world, for a moment, became a cascade of silver and pastry. The tray tilted, the tarts flew, and Elara, with a cry of pure, uncalculated dismay, fell headlong into the very centre of the great hall, where King Aurelian sat upon his throne of ivory and gold. The tarts, in their descent, performed a sort of aerial ballet, a brief and beautiful flight before they landed—one upon the king’s sceptre, one upon his crown, and the rest in a glorious, chaotic splatter across the polished floor. A single, perfect cherry rolled to a stop at the very tip of the royal shoe.

There was a silence. It was not the silence of a tomb, but the silence of a held breath, the silence that precedes either a thunderclap or a miracle. The courtiers turned pale. The ministers prepared to look the other way. Elara, her face crimson with shame, scrambled to her knees, her hands covered in cherry juice and crumbs, and began to stammer an apology that was more a series of incoherent squeaks than words.

And then it happened.

It began as a low sound, a sort of rumble in the royal chest, as if some long-dormant engine had been coaxed to life. It grew, it swelled, it gathered momentum. It was not a refined laugh, not a courtly titter. It was a great, gusty, unseemly roar of laughter, a sound of pure, unadulterated delight that echoed from the frescoed ceiling to the marble floor. The king, with a tart upon his crown and a cherry at his foot, was laughing. He was laughing at the sheer, magnificent absurdity of it all—at the ruined tarts, at the girl’s horrified face, at the solemn dignity of his own court, and most of all, at himself, a king who had sought laughter in wit and philosophy and had found it only in the chaotic poetry of a dropped tray.

He laughed until tears streamed down his face, until his sides ached, until the entire court, first in astonishment and then in relief, began to laugh with him. The fountains outside seemed to gurgle with a new, more joyful note.

When the mirth had finally subsided, the king wiped his eyes and looked down at the trembling kitchen maid. He was still smiling, a real smile, a smile that reached his eyes and warmed them like the sun.
