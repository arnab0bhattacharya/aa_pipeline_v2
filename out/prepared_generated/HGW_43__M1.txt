I had lost my way in a fog that had, by that time, settled upon London with the grim permanence of a geological stratum. For three weeks the city had been entombed in a yellow, dripping vapour that was not so much a weather as a condition. The familiar world had been replaced by a blind, groping chaos. The sun was a rumour, the stars a forgotten legend. Omnibuses had ceased to run; cabs were as rare and as expensive as diamonds. The great arteries of commerce, the Thames and the railways, had seized up, and the city’s life had contracted to a series of disconnected, shivering cells. In this suffocating blindness, I, Arthur Pym, a clerk of no particular distinction at the Consolidated Bank of Albion, had ventured out for a physician’s prescription and had become hopelessly, irretrievably lost.

I had stumbled for hours, my hands outstretched before me, my shoes squelching in a filth that was no longer mere mud but a kind of primordial ooze. The gas lamps, when I chanced upon them, were but pale, jaundiced blisters in the gloom. It was a sound that ultimately guided me—the throb of a gramophone, tinny and defiant, playing a music-hall ditty. Following it, I found myself descending a short flight of stone steps into a basement lit by a single, hissing gas-jet. The air was thick with the smell of damp wool, cheap tobacco, and something hot and savoury from a pan on a small stove. A dozen or so men and a couple of women were gathered there, their faces pale and earnest in the gloom. They looked up as I stumbled in, my spectacles fogged, my bowler hat a sodden ruin.

“Here’s another refugee from the surface,” said a large man with a magnificent red beard, who was evidently the master of the proceedings. His voice was deep and carried the ring of a public speaker. “Come in, brother, come in. The fog is no respecter of persons, but it is a great leveller.”

I stammered an apology, explaining that I was a bank clerk, that I had lost my way, that I meant no intrusion.

“A bank clerk!” roared the man with the beard. “Excellent! A man who understands the machinery of capital. Sit down, friend. We are the Islington Branch of the Socialist League for Immediate Reconstruction, and we are debating the very soul of the nation in this cellar.”

His name, I learned, was Silas Kettle. He was a fitter by trade and a demagogue by vocation. Beside him sat a thin, intense young woman with spectacles and a severe haircut, Miss Eleanor Vance, who spoke with a quiet, cutting precision that was more alarming than Kettle’s bombast. There were others: a docker with hands like shovels, a pale young poet who was sketching the scene on a notepad, and a variety of earnest, threadbare individuals who nodded at every pronouncement. They were debating, with a great deal of heat and very little light, whether the fog was the opportunity they had been waiting for.

“The city is paralysed!” Kettle declared, thumping the table so that the teacups jumped. “The masters are blind! The police are helpless! The machinery of oppression is stalled! This is not a disaster; it is a summons. The conditions for a general strike, for a seizure of the means of distribution, have never been more favourable!”

“But we cannot see to distribute anything ourselves, Silas,” countered Miss Vance, her voice dry. “We cannot even see from one street-lamp to the next. To seize the docks would be merely to seize a collection of sounds in the dark. It is a practical impossibility.”

“It is a crisis of the spirit!” cried the young poet. “The external world is obscured that we may see the inner light!”

The debate raged on. They argued about the proletariat, the bourgeoisie, the historic imperative, and the price of bread, which had trebled. They were, I thought, a collection of cranks and visionaries, full of a fine, impractical fire. I was huddled on a barrel in the corner, hoping to remain unnoticed until I could decently slip away. My greatest concern was the state of my trousers and the fact that my wife, Agnes, would be frantic with worry.

It was the question of the minutes that brought me into the affair. The previous secretary, a timid man named Abernathy, had apparently deserted his post, and the League’s records were in a state of chaos. They had motions without seconds and resolutions without dates.

“We must have order!” Miss Vance insisted. “We cannot remake society if we cannot keep our own books. We need a secretary. Someone with method. Someone who understands figures.”

All eyes, slow and dreadful, turned towards me.

“No, no,” I said, my voice a dry whisper. “I’m only a clerk. I know nothing of… of political economy.”

“Nonsense!” boomed Kettle, striding over and clapping me on the shoulder with a force that nearly sent me off my barrel. “A clerk is exactly what we need! A man of method! A man of precision! You see before you the forces of history, friend, and they are in desperate need of a filing system. What is your name?”

“Pym,” I stammered. “Arthur Pym.”

“Comrade Pym!” he announced to the room. “I propose that our new friend, Arthur Pym, be elected secretary of this branch, *pro tem*.”

There was a general chorus of “Hear, hear!” and “Seconded!” It was a madhouse. I tried to protest, to explain that I was a loyal servant of the Consolidated Bank of Albion, that I had a wife and a cat, that my political sympathies were, at best, of the mildest Liberal sort. But my objections were lost in a wave of revolutionary enthusiasm. Before I knew what was happening, a grubby notebook and a chewed pencil were thrust into my hands, and I was directed to record the motion.
