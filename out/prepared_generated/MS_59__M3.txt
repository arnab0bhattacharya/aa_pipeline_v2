The autumnal equinox had passed, yet the season of decay had made fearful haste upon the plains of Middlesex, enveloping the earth in a shroud of grey vapour and dead leaves, as if nature herself mourned the discovery that had blasted my youth. I was journeying towards a spot which, though scarce ten miles distant from the teeming heart of London, seemed separated from the abodes of men by a gulf impassable, a wilderness of woe. The carriage jolted heavily over the deep-rutted roads, which wound through desolate commons and trackless heaths, where the blasts of coming winter howled among the leafless branches, and the rain beat with melancholy violence against the windows of my vehicle. I looked forth upon the dreary prospect, and the gloom of the external world harmonized too truly with the anguish of my soul. My mind was a prey to the most agonizing reflections. Four-and-twenty hours had scarcely elapsed since I had been torn from the bosom of a cherished delusion, which had been carefully woven around me from my birth. I had been taught to believe that my mother had sunk under the exhaustion of my natal hour, and that her grave, far in the north of England, was sanctified by the tears of my bereaved father. Alas! the grave was empty, the tears were a mockery, and she, whom I had so often invoked in the silent agony of filial love, was a living tenant of a madhouse. My father! the author of my being, the guide of my youth, he had perpetrated this deed. He had torn the partner of his bosom from the light of day, and immured her in the dungeons of insanity, to cover his own guilt and pander to his selfish desires. The revelation had been made to me by the dying confession of a domestic, who had long been in the family, and who, with the tremulous malice of a departing spirit, had revealed the secret. My father had represented her as a maniac, and had thus, by the force of wealth and artifice, procured her confinement in the asylum of Dunmore, a place of gloomy fame, situated on a wild heath, near the banks of the Thames. I had left my father's roof in the dead of night, with a heart boiling with indignation and despair, and I was now hastening to rescue, if possible, the victim of his cruelty.

The carriage stopped at the gate of a long, low building, surrounded by high walls, over which the ivy clustered in wild luxuriance, as if to hide the miseries within from the eye of heaven. I alighted, and, passing through the iron wicket, found myself in a court, where a few sickly trees drooped their heads, and the wind sighed through their branches with a sound of sorrow. I rang the bell at the inner door, and it was opened by the keeper, a man of heavy and sullen aspect, whose features bore the stamp of his brutalizing occupation. I demanded to see the lady who had been confined there twenty years before under the name of Mrs. Mornay. He looked at me with surprise, and then, with a malicious grin, replied that she was still living, but that few persons ever inquired after her. He led me through a long, damp, and ill-lighted passage, the walls of which were damp with moisture, and the air loaded with the effluvia of the place. We passed several doors, from behind which came sounds that made my blood run cold—the laugh of frenzy, the shriek of despair, and the low, unintelligible moan of idiocy. At length we stopped before a door in the remotest corner of the building, and the keeper, unlocking it, motioned me to enter. I did so, and found myself in a small, scantily furnished room, with barred windows, looking out upon a wretched garden. Seated by the fire, in a large armchair, was a woman, whose appearance at once riveted my gaze and thrilled my heart with a mixture of love and horror. She was terribly altered, indeed, from what I had been led to conceive of her; her form was emaciated, her cheek was pale and hollow, and her dark eyes, once bright and beautiful, were wild and unsettled. Her hair, which hung in dishevelled masses over her shoulders, was interspersed with many grey hairs, and her dress, though clean, was of the coarsest kind. She looked up as I entered, and fixed her eyes upon me with a keen and searching glance, that seemed to penetrate my very soul. I advanced towards her, and, falling on my knees, I exclaimed, "My mother! oh, my mother!" She started, and a faint flush passed over her features; she laid her hand upon my head, and, passing her fingers through my hair, murmured, "Henry! Henry!" The sound of my name, uttered by lips that had been silent to me for twenty years, overcame me. I wept aloud, and clung to her knees in an agony of tenderness. She seemed to understand my emotion, and she smiled, a smile of such touching sweetness, that it banished, for a moment, the wildness of her look, and brought back the beauty of her happier days.

"My son," she said, in a voice that was musical even in its sorrow, "you have come at last. I knew that you would come. I have watched for you, and prayed for you, through the long, weary nights, when the wind howled round my prison-house, and the owls hooted in the old ivy. They told me that you were dead, but I knew that it was a lie, for I saw you in my dreams, growing up to manhood, strong and fair, and coming to deliver me. And now you are here, and we shall go away together, far away, to some peaceful land, where the wicked cease from troubling, and the weary are at rest." I could not reply; my heart was too full for utterance.
