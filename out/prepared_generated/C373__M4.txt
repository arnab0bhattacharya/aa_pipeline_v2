The rain had been falling since matins, a steady, sullen drizzle that turned the cobbles of the Graben into a sheet of greasy jet and made the spires of St. Leopold’s look like charcoal sketches against a soiled sky. From the window of the parlour in the Hôtel d’Albion, Miss Eleanor Vance watched a lone fiacre rattle past, its driver hunched and miserable beneath an oilskin cape. The hotel, that afternoon, felt like a ship in a bottle: a little, airless world of English chintz and coal smoke, becalmed in the heart of a foreign city.

“You are philosophical, Miss Vance,” a voice said from the doorway. “I had not thought the study of modern languages to be so conducive to melancholy.”

She turned. Mr. Cornelius Thorne stood there, a glass of sherry in his hand, his travelling coat still damp at the shoulders. He was a man of perhaps forty, with a ruddy, good-humoured face and the comfortable, well-tailored look of one who has never known a day of true inconvenience. He had arrived in the city a fortnight ago, a gentleman of leisure making a desultory tour of the Continent, and had installed himself at the d’Albion as though it were his club.

“It is not melancholy, Mr. Thorne,” Eleanor replied, managing a small smile. “It is merely the weather. It makes one consider the merits of a good fire.”

“And a good fire is precisely what I have ordered in the reading room,” he said, advancing into the parlour. “I have also taken the liberty of securing a fresh pot of tea. I find the coffee here has a most alarming effect upon the nerves. It is not at all the thing.”

She allowed him to lead her to the reading room, a space of dark panelling and worn leather chairs, where a fire crackled with a cheerful, imported vigour. It was his way, she had learned, to arrange comfort around himself with the same unthinking ease that other men arranged their affairs. He was a man accustomed to being pleased, and to pleasing.

Eleanor had been in the city for two years, engaged as a governess to the two young daughters of a merchant family, the Kellers. The position was respectable, the salary adequate, and the loneliness profound. She had no family to speak of and few friends in England; her life was a narrow, respectable corridor. The Hôtel d’Albion, with its English newspapers and its familiar tea, had become her refuge, and Mr. Thorne, with his talk of London and his easy manners, a welcome distraction.

“I had a letter from my man of business today,” he said, settling into the chair opposite her. “He writes that the new railway line from London to Dover is now quite complete. Imagine it! One can be at the coast in a matter of hours. It quite annihilates the romance of the journey.”

“I imagine it saves a great deal of time,” Eleanor said, pouring the tea.

“Time, yes. But what is one to do with all this time one saves?” He looked at her over the rim of his teacup. “I find myself at a loss. I have seen the cathedral. I have seen the picture gallery. I have even, God help me, attended a performance of *The Magic Flute* in German. The city is very fine, Miss Vance, but it is not London.”

“London is very fine, too,” she said. “I have not seen it in some years.”

“Then you must see it again,” he said, and his tone, for a moment, lost its jesting quality. “You must let me show it to you. The new buildings, the parks… it is a different city from the one you left.”

She felt a flush creep up her neck. “You are very kind, Mr. Thorne, but my place is here.”

“Your place,” he said, leaning forward. “Forgive me, but I cannot think that this is your place. A woman of your intelligence, your… spirit, buried in a schoolroom in a foreign town. It is a waste, Miss Vance. A dreadful waste.”

He set down his cup with a decisive click. “I am a plain man,” he said, though he was nothing of the sort. “And I have a plain proposition. I am returning to England at the end of the month. I have a house in Surrey, a small estate. It is quiet, but not dull. I have a library, and a garden that is the despair of my neighbours. What I do not have is a mistress for it.”

Eleanor stared at him. The firelight played on his face, making his eyes seem very bright and earnest. Her heart gave a sudden, painful lurch. It was not love, she knew, but it was something. It was the shock of a door being flung open in a dark room.

“Mr. Thorne,” she began, her voice unsteady, “you cannot be serious.”

“I have never been more serious in my life,” he said. “I am not a young man, and I am not a romantic one. But I know what I see. I see a woman who is lonely, as I am lonely. I see a woman who is proud, and who is perhaps afraid. And I see a woman who could be happy, if she would only allow herself to be. Come home with me, Eleanor. As my wife.”

The use of her Christian name was a small, intimate shock. She looked away, into the heart of the fire. The offer was absurd. It was the stuff of a circulating library novel. A wealthy traveller, a governess, a proposal of marriage… it was a fairy tale. And yet, she suspected, it was also a kind of game. He was bored. The cathedral was seen, the gallery was seen. She was a new sight, a new puzzle. He was a man who collected experiences, and she was a curious one: an Englishwoman alone, a governess with a sharp tongue and a sad history.
