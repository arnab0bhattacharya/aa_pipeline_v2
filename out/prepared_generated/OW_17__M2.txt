Lady Agatha Vane was a woman of such terrifyingly precise eccentricities that her relatives did not merely fear her, but respected her with the wary affection one feels for a landmine that has not yet exploded. She lived in a house in Bayswater that smelled permanently of camphor and expensive, wilting lilies, and she held the unshakable, and entirely unfounded, belief that she possessed the ability to converse with the deceased. Her nephews, Julian and Arthur, were men of the modern world, equipped with the cold, clear logic of the age, which is to say they believed in nothing but the stock exchange and the latest models of motorcars. They had come to Bayswater to inherit a fortune, or at least to witness its squandering, and found themselves instead trapped in a room that had been stripped of all comfort and draped in black silk.

The air in the drawing room was thick with the scent of myrrh and the heavier, more oppressive scent of Julian’s suppressed amusement. Lady Agatha sat in the center of the room, her hands clasped over a velvet cushion, her eyes closed in a posture of such profound self-importance that it seemed less like spiritual receptivity and more like a cat catching a sunbeam. The candles flickered, not with the gentle, natural dance of a flame disturbed by a draft, but with a jerky, unnatural rhythm that suggested the room was being vented by a hidden fan, or perhaps by the collective nervousness of the two men who sat in the high-backed chairs at either side of her.

“We are ready, my dears,” Lady Agatha said, her voice dropping to a register that was intended to be ethereal but sounded largely like a congested throat. “The veil between the worlds is thin tonight. The spirits are impatient. They say you are late.”

Julian checked his watch, a gold piece that had cost more than the house’s annual heating bill. “We are precisely on time, Aunt Agatha. It is seven o’clock. The spirit, if it is indeed our grandfather, will find that he is waiting for us, not the other way around. Grandfather was a man of punctuality. He believed that punctuality was the politeness of kings, and that lateness was merely a polite form of theft.”

Arthur, who was younger and possessed of a more delicate constitution, shifted uncomfortably in his chair. He was a man who preferred his mysteries to be literary, not atmospheric. He watched the shadows in the corners of the room with the unease of a man who suspects he has been invited to a dinner party where the host has been known to serve raw oysters to vegetarians. “Perhaps, Julian,” Arthur whispered, “we should simply wait. If the spirit is truly there, it will make itself known. We need not provoke it with our cleverness. It is rude to interrogate the dead, and it is worse to bore them with our own opinions.”

Lady Agatha opened one eye. “Bore them? My dear Arthur, the dead have nothing to do but listen. It is the living who are busy. But do not interrupt the communication. I feel a presence. It is cold. It smells of tobacco and old regrets.”

A chill wind, undeniably unnatural, swept through the room, extinguishing three of the five candles in a single, violent gust. The remaining two swayed dangerously, their flames turning a sickly, pale green. In the sudden darkness, the room seemed to contract, the shadows lengthening into shapes that defied geometry.

“Grandfather?” Lady Agatha called out, her voice trembling with a mixture of fear and theatrical delight. “Is it you? Do you remember me? I was the one who painted your portrait. You said I had the eyes of a demon and the soul of a saint. I have always preferred the second description, but I am fond of the first.”

From the darkest corner of the room, where the heavy curtains gathered like the folds of a burial shroud, a voice spoke. It was a low, rumbling sound, like the grinding of millstones, or perhaps the clearing of a throat that had been dry for a century.

“I remember,” the voice said. “And I remember the debt. Sit down, children. We have matters to discuss. I have come to collect.”

Julian leaned forward, his face illuminated by the greenish glow of the remaining candles. He did not look afraid. He looked analytical. He was looking for the source of the voice, for the mechanism, for the trick. In his experience, the world was a machine, and if it made a noise, it had a gear that had slipped.

“Collect what, Grandfather?” Julian asked, his tone perfectly reasonable, as if he were inquiring about a missing umbrella. “You have been dead for twenty years. You cannot collect money from the grave. The bank does not honor checks written in blood, and I am not a man who pays for secrets with my soul. I pay with checks, and even then, I prefer them to be made out to a living account holder.”

The voice chuckled, a dry, rustling sound. “You are a clever boy, Julian. But cleverness is a poor shield against truth. I know what you did in Paris. I know about the girl. I know about the money you took from the estate before you even knew it was yours. I know the stain on your conscience, and it is a darker color than the silk on the walls.”

Julian smiled, a thin, sharp expression that did not reach his eyes. “Paris was a mistake, certainly, but it was a very fashionable mistake. And the money? I was simply managing the assets until the lawyers could sort out the mess you left behind. I am an accountant, Grandfather. I do not steal. I allocate. There is a difference, and it is a very important one in the City. I do not think you, who spent your life in the country and your evenings in the club, would understand the nuance of fiscal responsibility.”
