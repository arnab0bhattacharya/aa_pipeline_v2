It is a truth not sufficiently acknowledged, that the most rigid determinations of the heart may be shaken by the mere report of a name. Miss Harriet Ashworth, who had long ago resolved to think of Mr. Edward Vance with a degree of civil contempt proportionate to his offence against her brother, found this resolution entirely overthrown at a crowded card party in Grosvenor Square. The cause of this sudden revolution was not the gentleman himself, who was happily absent, but the mention of him by her hostess, Lady Almeria, as a person of consequence lately returned to town.

Harriet had been prepared to hear the name and to feel nothing. The quarrel between Mr. Vance and her brother, Captain Ashworth, was an old story, a relic of their shared youth at Eton, of which she knew only the general outlines. Captain Ashworth, a man of unimpeachable honour and a temper to match, had broken with his friend over some matter of such delicacy that he would never speak of it, contenting himself with the assertion that Mr. Vance had acted in a manner no gentleman could forgive. For seven years, the name of Vance had been a closed door in the family conversation. To hear it now, spoken in Lady Almeria’s light and worldly tones, was to have that door flung open upon a scene she could not picture.

“He is quite the most sought-after man of the season,” Lady Almeria was saying to a small circle of ladies. “His fortune is handsome, of course, but it is his air of melancholy that gives him such a fascination. One feels there is a history there, a story of wounded sensibility. He refuses to speak of it, which only makes it more interesting.”

Harriet felt a pang of indignation so sharp it was almost a physical sensation. A story of wounded sensibility! It was her brother who had been wounded, her family whose name had been implicated in a disgrace they did not understand. That Mr. Vance should now be trading upon this very mystery, cultivating an atmosphere of romantic suffering to enhance his social standing, was a form of vanity so profound it struck her as a moral failing. She listened with a fixed smile as Lady Almeria detailed Mr. Vance’s accomplishments, his taste in art, his patronage of a certain Italian composer, and felt her opinion of him harden into a contempt far more personal than before. She did not know the cause of the quarrel, but she was now certain of its justice.

It was therefore with a sense of grim necessity, rather than any social inclination, that she accepted Lady Almeria’s invitation to a musical evening a fortnight later, knowing Mr. Vance would be of the party. She went with the intention of observing him, of finding in his manner some confirmation of the duplicity she had already assigned to him. She would be civil, for her brother’s sake required that no new cause for gossip be given, but she would be watchful.

The rooms were filled with the usual assortment of London society: dowagers of formidable consequence, young ladies displaying their accomplishments with varying degrees of success, and gentlemen who moved among them with an air of critical appraisal. Harriet was engaged in a conversation of exquisite dullness with a Mr. Finch, who was explaining the genealogy of his new carriage horses, when Lady Almeria approached, bringing a gentleman with her.

“Miss Ashworth,” she said, her eyes bright with the pleasure of a successful introduction, “allow me to present Mr. Vance. Mr. Vance, this is Miss Ashworth, the sister of your old friend, Captain Ashworth.”

For a moment, Harriet could only observe him. He was not at all as she had pictured. There was no sign of the practiced melancholy Lady Almeria had described. His face was intelligent and open, his manner composed, and his bow held a degree of natural grace that spoke of good breeding rather than studied effect. His eyes, a clear and serious grey, met hers with a frankness that was disconcerting. There was no shadow of a history in them, only a sudden, sharp attention.

“Miss Ashworth,” he said, and his voice was low and even. “It is a great pleasure.”

“The pleasure is mine, Mr. Vance,” she replied, her own voice sounding strangely formal to her ears.

An awkward silence fell, which Mr. Finch was mercifully too obtuse to notice. Mr. Vance, however, seemed to feel it keenly. He looked at her as if he were searching for something in her countenance—forgiveness, perhaps, or condemnation—and finding neither.

“I trust,” he began, after a pause that stretched the bounds of politeness, “that your brother is well? I have not had the pleasure of seeing him for some years.”

“He is very well, I thank you,” said Harriet. The words were correct, but they were delivered with a coolness that was an answer in itself.

A faint colour rose in Mr. Vance’s cheeks, and he looked down for a moment. “I am glad to hear it.” He looked up again, and his gaze was direct. “I am aware, Miss Ashworth, that our last parting was not a happy one. I would not wish you to think—”

“Mr. Vance,” Harriet interrupted, her tone gentle but firm, “it is an old matter, and one I am sure my brother would not wish to be revived. Let us speak of something else.”

She had intended to close the subject, to put him at a distance with a show of magnanimity. But the effect was the opposite of what she had planned. His face, already serious, grew graver still. He saw her words not as a release, but as a refusal to understand.

“Perhaps you are right,” he said quietly. “But it is a matter that has not been so easily forgotten by me. I would not have you form an opinion of me from a partial account.”
