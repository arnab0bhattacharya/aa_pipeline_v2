In that venerable and overgrown cul-de-sac of Soho, known to the wandering pedestrian as Pump-Blossom Court, there stood, in the autumn of the year seventeen hundred and ninety-three, a tall, melancholy house. It was a house that wore its plaster like peeling scales, and whose windows so protruded over the uneven pavement that they seemed to be perpetually craning their necks to listen to the quarrels of the scavenging dogs below. The lodging-house belonged to Mrs. Grumbleton, a widow of a martial and unyielding countenance, who carried her chin like a battering-ram and surveyed her domain with an eye that could spot a missing farthing in a publican’s till at forty paces.

It was within the narrow confines of the two-pair back of this edifice that Monsieur Leblanc, a gentleman of French extraction and most unimpeachable politeness, imparted the graceful arts of the minuet, the gavotte, and the allemande to the aspiring youth of the neighbourhood. Monsieur Leblanc was a man of a singularly wistful and languid disposition. He was a little man, very lean, who carried his head deferentially on one side, as if apologizing to the universe for taking up any portion of its atmosphere. He wore a faded coat of plum-coloured velvet, which had seen such a multitude of repairs that it resembled a geological map of some fictitious, threadbare country; and he possessed a pair of thin legs, which, when enclosed in his silken breeches, seemed to have been expressly designed by Providence to illustrate the principle of the tibia. When he was not dancing, he sighed; and when he was not sighing, he sneezed; for Pump-Blossom Court was damp, and Monsieur Leblanc’s constitution was as delicate as his steps were precise.

Upon the particular evening to which this narrative refers, Monsieur Leblanc was engaged in the melancholy task of counting his weekly earnings—twelve shillings and fourpence-halfpenny, principally in copper, which chinked with a sound pathetically disproportionate to the mental agonies they had cost him—when a heavy and imperative knocking shook his chamber door.

‘Enter, if you please!’ cried the dancing-master, starting so violently that a shower of halfpence cascaded upon the floorboards, rolling into corners with an ironical and mocking activity.

The door opened, not to reveal Mrs. Grumbleton, but a stranger; a man of a heavy, sullen, and earthy aspect, enveloped in a shaggy greatcoat, which appeared to have been recently employed in the transportation of root vegetables. He wore his hat very much on the back of his head, and carried in his hand a walking-stick of prodigious thickness, which he immediately planted firmly upon the floor, as if taking formal possession of the apartment in the name of whatever dark power he represented.

‘Monsieur Leblanc?’ said the stranger, in a voice like the grating of a rusty hinge.

‘At your humble service, Monsieur,’ replied the dancing-master, executing, from sheer force of habit, a profound and tremulous bow. ‘I am the individual. To what am I indebted for the extreme honour——’

‘No honour at all,’ interrupted the visitor. ‘I am come from Bercy-sur-Marne.’

At the sound of that name, Monsieur Leblanc’s pale face underwent a violent revolution. He clutched the back of a chair, and his knees, so flexible but a moment before in the service of Terpsichore, knocked together with a convulsive and irreverent clatter.

‘Bercy-sur-Marne!’ he faintly articulated. ‘Mon Dieu! My brother! Auguste! What of him? He is in the prison of the Abbaye. The sans-culottes, the terrible sans-culottes——’

‘Just so,’ said the stranger, sitting down unbidden, and depositing his hat upon the table, where it reposed with an air of insolent proprietorship. ‘He is in the Abbaye. And a pretty pass he has come to. They are sharp work with the guillotine down there, Monsieur. Sharp as a razor. Trinc, trinc, trinc! and a head drops into the basket like a ripe apple from a bough.’

‘Merciful heaven!’ cried Monsieur Leblanc, wringing his hands in an agony of distress, and pacing the room with a wildness of gesture that would have ruined his reputation in every drawing-room in Soho. ‘Do not, I implore you, speak so terrifically! Auguste! My little Auguste! And you have news of him? You have escaped from that dreadful country? Oh, the monsters! The tigers! The——’

‘The Citizen Robespierre,’ proceeded the stranger, entirely unmoved by this theatrical display, ‘is a very inconvenient sort of gentleman for your brother to be mixed up with. But I have a cousin who is an official at the Abbaye. He is a man of influence. He is a man who can turn a key, and wink at a passport. He says that for a consideration—for a very moderate and reasonable consideration, mind you—Auguste Leblanc might find himself on the high road to Dover before the month is out.’

‘A consideration?’ echoed Monsieur Leblanc, pausing in his distraught march, and looking anxiously at the emissary. ‘A sum of money, you would say? Alas, Monsieur, I am but a poor teacher of the dance! I live by the civility of my patrons! I have saved—Heaven knows how, by denying myself the very necessaries of life—I have saved——’

‘Fifteen pounds,’ said the stranger, with a hard, metallic emphasis on the sum.

‘Fifteen pounds!’ repeated the poor Frenchman, throwing up his hands as if the figure had struck him a physical blow beneath the chin. ‘But it is a fortune! It is a palace! It is——’

‘It is the price of your brother’s head,’ returned the stranger, doggedly. ‘Take it or leave it. The Citizen Fouquier-Tinville is not a man to wait. Trinc, trinc, goes the blade; and where is your brother? A deal shorter by the shoulders, Monsieur, and no more to be said about it.’

Monsieur Leblanc, overcome by the vividness of this dreadful picture, sank into his armchair, and buried his face in his hands, murmuring piteously in his native tongue, while his shoulders heaved with a grief that was terrible to behold.
