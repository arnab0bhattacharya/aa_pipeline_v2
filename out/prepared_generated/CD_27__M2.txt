The air in the Old Bailey was thick, not merely with the smoke of a thousand piped tobacco and the dust of centuries, but with a sort of moral stagnation that seemed to press down upon the heads of the spectators like a heavy woollen cap. It was a Tuesday morning, grey and drizzling, the kind of day that makes the cobblestones of London look as though they have been weeping tears of tar and sorrow. In the dock, a man of such colossal and undeniable physicality that he seemed less a defendant than a geological formation, stood with his shoulders hunched. He was Thomas Grime, a coal-heaver by trade, and a man of such limited intellect that even the most complex of legal arguments might have been lost on him if they were delivered in English, let alone the legalese which flowed from the counsel for the prosecution like a muddy stream from a broken pipe.

The prosecution’s case was simple, brutal, and damning. A crate of prime Welsh coal had vanished from the yard of Mr. Silas Vane, a merchant of considerable reputation and even more considerable avarice. Grime had been seen near the yard; Grime had coal dust on his boots; Grime had the strength to carry a crate. It was, in the eyes of the jury, a case of such overwhelming evidence that the only question remaining was whether the sentence would be transportation or the noose. The judge, a man of severe countenance and a wig that appeared to have been ironed flat by the sheer weight of his own judicial frown, looked down upon the defendant with an expression that suggested he had already calculated the cost of the rope.

In the bar, young Arthur Pym, a solicitor of twenty-four years, with a face as pale as a sheet of unsanded paper and a heart that beat with a frequency that threatened to shake the very bench, watched the proceedings with a mixture of terror and professional despair. He had taken the case because the fee was modest and the client was pitiable, but now, as the prosecutor wound up his speech with the flourish of a conductor leading an orchestra of injustice, Pym felt his legs trembling beneath the bench. He had no defense. He had no witnesses. He had nothing but the desperate, flapping hope that a miracle might occur, which in the law courts is usually synonymous with a mistrial, and even that was a slim prospect.

It was at this precise juncture, when the silence in the court was so profound that one could hear the pin drop of a juror’s conscience, that a commotion occurred at the back of the gallery. It was not a loud disturbance, but a shuffling, rustling affair, accompanied by the sharp click of a boot heel on the stone floor. The sound was trivial, insignificant, a mere footstep in the vast echo of the courtroom. Yet, to the ears of the boy sent by the clerk to deliver a summons to the opposing counsel, it was a thunderclap.

The boy, a creature of such extreme youth and thinness that he appeared to be constructed entirely of joints and string, had been standing in the shadows behind a pillar. His name was Jemmy, and he possessed a pair of eyes that saw everything and judged nothing. He had been watching the clerk for the defense, Mr. Vane’s own man, a sour-faced individual named Barnaby, who sat in the solicitor’s box. Barnaby was a man of few words and fewer manners, whose primary interest in the trial seemed to be the preservation of his employer’s assets and his own dignity.

Jemmy watched Barnaby as the latter shifted in his seat, a movement that was more of a twitch than a turn. In doing so, Barnaby’s leg extended slightly, and the light from the high, grimy windows caught the leather of his new boots. They were not old boots. They were not worn boots. They were not the scuffed, mud-caked footwear of a clerk who spent his days walking the damp streets of Wapping. They were fresh, bright, and remarkably expensive. They were the kind of boots that cost a week’s wages for a coal-heaver, and they bore, upon the toe of the left one, a faint, reddish stain that Jemmy, with the intuition of a stray cat, recognized instantly as the distinctive clay-red dust of the Vane coal yard.

Jemmy did not shout. He did not point. He simply turned to the nearest usher, a man of immense girth and limited mobility, and whispered, “Sir, the clerk’s boots are new. And red.”

The usher, who had been dozing, woke with a start. He looked at Jemmy, then at Barnaby, then at the judge. The look passed from one to the other like a spark of electricity. The judge, whose eyes had been closed in a moment of judicial fatigue, opened them. He saw the movement. He saw the red dust. He saw the boy’s face, which was a mask of terrified certainty.

The atmosphere in the room changed. The moral stagnation broke. The air seemed to inhale sharply. The judge leaned forward, his wig no longer flat but bristling with sudden alertness. “Mr. Vane’s clerk,” he said, his voice low and dangerous. “Explain the condition of your footwear.”

Barnaby, who had been in the act of biting the nail of his thumb, froze. His face, previously a study in smug indifference, drained of color until it matched the pallor of the judge’s wig. He looked at his boots. He looked at the judge. He looked at Jemmy. In that moment, the truth of his innocence was as clear as the drizzle outside, and the guilt of his employer was as dark as the coal they were accused of stealing.
