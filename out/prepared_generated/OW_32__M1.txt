The committee of the Society for the Advancement of Plain Living had, with a fortitude bordering on the heroic, assembled in the Lecture Hall of the Kensington Institute for Useful Knowledge. The hall itself was a masterpiece of moral earnestness, a temple of Victorian virtue constructed in the most unornamented brick, where the very air seemed to taste of oatmeal and duty. Here, beneath the disapproving gaze of a portrait of the late Duke of Wellington, the ladies prepared for their great intellectual triumph. They had, in a moment of sublime strategic genius, invited the celebrated aesthete, Mr. Cecil Thorne, to speak against the principles they held dear.

“It is a stroke of pure genius, Mrs. Arbuthnot,” chirped Miss Pringle, a lady whose bonnet was a testament to the triumph of austerity over art. “To have the enemy himself come and lay bare his own absurdities! He will speak of beauty and pleasure, and then we shall stand up, one by one, and refute him with the undeniable truths of economy, temperance, and common sense.”

Mrs. Arbuthnot, a formidable woman whose face was a triumph of principle over prettiness, nodded with the serene confidence of one who has never once doubted the efficacy of a well-argued point. “Precisely. His very presence, with all that silken nonsense he represents, will serve as the perfect foil. The audience will see the hollowness of his creed by simply looking at him, and then my closing remarks will deliver the final, logical blow.”

Mr. Cecil Thorne arrived not with a bang, but with the quiet rustle of exquisite fabric. He was a vision in dove-grey silk, with a single, perfect gardenia in his buttonhole, and he surveyed the severe assembly with an air of gentle, almost pitying, amusement. He did not seem the least bit daunted by the hostile territory; rather, he appeared to be an anthropologist who had discovered a particularly fascinating, if unattractive, tribe.

“Ladies,” he began, his voice a low, musical murmur that seemed to make the very gaslight flicker with pleasure. “I am told you wish me to speak against the virtues of Plain Living. It is a task of some difficulty, for I find I cannot speak against it at all. Plain Living is, I assure you, the most extravagant luxury in the world.”

A ripple of confusion went through the committee. Miss Pringle fanned herself, not from heat, but from intellectual vertigo.

“Consider,” Mr. Thorne continued, pacing slowly before them, “the immense cost of plainness. To live plainly requires an income of considerable magnitude. The coarse wool, the unadorned wood, the severe chair that costs more than a thousand embroidered cushions—it all speaks of a wealth that can afford to be indifferent to beauty. A man who lives plainly is a man who can afford to dismiss the entire world of art and sensation as a trifle. It is the ultimate expression of arrogance, the final, weary sigh of a soul too rich to be bothered with delight. Plainness, ladies, is not a virtue. It is a boast.”

He paused, letting the paradox settle like fine dust over the room. Mrs. Arbuthnot shifted in her seat, the starch in her collar suddenly feeling less like a moral statement and more like a simple nuisance.

“Your societies,” he went on, his gaze sweeping over them, “speak of duty. Of the moral imperative to be useful. But what is more useful than a beautiful thing? A plain cup serves to quench a thirst. A beautiful cup, painted with a flight of kingfishers or glazed in the colour of a twilight sky, not only quenches the thirst, but makes the water taste of joy. It elevates the act of drinking into a moment of communion with beauty. To deny oneself this is not an act of morality; it is an act of self-mutilation. You are all, in your own admirable way, engaged in a grand project of making the world a less interesting place.”

Miss Pringle clutched her reticule, her knuckles white. She thought of her own plain, serviceable teacups and, for the first time, felt a pang of profound and inexplicable regret.

“You speak of the soul,” Mr. Thorne said, his voice dropping to a near-whisper, “as if it were a grim and hungry thing to be starved into virtue. But the soul is a flower. It requires sunlight, colour, and rich, dark earth to grow. To feed it only on the dry bread of duty is to cultivate a weed. Beauty is the sun. Pleasure is the rain. A life lived without them is a life of spiritual famine, a grey and barren landscape of the spirit.”

He gestured towards the window, where the last rays of the setting sun were catching the dust motes in the air, turning them into a swirling galaxy of gold. “Look at that! Is that plain? Is that useful? It serves no purpose whatsoever. It is a momentary, fleeting, utterly useless piece of perfection. And it is worth more than all the moral treatises ever written. To be plain is to turn one’s back on the sun in order to better appreciate the quality of the mud.”

He concluded not with a grand statement, but with a simple, devastating question. “Why,” he asked, looking directly at Mrs. Arbuthnot, “should we be good, if we can be beautiful instead?”

For a moment, there was a silence so profound it seemed to have a texture. Mrs. Arbuthnot rose to her feet, her face pale. The audience, and the committee itself, braced for the logical refutation, the thunderous denunciation of this beautiful heresy.

Mrs. Arbuthnot opened her mouth. She had prepared her arguments carefully: the plight of the poor, the virtue of thrift, the sanctity of a life free from vanity. But the words would not come. All she could see was the useless, glorious gold of the dust motes. All she could hear was the echo of his question. Why be good, when one could be beautiful?
