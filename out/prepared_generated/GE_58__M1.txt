It is a common, and perhaps a consoling, opinion that the human mind, like a plant, is destined by its nature to unfold only so far as its situation permits; that the understanding of a man born to the spade and the plough will, like the root of a turnip, be necessarily confined to the soil that nourishes it. Yet the truth, as is often the case with such comfortable maxims, is of a more complex and less predictable character. For the mind, once kindled by the secret flame of a new idea, will seek for itself an atmosphere in which to burn, and will, with a strange and often inconvenient energy, bend the very circumstances of its life towards that end.

Such a phenomenon was to be observed in the village of Little Marlow, a hamlet so nestled in a fold of the Derbyshire hills that it seemed to have been overlooked by the wider world for a century, and to be content with its obscurity. Here, amongst the sturdy, earth-stained men whose lives were measured by the turning of the seasons and the price of barley at the Bakewell market, there lived a young labourer named Josiah Poole. To the casual eye, Josiah was much as his fellows: broad in the shoulder, quiet in his speech, his face weathered by the same winds and his hands calloused by the same work. But within Josiah there had taken root a seed of a different sort, a seed that had been sown by the chance gift of a tattered copy of *Robinson Crusoe* from the parson’s wife. From that single volume, a whole world of letters had opened to him, a world he pursued by the light of a tallow candle after a day of exhausting toil, his lips moving silently as he wrestled with the strange shapes of words until they yielded their meaning.

This solitary pursuit might have remained a private and harmless peculiarity, were it not for the natural human tendency to seek companionship in our discoveries. Josiah, finding in books a solace and a grandeur absent from the daily round, began to speak of them, at first cautiously, then with a growing passion that surprised both himself and his listeners. He found, to his astonishment, that he was not alone in his hunger; that the minds of other men, long dormant, were ready to be woken. And so, under his gentle and persistent guidance, the Little Marlow Reading Society was born. Its meeting place was the great barn of Squire Ashton, a man of some property and tolerable learning, who saw in the society a harmless and even laudable diversion for his men, a proof that his estate was not wholly given over to ignorance.

The Squire, a man whose benevolence was of a practical and uncomplicated nature, provided the barn, the candles, and a few volumes from his own library: a history of England, a book of natural philosophy, and the ever-popular novels of Mr. Fielding. For a year or more, the society flourished. On winter evenings, the great barn, which echoed all day to the lowing of cattle and the thud of the flail, was filled with the murmur of men’s voices reading aloud, and the quiet, intent silence of those who listened. Josiah, as the founder and most proficient reader, was the natural leader. He would explain a difficult passage, or draw a moral from a tale of ancient heroes, his plain face alight with an enthusiasm that made him seem a man transformed. He felt himself to be the steward of a precious fire, and he tended it with a devotion that was its own reward. Yet it was in the nature of this fire to grow, to demand new fuel, and the fuel it began to crave was of a kind the Squire had not anticipated.

The change began when one of the men, a journeyman carpenter named Seth, brought a copy of the *Derby Mercury*. It was but a few weeks old, and smelt of the printer’s ink and the world beyond the hills. Josiah, whose knowledge of history was drawn from books that treated events as settled and distant, was electrified. Here was history in the making; here were the great questions of the age—of Napoleon’s armies, of the price of corn in the face of a poor harvest, of the strange new machines in the cotton mills of Manchester—discussed not as fable, but as living reality. The society’s attention shifted at once. The novels and histories were pushed aside, and the weekly newspaper became their text.

It was this new development that came at last to the ears of Squire Ashton. He was not a man to be easily alarmed, but he was a man of his class and his time, and he understood the delicate machinery of the social order. A labourer reading of ancient Rome was one thing; a labourer reading of the Luddite riots and the high price of bread was quite another. One nurtured a quiet resignation to one’s lot; the other, he feared, could only breed discontent and dangerous questions. He summoned Josiah to the parlour of his farmhouse, a room of polished mahogany and deep silence that always made the young man feel the roughness of his own coat.

“Josiah,” the Squire began, his tone one of paternal disappointment, “I hear you are reading newspapers in the barn.”

“Aye, Squire,” Josiah replied, his heart beginning to thud against his ribs. “We find it a great instruction.”

“Instruction?” The Squire steepled his fingers. “Or agitation? My barn was opened for the betterment of your minds, not for the stirring up of grievances. These papers are full of politics and strife. They are not fit for men in your station. It leads to thoughts above your work, and a discontent with the Providence that has placed you where you are.”
