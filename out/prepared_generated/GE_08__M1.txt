It is one of the most curious habits of the human mind that it can occupy a certain space—a town, a country—for the whole of its conscious life, and yet possess no more real knowledge of it than a bird possesses of the grammar of the air it flies through. Such, at least, was the case with Mr. Arthur Lathom, whose father had left him an estate and a competence so ample that the law, which he had studied at the Inns of Court with a diligence that did him credit, was to him rather a species of intellectual athletics than a profession. He had returned to his native town of Greylingford, in the county of Hertfordshire, not to seek his fortune, but to live upon it in a manner that he believed to be useful, and which was certainly comfortable. He sat on the bench of magistrates, he subscribed to the new Mechanics’ Institute, and he read his newspaper in the library of the county club, where the leather chairs were as soft and the opinions as well-worn as himself.

His world was a well-ordered diorama, viewed from the comfortable elevation of his own drawing-room. He saw the town as a collection of pleasing features: the handsome stone front of the Assize Courts, the dignified prospect of the High Street with its prosperous shops, the verdant sweep of the park, and, at its edge, the huddled, indistinct mass of the poorer quarters, which he thought of as ‘the low end’, much as one might think of a smudge on an otherwise clean windowpane. He gave to the Charitable Society for the Relief of the Poor, and in doing so, believed he had discharged his duty to that smudge. The existence of the people who lived there was a matter of statistical report and philanthropic concern, but not of personal knowledge. Their lives were a problem to be managed from a distance, their characters a set of general assumptions drawn from the sermons of Mr. Capper and the reports of the Overseer.

It was therefore with a certain sense of novelty, almost of adventure, that he found himself standing in the cramped, ill-lit office of Mr. Jephson, the solicitor, listening to the case of one Silas Croft. Croft was a glove-maker, a man whose name Arthur had never heard, and whose world was as remote from the club library as the surface of the moon. He was accused of stealing a dozen pairs of fine doe-skin gloves from the warehouse of Mr. Pardoe, the chief manufacturer in the town. The evidence, as Jephson laid it out in his dry, dusty voice, seemed straightforward. Croft had been dismissed from his post a fortnight before for drunkenness; he had been seen near the warehouse on the night in question; and, most damningly, a pair of the stolen gloves, identifiable by a peculiar flaw in the stitching of the thumb, had been found in his lodgings.

“And his defence?” Arthur had asked, feeling the pleasant, sharp edge of a legal puzzle.

Jephson had shrugged, his shoulders rustling in his threadbare coat. “He says he found ’em. Says he was walking home from the ‘Bell and Anchor’, and saw a packet lying in the alley by the warehouse yard. He says he was drunk, and didn’t rightly know what he was doing when he picked ’em up and put ’em in his pocket.”

Arthur had smiled. It was the story of every thief, the pathetic fable of the innocent find. “And you believe him?”

“I am paid to defend him, Mr. Lathom,” Jephson replied, “not to believe him. But he is a poor man with a wife and three children, and he cannot afford a proper counsel. The magistrates will have him transported. It’s a clear case, I fear.”

It was the ‘clear case’ that decided Arthur. Here was a simple, almost a classic, problem: a poor man, a plausible story, a piece of circumstantial evidence. He would take the case *pro bono*, he declared, his mind already turning over the points of law, the cross-examination of the constable, the need to cast doubt upon the witness who had seen Croft near the warehouse. It was a challenge, a test of his powers, and he felt a surge of that particular energy which comes from the belief that one is about to do a good and clever thing.

His first interview with Silas Croft disabused him of the notion that he was dealing with a simple legal problem. Croft was not the cunning rogue of Arthur’s imagination. He was a small, bent man with a face like a piece of worn leather, and hands so gnarled and stiff with work and rheumatism that he could barely close them into a fist. His voice was a low, hoarse whisper, the dialect of the town’s poorest quarters thick upon it, and Arthur found himself straining to understand him. Croft sat twisting his cap in his lap, his eyes fixed on the floorboards as if he were reading his own despair there.

“I did na’ steal ’em, sir,” he said, for the tenth time. “I found ’em. I swear it on my mother’s Bible.”

“But why did you not take them to the constable at once, Croft?” Arthur pressed, his tone reasonable and firm.

The man looked up, and in his eyes was a look of such profound, weary confusion that it was more damning than any guilt. “To th’ constable? What for? He’d ha’ thought I’d stolen ’em, same as you. And I was… I’d had a drop. My head was full o’ bees. I thought, mebbe I could sell ’em. Get a bit o’ money for th’ little un’s shoes. I know it was wrong. I know it now.”
