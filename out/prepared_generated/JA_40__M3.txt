The drawing-room in Harley Street was already tolerably full when Mrs. Brandon and her nieces entered it. Mrs. Brandon was a woman of fashion and of sense, and knew the value of being early in claiming the attention of those whose acquaintance was worth having. Clara Fairley, the elder of the two nieces, was chiefly occupied in the contemplation of her own new muslin, and in the hope that Lord Ashbourne might be present; while Emma, the younger, followed her aunt with that quiet observation of human folly which is often the solitary amusement of a girl of nineteen who has not yet learned to be vain.

The rooms were handsome, the company numerous, and the noise of conversation such as to make every individual feel that he or she was contributing to the general good. Mrs. Brandon was soon engaged with a lady of her acquaintance, and Emma, having deposited Clara near a window where she might be seen to advantage, placed herself a little apart, and was beginning to examine the faces around her with more interest than the occasion perhaps demanded, when her aunt returned to her, bringing with her a gentleman whom Emma had not before seen.

"My dear Emma," said Mrs. Brandon, "this is Mr. Ellsworth, of Everton Hall in Derbyshire. Mr. Ellsworth is desirous of being known to you. I have been telling him that your brother is in town."

Mr. Ellsworth bowed. He was a man of perhaps eight-and-twenty, tall, and in his address perfectly easy, with that kind of well-bred composure which speaks of an early introduction to the best society and a subsequent familiarity with it. His features were agreeable rather than striking, and his whole appearance was such as to please without immediately fixing attention. He said everything that was proper, and said it with an air of interest, and Emma would have thought him a very pleasing man, had she not perceived, almost at the instant of their first address, a change in his countenance which she could not account for. It was but a momentary shadow — a brief alteration of the brow — gone before she could be certain she had seen it, yet leaving behind it an impression of something not altogether easy. She had the curiosity to observe him more narrowly.

They conversed for some minutes on the common topics of the day, and the London diversions of the season. He spoke with an air of real solicitude, asking several questions, but all in a style of elaborate indifference. He seemed to be considering how he might best make his inquiries without appearing to make them. Emma answered with her usual composure. She was not a girl to be discomposed by an inexplicable look from a stranger, yet she could not but feel that Mr. Ellsworth's attention was of a kind rather anxious than merely polite. His eye rested on her face with an expression of study, as if he were comparing her with some image already fixed in his own mind, and were not wholly satisfied with the result.

When Mrs. Brandon turned to address an acquaintance, Mr. Ellsworth, finding himself disengaged from her, drew his chair nearer to Emma's.

"Mrs. Brandon does me the honour to speak of your brother," said he. "Mr. Fairley, I believe. Is he long in town?"

"About a fortnight," said Emma. "He came up from Oxford for the remainder of the season."

"And does he like town?" said Mr. Ellsworth, with an air of such studied carelessness as immediately to awaken Emma's suspicions. "A young man's first season in London is generally a matter of some consequence to him."

"He has been in town before," said Emma, a little dryly.

"Indeed. Then I have the pleasure of a prior acquaintance with his opinions, though not with himself." He paused, and then added, with a slight smile which did not altogether commend itself to Emma, "Your brother's name has not been wholly unknown to me, Miss Fairley. I have had the pleasure — if I may so call it — of hearing it mentioned."

Emma coloured. She could not be mistaken. There was an emphasis on the word pleasure, a turn in his phrase, which, joined to what she had already observed, confirmed her in the belief that Mr. Ellsworth was not a stranger to her brother. The name of Fairley had been mentioned between them, and not in a way that either wished to remember. She knew that Edward had quarrelled, some years before, with a man whose name she had never been able to learn. The quarrel had been the subject of much family distress, and of some letters which Emma had seen but not fully understood. Her mother had spoken of it with tears, her father with anger, and Edward himself with that kind of gloomy silence which, in a young man of two-and-twenty, is generally the sign of a wound not yet healed. Emma had been told only that the quarrel was of a private nature, and that she need not trouble herself about it. She had obeyed, as a dutiful daughter should; but she had not forgotten.

It now appeared extremely probable that Mr. Ellsworth was the person concerned. She had not the smallest idea of the cause of the quarrel, nor whether it had been in any degree such as to reflect discredit on her brother. She was wholly in the dark, and Mr. Ellsworth knew it. She was sure he knew it. There was a consciousness in his manner, a something of suppressed intelligence, which told her as plainly as words could have done, that he was in possession of facts which she was not, and that he was not sorry to have it so.

She resolved to appear perfectly unconcerned.

"I dare say my brother's name is not unknown in many quarters," said she. "He has a large acquaintance, and is not remarkable for concealing his opinions."
