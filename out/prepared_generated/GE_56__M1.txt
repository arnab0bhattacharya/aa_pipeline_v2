It is a common enough experience for a man who has spent the best part of his youth in the pursuit of varied sensations to return to the soil of his own country and find that the landscape of his life has been utterly altered in his absence; yet the discovery always comes with the force of a personal shock, as if the very laws of nature had been suspended in order to humiliate him. Such was the condition of Mr. Arthur Wyndham, who, having dissipated a very tolerable inheritance upon the grand tour and the lesser tours that followed it, found himself at the age of thirty-eight in a London lodging-house, possessed of a few trunks of foreign curiosities, a constitution somewhat impaired by southern wines, and a reputation that was, to say the best of it, merely negative. He had not been wicked, perhaps, in the sense of the tragic poets, but he had been idle with a thoroughness that is its own form of ruin, and he had consumed his capital with the cheerful indifference of a man who believes that the future is a sort of elastic garment which will always stretch to accommodate the wearer.

He had come to London with a vague intention of looking about him for some employment—perhaps in the diplomatic line, where a knowledge of continental vices might be turned to account—but the first few weeks served only to convince him of his superfluity. The clubs he remembered were altered, the men he knew were married and grave, or dead and forgotten. It was in this state of chill desolation that he remembered his cousin, Miss Margaret Dacre, whom he had not seen for a dozen years. In those days, she had been a shy, silent girl, living with her widowed mother in a dull country house, a person whom he had regarded with the careless pity one feels for a weed growing in a shadow, certain that it could never bloom. He recalled her as plain, and possessed of a dreary habit of asking questions about the condition of the poor, which he had found tedious.

Now, however, the newspapers informed him that Miss Dacre was a person of some note. She had written a book—nay, several books—upon the 'Moral Economy of the Factory Districts' and the 'Sanitary Obligations of the Landlord'; she was reviewed in the *Fortnightly* and quoted in Parliament; she was, in short, one of those serious women of the age who seem to have a private dispensation from the necessity of being agreeable, and who command respect by the sheer weight of their usefulness. The news struck Arthur with a mingled sensation of incredulity and annoyance. It seemed a kind of injustice of Providence that the dull, quiet cousin he had dismissed should have achieved a solidity of reputation while he, with all his advantages of manner and education, was a mere drifting waif. He felt a sudden, burning curiosity to see her, a curiosity that was not unconnected with a hope that she might be dazzled by his return, or at least that he might be able to patronize her a little, and so recover some lost ground.

He found her address in a quiet street near Bloomsbury, a locality chosen, no doubt, for its proximity to the British Museum and its atmosphere of grave industry. When he was shown into her study, he was conscious of a distinct shock, not of dismay, but of readjustment. The room was not a boudoir, filled with the gimcracks of feminine idleness, but a workshop of thought. Books were stacked not for ornament but for use; there were maps on the walls, and a large table covered with papers, pamphlets, and a half-finished manuscript. And there was Margaret. She was not beautiful, certainly; her features were too decided, her mouth too firm, for the prettiness he admired; but she had a countenance of such intelligent serenity, such a look of having lived in the high company of ideas, that he felt his own face flush with a sudden sense of vulgarity. She wore a dress of some dark, serviceable stuff, and her hair was arranged with a simplicity that scorned the fashions; yet there was a dignity in her presence that made his own velvet coat and carefully knotted scarf seem like the trappings of a mountebank.

"Arthur," she said, rising and holding out her hand with a frank cordiality that had no trace of the old shyness, "this is a surprise. I heard you were abroad."

"I have returned, Margaret," he said, trying to infuse his voice with the old easy drawl, though he felt it ring false. "The world is weary of me, and I of it. I came to see if the old country had any place left for a prodigal."

She looked at him with a gaze that was uncomfortably penetrating; it was the look of a woman accustomed to analyze motives and to detect the flimsy scaffolding of self-deception. "There is always work, Arthur," she said gently. "Though perhaps not the kind of work you have been used to."

"I hear you have become a lioness," he said, glancing at the books. "A blue-stocking of the most formidable order. I confess I am amazed. I remember you as the quietest mouse in the shire."

"I have found my vocation, I think," she replied, without vanity. "The times demand it. There is so much to be done, Arthur; so much suffering that might be alleviated if we only brought our minds to bear upon the causes. I used to think it enough to feel pity, but I have learned that pity without method is a very idle emotion."

He felt a rebuke in her words, though she spoke them without heat. He sat down, feeling large and useless in the small, busy room. "And you are happy?" he asked, the question sounding foolish even to himself.
