It is a fact, not generally understood by those who have never felt the weight of the tropics upon their very soul, that a man’s judgment may be altered, not by the passions of the heart, but by the mere physical oppression of the atmosphere. I remember the time well—though the years have stretched out since then with the grey elasticity of a bad dream—when I was engaged upon the harbour works at Puerto Rico de la Estrella. It was a scheme of great ambition, conceived in the capitals of Europe and destined to bring order, light, and commerce to a coast that had known only the treachery of the tides and the darker treachery of men.

I was young then; young enough to believe in the rectitude of iron girders and the permanence of concrete, and young enough to be wholly disarmed by the appearance of a woman’s face in that land of shadows. Her name was Isabella. She was the daughter of Don Rafael de Avila, a gentleman of the old school, whose influence in the province was as diffuse and as difficult to define as the mist that rises from the river at dawn. He was a man of the council, a politician, though he would have scorned the title, preferring to be thought of merely as a guardian of the republic’s soul. He spoke in long, rolling sentences, full of classical allusions and a kind of weary benevolence that made him beloved by the poor and trusted by the restless.

To see Isabella was to understand the strange, magnetic pull of that country. She possessed a beauty that was not of the drawing-room, but of the forest—dark, silent, and possessing a stillness that demanded reverence. When she looked at me, her eyes—large, liquid, and the colour of the deep water beyond the breakwater—seemed to hold a knowledge of things I could not grasp. I fell in love with her, not with the sudden violence of a storm, but with the slow, inevitable creep of the tide, until my whole existence was anchored to the hope of her presence.

For a time, the world seemed to hold its breath. The works progressed. The piles were driven into the mud with a rhythmic, thunderous persistence that lulled the city into a sense of security. We dined at the Avila house, a cool, cavernous place of high ceilings and shuttered windows, where the air smelled of jasmine and old leather. Don Rafael would discourse upon the future of the nation, upon the necessity of order, and upon the sacred duty of the citizen. I listened, dazzled by the light of the oil lamps on the silver, and by the quiet smile Isabella bestowed upon me when her father’s rhetoric became particularly lofty. It was a fragile peace, a suspension of reality, maintained only by the heat and the wine.

Then, the change came. It did not arrive with a shout, but with a whisper that ran through the streets like a draft under a door. It began with the absence of certain men from the clubs, with the furtive glances exchanged in the plaza, and with the sudden, unnatural silence of the music in the evenings. The air, which had been merely hot, became heavy with a foreboding that had no name. I felt it in the vibration of the machinery; the engines seemed to labour under a new, invisible burden.

Don Rafael, my future father-in-law, the man of order and law, was seen less and less in the city. When I called at the house, the servants were evasive. Isabella received me with a gravity that chilled the blood in my veins. She sat by the window, her hands folded in her lap, looking out at the harbour where my ships lay waiting.

“There is a disturbance in the interior,” she said to me one evening. Her voice was low, musical, yet flat, devoid of the warmth I had come to crave. “My father has gone to attend to his estates. The harvest is poor.”

It was a lie, of course. A transparent, childish lie, which she offered with the desperate dignity of a queen protecting a crumbling kingdom. I knew the truth, as did every stevedore and clerk in the port. Don Rafael de Avila had thrown in his lot with the Generals of the North. The revolt, that eternal, cyclical madness of the republic, had broken out again. The man who had spoken of civic duty was now marching against the very government he had served.

I stood there, a foreigner, a builder of docks, caught in the toils of a tragedy I was not equipped to comprehend. I looked at her—at this woman who embodied the silent, inscrutable soul of the land—and I felt a terror that was greater than love. It was the terror of the void.

“The steamer *Orion* sails on the tide tomorrow night,” I said, my voice sounding strange and harsh in the stillness. “It is the last vessel for the season. After that, the blockade will begin.”

She turned her head slowly. The shadows of the room seemed to gather around her.

“And you?” she asked. There was no plea in the question, no demand. It was merely an inquiry into the nature of things.

“I am an engineer,” I said. “My work is here. But the work is finished. Or perhaps,” I added, struck by a sudden, bitter irony, “it has not yet begun.”

I wanted to say that I would stay for her; that I would throw my lot in with the rebels, with the chaos, with the ruin of everything I had built. But the words would not come. I saw the faces of the men on the revolt—the fanatics, the dreamers, the bandits with their high-sounding titles—and I knew that my presence would be an absurdity. I was a creature of calculation and reason. I could build a wall against the sea, but I could not build a wall against the madness of men.
