The storm had passed over the Valley of Aurelia like a great grey painter who, having washed the canvas with violence, had suddenly grown weary of his work and left the colours wet and trembling. It was in the silence that followed, when the air smelled of bruised violets and wet stone, that Amias the shepherd found the object lying in the clover. It was not a stone, though it possessed the coolness of the earth; nor was it a flower, though it held the pale luminosity of the moon. It was a bell, fashioned of silver so pure it seemed to weep light, and it was silent, which is the most eloquent of all sounds.

Amias, a man whose life was measured by the movements of sheep and the changing of shadows upon the grass, knew little of art, yet he possessed that instinct for beauty which is far better than knowledge. He picked up the bell. It was cold against his palm, like a snowflake that refuses to melt. He shook it, tentatively. There was no sound—no clang, no chime, no dull thud of metal—but a vibration that seemed to pass through his fingers and settle in his blood.

Suddenly, the hillside underwent a transformation that would have made a bishop renounce his faith. The sheep, those bundles of wool and stupidity, ceased their grazing. The hawks, circling high in the azure ether, folded their wings and drifted down. Even the great stags of the forest edge, creatures of pride and wildness, lowered their antlers. In a moment, every beast upon the mountain knelt. They did not kneel in fear, but in adoration, as if the bell had spoken a truth too holy for human ears.

Amias stood amidst this congregation of silence, holding the source of their worship. He was a simple man, yet he felt a sudden, terrible weight of responsibility. To own a thing of such power is to be hated by those who possess nothing; to possess beauty is to invite the envy of the ugly.

Word of the miracle traveled down the valley faster than the stream that fed the vineyards. It reached the ears of Lord Valerius, a man who collected curiosities with the same rapacity with which he collected taxes. Valerius was a connoisseur of the exquisite; he believed that the world was a garden planted solely for his amusement, and that any rare bloom plucked by a peasant was a theft from nature itself.

The summons came not as a command, but as a velvet invitation. Amias ascended to the Castle of Shadows, which sat upon the crags like a crown of iron. He was led through corridors hung with tapestries that depicted the follies of the gods, into a hall where the floor was a mosaic of lapis lazuli and gold. There sat Valerius, draped in robes of Tyrian purple, fingering a ruby the size of a pigeon’s egg.

"So," said the Lord, his voice smooth as oil poured over water, "you are the keeper of the Silent Chime. I hear you have taught the beasts to pray. It is a charming trick, though I suspect the sheep were merely tired."

"It is no trick, my Lord," said Amias, clutching the bell to his chest. "It is a thing of the storm."

"Everything of value is a thing of the storm," Valerius replied, smiling with teeth that were too white. "Diamonds are born of pressure; pearls of disease; and fame, of course, is merely the result of a lucky catastrophe. I desire the bell. I will give you ten acres of the best vineyard land, and a house with a roof that does not leak. You shall be a man of substance."

Amias looked at the lord, at the jewels that decorated his fingers like glittering spiders, and then at the silver bell in his hand. He thought of the sheep kneeling in the twilight, a scene of such unearthly peace that it made the lord’s gold look like cheap brass.

"The bell is not for sale," Amias said quietly. "For to sell it would be to say that gold is worth more than worship."

Valerius laughed, a sound like breaking glass. "My dear shepherd, everything is for sale. That is the only philosophy that has ever survived the winter. If you will not sell it, you must give it. To refuse a lord is to choose a grave. But I am a reasonable man. I do not wish to take the bell by force, for force bruises the soul of an object. I wish to hear it. Bring it here. Ring it in my hall. If it does what you claim—if it makes my hounds kneel and my canaries swoon—I shall let you keep it as a gift of my admiration. If it is a fraud, I shall take it as a penalty for your deception. Is that not a fair wager?"

It was the sort of trap that only a civilized man could construct, for it disguised greed as generosity. Amias knew that if he rang the bell and the animals did not kneel—perhaps because the stone floor of the castle was dead to magic, or perhaps because the lord’s atmosphere was poisonous to holiness—he would lose the bell and his liberty. Yet, if he refused, he would be cast out, and the bell would be stolen regardless.

"I will ring it," said Amias.

The hall was prepared. The Lord’s greyhounds, creatures of sinew and vanity, lay upon cushions of silk. A parrot in a cage of gold watched with a beady, cynical eye. The air was thick with the scent of incense, an attempt to mask the smell of old power.

Amias stood in the center of the room. The light from the high windows fell upon him, turning his rough wool tunic into a robe of silver. He looked at Valerius, who sat upon his throne of ebony, his fingers steepled in anticipation.
