The rain in Belgravia does not fall so much as it hangs, a grey, suffocating curtain that muffles the city and turns the polished brass of the town houses into dull, tarnished discs. It was under this heavy, wet sky that I first met Mrs. Elara Vance, a woman whose composure was as brittle as the porcelain on her mantelpiece. She sat in the drawing-room of my lodgings at 221B, her hands clasped tightly in her lap, the knuckles white with the effort of suppressing a tremor.

"He was not a man of health," she said, her voice low and clipped. "But he was not a man to die in a cab. My brother, Julian, had the constitution of an ox. He could have run the length of the park without breaking stride. And yet, here he lies in the cold storage of the mortuary, and the coroner speaks of a sudden stoppage of the heart."

Holmes, who had been pacing the room with that restless, kinetic energy that preceded a revelation, stopped abruptly. He turned, his eyes narrowing as he fixed upon the woman. "A sudden stoppage," he repeated, tasting the words. "And the cabman? He was a reliable man?"

"Old Thorne. Thirty years with the firm," Mrs. Vance replied. "He saw my brother collapse. He rang the bell at the embassy. He waited. And when the household staff came out, they were already busy. Very busy, Mr. Holmes. Burning things."

The detective’s head snapped toward me. "Burning things? In the street? In the rain?"

"In the garden," Mrs. Vance corrected, her voice trembling slightly. "The groundskeeper, a silent man, was seen feeding a large bundle of papers into the brazier. The smoke was thick, black against the grey sky. The diplomat, Mr. Aris Thorne, was watching. He did not stop it. He watched until the last scrap was ash."

Holmes walked to the window, pressing his face against the glass. Outside, the street was empty, the umbrellas of the few pedestrians creating a jagged skyline of black and grey. "The diplomat," he murmured, turning back to us. "Mr. Aris Thorne. A man of high standing, I presume? A man who would not be seen to act with haste or panic?"

"High standing, indeed," said Mrs. Vance. "He is the Envoy for the Duchy of Valcourt. A nation we are currently negotiating trade agreements with. A nation whose interests are, as they say, fluid."

"Fluid," Holmes repeated, a slight smile playing on his lips. "And your brother, Mrs. Vance. What was his position? A valet is a valet, but in the service of a diplomat, a valet is a key. He holds the keys to the rooms, the letters, the secrets. He hears what is whispered and sees what is hidden."

"He knew too much," she said, the words rushing out now. "He was not merely a servant. He was the eyes and ears of the family. And three days before he died, he asked to speak to me. He said he had seen something that could not be unseen. He said the papers he burned were not merely state documents. They were evidence of a crime. A crime that would sink the embassy, and perhaps more."

Holmes turned to me, his expression grave. "You see, Watson? The heart failure is a convenient fiction. It is the easiest way to silence a witness without the mess of a struggle or the noise of a gunshot. But why burn the papers? If the crime was state-related, the destruction of evidence would only draw the attention of the police. Unless... unless the papers were not meant for the police. Unless they were meant for the public. Or, more likely, for a rival."

"He was murdered," I said, though I knew it was a guess. "And the embassy is covering it up."

"Covering up a murder is a dangerous game," Holmes said, resuming his pacing. "It requires a degree of arrogance that only the very powerful possess. But the timing is the vital clue. The man dies in the cab, and the papers are burned within the hour. This suggests that the death was not an accident, but a signal. A trigger. Someone knew that Julian Vance was going to speak. They killed him to stop the speech, and they burned the papers to destroy the proof that he had been silenced."

Mrs. Vance looked up, her eyes wide. "How could they know?"

"Because, my dear lady, the valet is the only person who knows the daily routine of the diplomat with absolute certainty. He knows when the Envoy receives his visitors, when he writes his dispatches, and where he keeps his private correspondence. If Julian Vance had decided to take the documents to the authorities, or perhaps to a journalist, he would have had to move them. And in moving them, he would have been observed."

Holmes stopped before the fire, his eyes reflecting the dancing flames. "I suspect that the death was not a natural failure of the heart, but a chemical one. A subtle poison, administered in a glass of wine or a cup of tea, one that mimics the symptoms of cardiac arrest. And the burning of the papers? That was not a reaction to the death. It was a pre-arranged act. The diplomat knew that the valet would die. He had already ordered the destruction of the incriminating documents. The death was merely the final step in a plan that had been in motion for weeks."

Mrs. Vance stood up, her chair scraping harshly against the floor. "Then they know I am here. They know that my brother’s sister is asking questions."

"No," Holmes said softly. "They do not know that. They believe they have buried the secret with the man. They believe that the coroner’s report will settle the matter. They are arrogant, Mrs. Vance. And arrogance is the great flaw of the powerful. They assume that because they control the narrative, they control the truth. But the truth, like a leak in a hull, finds its way out, no matter how deep it lies."
