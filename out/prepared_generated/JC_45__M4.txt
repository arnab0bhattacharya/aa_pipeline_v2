The letter lay upon the marble-topped table of the café like a small, soiled flag of surrender, and for a long time I did not touch it. The afternoon had the thin, aqueous clarity of a Geneva autumn, a light that seemed to fall from a great height without warmth, and the lake beyond the windows was a sheet of hammered pewter, motionless under a sky the colour of a soiled bandage. I had been sitting there for perhaps an hour, watching the water-fowl and the slow, deliberate passage of a steamer, and all the while the envelope—with its foreign stamp and its cramped, familiar hand—had asserted its presence at the edge of my vision, patient and insidious as a creditor.

I knew what it contained, or believed I knew. The words would be the same as always: the appeals to a duty that had no meaning for me, the invocations of an old woman’s loneliness, the trembling suggestions that the authorities had softened, that the past might be permitted to bury itself, that a son might return to a house where a mother’s prayers had worn a groove in the floorboards. And behind the words, I would hear the other voice, the voice of the police, the voice of the Okhrana, the voice of the whole vast, patient, suffocating machinery of the Russian state, which had learned long ago that the heart of a mother is the most reliable of instruments, the most delicate of traps.

I took up the letter at last. The paper was thin, the kind that folds into a dozen creases, and it smelled faintly of lavender and of something else—of old wood, perhaps, of a house that has stood through many winters, of the particular, indefinable scent of a place one has fled. The handwriting was as I remembered it, small and upright, with the letters formed as though by a hand that had been taught to write in another century, and there were blots where the pen had pressed too hard, and in one corner a faint, brownish stain that might have been tea or might have been a tear.

She wrote of the garden. The old pear tree had been cut down, she said, because it had grown too tall and the neighbours complained of the shadow it cast upon their yard. She wrote of the icons in the corner of the parlour, of the little lamp that she kept burning before the Virgin, and of the priest who came now on Saturdays to read to her because her eyes had grown weak. She wrote of the cold. The winter had been severe, she said, and the wood had been poor, and she had been obliged to burn some of the furniture—the old chairs, the table that had belonged to my father—to keep the stove alive through the worst of it. And then, in a passage that I read three times before I could go on, she wrote that she had been ill, that she had been very ill, that she had thought she might not see the spring, and that in her sickness she had prayed for one thing only: that she might see her son once more before she died.

I laid the letter down and looked out at the lake. A flock of gulls was wheeling above the water, their cries faint and plaintive through the glass, and I thought of the gulls that used to follow the fishing boats on the Neva, and of the grey, leaden water of the river in autumn, and of the house in the Liteiny quarter where I had been born and where my mother still lived, alone now, with her icons and her memories and her prayers.

It was a good letter. It was, I thought, a very good letter. And that was the trouble.

I had been in Geneva for two years, and in those two years I had learned something of the methods of the men who served the Tsar. I had learned that they were patient, that they were ingenious, that they were not without a certain grim sense of humour. I had learned that they could forge a handwriting so perfectly that the closest friend could not detect the imitation, and that they had, in their service, men who could imitate the very cadence of a voice, the very rhythm of a style. I had learned that a letter from a mother was one of the oldest tricks in their repertoire, and that it had worked, more than once, upon men who should have known better.

And yet—and yet—there was the stain in the corner, the brownish stain that might have been tea or might have been a tear. Would the police think of that? Would they think to add a tear?

I laughed at myself, a short, bitter laugh that drew the attention of the waiter, a thin, cadaverous man with the face of a disappointed poet, who had been watching me with the patient curiosity of his kind. I ordered another coffee, and when it came I stirred it slowly, watching the sugar dissolve, and I thought of my mother’s hands, the knotted, swollen joints, the blue veins like rivers on a map, the way she would hold a pen as though it were a bird that might escape. I thought of her sitting at the old table in the parlour, with the lamp before the Virgin, writing to a son who had disgraced her, who had fled his country, who had been condemned in absentia to the mines of Siberia—writing to him, in her loneliness, with the patience of a woman who has nothing left but patience, and with the hope, perhaps, that even now, even at the end, there might be some way to bring him home.
