The light in Funchal did not fall so much as it poured, a thick, golden viscosity that seemed to suspend the very air, turning the courtyard into a vase of suspended time. Mrs. Armitage sat in the shade of the bougainvillea, her hand resting on the arm of the wicker chair, feeling the warmth seep through the fabric of her gloves. It was a strange warmth, not the crisp, dry chill of an English morning, but a wet, heavy heat that lay upon the skin like a cloak. Beside her, in a room that smelled faintly of camphor and old wool, lay Arthur. He had not moved since the nurse left an hour ago; his breathing was a shallow, rhythmic rasp, a mechanical sound that seemed to disconnect him from the world outside, from the sun, from the flowers, and from her.

She watched the lace. It was not much to look at, a delicate, intricate web of white cotton, spread out on a small wooden table. But it moved. As the breeze stirred the leaves above, the lace trembled, casting shadows that danced across the tile floor like quicksilver. The girl, Maria, stood behind the table, her dark eyes wide and serious, her hands folded in her lap. She was perhaps twenty, or perhaps twenty-five; in the daze of the afternoon, ages blurred. She wore a dress of faded blue, the hem slightly frayed, and her hair was pinned back in a way that suggested a practicality born of necessity rather than fashion.

"You are looking at the pattern," Maria said. Her English was halting, constructed with great care, as if each word were a stone laid across a stream.

"I am looking at the light," Mrs. Armitage corrected gently. "It catches the thread. It makes it seem alive."

Maria smiled, a tentative expression that did not quite reach her eyes. "In my village, we say the lace remembers. It remembers the hands that made it. My mother’s hands. My grandmother’s hands." She paused, listening to something in the distance, perhaps the sound of a cart on the cobblestones, or the cry of a gull. "They were strong hands. Not soft like these." She held up her own, slender and pale, the fingers long and dexterous. "But they were busy. Always busy. While I sit here, they were weaving."

Mrs. Armitage felt a pang, a sharp, sudden intrusion of unease. She looked at her own hands, manicured, adorned with a simple pearl ring, resting idly in her lap. They were hands that had held teacups, signed letters, turned pages. They were hands that had done very little, and yet they felt full, heavy with a kind of inert satisfaction. "And what do you hope to weave?" she asked, the question slipping out before she could weigh it.

Maria looked at her, and for a moment, the distance between them—the language, the age, the different worlds they inhabited—seemed to vanish. "I hope to buy a loom," Maria said simply. "A real one. Not just to sell lace, but to make cloth. To make things that last. My father... he is ill. Like your husband." The mention of Arthur was quiet, a shadow passing over the sun. "But I am not ill. I am young. I can work. I can wait."

Mrs. Armitage looked back toward the window of her room. She could see the silhouette of Arthur in the bed, a dark shape against the white sheets. She had come to Madeira for the air, for the warmth, to save him, or perhaps to save herself from the sight of him. She had thought of it as a retreat, a pause in the long, tedious performance of their marriage. But sitting here, in the thick air, listening to the girl talk of looms and fathers and the memory of hands, she felt the walls of her own life closing in. It was a comfortable life, a life of small routines and predictable comforts, but it was small. It was a room with no windows, where the light only came through the cracks.

"Why do you sell here?" Mrs. Armitage asked, her voice sounding thin to her own ears. "In the hotel. To the strangers."

"Because the strangers have money," Maria said, but there was no greed in her voice, only a clear-eyed assessment of the world. "And because they have time. They are waiting for something. To leave, to return, to die. They look at the lace and see beauty. But I look at it and see survival. I see the months of my life, thread by thread."

A bird sang, a bright, piercing note that cut through the humidity. Mrs. Armitage started, then relaxed. She realized she had been holding her breath. She looked at Maria, who was now folding the lace, her movements precise, economical. There was a dignity in the girl, a seriousness that Mrs. Armitage found both intimidating and inspiring. She thought of her own youth, of the years she had spent learning to play the piano, to speak French, to smile at the right moments. She had learned to be a hostess, a wife, a mother. She had not learned to weave. She had not learned to make anything that would outlast her.

"I should buy some," Mrs. Armitage said. It was a ridiculous thought, a trivial one, but it felt necessary. "A scarf, perhaps. For Arthur. To keep him warm."

Maria nodded, her face unreadable. "He will like it. It is soft. It will remind him of his mother, perhaps."

Mrs. Armitage felt a chill, though the air was hot. She looked at the lace, the intricate pattern that seemed to shift and change in the light. It was beautiful, yes, but it was also a record of labor, of pain, of life lived fully and intensely. Her own life, by comparison, seemed pale, washed out, like a photograph left in the sun. She had been safe. She had been comfortable. And in that safety, she had become invisible to herself.
