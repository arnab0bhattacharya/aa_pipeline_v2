The rain had been falling since midnight, a persistent, sooty drizzle that turned the cobblestones of Whitchurch Lane into a stretch of black ice. Inside my office, the gaslight hissed a low, steady protest against the damp. I was just reaching for the dregs of my tea when the door burst open, admitting a gust of cold river air and a man who looked as though he had been dragged behind a hansom cab rather than driven in one.

He was a cabman, that much was plain from his caped greatcoat and the worn, leather-reinforced gloves clutched in his trembling hands. His face was a map of distress, pale beneath the grime of the London streets.

"Mr. Sorensen?" he asked, his voice cracking on the second syllable.

"Close the door," I said, gesturing to the chair opposite my desk. "And sit down."

He did both, though he sat on the very edge of the seat as if prepared to bolt. He was a thickset man in his middle years, going by the name of Arthur Fenn. He had driven a hackney coach for hire for sixteen years without so much as a dented fender to his name, he told me. Now, because of a single fare, he was staring down the barrel of a conspiracy charge at Bow Street.

"They’re saying I’m a liar, Mr. Sorensen. They’re saying I helped that man disappear, or worse. But I swear on my mother’s eyes, I set him down at the corner of Garrick and Vane, right by the old chemist’s shop, at half past ten. He walked away whole. How could he be dead in a cellar on the other side of London at the same hour?"

I had read the morning papers. The deceased was a Mr. Lucius Davenant, a clerk of some means, found strangled in the coal cellar of a boarding house in Rotherhithe. The police had traced a cab Fenn had driven that night, and when they pressed him, Fenn had given his account of dropping Davenant in the West End. The police, naturally, concluded Fenn was lying to cover his tracks.

"The police have a habit of mistaking convenience for truth," I said. "Tell me about the ride itself. Was there anything unusual about Davenant when he entered the cab?"

Fenn shook his head slowly. "He flagged me down near the Lyceum. Quiet bloke. Muffled up in a heavy scarf, collar turned high. He gave me the address—Garrick and Vane—and sat in the back without a word. I heard him cough once or twice, but nothing strange. When we arrived, he paid the fare, stepped out, and walked off toward the chemist’s. That’s the last I saw of him."

"Did he carry anything?"

"A walking stick. Dark wood, silver handle. And he kept one hand inside his coat pocket."

I leaned back, studying Fenn’s weathered face. He was terrified, but he was not lying. Fear sharpens the features in a way that fabrication cannot; the muscles around the eyes do not tense in quite the same manner. Arthur Fenn believed every word he was saying.

"Very well," I said. "Where is your cab now?"

"Parked in the mews behind the Bull and Crown. The police have already searched it. They found nothing, which only made them angrier."

"Take me to it."

We walked in silence through the wet streets, the gas lamps casting long, wavering reflections on the slick cobblestones. The Bull and Crown was a sprawling, low-ceilinged tavern on the corner of Pritchard Street, its mews accessible through a narrow, echoing archway. Fenn’s cab was the last in a row of three, a four-wheeled growler with a faded maroon body and black leather mudguards, gleaming damply under a single lantern.

I began where any investigator should: with the obvious. The interior of the cab was cramped, as expected. The seats were upholstered in cracked black leather, worn smooth by years of use. I ran my hands along the cushions, feeling for tears, hidden compartments, anything that did not belong. The police had clearly done the same. They had pulled up the cushions, examined the floorboards, and found nothing.

"He sat on the left side," Fenn said, watching me from the mews. "His back to the horse."

I knelt on the floorboards, examining the left-hand seat. The leather was cold and slightly damp. I pressed my fingers into the seam where the backrest met the seat. Nothing. I moved to the right side, then the forward-facing seat. It was as I pressed my palm flat against the floorboard near the left door that I felt it: a slight give, a subtle depression no wider than a shilling.

I drew out my penknife and worked the edge of the board. It lifted with a faint click, revealing a shallow cavity beneath. Inside was a small, folded square of paper, no larger than a postage stamp, wedged between the joists. I extracted it carefully and unfolded it under the lantern light. It was a fragment of a map, torn from a larger sheet. Printed on it in faded ink was a single street name: Wren’s Passage.

"Mr. Fenn," I said, climbing out of the cab, "you said Davenant kept one hand in his coat pocket the entire ride."

"That’s right."

"And he carried a walking stick."

"Aye. Dark wood, silver handle."

"But he never struck it on the floor of the cab? Never leaned it against the door?"

"Not that I recall."

I turned the paper over. On the back, in a fine, hurried hand, someone had written: *Thurs. half ten. Garrick and Vane. Wait for the bell.*

The handwriting was not Davenant’s. I knew this because I had seen Davenant’s hand in the papers—a neat, sloping script. This was cramped and hurried, the letters formed by someone writing in motion, in a dark space, perhaps in the back of a moving cab.

"Someone else rode in this cab," I said.

Fenn blinked. "What? No. Only Davenant."
