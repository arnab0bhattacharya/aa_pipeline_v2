The sea had never been silent for Fingal, not in the fourteen years he had kept the light at Cairn Dhu. It was a ceaseless grinding of water against basalt, a sound that had worn its way into his marrow so deeply that he noticed it only in its absence. But on the night of the autumnal equinox, in the pitch hours after the great Fresnel lens had been extinguished and the island of Stroga lay under a sky salted with cold stars, the silence came. It came sudden and total, as though the ocean had drawn a breath and held it. Fingal woke in his cot in the tower's lower chamber and lay still, his heart knocking against his ribs, certain that some catastrophe had befallen—that the world had stopped turning, or that he had died in his sleep.

He pulled on his boots and oilskin and climbed the spiral stair to the gallery. The wind had dropped to nothing. The sea, which should have been clawing at the rocks forty feet below, lay smooth and black as poured pitch. He leaned against the iron rail and looked down, and there, at the base of the cliff where the rocks formed a kind of crude shelf above the deep, he saw the creature.

It was not a whale. It was not a seal. It was something that moved with a deliberation he had seen only in the great land animals, an unhurried massive grace that belonged to no thing he could name. It pulled itself from the water with what appeared to be forelimbs, thick and dark and jointed in places that suggested neither fin nor flipper. Its body was perhaps twelve feet in length, blunt at the head, tapering to a powerful tail that still trailed in the surf. Its skin caught the starlight with a wet sheen that might have been scaled or might have been merely smooth, and from its head or its back—Fingal could not tell which in the darkness—there rose a ridge or fin that gave it the profile of something ancient, something sculpted onto the prows of Viking longships. It settled on the rock shelf like a sultan taking a throne, and it turned its head upward, and Fingal saw its eyes. They were large and of a colour he could not determine, but they held the light of the stars with an intelligence that made him step back from the rail.

He watched it for an hour. It watched him back. Neither moved, and the silence held between them like a covenant, and then the creature turned and slid back into the black water, and the sound of the sea returned as though a door had been opened on a roaring room.

Fingal wrote his report the next morning in the careful hand the Northern Lighthouse Board required of its keepers. He described what he had seen without embellishment, without the words that pressed against his teeth—words like beautiful and sorrowful and known. He sent the report with the supply boat on its next run, addressed to the Board's office in Stromness, and thought nothing more would come of it. The Board had received his reports before: a rogue wave of unusual height, a migration of puffins out of season. These things were noted and filed and forgotten.

But three weeks later, a boat came through the channel off Stroga bearing not only the quarterly provisions but a man. He was tall and narrow and dressed in a tweed suit that was quite wrong for the Hebrides, its elbows already darkened with salt. He carried two leather cases. In one was a camera of the box-and-bellows type, a Sanderson, with plates and developing chemicals wrapped in oilcloth. In the other was a rifle, a Martini-Henry, its barrel protruding from the case like an accusing finger. His name was Aldous Crewe, and he presented himself as a naturalist associated with the Edinburgh Museum of Science and Antiquity, though Fingal had never heard of him and the letter of introduction he produced bore a seal Fingal did not recognise.

"I have read your account," Crewe said, standing in the lighthouse kitchen while Fingal put the kettle on. "I found it remarkable for its restraint. Most men in your profession, when they report such things, reach for the language of the mariner's tavern. You did not. That is why I am here rather than dismissing your report as drink or madness."

Fingal said nothing. He set two cups on the table.

"You saw a large marine animal of unknown classification," Crewe continued. "If what you describe is accurate, and I believe it is, then you have seen a creature that may represent a surviving relic of an earlier epoch. A plesiosaur, perhaps, or something closer to the desmostylians. The Hebridean waters are deep and cold and poorly charted. It is not impossible that such a creature could persist here, undetected, feeding on the cephalopods of the deep trench." He opened the case with the rifle and began to clean the barrel with an oiled rag. "I mean to photograph it, of course. But I also mean to obtain a specimen. A photograph alone will not satisfy the museum. Science requires evidence that can be measured and preserved."

Fingal watched the oiled rag move up and down the barrel. "You mean to kill it," he said.

"I mean to collect it," Crewe said. "There is a difference."

Fingal did not argue. He was not a man given to argument. He had taken the posting at Cairn Dhu because it required no speech, because the work was simple and necessary and the world asked nothing of him beyond vigilance. He had been a fisherman before, on the west coast of Lewis, and he had seen enough of what men did to the sea and to the things that lived in it. He poured the tea and showed Crewe where he would sleep, in the storeroom below the tower, and then he went up to trim the wicks for the night's lighting.
