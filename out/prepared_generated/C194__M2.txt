The carriage wheels ground against the cobbles of Cavendish Square, a jagged, final sound that seemed to shatter the last remaining illusion of their importance. Elara stepped out first, her skirts dragging through the wet slush that had turned the pristine pavement into a gray slurry. Behind her, her younger sister, Millicent, climbed down with a grimace, clutching her shawl tighter as if the London air were a personal enemy. The townhouse looming above them was a monolith of grey stone, its windows dark and unblinking under the overcast sky. It was a house built for giants, or perhaps for ghosts, and the two of them, with their traveling dust and their threadbare coats, felt like insects at the door of a cathedral.

The door opened not by a servant, but by a footman whose eyes slid over them with the practiced indifference of a man who had long since stopped seeing individual faces and began seeing only bills to be paid. He did not speak. He merely stepped aside, gesturing to the vestibule, which smelled of beeswax and stale lavender. There was no greeting. No embrace. The brass bell for the family suite was not rung; instead, a maid appeared from a side corridor, her face a mask of polite disinterest, and handed them a slip of paper.

"Miss Hartwell and Miss Hartwell," the maid said, her voice flat. "The drawing room is not prepared for guests. You will find your rooms in the East Wing, behind the library. Dinner is at seven. Please do not linger in the corridors."

Elara took the slip, her fingers trembling slightly. She looked back at the closed door, waiting for a sign of life, a shout of surprise, a rush of arms. But the house remained silent, a vaulted tomb of polished mahogany and silence.

Millicent, however, was not waiting. She was already pacing, her boots clicking sharply against the marble floor. "They are monsters," she hissed, her voice tight with a fury that seemed disproportionate to the slight. "We are family. We are blood. And they treat us as if we were beggars seeking alms. I told you, Elara, I told you not to come. I told you that marrying into the Ashworths would only bring us shame."

"Quiet, Milly," Elara whispered, though there was no one to hear them save the echoing shadows of the hallway. "They are busy. The Ashworths are... much more complicated than we are."

"Complicated is a word for people who feel something," Millicent snapped, spinning around. "They feel nothing. That is the difference. And I will not stand here and let them humiliate us. I will go to my room and I will pack. We will leave by morning."

Elara placed a hand on her sister’s arm, a gentle restraint that Millicent shrugged off. The younger girl stormed past her, her heels striking a frantic rhythm as she disappeared into the darkness of the corridor. Elara stood alone in the center of the vast entrance hall. A chandelier, heavy with crystal, hung above her, refracting the dim light from the streetlamps into a scattered, cold constellation on the floor. It was beautiful, in a sterile, dead way.

She began to walk, following the instructions on the slip of paper. The East Wing was distant, a labyrinth of long corridors lined with portraits of ancestors who all looked back with the same expression of weary disdain. The walls were hung with tapestries depicting hunts and battles, the figures frozen in eternal struggle. As she walked, she felt the weight of the house pressing down on her. It was a place of history, of legacy, of bloodlines that had been carefully pruned and polished for generations. They, with their modest means and their lack of title, were weeds in this garden.

But as she turned the corner into the library, she paused. The door was ajar, and a sliver of light spilled out onto the carpet. Curious, and perhaps driven by a need to find some human warmth in this cold place, she pushed the door open.

The library was a cavern of books, the shelves rising to the ceiling, filled with leather-bound volumes that smelled of age and dust. In the center of the room, sitting at a large writing desk with his back to the door, was Julian Ashworth. He was Millicent’s cousin, the man who had married into this gilded cage.

He did not turn. He was writing, his quill scratching furiously against the paper. The sound was loud in the silence, a sharp, aggressive rasp. Elara took a step forward, her footfall soft on the thick Persian rug.

"Julian?" she called softly.

He stopped. For a moment, he remained perfectly still, his shoulders hunched. Then, slowly, he turned. His face was pale, drawn, and there were dark circles under his eyes that spoke of sleepless nights. He looked at Elara, and for a second, his expression was one of surprise, but it was quickly masked by a look of profound exhaustion.

"Elara," he said, his voice rough. "I did not know you were coming. I thought... I thought you would not come."

"We are family," Elara said, stepping further into the room. "We are here to visit."

Julian let out a short, dry laugh that held no humor. "Yes. Family." He looked down at his hands, which were stained with ink. "It is a heavy word, is it not? In this house. It means nothing. It is just a name on a list. A line in a ledger."

He gestured to the chair opposite his desk, an invitation so subtle that Elara might have missed it. She sat, watching him. He looked diminished, smaller than she remembered. The man who had played cricket with them in the summer, who had laughed loudly and easily, was gone. In his place was a man who seemed to be slowly disappearing into the walls.

"Millicent is angry," Elara said carefully.
