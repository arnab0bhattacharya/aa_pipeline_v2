The malady, when it first came to the notice of Mr. Arthur Penhallow, was not a matter of dramatic swooning in the streets or of ghastly, lurid faces in the lamplight. It was a matter of numbers. It was a matter of ink on flimsy paper, of neat columns filled in by a hundred different hands in a hundred different parishes, and of the slow, inexorable rise of a single, stark total. Penhallow, a junior clerk in the statistical department of the Board of Health, was a man whose world was bounded by the ruled lines of a ledger. His duty was not to feel, but to collate; not to interpret, but to add. And for three weeks, he had been adding.

The official name for the thing was ‘London Languor’, a term of such bland and cowardly imprecision that it made Penhallow’s teeth ache. The newspapers, with their usual genius for the lurid, had christened it ‘The Gloom Grippe’ or ‘The Gaslight Fever’, and filled their columns with breathless accounts of a few theatrical cases in Belgravia while ignoring the grim arithmetic accumulating in the tenements of Bethnal Green and Southwark. The Minister, a man whose health was a public concern in itself, had given an interview to *The Times* assuring the nation that the situation was entirely in hand and that the so-called epidemic was nothing more than a seasonal disorder, exaggerated by a nervous populace. But Penhallow had the figures. He had the certificates of death, signed by weary doctors in ink that was sometimes spattered with rain or blurred by haste. He saw the pattern that no one else seemed to see—or at least, no one in the corridors of power cared to admit.

It was a strange illness. It did not kill with the brutal efficiency of cholera or the foul touch of the plague. It began with a profound weariness, a grey apathy that settled upon its victims like a London fog. They would complain of a chill in their bones that no fire could warm, a persistent headache that was less a pain than a dull pressure behind the eyes. Then came the sleep. A deep, heavy, unnatural slumber from which it was increasingly difficult to rouse them. Their skin would grow cool and pale, their breathing shallow, and their pulse a faint, fluttering thing. Some simply did not wake up. Others would rouse for a few hours, confused and weak, only to sink back into that profound stupor a day or two later. It was a quiet, insidious thing, and its quietness was what made it so terrifying to Penhallow’s methodical mind.

He tried to speak to his superior, a Mr. Abernathy, a man whose chief talent was for avoiding difficult questions. Penhallow had entered his office with a sheaf of papers, his heart thumping with a sort of terrified earnestness. “Sir,” he had begun, “the returns from the East End parishes for the week are… most alarming. The mortality from the Languor has doubled in St. Jude’s, and the new cases in Stepney are—”

Abernathy had not looked up from the *Financial News*. “Doubled, Penhallow? Doubled from a very small number to a slightly larger, but still very small number, I presume? The Minister has made the government’s position quite clear. We are not to encourage panic with uncontextualised statistics.” He had turned a page, the rustle of the paper a sound of finality. “Your job is to record the figures, not to editorialise upon them. See to it.”

Penhallow had retreated, the sheaf of papers feeling cold and heavy in his hands. Uncontextualised statistics! The context was a row of cold beds in a workhouse infirmary. The context was the silence that was falling over whole streets. He walked home that evening through a city that seemed to have caught the disease itself. The usual raucous energy of the Strand was muted, the faces that passed him under the gaslights were pale and drawn, their eyes holding a flicker of the same weariness he saw in the reports. He passed a news-stand where a placard for *The Daily Comet* screamed: ‘MINISTER DECLARES ALL IS WELL!’. The irony was a bitter pill.

That night, sleep would not come. He lay in his narrow bed in his Bloomsbury lodging, listening to the rattle of a distant cab and the sigh of the wind. He thought of the faces he had seen, the grey, listless figures in the reports. He thought of Abernathy’s fat, comfortable indifference. The numbers were not abstractions. Each one was a person, a life, extinguished by something the world refused to name. He sat up, his mind made up with a sudden, shocking clarity. He was a clerk, yes. A functionary in a great, grinding machine. But he was also a man with eyes, and he had seen. To do nothing now would be a kind of murder.

The next morning, he did not go to the Board of Health. Instead, he took a cab to Fleet Street, his precious sheaf of papers wrapped in brown paper and tucked under his arm. He had heard of a man named Silas Croft, a journalist for *The Evening Chronicle* who had a reputation for a certain cynical honesty and a nose for a story the authorities would rather keep buried. He found Croft in a small, smoke-filled office above a printer’s shop, the air thick with the smell of ink, cheap tobacco, and wet paper.

Croft was a wiry man with sharp, intelligent eyes and ink-stains on his cuffs. He looked Penhallow up and down as if he were a specimen under a microscope. “Board of Health,” he said, not as a question. “I can smell the official stationery. What is it? A new regulation on the proper colour of a chimney-sweep’s hat?”

Penhallow placed the bundle on the desk. “It’s about the Languor,” he said, his voice steadier than he expected. “The official reports. I’ve… I’ve compiled the figures for the last month.”
