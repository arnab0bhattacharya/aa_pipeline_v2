The bell above the door gave its little cracked tinkle—a sound she had come to hear as a kind of question, tentative, apologetic—and there he stood, Mr. Ashworth, removing his hat with that careful, unhurried gesture of his, as though the hat itself were a thing to be respected, and the shop, with its dim caverns of print and its smell of dust and glue and slow-decaying paper, a place where one must mind one’s manners. He was not a reader; she knew that now, had known it from the first afternoon when he had asked, with genuine bewilderment, whether the books were arranged “in some sort of order,” and she had explained the alphabet to him as one explains a game to a child, and he had nodded, gravely, as though filing away a fact of considerable importance. He came, she understood, for her—for the way she stood at the counter with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a pencil behind her ear, for the way she said “Good afternoon” without looking up, for the way, perhaps, the light from the single gas-jet caught the loose hair at her temple. He came, and he bought a book—always a book, as though the purchase were a ticket of admission—and he lingered, and he spoke of the weather, of the new motor-cabs, of a house he had seen at Richmond with a garden that ran down to the river; and his voice, which was the voice of a man accustomed to being listened to, grew softer and more hesitant, and his large hands, which had never in their life turned a page with any true attention, turned and turned the brim of his hat.

And she—what did she feel, standing there in the half-dark with the ledgers and the unopened parcels and the faint, sweetish smell of the sizing in the bindings? She felt, she supposed, the warmth of it; the warmth of being wanted, of being chosen, of being for someone the centre of the room, the point towards which all the dusty volumes and the fading afternoon and the whole indifferent apparatus of London seemed to lean. He was kind. That was the thing she could not get past—he was kind, and kindness, in the long grey afternoons of a Charing Cross bookshop, in the years of standing behind a counter while the rain streaked the windows and the customers came in with their wet umbrellas and their small, sharp, particular wants, was not nothing. It was, in its way, a kind of wealth. And he had wealth of the other kind too—the house at Richmond, the garden, the motor-cab that waited at the kerb with its engine muttering, the future that opened out before her like a room she had never entered but could imagine, papered and furnished and warm.

“Miss Ellery,” he said—for that was her name, and he said it always with a little pause before it, as though the syllables were something he had to lift with care—“Miss Ellery, I wonder whether you might do me the honour of dining with me on Friday. There is a place in Jermyn Street—I am told the sole is very good.”

She looked at him. She looked at the hat in his hands, at the small crease of anxiety between his brows, at the way his collar sat, a little too high, a little too stiff, as though it had been chosen by a man who did not quite trust his own judgement in such matters. And she felt, suddenly, a tenderness for him so sharp it was almost pain—for his clumsiness, his earnestness, his great, blundering, well-meant kindness. “I should like that,” she said.

It was later, in the little room off the Tottenham Court Road where she lodged, with the gas-fire hissing and the fog pressing its grey face against the window, that she told Felix.

Felix Marsh—poet, or so he called himself, though the poems were few and the rejection slips many, and he lived on bread and tea and the occasional generosity of a cousin in the City—sat on the floor with his back against the bed and his long legs stretched out before him, and he looked at her with those eyes of his that always seemed to be seeing something just behind her, some other thing, some phrase or image that she could not see. “Dining,” he said. “At Jermyn Street. The sole is very good.”

“Don’t,” she said.

“And what will you wear?” he asked. “Have you a dress for Jermyn Street? Have you a dress for the sole?”

She turned away from him, toward the fire, toward the little blue flames that ran along the artificial coals. “He is kind,” she said. “He is not clever, but he is kind.”

“Kind,” said Felix. He said it slowly, turning it over, as though it were a word in a foreign language. “Kind. And wealthy. And you will sit at his table, and you will pour his tea, and you will listen to him talk of the house at Richmond and the garden that runs down to the river, and you will be—what? A ornament. A pretty thing in a glass case. A book he has bought and will never read.”

“That is not fair,” she said, but her voice was thin, and the fire hissed, and the fog pressed closer.

“You would be selling yourself,” he said. “You would be selling the thing that is you—the thing that reads, that thinks, that stands behind that counter and knows the difference between a first edition and a second—you would be selling it for a garden and a motor-cab and a man who does not know the difference between Keats and a railway timetable.”
