The grey light of the Channel did not so much illuminate the sea as it veiled it, a soft, bruised purple that seemed to breathe against the hull. Elara sat on the deck, her hands folded in her lap, watching the water chop and settle, chop and settle, in a rhythm that had become, over the past year, as familiar and indifferent as her own pulse. The ship groaned, a low, tectonic sigh that traveled up through the wooden planks and into the soles of her shoes, a sound that was less heard than felt, a vibration in the marrow. She was tired. It was not the sharp, aching fatigue of the journey, which had been long and stormy, but a deeper, more profound weariness, the kind that settles into the bones when one has seen too much of the sun and too little of the shadow.

She thought of the teak table in the veranda, the way the light fell across it in the late afternoon, turning the dust motes into a slow, golden snow. She thought of the jasmine, thick and white, clinging to the trellis, its scent so heavy it seemed to have weight, to press down upon the air. She thought of the servants, their faces smooth and unreadable, moving through the house like shadows, knowing everything and revealing nothing. She had been there for a year. A year is a long time. It is enough time for a tree to grow a new ring, for a river to change its course, for a woman to forget the sound of her own name in the language she was born with.

Elara looked down at her hands. They were pale, the skin stretched tight over the knuckles, the nails cut short and clean. They did not look like the hands of a woman who had sat in the shade of the mango trees, who had watched the monkeys leap from branch to branch, who had felt the heat rise from the earth in waves. They looked like the hands of a girl in London, waiting for a tea that would never come. There was a disconnect, a gulf between the woman who had been there and the woman who was here. The one who had been there was gone, dissolved into the heat, into the dust, into the endless, shimmering haze of the afternoon. The one who was here was a stranger, a shell, empty and waiting to be filled.

What would she say? When she reached the pier, when her friends came to meet her, their faces bright with expectation, their eyes searching for the exotic, the dramatic, the tale of adventure. What would she say? She would say, *The light was very beautiful.* She would say, *The air was very still.* She would say, *I saw a peacock.* And that would be all. She would not tell them of the silence that hung in the rooms, heavy and suffocating, the silence that was not empty but full, full of a presence that could not be named. She would not tell them of the way the time moved there, slow and viscous, like honey, how an hour could stretch into a day, a day into a week, until one forgot where one ended and the world began. She would not tell them of the fear, the quiet, creeping fear that had taken root in her heart, the fear that she was becoming someone else, someone who did not belong, someone who was being erased.

No, she would tell them nothing. The words would die in her throat, choked by the salt air, by the grey light, by the memory of the jasmine. It was better to keep it, to hold it close, to let it fester and grow, to let it be a secret, a dark garden in the heart of the self. To speak it would be to destroy it, to reduce it to a story, a trifle, a piece of conversation to be consumed and forgotten. She wanted it to remain real, to remain true, to remain a part of her, even if it was a part that no one else could see.

The ship turned, and the coast came into view, a pale, indistinct line on the horizon. It was not what she had expected. It was not green, not lush, not vibrant. It was grey, flat, and ordinary. The buildings on the shore were small, huddled together, like children afraid of the dark. The trees were bare, their branches scratching at the sky, dry and brittle. It looked cold. It looked hard. It looked like a place where things were finished, where nothing new was born.

Elara felt a pang of sorrow, a sharp, acute pain in her chest. She missed it. She missed the heat, the dust, the jasmine, the silence. She missed the feeling of being lost, of being adrift, of being suspended in time. Here, in this place, she was anchored, fixed, defined. She was Elara, the daughter, the friend, the woman who had been away. She was expected to be a certain way, to say certain things, to feel certain emotions. She was expected to be the same as she had been before, and she knew, with a certainty that was almost terrifying, that she was not.

The gap between who she was and who she had been was widening, stretching out like the sea, vast and unbridgeable. She could not cross it. She could not go back. She could only move forward, into this grey, flat world, carrying with her the weight of the invisible, the unsaid, the unshared. It was a burden, but it was also a treasure. It was the price of truth, the cost of seeing. And she would pay it, gladly, silently, without a word.
