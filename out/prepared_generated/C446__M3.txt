The bell of St. Bartholomew’s had scarce ceased its tolling when the Reverend Simon Catesby descended the three steps of the pulpit, his hands trembling against the worn oak. The nave of the church was filled with the coarse woollens and soot-stained faces of the weavers and croppers of Bridgeworth, and beyond them, in the family pew that bore the brass plate of the Holdsworth name, sat the object of his morning's terror and his evening's contemplation. Miss Margaret Holdsworth did not look at him as he passed into the vestry, but she kept her head bowed over her prayer book long after the final amen, and Simon knew, with the painful certainty that had possessed him these three months past, that she had listened to every word.

He had not meant to preach as he did. The text had been chosen weeks before, a mild exhortation from the Epistle to the Ephesians, but the sound of the looms had reached him even through the thick walls of the church, that ceaseless clatter that began before dawn and did not end until after dark, and he had thought of the children he had seen that morning stumbling through the churchyard, their faces grey with fatigue, their fingers blackened and crooked from the threads. And so he had set aside his prepared remarks and spoken instead of those who grind the faces of the poor, and of the master who withholds the wages of his labourers, and though he had not named Mr. Holdsworth, every soul in that church knew of whom he spoke, for there was only one mill owner in Bridgeworth, and the mill stood at the foot of the hill like a dark palace, its chimney reeking into the Yorkshire sky.

In the vestry Simon unfastened his surplice with unsteady fingers. Mr. Vicars, the elderly rector, sat by the fire and regarded him with an expression of weary apprehension. "You go too far, Catesby," the old man said. "Holdsworth is not a man to be crossed. He built the school, he paved the lane, he gives coal at Christmas. You will find there is little patience for a curate who bites the hand that feeds his parish."

Simon said nothing. He folded the surplice and placed it in the press. What could he say? That he had not sought this quarrel, that it had found him in the persons of the sick and the maimed who came to his door and could not pay for the doctor because the mill had cut their wages? That he had tried, God knew he had tried, to keep silent, and had found that silence sat in his throat like a stone?

He walked out through the churchyard into the thin October sun. The leaves on the beeches were yellow, and the river ran brown below the bridge. He had not gone twenty paces when he heard his name called, and turned to see Margaret Holdsworth coming toward him along the path, her bonnet strings blowing in the wind.

She was not beautiful in the fashion of the ladies he had seen in York, whose faces were soft and pale and unmarked by weather or care. Her face was brown from walking the hills, and her hands, though those of a gentleman's daughter, were large and capable, and he had seen her stop to help a child who had fallen in the lane, lifting the boy and wiping his tears with no more fuss than if he had been her own. She was four-and-twenty, a year younger than Simon himself, and she had refused, so the parish gossips said, at least two offers from the sons of neighbouring manufacturers.

"Mr. Catesby," she said, and stopped, and he saw that she was struggling with something she wished to say. "I wanted to tell you that I heard your sermon. All of it."

"I would that you had not," Simon said, before he could stop himself, and felt the blood rise to his face. "That is to say, I mean, it cannot have been agreeable to hear one's father spoken of in such terms."

"My father is a good man in many things," Margaret said quietly. "But he is wrong in this. I have seen the books at the mill, the wages and the fines. I have spoken to the women who work the carding engines. You did not say anything I have not thought myself."

She looked at him then with such directness that he found he could not meet her gaze, and fixed his eyes instead on the distant smoke of the mill. "Miss Holdsworth," he began, and did not know what would follow, for at that moment the gate of the churchyard swung open and Mr. Holdsworth himself appeared, a tall figure in a greatcoat, his face set in the expression of controlled displeasure that Simon had come to know as well as he knew the lines of his own Prayer Book.

"Margaret," her father said. He did not look at Simon. "Your mother is waiting in the carriage."

Margaret hesitated, and for an instant Simon saw in her face the same struggle that tore at his own heart, the war between the wish to speak and the knowledge that some words, once spoken, could not be unspoken. But she only said, "Yes, Father," and turned and walked away, and Simon stood alone in the churchyard with the wind turning the leaves about his feet.

He saw her again the following week, not by chance but by arrangement, for she sent a note to the vicarage asking him to call at the schoolroom, where she taught the younger children of the mill workers their letters. He went with a heavy heart, for he had begun to understand the nature of the feeling that had grown in him, and he knew it for a feeling that could bring nothing but pain to them both. But he went, because she had asked, and because he found that he could refuse her nothing.
