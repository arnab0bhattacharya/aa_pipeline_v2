The tide had come in with a kind of reluctant fury, dragging the pale, swollen body of the soldier from the deep water and depositing him upon the black shingle with the dull finality of a thing that had been thrown down and forgotten. It was early, before the sun had yet risen high enough to burn the mist from the clefts of the jagged rocks, and the air was thick with the smell of brine, rotting kelp, and the faint, sweetish odor of the dead. Doña Elena stood at the edge of the waterline, her hands clenched at her sides, her fingers blackened by the cold and the salt. She did not scream. In this place, on this coast where the republic was tearing itself apart in the name of order and liberty, screaming was a luxury one could not afford. It was a sound that attracted attention, and attention, in times like these, was a dangerous currency.

The man lay on his side, his face turned toward the sea, his skin the color of wet clay, bloated and slack. He wore the uniform of the National Guard, the blue wool stained with the grey of the surf, but the insignia on the shoulder had been torn away, as if by a hand that had tried to erase his allegiance before the water erased his life. Beside him, half-buried in the wet sand, lay a leather satchel, swollen and dark. Elena’s heart, which had long since grown hard as the flint stones of the beach to protect her from the grief of losing her husband to the same indifferent sea, gave a single, sharp throb. It was not pity. It was fear. Fear is a cold thing, creeping into the bones, but it is also a sharp thing, cutting clear. She knew what lay in that satchel. She had seen men like this before, men who died not for glory, but for secrets.

She approached the body slowly, testing the shifting sand with the soles of her heavy shoes. The silence of the village was heavy, oppressive, broken only by the rhythmic crashing of the waves and the distant, mournful cry of a gull. There was no one else on the beach. The fishermen were already out, their sails like white ghosts against the grey horizon, or they were huddled in their huts, sleeping off the previous night’s work. But Elena knew that eyes were watching from the windows of the whitewashed houses that clung to the cliff above. They were watching to see what she would do. They were watching to see who she would tell.

She knelt beside the satchel. It was locked, the brass clasp green with verdigris. She pulled at it, but it held fast, swollen by the water. She looked at the face of the dead man. It was a young face, still, with the softness of boyhood not yet fully erased by the hardness of war. He looked like her son, though her son was dead, and had been dead for three years, taken not by the sea, but by the fever that swept through the lower quarters of the city. There was a terrible symmetry in it, a cruel joke played by the universe. She felt a surge of anger, hot and sudden, rising in her throat. She was illiterate. She could not read the words that were locked inside that leather case. She could not know if they were pleas for mercy, or orders for execution, or love letters to a woman who would never know. She was blind to the truth, and in this republic, blindness was a kind of death.

She stood up, her knees stiff and aching. The wind picked up, whipping the mist into her face, stinging her eyes. She looked up at the village. The houses were silent, their doors shut tight. Two men stood on the porch of the house at the end of the lane, the house of the mayor, Señor Valdes. They were watching her. Valdes was a man of many faces, a man who had served the government when it was convenient and whispered to the rebels when it was safe. He was a man of the middle, the man who stayed alive by being everyone and no one. Beside him stood Captain Rios, a man of iron, whose loyalty to the government was absolute and whose methods were equally absolute. They stood side by side, a strange alliance of compromise and ruthlessness, their eyes fixed on her.

Elena made her decision. It was not a decision born of wisdom, but of necessity. She could not burn the letters; the fire would be seen, and the smell of burning paper would carry on the wind. She could not throw them back into the sea; the tide would bring them back, and the men would find them, and they would find her. She had to take them to a reader. She had to choose.

She picked up the satchel. It was heavy, weighed down by the water. She slung it over her shoulder and began to walk up the path toward the village. The sand crunched under her feet, a sound that seemed too loud in the stillness. She did not look at the men on the porch. To look was to acknowledge them, and to acknowledge was to invite them. She walked with her head down, her eyes fixed on the path before her, counting her steps, trying to steady her breathing.

The path was steep, lined with scrubby bushes that clung to the rock. As she climbed, the view of the sea opened up before her, vast and grey and indifferent. The waves continued their endless, rhythmic assault on the shore, oblivious to the dead man, to the letters, to the woman walking up the hill. The world was large and cruel, and it did not care about the small, fragile lives that moved across its surface.
