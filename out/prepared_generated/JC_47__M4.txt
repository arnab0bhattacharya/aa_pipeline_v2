The mule track, which was no more than a scar upon the mountain’s flank, had brought me to the plaza of Santa Maria de la Nieve in the late afternoon, and the silence of that high place was a thing I felt upon my skin, a fine, dry powder of quiet that had settled over the whitewashed walls and the red-tiled roofs and the two squat towers of the church. It was a silence that seemed to have been there for centuries, and the arrival of a stranger, even one so weary and so insignificant as myself, was an event that the very stones appeared to note with a kind of brooding patience. I had come, I confess, with no clear purpose beyond a vague curiosity, a desire to see with my own eyes the theatre of certain rumours that had drifted down to the coast, rumours of a priest who was said to be a saint and of a treasure that was said to be a legend, and of a war that was said to be a butchery—tales that had mingled in the telling until the truth of them was as lost as a river in a swamp.

The church itself was the only substantial building; it stood at the far end of the plaza, its façade cracked and stained by the weathers of a hundred seasons, its great door of dark, studded wood standing open upon a darkness that seemed to breathe out the coolness of the tomb. Within, I found the father—a small, wiry man with a face like a withered apple and eyes that held the still, deep gleam of a mountain tarn. He was kneeling before the altar, and he did not rise at my entrance, but only turned his head slightly, as if he had been expecting me, or someone very like me, for a long time. His name, he said, was Father Anselmo, and when I spoke of the war and of the rumours, he nodded slowly, as a man nods at the recollection of a familiar pain.

“The treasure,” he said, in a voice that was low and unhurried, “is not of gold, señor, though there is gold in it. It is the heart of this village. And the colonel who comes for it—he was a boy here once. He served at this altar. He was my altar boy.” He paused, and his gaze went past me to the open door and the white blaze of the afternoon beyond. “He will come before the week is out. I have known it for a month. And I have hidden it where he will not find it, though he will turn every stone in this place to look.”

I asked him where he had hidden it, and he smiled a little, a smile that had in it more of sorrow than of mirth. “In the one place, señor, that a man who has ceased to believe will never think to search. In the tabernacle. Behind the ciborium. He will look in the crypt, in the sacristy, in the hollow of the great bell—he will even dig up the floor of the nave, for he was ever a thorough boy. But he will not look in the tabernacle. To him it is an empty box. And so the thing he desires most in the world will be within arm’s reach of him, and he will not see it.”

The days that followed were days of a strange, suspended quality, the heat lying heavy upon the village like a hand, the silence broken only by the distant mutter of the guns, which grew nearer each day, and by the thin, persistent ringing of the church bell at the hours of prayer. The people of Santa Maria de la Nieve went about their business with a fatalistic calm, as though they had long since surrendered the outcome to powers beyond their control. And Father Anselmo moved among them like a man already dead to the world, his step light, his words few, his eyes fixed upon some inward vision that none of us could share.

Then, on the fifth day, the soldiers came. They came not with the rush and clamour of a charge, but with a slow, deliberate tramp that was somehow more terrible, and they filled the plaza with the dust of their coming and the smell of sweat and leather and gun-oil. The colonel was a tall man, lean and hard, with a face that might have been handsome had it not been for the tightness about the mouth and the cold, appraising look of the eyes. He dismounted slowly, and he stood in the centre of the plaza and looked around him at the church, at the houses, at the people, with the air of a man returning to a place he had known long ago and found smaller than his memory had painted it.

Father Anselmo came out to meet him, and for a long moment the two men stood facing each other in the white light, the priest small and worn and serene, the soldier tall and powerful and uneasy. Then the colonel spoke, and his voice was harsh and flat, as though he were reading from a document.

“You have something that belongs to the army of the Republic, padre. You will give it to me.”

“I have nothing, my son,” the priest said, “that belongs to any army. What I have belongs to God and to these poor people.”

The colonel’s face darkened. “Do not play with me, old man. I know what you have. I know where it came from. I know what it is. And I will have it, if I have to pull down this church stone by stone.”

“Then you will have to pull it down,” the priest said, and his voice was very gentle. “But you will not find it. Not because I am clever, but because you have forgotten the place where you yourself once kept it.”
