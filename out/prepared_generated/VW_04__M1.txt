The bus heaved itself up Ludgate Hill with a groan of its engine, a sound that seemed to come from the very stones of the city, and the conductor, steadying himself against the rail, saw her again. Not that he knew her name; she was only the girl with the hair the colour of weak tea, always in the same grey coat, always with her eyes fixed on some point in the middle distance, as if the whole of London—the press of bodies, the clatter of the horses, the sudden chill of a shadow thrown by St Paul’s—were a picture she was looking at rather than a place she was in. She was not pretty, not with the sharp, knowing prettiness of the girls from the West End, but there was a stillness about her that made the other passengers seem like moths fluttering at a lamp. She paid her halfpenny with a hand that was always cold, and took her ticket, and her silence was a kind of weight in the crowded vehicle. He wondered, as he swung the bell for the next stop, what she did, where she went; a question that dissolved the moment she stepped down into the grey stream of humanity and was gone.

The stream carried her to a tall, narrow house in a street of similar houses, and here Mrs. Fothergill, who was the soul of the house, received her. Mrs. Fothergill believed in the shaping of young women, in the gentle pressure of Christian example and regular meals. She saw in Miss Pritchard a blank page, and it was her duty to inscribe upon it the virtues of punctuality, thrift, and a hopeful spirit. "And how was the day, my dear?" she would ask, her voice as soft and persistent as the ticking of the grandfather clock in the hall. And the girl would murmur, "Very well, thank you, Mrs. Fothergill," and her eyes, which were the colour of the winter sea, would give nothing away. It was a disappointment, for Mrs. Fothergill felt she had so much to give. She would watch the girl eat her supper, a small, precise creature at the long table, and feel a pang of something not quite pity, but a kind of frustration. What did she think of, in that little room at the top of the stairs, with its view of chimney pots? Did she pray? Did she read the tracts left carefully on her dressing-table? The girl was a mystery, a quiet island in the sea of chatter and shared anxieties that was the hostel, and Mrs. Fothergill, who loved a mystery only so she might solve it, felt herself quite baffled.

In the office, Mr. Hemming noticed her for a different reason. He was a junior clerk, with ambitions that outstripped his salary, and he saw in the world a series of transactions. Miss Pritchard was a transaction of the most inefficient sort. She typed slowly, her fingers hovering for a second too long over the keys, and she would sometimes stop altogether, her gaze drifting to the window, where the soot-stained brick of the opposite building was the only view. He saw the tremor in her hand as she placed a fresh sheet of paper in the machine, and felt a flash of impatience, which was followed, to his annoyance, by a flicker of something else. A curiosity. Her letters, which he often had to check, were correct but lifeless, like pressed flowers. He imagined her evenings were as grey and ordered as her days. He once, in a moment of weakness, said to her, "It's a fine day, Miss Pritchard," and she had turned to him with a look of such startled, unguarded surprise that he felt he had stumbled into a private room. "Is it?" she had said, and looked at the window as if for the first time. He went back to his columns of figures, feeling oddly unsettled. She was a problem, an anomaly in the neat ledger of his day, and he found, to his consternation, that he was beginning to look forward to the sight of her quiet face.

And in a stone house in Yorkshire, where the wind came down from the moors with a clean, sharp sound, Mrs. Pritchard sat by the fire and read the letters that came every week. She read them once for the news, which was always the same—work is well, the weather is changeable, I am keeping well—and a second time for what lay between the lines. She held the thin paper close to the lamp, as if proximity could unlock its secrets. She knew her daughter’s hand, the way the 't' was crossed with a sharp, decisive stroke, the way the lines were kept perfectly straight. But the words themselves were like little boats on a vast, grey sea. They told her nothing of the girl she had sent away, the girl who had loved the scent of wet earth and the cry of the curlew. London, she imagined, was a great machine, grinding up lives and spitting out these neat, colourless reports. She would fold the letter and place it with the others in her workbox, a small, pale bundle of a life she could no longer touch. Outside, the wind rattled the windowpane, and the familiar, lonely sound of the moor filled the house. She was not angry, only sad, with a deep, quiet sadness that was as much a part of the landscape as the stone walls and the grey sky. She thought of the girl on the bus, in the office, in the little room, and she wondered if she was happy, a question as vast and unanswerable as the moor itself. The fire crackled, and in its shifting light, the room seemed very empty.
