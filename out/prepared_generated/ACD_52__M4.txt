I had taken my rooms in Hatton Garden for the sake of the light, which falls slantwise through the tall windows of the upper storey in a manner most agreeable to a student of the pencil. The neighbourhood is, as you may know, the very heart of the diamond trade, and the streets beneath my window were for ever alive with the bustle of jewellers’ messengers and the cautious opening and shutting of iron-shuttered doors. It was a quarter of quiet fortunes and loud silences, and I had thought it the most peaceable spot in London until the affair of Mr. Ezra Gannet.

My lodgings were upon the second floor, and beneath me lay the establishment of Mr. Gannet, a jeweller of long standing, whose window displayed a modest but choice assortment of rings and watch-chains upon beds of faded velvet. He was a small, precise man of sixty, with a bald head that shone like a polished stone, and hands that were never still. He spoke little, but always with a certain formal courtesy, and I had set him down in my own mind as one of those harmless eccentrics whom London breeds in her quieter corners.

It was in the third week of my residence that I first heard the hammering. It began each night at about the hour of eleven, and continued, with intervals of rest, until two or three in the morning. It was not the heavy, ringing blow of the smith, but a light, quick, persistent tapping, as though some small and delicate work were being done with an infinite patience. The first night I took it for the repairing of a clock, and thought no more of it. But when it had continued for a fortnight without intermission, and when I had observed that the shop was dark and the shutters up during the whole of the time, I began to feel that curiosity which is, I suppose, the common property of our sex.

I met Mr. Gannet upon the stairs one morning and put the question to him with as much indifference as I could command.

“You are a light sleeper, Miss Vane,” said he, with a little bow. “I fear I disturb you. It is my clocks. I have a fancy for repairing them after hours, when the day’s business is done. The quiet of the night is favourable to delicate work.”

I accepted the explanation, as one does, and yet it did not wholly satisfy me. For one thing, I had never seen a clock in his window, nor any sign of that trade which he professed. For another, the tapping had a rhythm of its own, a pause and a resumption, which seemed to me more like the cautious labour of a man who works at something he would not have known.

It was upon a Tuesday that the hammering ceased altogether. I had grown so accustomed to it that I slept but ill in its absence, and lay listening for the familiar sound until the grey dawn crept over the chimney-pots. In the morning the shop was shut, and the shutters remained up all day. In the evening a constable came and rattled at the door, and went away again. By the Thursday the whole street was talking, and I had my first visit from the police.

The inspector was a stout, red-faced man named Calder, who took my statement with a labouring pen and asked me a great many questions which seemed to me to have no bearing upon the matter. I told him all I knew, and he thanked me and went away, and I heard afterwards that he had formed the theory that Mr. Gannet had gone off upon a journey and would return in good time. But the days passed and he did not return, and the shop remained shut, and the street began to whisper of things more serious than a journey.

It was then that I bethought me of a man whom I had met at a studio in Chelsea, a quiet, sallow, hawk-faced person who had once solved a little mystery of a missing sketch-book in a manner that had astonished us all. His name was Mr. Sebastian Thorne, and he had rooms in Baker Street, and was said to be consulted by those who had exhausted the ordinary channels of inquiry. I wrote to him, and he came to see me upon the Friday afternoon.

He was a tall, lean man, with a stoop that spoke of much bending over small objects, and eyes that had a way of fixing upon you as though you were a specimen under glass. He listened to my story without interruption, his long fingers pressed together, and when I had done he asked me a single question.

“You are an artist, Miss Vane. Tell me, was the hammering regular in its beat, or did it vary?”

“It varied,” said I. “There would be a run of quick taps, and then a pause, and then a slower stroke, as though something were being fitted.”

He nodded, and a thin smile crossed his face. “You have described the very rhythm of the jeweller’s trade,” said he. “A quick series of taps to close a setting, a pause while the stone is examined, and a slower stroke to fix the bezel. Mr. Gannet was not repairing clocks, I fancy. He was setting stones.”

“But the shop was shut,” I objected.

“Precisely. And that is why the work was done at night. A man who sets stones in a darkened shop, with the shutters up, is a man who does not wish his work to be seen. The question is, what work was it, and why has he vanished?”

He rose and walked to the window, and stood for a moment looking down into the street. “You have been most useful, Miss Vane,” said he. “I shall ask you to do one thing more. To-night, if you will be so good, leave your window open a little and listen. If the hammering should begin again, note the exact hour and the exact rhythm. And above all, do not go down.”
