The autumn winds howled through the decayed turrets of Grasmere Hall, bearing upon their gusts the heavy, unintermitting rains of a Cumberland November. I sat alone in the vast and wainscoted chamber which had once been the pride of my ancestors, listening to the rattling of the casements and the distant roaring of the river Wyer, which, swollen by the tempest, rushed with a savage fury through the valley. I was entirely alone. The vast pile of stone, whose walls had echoed to the footsteps of my forefathers and the joyous voices of my offspring, had become a mighty sepulchre, holding within its dreary and mouldering courts nothing but the ghostly relics of my departed race. My sons, in whose youthful brows I had read the promise of a long and honourable line, were laid beneath the yew-trees of the village churchyard; and the gentle partner of my youth, whose smile had been the bright luminary of my existence, had been taken from me in a season of peculiar bitterness, leaving me to drag out a joyless and desolate old age, a solitary trunk from which every tender branch had been ruthlessly stript.

For many years I had lived thus, feeding upon the bitter bread of remembrance, shunning the light of the sun and the faces of my fellow-creatures. My tenants looked upon me as a being of another and a darker world, and even the hardy dalesmen, as they pursued their cattle over the fells, would point to the shattered tower of the Hall and speak of its master as one whose heart was buried in the graves of his kindred. It was during this period of intense and unvarying gloom, when the very consciousness of existence seemed a burden too heavy to be borne, that a gentle and unexpected influence stole upon my solitary life, as the soft breath of spring sometimes steals upon the frozen and desolate crags of the mountains, awakening a sudden, though transient, verdure.

Among the few who yet resided upon my wasted estates was a poor and honest tenant, whose farm lay in the hollow of the hills at some distance from the manor. His daughter, a young girl of scarce fourteen summers, named Ellen, had been accustomed in her childhood to bring the rent to the Hall, accompanied by her father; but of late, owing to the old man’s declining health, she had come alone. I had regarded her at first with an indifference bordering upon disgust; for her presence broke in upon the solemn and majestic silence which I had cultivated as my only solace. But there was in her clear and open countenance, in the deep and melancholy interest with which she regarded my solitary and decaying state, an expression of such pure and touching sympathy, that I found myself, in spite of my habitual austerity, looking for her coming, and welcoming her with a softened and almost paternal regard.

It was on an evening of the latter end of October, when the blasts of the approaching winter had already stripped the last lingering leaf from the branches of the ash and the oak, that Ellen came to the Hall. She found me seated by the expiring embers of a wood fire, wrapped in a heavy cloak, and gazing with a fixed and vacant eye upon the decaying tapestry that hung upon the walls. She approached me with a timid and hesitating step, and seating herself upon a low stool at my feet, she looked up in my face with an expression of such deep and tender commiseration, that the long-frozen current of my affections seemed suddenly to melt and rush with an impetuous and overwhelming force through my veins.

“Alas!” she said, in a voice whose melancholy sweetness thrilled to my very soul, “how wretched must be the heart that is bereft of all it loved! Tell me, sir, for I would fain know, have you then no one to love, no one to whom you can unfold the heavy griefs that weigh upon your spirits? Is there no living being to whom you can speak of the departed?”

I started, as if a voice from the tomb had suddenly addressed me. I had so long been a stranger to the language of affection, that it fell upon my ear with a startling and almost painful novelty. But the earnest and imploring look of the gentle girl reassured me, and I felt, I know not why, a sudden and irresistible impulse to pour into her youthful and sympathetic bosom the long-hoarded and burning tale of my misfortunes.

“Yes, Ellen,” I replied, while the tears, which for years had seemed dried up in the fountains of my heart, started unbidden to my eyes, “I have known love, and joy, and the sweet communion of kindred souls; but they are gone, all gone, and I am left a solitary and blighted trunk, a mark for the storms of heaven and the scorn of men. Listen, then, and I will tell thee the story of my life; a story of such wild and terrible vicissitudes, that thou wilt wonder how one frail and mortal frame could have endured the weight of so much wretchedness. I had a wife, Ellen, as gentle and as beautiful as the spring flowers that enamel thy native valleys; and I had children, the pride and the delight of my age. But they were taken from me, one by one, by the relentless hand of death; and the light of my dwelling was extinguished, and the music of my hearth was hushed for ever. I have wandered since, a lonely and miserable outcast, among the ruins of my former happiness, seeking in vain for some alleviation of my woe, some refuge from the gnawing and never-dying worm of memory. I have seen my friends drop around me like leaves in autumn; I have stood by the deathbed of all I held dear on earth; and now, in the decrepitude of age, I am left to drag out a miserable and lingering existence, without hope, without comfort, without a being to whom I can look for sympathy in the hour of dissolution.”
