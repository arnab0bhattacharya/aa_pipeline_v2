It is a common observation, perhaps none the less true for being common, that the mind of man, when once awakened to the desire of knowledge, finds in that very desire a fatality which no prudential reasoning can lay. To the young farm labourer in the Derbyshire dales, whose daily existence is bounded by the drystone walls of his parish and the slow revolutions of the agricultural year, the sudden opening of a book is like the breaking of a seal upon a wider universe; and having tasted of that intellectual fruit, he finds the appetite henceforward irremediable, working in him as a hidden ferment that changes the whole texture of his thoughts and inclinations.

Such was the case with Seth Bowden, a young man of five-and-twenty, employed on the lands of Mr. Thomas Heywood at Upper Binton, a village lying some four miles from Bakewell. Seth had been fortunate—or unfortunate, as the event might prove—in having been sent, during a long convalescence from a fever in his eighteenth year, to lodge at the cottage of his aunt in Wirksworth, where his cousin, a Methodist of some book-learning, had left behind a small shelf of battered volumes. In those weeks of slow recovery, Seth, who until then had known only the letters of his own name and the rough cipher necessary for market reckonings, had laboriously taught himself to read. The labour was immense, for the type was smudged and the grammar of the volume—an abridged history of England—was of that pompous and involved character which might have tried the patience of a far more educated intelligence; but Seth persevered with the same stubborn patience with which he would have broken a stubborn piece of ground, and emerged from his convalescence a reader, though a secret one, knowing well that such accomplishments were viewed with suspicion by his fellows, and with something approaching resentment by his superiors.

For a while he read in private, borrowing what he could, and spending his Sunday afternoons not at the alehouse but in the meadow by the river, with a book open on his knee. But solitude in the exercise of a newfound faculty soon becomes wearisome; the mind craves the corroboration of another mind, the test of discourse, the warmth of shared astonishment. It was thus that Seth, by slow degrees, gathered around him a small company of like-minded young men—labourers, a carpenter's apprentice, a young woman who worked at the cotton mill in the next valley, and an elderly self-taught weaver whose radical opinions were mellowed by a natural mildness of temper. They began to meet on winter evenings in the loose-box at the end of Mr. Heywood's great barn, where the straw was dry and the walls, though draughty, kept out the worst of the wind. Mr. Heywood, a man of liberal disposition for his time and station, had given his consent with a good-natured wave of the hand, saying that if the young folk chose to read instead of drinking, it was no skin off his nose, and might even keep them out of mischief.

For some months the society flourished. They read the abridged history, they read a copy of Bunyan which one of their number had inherited from a grandmother, they read sermons, and they read, with much puzzling over hard words, a treatise on the improvement of soils which Seth had obtained from a bookseller in Bakewell. The weaver, old Joshua Lambert, would sometimes bring a sheet of verse, printed on broadside, which he read with trembling voice and glistening eyes; and the young mill-woman, whose name was Martha Clay, would listen with that still attention which is the highest compliment the unlettered can pay to literature. Seth himself read with a gravity and carefulness that gave weight to every sentence, as though he were handling something fragile and precious, which indeed he was.

The difficulty began, as difficulties so often do, not from any deliberate intention of rebellion, but from the natural and almost insensible extension of an appetite. A pedlar, passing through Upper Binton in the autumn of 1817, had among his wares a bundle of old newspapers—some from Nottingham, some from London, a few weeks stale but still full of the ferment of public affairs. Seth bought them for a few pence, carried them to the barn, and read aloud to the society an account of a meeting of weavers in Manchester, who had petitioned for parliamentary reform, and of the speeches made therein. The effect was electrical. These young men and women, who had never conceived of their lives as part of any larger current, who had thought of Parliament as a distant and unapproachable institution, like the weather or the plague, suddenly heard their own condition reflected back to them through the printed word, as a man hears his own voice for the first time in an echo. The discussion that followed was heated, confused, and not always wise; but it was alive in a way that the reading of old sermons had never been.

Mr. Heywood heard of the matter within a week. In a village, news of any departure from the accustomed round travels with a speed and precision that would do credit to a military dispatch. The farmer was told, by his dairymaid, who had been told by the carpenter's wife, who had been told by her son, that Seth Bowden's reading society had taken to reading newspapers and talking sedition. Mr. Heywood was a man who, in the ordinary course of things, was neither harsh nor suspicious; but the year 1817 was not an ordinary year. The country was uneasy; reports of seditious meetings, of drilling on the moors, of inflammatory handbills, were in every mouth; and a country gentleman, however well-disposed, could not but feel that the name of reform was a name which, once admitted into his barn, might bring consequences he could neither foresee nor control.
