The fog had lain over Belgravia since morning, thick and yellow, muffling the clatter of hansoms in Eaton Place and reducing the gas-lamps to pale smudges of light. It was upon such an evening, the twenty-third of November, that Lady Cynthia Mowbray stood in the morning-room of her house in Chester Square, holding between her finger and thumb a card of invitation which she had read, for the fifth time, with an expression of profound bewilderment.

The card was of cream-coloured pasteboard, engraved with her own crest, and bore these words in a fine copperplate hand:

*Lady Cynthia Mowbray requests the pleasure of the company of Mr. and Mrs. Alistair Finch at dinner on Tuesday, the twenty-third of November, at eight o'clock.*

*R.S.V.P.*

The handwriting was her own. Of that there could be no manner of doubt. The loop of the capital M, the peculiar flourish upon the final y, the very slant of the letters—all were as familiar to her as her own reflection. Yet she had written no such card. She had, in fact, issued no invitations whatsoever for this evening, having planned a quiet night with a novel and a tray in her own apartments.

"Barker," said she, addressing her butler, who stood in the doorway with the wooden composure of his class, "you are certain that no other cards were delivered?"

"Quite certain, my lady. This was brought by hand this afternoon, and there have been no others."

"And you say that Mr. and Mrs. Finch have already arrived?"

"They are in the drawing-room, my lady, together with Colonel Prescott and Mr. Villiers."

Lady Cynthia pressed her fingers to her temples. The situation was absurd, and yet it filled her with a vague disquiet which she could not entirely explain. She was a woman of five-and-forty, widow of the late Sir George Mowbray, whose fortune had been made in the China trade, and she was accustomed to the small eccentricities of society. But this was beyond eccentricity. This was a practical joke of a singularly ill-bred character, or it was something else—something which she did not care to name.

"Barker," she said at last, "send a boy at once to number twenty-two, Baker Street, with this note. It is for Mr. Sherlock Holmes. If he is not at home, let the boy wait. Tell him that Lady Cynthia Mowbray desires his presence immediately upon a matter of urgency. Take a cab yourself if necessary."

The butler received the note with a slight inclination of his head, and withdrew.

---

The drawing-room of the house in Chester Square was a long, lofty apartment, furnished in the heavy manner of the early Victorian era, with damask hangings of a deep crimson and furniture of carved mahogany. When Lady Cynthia entered, she found her four guests disposed about the room in attitudes of varying unease.

Mrs. Finch, a plump and voluble woman of fifty, was seated upon the sofa, fanning herself with a lace handkerchief, though the evening was by no means warm. Her husband, a thin, nervous man with a pronounced stoop, stood by the window, peering out into the fog with the air of one who wished himself elsewhere. Colonel Prescott, a bluff, red-faced man with a white moustache and the unmistakable bearing of the Indian Army, was helping himself liberally from the decanter upon the sideboard. And in the corner, half-hidden in the shadow of a great standard lamp, sat Mr. Villiers.

It was Mr. Villiers who drew Lady Cynthia's eye, and held it. He was a man of perhaps thirty-five, with a pale, handsome face and dark hair worn rather longer than the fashion. His evening dress was correct, his manners were impeccable, and yet there was about him an indefinable air of strain—a tension in the way he held his glass, a flicker in the glance which he cast about the room. Lady Cynthia observed that his hands were trembling.

"Lady Cynthia," said Mrs. Finch, rising with a rustle of silk, "I must say that I was vastly surprised to receive your invitation. I had thought you were quite out of society this season."

"I fear there has been some mistake," said Lady Cynthia, gravely. "I sent no invitation."

A silence fell upon the room. Colonel Prescott set down his glass with a little clink.

"No invitation?" said he. "But my dear lady, I received your card myself. It was in my letter-box this morning."

"As did I," said Mr. Finch, turning from the window. "And I confess I thought it strange, for we had not heard from you in a twelvemonth."

Lady Cynthia looked from one to the other. "And you, Mr. Villiers? Did you also receive a card?"

The young man started, as though he had been lost in thought. "I—yes, Lady Cynthia. It was delivered by hand."

"And you came?"

"I came."

There was something in his tone—a note of desperation, almost of appeal—which struck Lady Cynthia forcibly. She was about to speak, when the door opened and Barker appeared.

"Mr. Sherlock Holmes," he announced.

---

The man who entered was tall and lean, with a hawk-like nose and eyes of a cold, grey brilliance which seemed to take in every detail of the room in a single sweeping glance. He was followed by a shorter, stouter man with a military moustache and a doctor's bag in his hand.

"Lady Cynthia," said Holmes, advancing with a slight bow. "Your note reached me at a fortunate moment. I was upon the point of going out. This is my friend and colleague, Dr. Watson, whose discretion you may rely upon absolutely."

"You are very good to come so quickly," said Lady Cynthia. "I confess that I am at a loss to understand the events of this evening, and I had heard that you were accustomed to—to unravel mysteries."

"Mysteries," said Holmes, with a faint smile, "are my trade. Pray tell me everything, and omit no detail, however trivial it may appear."
