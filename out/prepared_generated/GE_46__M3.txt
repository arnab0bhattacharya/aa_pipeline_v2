It is a common error in our arithmetic of human felicity to suppose that an elevation in worldly circumstance must needs bring with it a proportional increase of that tranquil satisfaction which we call happiness. The young woman who walked along the deep-rutted lane towards Yelden village, on a damp afternoon in the latter end of October, carried about her an air of provincial refinement that sat upon her with a somewhat anomalous weight. Prudence Galloway had been for three years an upper housemaid at Marston Hall, and the strict discipline of that great establishment, together with the sight of fine linens and the hearing of high-born speech, had altered her outward bearing in a fashion that was not altogether pleasing to the rustic eye. She wore a cloak of dark grey wool, neatly darted, and her bonnet, though of plain straw, was tied with a ribbon that spoke of town fashions; yet beneath this composed exterior there was a heavy, beating consciousness of a secret that made her fingers tremble against the latch of her father's cottage gate.

The cottage stood at the end of the village street, its whitewashed front half hidden by a venerable damson tree, the boughs of which were now almost bare. As Prudence lifted the latch, the door was opened from within, and her mother, Mrs. Galloway, stood on the stone floor, shading her eyes against the grey afternoon light. Mrs. Galloway was a woman of that ruddy, robust aspect which belongs to the Staffordshire housewife accustomed to the baking of oat-cakes and the brewing of strong ale. Her speech was of the broadest local kind, and her greeting, though warm, was mingled with a shrewd, motherly observation that quickly penetrated the girl's fine manners.

"Eh, lass, tha'rt coom then," she said, drawing her daughter in and closing the door against the chill. "Tha looks main peaked, Prudence. Sit thee down by th' fire; I'll put th' kettle on. I thought tha'd ha' been coom yesterday."

Prudence sat down on the wooden settle, her hands folded in her lap, and looked at the fire with an absent gaze. The familiar sizzle of the kettle and the smell of the damp stone floor brought back to her the narrow round of her early life with a vividness that was almost oppressive; but the sense of her secret, like a heavy stone in her bosom, made these familiar objects seem far off, as if she were viewing them across a gulf.

Her mother, bustling about with the teapot, cast frequent glances at her daughter. Mrs. Galloway had a natural sharpness of perception, sharpened still further by maternal solicitude. She noted the unusual paleness of Prudence’s cheek, the nervous twitching of her fingers, and a certain heavy droop of the eyelids that spoke of sleepless nights.

"Tha'rt noane thyself, Prudence," she said at last, setting down the teapot with a decided thud. "What ails thee? Is it a bad business up at th' Hall? Has that old madam been giving thee trouble?"

"No, Mother," said Prudence, with a faint smile. "It is nothing. I am only tired with the journey. The coach was crowded, and I had to sit beside a woman with a squalling infant."

But Mrs. Galloway was not to be so easily put off. She sat down on a stool opposite her daughter, and, folding her arms, looked at her with a gaze that was both affectionate and searching.

"Nay, nay, lass; I can see further than that. Tha'rt hiding summat. I know thee, Prudence, from thy childhood up. Is it a man?"

Prudence flushed crimson, and then grew pale again. She turned her face away, and her lip quivered. Her mother's directness had struck home. It was indeed a man—a footman at the Hall, one who had promised her marriage and then, under pressure from his family, had withdrawn his suit, leaving her with a secret that was already beginning to manifest itself in a certain fullness of her figure and a frequent faintness. But she could not, she would not, tell her mother this. The shame of it seemed to her worse than death.

"Mother," she said, in a low, steady voice, "I wish you would not question me. I am tired. Let me be."

Mrs. Galloway, seeing her distress, forbore to press her further at that moment, but she went about her household tasks with a heavy heart, casting many an anxious look at the silent figure on the settle.

When Mr. Galloway came in from his work in the fields, his great boots caked with mud, he greeted his daughter with a rough, hearty affection. He was a man of few words, slow of speech and thought, but with a kind heart underneath his weather-beaten exterior. He sat down to his supper of bacon and oat-cake, and after a time, when the meal was drawing to a close, he cleared his throat, as a preliminary to speech.

"Well, Prudence," he said, "and how long art thou going to stop wi' us?"

"I don't know, Father," she replied. "Perhaps a week or two."

"Eh, but tha' must think of staying longer than that," he said, leaning back in his chair and looking at her with a kind of stubborn determination. "Tha'rt twenty-two now, and it's time tha wert settled. There's young Seth Arkwright, down at the smithy—he's been asking after thee again. He's a good lad, Seth, and a good workman. He's been waiting for thee these three years, and it's time tha'rt making up thy mind."

Prudence's heart gave a great throb. Seth Arkwright! The very name brought back a flood of memories—memories of her early girlhood, of walks along the lanes, of his strong, honest face and quiet, steadfast ways. She had liked him once, before she went to the Hall and learned to despise the rustic life. But now, with her secret weighing upon her, the thought of marriage with Seth seemed a mockery—a thing impossible.
