The great hall of the Institut de Sainte-Clotilde was a furnace of expectation. Outside, a November rain fell on Brussels with a cold, insistent fury, but within, the air was thick with the scent of hot wax, damp wool, and the cloying perfume of the hothouse flowers arranged for the occasion. Rows of chairs were filled with the city’s dignitaries—bankers with their wives in silks, a solemn-faced bishop, and a general whose medals glinted in the gaslight. They had come to witness the intellectual bloom of their daughters, and I, Margaret Vance, an Englishwoman in a foreign land, stood with the other teachers at the back, a plain grey sparrow amongst birds of paradise.

My heart, usually a stern and disciplined organ, beat with a painful, irregular rhythm. For weeks, I had coached my most promising pupil, a girl named Hélène, in the recitation of a poem. It was my own work, written in the quiet hours of the night, a piece I had dared to call ‘The Moors of Yorkshire’. It was an attempt to capture in verse the wild, stark beauty of my homeland, the fierce freedom I felt in the wind that swept over the heather, and the deep, unshakeable truth I found in its stony silence. To Hélène, I had imparted not merely the words, but the very spirit of the thing—the necessary passion, the defiant pride.

Now, the moment of her performance had arrived. Hélène, a slender girl with a face as pale and earnest as a martyr’s, stepped onto the small, draped stage. Her eyes, wide and dark, found mine for a fleeting second. I gave her a small, encouraging nod, praying she would not be overcome by the oppressive weight of the room.

Then, Monsieur Dubois, the Director of the Institut, a man whose portly figure and placid smile concealed a soul of polished granite, stepped forward to the lectern. He cleared his throat, a sound that silenced the last rustlings of the audience.

“Esteemed guests,” he began, his voice a smooth, oily baritone that filled the hall without effort. “What you are about to hear is the fruit of my own leisure hours, a small tribute to the rugged landscape of a sister nation, and a meditation on the strength of the human spirit. I have entrusted its recitation to one of our finest pupils, Mademoiselle Hélène de Vos.”

A murmur of appreciation went through the crowd. The Director smiled, a benevolent proprietor displaying a prize ewe. My own blood ran cold. A chill, far deeper than any November wind, seized my vitals. It was not surprise, not exactly. I had long known the man’s character—his vanity, his unscrupulous hunger for the credit of others’ labours. But to hear it done so boldly, so publicly, was a violation so profound it felt like a physical blow. The words he had claimed were my own; the thoughts, born of my own soul’s travail, were now presented as the casual product of his ‘leisure hours’.

For a moment, the room swam before my eyes. The gaslights blurred into fiery stars. I felt a tremor in my hands and clenched them into fists, the nails biting into my palms. Hélène began to speak.

“The wind that scours the naked fell…”

Her voice, at first thin, grew in strength. She had learned the lesson well. She spoke of the granite tors, of the purple heather bleeding into a bruised twilight sky, of the lonely cry of the curlew. She spoke of a soul finding its truest church not in cathedral walls, but under the vast, unjudging vault of heaven. It was my heart speaking through her lips, and as she recited, my anguish slowly hardened into a cold, clear rage. To be silent now was to be an accomplice in my own erasure. It was to permit injustice to flourish in this gilded hall, to let a lie stand as truth before these powerful people. It was to deny the very essence of the work—the fierce, unvarnished truth I had tried to capture.

The poem reached its final, defiant stanza. Hélène’s voice rang out with a passion that made the bishop lean forward in his seat.

“Let others prize the gilded cage, / The soft applause, the sheltered stage; / Give me the moor’s uncompromised soul, / That, buffeted, remains a whole, / And in its wildness, answers God!”

As the last word faded into the expectant hush, the hall erupted in polite, yet enthusiastic, applause. The Director beamed, accepting the ovation as his due. He stepped forward again to thank the audience. This was the moment. The fork in the path. One way led to the continued security of my post, the quiet respect of my pupils, the avoidance of ugly, public scenes. It was the path of self-preservation, the path of a woman who knew her precarious place in the world. The other path led to confrontation, to the very real possibility of dismissal, of shame, of being cast out once more into the cold.

But what was a place, if it was bought with the surrender of one’s own soul? What was security, if it meant allowing a thief to wear one’s own face? I thought of the moors in my poem, their unyielding integrity. I could not be less than the landscape I had praised.

My decision was made. It was not a choice, but a necessity. I moved forward, my plain grey dress a stark slash against the rich colours of the assembly. The other teachers parted for me, their faces a mixture of curiosity and alarm. The Director saw me approaching and his smile tightened at the edges.

“Monsieur Dubois,” I said. My voice was not loud, but it was clear, and it carried a weight that made the murmuring cease at once. Every eye in the hall turned upon me.

He looked down at me, his expression one of pained surprise, as if a servant had spoken out of turn. “Miss Vance? Is there some trouble?”
