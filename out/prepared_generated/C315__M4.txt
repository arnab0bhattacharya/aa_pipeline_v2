The lodgings in Clarence Crescent were, Mrs. Ellwood had declared upon their first inspection, everything that was genteel and airy; and so they were, if one did not mind the smell of boiled cabbage that drifted up from the kitchen below, or the way the wind moaned through the ill-fitting sashes of the drawing-room windows. To Miss Lucy Ellwood, however, the house was a place of strange, suspended animation. The days passed in a rhythm of promenades and pump-room gossip, of taking the waters that her mother did not need and resting in rooms that were not their own. They had come to Cheltenham for Mrs. Ellwood’s health, a vague and convenient malady that manifested itself in a general languor and a need for the restorative air of the spa. Lucy, at three-and-twenty, with no prospects and a dwindling dowry, had been glad enough of the change from the damp vicarage in Devonshire, but she could not quite shake the feeling that they were actors in a play whose plot she had not been given.

Her mother, far from being an invalid, seemed to have shed ten years since their arrival. Her step was lighter, her cheeks had regained a colour that Lucy had not seen since her father’s death, and she had developed a sudden, voracious appetite for the daily newspaper, scanning its columns with a focus that had little to do with the price of bread. It was on a Tuesday, during their third week, that the mystery began to resolve itself. They were taking a dish of tea at a confectioner’s on the Promenade when a gentleman, tall and still handsome in a grave, autumnal way, paused by their table. He was dressed in excellent black, and his voice was a low, pleasant rumble.

“Mrs. Ellwood, I believe,” he said, with a bow that was both formal and deeply familiar. “It has been a great many years.”

Lucy watched, fascinated, as a flush of pure, girlish pink suffused her mother’s cheeks. “Mr. Ashworth,” she breathed, her hand fluttering to her throat. “Indeed, it has. You are… you are looking well.”

“As are you,” he replied, his eyes holding hers for a moment too long. He then turned to Lucy, his gaze softening. “And this must be your daughter. You have your mother’s look about you, Miss Ellwood.”

Lucy murmured a suitable reply, but her mind was racing. Mr. Ashworth. The name was a ghost from her mother’s past, a figure from the stories of her girlhood in Bath, a suitor who had been deemed unsuitable by her grandfather for reasons that had never been fully explained. He had been a younger son, she recalled, with a commission in the army, and her mother had been forbidden to see him again. The memory of her mother’s tears over that long-ago romance had been a staple of Lucy’s childhood imagination. Now, here he was, flesh and blood, and a widower, if the sombre black and the lack of a Mrs. Ashworth were any indication.

The encounter was brief, but it changed everything. The following afternoon, as they sat in the drawing-room, a note arrived, borne by Mr. Ashworth’s manservant. Lucy watched her mother read it, her expression a shifting landscape of anxiety and delight.

“Mr. Ashworth,” her mother said, her voice carefully neutral, “has invited us to take a turn about the Montpellier Gardens with him tomorrow. He says the walks are particularly fine at this hour.”

“How kind,” Lucy replied, her eyes fixed on her mother’s face. “We have not yet seen the gardens.”

From that day, Mr. Ashworth became a fixture of their lives. He was unfailingly polite, his conversation a gentle current of shared reminiscence with her mother and courteous enquiry towards Lucy. He escorted them to the circulating library, secured them the best seats at a concert of Italian music, and walked with them each morning along the Promenade. The town, which had been a collection of strangers, now had a focal point. Lucy saw the glances, the whispered consultations between other ladies of their acquaintance, and she felt a strange, conflicted pride. Her mother was being courted, and it was a spectacle both wonderful and deeply unsettling to witness.

It was a fortnight later, on a bench overlooking the lily pond in the gardens, that Lucy finally understood the true nature of their visit. Mr. Ashworth had gone to procure them some lemonade, and her mother, watching his retreating back, sighed.

“He is very kind, is he not, Lucy?” she said, her voice soft.

“He is, Mama,” Lucy agreed. “And he seems very… attached to your company.”

Her mother was silent for a moment, her fingers tracing the seam of her glove. “We were very foolish, he and I,” she said, so quietly that Lucy had to lean in. “We thought the world was ours to command. My father, as you know, had other ideas. Mr. Ashworth was sent to the Peninsula, and I was married to your father within the year. I did not see him again until the day we met at the confectioner’s.”

The confession hung in the air between them, fragile and momentous. Lucy’s first instinct was a pang of something like shock. Her mother, the paragon of quiet duty and widowhood, had been carrying this torch for nearly thirty years. The whole journey to Cheltenham, the vague talk of health, it had all been a pretext. She felt a flash of indignation on her father’s behalf, a man who had been kind, if dull, and who had deserved a wife whose heart was not elsewhere. But then she looked at her mother’s face, at the light in her eyes that had been absent for so long, and the indignation melted away, replaced by a wave of tenderness so fierce it almost hurt.

“You came here for him,” Lucy said, and it was not an accusation, but a statement of fact.
