The wind off the moors had a voice that day, a low, ragged moan that seemed to tear at the very stones of Ashby Hall. It was the month of November, that bleak, grey sentinel between the warmth of summer and the dead weight of winter, and the air bit with a sharpness that made the blood dance in the veins. I sat by the fire in my drawing-room, a small, confined space within the vast, echoing emptiness of the manor, and watched the shadows lengthen on the walls. The room was cold, despite the coals; for I had a habit of drawing back from the heat, as if the very comfort of it were a sin I could not afford.

A knock came at the door—three sharp, irregular raps. Not the soft, hesitant tap of a servant, nor the confident, heavy thud of a suitor seeking an audience with the heiress of Ashby. It was the knock of a man who knew no quarter, who had never learned the art of curtsying to fortune.

"Come in," I said, my voice sounding strange to my own ears, thin and brittle.

The door opened, and Mr. Arthur Penrose entered. He was a slender man, with a face that had been polished by years of wind and poverty into a rough, honest texture. His coat was threadbare at the elbows, and his boots were scuffed with the red clay of the village lane. He did not look at the Persian carpet, nor at the silver on the mantelpiece, nor at me with the hungry, assessing gaze that men usually wear when they come to weigh my dowry against their own ambitions. He looked at the fire, and then at me, with a directness that was almost violent.

"Miss Ashby," he said. His voice was quiet, but it carried the weight of a stone dropped into a well.

"Mr. Penrose," I replied. "You are far from your schoolroom. The wind is bitter."

"It is bitter," he agreed, standing in the center of the room as if the floor were made of glass. "I came to see you because I heard that you had refused another suit. Colonel Vane, I believe?"

I did not answer immediately. I looked at the flames, which leaped and hissed, casting dancing shadows on the ceiling. "He is a good man, Mr. Penrose. Gentle, respected, with a estate of his own."

"And?"

"And I cannot love him. I cannot breathe in a house where I am expected to be grateful for the roof over my head, or to smile because it is the season. He speaks to me as one speaks to a prize horse—fondly, perhaps, but always with an eye on the price tag."

Penrose turned to face me fully. His eyes were dark, deep-set, and held a fire that did not match the coldness of his appearance. It was the same fire I had seen in him ten years ago, when he was my brother’s tutor, before the debts that had swallowed his father’s legacy had swallowed him whole.

"You mistake me, Miss Ashby," he said, his tone sharp, cutting through the air. "I do not come to judge your refusal. I come to tell you that your pride is a wall, and I am tired of throwing stones at it."

I looked up, startled. "Pride? You call it pride? I call it self-respect. I have no choice but to be proud, Mr. Penrose. If I am not, I am nothing but a bag of coin with a face."

"Is that all you see?" he asked, stepping closer. The movement was sudden, a breaking of the polite distance that had separated us for a decade. "Do you see no mind? No soul? Or is your soul so wrapped in your inheritance that you have forgotten it exists?"

The question struck me with the force of a physical blow. I felt a surge of anger, hot and blinding, but beneath it, a deeper, more terrifying pain. I had spent years building this fortress of indifference, this citadel of 'no,' because it was the only weapon I had against the world’s voracity. But here was this man, this man of no consequence in the eyes of society, stripping the armor away with words.

"I see a man who is poor," I said, my voice trembling. "I see a man who could not afford the ink for his last letter to my brother. I see a man who stands in my drawing-room in the rain, shivering, while I sit by the fire. Is that what you see in me? A source of warmth?"

"I see a woman who is lonely," he said, and the word hung in the air, heavy and undeniable. "And I see a woman who is brave. You have said 'no' to every man who has come to ask for your hand, not because you are proud, but because you are afraid. Afraid that if you said 'yes,' you would lose the only thing that is truly yours: your judgment. But you are losing something else, Miss Ashby. You are losing your life. You are sitting here, in this beautiful, cold room, and you are dying of safety."

I wanted to deny it. I wanted to call him insolent, to have him thrown out, to retreat into the safe, familiar mask of the icy heiress. But the fire in his eyes held me. It was a challenge, a dare, and it was the first time in years that anyone had dared to look at me as I was, not as I was worth.

"You are overstepping your bounds, Mr. Penrose," I whispered, but there was no heat in my voice. "You were my brother’s tutor. You are a stranger to me now."
