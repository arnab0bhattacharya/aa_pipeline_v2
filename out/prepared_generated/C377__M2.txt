The light in the studio on Marine Parade was always too bright, a harsh, bleached white that seemed to strip the soul from the face before it could even settle into the pose. Arthur Pendelton, who had spent twenty years capturing the vanity of the Brighton bourgeoisie, had grown accustomed to the silence of his craft. It was a silence he had curated, a void where only the sitter’s breath and the click of the shutter existed. But since he had acquired the new lens—a heavy, brass-rimmed piece of glass sent from a reclusive optician in Prague who had died the very week the package arrived—that silence had begun to hum.

It started with Mrs. Halloway, a stout woman who wanted a portrait for her new dining room. She had posed with her hand resting on a velvet chaise, her chin tilted in that practiced aristocratic defiance. Arthur had framed her through the viewfinder, adjusting the focus until her eyes were sharp, her skin flawless. But as he watched through the glass, a distortion rippled at the edge of his vision. He blinked, assuming it was a smudge on the element. He looked again.

Behind Mrs. Halloway, just over her left shoulder, stood a shape. It was not a person, not exactly. It was a smear of shadow, a negative space that refused to hold its form. It had no face, no distinct limbs, but it possessed a terrible, static weight. Arthur had snapped the shutter without thinking. When the wet plate was developed later that evening in the red glow of the darkroom, the shape was there, faint but undeniable. It looked like a ghostly silhouette, a smudge of ink that had seeped through the paper from the other side.

Mrs. Halloway had been thrilled. “How mystical!” she had gasped, tracing the blur with a manicured finger. “It looks like a spirit from my youth! I will hang it above the mantelpiece.” She had paid him double his usual rate for the novelty, convinced it was a trick of the light, a special effect Arthur had perfected. He had smiled, his stomach churning with a cold dread that he could not name. He had told himself it was a flaw in the lens, a refraction error, a trick of the eye. He had not told himself the truth: he had felt the cold breath of that shape against his neck as he focused.

The next week brought Mr. Thorne, a sea captain with a beard like iron filings. He sat rigidly, trying to project authority. Through the lens, Arthur saw the shape again. This time, it was closer. It hovered directly behind Mr. Thorne’s head, its form less diffuse, more defined. It seemed to be leaning in, listening. The shadows in the studio, which should have been cast by the large windows and the draped curtains, seemed to bend toward the shape, as if it were a black hole pulling the darkness into itself. Arthur clicked the shutter. The flash powder erupted, blindingly white, and for a second, the studio was illuminated by a light that came from nowhere.

When the print came out, the shape was darker. It looked less like a smudge and more like a figure hunched in waiting. Mr. Thorne was delighted. “A guardian,” he declared, slapping Arthur on the back. “A sign of good fortune. I’ll have three more made. One for my wife, one for my mother, and one for my office.”

Arthur tried to return the lens. He went to the optician’s shop, a dusty, cluttered place that smelled of turpentine and old paper. But the shop was closed. The windows were boarded up. The landlord, a nervous man who lived above the shop, told Arthur that the optician, a Mr. Vane, had vanished three months ago. No one had seen him since. There was no forwarding address. The lease was void.

Arthur returned to his studio, his hands shaking. He locked the door and barred the windows. He did not take any sitters for two days. He sat in the dark, staring at the lens sitting on its velvet stand. It looked innocent, just glass and brass. But he knew. He could feel the pressure of it, a low-frequency vibration that resonated in his teeth.

On the third day, a young woman arrived. Her name was Elara. She was pale, with eyes the color of sea glass, and she wore a dress that seemed to absorb the light rather than reflect it. She wanted a simple portrait, she said. No frills. Just her.

Arthur hesitated. He wanted to turn her away, to lie and say he was ill. But there was something about her stillness that demanded he look. He guided her to the chair. She sat, and she did not fidget. She did not smile. She simply existed.

Arthur lifted the camera. He looked through the viewfinder.

The shape was not behind her.

It was in front of her.

It was standing between the lens and Elara, so close that its blurred edges obscured her face. It was no longer a smear. It had structure now. It had a head, a torso, long, spindly limbs that ended in points that seemed to pierce the air. It was looking at Arthur. Not at the camera, not at the lens, but at him, through the glass.

Arthur’s heart hammered against his ribs like a trapped bird. He raised his finger to the shutter release, his breath caught in his throat. He knew that if he took the picture, the shape would be captured. But he also knew, with a certainty that felt like a physical blow, that the shape was not a reflection. It was an invasion. The lens was not a tool; it was a door. And the thing on the other side was not just watching. It was waiting for him to let it in.
