It was a fact often remarked in the town of Middleton—a place of reputable trade and limestone dust, seated where the canal took a meditative bend and the quarries opened their pale wounds in the hillside—that Mr. Cornelius Seabright, though a man of independent means and unblemished character, had never been able to acquire that species of consideration which attaches to a gentleman who spends his money in the usual way. He kept no horse, he gave no dinners, he subscribed to no hunt; but he was to be seen on any fine afternoon, in a coat of antiquated cut and a hat which had outlived three fashions, climbing the stony paths above the town with a hammer at his belt and a canvas bag at his side, and stopping ever and anon to smite the reluctant rock, as though he would force from it the confession of its ancient secrets. The quarrymen, who are not a speculative race, regarded him with that tolerant contempt which the practical man reserves for the enthusiast; and the town, which takes its cue from its tradesmen, had long ago settled it that poor Mr. Seabright was a harmless gentleman, very well off, but sadly deficient in the ordinary sense, and given over to stones.

His household consisted of his daughter Lucy, now in her two-and-twentieth year, and one servant of long standing, who ruled the kitchen with the despotism of a faithful retainer, and was wont to observe, when the master brought home a fresh specimen of unusual size, that if the parlour was to be turned into a stone-yard, she must beg to be excused from dusting it. Lucy herself had grown up in the midst of these fossil remains with a patience which had in it more of love than of taste; for she was one of those quiet natures that find their happiness in persons rather than in pursuits, and to whom the father’s passion, however unintelligible, was sacred because it was his. She had learned the names of the ammonites and the encrinites as other girls learn the names of flowers, and could distinguish the lias from the oolite with a fidelity which her father declared to be a natural genius; though in truth it was nothing but the affectionate attention of a daughter who had early discovered that to listen was the easiest form of devotion.

It was in the summer of the year 1836 that the town of Middleton was thrown into a commotion by a report which travelled from the quarry to the tavern, and from the tavern to the drawing-rooms of the place, with a rapidity that would have done credit to the propagation of a falsehood. Mr. Hoskins, the proprietor of the great quarry on the eastern side of the hill—a man of substance and of that practical sagacity which is apt to be sceptical of everything but profit—had found, in blasting a new face of rock, a slab of limestone containing the remains of a creature such as had never before been seen in that country. It was described, according to the taste of the narrator, as a fish, a bird, a lizard, or a dragon; but all accounts agreed that it was marvellous, complete, and of a size to make the fortune of any museum. Mr. Hoskins, who had no more idea of what to do with it than with a bale of cotton, had caused it to be removed to his counting-house, where it lay upon the floor, and was shown to the curious at the rate of sixpence a head, to the great scandal of the minister, who preached against it as a snare of mammon.

When the report reached Mr. Seabright, he was in his study, engaged in the delicate operation of freeing a fragment of a crinoid from its matrix with a knife and a brush. He laid down his tools, put on his hat, and walked to the quarry with a step that had in it a something of the eagerness of youth. He returned in the evening, silent and abstracted, ate no supper, and sat by the window till the candles were burned low, looking out upon the darkening hillside as one who beholds a vision. Lucy, who knew the signs, asked no questions, but sat with her work in her hands, and waited.

On the following day he went again, and on the third day he came home with a countenance of settled purpose, and said to his daughter,—

“Lucy, I have seen the specimen. It is the most perfect thing of its kind that has ever been discovered in this island. It belongs to a genus hitherto unknown; I shall name it after Hoskins, though he does not deserve it, for he would sell it to-morrow to a tea-dealer for a curiosity. He asks three hundred pounds for it. I have two hundred and fifty in the funds, which I had laid by for your marriage portion. I shall offer him the whole.”

Lucy looked up from her work, and the colour came into her face, not with anger, but with a gentle surprise.

“Papa,” she said, “do you think it right?”

“Right?” said Mr. Seabright, and he stopped in his walk, for he had been pacing the room. “My dear, it is a question of science. A specimen like this may be the means of correcting the whole classification of the saurians. It is not a mere stone; it is a document, a page out of the book of nature, which has been sealed up for a hundred thousand years. If I do not secure it, it will go to some private cabinet, or be broken up for the sake of the novelty, and the world will be the loser. Your marriage portion—heaven knows I would not wrong you—but you are young, and you may marry a man who does not want a portion. And if you do not marry at all, you will have a father who has done his duty by science, and who will love you none the less.”
