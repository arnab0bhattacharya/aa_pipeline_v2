The rain had stopped by the time the mourners began to arrive, leaving the churchyard of St. Botolph’s-in-the-Fields glistening under a sky the colour of wet slate. Mr. Silas Crumb, undertaker of modest means and considerable imagination, had positioned his man at the very edge of the grave, a little behind the principal family, where he might be seen to best advantage. The man’s name was Abel. He was mute, a circumstance Mr. Crumb had acquired him for at a considerable discount, and his face, in its natural state, was a masterpiece of desolation. With the addition of a black cravat and a coat of Mr. Crumb’s own tailoring, he presented a figure of such profound and wordless grief that several ladies had already remarked upon it.

Abel, for his part, was cold. The damp had seeped through the soles of his boots, and his stomach, empty since a meagre breakfast of bread and small beer, complained softly. He kept his eyes fixed on the rich, dark earth that was piled beside the grave, and thought of nothing at all. This was his talent. He had learned, in the years since his affliction had left him without a voice, to make his mind a blank, a slate upon which others might write their own sorrows. It was a kind of service, he supposed, and it paid for his lodgings.

The funeral was a grand affair. The deceased, a Mr. Ebenezer Fitch, had been a man of property, and the crowd that gathered was large and well-fed. The chief mourner, a thin, severe-looking woman in a heavy black veil, was Mr. Fitch’s sister, Mrs. Prudence Gable. She stood with her back ramrod straight, her gloved hands clasped before her, and did not weep. Beside her, a portly gentleman with a face like a boiled ham, Mr. Fitch’s solicitor, dabbed at his eyes with a handkerchief that had been scented with lavender water.

Abel stood where he was put, his gaze lowered, his hands hanging limply at his sides. He was aware of the whispers, the sidelong glances. He was a professional, and he knew his part. When the vicar’s voice droned to a halt and the first clods of earth began to fall, he allowed a single, slow tear to trace a path down his cheek. It was a trick he had perfected. He thought of nothing, felt nothing, but the tear came, and it was, he knew, a very fine touch.

It was this tear that sealed his fate. As the mourners began to drift away, a hand, heavy and proprietorial, clamped onto his shoulder. It was the boiled-ham gentleman, the solicitor. His small, moist eyes were fixed on Abel’s face with an expression of profound and unexpected sympathy.

‘My dear fellow,’ the solicitor murmured, his voice thick with feeling. ‘My dear, dear fellow. I had no idea. No idea at all. To think of you, standing here all this while, so quiet, so brave. Mrs. Gable did not mention… but then, she would not, would she? Such a proud woman. Come, come. You must come back to the house. You must be with the family. It is what Mr. Fitch would have wanted.’

Abel, who had been addressed by no one but Mr. Crumb for the better part of a year, felt a jolt of pure terror. He shook his head, a small, frantic motion, and tried to step back. But the solicitor’s grip was firm.

‘No, no, I insist,’ the man continued, mistaking Abel’s panic for a fit of modest grief. ‘You must not be alone. Not today. The reading of the will is at three. You have a right to be there. A moral right, if not a legal one. Come along now. My carriage is just there.’

Abel’s eyes darted wildly, searching for Mr. Crumb. But his employer had already disappeared, doubtless in pursuit of the next grieving client. He was alone, caught in the inescapable trap of his own silent, sorrowful face. He opened his mouth, but no sound came out, only a faint, dry rasp. The solicitor patted his back, his expression one of tender understanding.

‘There, there. No need for words. I understand perfectly.’

The carriage was warm and smelled of leather and the solicitor’s lavender-scented handkerchief. Abel sat rigidly in the corner, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. He was being borne away to a house he had never seen, to be presented to a family he did not know, as a relative he was not. The sheer impossibility of his situation pressed down on him, heavy as a tombstone.

The house in Kensington was a great, grim pile of brick, its windows like so many dark, unblinking eyes. Inside, the air was thick with the smell of lilies and old money. The solicitor, whose name, Abel learned from the murmured greetings of others, was Mr. Ponder, guided him into a vast, dim drawing room where a fire roared in a marble hearth. Mrs. Gable stood by the window, her veil now thrown back to reveal a face of hawk-like severity.

‘Mr. Ponder,’ she said, her voice like the crack of a dry twig. ‘Who is this?’

‘My dear Mrs. Gable,’ Mr. Ponder said, his voice oozing with unctuous sympathy. ‘This is… a gentleman who knew your brother. A dear friend, I believe. He stood at the very graveside, utterly overcome. I felt it my duty to bring him back. He is quite speechless with grief.’

Mrs. Gable’s sharp eyes swept over Abel. He stood, frozen, his undertaker’s coat suddenly feeling like a suit of armour. He tried to look as grief-stricken as possible, which was not difficult, as he was terrified. He gave a small, jerky nod.

‘I see,’ Mrs. Gable said, her tone suggesting she did not see at all, but was too well-bred to make a scene. ‘Well. He had better stay for the will, then. I will not have it said that Prudence Gable turned a mourner from her door.’
