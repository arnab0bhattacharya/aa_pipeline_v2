The station at Krasnopol was a place of perpetual, grayish twilight, even in the height of summer. It was not that the sun had failed to rise, but that the air itself seemed to have thickened with a kind of moral humidity, a heavy, stagnant mist that clung to the iron girders and the soot-blackened faces of the travelers. I stood by the dispatch desk, watching the clock that ticked with a slow, deliberate malice, and I felt that the very mechanism of the world had begun to grind against the gears of my conscience. I had always prided myself on the purity of my neutrality. In a country where the wind blew from the east or the west, and the trains carried men who dreamed of empires or anarchy, I had made it my business to be neither. I was a man of schedules, of timetables, of the immutable physics of steam and steel. I did not ask who sat in the first-class carriage or who huddled in the cattle trucks; I only ensured that the wheels turned and the line remained open. It was a safe philosophy, a shield against the chaos that swirled just beyond the platform edges. But safety, I was about to learn, is a fragile thing, easily shattered by the voice of a brother.

The telegram had come in a burst of static, the wire buzzing with an urgency that felt almost obscene against the quiet droning of the waiting room. It was from Vasiliev, my brother, who was away in the capital, a man who had long ago abandoned the rigid propriety of our provincial life for the turbulent waters of the political currents. I had tried to warn him, in our letters, that the water was deep and the currents treacherous, but he had dismissed my caution as the mere squeamishness of a man who had never had to swim. Now, standing in the dim office with the dispatch key in my hand, I looked at the message again. It was not a request. It was an order, wrapped in the familiar, affectionate tone that made it all the more devastating. *Hold the 4:15 for twenty minutes. Do not question. Think of our mother. Think of the honor of our name.*

I knew what the 4:15 carried. I did not need to be told. The rumor had spread through the station like a smell of burning oil, invisible but suffocating. There was a prisoner in the secure compartment, a man named Koval, whose name was whispered with a mixture of fear and reverence by the workers in the yard. They said he was a man of ideas, a man who had looked at the world and seen it not as it was, but as it might be, and for that sin, he was to be dragged to a fortress in the north. I had seen the guards, men with eyes that did not blink, moving with the silent, predatory grace of cats through the corridors. I had told myself that it was none of my concern. The law was the law. The train was a vehicle, not a vessel of judgment. But the telegram sat on the desk, a small white rectangle that seemed to pulse with a light of its own, and I realized that my neutrality had been an illusion, a comfortable lie I had told myself to avoid the pain of choosing.

I walked out onto the platform. The heat was oppressive, a physical weight that pressed against my chest. The train was already arriving, the whistle blowing a low, mournful note that seemed to vibrate in the bones. The locomotive, a beast of black iron and steam, groaned as it settled onto the tracks, the pistons hissing in the humid air. I could see the faces in the windows, pale and exhausted, eyes that had seen too much of the road and too little of the sky. And there, in the middle of the carriage, behind the glass, I saw him. Koval. He sat with his hands folded on his lap, his face turned toward the window, watching the scenery pass with an expression of such profound, detached sadness that it made my throat tighten. He did not look like a criminal. He looked like a man who had lost something essential, something that could not be replaced, and was now waiting for the next leg of his journey into the void.

I had twenty minutes. The thought of it was absurd, a trivial span of time to measure the weight of a soul. I went to the signal box, where the old switchman, a man named Ilyin, was cleaning his spectacles with a rag that was dirtier than the lens. I told him to hold the line. He looked at me, his eyes narrowing, and I saw in his face the same shock that had struck me. He knew. He had always known. We were all complicit in this machinery, each of us turning a wheel, pulling a lever, believing that our small actions did not add up to a larger, terrible whole.

"Twenty minutes," I said, my voice sounding strange to my own ears, hollow and thin.

Ilyin nodded slowly, a movement that seemed to cost him a great deal of effort. He did not ask why. He did not need to. The silence between us was heavy, filled with the unspoken understanding that we were both about to commit an act of treason, not against the state, but against the order of things we had sworn to uphold. I returned to the dispatch office and sat down, waiting. The clock ticked on, each second a small hammer blow on my heart. I thought of Vasiliev, my brother, who had sent the telegram. I thought of our mother, who had died when we were young, her face still fresh in my memory, a face of gentle, uncomprehending kindness. She would have said nothing. She would have simply looked at us, her eyes full of a sorrow that had no name, and we would have felt the weight of her gaze, a judgment that was kinder than any law.
