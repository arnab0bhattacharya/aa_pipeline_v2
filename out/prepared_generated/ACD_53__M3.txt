It was upon a raw, foggy morning in the latter part of November, 1894, that Mr. Silas Gilderthorpe, the well-known railway financier and philanthropist, presented himself at my friend’s chambers in Montague Street. I had been engaged in my own professional duties when Gilderthorpe arrived, but I was subsequently called into the room to witness the singular narrative, and to lend my medical opinion to the matter. Gilderthorpe was a portly, florid man, accustomed no doubt to the management of boards and directors, yet upon this occasion his hands were unsteady, and the great gold chain which traversed his waistcoat rose and fell with the rapid breathing of a man under severe mental strain.

He plunged at once into his story with the directness of one who has little time for preamble. “I am a plain man of business, Mr. Marlow,” said he, “and I have no taste for romance. But I am being haunted, sir—haunted by the man who saved my life.”

Marlow, who had been leaning back in his armchair with his finger-tips pressed together, opened his grey eyes with a flash of keen interest. “Pray be precise, Mr. Gilderthorpe,” said he. “Let us have the facts in their exact order.”

“It was upon the evening of the third instant,” the visitor began. “I was crossing the thoroughfare near the Holburn Viaduct, the fog lying very thick, when a four-wheeler came bearing down upon me. I was deafened by the clatter of a costermonger’s barrow, and I swear I did not see the horse until it was upon me. In that very instant, a man sprang from the kerb, seized me by the collar with a grip of iron, and hurled me sideways into the mud. I fell heavily, striking my shoulder, and when I looked up, the cab had passed, and my rescuer was standing over me. He was a short, thickset fellow, dressed in a shabby dark coat, with a soft felt hat pulled low over his brow. I caught a glimpse of a pale, sharp-featured face, and a peculiar livid scar, shaped like a half-moon, upon his left cheekbone. Before I could thank him, or even get upon my feet, he turned and was swallowed up by the fog. I made inquiries at once, but no one could tell me who he was.”

“A scar upon the left cheekbone is a singular and unmistakable mark,” observed Marlow. “You have, of course, caused inquiries to be made?”

“I have spared no expense, Mr. Marlow. I have advertised in the agony columns of every London newspaper. I engaged a private detective from a well-known agency, who proved entirely incompetent. But I am not here to-day merely because I am ungrateful. I am here because my gratitude has turned to a most dreadful apprehension. I am here because the man appears to be ubiquitous, and because I believe he is watching me with a sinister purpose.”

Marlow’s brows drew together. “You speak of ubiquity, Mr. Gilderthorpe. Do you mean to say that you have seen this man since the accident?”

“I have seen him, or his exact double, in four different places, all within the space of the last fortnight, and all at hours when it was physically impossible for him to have travelled between them. On the seventh, I saw him standing at the booking office of Paddington Station at ten minutes past nine in the morning. I was upon the very point of accosting him when the crowd shifted, and he was gone. On the ninth, I was at my club in St. James’s Square at half-past one in the afternoon. I looked out of the bow window, and there he was, standing upon the opposite pavement, staring up at the window. I rushed out, but the street was entirely empty. On the twelfth, my niece, Miss Verner, who had heard me describe the man, declared she saw him in the garden of my country house at Woking, staring up at the library window. And lastly, Mr. Marlow, at a quarter to eleven last night, my butler, a man of the most stolid and unimaginative nature, knocked upon my door to ask if I had rung. I had not. He informed me that a man answering the description had presented himself at the servants’ entrance, asking to see me upon a matter of business. The butler, knowing my wishes, had turned him away. I hurried to the door, but the man had vanished.”

“A most remarkable sequence of events,” said Marlow, his eyes brightening. “The evidence points to one of two conclusions. Either you have been pursued by a gang of men who bear a singular and identical likeness, or your imagination has been overwrought by the shock of your accident.”

“It is no imagination, Mr. Marlow,” said Gilderthorpe, with a sudden flash of anger. “I am a man of sound mind, and I know what I have seen. I have the feeling of a rat in a trap. I am a wealthy man, and I have made enemies in the course of my business. I cannot sleep, I cannot eat. I implore you to take up this matter, and to discover who this man is, and what he wants of me.”

Marlow considered for a moment, his gaze fixed upon the fire. “I will take the case, Mr. Gilderthorpe,” he said at last. “But I must ask you a few questions. You say you have enemies. Can you name any man who would have cause to wish you ill?”

Gilderthorpe hesitated, and I observed that his face grew a shade paler. “There is one man,” he said, in a lower tone. “His name is Ephraim Crale. He was a contractor who was ruined in the collapse of the Northumbrian Iron Syndicate three years ago. He held me responsible, and indeed, there were circumstances in that transaction which I do not care to recall. Crale was a violent man, and he swore vengeance upon me. But Crale is dead, Mr. Marlow. He died in the workhouse infirmary at Lambeth nearly two years ago.”
