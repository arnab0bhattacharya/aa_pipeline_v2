It was a truth universally acknowledged, in the quiet, moss-covered village of Ashford, that a gentleman of limited fortune and limited wit required a woman of infinite patience to render his existence tolerable. Mr. Arthur Penhaligon had possessed such a woman in Miss Eliza Thorne for the better part of two years. During that time, the engagement had been conducted with a regularity that suggested the management of a small estate rather than the cultivation of a romantic attachment. Arthur was punctual; he was polite; he was, in the estimation of his future father-in-law, a man of unimpeachable moral rectitude. He was also, to Eliza’s increasing and secret dismay, profoundly dull.

The doubt had not arrived with the dramatic force of a thunderbolt, but rather with the creeping, insidious nature of damp in an old house. It began in the library, where Arthur had spent an entire evening reading aloud from a treatise on agricultural rotation. Eliza had listened, her needlework progressing steadily, her eyes fixed on the pattern of the carpet, while her mind wandered to the memory of a different voice, one that had spoken of starlight and sorrow rather than soil and seed. When she had finally interrupted to ask his opinion on the matter, Arthur had smiled with the self-satisfied serenity of a man who believes he has just delivered a profound truth. "It is the only sensible way to manage a farm, my dear," he had said. "Folly is expensive."

Eliza had agreed. She always agreed. It was the price of her comfort, or what she had once mistaken for it.

The crisis, when it came, was precipitated by the arrival of her sister, Miss Clara Thorne, who had been sequestered in London for the season and now returned to Ashford with the particular energy of one who had missed the provincial peace and yet intended to disrupt it. Clara was clever, sharp-tongued, and possessed of a vanity that Eliza, in her more charitable moments, could forgive. She had been in London, she declared, and had seen everything. She had even seen Mr. Penhaligon, who had come to the capital to await a decision regarding a living that had been promised to him by a benevolent bishop.

"He looks well," Clara said, settling into the chair opposite Eliza in the drawing room. The afternoon light was fading, casting long, grey shadows across the floor. "Though he did ask me if I had heard any news of the price of turnips. I confess, I did not. I was too busy wondering if he had ever heard anything of the price of conversation."

Eliza’s hand tightened around her teacup. "Arthur is a good man, Clara. He is steady. He will provide for me."

"Steady," Clara repeated, the word taking on a metallic edge. "Like a fence post. Like a clock that keeps perfect time but has no music in it. Eliza, do you love him?"

The question hung in the air, unanswerable and yet demanding an answer. Eliza looked out the window at the bare branches of the elm tree in the garden. "I have promised him," she said softly. "I am bound by my word. And by my duty to my father, who has placed his hopes in this match. It would be cruel to break it now."

"Is it cruelty to be false?" Clara asked, her voice low. "Or is it cruelty to remain in a cage because the door is locked, when you have the key in your pocket? You have known him for two years, Eliza. You know every habit, every silence, every minor irritation. Do you not see him clearly? Do you not see that you are marrying a shadow?"

Eliza felt a chill that had nothing to do with the November air. She thought of Arthur’s face, so familiar, so safe, and so utterly empty of surprise. She thought of the way he looked at her, with a gratitude that was almost pitiful, as if he considered her a prize to be guarded, not a partner to be engaged. She thought of the letters she had written to him, careful and measured, devoid of passion, filled with inquiries about the weather and the health of the horses. She had been performing a role, and she had been so tired of the part.

"I do not know," she admitted, and the admission felt like a fracture in the ice. "I do not know if I love him. I know that I respect him. I know that he is kind. But I find that my heart does not beat faster when he enters the room. I find that I wish, often, to be alone. Or to be with someone who can make me laugh without effort. Someone who sees the world not as a ledger to be balanced, but as a thing to be experienced."

Clara leaned forward. "And if you were to break it? What then? Your father would be wounded. The village would talk. Arthur would be hurt. But you? You would be free. Or perhaps you would simply be a woman who has wasted two years in a state of suspended animation."

Eliza closed her eyes. The weight of her decision pressed upon her chest. To break the engagement was to invite scandal, to disappoint her father, to wound a man who had done her no harm. It was, by all the standards of their society, an act of selfishness. But to continue was to commit a greater sin: the sin of living a lie. She thought of the years that lay ahead, the decades of polite conversation and shared silence, the slow erosion of her own spirit against the stone of his routine.

"I must speak to him," she said, opening her eyes. "I cannot wait for his next visit. I cannot let him believe that I am waiting for a future that I do not desire."
