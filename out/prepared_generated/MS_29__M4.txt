The candle guttered, and the shadow of my hand upon the wall seemed to start and quiver like a living thing, as though it would fain escape the confinement of this chamber. I set the light aright, and looked upon the face of my young visitor, who sat with her hands folded in her lap and her eyes, wide and earnest, fixed upon me with an attention that I had not known for many a long year. Ellen Whitgrave—for such is the name of the farmer’s daughter who has of late taken upon herself the kindly office of visiting the hermit of Winderdale—possesses a spirit of inquiry that does her credit, and a gentleness that disarms the natural moroseness of my temper. She had asked me, in her simple fashion, whether I had always dwelt in this house, and whether the portraits that moulder on the walls were those of my kin; and I, who have spoken to no living soul of these things since the last of my companions was laid in the earth, found the floodgates of memory burst within me, and the torrent of the past sweep over my soul with a violence that I had thought the years had quelled.

“You ask me, child,” I began, “whether I have always been solitary. Alas! I was not always thus; I have known the fullness of human affection, and the bitterness of its loss; I have been surrounded by friends, by kindred, by all that makes existence dear, and I have seen them one by one pass from my side into the silent land, until I remain alone, like a tree that has been stripped by the winter’s blast, and stands bare and desolate amid the ruins of the forest. This house, which now seems to you so dreary and so desolate, was once the abode of hospitality and joy. The halls that now echo only to the wind, then rang with the voices of the happy; the chambers that are now closed and shrouded in dust were then bright with the smiles of those I loved. But the hand of death has been busy with my household, and I am left to keep watch over the ashes of my former happiness.

“My name, Ellen, is Reginald Ashcombe, and I was born in this very chamber, in the year 1756. My father was a man of ancient lineage but of fallen fortunes; the estate that you see around you was then, indeed, more extensive than it is now, for the lands that your father tills were once a part of the domain of my ancestors. But the extravagance of my grandfather had reduced the family to narrow circumstances, and my father, who was a man of proud and unbending spirit, retired to this manor, and devoted himself to the education of his only son. My mother I never knew; she died when I was but three years old, and her portrait—that faded picture yonder, which the damp has well-nigh defaced—is the only memorial that remains to me of her who gave me birth. My father was both father and mother to me; he watched over my infancy with a tenderness that seemed almost womanly, and he instilled into my mind the principles of virtue and of honour with a care that has never been effaced.

“I was a solitary child, for my father’s pride forbade him to seek the society of the neighbouring gentry, who, he conceived, had looked coldly upon him in his adversity; and I grew up in the seclusion of these woods and hills, with no companions but my books and my own thoughts. The scenery around us, which now seems to me so melancholy, was then the source of the most exquisite delight. I loved to wander by the banks of the Winder, to climb the craggy heights of Dunmore, to watch the sun set behind the distant mountains, and to listen to the wild music of the winds as they swept through the ancient oaks. The poetry of nature sank deep into my soul, and I became a dreamer of dreams, a weaver of visions, a being of imagination and of feeling.

“When I had reached my twentieth year, my father deemed it time that I should see something of the world, and he sent me to London, to the house of an old friend, a merchant of great wealth and influence, whose name was Alderman Thorpe. There I was introduced into a society that was altogether new to me; I mingled with the gay and the learned, the witty and the wise; and I was received with a favour that, perhaps, turned my head, for I was not insensible to the charms of admiration. But amid all the splendour and the gaiety of the metropolis, my heart turned with fond regret to the peaceful shades of Winderdale, and I sighed for the quiet of my native hills.

“It was in London that I first saw my beloved Amelia. She was the daughter of a country clergyman, who had come to the metropolis to seek medical advice for a lingering disorder that had long preyed upon his constitution. She was not beautiful, according to the common estimation of beauty; but there was a sweetness in her countenance, a grace in her manner, a melody in her voice, that won upon the heart more surely than the most perfect symmetry of feature. Her mind was cultivated and refined; her temper was gentle and affectionate; her piety was deep and sincere. I loved her with a passion that absorbed every other feeling of my soul, and I had the inexpressible happiness of finding that my affection was returned.
