The silver teapot exhaled a thin, breathless plume, catching the afternoon light that fractured so beautifully, so impermanently, through the drawing-room windows at Elmleigh. Clara poured, her wrist trembling under the weight of the porcelain, feeling the sudden, sharp responsibility of hostessing, as if the fragile ties binding them all might dissolve should the milk be too cold. There was Eleanor, turning a slice of seed-cake over and over on her plate with a melancholy, absorbed attention; and Margaret, already leaning forward, her voice a bright, hard jet of sound cutting across the dust-motes; and beside her, touching the fringe of her shawl, sat Alice, whose pale eyes held the vague, watery depths of a pond in November. They were eighteen; they had left the schoolroom only yesterday, or so it seemed, yet time was already laying its dusty, invisible fingers upon them. "I shall absolutely refuse," Margaret was saying, "if he expects me to live in the north." The words fell into the space between them, hard as pebbles. Clara looked at the tea leaves, dark and damp, clinging to the white bone china, and thought how strange it was that words, mere breath, could build a wall between two people sitting on the same brocade sofa. Eleanor smiled, a slow, painful lifting of the lips that did not reach her eyes, and Alice sighed, plucking at a thread until it unravelled, long and thin as the afternoon itself. They were all, Clara felt, holding separate, secret universes within their breasts, universes that must inevitably, violently, collide with this drawing-room, this tea-table, this fragile afternoon; and the light shifted, a cloud passing over the sun, plunging the room into a sudden, greenish dusk that made the silver seem tarnished and old.

The church at Haversham smelled of bruised lilies and damp stone. Clara sat in the third pew, crushed against the velvet, watching the dust motes whirl in the coloured light that poured through the stained glass, turning Eleanor’s veil into a shimmering, golden mist. She is going away, Clara thought, going down into that dark, unfathomable sea of marriage; and she felt a sudden, piercing sorrow, not for Eleanor, but for the girl Eleanor had been, the girl who had turned the seed-cake on her plate at Elmleigh, now swallowed up entirely by this solemn, repetitive music. Beside her, Margaret was weeping, her tears falling fast and uncontrollable, though whether for Eleanor or for some private, unspoken grief of her own, who could say? Alice sat rigid, her hands folded in her lap, staring straight ahead at the altar where the white flowers blazed, a beacon of terrifying purity. When the ring was slipped on, a small, definite sound in the vast, echoing silence, Clara felt the tie between them snap, or rather, stretch thinner and thinner, a gossamer thread spanning a chasm. The organ swelled, filling the high, vaulted roof, and as they rose to sing, the vibrations shook the very air in Clara’s throat. She looked at Eleanor’s bent head, the white flowers trembling in her hair, and thought: we are losing her; she is becoming someone else; and the light through the stained glass fell in patches of blue and red upon the stone floor, like scattered jewels, brilliant and cold.

The cemetery at Highgate was a desolate expanse of black earth and dripping umbrellas. Rain fell, a fine, persistent mist that blurred the edges of the gravestones and made the black coats of the mourners gleam with a hard, metallic sheen. Margaret was dead. Clara stood between Alice and a woman she did not know, staring at the hole in the ground, the raw earth piling up beside it, wet and heavy and final. It was impossible, utterly impossible, that Margaret, who had poured out that bright, hard stream of words at Elmleigh, should be silenced; that the voice which had filled so many rooms, so many silences, should be stopped, packed away in that narrow box. Alice’s face was hidden by her veil, but Clara could see her hands, clenched so tightly that the knuckles shone white through the wet black gloves. The clergyman’s voice droned on, rising and falling, a meaningless tide of sound that broke against the deaf ears of the dead. Clara thought of the silver teapot, the seed-cake, the dust-motes in the drawing-room; she thought of the lilies at Haversham; she thought of the thread, stretched so thin, now snapped entirely. There was a gap, a vacancy, where Margaret had been, a hole in the fabric of their shared lives that the rain fell into and could not fill. The earth struck the coffin lid, a dull, heavy thud, and Clara felt the sound reverberate through her own body, a physical blow. She reached out and took Alice’s hand, cold and wet and stiff, and they stood together, two figures in a vast, indifferent landscape, bound not by love now, but by loss.

King’s Cross was a cavern of smoke and steam, the great engines hissing and panting like beasts in a menagerie. Clara, her bag in her hand, was walking toward the platform, her mind already leaping ahead to the journey, the compartment, the book she would read, when she saw her. A woman, grey-haired, stout, wrapped in a thick, dark coat, standing by the bookstall, turning over the pages of a magazine with an absorbed, intent expression. Clara stopped. The face was older, lined, the flesh fallen, but the eyes were the same, the pale, watery eyes of the girl at Elmleigh. Alice. For a moment, Clara hesitated. The past rushed up at her, a great wave of memory, bearing on its crest the silver teapot, the lilies, the black earth, the rain. Should she speak? Should she break through the barrier of years, of silence, of separation, and claim this woman, this stranger, as her own?
