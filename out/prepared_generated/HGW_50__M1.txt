The difficulty, as I have often observed, is not in the discovery itself but in the public’s obstinate refusal to accept the obvious. My name is Arthur Penhaligon, and until the Tuesday in question, I was a chemist of no particular reputation, employed in the analysis of alkaloids at a laboratory in Bloomsbury. The weed in question—*Convolvulus verax*, as I have provisionally named it—grows with such aggressive ubiquity in the hedgerows of Sussex that it is generally dismissed as a nuisance to the farmer and an eyesore to the aesthete. Yet, within its humble, funnel-shaped blossoms, I had isolated a crystalline derivative which, when introduced to the human system in a solution of ethanol, produced a physiological effect as singular as it was socially inconvenient. It did not induce madness, nor did it provoke the hallucinations associated with the Datura species; rather, it temporarily paralyzed the centers of the brain responsible for the construction of polite fictions. In short, the subject became physically incapable of uttering a falsehood.

I had tested this substance on myself, of course, and upon my landlady’s cat (which, to my surprise, merely purred and refused to speak at all), but the opportunity for a wider field test arrived in the form of an invitation from my Aunt Agatha. Agatha Pemberton was a woman of formidable social ambition and a moral elasticity that I found scientifically fascinating. She resided in a substantial villa in Kensington, a house crowded with French clocks and the sort of aggressive bric-a-brac that screams of new money. She was giving a dinner party to impress a certain Sir Reginald Vane, a man whose influence at the War Office was as great as his waistcoat was tight.

"You must come, Arthur," she had written. "You look so shabby, but it lends an air of Bohemianism to the table. Do wear a clean collar."

I arrived with a small phial in my waistcoat pocket, my heart beating with the anticipatory tremor of the true experimentalist. The dining room was a suffocating chamber of crimson velvet and gilt. The guests were assembled: Sir Reginald, looking pompous and red; a Mrs. Vance-Lee, a woman of indeterminate age and terrifying bonnets; and a young curate named Dobbs, who appeared to be sweating with the terror of his own vocation.

Agatha greeted me with the effusive, false warmth of a woman who views her relatives as decorative assets. "Arthur! You are late. But no matter. You know Sir Reginald?"

"I have not that pleasure," I said, "though I have read his memorandum on the supply of boots to the infantry, which I found to be riddled with statistical errors."

A silence fell over the room, heavy as a wet blanket. Agatha’s smile froze. "Arthur is so... droll," she tittered. "Always with the jokes. Come, dinner is served."

The soup was a thin, grey consommé, the sort of liquid that suggests a chef who has given up on life. I waited until the fish—a turbot of dubious freshness—was served. The conversation was a dull drone of the weather and the Queen’s health. It was time to act. I withdrew the phial. It contained a tincture of pale violet liquid. I had calculated the dosage to be effective within ten minutes of ingestion and to last approximately one hour. I poured a liberal measure into the claret decanter that stood at my elbow.

"More wine, Arthur?" Agatha asked, her eyes narrowing as she saw me handling the bottle.

"Allow me to pour, Aunt. It is a vintage of some complexity. I am sure Sir Reginald would appreciate a glass."

Sir Reginald, being a man of habit, extended his glass. Mrs. Vance-Lee and the curate followed suit. Agatha, seeing her guests drinking, felt compelled to join them, lest she seem inhospitable. We drank. The wine was actually quite good, and the tincture added a certain... piquancy.

For ten minutes, nothing happened. Agatha was regaling Sir Reginald with a story about her charitable work at the East End missions—a tale involving the reformation of a drunkard and the knitting of socks. It was a story I had heard before, and I knew for a fact that Agatha had never visited the East End in her life, preferring to send her checks by post.

"And the poor man," Agatha was saying, "he wept, Sir Reginald. He wept and called me his saviour."

Sir Reginald nodded benevolently. "A noble work, Mrs. Pemberton."

Suddenly, Agatha stopped. Her hand flew to her throat. Her eyes widened. She opened her mouth, and instead of the sentimental drivel we expected, she spoke in a clear, metallic voice.

"It is utter rot," she said. "I have never been to the East End. I hired a woman to write those reports for me. The man was a paid actor. I do this to secure a seat on the Board of the Foundling Hospital because the position offers excellent opportunities for meeting the aristocracy."

The silence that followed was of a different quality to the previous one. It was the silence of the vacuum. Sir Reginald’s fork, laden with fish, hovered in mid-air. The curate looked as though he had been struck by lightning.

"Agatha!" I said, feigning shock. "Surely not!"

"I cannot lie," Agatha said, looking at her hands in horror. "The words just come out. I hate this dinner party. I think Sir Reginald is a pompous bore and his waistcoat makes him look like a pear. I only invited him because my husband wants a government contract for his textile mills."

Sir Reginald turned a shade of purple that clashed violently with the crimson wallpaper. "Madam!"

"I know!" Agatha cried. "It’s the wine! But I can’t stop! Mrs. Vance-Lee, I have been spreading rumours that you dye your hair because you flirted with the Vicar at the garden fete."

Mrs. Vance-Lee stood up, her face white. "It is true!" she shrieked, evidently under the influence of the drug herself. "I do dye it! And I flirted with him because my husband is a cold fish who cares more for his stamp collection than for my affections!"
