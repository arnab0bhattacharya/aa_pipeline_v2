The heat lay upon the water like a second skin, a breathless, golden imperviousness that held the lake motionless beneath the cypresses. It was a heat that did not oppress so much as suspend, as if the very minute-hand of the clock had melted in the sun and ceased to tell the hour. And indeed, what hour was it? Time had slipped its moorings; it drifted, like the white petals of the oleander, idly upon the current. Mrs. Arbuthnot dipped her pen into the inkwell. The nib scratched, a tiny, dry sound in the immense, shimmering silence of the afternoon. *My dearest Margaret,* she wrote, *the weather continues most balmy. We have taken to the boat every morning before breakfast, and the water is as warm as milk. The children are in excellent health, though the heat makes them somewhat languid.* She paused, and through the open window, framed by the crumbling stucco of the loggia, came the scent of lemons, sharp and sweet, and the drowsy drone of bees. *Languid*—yes, that was the word. It covered everything. It covered Arthur, sprawled in the wicker chair with the *Times* a week old draped over his face; it covered the servants who moved like ghosts through the cool, tiled rooms; and it covered, she supposed, the strange, feverish brightness in Elena’s eyes.

Elena was standing by the stone balustrade, looking down at the water. A boat was approaching, a slim, dark line cutting through the glare, and at the tiller stood the Count. He was nothing but a silhouette against the blinding silver of the lake, yet Elena felt him draw near long before the engine’s thrumming reached her ears. It was a vibration in the air, a disturbance in the pattern of the light. She did not turn her head; she felt that to look at him directly would be to break some fragile spell woven of heat and distance. He was coming for her. The thought did not excite her so much as confirm something she had always known, as if the landscape itself had been arranged for this singular arrival. The cypresses stood like dark sentinels; the mountains, blue and hazy in the distance, were merely the backdrop to this moment. When he looked up and raised a hand, a flash of white against the dark hull, she felt a surge of life so intense it was almost pain. It was not love, she thought, not as the novels described it—no throbbing heart, no sighing. It was rather a recognition. She was the key, and he the lock; or perhaps it was the other way round. The boat bumped against the stone steps, and the Count leapt out, his movements quick, lithe, entirely of this place, while she stood frozen, a creature of the north, pale and heavy in her muslin dress.

“The Signorina,” he said, and his voice was like the lake water, deep and smooth. He took her hand, and his touch was cool, dry, and utterly possessive.

From the high path above the villa, where the scrubby earth burned the soles of his boots, Julian watched the scene below. He saw the boat, the man, his sister standing like a statue. It looked to him like a painting in a gallery, stiff and posed and utterly ridiculous. He spat onto the dusty ground. The heat up here was different; it was a living thing, fierce and wild, smelling of thyme and baking rock. Down there, in the civilized shade of the loggia, his mother was writing her letters, sealing up the summer in neat, white envelopes, while Elena was letting herself be swept away by a man with oiled hair and a boat. Julian wanted none of it. He scrambled higher, his breath coming in short gasps, the stones sliding away beneath him. He wanted to reach the ridge where the wind would hit him, a clean, sharp wind that didn't care about Counts or mothers or the suffocating weight of family. He was fifteen, and his limbs felt too long for his body, his thoughts a jumble of resentment and a fierce, inarticulate need for something—anything—that was not this. A lizard, green and jewel-bright, skittered across a rock and vanished into a crevice. That was freedom, Julian thought. To vanish. To be a flash of green in a world of grey stone.

Below, Mrs. Arbuthnot dipped her pen again. *The lake is teeming with fish,* she wrote. *Arthur has tried his hand at angling, but with little success. Elena has made a charming friend, a local gentleman of some standing, who has been most attentive. I am sure you would like him, Margaret. He has very good manners.* She did not mention the way Elena looked at the man, a look that seemed to burn through the polite chatter of the afternoon. She did not mention the silence that fell over the villa whenever he was gone, a silence more profound than the midday heat. She did not mention Julian, who appeared only at meals, sunburned and sullen, with dirt under his fingernails and a wildness in his eyes that frightened her. These things were not for the page. They were mists that would surely burn off with the morning sun, leaving the solid, reassuring facts of the summer intact. The light was changing now, the fierce gold softening to a gentle amber. The Count was leaving, his boat a receding speck on the water. Elena still stood at the balustrade, but she was no longer part of the landscape. She was a solitary figure, her hand raised to her throat, watching him go. The sun dipped behind the mountains, and the lake turned from silver to a deep, bruised purple. A bell began to toll from a village across the water, a slow, melancholy sound that seemed to count not the hours, but the passing of light itself. Mrs. Arbuthnot blotted her letter. *We are all most happy and well,* she wrote, and folded the page. The truth of it, whatever it was, was too bright, too fleeting, to be captured in ink. It existed only in the heat, in the silence, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, and then it was gone.
