The wind from the moor came not as a guest, but as an invader. It shrieked against the stone walls of Thornelea, finding every crevice, every fault in the mortar, as if seeking to tear the house from its foundations and cast it into the bog below. I sat in the schoolroom, my hands folded over a mended primer, watching my pupil. Aloysius Lavenham, nine years of age, stood at the high window. He did not look out upon the bruised heather or the lowering sky; he looked at the thick oak door, his small jaw set, his dark eyes holding the fixed, wary blankness of a creature awaiting the lash.

"Aloysius," I said, breaking a silence that had grown too heavy for endurance. "Your Latin grammar will not master itself, and I believe we have lingered over the declensions long enough."

He did not turn. "I prefer to stand, Miss March."

"You have stood for an hour. The hour is passed. Come to the table."

He came, but slowly, dragging his feet as though they were weighted with lead. When he sat, he flinched—a sudden, involuntary contraction of his thin shoulders—though no hand had touched him. I set the book before him. He stared at the page. His lips moved, but no sound came. I observed a tremor in his fingers, sallow and thin, gripping the edge of the desk. A sudden, fierce indignation took hold of me. It was not the first time I had seen him thus—cringing, mute, his spirit gagged by an invisible terror. I had been at Thornelea three months, engaged by Mr. Lavenham to instruct his only son, and in that time the boy had spoken to me scarcely a dozen sentences of his own volition.

"Aloysius," I said again, and now my voice was lower, stripped of its governess-edge. "Look at me."

He raised his eyes. They were his mother’s eyes, I had been told—large, grey, fringed with black. At present they held a look that checked my breath: a look of cornered, desperate cunning.

"You are afraid," I said. "I am not blind. Tell me what it is that frightens you, and I will do what I can to shield you."

He swallowed. "You cannot shield anyone."

"Perhaps not. But I can listen."

The wind howled. A log shifted in the grate, sending up a shower of sparks. The boy’s gaze flickered to the fire, then back to me. His mouth twisted. "She said that, too."

"Who said it?"

He was silent. He had gone too far, or too near, and the old wariness clamped down. He returned to his book, and I let him be. To press him then would have been to seal him forever.

It was two days later, in the hour before dusk, when the house was wrapped in a stillness that seemed almost deliberate, that I found my chance. Mr. Lavenham had ridden to Helmsley on business. The servants were below stairs. I came upon Aloysius in the long gallery that ran above the hall, a cold, oak-panelled corridor where the portraits of Lavenham ancestors stared down with flat, hard eyes. He was standing before a picture at the gallery’s end—a small portrait, half-shadowed, of a young woman in a green gown. He did not hear me approach.

"Aloysius."

He started violently, then, seeing it was I, did not run. He stood his ground, his face pale, his hands clenched at his sides.

"That is your mother," I said. It was not a question. The face was his face, refined and feminised: the same wide brow, the same set of the mouth.

"They say she died," he whispered.

"They tell me she was ill. A fever. They tell me she died when you were five."

He turned to me. The blankness was gone; in its place, a sudden, terrible clarity, the look of a child who has kept a secret so long it has become a poison in his blood. "She did not die."

I did not speak. I let the silence hold him, as one holds a vessel beneath a spring.

"I was awake," he said. His voice was barely audible, a thread of sound drawn tight. "I had been asleep, but I woke. There was a noise—not a loud noise, a moving noise. Someone moving things. I came out of my room. The hall was dark. I saw her at the door. She had a cloak. She had a bag. She was—she was going."

He stopped. His breath came short and hard, and I saw that the effort of speech, after years of suppression, was tearing something loose in him. I put my hand on his shoulder. He did not pull away.

"Did she see you?" I asked.

"She saw me. She put her hand on my head. She said—she said, 'Be brave, my own. I will send for you.' She said she would send. She kissed my head. Then the door opened, and the wind came in, and she was gone. I stood there. I do not know how long. Then he came—the master. He came down the stairs. He saw me at the door. He saw it was open. He did not go after her. He shut the door, and he locked it, and he took me by the arm, and he said—" The boy’s voice cracked. "He said, 'Your mother is dead. You will say nothing of this. If you speak, I will know. And you will be sorry.'"

The words fell into the cold air of the gallery. I stood motionless, my hand still on his shoulder, and I felt the heat of my own blood rise, a fierce, dark tide of anger. The lie. The brutal, calculated lie. Four years the child had carried this knowledge alone, hemmed in by threats, with no soul to tell. I thought of the household, the servants with their smooth, practised faces, repeating the tale of the fever. I thought of Mr. Lavenham, with his cold eye and his measured speech, and I understood, at last, the nature of the tyranny under which this boy had been made to live.
