The smell of the shop was the smell of my father’s ambition, sharp with rust and tallow, and it had seeped into the very fibers of my soul long before I understood that I did not want it. I was the son of Josiah Bellow, ironmonger of Faversham, and by the laws of inheritance and common sense, I was destined to count bolts and sell hinges to farmers who smelled of wet wool. My father was a good man, if a dull one. He believed in the solidity of things. A nail was a nail. A key was a key. There was no mystery in the mechanism of a lock, only the necessary friction of metal on metal.

But I had eyes that saw differently. Where my father saw a pile of galvanized pipes, I saw the rhythmic curve of a serpent; where he saw a rusted gate, I saw the skeletal architecture of a cathedral. In the attic, behind a loose board in the floor and beneath a heap of moth-eaten coats, I kept my heresy. There, by the grey light of the Kentish sky, I mixed pigments that my father would have called waste. I painted. It was a secret more dangerous than any theft of stock, for it threatened the very foundation of our existence.

My name was Arthur. I was twenty-two, and I had not yet found the courage to tell my father that I preferred the blue of the sky to the grey of his ledger. I worked in the shop, standing on a stool to reach the high shelves, while my hands itched with the memory of the brush. I would touch a piece of iron and feel not its weight, but its potential. It was a stifling life, a life of small, precise movements, devoid of the great sweeping gestures of the soul.

It was in the "Red Lion" that the accident occurred, or perhaps it was not an accident at all, but the inevitable collision of two worlds that had been rubbing against each other for too long. I had gone into the village to buy ink, a task that required me to cross the threshold of my father’s domain. I was sitting in the corner, nursing a bitter beer, when the dealer arrived.

He was a man of London, I knew that by the cut of his coat and the way he held his hat. He had the look of a man who had seen the world and found it wanting, yet still hungry for novelty. His name, I later learned, was Mr. Vane. He sat down across from me, not by choice, but because the room was full of farmers and their wives.

"You look like a man who is thinking too hard," he said, with a smile that did not quite reach his eyes.

"I am a clerk," I said. "We are paid to think about inventory."

He laughed, a dry, brittle sound. "Clarks rarely have eyes like that. They look through things, not at them. You look at the dust on the table as if it were a landscape."

I felt a chill run down my spine. It was a rare sensation, to be seen. "I am merely tired," I said.

"I have a sketchbook in my bag," Vane said. "I am an artist of sorts, though I deal more in the commerce of art than its creation. May I see your hands?"

I hesitated. The shop was just outside. My father was in the back room, arguing with a supplier about the price of nails. If I left now, I would be safe. If I stayed, I might be ruined. But the curiosity in his eyes was a hook in my flesh. I pulled out the small, leather-bound sketchbook I kept in my waistcoat pocket. It was filled with charcoal studies of the local church, the river, the strange, twisted shapes of the hedgerows.

Vane took it. He did not speak. He turned the pages slowly, his face impassive. The room seemed to shrink around me. The noise of the pub faded into a dull hum. I watched him. He was a tall man, thin as a reed, with a face that seemed to be carved from pale stone. When he finally looked up, his eyes were bright.

"This is not the work of a clerk," he said. "This is the work of a man who is starving."

"I am apprenticed to my father's trade," I said, the words tasting like ash.

"Then you are dying," Vane said simply. "I have a gallery in London. It is small, and the prospects are uncertain, but I am looking for new blood. I do not deal in the polished veneers of the Academy. I deal in the raw, the strange, the true. Come to London with me, Arthur. Leave the nails and the hinges. Leave the smell of rust."

The offer hung in the air, fragile and terrifying. London. The city of dreams, of crowds, of anonymity. It was a place where a man could disappear and reappear as something else. But it was also a place where a man could starve, truly starve, not just for food, but for purpose.

I thought of my father. I thought of the shop, the solid, unyielding reality of it. I thought of the attic, the secret, the shame. I looked at Vane, this strange, pale man who had seen me for the first time in my life.

"I must go back," I said. "My father is waiting."

"Then you will remain a clerk," Vane said, closing the sketchbook and handing it back to me. "And you will never know if you were a fool or a genius. That is the greatest tragedy of the common man: the doubt."
