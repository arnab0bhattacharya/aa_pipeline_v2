The great Congress of Nations was assembled in Vienna, in a palace of glass and steel that rose beside the ancient Danube, and I, Edward Alistair, the Envoy of England, stood among them as one already condemned. The year was late in the twenty-first century; a century that had believed itself to have banished the ancient spectres of war and famine, only to find them returned in a form more terrible and insidious than before. For two generations, the nations of the earth had dwelt in a concord so profound, so seemingly absolute, that we had spoken of it in the language of paradise. Yet now, a blight, silent and inexplicable, had fallen upon the harvests of all Europe, and the bonds of our long peace were stretched to the point of mortal agony.

My heart was a tempest of conflicting duties. From my own shores, the voice of my countrymen rose in a single, furious clamour: “England for the English! Let the foreigner starve, that our own children may live!” They saw our granaries, our sealed and precious reserves, as the sole bulwark against our own dissolution, and they charged me with treason for even entertaining the thought of their distribution. But as I stood in that hallowed hall, and looked upon the faces of the delegates—from the sun-scorched plains of the south to the cold, iron-bound coasts of the north—I saw not foreigners, but brothers, bound to me by the sacred pact of our shared humanity, now facing a common doom. To obey the frantic cry of London would be to betray the very principle for which our age was lauded; to defy it would be to invite my own ruin, and perhaps, that of my nation.

The Austrian Chancellor, a man whose countenance seemed carved from the very granite of the Alps, and whose voice held the chill of their eternal snows, called upon me to speak. A hush fell upon the assembly, a silence more profound and dreadful than any clamour, for in it lay the concentrated hope and despair of a continent. I advanced to the rostrum, my limbs trembling, my spirit wracked by a sorrow so deep it threatened to unman me. I saw before me not the polished assembly of a triumphant age, but a gathering of chieftains, their faces gaunt with want, their eyes burning with the primal fear of the empty larder. The sublime architecture of the hall, designed to celebrate our unity, now served only to mock our division.

“Fellow delegates!” I began, my voice hoarse, and I had to pause to command its steadiness. “Nations of Europe! We have assembled in this place, which has so often witnessed the signing of treaties and the birth of peace, to confront a peril that no treaty has foreseen. Look about you! The face of the enemy we now confront is not that of a rival power, nor the flag of a hostile state. It is the hollow, spectral visage of Famine herself, who knows no borders, respects no sovereignty, and makes no distinction between the powerful and the weak. She is at our doors—she is in our fields—she is in the very air we breathe!”

A murmur of assent, mingled with anguish, swept through the hall. I felt my own conviction swell, a desperate fire in my breast. “We have built a world of wonders. We have spanned the oceans with silent vessels that fly beneath the waves, and we have tamed the lightning to carry our thoughts from one hemisphere to the other in the blink of an eye. We have declared that the age of conquest is over, that the wealth of one nation is the concern of all, and that the suffering of any is a stain upon the whole. Was this a noble lie? Was it a pretty sentiment to be admired in times of plenty, and cast aside at the first whisper of privation? If so, then all our vaunted progress is but a gilded tomb, and the peace we have cherished is a phantom, which will vanish at the first touch of reality!”

My appeal grew more impassioned, my sentences flowing in a torrent of desperate eloquence. I painted for them the ruin that awaited us should we retreat into selfish isolation: the borders closed, the old hatreds reawakened, the thin veneer of civilisation cracking to reveal the beast of self-preservation that lurked beneath. I spoke of the children in the villages of the Rhineland, their faces pale, their eyes large with a hunger they could not comprehend; of the fishermen on the coasts of my own England, who now drew up their nets empty, and whose boats lay rotting at the quays. I held up the spectre of our own dissolution, not at the hands of an army, but by the slow, grinding starvation that would turn neighbour against neighbour, and nation against nation, until the silence of the grave was the only peace left to us.

When I had finished, I stood drained, my soul laid bare before them. For a long moment, there was only the sound of my own ragged breath. Then the German delegate rose, his face a mask of stern resolution. “Your words are beautiful, Envoy of England,” he said, his voice devoid of warmth. “They are the words of a poet, not of a statesman. Germany will not see its children perish while its stores are emptied to feed the ambitions of others. We have a duty first to our own people.”

A cry of agreement went up from several quarters. My heart sank within me, a leaden weight of despair. But then, another rose—the delegate from the Iberian peninsula, her voice ringing with a fierce and sorrowful passion. “And what is a people?” she cried, her eyes flashing. “Is it only those who live within our arbitrary lines? Are not the hungry in Provence our people? Are not the starving in the Tyrol our brothers? To speak of ‘our own’ in this hour is the language of the barbarian, the creed of the wolf! We will share our last crust, or we will all perish together, and our vaunted civilisation will be remembered only as the most elaborate suicide in the history of mankind!”
