The courtroom seemed to float in a sluggish tide of heavy air, thick with the scent of damp teak and the slow decay of varnish. Through the tall, shuttered windows, which admitted a grudging, aqueous light, one could hear the soft, viscous slapping of the harbour water against the stone quays of Tanjong Pagar, and the muffled, far-off shouts of the Malay boatmen in the creek. It was a sound that, to Captain Elias Royle, sitting motionless and isolated on the hard bench at the back of the room, seemed to echo the very rhythm of his own exposed and wavering thoughts. He sat there, a figure of profound gravity, his hands clasped tightly upon his knees, staring at the bare, sweating nape of the neck of the Assessor who sat beneath the heavy carved lintel of the magistrate’s dais. The room was pervaded by an oppressive stillness, the kind of silence that seems to thicken under the low beams of tropical ceilings, holding within its suspension the innumerable, microscopic particles of human destinies unfolding in obscurity.

The proceedings had the formal, detached rhythm of a ritual. To Royle, the words spoken by the magistrate, by the marine superintendent, by the clerk who scratched endlessly with his pen, fell upon his consciousness with a strange, muffled effect, as though they were sounds travelling to him through a fathom of green water. He had given his own evidence an hour ago. He remembered the sensation of standing in the witness box, the wood warm and slightly greasy under his gripping fingers. He had spoken clearly, he believed, with the measured, deliberate tones of a man confronting a vast, uncomprehending darkness. He had spoken of the squall off the coast of Sumatra, of the sudden shifting of the currents, and of the inexplicable, fatal set of the tide towards the coral shelf of the Karang Reefs. He had spoken of these things as external forces, immense and shadowy, against which his vigilance had been unavailing. He had not mentioned the heavy, drugged sleep that had overtaken him in his chair, nor the sharp, warning voice of his mate that had failed to penetrate the profound lethargy of his exhaustion. He had constructed a version of events that bore the outward shape of truth, but which was, in its secret spirit, a monstrous evasion.

Now, it was the turn of Mr. Archibald Vale, his chief mate. Royle watched Vale’s figure as it moved from the body of the court towards the witness box. Vale walked with his usual, almost mechanical precision, his thin, upright back expressing a sort of rigid probity. He was a young man, still in his early thirties, but with a face that seemed to have been prematurely aged by the wind and the salt, a face of sharp, ascetic lines, with eyes of a pale, watery blue that held a singular, unblinking directness. Royle, observing him, felt a sudden, profound sense of his own moral isolation. Between these two men, who had shared the narrow, responsible life of a ship for eighteen months, there had always existed a barrier of temperament, an unspoken reserve. Vale was a man of method, of exact calculations, of a literal, uncompromising honesty that Royle, in the secret recesses of his heart, found both admirable and vaguely oppressive.

Vale took the oath. His voice, when he replied to the first formal questions of the magistrate, was low, distinct, and entirely devoid of emotion. He stated his name, his rank, his certificate number. He spoke of the weather conditions on the night of the grounding. The rain had been torrential, the visibility reduced to a few yards. He had been on the bridge, he said, from the second dog watch until midnight. The magistrate, a man with a heavy, pendulous face and eyes that seemed to look inward upon some perpetual, weary disgust, leaned forward, his chin resting upon his steepled fingers. He asked, in a tone of weary formality, whether the mate had any knowledge of the ship’s position at the time of the accident.

Vale’s pale eyes shifted, for a fraction of a second, towards the back of the room. Royle felt the glance like a physical impact, a sudden, sharp intimation of the immense, silent struggle that was taking place within that precise and orderly mind. The mate’s hands, resting on the ledge of the box, tightened almost imperceptibly. He began to speak, and his words, at first, seemed to flow with the same mechanical certainty as before. He spoke of the bearings taken at sunset, of the log line, of the estimated speed. Then, there was a pause. It was a very brief pause, but to Royle, waiting in the breathless heat of the court, it seemed to expand into an immense, echoing chasm of time. The scratching of the clerk’s pen ceased. The slapping of the harbour water outside seemed to grow louder, more insistent, like the slow beating of a muffled drum.

In that pause, Royle saw, or imagined he saw, the whole obscure moral drama of the sea enacted in the slight, almost imperceptible tremor of Vale’s jaw. He knew that Vale had warned him. He knew that Vale had entered his cabin, had found him sunk in a heavy, unnatural slumber, his face flushed with the brandy he had taken to ward off the chill of the wet deck. He knew that Vale had shaken him by the shoulder, had spoken words of urgent, specific warning concerning the set of the current, words that Royle, in his drugged stupor, had acknowledged with a vague, irritated gesture before sinking back into oblivion. Vale knew this. And Royle knew that Vale knew.
