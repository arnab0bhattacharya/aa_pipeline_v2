The rain had been falling for three days, a persistent, grey curtain that turned the streets of Paris into a smear of mud and reflections, blurring the sharp edges of the city into a kind of watery indifference. It was a light that did not illuminate so much as it concealed, a heavy, suffocating dampness that crept into the bones of the old house on the Rue des Martyrs, where Madame Elżbieta Nowak sat in the dimness of her drawing room. She was a woman of sixty winters, her face a map of lines drawn by a life of waiting and silence, and she watched the clock on the mantelpiece with a patience that had long since ceased to be a virtue and had become a form of endurance. The clock ticked with a mechanical, indifferent rhythm, counting out the seconds of a life that felt, to her, suspended in a void between memory and expectation.

When the knock came, it was not the sharp, demanding rap of a creditor or the timid tap of a neighbor, but a hesitant sound, as if the person on the other side of the door were unsure of their own existence, or of the right they had to disturb the quiet of this foreign, indifferent place. Elżbieta rose, her joints protesting with a dull ache, and crossed the room. The floorboards groaned under her feet, a familiar sound that had accompanied her through years of solitude. She opened the door to a young man, barely twenty, dressed in a coat that was too thin for the weather, its fabric darkened by rain and wear. He stood there, hunched against the chill, his face pale and drawn, with a look of such profound exhaustion that it seemed to have stripped him of any pretense.

He spoke in Polish, his voice low, trembling slightly, as if the words were heavy stones he was struggling to lift. He introduced himself as Janek, and then, with a caution that felt like a physical barrier between them, he mentioned the name of her son, Andrzej. The name hung in the air, a ghost that had no body, a sound that carried the weight of a grave. Elżbieta did not speak immediately. She looked at him, searching his face for the familiar features, for the shadow of the boy she had known, but found only a stranger, a vessel for a message that she was not sure she wanted to receive. He claimed to have been in the same cell as her son in Warsaw, in the old fortress, before the deportation, before the silence that had swallowed them both. He carried, he said, a final message, a scrap of paper folded and folded again until it was the size of a fingernail, preserved against the damp and the darkness.

Elżbieta invited him in, not out of trust, but out of a curiosity that was tinged with a desperate, morbid hope. She wanted to believe him. The desire was so strong it felt like a physical pain, a sharp needle in her chest. She watched him as he entered, his movements stiff, his eyes darting to the corners of the room, to the curtains, to the shadows, as if he were expecting someone to follow him in. He could not meet her eyes. This was the first sign, the first crack in the facade of his story. When a man brings a message from the dead, she thought, he should look at you, should hold your gaze, should share the burden of the truth. But he looked at the floor, at the hem of her skirt, at the dust motes dancing in the faint light from the window. He was avoiding the truth, or perhaps he was protecting himself from it.

He placed the paper on the table with a hand that shook. It was a small, dark square, stained with what might have been water or sweat. Elżbieta did not touch it immediately. She sat down, her hands folded in her lap, and watched him. He remained standing, his shoulders hunched, his face a mask of solemnity that did not quite fit his young features. He spoke of the conditions in the prison, of the cold, of the hunger, of the songs they sang to keep the darkness at bay. His words were halting, fragmented, full of pauses that seemed to stretch the time between them. He described the cell, the stone walls, the lack of light, the way the air tasted of rust and despair. It was a detailed account, too detailed, perhaps, or perhaps just too familiar. It was the language of the damned, spoken by the living.

Elżbieta listened, her mind working in the shadows of her thoughts. She remembered Andrzej, not as the man he had become, but as the boy he had been, bright and full of life, before the world had taken him from her. She remembered his voice, his laughter, the way he had held her hand. Now, this young man stood before her, a stranger in a thin coat, holding a piece of paper that might be the last breath of her son. The irony of it was bitter, a taste she could not swallow. She wanted to believe that the message was real, that it was a thread connecting her to the past, to the life she had lost. But as she looked at Janek, she saw the uncertainty in his eyes, the way he shifted his weight from one foot to the other, the way he avoided the directness of her gaze. There was something else there, a secret, a lie, or perhaps just a fear so deep it had calcified into a performance.
