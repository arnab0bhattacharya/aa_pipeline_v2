The harbour of Saint-Amable-des-Brumes, on the night of the twenty-second of October, in the year one thousand seven hundred and ninety-three, was a place where the wind came off the water with a knife in its teeth and the lanterns of the fishing-smacks swung like drunken eyes, and where every shadow might be a patriot with a pike, or a priest with a price upon his head, or a poor wretch who was both, and neither, and wished only to be permitted to grow old. It was a harbour of mud and mystery, of tar and terror, and of such a prodigious quantity of fog that a man might lose his own boots in it and be obliged to send a boy to look for them.

I had brought the *Mermaid's Fancy* — a lugger of eighty tons, as sound as a bell and as ugly as sin — into the outer basin at the turn of the tide, and lay there with my crew of five and my cargo of contraband brandy that was never intended to be drunk by Frenchmen, and my business, which was of a nature that I shall not particularise, being a man who keeps his own counsel and expects the same of others. I was, at that time, what is called a smuggler by those who do not profit by my labours, and a benefactor of the distressed by those who do; and I had carried out of France, in the course of that autumn, eleven noblemen, four priests, a dentist, two sisters of charity, and a gentleman who had been a dancing-master at Versailles and who wept the whole passage because he had left his fiddle behind him.

It was near upon midnight, and I was standing by the capstan with my pipe in my mouth and my hat pulled down over my eyes, when there came a tapping at the gangway, and a voice, very small and very precise, said:—

"Captain Tregellick, I believe?"

I looked over the side and saw a woman. She was not young, nor yet old; she was dressed in the plain grey of a servant, and she had a face that might have been carved out of a walnut, so brown and so wrinkled it was, with two bright eyes in it that went over me and my vessel and my crew as if she were taking an inventory and marking down the prices. She had a bundle in her arms, and the bundle moved.

"You have the advantage of me, mistress," said I, "for you have my name and I have not yours."

"I am called Marthe," said she, "and I am nurse to the little citizen Vernier, whom I have the honour to bring to you."

"Citizen?" said I. "That is a word I do not love."

"It is a word that keeps the guillotine from my neck," said she, "and the child's, and perhaps yours, captain, if you will be so good as to remember it."

She came up the gangway as bold as a duchess, and set the bundle down upon the deck, and unfolded it, and there was a child of some five years, with a white face and great dark eyes that had no tears in them but a great deal of wondering, and a little coat that had been good once and was now patched at the elbows.

"This is the son of the citizen Vernier who was a physician," said the woman, "and of his wife who was a lady of the old court, and both are now in the prison of the Conciergerie, and will be dead by the morning, and they have sent the child to you to be carried to England, and I have brought him, and I am coming with him."

"You are coming with him?" said I.

"I am coming with him," said she, "and I will thank you not to argue, for I have walked nine miles with him in my arms and I have not the patience for argument."

Now, I am a man who has seen a great deal of the world and has been cheated in most of the ports of it, and I have learned to look at a face and to say to myself, "This one is honest," or "This one would sell his grandmother for a sou and throw in his grandfather for the boot." And this woman Marthe, with her walnut face and her bright eyes, I could not read at all. She was too ready with her answers, and she looked at my crew too much, and she had a way of standing with her head a little on one side, as if she were listening for something — for the tramp of soldiers, perhaps, or for the click of a pistol, or for the footsteps of the Committee of Public Safety coming down the street with their lists in their hands.

"You will forgive me," said I, "but in these times a man must be careful. There are those who carry children out of France who are not nurses."

"There are those who carry children out of France," said she, "who are mothers, and there are those who are neither, and I am one of the last, and you may search me if you please, and you will find a prayer-book and a pair of scissors and a piece of bread and a little wooden horse that the child will not go to sleep without, and nothing else in the world."

She held out her arms and let them fall, and there was something in the gesture that was either the most perfect innocence or the most perfect art, and I could not tell which, and I have thought of it many a time since.
