It is a strange and fearful thing to look back upon the hours of one’s existence, and to trace the very moment when the light of the world was extinguished for ever, leaving the soul to grope in the darkness of an altered and solitary state. I, who write these lines, am but nineteen years of age; yet my heart feels the weight and the weariness of a hundred winters, and my spirit is as a ruin, from which all that was young and hopeful has long since fled. For three long years I had been the sole attendant upon my mother’s sick-bed—three years during which the sun rose and set for me only to measure the stages of her decline, and during which the whole universe seemed to contract to the four walls of her chamber, and the single, agonizing point of her suffering. I had watched the roses fade from her cheek, and the fire of her noble mind grow dim, until at last the shadow of the grave fell upon her, and I was left alone in the world—a solitary, trembling creature, cast upon the shore of life without a guide, without a companion, and without a home.

The village of Llanfawr, where my mother’s cottage stood, looked upon me with a kind of wondering pity that was more painful than scorn. They saw a young girl, pale and wan, with eyes that had looked too long upon the face of death, wandering among the graves of the churchyard, or sitting for hours upon the moss-grown rocks that overlooked the wild and desolate sea. They whispered among themselves that my mind was unhinged by grief, that the long vigils I had kept had unsettled my reason, and that I was no fit companion for the living. But they knew not the depth of the desolation that had taken root in my soul. The cottage, with its low roof and its ivy-mantled walls, was now a sepulchre, and every object within it—the empty chair, the worn prayer-book, the cup from which she had last drank—spoke to me of a loss that was irreparable and eternal.

It was at this period that a letter reached me from a distant kinsman, a Mr. Vaughan of Shrewsbury, whose name I had scarcely heard before. He wrote in terms of formal compassion, offering me a home in his house, and the protection of his family. He spoke of the impropriety of my living alone, of the necessity of society for one of my tender years, and of the duties I owed to the world. As I read his eloquent and well-reasoned appeal, a feeling of horror and repulsion seized me. To leave the spot where my mother had breathed her last—to exchange the silence of her grave for the noise and frivolity of a stranger’s dwelling—to be looked upon as a dependent, a pensioner on the bounty of one who knew not the sacred nature of my sorrow! It was a thought not to be borne. I would rather have dwelt in the caverns of the earth, or braved the storms of the mountain-tops, than have submitted to the humiliation of such a bond. I wrote him a brief and resolute refusal, telling him that my place was here, and that I was content to abide in the solitude which was now my only friend.

From that hour I became a marked and solitary being in the eyes of Llanfawr. I refused the proffered kindness of the neighbours, and shut myself up in my desolate dwelling, as in a tomb. My days were spent in a kind of waking dream, and my nights in watching the stars, which seemed the only things in the universe that understood my grief. Sometimes, in the dead of the night, when the wind howled among the trees and the rain beat against the casement, I would fancy that I heard my mother’s voice calling to me, and I would rise and wander forth into the tempest, as if to seek her among the shadows of the hills. The villagers saw me, and crossed themselves, muttering that I was visited by evil spirits, and that the cottage was become a place accursed. But I heeded them not. The world of man had become a phantasmagoria, a hideous mockery, and I turned from it with loathing to the sublime and terrible companionship of nature.

One evening, as the sun was setting in a flood of crimson and gold behind the mountains, I sat upon the cliffs, looking out upon the vast and heaving ocean. The waves rolled in with a mournful and eternal sound, and the cry of the sea-birds seemed like the wailing of lost souls. In that moment of intense and solitary communion with the elements, I felt a strange and wild exaltation. I was alone, it is true; but I was free. I was the mistress of my own destiny, however sad and lonely that destiny might be. I had refused the chains of society, and had chosen the liberty of the desert. Let them think me mad—let them shun me as a pestilence. I had a home in the heart of the storm, and a companion in the silence of the night. I was the daughter of grief and the child of solitude; and in that wild and desolate state, I found a fearful and melancholy peace.
