The village of Cinderby Rigg lay in a hollow of the Durham hills like a handful of soot in a cupped palm, and at its heart, or rather at its mouth, stood the pithead of the Black Seam Colliery, where the great wooden winding-gear creaked and groaned from dawn till dusk, and the coal-waggons rattled down the incline with a noise like the grinding of a thousand iron teeth. It was a place of perpetual twilight, where the sky was a pale and grudging stripe above the chimney-stacks, and where the very air tasted of sulphur and wet earth and the slow, patient sorrow of the poor.

In this agreeable locality, at the hour of six in the morning, with a fog as thick as a blanket and twice as damp, there might be seen a small boy of some eleven winters, whose name was Timothy Gubbins, and whose occupation was to sit upon a wooden bench beside the coal-screens and pick out the stones from the tumbling stream of black diamonds as they rattled past him. He was a boy of a pale and pinched countenance, with a great deal of forehead and very little flesh, and eyes that seemed to have been put into his head by a thoughtful Providence in order that he might see the coal and not the sky. His jacket was a thing of shreds and patches, his breeches were his father's before him, and his boots—or rather the conglomerate of leather and string which did duty for them—had been mended so often that they were more darning than boot.

Now, it happened upon this particular morning that the machinery, which was old and cross and subject to fits of temper, as machinery will be, gave a sudden and violent lurch; the great chain snapped with a report like a pistol-shot; and the coal-waggon, which had been ascending the incline, came crashing down upon the screens with a noise that brought every woman in Cinderby Rigg to her door with her heart in her mouth. When the dust and the shouting had subsided, and the men had picked themselves up, it was found that Timothy Gubbins had been caught between the waggon and the bench, and that his left leg was broken in two places, and his shoulder dislocated, and his head cut open in a manner that would have been frightful to behold had not his mother, Mrs. Gubbins, been a woman of that stern and silent school which considers any display of feeling to be a species of self-indulgence.

Mrs. Gubbins was a widow, and she had four children, of whom Timothy was the eldest and the breadwinner; and when she saw her son carried into the cottage on a hurdle, with his face as white as the driven snow and his leg dangling at an angle that made the neighbours click their tongues, she did not scream nor faint, but she took her apron and twisted it in her hands, and said, "Well, there's a mercy in it, anyhow; he might have been killed outright." Which was the nearest approach to a thanksgiving that her theology permitted.

For three weeks the boy lay in the little back room, while the doctor came and went and shook his head, and the parish allowed two shillings a week, and the mother sold the clock and the tea-kettle and her wedding-ring to buy beef-tea and lint. And at the end of the third week, when the boy was just beginning to sit up and look about him with the wondering eyes of a convalescent, there came to the cottage door a smart young man in a black coat and a white choker, with a face as smooth as a new pin and a manner as oily as a lamp-wick, who introduced himself as Mr. Silas Crummles, the confidential agent of Mr. Bartholomew Grind, the proprietor of the Black Seam Colliery.

"Good morning, Mrs. Gubbins," said Mr. Crummles, bowing with the air of a man who has practised before a looking-glass. "I trust I find you well. And the dear boy—improving, I hope? Dear, dear, what an accident! What a shocking accident! Mr. Grind was quite overcome when he heard of it. 'Crummles,' he said to me—I remember his very words—'Crummles, that boy must not suffer. See to it, Crummles; see to it.' And so I have come, my good woman, with a little paper—a mere formality—which will put the matter upon a proper footing, and enable Mr. Grind to do the handsome thing by you and yours."

And with that he laid upon the table a document, which he smoothed out with the palm of his hand, and a crisp new five-pound note, which he laid upon the document with the air of a conjurer producing a rabbit from a hat.

Mrs. Gubbins looked at the note, and then at the paper, and then at the note again; and her hand went out towards it, and then came back to her apron, and she stood there twisting the apron in her fingers, as she had done on the day of the accident, and said nothing at all.

"You will observe," said Mr. Crummles, "that the sum is five pounds. Five sovereigns, my good woman. It is not every proprietor who would do as much. Indeed, I may say it is not every proprietor who would do anything at all. The law, you know—the law is a very curious thing—the law would probably say that the boy was guilty of contributory negligence; that he ought not to have been sitting where he was sitting; that—"

"He was sitting where he was put," said Mrs. Gubbins, in a voice like the scraping of a rusty hinge.
