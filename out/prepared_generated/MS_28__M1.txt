The letter which I held in my trembling hand was written upon the coarsest and most sordid paper that the poverty of the wretched could procure; yet, as the characters danced before my eyes, blurred by the sudden rush of tears, it seemed to possess a weight and a power greater than any legal instrument of conveyance or royal decree. I, the Lady Eleanora Vance, the widow of the late Sir Arthur Vance, the mistress of Wychwood Hall with its hundred windows and its ancient oaks, stood in my boudoir, a temple of luxury and refined solitude, and felt the solid floor of my existence give way beneath my feet. Twenty years! Twenty years had I lived in a delusion of security, building up my reputation as a fortress of marble against the world, and now a single stroke of a pen had crumbled my ramparts to dust.

It was a calm evening in November; the fog that usually enwraps this great city had lifted for a while, leaving the air chill and clear. I looked out upon the garden, where the last yellow leaves of the chestnut trees were fluttering to the earth, a fit emblem of my own fallen hopes. The sun, broad and red, was sinking behind the distant spires of the metropolis, casting long, mournful shadows across the lawn. How often had I gazed upon this prospect with a sense of serene triumph, congratulating myself on my escape from the abyss of poverty and shame! I had been young, and beautiful, and loved; but the world was a stern judge, and the law of society a tyrant more cruel than any eastern despot. To save my name, to preserve the glittering destiny that awaited me, I had committed a deed which now rose up before me, a phantom of horror and remorse. I had abandoned my child—my Agnes—at the gates of the Foundling Hospital, leaving her to the mercy of strangers and the cold charity of the parish, while I fled to safety and wealth.

For twenty years I had heard nothing of her. I had suffered the agony of that silence, it is true; in the dead watches of the night, when the rain pattered against the casement and the wind sighed through the trees like the voice of a reproaching spirit, I had started from my pillow, imagining that I heard the cry of the infant I had forsaken. But Time, that benevolent friend to the guilty, had dulled the edge of my anguish. I had married Sir Arthur; I had gained a position in the world; I had become a personage to be respected and courted. I told myself that the child was better off in the obscurity of her humble lot, that she was spared the temptations of wealth and the burden of my sin. I had built a wall of sophistry around my heart, and I had believed it impregnable.

But now, the wall was shattered. The letter came from a woman in the employ of a benevolent society, a Mrs. Pringle, who, in her zeal for the welfare of the friendless, had discovered the identity of the seamstress who worked in the squalid attics of Gray’s Inn Lane. "A young woman of twenty years, of delicate frame and gentle manners, though wasted by toil and want," the letter ran. "She bears a striking resemblance to the portrait of your late mother, which hangs in the gallery of the National Institution. She is known by the name of Agnes Leigh. She toils from sunrise to sunset for a pittance that scarcely keeps the life in her body, yet she endures with a patience and a resignation that are truly angelic."

Agnes! The name struck upon my ear like a knell. It was the name I had whispered to the nurse before I turned away. She was alive! She was here, in this very city, within a few miles of my luxurious home. She was a seamstress! The image rose before me with terrible distinctness: a pale, emaciated figure, bending over the needle by the light of a tallow candle, her eyes dim with the dust of the garret, her fingers pricked and bleeding, while I, her mother, sat by the fire in silks and satins, eating the bread of idleness. The contrast was too dreadful to be borne. A cry of anguish escaped my lips, and I sank upon the sofa, burying my face in my hands.

"Oh, God!" I exclaimed. "Is this thy justice? Have I not suffered enough in the long years of separation, in the dreadful uncertainty of her fate, that she must now be presented to me in this guise of abject misery? To see her, and not to claim her, is impossible; to claim her, is to confess my shame to the world!"

The struggle that ensued in my mind was of that nature which can only be conceived by those who have lived for opinion, and who find their idol suddenly transformed into a source of destruction. If I acknowledged her, what would become of me? I should be cast out from the society of which I was the ornament; my name would be a byword for dishonour; the friends who flattered me would turn away in disgust. I should lose my fortune, perhaps; for the will of Sir Arthur, though generous, had conditions attached to it which my exposure might invalidate. And yet, was I to leave her to perish? Was I to allow the fruit of my womb to rot in the gutter, while I lived in luxury? The voice of nature, long stifled, now cried out within me with a force that drowned the whispers of prudence and fear. It was a wild, tempestuous emotion, a mixture of love, remorse, and terror, that swept over my soul like a storm upon the ocean.
