# The Photograph

The bell above the door gave its usual thin clatter, and Edwin Marsh looked up from the ledger to see a tall man step into the shop. He was well-dressed, perhaps forty, with a clipped grey beard and the bearing of someone accustomed to authority. He asked for a particular brand of cigarettes, and Marsh turned to the shelves behind him, glad of the excuse to look away. His hands were not entirely steady.

Marsh had last seen that face fifteen years before, across a courtyard in Meean Meer, illuminated by the flare of a lantern. The man had been standing over the body of a young subaltern named Carrow, and the knife in his hand had caught the light as he turned. Marsh, then a sergeant in the 84th, had been passing through the yard on his way back from the guardroom. He had frozen. The man had looked directly at him, then walked calmly away into the darkness. By dawn the body had been discovered, and the killing attributed to one of the camp followers. Marsh had said nothing. He had told himself that there was no proof, that the man was an officer, that his word would carry no weight against a captain's. He had told himself this so often that it had become a kind of truth, though not the kind that let a man sleep.

Now the man stood in Marsh's shop in Croydon, examining a box of cigarettes with mild attention, and showed no sign of recognition whatsoever. He paid with a half-sovereign, waited for his change, and departed with a polite nod. Marsh stood behind the counter for a long time afterwards, listening to the ordinary sounds of George Street—the rattle of a brewer's dray, the distant shout of a paper boy—and felt the years collapse beneath him like a rotten floor.

He considered whether he might be mistaken. The man was older, fleshier, dressed in the quiet tweeds of a gentleman at leisure rather than the khaki and puttees of a soldier. But Marsh knew he was not mistaken. He had seen that face in dreams for a decade and a half, and the dreams had not softened its outlines but sharpened them, until every feature was as precise and terrible as a steel engraving.

That evening he closed the shop at the usual hour and walked home through the lamplit streets with his head down, turning the matter over. He could go to the police, but what would he say? That he had recognised a customer as a murderer from an event fifteen years past in India? That he had witnessed the crime himself but had never reported it? He could see how that would unfold. The desk sergeant would take his name and address, and nothing would come of it except that Marsh would be marked down as a man of unreliable character. He had built something here in Croydon—a respectable business, a place in the parish, a reputation that had taken him ten years to construct out of nothing. He was not eager to see it dismantled.

He decided to do nothing. He went to bed and lay awake until the small hours, then fell into a shallow sleep punctuated by dreams of lantern-light and blood.

The following morning was a Thursday. Marsh opened the shop at eight, served his regular customers, and thought that perhaps the matter would end there. The man had been a stranger passing through, a casual customer who would not return. There was no reason to think otherwise.

But that night the shop was broken into.

Marsh arrived at quarter to eight to find the back door forced open with a crowbar, the till smashed, and several packets of cigarettes and a quantity of loose tobacco taken. The damage was considerable but not catastrophic. What troubled him was not the theft but the fact that the small desk in the back room, where he kept his private papers, had been opened and its contents scattered across the floor. His marriage certificate, his army discharge papers, old receipts and ledgers—all of them pulled out and examined. And the framed photograph that had stood on the shelf above the desk was gone.

It was a regimental photograph, taken outside the barracks at Meean Meer in 1882. Marsh was in it, standing in the back row with the other non-commissioned officers. So were a dozen other men. It had no value to anyone except Marsh himself. It was not the sort of thing a common thief would take.

He stood in the back room looking at the empty space on the shelf where the photograph had been, and he felt a fear that was quite different from the fear of burglary. A thief wanted money or goods. Someone who took a regimental photograph from a shopkeeper's desk wanted information. They wanted to know who Marsh had been, and where, and when.

He went to the police, as any respectable shopkeeper would, and reported the break-in. The constable who came was a young man with a fair moustache and an air of conscientious dullness. He noted the details, looked at the forced door, and offered the opinion that it was likely the work of opportunists. He did not seem interested in the missing photograph, and Marsh did not press the point. He did not want to explain why it mattered. He did not want to explain anything.

Instead, the following morning, he closed the shop for the day and took the train to London Bridge, and from there a hansom to an address in a quiet street off the Strand. The building was unremarkable, a set of rooms above a stationer's shop, with a small brass plate beside the door that read: *Mr A. J. Voss, Enquiries*. Marsh had seen the name in a newspaper advertisement some months before and had noted it without any particular reason. Now he had a reason.
