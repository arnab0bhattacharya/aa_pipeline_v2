The wind had swept without ceasing over Blacklees Moor for three months. It rattled the lattice of the low chamber where my aunt lay, and it seemed to me that the gale and the fever within her veins kept pace, both fierce, both unyielding, both bent on final ruin. I was twenty-two; I had known little of life but the turning of heavy pages, the boiling of bitter draughts, and the long, grey watching of the night. My hands were chapped; my eyes were strained; yet I did not repine. I believed myself to be performing a duty, and in that belief there was a kind of stern sustenance. I did not know then that duty, faithfully discharged, is no shield against injustice.

My aunt, Mrs. Marsden, had been a hard woman. She had ruled her household with a will that admitted no question, and she had ruled me doubly so, for I was dependent. Yet beneath her severity I had always detected a vein of iron integrity, and I had loved her for it, as one loves a crag that resists the weather. I believed she loved me too, after her fashion. She had spoken, in the early days of her illness, of my future. "Elinor," she had said, "you will not be rich, but you will be provided for. I have made provision." I had pressed her hand, and said nothing. The words were enough. I built on them as on a rock.

She died at dawn on a Tuesday. The wind dropped for an hour, and a pale, spectral light lay on her face. I closed her eyes, and knelt beside the bed, and prayed. Then Mr. Thorne, the attorney from Hepton, arrived. He was a cold, precise man, who smelled of damp wool and looked at me with a sort of dry pity. He laid the will on the table. I read it. The farm, the savings, the stock, the furniture—everything went to my cousin, Henry Marsden, of Liverpool. There was a clause. I was to have nothing, "having been already maintained by the testatrix for the space of five years, in lieu of all expectation." I read it twice. The letters swam. I looked up at Mr. Thorne. "Henry never came near her in five years," I said. My voice sounded strange to me—flat and hard, like a stone dropped into a well. "He did not write. He did not send so much as a message at Christmas." Mr. Thorne folded his papers. "The law, Miss Fenwick, is plain. Your aunt was at liberty to dispose of her property as she saw fit." "And if she was wrong?" I said. He did not answer. He pressed his hat to his chest, murmured some conventional phrase of condolence, and left me standing in the kitchen with the will in my hands and the fire gone grey in the grate.

I was free. I was penniless. The two facts presented themselves to me with a force that was almost physical, a blow to the chest. For five years I had been bound to that house, bound to that bed, bound to the slow, inexorable extinction of a life I could not save. Now the bond was snapped, and I stood upright in the sudden, terrible emptiness of liberty, and found it was a desert. I had nowhere to go. I had no money for the coach. I had no reference, no patron, no friend within thirty miles, save one. Mrs. Clarkson of High Riggs had offered me a place, months ago, when I had met her at the Hepton market. She was a woman of property, a widow, and she had looked at me with a gaze I can only call appraising. "You are a good nurse, Miss Fenwick," she had said. "If ever you are at liberty, come to me. I want a companion—someone who can read aloud and manage my accounts. I can offer thirty pounds and a home." I had thanked her coldly, for my aunt was still alive, and the offer seemed to me then like a hand stretched across a gulf I had no wish to cross. But I had remembered it. Now it returned to me, and I tasted its bitterness.

I knew Mrs. Clarkson. I knew her house. It was a tall, stone-built place on the edge of the moor, respectable and bleak, with windows that seemed to watch the road like suspicious eyes. I knew her sons—two narrow, starched young men who looked through me as if I were a pane of glass. I knew her manner of speech, which was measured and correct, and which left no room for warmth. I knew that she had once turned away a servant girl for smiling too often at the door. I knew that her charity was of the kind that gives bread with one hand and holds a sermon in the other. To enter that house would be to exchange one bondage for another. I should be fed, and housed, and I should be nothing. I should read aloud to a woman who did not listen, and manage accounts that were not mine, and sit at table in the place allotted to the dependent, and feel my soul contract by slow degrees, as a hand contracts in frost.

And yet. I had nothing. The frost was in the very walls. I had no bread in the cupboard. The vicar might give me a reference, but a reference is not a livelihood. I could not live on the moor like a wild creature, though the impulse was strong in me to do so—to run out into the wind and the heather and be answerable to none. But the body makes its claims. Hunger is not a sentiment; it is a fact. I thought of my aunt's face in death, and of the clause in the will, and a slow, hot anger rose in me—not against Henry, who was merely selfish and remote, but against the system that had made me a nurse for five years and then cast me out with nothing.
