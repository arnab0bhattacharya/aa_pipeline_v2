The fog came up the river as if the whole German Ocean had been boiled down to a vapour and were being ladled out by the pint and the gallon among the shipping; so that the tier of colliers at Gravesend, the coal-brigs, the oyster-boats, and the Custom-house cutter herself, with her bright brass guns, lay all in a huddle of brown wool, and a man might have touched his own bowsprit before he knew it for his own. It was on such a night—a night of the kind that swallows up the water and gives it back again in the form of a general damp upon the spirits—that the ferry-lamp of Abel Vellum, the waterman, burned like a small malignant eye in the midst of the general blindness, and the ferry itself, a stout, clumsy, tarred and pitchy craft, with a great blunt nose and a tail of rope, lay moored at the bottom of the slippery wooden stairs, waiting for custom.

Now, Abel Vellum was a man who loved a quiet life with a passion that amounted in him to a kind of genius; and he had a daughter, Miss Polly Vellum, who was the very opposite of quiet, being brisk, bright, dark-eyed, and of a spirit so lively that it seemed to have been given her in place of a dowry. And at the door of the little ferry-house—a cottage built of ship's timber, with a chimney like a squat funnel—there stood, on this same foggy evening, two men, as opposite as the fog and the lamp.

The first was Mr. Tobias Cringe, of the Customs, a tall, thin, melancholy gentleman, who wore his hat with the air of a man who expected it to be taken off by somebody in authority, and who had a habit of clearing his throat before he spoke, as if to apologise in advance for the liberty of making a remark. The second was Captain Dan Dusk, of no service at all, a broad, brown, black-whiskered mariner, with a great-coat of the colour of the sea in a storm, and a laugh that came out of him like the boom of a gun, and who was understood to be engaged in the free trade, which is to say, in bringing in tobacco and brandy without troubling the King's officers for their opinion on the subject.

'Miss Vellum,' said Mr. Cringe, with a bow that was half a shiver, 'the fog is unusually—hem—dense. I have the honour to request the loan of your father's boat, to proceed down the river as far as the Nore, upon the King's business.'

'And I, my pretty Poll,' said Captain Dusk, with a slap of his great hand upon his thigh, 'want the same boat, to proceed up the river, upon my own business; and I'll pay double, and stand a supper into the bargain.'

'You'll do no such thing, Dan Dusk,' said Polly, tossing her head. 'My father's boat is my father's boat, and my father's boat is not to be hired to—to persons who go about at night.'

'Which persons?' said the Captain, laughing. 'There's two of us, Polly.'

'You know very well which persons,' returned Polly, with a flash of her eye; and she turned to Mr. Cringe, and said, 'And you, sir, may go and catch your smuggler in a sieve, for all the help you'll get from us.'

'Miss Vellum,' said Mr. Cringe, with dignity, 'I am upon my duty. There is a notorious contrabandist—hem—known to be upon the river this very night, and I am resolved to—to intercept him.'

'You'll intercept nothing but a cold in your head, Mr. Cringe,' said Polly.

At this point, Abel Vellum himself came out of the ferry-house, with a pipe in his mouth and a lantern in his hand, and looked from one suitor to the other with the face of a man who wished himself a hundred miles inland, upon a dry road, with a good fire and no callers.

'Now, father,' said Polly, 'here's Mr. Cringe wants the boat to go down, and here's Dan Dusk wants it to go up, and I say the boat stops where it is.'

'Abel,' said Captain Dusk, 'I'll give you a guinea.'

'Mr. Vellum,' said Mr. Cringe, 'I must remind you that the boat is—hem—licensed; and that a refusal to assist the Revenue is a matter that may be—that may be looked into.'

'Abel,' said Captain Dusk, 'I'll give you two guineas, and a keg of the best French brandy that ever came over the side of a lugger.'

'Father,' said Polly, 'don't you listen to either of them.'

Abel Vellum took his pipe out of his mouth, looked at the fog, looked at the lamp, looked at his daughter, and then, with the air of a man who has solved a great problem, said, 'I'll take the boat down with Mr. Cringe, because it's the King's business, and I dursn't refuse; and I'll take the boat up with Dan Dusk afterwards, because—because the fog's that thick, no one'll know.'

'Father!' cried Polly.

'Polly,' said Abel, 'go indoors and get the supper.'

So it was settled; and Mr. Cringe, with many apologies, got into the boat, and Abel Vellum took the oars, and they went off into the fog, which closed over them like a brown curtain. And no sooner were they gone than Captain Dusk, who had stood watching them with a queer smile upon his brown face, said, 'Polly, my dear, I'll trouble you for the loan of your father's other boat.'

'There isn't any other boat,' said Polly.

'There's the little wherry under the bank,' said the Captain, 'and I know it, and you know it, and your father knows it, and I'll give you a kiss for it.'

'You'll give me nothing of the kind,' said Polly; but the Captain was already gone, with a laugh that seemed to hang in the fog after him, and Polly, after a moment's hesitation, ran down the slippery stairs and jumped into the wherry herself, and pulled off after him, with a stout heart and a very poor pair of oars.
