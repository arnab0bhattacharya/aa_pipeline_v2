It was a truth universally acknowledged, at least in the parish of Little Harrowden, that a clergyman’s wife of considerable intellect and moderate ambition must be in want of a scheme for the improvement of her neighbors. Mrs. Thistlethwaite possessed both qualities in such abundance that she found her domestic duties insufficient to occupy the vast expanse of her faculties. Her husband, the Reverend Mr. Thistlethwaite, was a man of such profound simplicity and gentle disposition that he served as a perfect mirror for her own reflections, offering no resistance and no counterpoint. Consequently, Mrs. Thistlethwaite turned her attention outward, seeking in the village of Little Harrowden the raw materials for a masterpiece of social architecture.

Her eyes, sharp and discerning behind the spectacles she wore only for reading, alighted upon two individuals who seemed, by all outward appearances, to be destined for one another. The first was Dr. Arthur Penhaligon, the village physician, a man of thirty-two with a complexion of the pale sort that suggests a life spent more among books than in the garden, and a manner of such quiet reserve that it was frequently mistaken for indolence. The second was Miss Eliza Vance, niece to the local squire, a young woman of twenty who possessed the advantage of a very comfortable income, a complexion of the ruddy sort that suggested a life spent more in the saddle than in the drawing room, and a spirit of such lively independence that it was frequently mistaken for rudeness.

Mrs. Thistlethwaite had observed them both with the keen eye of a naturalist studying specimens in a jar. She noted that Dr. Penhaligon had a habit of visiting the squire’s estate on Tuesdays to inspect the health of the horses, and that Miss Vance had a habit of being in the garden on those same days, tending to a collection of roses that were largely decorative and entirely unnecessary. The coincidence was too striking to be accidental; it was, she decided, a whisper from Providence.

The plan was set in motion with the subtlety of a cannon blast. Mrs. Thistlethwaite invited Dr. Penhaligon to tea on a Thursday, citing the need for a professional opinion on her mother’s gout, a condition that had been cured three years prior but remained a useful conversational prop. Simultaneously, she sent a note to the squire, expressing her concern for Miss Vance’s recent melancholy and suggesting that a change of scene might do her good. The result was that both parties found themselves in the Thistlethwaites’ drawing room, separated by a table laden with biscuits and a samovar, and united by the presence of their hostess.

Mrs. Thistlethwaite began with the delicacy of a surgeon. “Dr. Penhaligon,” she said, pouring tea with a steady hand, “you must be aware that the season is advancing, and that our young people are in need of guidance. I have been thinking of Miss Vance.”

Dr. Penhaligon, who had been studying the pattern on the carpet with an intensity that bordered on the devotional, looked up. “Miss Vance?” he asked, his voice mild and uninflected. “I find her quite charming. Her views on the proper pruning of rose bushes are, I confess, rather more advanced than my own.”

“Indeed?” said Mrs. Thistlethwaite, her eyes twinkling with a light that suggested she had struck a vein of gold. “And does she not possess a spirit that might well suit a man of your profession? A doctor requires a wife who is not easily cowed by a pulse or a prescription.”

At this, Miss Vance, who had been sitting in the corner by the fire, looking at a book she was not reading, turned her head. “A spirit, Mrs. Thistlethwaite?” she said, her tone bright and clear. “I should think so. I find that I am rarely cowed by anything, except perhaps the wind. But I do not think I am the sort of woman who would wish to be cowed in the first place. I prefer to stand in the breeze.”

Dr. Penhaligon turned his head slowly, as if a bird had landed on the windowsill. “The breeze,” he said. “Yes. That is a sensible preference. I, myself, am quite averse to being blown about. I prefer a still air. A still air is conducive to thought, and thought is, I believe, the primary function of a human being.”

Mrs. Thistlethwaite clapped her hands together softly. “But a still air can become stagnant, my dear Dr. Penhaligon. It needs a gentle current to keep it fresh. And what better current than the conversation of a woman of Miss Vance’s vivacity?”

Miss Vance looked at the doctor. The doctor looked at Miss Vance. There was a silence in the room that was not empty, but full of a strange, cold electricity. They were looking at each other with the same expression, a mixture of polite bewilderment and intense irritation. It was the look of two mice who have been placed in the same cage and have realized that the other is not a friend, but a threat to their comfort.

“I am afraid,” said Dr. Penhaligon, after a pause that lasted long enough for the teakettle to boil and cool again, “that I am not a man of much conversation. I am a man of facts. I deal in symptoms and remedies. I do not deal in metaphors or breezes.”

“Nor do I,” said Miss Vance. “I am a woman of actions. I prune roses. I ride horses. I do not sit in drawing rooms and discuss the nature of the soul. I find such discussions to be as tedious as a long sermon, and I have no patience for either.”

Mrs. Thistlethwaite frowned. This was not the dialogue she had scripted. She had expected a spark, a flicker of interest, a tentative step toward the altar. Instead, she was watching a mutual rejection of the very premise of her scheme. They were both, she realized with a shock, perfectly content to be alone. They were, in fact, delighted by the idea of remaining so, provided they could do so without offending the hostess who had engineered the encounter.
