I remember the evening when he came to me as one remembers a certain quality of light upon water—a thing apprehended before it is understood, and understood, perhaps, never. The Pension Vaudois, perched above the lake at Lausanne, was a place of great quietude, a house of expatriates where the past was a matter of settled, silent habit. The air in the drawing-room was thick with the scent of wax and the breath of the damp lake, and the long windows, dark against the early dusk, seemed to hold back not the cold, but a profound and liquid silence from the vast, steely expanse of the Léman. I had been reading, or had been holding a book in my lap, a thing of no consequence, for my thoughts were far away, adrift in that sea of memory where all forms are blurred and all sounds are muffled.

It was the elderly concierge, Madame Dupré, who broke the stillness. She stood in the doorway, a rigid silhouette against the lesser gloom of the hall, and announced, with the grave disapproval of her class for all that is irregular, a Monsieur Volkov. The name reached me through a haze of years, a sound from another world of parquet floors and court uniforms, of whispered conspiracies in the anterooms of ministries. My husband’s secretary. A young man then, with a scholar’s stoop and eyes that held a restless, hungry light. He had been a fixture of my life for five years, a shadow who managed my husband’s correspondence and his appointments, who saw everything and said little. And then, one winter, he had simply vanished, leaving behind a note of formal resignation and a profound, unsettling absence.

I bade her show him in. The man who entered was not the young man I remembered, and yet he was the same, as a ruin is the same as the house it once was. The stoop was more pronounced, the face was thinner, sharpened by some internal fire, and the restless light in his eyes had been banked to a steady, consuming glow. He wore a shabby, foreign-cut overcoat and carried a damp felt hat in his hands, which he turned and turned as if seeking some purchase in the polished air of the room. He did not advance far, but stood there on the edge of the Aubusson carpet, a creature of a different element, smelling faintly of wet wool and the city’s smoke. He bowed, a stiff, awkward gesture that was both an echo of the old formality and a rejection of it.

‘Countess,’ he said, and his voice was lower, rougher than I recalled. ‘I have taken a great liberty in coming here.’

I made a small motion towards the armchair opposite my own. ‘There are no liberties between old acquaintances, Monsieur Volkov. Only… interruptions. Please.’

He did not sit. He remained standing, a dark pillar in the warm, lamplit room. The silence stretched, filled by the ticking of the great clock in the corner, a sound like the slow, patient dripping of water in a deep cave. He seemed to be gathering himself, or perhaps searching for words in a language he no longer trusted. Outside, the wind had begun to move across the lake, and the window panes vibrated with a low, humming resonance.

‘I have not come for pleasantries,’ he began at last, the words falling heavily. ‘My business is… of a difficult nature. I have joined the Committee for Popular Liberation.’

He said the name as one might name a ship or a faith. I looked at him, at the gaunt planes of his face, and felt not surprise, but a kind of weary recognition. It was the logical, the terrible, outcome of that hungry light I had seen in him years ago. The abstract ideals he had once discussed in my husband’s study, the theories of German philosophers and French anarchists, had at last found their soil, and had grown into this.

‘Indeed,’ I said. My voice sounded distant, as if it belonged to another woman. ‘An honourable, if arduous, path.’

A flicker of something—disappointment, perhaps—crossed his features. He had expected shock, condemnation. He was prepared for a battle, and I had offered him only a placid observation.

‘It is not a question of honour, Countess. It is a question of necessity. Of justice. The old world is a corpse, and it poisons the air we breathe. It must be swept away.’ He took a step forward, and the worn boards of the floor creaked beneath his feet. ‘We are few, and we are poor. Our struggle is not with pamphlets and speeches alone. It requires… resources.’

And there it was. The purpose of his visit, laid bare between us like a weapon on a table. He had come to my quiet, ordered world to ask for the means to destroy it. He wished me to finance the dynamite, the printing presses, the passages for agitators, the very chaos that would one day sweep through these silent corridors and shatter the dark glass of the windows looking out on the placid lake. I felt a coldness creep into my limbs, a chill that had nothing to do with the evening air. It was the chill of a moral abyss opening at my feet.

He mistook my silence for calculation. ‘We are not bandits,’ he insisted, his voice rising with a desperate earnestness. ‘We are builders. We fight for a new dawn for the people, for a world without masters, without the suffocating weight of… of all this.’ His gesture was a vague, all-encompassing wave that seemed to take in the heavy curtains, the silver-framed photograph on the mantelpiece, my own placid existence.
