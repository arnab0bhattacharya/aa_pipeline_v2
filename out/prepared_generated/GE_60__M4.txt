It was a fact upon which the parish of St. Ogg’s had long since settled its opinion—and in a provincial town an opinion, once settled, hardens into a species of geology—that Mr. Ambrose Thorne, of Harrowden Grange, was the most fortunate of men. He had inherited a fine estate at five-and-twenty, he had a clear ruddy countenance that bespoke an easy digestion and an easier conscience, and he had married, within the year, Miss Honoria Bell, of Bell’s Cross, a young lady whose accomplishments were the subject of so much admiring comment among the county families that the match was held to be a kind of natural apotheosis, the reward of virtue in a world where virtue is not always so punctually rewarded. The wedding had been spoken of for months; the bride’s white satin had been described in the county paper with a minuteness that would have done honour to a court chronicle; and the tenantry had been regaled with beef and ale at the park gate, in the belief—which is one of the pleasantest fictions of rural society—that the happiness of the landlord and the digestion of the tenantry are mysteriously connected.

Yet it is in the nature of things that the chronicle should stop at the wedding, and that the years which follow, being unadvertised, should be left to work their own slow chemistry upon the characters of those whom the wedding had united. Honoria Thorne, standing at her chamber window on a certain October morning, some ten months after the satin and the beef, looked out upon the lawn with the eyes of a woman who has made a discovery—not a sudden, dramatic discovery, such as the stage affords, but the discovery which is the more terrible for being gradual, that the man she has married is a stranger to her in the only region where she had hoped to find a companion.

Ambrose Thorne was not a bad man; it is necessary to say so plainly, because the reader, if he be of Honoria’s temperament, will be inclined to think him so. He was, on the contrary, a thoroughly good fellow, in the sense in which that phrase is used of men who keep their accounts honestly, ride well to hounds, and are never unkind to a dependent. He had a hearty laugh, a hand that never refused a grasp, and a way with the farmers that made him beloved at every market ordinary. But his mind was of that order which finds its sufficiency in the visible and the immediate: he could tell you the price of wheat in three markets, the pedigree of every horse in his stable, and the exact state of the drainage in the lower meadow; and he could not tell you—it was not in him to conceive that any one should wish to know—what Plato had thought about the soul, or why the stars, which he saw every night, should be a matter of more than idle wonder. Books, to him, were furniture; he had a handsome library, and he was rather proud of it, in the way in which he was proud of his conservatory, as a proof that he was a man of taste; but he had never read a volume through since he left school, and he regarded his wife’s reading as a charming accomplishment, like her skill upon the pianoforte, to be exhibited before company and forgotten afterwards.

For the first months Honoria had not perceived this. She had been busy with the thousand small duties of a new household, and she had been happy in the mere novelty of love, which is a great deceiver. But as the autumn evenings lengthened, and the two sat together in the drawing-room, she became aware of a silence which was not the silence of content. She had brought with her from Bell’s Cross a small collection of books, the companions of her girlhood—a Shakespeare, a volume of Wordsworth, some essays of Montaigne in an old French edition, a history of the Crusades—and she had imagined herself reading them aloud to her husband, and discussing them with him by the firelight. She made the experiment. She read him a passage from the Wordsworth—the lines upon Tintern Abbey, which had been to her a kind of sacred text. Ambrose listened with polite attention, his eyes fixed upon the fire, and at the end he said, “Very pretty, my dear; very pretty indeed. It reminds me of the verses in the song-book your father used to have—‘The rose that blooms in yonder vale.’ Do you remember it?” And he hummed a bar or two, well pleased with his own application.

Honoria closed the book, and said no more that evening. But the next day she tried again, with a passage from the history—something about the taking of Jerusalem, which she thought might interest him, as he was fond of hearing of battles. He listened, and said, “Ah, the Crusaders—fine fellows, those; my grandfather used to say that the Thornes came over with the Conqueror, but I never could make out the pedigree. I must have it looked up one of these days.” And he fell into a pleasant reverie about the family tree, and the possibility of quartering the arms with something more ancient still.

It was then that Honoria understood. She was not angry; she was too just to be angry with a man for not being what nature had not made him. She was not contemptuous; she had too much sense to despise a good husband because he could not talk of Montaigne. But she was lonely, with the loneliness that is the more bitter because it is unsuspected by the person who causes it. She saw, with a clearness that was almost cruel, the whole tenor of the years before her: the children, the household, the visits, the dinners, the long evenings, and always this silence, this kind, well-meaning, impenetrable silence, in which her mind, if it were to live at all, must live alone.
