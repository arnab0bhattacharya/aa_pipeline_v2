I had thought the arrangement would last my life. For five years, since the fever carried my mother away, I had held the keys of Netherfold Parsonage. I had learned the price of mutton in Halifax, the temper of the kitchen grate, and the precise silence required by my brother, the Reverend Arthur Pemberton, when he prepared his Sunday sermon. It was a life of narrow walls and wide skies, of mending linen and bearing with the complaints of the poor, and I believed I had settled into it as a stone settles into a wall—necessary, unnoticed, and fixed.

That illusion was shattered on a Tuesday in November. The wind was high, tearing the last brown leaves from the elms, and the rain beat against the parlor window with the sound of a thousand tapping fingers. I was darning a sock by the fire when Arthur entered. He stood with his back to the hearth, a position he assumed when he meant to deliver a pronouncement.

“Agnes,” he said, “I have news.”

I did not look up, for the needle required my eye, but I felt the shift in the air—the sudden tension that precedes a storm. “Indeed? Is the Bishop coming to inspect the roof?”

“It is not the Bishop. It is Miss Fairweather.”

I pricked my finger then. A bead of blood rose, red and startling against the grey wool. Miss Fairweather. The name was like a drop of oil on water—clear, smooth, and separating everything else.

“She has accepted me,” Arthur continued, his voice vibrating with a self-satisfaction he rarely showed. “We are to be married in the spring. She will be mistress here.”

The needle fell from my hand. I sat very still, feeling the blood drain from my face, leaving me cold and hollow. Mistress here. The words echoed in the small room, louder than the gale outside. I had been the mistress here. I had been the one to soothe his tempers, to manage his accounts, to make the parsonage a habitable place for a man of God who cared nothing for the world of dust and soot.

“And what of me, Arthur?” I asked. My voice was calm, a hard-won calm that cost me much.

He looked at me, genuinely surprised, as if the thought of my existence had only just occurred to him. “You? You shall remain, of course. As long as you like. Miss Fairweather is very amiable. She speaks of you with the greatest kindness.”

Kindness. I knew that kindness. It was the patronage of the conqueror to the conquered. To remain in the house I had built, while another held the keys? To be the poor relation, the spinster sister, the unpaid companion, waiting upon the whims of a bride? A fierce fire lit in my breast—a fire of injustice.

“I shall not remain,” I said.

“Nonsense, Agnes. Where would you go? You have no money. You have no training. You are thirty years old. Do not be hysterical.”

“I am not hysterical,” I replied, rising to my feet. The room seemed to sway, but I gripped the back of the chair. “I am resolved. I will not be a pensioner in your wife’s house.”

Arthur sighed, the long-suffering sigh of a man dealing with a difficult child. “You are proud, Agnes. It is your besetting sin. Pride and a lack of faith. You must submit to Providence. God has provided for me a helpmeet. He will provide for you.”

“Providence does not often work through the parish poor box, Arthur,” I retorted. The words were bitter, and I saw him flinch. I had touched his conscience, which was a tender, if small, organ.

We ate dinner in silence. The mutton was dry, and the pudding heavy, but neither of us tasted it. I looked at my brother across the table. He was a handsome man, with a high forehead and a smooth, clerical manner. I had loved him once with the fierce devotion of a sister. Now, I looked at him and saw a stranger—a man who would trade his sister’s home for a soft hand and a pretty face.

That night, I could not sleep. The wind howled around the eaves like a lost spirit. I rose and went to the window. The darkness was absolute, save for the distant lights of the mills in the valley, glowing like the fires of hell. I thought of Miss Fairweather—dainty, accomplished, with her watercolors and her music. What did she know of scrubbing stone floors or haggling with the butcher? She would come here and find me in the way. She would smile at me over the breakfast urn, a smile of pity, while I sat in the corner, useless and withered.

No. I would rather face the workhouse than such humiliation.

The next morning, the storm had broken. A cold, grey light fell upon the moors. I went to my room and unlocked the small trunk where I kept my mother’s few trinkets. There was a gold brooch and a silver watch. It was not much, but it was something. I packed my portmanteau with my black dresses and my Bible.

When I came down, Arthur was reading the *Leeds Mercury*. He did not look up.

“I am going to Halifax,” I said. “I shall look for a situation.”

He lowered the paper. “A situation? As what? A governess? You have no accomplishments, Agnes. You are not fitted for it.”

“Then I shall be a housekeeper,” I said. “Or a companion to the sick. I am strong. I am honest. I shall find work.”

“You are throwing yourself away,” he said, his voice rising. “You are acting from spite. You wish to punish me.”

“I am acting from necessity,” I said. The restraint I had imposed upon myself for years began to crack. “You have taken my home, Arthur. You have taken my purpose. You leave me with nothing. Would you have me linger here, a ghost in your new paradise? No. I will go out into the world, even if it is a hard world. I will not be beholden to Miss Fairweather’s charity.”
