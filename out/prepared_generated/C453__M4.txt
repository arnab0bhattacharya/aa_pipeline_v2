The day had been planned with the precision of a naval engagement. The hampers were packed, the cold chicken wrapped in its waxed paper, the lemonade bottles corked and nestled in the shade of the cart. Mr. and Mrs. Ellsworth had even, at some expense, procured a small quantity of ice from the village to keep the butter from melting, a luxury that spoke volumes of their hopes for the afternoon. For it was not merely a picnic. It was a campaign, and the prize was the good opinion of Mr. Julian Ashworth, a gentleman of sober mien and considerable property in the next county, who had lately begun to show a marked, if restrained, interest in their eldest daughter, Clara.

Clara, a girl of sensible disposition and a pleasing countenance, was herself a bundle of nerves, a state her mother sought to soothe with assurances that the day would be a triumph of pastoral simplicity. The setting was to be the old Roman ruin at Hawthorn Reach, a picturesque jumble of tumbled stone and wildflowers that commanded a fine view of the valley. It was to be a scene of elegant repose, of genteel conversation and delicate sandwiches.

The plan, however, had not accounted for Great-aunt Agatha.

She descended upon them from her chambers just as the cart was being loaded, a vision in puce silk and a bonnet of truly alarming proportions, its ribbons a-tremble in the morning breeze. In her wake, perched upon her wrist with the solemnity of a judge, was her parrot, a large, iridescent green creature by the name of Captain Flint.

“Agatha, dear,” Mrs. Ellsworth began, her voice strained with a premonitory dread, “we had not thought you would wish to exert yourself so soon after your journey.”

“Nonsense, Margaret,” Great-aunt Agatha declared, her voice a cheerful rasp. “The air will do us both good. And the Captain has been in a dreadful sulk all morning. A change of scene is just the thing.” She fixed her bright, bird-like eyes on Clara. “And I should so like to meet this paragon of a Mr. Ashworth. I have a sense for these things, you know. A nose for character.”

Mr. Ellsworth, a man who had long ago learned the futility of opposing his aunt’s whims, merely sighed and gestured for the footman to make room in the cart for her capacious form and the bird’s ornate brass cage. Clara’s heart, which had been fluttering with hope, now sank to the level of her half-boots. The Captain, as if sensing her distress, let out a low, guttural chuckle that sounded unnervingly like a human throat clearing.

The ruin at Hawthorn Reach was as lovely as promised. The ancient stones were warm in the sun, and the air hummed with bees and the scent of wild thyme. They had just laid out the cloth and arranged the plates when Mr. Ashworth arrived, his groom holding his horse some distance away. He was impeccably turned out in a bottle-green coat, his manners as correct as his cravat.

“Miss Ellsworth,” he said, bowing over Clara’s hand. “A pleasure. The day is fine, is it not? A fitting backdrop for such agreeable company.”

Clara managed a smile, her cheeks flushing. For a few glorious moments, all was as it should be. Mr. Ashworth spoke of the harvest and the price of wool; Mrs. Ellsworth beamed; Mr. Ellsworth offered him a glass of the precious lemonade. Great-aunt Agatha, who had been installed in a camp chair in the shade of a crumbling archway, watched the proceedings with the unnerving intensity of a hawk.

The Captain’s cage had been placed beside her, and for a time, the bird was silent, its head cocked as it surveyed the scene with one malevolent yellow eye. The conversation turned, as it inevitably must, to the subject of the sea, for Mr. Ashworth’s elder brother was a captain in the Navy.

“Ah, the sea,” Great-aunt Agatha interjected, seizing her moment. “A life of adventure and discipline. My late husband, you know, was a great friend to a sea captain. A man named Pemberton. A most instructive companion. He taught my dear Captain Flint everything he knows.”

Mr. Ashworth, ever polite, regarded the parrot with a look of mild curiosity. “Indeed, ma’am? A clever bird, is he?”

“Clever?” Great-aunt Agatha chuckled. “He is a repository of wisdom. Weren’t you, my darling?” She leaned towards the cage. “Tell the gentleman, Captain. Tell him what you learned from Captain Pemberton.”

The parrot shifted its weight from one claw to the other. It fluffed its feathers, a low growl rumbling in its throat. The entire party fell silent, watching. Clara prayed for the earth to open and swallow her whole.

Captain Flint fixed Mr. Ashworth with a beady stare. Then, in a voice that was a perfect, gravelly baritone, thick with the salt of a hundred years, he spoke.

“Avast, ye scurvy dog! I’ll have your guts for garters!”

A profound silence fell over the picnic. Mr. Ashworth’s polite smile froze on his face. Mrs. Ellsworth dropped her napkin. Mr. Ellsworth choked on a mouthful of chicken. Clara felt a wave of heat so intense she thought she might faint.

Great-aunt Agatha, however, was delighted. “There! You see? A fine, commanding tone. He had a way with a phrase, did Captain Pemberton.”

The parrot, encouraged, was not finished. It sidled along its perch and let out a string of further observations, each delivered in the same authoritative nautical growl.

“Mr. Ashworth, I presume!” it barked, with startling accuracy. “I’ll wager you’re a man who knows a well-turned ankle from a loose cannon!”

Mr. Ashworth’s gaze flew to Clara’s ankles, which were, thankfully, hidden beneath her skirts, and then back to her crimson face. He seemed caught between horror and a dawning, bewildered amusement.

“The rum’s gone off!” the parrot shrieked. “We’re all doomed! Doomed, I say! Ha ha ha!”
