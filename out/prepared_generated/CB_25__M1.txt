It was a house of stone and silence, built to withstand the fierce winds that swept down from the moors, but I think it was built more to hold in its secrets. From the first day I set foot in Grayfell Hall, I felt the oppression of it. The air, even in the height of summer, carried a damp chill, and the great iron-hinged door, when it closed behind me, seemed to shut out not merely the weather, but the very world of light and warmth. Mr. Vance, my employer, was a man cut from the same granite as his house. Tall, spare, with eyes the colour of a winter sky, he received me with a courtesy so formal it was a kind of wall. He spoke of his son, my pupil, in terms of duty and correction, and of his late wife with a brevity that sealed the subject like a tomb.

My charge was a boy of nine, named Leo, though he possessed none of the lion’s spirit his name implied. He was a slight, silent child, with his mother’s fine-boned face but his father’s watchful, apprehensive eyes. He did his lessons with a dull obedience, but the spark of curiosity, that vital flame in a young mind, was utterly extinguished. He moved through the grand, cold rooms of Grayfell like a little ghost, and the only time I saw a flicker of true life in him was when the sound of his father’s footsteps echoed in the corridor outside the schoolroom. Then, he would grow rigid, his face would drain of colour, and he would fix his gaze upon the floor as if it were his only sanctuary.

I resolved to win his confidence. It was a task that required not force, but a slow, patient siege. I began by abandoning the dry grammar books for tales of chivalry and adventure, reading of knights and dragons with all the passion I could muster. He listened, but the fear remained a shield before his heart. One afternoon, a squall broke over the moors, lashing rain against the leaded panes of the schoolroom. Leo, who had been tracing letters with a trembling hand, flinched at a particularly loud clap of thunder. I set aside my book and went to the window.

“Listen to it, Leo,” I said, keeping my voice soft. “It is only the sky’s great drum. It cannot hurt us here.” I drew the heavy velvet curtains, plunging the room into a warm gloom lit only by the fire. “There. Now it is just a story outside, and we are safe in our own castle.”

He looked at me then, a long, searching look. “You are not afraid?” he whispered.

“Of the storm? No. There is little in this world to fear that is not made by the hands of men.” I sat on the low stool near his chair, bringing myself to his level. “What is it you fear, Leo?”

His small hands clenched into fists in his lap. He did not answer. The firelight danced across his face, illuminating the unshed tears that made his eyes brilliant.

“Is it your father?” I asked, the question bold, but necessary.

A tremor ran through his whole body. He gave a single, sharp nod, his gaze fixed on the flames. “When he is angry,” he choked out, the words barely audible, “his voice changes. It is not his voice any more. It is… like the stones in the wall.”

My heart ached with a fierce pity. I reached out and gently covered one of his fists with my hand. It was ice-cold. “Has he hurt you, child?”

“No,” he said quickly, a shudder of revulsion passing over him. “Not like that. But he looks at me… and I feel as if I am being turned to stone, too. I cannot move. I cannot breathe.”

We sat in silence for a long time, the only sounds the hiss of the fire and the muffled drumming of the rain. I knew I must press on, for a door had been opened, and I dared not let it close again.

“And your mother?” I ventured. “Did she not make you feel safe?”

The effect of this question was more violent than the thunder. Leo snatched his hand from mine and scrambled from his chair, backing away until he hit the wall. His face was a mask of terror and a strange, wild grief.

“They say she is in heaven,” he whispered, his voice thin and reedy. “They say a fever took her. But that is not true.”

A cold dread, colder than any draft in that old hall, settled upon me. “What is the truth, Leo?”

He was shaking so hard I thought he might shatter. He pressed his palms to his eyes, as if to shut out a vision. “I saw her,” he sobbed. “I was in my bed, but I was awake. The moon was full and white. She came into my room. She did not wear her nightdress. She had on her travelling cloak, the dark blue one. She knelt by my bed and she kissed me, and her cheek was wet. She said, ‘Be brave, my little lion. Be brave for me.’ Then she went away. I heard the big door open and close. I went to the window and I saw her walking down the drive, towards the moor path. She did not look back. And in the morning, she was gone. And father said… father said the fever had taken her in the night.”

The words poured out of him in a torrent, a confession held too long. The firelight cast our shadows, his small and trembling, mine long and still, upon the wall. The house, which had seemed merely silent, now felt watchful, its very stones listening. The story of a quiet death from illness was a lie, a shroud drawn over a far more terrible event. She had not died; she had fled. She had left her child to the mercy of the man who had turned her son to stone with a look.
