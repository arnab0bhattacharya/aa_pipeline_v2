The discovery of my son’s duplicity came about, as such discoveries so often do, through the good offices of a busybody. I am an ironmonger, a dealer in nails, hinges, grates, and the thousand and one metallic necessities of a substantial market town, and my name is Albert Pounce. My establishment, Pounce & Son—a hopeful style I had adopted upon the boy’s birth—stood on the High Street of Wrenchester, a thoroughfare of respectable antiquity and unrespectable drainage. My son, Edwin, was seventeen, a pale, silent lad with a habit of looking at his hands as if he suspected them of some secret treachery. I had apprenticed him to the trade in the firm belief that a man’s future is best secured by a solid knowledge of his present, and that the smell of iron filings and machine oil was a more wholesome atmosphere for a growing boy than the perfumed and altogether unprofitable airs of an artist’s studio.

The busybody in question was a Mr. Pettigrew, a commercial traveller in sundries, a man with a face like a kindly and intelligent frog, who was wont to take his evening refreshment at the “Crown and Sceptre.” It was there, one Thursday, that he encountered me and, after the usual preliminaries of the weather and the state of trade, he fixed me with a peculiar, glistening eye.

“Pounce,” said he, tapping my arm with a forefinger that smelled of inferior tobacco, “I saw a picture last week in a pub in London—the ‘Goat and Compasses,’ in a lane off the Strand—that fair took my breath away. A bit of Kentish country, it was, all mist and a sort of melancholy light. And the funny thing, Pounce, was the signature. ‘E. Pounce,’ it said, as plain as a pikestaff.”

I laughed, a little uneasily. “Some other Pounce, Pettigrew. A common enough name in these parts.”

“Maybe,” he said, with a shrug that was far too knowing for my liking. “But I could have sworn it was your boy’s face, done up in a smock-frock and a great slouch hat. A sort of self-portrait, you know. He’s a deep ‘un, that lad of yours.”

I dismissed the matter with a snort, but it returned to me that night with the persistence of a bad toothache. Edwin had always been a quiet boy, given to drawing on the backs of ledgers and the margins of my order books—a habit I had corrected with a firm hand, seeing in it the seeds of a fatal idleness. He had been sent to the attic to fetch a box of obsolete brass fittings, and I had noted he took an uncommon long time about it. The thought now sent a cold trickle of suspicion down my spine. I ascended the stairs myself, my heart thumping a dull, indignant tattoo against my ribs.

The attic was a place of dust and shadows, of forgotten lumber and the ghosts of departed stock. But in one corner, near the dormer window where the grey Kentish light fell in a pale cascade, I found the truth of the matter. There was an easel, a flimsy, homemade affair of deal, and upon it a canvas. Around it were scattered tubes of paint, their labels bearing the strange, exotic names of the artist’s trade—Vermilion, Ultramarine, Burnt Sienna—and a palette smeared with a glorious, chaotic mess of colour. I stood there, an ironmonger in a world of pigment, and I felt a strange, unwelcome emotion. It was not anger, not at first. It was a kind of baffled awe.

The painting on the easel was a view of the town from the mill-field, the very scene I saw every morning on my way to the shop. But it was not the scene I knew. The river, a sluggish, muddy stream in reality, was a ribbon of silver, the old mill a romantic silhouette, and the whole was bathed in a light that seemed to belong to a more gracious and ancient world. It was a lie, a beautiful, seductive lie, and I knew at once that it was Edwin’s. The signature, a small, neat ‘E. Pounce,’ was in the corner. It was the same, I had no doubt, as the one Pettigrew had seen in that London pub.

I heard his step upon the stair and turned to face him. He stopped in the doorway, a tin of paint in his hand, and the colour drained from his face, leaving it the hue of the white lead he used. For a long moment we were silent, the dust motes dancing in the light between us.

“So,” I said, and my voice sounded strange to my own ears, “this is what you do in the attic.”

He did not deny it. He set down the tin with a quiet, deliberate care that was more damning than any outburst. “Father,” he began, “I can explain.”

“Explain?” I said, my voice rising. “You explain this to me? You are an ironmonger’s son, Edwin. Your hands are for wielding a hammer, not a… a brush. What is this nonsense? This… this daubing?”

“It is not nonsense,” he said, and for the first time, a flash of the man he might become appeared in his eyes. “It is the only thing I have ever wanted to do. I have tried, Father. I have tried to love the shop, the nails, the hinges. But I cannot. When I am painting, I am… myself.”

“Yourself?” I scoffed. “A fine self it is, that dreams in a dusty attic while your father works to put bread on the table. This is a fantasy, boy. A dangerous, unprofitable fantasy. And this!” I gestured to the canvas. “This is to be your life? A few guineas from some London publican for a pretty picture? It is not a trade. It is a gamble.”
