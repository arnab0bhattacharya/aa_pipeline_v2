# The Fall

The letter arrived on a Tuesday morning in March, brought up by the maidservant along with the chocolate and the newspapers. Clara set it aside, thinking it was one of her uncle's business correspondences, until she saw her own name in a hand she did not recognise. The paper was cheap, the ink faintly blotted, and the address — a hotel in Marseilles — was written in a script that trembled like a man in fever.

She read it twice before she understood. Then she read it a third time, slowly, as though the words might rearrange themselves into something less ruinous. Her uncle had written it. Six weeks earlier, somewhere between Lyon and the coast, he had set down the facts of his undoing with a strange, defeated clarity. The railway speculation — that grand venture he had spoken of so confidently at Christmas dinner, the one that was to double his fortune and secure Clara's settlement for life — had collapsed entirely. Not merely declined, not diminished, but collapsed, the company dissolved, the investors ruined, the directors fled or prosecuted. He had lost everything. The house in Clapham, the carriage, the accounts at the bank — all of it entangled in debts he could not now hope to pay. He was sailing for the Continent, he wrote, to escape the writs and the shame, and he begged her forgiveness with a brevity that was, in its way, more devastating than any lengthy confession.

Clara sat very still for a long time. The chocolate grew cold. Outside, the villa's garden was bright with early crocuses, and she could hear the gardener's boy raking the gravel path. She thought, with a peculiar precision, that the crocuses would come up next year whether anyone lived in the house or not, and that this thought, which ought to have been melancholy, was instead merely factual, as though she were already beginning to see the world from a great and indifferent distance.

Within a fortnight the bailiffs came. Clara had spent those fourteen days in a state of suspended unreality, opening cupboards and drawers as though she might find some forgotten store of banknotes behind the linen, writing letters to her uncle's solicitors that went unanswered, walking through the rooms of the villa — her home since she was nine years old — and trying to imagine herself elsewhere. She could not. She had been brought up here in comfort, in gentility, in the particular softness that comes from never having asked the price of anything. Her uncle had been kind to her. He had given her books and music lessons and a pony and a bedroom with pale green curtains, and he had never once suggested that any of it might end, because it had never occurred to him either.

The bailiffs were civil, at least. They allowed her to pack a trunk of personal effects — clothing, her small collection of books, a miniature of her mother — and then they stood in the hallway with their hats in their hands while she walked out through the front door and into a hired cab, as though she were merely going visiting. She did not look back.

She had one piece of good fortune, or what passed for good fortune in her new circumstances. Her former governess, Miss Aldwinkle, had left the household three years earlier to establish a small school for girls in a terraced house near Clapham Common. Clara had always liked Miss Aldwinkle, who was a plain, sharp-featured woman with a dry manner and an unexpected kindness that surfaced in moments of distress rather than in ordinary conversation. Clara wrote to her from the lodging house in Brixton where she had taken a single room, and two days later received a reply. Miss Aldwinkle would be glad to offer her a position as assistant teacher. The salary would be twelve pounds per annum, with board and lodging provided. The letter closed with the words: *It is not much, I know. But it is honest work, and you will not be treated unkindly.*

Clara arrived at the school on a grey, wet morning in April, carrying her trunk up the narrow steps of a house that seemed to lean slightly inward, as though shrinking from the weather. The street was modest, the houses identical, each fronted by a small area with iron railings and a scrap of garden where nothing much grew. Miss Aldwinkle met her at the door. She had aged. Her hair, once a severe brown, was now streaked with grey, and her face had taken on the drawn, papery look of a woman who eats little and sleeps less. But her eyes were the same — dark, intelligent, appraising — and she took Clara's hand with a grip that was firm and brief.

"Come in," she said. "I will show you the schoolroom first. The girls are at prayers."

The school occupied the ground floor and the basement of the house. There were fourteen pupils, ranging in age from six to fifteen, daughters of tradesmen and minor clerks whose parents paid a guinea a quarter for their education. The schoolroom was the back parlour, stripped of its carpet and furnished with three long tables and a blackboard. The walls were bare but for a map of Europe and a printed copy of the multiplication tables. The air smelled of damp slate and boiled milk.

"You will take the younger girls for reading and sums in the mornings," Miss Aldwinkle said, "and the older ones for French and geography in the afternoons. I will handle history and scripture. You will have Sundays free after church. Your room is on the second floor, next to mine."

She said all of this without sentiment, without preamble, in the tone of someone who had long ago dispensed with anything that was not practical. Clara found it steadying. She had feared sympathy more than anything — the gentle, pitying looks, the careful words — and Miss Aldwinkle offered none. She treated the arrangement as a transaction, which was precisely what it was, and Clara discovered that she could bear transactions far more easily than she could bear kindness.
