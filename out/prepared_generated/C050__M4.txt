The bet was struck, as such things invariably are, in the fug of tobacco smoke and brandy fumes that clung to the leather armchairs of the Cipher Club. It was Lucien Vance who proposed it, his long, pale fingers sketching a dismissive gesture in the air. "The Icicle of Mayfair," he drawled, his voice thick with the boredom of a man who has exhausted all ordinary pleasures. "Miss Eleanor Whitlock. I wager five hundred pounds that no man in this room could thaw her within a calendar month."

A general murmur of amusement rippled through the assembled company. Eleanor Whitlock was a legend, a woman of formidable intellect and glacial reserve. An heiress in her own right, she moved through society with the detached precision of a surgeon, her wit a scalpel that could flay a man’s pride from his bones in a single, well-turned phrase. To make her fall in love was a task that seemed not merely difficult, but faintly blasphemous.

I, Julian Croft, was twenty-eight years old and had never been in love. I had, in my time, been in lust, in fondness, and in a state of calculated pursuit, but never in love. The emotion struck me as a species of temporary insanity, a chemical imbalance that clouded the judgement and made men act the fool. I prided myself on my rationality, on seeing the world as a chessboard and its inhabitants as pieces to be moved. The bet, therefore, was not a challenge to my heart, but to my mind. It was an intellectual problem, a puzzle to be solved.

"I accept," I said, setting down my glass. The silence that followed was more profound than any applause. Lucien’s eyes lit with a malicious glee. "Croft, you are either the bravest man in London or the most foolish." I smiled, a thin, confident curve of the lips. "I am merely a man who enjoys a challenge."

My campaign began the very next evening at a soirée hosted by the vapid but well-connected Lady Ashworth. Miss Whitlock was there, a figure of cool elegance in a gown of deep sapphire velvet, standing apart from the chattering throng as if she were a creature of a different, more refined species. Her hair, the colour of dark honey, was swept up in a style that brooked no frivolity, and her eyes, a startling shade of grey, held the light of a winter sky.

My opening gambit was calculated to appeal to her intelligence. I spoke of Schopenhauer, of the futility of human striving, and she listened with a faint, almost imperceptible tilt of her head. "You speak of his pessimism as a parlor trick, Mr. Croft," she said, her voice low and musical, yet devoid of warmth. "You wear it like a fashionable coat, without considering the cold."

It was a rebuke, but a brilliant one. I was delighted. For the next two weeks, I orchestrated our encounters with the precision of a military campaign. I discovered her preferences: she favoured the company of the elderly and the brilliant, disdained the frivolous, and had a deep, abiding passion for the Pre-Raphaelites. I arranged to be at the gallery where she was studying a Rossetti, and I engaged her in a debate about the nature of beauty that lasted a full hour. I learned she was a patron of a small, struggling orphanage in the East End, and I began making anonymous, and then not-so-anonymous, donations.

My friends watched my progress with the keen interest of spectators at a horse race. Lucien would quiz me nightly at the club. "Has she smiled yet, Julian? A genuine smile, not that terrifying rictus of social obligation?" I would report my findings with clinical detachment. "She has agreed to a walk in Hyde Park on Thursday. A tactical victory."

I was a spy in the house of love, cataloguing her vulnerabilities. I found that beneath the ice was not a hidden fire, but a profound and carefully guarded loneliness. She was an only child, orphaned young, and had been raised by a succession of cold, indifferent guardians. Her intellect was her armour, her wit her sword, and she wielded them to keep the world at a safe distance. My task, I realised, was not to melt the ice, but to convince her it was safe to step from behind the fortress walls.

The turning point came on a blustery afternoon in late October. We were walking along the Embankment, the wind whipping the Thames into choppy grey waves. I had abandoned my usual tactics of intellectual sparring. Instead, I spoke of my own childhood, of the crushing weight of my father’s expectations, of the loneliness of being the cleverest boy in the room. I did not do this to manipulate her, or so I told myself. I did it because, for the first time, I felt a strange kinship with her silence.

She stopped walking and looked at me, truly looked at me, her grey eyes searching my face. "You are a collection of paradoxes, Mr. Croft," she said softly. "You pretend to be a cynic, yet you speak of loneliness with the voice of a poet."

It was then I saw it. A flicker in her eyes, a slight softening of the line of her jaw. It was the chink in the armour I had been seeking. A week later, I found myself in her drawing room, a place of dark wood and Pre-Raphaelite paintings. We were alone. The fire crackled in the grate. I had planned my final move with the precision of a grandmaster. I would confess a deep, profound feeling I did not possess, and claim my prize.

But as I looked at her, illuminated by the firelight, a strange thing happened. The words I had rehearsed died in my throat. The calculated speech dissolved, and in its place came a raw, unvarnished truth. "I find that my thoughts are no longer my own," I heard myself say, my voice rough. "They are all of you. I find that the world has been drained of colour, and you are the only source of light. It is a most unsettling and irrational condition."
