The light struck the Arbia—no, the Mugnone, which tumbles its green-glass water over stones just behind the Casina, where Margaret's sister Cecily had taken a villa for the spring—striking the water so that it broke into a thousand facets, each one containing a fragment of the sky, the cypresses, the scrubbed white wall opposite, and the shadow of a wisteria that hung like a mauve beard from the trellis. Eleanor, for that was her name—Eleanor Vance, come down from Bayswater with a single trunk and a copy of Pater, feeling herself very plain and very provincial and clutching at both conditions as though they were virtues—stood at the window and pressed her forehead against the warm glass. The glass was imperfect; it had been blown a century ago and still held the breath of its maker in faint green ripples, so that the garden beyond swam and trembled as if seen through tears, or through the surface of a bath. She was nineteen and had not yet been kissed. She suspected this was important, though she could not have said why, since the books she read in secret suggested that kisses led invariably to ruin and the books she read in public suggested that they led to marriages such as Cecily's, which was conducted, as far as Eleanor could observe, in a perpetual atmosphere of muffled irritation and separate bedrooms.

They were coming for dinner. The artists. The writers. The English.

Cecily had said it with a particular inflection—*They are coming*—as though she were announcing a weather system, or a migration of birds, something large and atmospheric and slightly dangerous that one could not prevent and must simply endure. She had been married for three years to Harold, who had taken a position with a British shipping concern whose name Eleanor could never retain, and who spent his mornings at a desk in the Via Tornabuoni and his evenings drinking Chianti in the garden with his shirt open at the collar, his face turning the colour of a brick in rain. Cecily had grown thin. Cecily had grown sharp. She had a way now of standing in doorways with her hip thrust out and a cigarette burning between two fingers, saying nothing, watching, which struck Eleanor as being not so much an attitude as a wound, though a wound Cecily had learned to wear as one wears a particularly striking piece of jewellery—conscious of it, displaying it, half-ashamed.

The garden of the Casina Calzaiuoli sloped down to a low wall above a road where carts moved in the evening with a sound like muffled drums, and beyond the wall the olive trees began, silver and grey, and beyond the olives the hills rose in their dry Italian way, the colour of old bread, of a lion's flank, of nothing in England at all. Eleanor had been in Florence for eleven days and already the English hills—the green, the damp, the perpetual softness—had begun to seem slightly ridiculous to her, like a person who apologises too much. Here everything was hard. The light was hard. The shadows were hard, falling in geometric blocks across the terracotta as if cut with a knife. Even the English were harder here. She had noticed it at the gallery that morning, where she had gone with Cecily and had seen a party of her sister's acquaintances standing before a Botticelli, and one of them, a man with a red beard and a velvet coat, had said, in a voice that carried across the entire Sala di Lorenzo Monaco, *Well, of course, he's all surface, isn't he, all spring and decoration, no bones to speak of*—and Eleanor had thought, with a sudden flush that rose from her collar to her hairline, that this was the cruelest thing she had ever heard, because the painting was beautiful, she could see that it was beautiful, the figure floating in its almond-shaped halo with the wind braiding its hair, and she had wanted to say so but had not, because she had caught Cecily's eye and Cecily had made a small motion with her head, a motion that meant *don't*.

Now they were in the drawing room—the artists, the writers, the English in Italy—and the candles were lit and the wine was open and the air smelled of bergamot and stone dust and the particular heavy sweetness of white flowers that Eleanor could not name. There was Philip Royce, the red-bearded man, who wrote reviews for a weekly paper and spoke in paragraphs. There was Dora Llewellyn, who painted watercolours and had a long neck and an air of being perpetually offended by something she could not locate. There was a young man whose name Eleanor had not caught, who sat in the corner with a book open on his knee and did not speak. The others moved around him as one moves around a piece of furniture.

Royce was holding forth. He had a way of speaking that was like a man laying bricks—each sentence solid, placed with precision, building a wall against which the listener was meant to press and be repelled. He was talking about Henry James. No—he was talking about a novel by Henry James and also, simultaneously, about himself, about his own capacity for judgement, which he presented as being like a surgical instrument, delicate and merciless, and Eleanor watched him and thought that his eyes were the colour of wet pebbles and that his hands, which held a glass of wine, were extraordinarily clean, as though he had scrubbed them in preparation for some procedure, and she thought, *he would cut a painting open to see if it bled*. Dora Llewellyn was laughing, a high bright laugh that did not reach her eyes, and Cecily was smoking, and Harold was telling an anecdote about a customs official at the frontier which no one was listening to, and the candles guttered and righted themselves, and Eleanor sat on the edge of a chair and felt herself growing transparent, as if the Florentine light were doing something to her that the Bayswater light had never done—dissolving the surface, the skin of her, so that what was underneath, the raw live nerve of her, was exposed and trembling.
