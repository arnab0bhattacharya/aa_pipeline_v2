She had come, perhaps, with the foolish, desperate notion that an appeal to human compassion might still find a lodging within those official walls; as though mercy were a tangible substance that could be extracted from the grim machinery of the State by the mere force of a sister’s anguish. The gas-jets in the corridor of the great building flickered with a yellow, sickly flame, as if the very air of the place were too heavy, too charged with the collective breath of innumerable confessions, to burn with a clear fire. Miss Vane—for it was by this name that she had presented herself, a slight, pale figure enveloped in a dark cloak that seemed to absorb the meagre light—sat upon the edge of the hard wooden bench, her gloved hands clasped tightly in her lap, her eyes fixed upon the frosted glass of the door that bore the name of Inspector Heath. It was the third day. She had returned, and she had returned again, driven by that obscure, blind tenacity which is less a virtue of the will than a fatalism of the heart.

Inside the room, Inspector Heath stood by the window, looking out upon the wet, glistening stones of the courtyard. The rain fell with a monotonous, weary persistence, washing the soot from the grimy bricks into the gutters, where it mingled with the dark, oily water of the Thames fog that had crept inland. He was a man of a heavy, deliberate countenance, whose courtesy was a thing of habit, polished smooth by long years of service, yet entirely devoid of warmth—a mask of civil formality behind which the real man, if there was a real man, had long since retreated into some inner fastness of official routine. He turned from the window as the constable ushered her in, and inclined his head with a slow, measured politeness that was almost an insult in its very perfection.

“Miss Vane,” he murmured. “You are here again.”

It was not a question. It was a statement of fact, delivered with that flat, toneless precision which seemed to strip the words of all human significance, leaving them bare, like the bones of some long-dead argument. He gestured toward the chair opposite his desk, and she sat down, her movements quick and nervous, the cloak falling open to reveal the plain, dark dress beneath. There was a moment of silence, heavy with the unspoken, with the futility of words that had already been spoken and found wanting. The gas-fire hissed softly in the grate, casting a faint, unsteady glow upon the dark wood of the desk, where a single folder lay closed, marked with a number. It was the dossier of her brother, Theodore Vane. It might have been the dossier of a stranger, for all the emotion it seemed to hold.

“I have come to ask you, Inspector,” she began, her voice low and steady with an effort that cost her visibly, for a faint tremor passed across her lips, “if there is any news. Any word of him. He has been held now for—”

“For the requisite period, Miss Vane,” Heath said, gently, as one corrects a child in a matter of arithmetic. “The matter is not within my discretion. I have explained this. The detention of individuals suspected of conspiring against the peace of the realm is a process governed by regulations far above my station. I am merely—” he paused, as if searching for the precise word, and his small, grey eyes rested upon her face with an expression of distant, almost clinical interest—“an instrument. A mere instrument.”

She looked at him, and in her eyes there was that which he had observed on the two preceding days: a fierce, unquenchable flame of personal devotion that seemed out of place in that bleak, institutional air, like a tropical flower blooming in a cellar. It was not, he reflected, the wild, frantic look of a woman unhinged by grief; it was something more dangerous, more insidious—a quiet, reasoned determination that had fastened upon his refusal as a thing to be worn down, eroded, by the sheer attrition of her presence. He found it vaguely troubling, this persistence, though he could not have said why. He had dealt with the mothers and wives and sisters of criminals before, with their tears, their entreaties, their occasional threats; but this woman did not weep, did not plead, did not threaten. She merely returned.

“He is not a criminal,” she said. “He is a boy. A boy with ideas—foolish ideas, perhaps, I grant you that. But they are only ideas. He has done nothing. He has harmed no one.”

“The law, Miss Vane,” Heath replied, in that same measured, unhurried tone, “does not distinguish between the idea and the act when the idea tends toward the subversion of the established order. Your brother was found in possession of certain documents—pamphlets—which advocate, in terms of considerable clarity, the violent overthrow of—”

“They were not his. They were given to him by a man he scarcely knew. He was curious—that is all. The curiosity of a young man. You must believe me, Inspector. You must see that.”

But he did not see, or if he saw, the seeing was swallowed up in the vast, impersonal mechanism of which he was a part. He leaned back in his chair, and his fingers, thick and pale, drummed a slow, absent rhythm upon the armrest. Outside, the rain had intensified, and the sound of it upon the window-pane was like the murmur of a distant, indifferent crowd. He regarded her over the tips of his interlaced fingers, and for a moment, as the light from the gas-jet flickered across his face, there passed over his features a shadow—not of compassion, exactly, but of something adjacent to it; a troubled recognition of the futility of her hope, and perhaps, too, of his own complicity in that futility. It was as if her persistence had begun to act upon him not as a nuisance, but as a moral irritant, a grain of sand in the smooth workings of his conscience.
