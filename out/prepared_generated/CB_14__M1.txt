I do not think I shall ever forget the room in which I first encountered Agathe Vondel. It was the anteroom to the Director’s study in the Pensionnat de Sainte-Claire, a place of heavy Flemish oak and the smell of floor-wax and damp wool. The city of Ghent lay beyond the high windows, its grey spires pricking a sky the colour of unpolished pewter, but inside, the atmosphere was one of stifling propriety.

Madame Vondel, the Directress, sat behind her desk, a woman whose starched lace collar seemed to choke the very life out of her throat. Beside her stood the girl—fifteen years of age, yet possessing eyes that held the cold, calculating light of a much older soul. This was Agathe, the Director’s daughter.

"She is clever, Miss Arden," Madame Vondel declared, her voice thin and reedy, like the wind through a cracked pane. "Perhaps too clever. She has tested the patience of every mistress here. I require you to be firm. You are English; you have the requisite... rigour."

I looked at the girl. She did not look at me. She was examining a loose thread on her sleeve with an air of profound boredom, yet I sensed she was listening to every vibration in the air.

"I do not intend to be cruel, Madame," I said, speaking with a calmness I was far from feeling. "But I shall not be deceived."

Agathe lifted her head then. Her gaze met mine. It was a bold, unblinking stare, devoid of the deference usually expected in a pupil. "Mamma," she said, her voice melodious but mocking, "Miss Arden looks as though she would break a ruler over a knuckle for a misplaced comma. Shall I fetch my grammar?"

"Be silent, Agathe," the mother said, though there was no heat in the reprimand, only a weary resignation.

The days that followed were a battle of silent artillery. Agathe was, as her mother had warned, deceitful. She possessed a memory like a trap; if I turned my back for a moment to write upon the slate, a paper dart would fly across the room, or a snicker would ripple through the class. When I turned, Agathe was the picture of innocence, her hands folded, her eyes wide.

Worse, she was a spy. If I permitted the girls five minutes of fresh air at the window because the room grew stiflingly hot, or if I overlooked a minor infraction of the dress code out of pity for a shivering child, Agathe would vanish during the recreation hour. She would reappear with a flush of triumph on her cheeks, and soon after, Madame Vondel would summon me to the study to reprimand me for "laxity" or "un-English softness."

"She says you allow them to chatter like magpies, Miss Arden," Madame Vondel told me one afternoon, her finger tapping the desk. "I expect silence. Absolute silence."

"Madame," I replied, struggling to keep the tremor of indignation from my voice, "a classroom is not a tomb. There is a difference between the noise of rebellion and the murmur of study. Your daughter reports the latter as the former because it serves her purpose to see me disciplined."

Madame Vondel waved a hand. "Do not make excuses. Control the child."

I returned to the classroom, my blood hot with a sense of injustice. To be spied upon by a child, to be judged by a woman who cared more for the appearance of order than the reality of learning—it was a humiliation I could scarcely bear. Yet, I had my bread to earn, and I had my dignity to keep. I would not weep; I would not crumble.

The crisis came on a Tuesday in November. The rain lashed against the windows, a ceaseless, driving torrent that drowned out the sound of the bells from the Belfry. The room was dim, lit only by the oil lamps, and the air was thick with the scent of wet ink.

I had set the girls a task of transcription. Agathe, however, was engaged in a different sport. She had concealed a small mirror beneath her desk and was using it to catch the light, flashing it into the eyes of a timid, consumptive girl named Elise, who sat in the front row. Elise flinched, blinking back tears, terrified to speak.

I saw it. I saw the cruelty of it—the petty, vicious tyranny of a strong nature over a weak one. A surge of fury rose in my throat, hot and choking. I walked down the aisle, my boots striking the floorboards with a sound like a judge’s gavel.

I stopped before Agathe. "Give me the mirror," I said.

She looked up, feigning surprise. "Mirror, Miss Arden? I have no mirror. I am studying my verbs."

"Do not lie to me," I said, my voice low but carrying to the back of the room. "Lying is the refuge of the coward. Give it to me."

"I assure you, you are mistaken," she said, her tone insolent. "Perhaps you are seeing things. Mamma says you look tired."

The class was deathly still. They watched us, sensing the storm.

"I see very clearly," I said. "I see a girl who torments the weak and mocks those who try to guide her. You think because you are the Director’s daughter you are above the law of this room. You are mistaken. In this room, there is but one law, and I am its keeper."

I held out my hand. She did not move.

"If you do not give it to me," I continued, feeling a strange calm descend upon me, "I shall take it. And I shall write a report to your father—not your mother, but the Director himself—detailing how his daughter abuses her position to frighten the sick. Let us see if he values your deceit as much as your mother does."

For the first time, a flicker of uncertainty crossed Agathe’s face. She feared her father, the great man who was rarely home but whose word was final.
