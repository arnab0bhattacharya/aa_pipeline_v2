The brass bell above the door of Galloway & Finch, Ltd., rang with a dull, heavy thud that seemed to vibrate through the floorboards of the counting house. Arthur Penhaligon, junior clerk, looked up from his ledger, his quill tip hovering over a line of figures. Through the glass partition, he watched Mr. Silas Vane enter. Vane was a fixture of the bank’s morning routine, a man who seemed to have been carved from the same gray stone as the City’s ancient walls. He was small, hunched, and dressed in a coat that had been fashionable perhaps two decades prior.

Arthur watched with the detached curiosity of a man who had spent three months observing the same cycle. Vane would approach the teller’s cage, place a single gold sovereign on the velvet counter, and wait. The teller, a bored woman named Mrs. Halloway, would count the coin, note the deposit in her book, and return it to Vane. Vane would pocket the coin, bow stiffly, and leave. Every morning, without fail, for six months. And every evening, precisely at four o’clock, he would return to withdraw the same sovereign, which sat in the bank’s safe until his return. It was a ritual devoid of financial sense. The interest earned on a single sovereign was a pittance, less than the cost of the ink used to record it.

But Arthur had noticed something else. He noticed the way Vane’s eyes scanned the room. Not the customers, not the staff, but the shadows. He noticed that Vane never removed his gloves, even in the stifling heat of the July afternoons. And he noticed that the sovereign, though genuine, was worn smooth, its edges blurred as if it had been handled not just by Vane, but by thousands of hands before him.

On this particular Tuesday, the morning arrived with a chill that did not belong in the season. Arthur arrived early, as was his habit, to sharpen his pencils. At nine o’clock, the brass bell did not ring. At ten, it remained silent. By noon, Arthur’s unease had curdled into something colder. He checked the ledger. No deposit. He looked through the glass. The street outside was busy, trams clattering, carriages rumbling, but Silas Vane was nowhere to be seen.

The absence felt like a missing tooth in a jaw. The rhythm of the bank, the subtle pulse of Galloway & Finch, seemed to stutter. Arthur knew he should wait. It was not his place to worry about an eccentric customer. But as the afternoon light slanted across the marble floor, casting long, distorted shadows, Arthur made a decision. He left his post, citing a sudden headache to his senior, and stepped out into the foggy streets of the City.

He did not go to the police. The police were for crimes, and this was not a crime, at least not yet. It was a mystery, and mysteries required discretion. Arthur walked to the address scrawled on a card he had found tucked inside the spine of a discarded newspaper three months ago. The card bore no name, only a location: 14 Bittern Lane, near the Temple.

The building was a narrow townhouse, dark and unprepossessing, hidden behind a curtain of ivy that looked less like decoration and more like a cage. Arthur knocked. The door opened a fraction, revealing an eye that was pale and unnervingly sharp.

"Mr. Penhaligon," a voice said, low and smooth. "I expected you."

Arthur’s heart hammered against his ribs. "Who are you?"

"My name is Mr. Thorne. And you are the clerk who watched too closely." Thorne stepped aside, inviting Arthur into a room that smelled of pipe tobacco and old paper. The walls were lined with books, but they were not novels. They were ledgers, maps, and folders of correspondence. Thorne sat behind a desk that was cluttered with items that seemed out of place: a pocket watch, a glass of water, and a single, worn gold sovereign.

Arthur stared at the coin. It was the same one.

"You deposit it," Thorne said, not looking at Arthur, but at the coin. "You withdraw it. You ensure it is safe. You ensure it is seen."

"I don't understand," Arthur whispered. "Why? Why would a man do such a thing?"

Thorne finally looked up. His face was gaunt, his features sharp enough to cut glass. "Because it is not a deposit. It is a lock. Or rather, a key. See, the coin is not merely gold. It is a marker. A signal. To those who know, the presence of this specific sovereign in the accounts of Galloway & Finch indicates that the 'Channel' is open. It signifies that a particular ledger, hidden elsewhere, is valid. It is a ritual of trust, Arthur. A trust that has been maintained for forty years."

Arthur felt the room tilt slightly. "And Vane?"

"Silas Vane is not a customer. He is a custodian. He is the last of the line of men who guard this secret. The coin is a token of his life. As long as it moves, he lives. As long as he lives, the secret remains sealed within the bank's system, invisible to the outside world."

"Then why is he gone?" Arthur asked, his voice trembling.

Thorne’s expression darkened. "Because the secret has been compromised. Or rather, because the time has come for it to be revealed. Vane did not fail to appear, Arthur. He was taken. The people who have been watching him, the same people who have watched him for decades, have grown impatient. They want the ledger. They want the names, the dates, the transactions that are not in your books."

Arthur’s mind raced. The shadows Vane had watched. The gloves he never removed. It wasn’t paranoia; it was survival. And now, the ritual had been broken. The coin was here, in Thorne’s hand, but it was no longer a lock. It was a target.

"What do I do?" Arthur asked, though he knew the answer before he spoke it.
