The rain, which had begun at noon as a fine, spiteful drizzle, was now a steady, penetrating downpour, and it drummed upon the roof of the mill like a thousand tiny hammers, a sound that was the very pulse of the town of Cinderwick. Within the great, soot-blackened shed of the weaving-room, the noise was a very Babel of shuttles and looms, a clatter and a roar that had worn the sharpness from the voices of the people who tended them, leaving them flat and toneless as the worn flags of the floor. It was here that Sarah Ann Baguley stood, her frame a monument to twelve hours’ labour, her fingers, though they flew with the memory of long practice, feeling as though they belonged to some other, clumsier woman. The air was thick with the smell of oil and cotton-fluff and the warm, animal scent of too many bodies in too small a space, and the flue, a malady of the chest as common as a cold, had settled upon her with a permanence that made her breath a shallow, painful business.

Her husband, Joseph Baguley, worked in the carding-room below, a man whose pride was a fierce and unyielding thing, a hard, bright pebble lodged in the soft earth of his poverty. He was a skilled man, a piecer and a grinder, and he held his head high among his fellows, not because he had more, but because he would take less. He would not be beholden; he would not be patronised; he would not, as he put it with a curl of his lip, ‘be fed with a silver spoon that had a master’s name upon the handle.’ It was this pride that had kept them in their two-roomed cottage in Croft’s Yard, with its single pump shared by nine families, when a word to the right overseer might have got them a better place; and it was this pride that now sat at their table like an uninvited guest, gaunt and silent, while the children—there were four of them, the youngest a babe in arms—looked with hollow eyes at the thin broth that was their supper.

The offer had come that very afternoon, brought by Mrs. Hesketh, the widow of the former manager, a woman whose bonnet was a miracle of black crape and whose voice was a confidential whisper. Mr. Ambrose Cheetham, the mill-owner, had a brother, a Mr. Godfrey Cheetham, a widower of some ten years’ standing, who lived in a quiet, red-brick house on the Manchester road, a house with a garden and a green gate and a general air of mournful respectability. He was a kind man, this Mr. Godfrey, a man of books and quiet habits, whose health was delicate and whose chief need was for a sensible, sober woman to manage his household and to be a companion to his motherless daughter. The place was one of comfort and comparative ease; the wages were three times what Sarah Ann earned at the loom; and the situation, Mrs. Hesketh had whispered, with a significant glance at Sarah Ann’s pallid cheek, was one that might be held for life.

‘It is a providence,’ Mrs. Hesketh had said, her black gloves clasped together. ‘A very providence, Mrs. Baguley. For the children’s sake, you must consider it.’

And Sarah Ann had considered it, all the way home through the rain, with her shawl drawn tight over her head and the baby a heavy, warm bundle against her breast. She considered it now, as she set the coarse brown loaf upon the table and saw the way her eldest, a boy of nine named Enoch, eyed it as a starving man might eye a jewel. She considered it as she looked at Joseph, his great hands, still grimy from the carding-engine, resting upon the table, his brow furrowed, his jaw set in that line she knew so well.

‘They say,’ she began, her voice a thin, reedy thing, ‘that Mr. Godfrey is a very kind gentleman. That he has a library of books, and that the house is warm.’

Joseph made a sound that was not quite a word. ‘Kind,’ he repeated, the syllable dropping from his lips like a stone. ‘Kind is a word for them that can afford it. We are not to be objects of kindness, Sarah Ann. We are to be workers. We are to be free men and women, and not the paid servants of a master’s charity.’

‘It is not charity, Joseph. It is work. Honest work, and better paid than this.’ She gestured with a weary hand at the room, at the damp-stained walls, at the single candle that guttered in its tin holder. ‘Look at them. Look at your children. Enoch’s cough is worse, and the baby is not thriving. The money would buy milk, and meat, and a fire that does not smoke.’

‘And what would it buy for me?’ he demanded, his voice rising for the first time. ‘What would it buy for Joseph Baguley? The name of a man whose wife goes out to service! The name of a man who cannot keep his own hearth! I would rather starve in a ditch than have it said that I lived on the bounty of a Cheetham.’

He brought his fist down upon the table, and the bowls jumped, and the baby, startled, set up a thin, wailing cry. Sarah Ann took the child and hushed it, her own heart a cold, heavy weight in her chest. She saw the scene with a terrible clarity: the man, for all his bluster, was afraid; afraid of the world’s opinion, afraid of his own inadequacy, and his pride was the armour he wore to hide the fear. But the children had no such armour. They had only their hunger, which was a fact, and their mother’s worn face, which was another.
