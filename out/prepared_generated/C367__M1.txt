The wheels of the carrier’s cart made a particular sound on the road from Kegworth to Loughborough, a deep, rhythmic complaint that Thomas Pike had come to think of as the world’s only honest voice. It spoke of mud, of stones, of the slow, grinding work of moving things from one place to another. Twice a week, on Tuesdays and Fridays, Thomas drove his two horses, Ned and Bess, along the network of lanes that stitched the small farms and hamlets together. He was a carrier, a man of business. He carried sacks of grain, bolts of cloth, crates of chickens, and, for a penny a time, the letters and small packages that were the lifeblood of a scattered community. But he carried more than that. He carried secrets.

His cart was a mobile confessional. As he waited for a farmer to heave a sack of turnips onto the back, or for a farmer’s wife to count out coins with her tongue between her teeth, they would talk. They told him of ailing livestock, of a daughter’s prospects, of a neighbour’s suspected poaching. Thomas, with his broad, impassive face and his eyes fixed on the horse’s ears, would simply nod. He was a fixture, like the stile by the church or the oak on the hill. He was trusted because he was part of the landscape, and because he rarely offered an opinion. His job was to carry, not to judge.

It was a Tuesday in late autumn when Eliza Vance came to him. She was the daughter of Silas Vance, a man whose dairy herd was the pride of the parish and whose temper was its terror. Eliza was seventeen, with hair the colour of straw and a quietness about her that seemed more like a held breath than a natural state. She stood by the gate of her father’s farm, twisting a piece of ribbon in her hands, as Thomas finished loading a churn of butter destined for the market stall of a Mr. Gable.

“Mr. Pike,” she said, her voice barely louder than the rustle of the dead leaves in the ditch.

Thomas touched the brim of his hat. “Miss Vance.”

She glanced back towards the farmhouse, a low, formidable building of grey stone. “I have a letter. I need it taken to Loughborough.”

Thomas nodded. It was his trade. “A penny, then. To the post office?”

“No.” She stepped closer to the cart, her face pale. “Not the post office. It’s for… it’s for someone at the Bell Inn. A Mr. John Croft. He is a clerk there.”

Thomas’s hands stilled on the reins. A letter to a man at an inn, not to the official post. A secret letter. This was different. This was the kind of cargo that had weight beyond its paper and ink.

“It is a private matter,” Eliza whispered, pressing a folded letter into his hand. It was sealed with a plain dab of red wax. “My father must not know. No one must know. You are the only one who goes back and forth. I can trust you.”

She did not ask it as a question. She stated it as a fact. Thomas looked at the letter in his calloused palm. It felt warm. He thought of Silas Vance, a man who looked at the world as if it owed him a debt he was determined to collect, violently if necessary. He thought of the gossip he carried, the small, harmless tidings of a village. This felt different. This was a spark in a barn full of dry hay.

He could refuse. He was a man of business. He carried what he was paid to carry, and the terms were clear: from one known party to another, openly. This was a subterfuge. But he looked at Eliza’s face, at the desperate hope and fear swimming in her eyes, and he saw not a co-conspirator, but a prisoner. He thought of his own daughter, safe at home in Kegworth, and a strange, heavy feeling settled in his gut.

“Half a crown,” he heard himself say.

Eliza’s eyes widened. It was a fortune.

“My silence costs more than my carriage,” Thomas said, his voice flat. It was a lie. His silence had always been free. But he needed to put a price on it, to make it a transaction, to keep it from becoming a moral burden.

She fumbled in her purse and produced the coins, her hand trembling. As she placed them in his, her fingers brushed his, and he felt the coldness of her skin.

He tucked the letter into the deep inner pocket of his coat, next to his own heart. It felt like a stone. The rest of the journey to Loughborough was a torment. Every bump in the road seemed to jostle the letter, to remind him of its presence. The rhythmic complaint of the wheels now sounded like an accusation. He was no longer just a carrier; he was a conspirator. The familiar faces along the road, the people who trusted him with their small talk, now seemed to look at him differently, though he knew nothing had changed but the contents of his coat.

At the market in Loughborough, he went about his business with a grim efficiency. He delivered the butter to Mr. Gable, who complained about the price of feed. He collected a payment from the ironmonger. All the while, the letter burned against his ribs. Finally, with his tasks done, he drove the cart to the yard of the Bell Inn.

He found John Croft in the taproom, a young man with a scholarly stoop and ink-stains on his fingers, polishing glasses behind the bar. He was not what Thomas had expected. There was no swagger, no hint of a rogue. He looked as nervous as a hare.

Thomas leaned against the bar. “A word, Mr. Croft.”

The young man looked up, his eyes wary. “Do I know you?”
