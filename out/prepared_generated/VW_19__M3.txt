The key grated, stiff with frost, and the door gave way, letting Mrs. Creak into the dark, profound silence of Blear, Swinburne, and Peake. It was half past six, and the Thames outside lay under a sky of bruised purple, heaving softly, a great wet beast turning in its sleep. She turned on the electric light; it clicked, buzzed, and caught the yellow dust-sheet she had left thrown over the typewriter yesterday; but the dust was already settling again, fine as pollen, over the inkwells and the brass lips of the fountain pens. Mrs. Creak set down her bucket; it clanked, a hollow, echoing sound in the empty chambers, and the smell of wet mackintosh and carbolic rose with the steam from her boiling kettle. She stood a moment, breathing heavily, her hand on her broad hip, surveying the desks, the high windows, the wire baskets piled high with yellow envelopes.

She began with Mr. Peake’s room. Mr. Peake was the senior partner; he had a bald head, mottled like a tortoise-shell, and a habit of sucking peppermints which left a faint, sweet, musty smell lingering in the plush curtains. Mrs. Creak shook them; the dust rose, dancing in the slanting light that had begun to creep between the clouds, catching the eddies and making them glitter like tiny, aimless worlds. She paused, her thick red hand resting on the mahogany desk. There was a letter, unfinished, lying beneath a paperweight shaped like a lion. The writing ran uphill in sharp, black strokes. *My dear Fortescue,* she spelt out, moving her lips, *the matter of the estate cannot be indefinitely—* and there it broke off, a long wavering dash, as if the pen had slipped, or the mind behind it had suddenly flown out of the window to the river, to the barges, to the grey, slipping water. She wiped the blotter with a damp rag. What did estates matter? Vast properties in the country, no doubt; deer cropping the grass under dripping oaks; but here was the crumb from Mr. Peake’s biscuit, lodged in the groove of the desk, and a stain where his teacup had rested. The weight of the law pressed down on the room; it was in the calf-bound books, heavy, gilded, lining the walls; yet the biscuit crumb was somehow more real, more poignant, a little monument to human hunger. She flicked it away with her nail.

She moved on, dragging the heavy tin pail, to the outer office where the clerks sat. Here the desks were close together, partitioned by low wooden screens, and the air smelled of stale pipe tobacco and India paper. The river was wider from this side; one could see the whole bend, the warehouses on the far shore, and a tugboat hooting, a long, melancholy note that hung in the frozen air and died away. Mrs. Creak wrung out her mop; the water splashed, grey and sudsy, into the bucket. She thought of the tugboat, plunging through the dark waves; she thought of her son, Arthur, who was in Canada now, in a place called Saskatoon—she liked the word, it sounded like wind rattling through a dry cornfield—driving a tractor, or was it threshing? He had written a letter, which she had in her pocket now, worn thin at the creases. *The snow here is up to the knees, Mother, and the sky is a sheet of white iron, but we are getting on.* A sheet of white iron. She looked out at the London sky, which was lifting now, turning a pale, watery yellow, streaked with mackerel clouds, and tried to imagine a sky of white iron, but could not. She could only see the Thames, sliding, oily and black, carrying a wisp of straw on its bosom, and the gulls wheeling, crying, their wings flashing like knives.

She paused at young Mr. Trench’s desk. He was always so neat; his pens were aligned, his papers stacked with mathematical precision. Yet there was a photograph, propped against the lamp, of a girl in a flounced dress, holding a tennis racket, smiling with a bright, hard, stare. Mrs. Creak bent down. The girl had thin ankles, crossed very delicately. She looked at the photograph with a mixture of pity and contempt. What did they know, these girls in their tennis dresses, of the grind, the wet flagstones, the ache in the lower back? She wiped the desk, avoiding the photograph, leaving it its little island of dust. But the girl stared on, smiling, relentless, as if she knew she would conquer, would be married, would have a house with a garden and a perambulator, and all the law and the ledgers would be as nothing to her.

The kettle in the corner boiled over, hissing on the spirit stove. Mrs. Creak started, then went to make the tea. She poured the water into the brown teapot, her own, which she kept hidden behind the waste-paper basket in the filing room. The steam rose, fragrant, warm, blurring the glass of the door. She thought of her tea at home, at half past four, in the kitchen in Lambeth, with the gas fire hissing, and the cat rubbing against her ankles, and the streetlamp outside making a yellow pool on the damp pavement. But the kitchen was empty now. Arthur was in Canada; and Tom—Tom was in the churchyard at St. Mary’s, under the yew tree, where the earth was always damp and smelled of dead leaves. Tom had been a porter at the railway station; he had had a cough, a terrible, tearing cough that shook the bed, and the doctor had said, *Keep him warm, Mrs. Creak, keep him warm.* But the fire was not enough; the damp had got into his lungs; and one morning, when the milk was frozen on the step, she had looked at him, and his face was grey, like the ashes in the grate, and he was still.
