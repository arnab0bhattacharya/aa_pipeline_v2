The heat in the station was not merely a physical sensation but a presence, a thick, stagnant thing that seemed to press against the very thoughts of men, blurring the edges of memory and making the air taste of dust and old sweat. It lay upon the compound like a heavy blanket, muffling the sounds of the river, which ran dark and slow beyond the tangle of reeds, and silencing even the insects that usually hummed their ceaseless, frantic hymn in the twilight. I sat in the veranda, the only place where the stillness of the air might be mistaken for a breeze, watching the shadow of the mango tree creep across the whitewashed wall of the storehouse. The letters from the Company were on the table before me, the paper stiff with the damp of the journey, the ink faded by the sun’s relentless assault. They were not polite; they were not even cruel, in the way that human cruelty can be, but they were absolute. The station at Omu was to be abandoned. The agents were to withdraw to the coast, where the sea air was cleaner and the prospects, though fewer, were at least visible. It was a decision made in the cool, dry offices of a city I had not seen in three years, by men who looked at the map and saw only lines and distances, not the red earth or the faces of the men who lived upon it.

Kofi came to me as the light began to fail, that strange, deepening purple that falls over the river just before the darkness becomes total. He did not speak at first. He stood in the doorway, his silhouette sharp against the dim interior of the hut, his hands resting on the staff that was more a symbol than a tool, for he walked as if the ground beneath him was a thing to be respected, not trodden. I had known Kofi for two years, long enough to learn that his silence was a language in itself, a dialect of caution and sorrow that he spoke to those who had not yet understood the weight of things here. He was the chief of the Omu people, a man whose authority was derived not from any treaty or gunboat, but from the sheer, unyielding fact of his existence in a land that had been carved up by strangers. He had protected the station, not out of kindness, but out of a cold, pragmatic respect for the trade, and perhaps, I dared not think it, out of a curiosity for the strange, pale men who came from the sea and brought with them things that hummed and clicked and tasted of iron and smoke.

"Agent," he said, his voice low, roughened by the years of calling out to the river and the forest. "The boat is ready."

I looked up from the letter, which I had been rereading, searching for a clause, a hesitation, a crack in the armor of bureaucracy that might allow me to stay. "Yes, Kofi. The boat is ready. We leave at dawn."

He stepped into the veranda, and the shadow of the mango tree fell across his face, half of it in light, half in darkness. It was a fitting image for the man, I thought, a man divided between the old world and the new, between the river and the sea, between the duty to his people and the curiosity of his mind. "The Company has spoken," he said, not a question.

"The Company has spoken," I agreed. "And I must obey."

Kofi nodded, a slow, heavy movement, like the swaying of a tree in a wind that cannot be felt. He sat on the bench opposite me, his staff leaning against the pillar, and for a long time we sat in silence, listening to the river. It was a silence that was not empty, but full, filled with the sound of our own breathing and the distant cry of a bird that had forgotten the name of its song. I felt a strange, heavy feeling in my chest, a sense of loss that was not yet fully formed, a grief for a place that I had never truly owned, but which had come to define the shape of my life. I had grown here, in the heat and the dust, in the company of men who spoke in fragments and chiefs who spoke in proverbs. I had become part of the rhythm of the station, the rise and fall of the river, the coming and going of the canoes. To leave was to sever a connection that I did not know I had made, to cut myself off from a part of myself that had taken root in this red earth.

"You will go back to the sea," Kofi said at last. "You will forget the taste of the water here. You will forget the sound of the rain on the roof."

"I will not forget," I said, and the words sounded hollow, like a drum struck in an empty hall. "But I must go. My orders are clear."

Kofi looked at me, and in his eyes I saw a question that he did not ask. He did not ask if I was brave, or if I was wise, or if I was right. He asked, in the way that only men who have lived with the consequences of their choices can ask, whether I was aware of what I was giving up. He knew that the Company would not care if the station was left to rot, that the men who had built it would be scattered, that the trade would be taken up by others, perhaps less careful, perhaps more cruel. He knew that my presence here had been a check on the chaos, a small, fragile thing that held back the tide of disorder. To leave was to let go of that check, to let the current run its course.
