It was a truth universally acknowledged, in the drawing-rooms of Bath, that a naval officer of modest means and severe temper was ill-suited to the management of a sister of twenty. Captain Edward Harcourt, however, possessed neither the modesty to acknowledge his limitations nor the temper to conceal them. He had brought his sister, Miss Eleanor Harcourt, to the city not for the pleasures of the pump-room, but for the necessity of her health, a circumstance which he had exploited with a grim economy that left little room for the luxuries of society. Eleanor, whose constitution was delicate and whose spirit was unusually pliable, found herself the subject of a domestic tyranny that was less a matter of affection than of administrative control.

Their lodgings were situated in a respectable but unadorned street, far enough from the fashionable throng to ensure privacy, yet close enough to permit the sound of carriages on the gravel. It was here, on a morning of grey light and damp air, that the crisis of their stay was precipitated by the arrival of Mr. Julian Vane.

Mr. Vane was a gentleman of no particular rank, though he possessed the ease of manner and the polished vocabulary that often pass for it in circles where birth is not immediately scrutinized. He had been introduced to Eleanor by a mutual acquaintance, a lady of limited intellect who saw in Vane’s agreeable smile a guarantee of character. He was not handsome in the conventional sense, nor was he rich in the way that invited immediate speculation; but he had a certain quiet confidence, a habit of listening with an air of profound interest, and a ready wit that was never sharp enough to offend, though it was often sharp enough to cut.

When Eleanor returned from their morning walk, her cheeks flushed with the cold and her eyes bright with a pleasure she could not entirely suppress, Captain Harcourt was seated by the fire, a newspaper in his hand, which he did not read. He looked up as she entered, and the change in his countenance was so instantaneous and distinct that Eleanor felt a cold prickle of apprehension.

"You are later than usual, Miss Harcourt," he said, his voice level but stripped of all warmth. "And you have returned with a companion."

Eleanor’s heart sank. "Only for a moment, brother. Mr. Vane was passing, and we exchanged a few words regarding the weather."

"Mr. Vane," he repeated, the name falling from his lips like a stone into deep water. "The same Mr. Vane who was seen in the pump-room last evening, dancing with Mrs. Gower?"

Eleanor looked down at her hands. "I am sure I did not see him. I was not in the pump-room last evening."

"And yet, it is said," Harcourt continued, folding his paper with a crisp snap that seemed excessive for the action, "that he is a man of no profession, no estate, and no fixed abode. A man who lives on the charity of friends and the goodwill of women. It is a common sort of creature, Miss Harcourt, but one that a sensible girl would avoid."

"He is not what you suppose," Eleanor said, her voice trembling slightly, not from fear, but from the indignation of being judged by hearsay. "He is polite, and he is well-read. He speaks of literature and travel with a knowledge that does not seem affected."

"Knowledge," Harcourt sneered, rising from his chair. "It is the easiest ornament to acquire, and the least reliable. A man may learn to quote Shakespeare and still be a parasite. I am not concerned with his conversation, Eleanor. I am concerned with his character, or rather, his lack of it. To associate with such a man is to lower one’s own standard. It is a mark of weakness, of a susceptibility to flattery that is unbecoming of a Harcourt."

Eleanor stood her ground, though her hands were cold. "I am of age, brother. I am not a child to be kept in a closet. If I am to be blamed for being polite to a man who addresses me with respect, then I am to be blamed for the very virtue you claim to value."

"Politeness is not the same as intimacy," he replied, stepping closer, his shadow falling over her. "And respect, in a man of no consequence, is often a mask for interest. Do you think he is interested in your mind? Or in your prospects? He sees a young woman without a fortune, and he sees a chance to make a friend of her. It is a common calculation. I would not have you subject yourself to such calculations. I forbid the acquaintance."

The word hung in the air, heavy and absolute. Eleanor felt a sudden surge of anger, not at his severity, but at the presumption of it. He had no right. He had no legal claim over her, no moral authority that could withstand the scrutiny of reason. He was her brother, yes, but he was not her master.

"I am not a child," she said quietly. "And I am not so foolish as to be led by every smile. But I am also not so foolish as to be ruled by fear. If you wish to banish me from Bath, you may do so. But you cannot dictate to whom I speak."

Harcourt stared at her. For a moment, the air between them was thick with the tension of two strong wills colliding. He looked at her, really looked at her, and for the first time, he seemed to see not a dependent sister, but a woman who had reached a maturity he had not expected. He saw the firmness in her eyes, the quiet dignity of her refusal.

"You are right," he said, and the admission was so grudging, so ill-fitting to his usual tone, that it surprised them both. "I am out of my depth. I have treated you as a child, when you are a woman. And I have judged a man on rumor, without giving him the benefit of the doubt."
