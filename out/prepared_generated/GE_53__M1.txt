It is a truth generally acknowledged by those who have the management of young females, that the mind of a girl is a sensitive instrument, liable to be thrown out of tune by the slightest jarring of circumstance, and that the education of such an instrument is a task demanding not merely knowledge, but a certain moral tact—an instinct for the precise degree of tension to be applied to the strings of feeling and duty. Mrs. Eliza Thorne, the widow of a clergyman and the proprietor of a select academy for young ladies in the town of Blackwood, was a woman who believed in this principle with the firmness of a Newtonian physicist; she had organized her establishment on the model of a well-ordered solar system, where she herself was the central sun, about which the orbits of her pupils revolved in regulated, predictable ellipses.

But human nature is not a celestial body; it is a compound of volatile elements, subject to laws of its own which often refuse to defer to the regulations of a boarding-school prospectus. And so, when the post brought the news that Miss Cordelia Vance, the only daughter of the iron-master, had fled the institution not in the company of her governess to the botanical gardens, but in the company of Mr. Augustus Fitch, the music-master, to the Great Western Railway station, Mrs. Thorne felt the shock of a system thrown into chaos. It was a blow not only to her affection—which was real, though disciplined—but to her reputation, that invisible fabric woven from the opinions of the neighborhood, which she must now defend against the storm that was surely coming.

The storm arrived in the form of Mr. Josiah Vance himself, a man whose wealth was derived from the smelting of iron and whose manners had been formed in the blast-furnace of commerce. He was a man of large presence and small patience; he stood in Mrs. Thorne’s drawing-room like a great oak that has been uprooted and thrust into a flower-pot, filling the space with a sense of impending fracture. His face was red, not with the flush of health, but with the congestion of wrath, and he held his hat in his hand as if he were preparing to strike a blow with it.

"Madam," he began, his voice rumbling like the machinery of his own mills, "I placed my girl—my Cordelia—in your care. I paid you a sum that would keep a working man’s family in bread and bacon for a twelvemonth. I expected that she would be taught her deportment, her needlework, and her scripture. I did not expect that she would be taught how to run off with a fiddler."

Mrs. Thorne, who was seated at her desk, did not rise. She knew that to stand would be to admit equality in a contest where she must remain the moral superior. She adjusted her black silk shawl and looked at him with the steady, mournful gaze of a woman who has long been acquainted with the ingratitude of the world.

"Mr. Vance," she said, her voice cool and melodious, contrasting sharply with his rough timbre, "I share your distress. I share it profoundly. But you must allow me to observe that the seeds of such a rebellion are rarely sown in a schoolroom. They are brought here, like the seeds of weeds in a sack of wheat, from the soil of the home."

"Stuff and nonsense!" cried Mr. Vance, pacing the rug. "She was a quiet child. A biddable child. It is that fellow Fitch. You hired him. You brought him into the house. A man with long hair and a velvet coat, who talks about 'soul' and 'harmony' instead of teaching the scales. If you had hired a man of substance, a man who knew the value of a shilling, this would not have happened."

"We cannot always distinguish the artist from the adventurer, Mr. Vance," Mrs. Thorne replied, folding her hands. "We engaged Mr. Fitch upon the recommendation of the Bishop’s wife. He possessed the talent which we desired for the refinement of the pupils. But talent, as we know, is often a chariot drawn by wild horses. I have always maintained that the discipline of the mind must be the charioteer. It appears that in your daughter’s case, the charioteer was asleep."

Mr. Vance stopped and glared at her. "Are you telling me it is my girl’s fault? Are you telling me I have raised a fool?"

"I am telling you, sir, that adolescence is a period of chemical instability," Mrs. Thorne said, warming to her theme, for she was a woman who found consolation in general truths. "We provide the vessel, we keep it clean, we guard it from drafts; but if the mixture inside chooses to boil over, the chemist is not always to blame. Cordelia is young. She has been fed upon novels, I fear, and she has mistaken a romantic infatuation for a permanent destiny. She has erred, certainly. But the error lies in the judgment, a faculty which is the last to ripen in the human plant."

"I don’t care about the plant!" shouted the manufacturer. "I care about my standing in the town! I care that my partners will hear that my daughter has gone off with a pauper who cannot keep her in bonnets. And I hold *you* responsible. Your school is compromised. If you think I will pay the remainder of the quarter’s fees, you are mistaken. And if you think I will not speak of this, you are doubly mistaken."

Here, Mrs. Thorne felt a coldness in her chest, the dread of the respectable woman facing financial ruin. But she drew herself up. She had faced creditors before her husband’s death; she could face a manufacturer now.
