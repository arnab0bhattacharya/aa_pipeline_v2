The heat of the afternoon lay thick upon the harbour of Sunda Laut, a wet wool blanket that trapped the smells of salt, drying fish, and the sweet rot of the mangroves. Elias van der Meer, Harbour Master, sat in his office, the ceiling fan turning with a slow, listless sigh that did little more than stir the humid air. He was a man made of angles and patience, his face a roadmap of equatorial sun and long years spent watching the sea. Outside, the Java Sea shimmered under a white, punishing sky, its surface broken only by the masts of schooners and the low, dark shapes of local perahus.

Then, it appeared. A ship, crawling towards the mouth of the river, its hull the colour of a deep bruise and its sails patched and stained. It flew no flag. No Dutch tricolour, no British ensign, no rag of a shipping company. It was a ghost, a vessel without a country, and in the orderly world of the Dutch East Indies, such a thing was an affront. Elias felt the familiar prickle of official duty. He reached for his brass telescope, the metal warm in his hand, and trained it on the approaching vessel. It was a barque, old but seaworthy, its name painted on the bow in faded letters: *Kompas*. A compass, without a direction.

The Resident’s telegram had arrived that morning, a terse and unambiguous command from the administrative capital at Batavia. *Vessel flying no colours. Deny entry. Quarantine at outer anchorage. Await further instructions. Do not engage.* The words were stark, the authority absolute. Elias had spent twenty years in this post, a life of meticulous ledgers and harbour regulations. He understood authority. He would follow the order.

He ordered his small steam launch prepared, the put-put of its engine a familiar, reassuring sound against the quiet lap of the water. As he motored out to meet the *Kompas*, he saw the faces of the men on deck, dark-skinned and watchful, their expressions unreadable. A European stood at the rail, a man of medium height with a sun-bleached beard and a worn captain’s coat. He raised a hand in a gesture that was neither a wave nor a salute, but something in between.

Elias did not return it. He drew his launch alongside the barque’s hull, the wood scarred and salt-eaten. "In the name of the Governor-General of the Netherlands East Indies," he called up, his voice flat and official, "this vessel is denied entry to the port of Sunda Laut. You will anchor at the designated quarantine point and await instructions."

The captain looked down at him, his eyes shadowed by the brim of his hat. He said nothing for a long moment, then simply nodded. From his coat, he produced a leather tube, the kind used to keep charts dry. He tossed it down. It landed on the deck of the launch with a soft thud. "For the Harbour Master," the captain said, his voice rough, accented with a lilt Elias could not place. "A matter of navigation."

Elias picked up the tube. It was old, the leather cracked and supple. He felt a strange reluctance to open it, a sudden, inexplicable dread. He gave a curt nod, ordered his coxswain to turn the launch about, and returned to the shore without looking back.

Back in the suffocating quiet of his office, he placed the tube on his desk. The fan continued its weary rotation. He poured himself a small measure of genever, the juniper sharp and clean in the thick air. He drank it in one go. Then, with careful fingers, he unrolled the stiff leather. Inside was not a chart, but a single sheet of paper, folded once. The paper was of a kind he had not seen in years, a coarse, yellowed stock.

He unfolded it.

The script that met his eyes struck him with the force of a physical blow. It was a lean, angular hand, with a distinctive sharp angle to the crossbar of the ‘t’ and a deep, looping flourish to the capital ‘S’. It was a hand he had seen a thousand times, filling the margins of books, scrawling letters home from a university in Leiden, drawing maps of imaginary lands on the back of shipping manifests. It was his own. Or rather, it was the hand of his brother, Pieter, whose own writing had always been an echo of Elias’s, learned from the same tutor, shaped by the same world. A hand he had not seen in twenty years.

*Elias,* the letter began. The ink was faded, the words spaced with a strange deliberation.

*If you are reading this, then the sea has finally brought me to your door. I know the orders you have received. I know what you must do. I do not ask you to disobey. I only ask you to remember.*

Elias’s hand trembled. He sank into his chair, the letter held tightly in his grasp. He remembered the last time he had seen Pieter. They had stood on the docks at Vlissingen, the North Sea wind whipping their coats. Pieter, always the dreamer, his head filled with tales of the East, of spice islands and golden temples. Elias, the pragmatist, bound for a steady career in colonial administration. Their father’s shipping company had been their inheritance, a future for them both. But Pieter had refused it. He had signed on with a privateer, a vessel of dubious allegiance, chasing rumours of a sunken Spanish galleon. Their father had disinherited him on the spot. Elias, torn, had chosen duty, his father, the known world. He had watched Pieter’s ship sail for the horizon and had not seen or heard from him since. The silence had stretched into years, then a decade, until it became a solid, unbreached wall.
