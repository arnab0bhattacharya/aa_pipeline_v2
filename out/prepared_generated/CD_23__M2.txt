It was a morning of peculiarly damp and sullen grandeur, the kind of day that seemed to sit upon the shoulders of London like a heavy, ill-fitting coat, pressing the breath out of the streets and the spirits out of the men who walked them. In the heart of Mayfair, where the pavements were swept with a zeal that suggested they might be polished into mirrors of moral rectitude, stood the establishment of Monsieur Etienne Vauquelin. It was a shop of such dazzling, gaudy, and aggressive elegance that it seemed to shout its own prosperity at the passers-by. The windows, framed in gilt that had not yet lost its luster, displayed mannequins of such exquisite, if somewhat rigid, beauty that one might have mistaken them for the ghosts of fashionable ladies who had died of vanity. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of lavender, burnt hair, and the faint, cloying aroma of the pomades that Monsieur Vauquelin compounded with the seriousness of an alchemist.

Monsieur Vauquelin himself was a man who had long since ceased to be merely French, though he retained, with the tenacity of a barnacle clinging to a hull, a certain theatrical flair in his gestures and a pronunciation that curled around the English vowels like a cat around a warm hearth. He was a creature of flattery, and flattery was his livelihood, his religion, and his shield. To the Duchess of Brindlebury, he was the architect of her eternal youth; to Lord Puffington, he was the sole confidant of his masculine vigor; to the Countess of Glimmer, he was the guardian of her delicate nerves. He knew their secrets, their blemishes, their insecurities, and he wielded this knowledge with the skill of a swordsman who knew exactly where to strike to draw the most blood—and the most guineas.

But on this particular May morning, the mirror in the corner of the shop did not reflect the usual confident smirk. Instead, it caught the eye of a young man in the street, a clerk of the sort who moved through London like a ghost, unseen by the busy and the bold. This clerk, whose name was Mr. Silas Trott, paused on the pavement, his ink-stained fingers trembling as he held a letter. The letter had come that very morning, wrapped in paper that smelled of tobacco and revolution. It was from his brother, a man whose name had once been spoken in the same breath as "Vauquelin," but was now spoken in the same breath as "Traitor" and "Radical."

"Etienne," the letter began, in a hand that shook with the fervor of a cause that had long since abandoned its initial promises of liberty. "The club in our village is hungry. Not for bread, for bread is cheap, but for gold. The people are restless, and the King’s agents are close. We must move. We must act. Send the sum agreed upon. If you do not, you are a dead man, Etienne. And you will die in a land where no one knows the taste of your perfumes."

Monsieur Vauquelin stood before his basin, a towel draped over his shoulder like the robe of a priest awaiting a baptism of soaps and suds. He felt a coldness in his stomach that had nothing to do with the damp air. He looked out the window at Mr. Trott, who was now watching him with an intensity that was almost painful. Trott was a man of no particular distinction, save for the way he observed. He observed everything: the way the dust motes danced in the shafts of light, the way the servants scurried, the way the aristocrats sat in their chairs, allowing themselves to be transformed into something other than themselves. And now, he was observing Vauquelin, and his eyes held a new quality—a suspicion that was sharp as a razor.

It was the Duchess of Brindlebury who first broke the silence. She was seated in the chair of state, her wig removed, her hair hanging in damp, unadorned tresses that looked like the roots of an ancient tree. She was a woman of considerable size and considerable temper, and she had a way of speaking that could strip the paint off a wall.

"Etienne," she said, her voice low and dangerous. "I have heard things. Things that do not sit well with the ear. They say that the French are not merely hairdressers. They say they are spies. They say they carry messages in their wigs and secrets in their soaps."

Monsieur Vauquelin felt a sweat break out on his forehead, despite the coolness of the room. "Madame," he said, bowing so low that his nose nearly touched the floor. "I am but a servant of beauty. I know nothing of politics, save that it is bad for the complexion."

The Duchess smiled, but it was a smile that did not reach her eyes. "Is that so? And yet, I hear that your brother is in Paris. And that Paris is... turbulent."

Monsieur Vauquelin stiffened. "I have no brother, Madame."

"Have you not? Or is he a brother only in name? A brother of the mind? Of the club?"

The word "club" hung in the air, heavy and suffocating. It was the word that had been whispered in the coffee houses, in the drawing rooms, in the shadows of the streets. It was the word that meant danger, and danger meant death. And death, in London, was not a gentle thing.

Monsieur Vauquelin looked at the mirror again. He saw his own face, pale and drawn, the lines around his eyes deepened by fear. He saw the reflection of the Duchess, rigid and watchful. He saw the mannequins in the window, frozen in their poses of eternal grace, unaware of the storm that was gathering.

"I must go, Madame," he said, his voice trembling. "I have an appointment. A client of the highest order. You know how it is. One cannot keep the great from waiting."
