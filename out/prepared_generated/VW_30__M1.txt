The light in the room was already the colour of a weak tea, the grey London light of a winter afternoon that had never really fully arrived, and now, with a faint clatter of wheels from the Euston Road and the sudden flare of a gas lamp opposite, was beginning to take its leave. Ellen sat on the floor, the cold of the boards seeping through the thin wool of her stockings, and surrounded by the great pale mountains of her mother’s clothes. The scent of them was the scent of her mother—lavender, and cedar, and something else, a faint, powdery sweetness that was the smell of her skin, of her presence in a room. She had been folding, methodically at first, then with a growing sense of futility, for the piles only seemed to grow, a landscape of memory that she must now somehow pack into trunks.

It was in the pocket of the grey wool coat—the good one, with the astrakhan collar, that had been worn for winter walks and for funerals—that her fingers found it. Not a handkerchief, not a shopping list, but a small, stiff rectangle of card. She drew it out. The paper was cream, now yellowed at the edges, and the printing was a faded, elegant black. ‘His Majesty’s Theatre,’ it read, in a grand, looping script, and below, ‘Stalls, Row G, Seat 12.’ And then the date, which was the thing that stopped the small, tidy world of her thoughts. The fourth of May, 1897.

1897. The year was a room she could not enter. It was before her own life had begun its quiet, steady rhythm in this very flat. Before her father’s illness, before the long, slow fading of her mother’s laughter into the soft, watchful silence of her later years. Ellen turned the ticket over in her hand. It was smooth, almost cool to the touch. Who had sat in that seat? Her mother, of course, but which mother? Not the woman whose grey hairs now lay like a thread of silk on the coat’s collar, but a younger creature, a woman with a future as wide and unknowable as the darkening sky outside the window. A woman who had bought a ticket to a theatre, who had stood perhaps in this very coat, feeling the press of the crowd, the gaslight, the anticipation.

She let the coat fall from her lap and sat very still, the ticket held between her finger and thumb. The room around her, with its familiar shapes of the heavy wardrobe, the writing desk, the armchair where her mother had read, seemed to dissolve. She was trying to build an evening around this small piece of card. May. The air would be soft, the London plane trees just breaking into leaf, their new green a startling, temporary thing against the soot-stained brick. Her mother would have dressed with care. Not this heavy grey wool, surely, but something lighter, something with a rustle to it. Ellen could almost hear the sound of it, a sound like dry leaves. A dress of blue, perhaps, or a deep rose. She would have done her hair up, pinned it high, and the face in the looking-glass would have been one of expectation, of a life still to be lived. Was she happy? The question seemed too large, too crude. She was going out. She was going to a play. There was a whole world in that.

And she would not have gone alone. A man’s arm to take. Her father, of course. But her father as Ellen had never known him: a young man, full of talk and laughter, his voice clear and strong, not the thin whisper of the sickroom. They would have walked, perhaps, or taken a hansom, the two of them huddled together as the horse’s hooves clopped on the cobbles. What did they talk of? The play they were to see? The future? A house of their own, children, the small, domestic dreams that build a life. Ellen tried to imagine her mother’s face in the dark of the theatre, lit only by the glow from the stage. The play would be a swirl of colour and sound, of heightened emotions and grand gestures. Her mother would have leaned forward, her lips slightly parted, utterly absorbed. And in that absorption, she would have been utterly herself, a woman entirely separate from the role of mother, of wife, of widow-to-be. A woman with a secret life of her own, a life of theatre tickets and evening dresses and conversations that Ellen could only guess at.

A shiver went through her, not from the cold of the floor, but from the sudden, profound sense of distance. It was like looking down a very long corridor at a door that was closing. The woman who had sat in Row G, Seat 12, was a stranger. She had lived and breathed and felt things—joy, boredom, love, irritation—on that May evening, and that entire evening was now as lost to her as it was to Ellen. It had vanished, leaving only this flimsy ghost of a ticket in a coat pocket. Ellen looked at the woman she had known, the mother who had soothed her fevers and mended her clothes, whose hands were now folded in her lap in the stillness of the photograph on the mantelpiece. That woman was a culmination, a result. But this other one, the one from 1897, she was a beginning. She was all possibility.

The gas lamp outside hissed, and the light in the room deepened to a soft, velvety blue. The piles of clothes around Ellen were no longer just clothes; they were the shells of a life, the evidence of a hundred evenings, a thousand mornings, most of them as lost and as vivid as this one. She sat there for a long time, holding the ticket, feeling the silence of the flat press in around her.
