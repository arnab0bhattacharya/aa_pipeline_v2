The vast silence of the lake seemed to creep up the hill and into the dim, severely furnished room. The season was late autumn; the mountains opposite were hidden by a low, driving mist that wrapped the landscape in a grey, featureless shroud. Within the walls of Madame Vex’s pension on the Chemin de Fleuri, the atmosphere was close, smelling faintly of boiled mutton and heavy with the unspoken respectability of transient lives. Countess Katerina Alexeevna sat near the window, a woman of an imposing stillness, her dark hair severely drawn back from a face that was like a beautiful, pale mask, impenetrable and cold. She had lived in this place of exile for two years, breathing the damp, neutral air, surrounded by the heavy, carved furniture of a foreign bourgeoisie, a captive to the catastrophic improvidence of her husband.

The door opened without a preliminary knock, and the landlady’s maid ushered in a visitor. He stood for a moment on the threshold, blinking in the grey light—a young man, thin, with a pale, ascetic face and hollow eyes that held the luminous, feverish glow of a man who has looked too long into the fires of an abstract faith. It was Peter Ivanovitch, once the secretary to the Count, a man of quiet, melancholy efficiency, who had spent his youth copying dispatches in the damp corridors of the Petersburg estates. Now, as he advanced into the room, he seemed to carry with him the shadow of a vast, sombre destiny. He was enveloped in a shabby, damp overcoat, and the rain dripped from his cap onto the faded Axminster carpet.

He bowed with an awkward, formal gravity, and Katerina Alexeevna, without rising, motioned him to a chair. He sat down on the edge of it, his hands clasped between his knees, staring at the floor. The silence in the room was heavy, broken only by the persistent ticking of the ormolu clock on the mantelpiece, measuring out the cold, indifferent seconds of their exile.

“You have come from far,” the Countess said at last, her voice low, with the heavy, deliberate cadence of one accustomed to command but weary of speaking. “I trust my husband is in good health.”

Ivanovitch raised his head, and a faint, ironic smile touched his lips. “The Count is in excellent health, Katerina Alexeevna. He is, as ever, occupied with his… affairs. But I have not come to speak of him. I have severed my connection with that house.”

She looked at him steadily, her dark eyes reflecting nothing of the turmoil that might have stirred in a less disciplined breast. She had no illusions; her husband’s house was a ruin of debts and dissipations, a colossal monument to a futile existence. Yet the abruptness of the statement, the faint tremor in his voice, struck a note of profound melancholy.

“You have left his service,” she murmured. “And what, then, is your purpose? To return to your family in the provinces? To seek a post in the administration?”

“No,” he said, and the word fell into the quiet room with the heavy, final thud of a stone dropped into a deep well. “I have joined the Committee, Katerina Alexeevna.”

The Committee. The word hung in the damp air, a shadow of terror and blood. She looked at him, and for the first time a faint line appeared between her brows. The Committee meant the revolution, the blind, destructive force that was tearing at the vitals of the vast, tottering empire they had left behind. She had no sympathy with such men. They were the enemies of order, of the slow, patient civilization that she, despite everything, represented. They were the architects of chaos, and their hands were steeped in the blood of the innocent and the guilty alike. She had watched them from afar, these men of the shadows, with a cold, disdainful horror. They were the negation of everything she believed in—the sacredness of tradition, the quiet, enduring dignity of an aristocratic lineage that had outlived monarchs and invasions.

“And what,” she asked slowly, “has the Committee to do with me?”

Ivanovitch leaned forward, his eyes burning with a sombre, almost religious intensity. “We need money,” he said, his voice halting, as if the words were dragged from him against his will. “We need funds to carry the work forward. The printing presses must be kept running. The transportation of… materials… requires resources. The Countess Natalie Ivanovna, in Geneva, told me that you alone, of all the exiles, have resources. She said that you…”

“Countess Natalie is a foolish woman who talks too much,” Katerina Alexeevna interrupted, her voice sharp and cold. “And you, Peter Ivanovitch, are a fool to listen to her. You come to me, asking me to finance the destruction of my own class, the ruin of my world? It is… a singular audacity.”

He looked at her, and his gaze did not falter. “I do not ask you to finance destruction,” he said. “I ask you to finance the birth of a new justice. The old world is dead, Katerina Alexeevna. It is a corpse, though it does not yet know it. We are the gravediggers, yes, but we are also the sowers of the new seed. You have seen the misery, the ignorance, the unspeakable degradation of the people. You, who have a heart, cannot be indifferent to it.”

“I am indifferent to nothing,” she replied. “But I do not believe that a new world can be built upon a foundation of murder. You speak of justice, but your methods are those of the assassin. You will bring not peace, but a sword, and the blood you spill will cry out against you.”
