The express which arrived at Captain and Mrs. Ashworth’s lodgings in Bath on a Tuesday morning was of such an alarming nature as to throw the whole establishment into commotion. It was from Mrs. Thorneycroft’s apothecary at the Manor, and spoke of a “sudden and violent seizure of the lungs” which had prostrated its mistress. The summons to her daughter, Mrs. Ashworth, was imperative.

“My dear Catherine,” said Captain Ashworth, observing his wife’s countenance, which had lost all its colour, “we must go directly. I shall order the carriage.”

Catherine, whose filial anxiety was a principle as deeply engrained as her good sense, could only agree. Her mother, Mrs. Thorneycroft, was a woman of a delicate constitution and a still more delicate sensibility; a cold was to her a matter of life and death, and the fact of her having summoned her married daughter from Bath was proof enough, to Catherine’s mind, of a very serious indisposition indeed.

The journey to Thorneycroft Manor was accomplished with a speed that did justice to the Captain’s concern. He was a man of few words, but his actions were ever prompt and considerate. Upon their arrival, however, they were met not by a scene of hushed despair, but by the robust Mrs. Pickett, the housekeeper, who informed them that her mistress was in her chamber, and had taken a little gruel.

“A little gruel!” Catherine exclaimed, her spirits rising at once. “Then she is not in immediate danger?”

“Oh, no, ma’am,” said Mrs. Pickett, with a look that seemed to imply a certain familiarity with such alarms. “She has a dreadful cold in the head, to be sure, and a tickle in the throat, but the doctor says with rest and quiet, she will be quite herself in a sennight.”

Catherine felt a pang of disappointment, which she immediately castigated as un-daughterly. A cold, however bad, was not the mortal fever she had pictured. Her journey, it seemed, had been an over-reaction; a fact which she foresaw would be a source of some little triumph to her mother.

They were shown into the blue drawing-room, where Mrs. Thorneycroft was established upon a chaise longue, propped up by a veritable fortress of pillows. A shawl was drawn about her shoulders, though the May sun was pouring warmly through the window, and a single handkerchief was clutched in one pale hand.

“My dearest child!” she cried, in a voice of faint but touching reproach. “To think I should have brought you all this way! But the apothecary was so grave, and my own sensations were so peculiar—”

“My dear mother,” Catherine said, rushing to her side, “we are only thankful to find you no worse. The journey was nothing.”

It was at this moment that Captain Ashworth stepped forward. He did not rush; he advanced with a calm, measured tread, and taking Mrs. Thorneycroft’s free hand, pressed it with a degree of tender solemnity.

“Madam,” he said, his voice full of respectful sympathy, “we have been most seriously alarmed. Nothing but the assurance of your own physician could have calmed our fears. I hope you find this cushion to your liking? It seems to me you might be more at your ease.”

Mrs. Thorneycroft, who had expected a degree of impatience from a military man torn from his amusements in Bath, was quite overcome. “Oh, Captain! You are too good. It is this draught, I think; it plays so upon the nerves.”

“Then we shall have it stopped up at once,” he declared, with a decision that was infinitely soothing. “Your comfort must be our first object.”

Catherine observed this exchange with a mixture of gratitude and a new, unsettling emotion. Her husband’s patience was a quality she had always admired. It was the very foundation of his character. Yet, to see it deployed with such… enthusiasm, in the face of what was so plainly a very small illness, began to strike her as peculiar. He listened to a description of her mother’s symptoms with the rapt attention of a general hearing a report from the front. He adjusted her shawl not once, but three times. He enquired after the specific properties of the gruel.

Throughout the afternoon, as Mrs. Thorneycroft recounted the story of her cold from its first faint whisper to its present dramatic crescendo, Captain Ashworth was the model of an attentive audience. He asked intelligent questions. He offered quiet assents. He appeared to find the narrative not merely tolerable, but genuinely absorbing.

When Mrs. Thorneycroft, exhausted by her own eloquence, finally drifted into a light doze, Catherine drew her husband aside.

“Edward,” she said, lowering her voice, “you are wonderfully good.”

He smiled his quiet, handsome smile. “It is no trouble, my dear. Your mother is an invalid; she requires a certain management. And she is very far from being a fool, you know.”

This was true, and yet it did not explain the matter. “I had feared you would find it all a dreadful bore,” Catherine confessed. “The express was so alarming.”

“A little alarm is a very trifling price to pay for the assurance of her well-being,” he replied. “And I confess, I find the situation here rather… interesting.”

“Interesting?” Catherine repeated, aghast. “You find my mother’s cough interesting?”

“Not the cough, precisely,” he said, his eyes twinkling with a secret amusement she could not fathom. “But the whole domestic theatre of it. The pillows, the shawl, the careful selection of the gruel. It is a study in character.”

A suspicion, faint but definite, began to dawn in Catherine’s mind. It was a suspicion so uncharitable, so contrary to her own sense of duty, that she tried at once to banish it. Her husband, a man of unimpeachable honour, could not possibly be… enjoying this.
