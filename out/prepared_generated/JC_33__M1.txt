The heat did not come from the sun alone. That was the first, the essential, error one made in those waters. The sun was a thing of brass, a malevolent disc nailed to a sky of hammered copper, but the heat rose from the sea itself, from the very substance of the Red Sea. It rose in a palpable shimmer, a liquid atmosphere that coated the skin and entered the lungs, carrying with it the taste of salt and ancient dust. For a week, we had steamed through this furnace, the *Sultan’s Pride* a solitary iron intruder in a world of desiccated elements. The water, a deep and sullen purple, held the heat like a vast, dark stone. The silence was not an absence of sound but a presence—the deep, thrumming silence of immense power held in check, punctuated only by the weary pant of our own engines and the sigh of the wake astern.

I had been on the bridge since four o’clock. The night watch, that period of false coolness, had offered no relief. Now the day was asserting its dominion, and with it came a certain unease, a subtle wrongness in the ship’s motion that I could not name. It was a feeling, an impression, like the premonition of a squall on a clear horizon. Mr. Vance, my chief officer, was due to relieve me. I found him already there, standing by the chart table, his back to me. He was a man of good family, they said, with a handsome, if somewhat soft, profile and hands that had never known a day’s real labour before the sea claimed him. He was a good seaman in the conventional sense; his knots were neat, his voice on deck was clear. But there was a certain brittleness to his competence, a lack of that deep, intuitive feel for the ship that comes not from instruction but from a long communion with the sea’s moods.

“Good morning, Captain,” he said, turning. His face was pale, his eyes shot with a faint network of red. He smiled, a little too readily.

“Mr. Vance.” I nodded towards the chart. “The morning sights?”

“Taken and worked, sir. We are on our course.” He spoke with an assurance that seemed to me, in the heavy air of the chart-room, a little too loud, a little too precise. It was the tone of a man reading a lesson he has learned by rote.

I did not look at the chart immediately. I watched him. There was a tremor in his hand as he reached for a pair of dividers, a fine, almost imperceptible vibration that had nothing to do with the ship. And there was an odour, faint but distinct beneath the cloying sweetness of the coal smoke and the hot oil of the lamps. It was a sweet, cloying smell, the ghost of fermented sugars, of illicit stills and stuffy cabins. It was the smell of brandy. A taint. A flavour of mortality in the very air of the bridge.

I said nothing. To speak was to make it real, to give it a name and a consequence. I turned to the chart table. The great sheet of paper, with its delicate tracery of soundings and currents, lay under the glass. Our track was pencilled in a neat, unwavering line. It was a lie made manifest in ink. A week ago, I had trusted this line, this representation of our progress through the world. Now it seemed a child’s drawing, a pathetic assertion of order in a universe that was fundamentally chaotic. I took up the sextant, a heavy, familiar weight in my hands, and went out to the wing of the bridge.

The sun was climbing, its rays already fierce enough to make the brasswork of the instrument hot to the touch. I brought it to my eye, and the world dissolved into a series of brilliant, disjointed images: the curve of the earth, the sharp line of the horizon, the sun itself, a wavering, impossible disc. I brought it down, swinging on its arc, and read the altitude. My fingers, long practiced, moved with an economy of motion that was second nature. Back in the chart-room, the pencil felt alien in my grasp. I worked the sight, the figures falling into place with a dreadful, mechanical certainty. And then I laid the point of the pencil on the chart.

It did not fall on our pencilled track. It fell some thirty miles to the west. A week of such error, compounded daily, had carried us far from the safety of the deep-water channel. We were steaming towards the outlying teeth of the Suakin Archipelago, a region of uncharted reefs and treacherous shoals that could open the belly of an iron steamer like a tin of sardines. The silence in the chart-room was now absolute, profound. I could hear the faint ticking of the chronometer in its gimbal box, a sound like a tiny, frantic heart. Mr. Vance stood beside me. I could feel the rigidity of his posture. He knew. Of course, he knew. His lie had been a desperate attempt to hold back the morning, to postpone this inevitable collision of fact with fiction.

“Captain…” he began, his voice a dry whisper. The pretence had fallen away, leaving the raw, trembling man. “I… I must have made an error in the reckoning. A simple transposition of figures…”

I looked at him then, truly looked at him, and saw not the chief officer of the *Sultan’s Pride* but a creature caught in a trap of his own making. The brandy had not created this weakness; it had merely given it a voice, a temporary, borrowed courage that was now draining away, leaving him utterly exposed. To report him would be to sign his professional death warrant. The Company was not forgiving of such things. He would be disgraced, cast adrift in a world that had no place for a failed officer. I saw the bleak future in his eyes—the cheap boarding houses, the slow, ignominious slide into oblivion.
