The letter lay on the breakfast table between the silver coffee pot and the butter dish like a dead thing. Mr. Fitzwilliam Vance read it for the third time, the creases in the paper softened with handling. The figures were merciless. His son, Arthur, had returned from Cambridge not merely with a degree but with a ledger of debts that made the Lincolnshire frost seem warm. Five hundred pounds to a gaming hell in London. Another two hundred to a tailor. A hundred to a horse dealer for a beast that had thrown him and broken its own leg. The total was a sum that the Vance estate could not conjure without a reckoning.

"The oaks," Mr. Vance said at last. "The old oaks in the east paddock. They must come down."

Mrs. Vance set down her teacup. Her face, usually placid, took on a hard, porcelain quality. "You would sell the timber? The trees your grandfather planted? That is not a solution, Fitzwilliam. That is a surrender."

"It is arithmetic," he replied, his voice weary. "The timber merchant in Grantham offered a price last Michaelmas. It would cover the boy's nonsense and leave a little for the roof."

"It would leave us with a naked hill and the reputation of a man who must sell his inheritance to pay for his son's gambling. There is a better way." She looked toward the window where Arthur was visible in the distance, a dark figure against the grey mist of the parkland. "Miss Beatrice Sterling. Her father’s coal mines are the talk of the county. She is an heiress of considerable means, and she is fond of Arthur."

Mr. Vance rubbed his temples. "Arthur is fond of Arthur. Does the girl know what she would be buying?"

"She knows he is a gentleman," Mrs. Vance said sharply. "She knows he is handsome and that he will one day have this house. That is enough for a sensible girl. You must speak to him. Tell him the oaks must go unless he does his duty."

Arthur Vance did not come to the library to discuss duty. He came in smelling of wet wool and cordite, his boots leaving damp prints on the Aubusson rug. He was tall, with the careless beauty of a man who has never been told 'no' until the bill arrives. He stood by the fireplace, warming his hands, while his father laid out the ultimatum.

"The Sterling girl," Arthur said, his tone light, detached. "She has a laugh like a startled pony, Father. And she speaks of nothing but her father's new steam engine. It is hardly a romantic prospect."

"Romance is a luxury we can no longer afford," Mr. Vance said, gesturing to the letter. "Five hundred pounds, Arthur. Do you understand what that means? It means the oaks."

Arthur shrugged. "Then cut the oaks. They are only trees. We have hundreds."

"They are the boundary of the estate! They are the shelter! Without them, the wind will scour the fields. It is not just wood; it is the land's defense."

"Then I shall marry the coal heiress," Arthur said, though his eyes were already drifting toward the gun rack. "But not today. I promised the gamekeeper I would see to the pheasants in the lower copse. The frost has brought them out."

"Arthur, we are speaking of your future. Your ruin."

"I am speaking of a morning's sport," Arthur said, reaching for his fowling piece. "The future will keep until luncheon."

He left them there, the silent father and the rigid mother, and walked out into the cold.

The lower copse was a tangle of hawthorn and bramble, the ground hard with rime. Arthur moved through it with an ease that belied his dissipation. His debt was a heavy coat he had taken off and left in the house; here, in the sharp air, he felt light. The world was reduced to the weight of the gun in his hands and the search for movement in the undergrowth.

He did not want to marry Beatrice Sterling. He did not want to think about the oaks. He wanted the clean, sudden violence of the shot. He wanted the satisfaction of a clean kill, a thing done and finished, unlike the endless, nagging arithmetic of his life.

A cock pheasant burst from the brambles, a flash of copper and green against the grey sky. Arthur swung the gun smoothly. The recoil kicked his shoulder, a familiar, friendly blow. The bird folded in mid-air and fell into the wet grass.

He walked to retrieve it. As he picked up the limp, cooling body, he looked back toward the house. It sat on the ridge, a dark block against the clouds. He could see the line of the east paddock where the old oaks stood. They were massive, their bare branches like veins against the sky.

His father was right, of course. The trees were old. They were the spine of the place. But Arthur felt a strange, perverse satisfaction in knowing that his pleasure had a price, and that the price was being paid by the land itself. The trees would fall, or the heiress would be won. Either way, the silence of the morning would be broken by the noise of industry—saws or wedding bells.

He slung the pheasant over his shoulder and raised his gun again. There was more shooting to be done. The day was young, and the debts, like the winter, were only just beginning to bite.
