I had been seated by my window on a close and sultry afternoon in the July of ’94, watching the sluggish life of the Strand and endeavouring to extract some relief from an indifferent cigar, when my friend Sherlock Holmes set down his violin and observed that the weather was enough to make any man’s wits curdle in his skull. It was at that precise moment that there came a hurried step upon the stair, a fumbling at the latch, and there entered to us a young man whose appearance at once commanded my professional attention. He was perhaps eight-and-twenty, fair and ruddy of countenance in the way of a man who lives much in the open air, yet so pale beneath his colour and so disordered in his linen that I saw at a glance he had not slept for several nights. His collar was awry, his coat was buttoned awry, and he held in his hand a bundle of telegraph flimsies, which he clutched as a drowning man clutches a spar.

“Mr. Holmes,” said he, in a voice which he strove to hold steady and could not, “my name is Edmund Calverley. My father is Calverley of Calverley’s Brewery, at Ashford in Kent. I have come to you because I am afraid—not of a danger that I can see, but of one that I cannot. I am being told of deaths before they happen, sir, and the last name on the list is my own.”

Holmes waved him to the chair by the hearth, and, having pushed the tobacco into the toe of his slipper, sat down opposite him with his long fingers joined and his eyes fixed upon the young man’s face. “You will tell me everything from the beginning,” said he, “and you will tell it in order, and you will not omit the trifles, for it is upon the trifles that everything depends.”

Our visitor drew a long breath and smoothed the flimsies upon his knee. “Six weeks ago,” said he, “I received this.” He held out the first telegram. It was despatched from Ashford, and read: *“Beware. Henry Rusk will be dead before Friday.”* “Henry Rusk,” he continued, “is—I should say was—the head drayman at our works, a great, strong fellow who could have carried me on his shoulder. I laughed at the message, sir, as a piece of stupid malice. On the Thursday morning he was found at the foot of the malt-house stairs with his neck broken, and the doctor said he had fallen in a fit of giddiness, for he was a heavy man and had been a heavy drinker.”

Holmes’s eyelids drooped. “And the second?”

“Came a fortnight later, from the same office, in the same hand, so far as a telegram can be said to have a hand. *‘Beware. Thomas Gedge will be dead before Sunday.’* Gedge was our head maltster, a sober, careful, God-fearing man of sixty, who had not had a day’s illness in his life. I went to him at once, sir, and begged him to take care of himself, and he laughed at me for my pains. On the Saturday he was found in the cooling-room, dead, with no mark upon him and the physician’s talk of the heart. Two deaths, Mr. Holmes, each foretold within a day, and each of a man in my father’s employ.”

“And the third?” asked Holmes, with an eagerness which he did not attempt to conceal.

The young man’s hand shook so that he could scarcely disentangle the last flimsy from the rest. “It came this morning,” said he. “It is addressed to me at my own house, and it is not from Ashford, but from London. Read it, sir.”

Holmes took the paper and read aloud: *“Beware. Edmund Calverley will be dead before the twenty-first.”* He turned it over, held it to the light, and examined the gummed edge with his pocket-lens. “The twenty-first,” said he. “To-day is the seventeenth. You have, then, four days, Mr. Calverley—if we accept the prophecy. You have brought the other two, I hope?”

“I have them here.”

Holmes spread the three flimsies side by side upon the table, and for a long time he studied them in silence, stooping over them with his thin nose within an inch of the paper. Then he took down a great book of reference from the shelf, and turned the pages quickly. “You will pardon me,” said he at last, “but I wished to satisfy myself upon a point of no importance. Now, Mr. Calverley, I must ask you some questions which may seem to you impertinent. Who is your father’s partner?”

The young man stared. “My father has no partner, Mr. Holmes. The business is his own, though my uncle, Reuben Calverley, has a life interest in a portion of the profits, and sits at the board.”

“Your uncle Reuben. Is he a brewer?”

“He is nothing, sir. He is a man of leisure—of very great leisure. He has a house at Folkestone, and he comes to the works perhaps twice in a year. He and my father have not spoken civilly for a decade.”

“Indeed. And who manages the men in the yard?”

“Our foreman, Caleb Stint, who came to us from a brewery at Maidstone some three years back.”

“A newcomer. And who has access to the telegraph forms at the works?”

“Any of the clerks, I suppose. But the messages were handed in at the post-office, Mr. Holmes, not at our own office. The clerk there knows nothing but that a boy brought them with the money.”

Holmes rose and walked twice up and down the room, his hands behind him and his chin sunk upon his breast. “You will return to Ashford to-night,” said he at last, “and you will say nothing to any living soul of your visit to me. You will go about your business as usual. You will not sleep in your own room, but in some other chamber, and you will not go unarmed. Above all, you will not go near the brewery after dark. I shall be in Ashford before morning, and I shall send you word where to meet me. Watson, the Bradshaw.”
