The great house at Wrenscote stood in a park of such studied and melancholy beauty that a stranger, viewing it from the eastern terrace at the hour when the sun declines, might have supposed its inmates to be the happiest of mortals. The cedars drooped their heavy branches over the smooth turf with a benediction; the deer, moving in slow and shadowy groups, gave the landscape that air of antique peace which is the peculiar gift of an English domain; and within, the long galleries, the storied windows, the very silence of the corridors, spoke of a race whose roots had struck deep into the soil of the county for generations before the first George had come to the throne. Yet it is a truth, which the wise have observed in every age, that the external fitness of a life is no sure measure of its inward satisfaction; and the mistress of this fair domain, had you been gifted with the power of reading the human countenance, would have appeared to you less like a woman in possession of her heart's desire than like one who walks in a dream from which she cannot awake.

Lady Ormsby—for it was by this title that Mrs. Anne Ferrers, late of the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane, was now known to the county—had been, in her day, the most celebrated tragic actress of the English stage. There were those still living who declared that her Lady Macbeth had never been equalled, and that her Beatrice, her Portia, her Hermione, had a sweetness and a dignity which no subsequent performer had approached. She had retired, at the age of thirty-eight, in the full blaze of her reputation, to marry Lord Ormsby, a widower of grave and somewhat taciturn disposition, whose estates in Northamptonshire were among the most considerable in the kingdom, and whose sense of what was due to his name and position was as inflexible as the oak that had been his family's emblem for three centuries. The marriage had been, in the estimation of the world, a brilliant one; and Lady Ormsby, who had never known the comforts of a settled home, had entered upon it with a sincere determination to be all that her new station required. She had laid aside her art as a garment that is done with; she had studied the duties of a great lady with the same conscientious ardour she had once bestowed upon the study of a part; and if she had sometimes found the days long and the conversation of her neighbours barren, she had attributed the feeling to a natural regret for the excitements of her former life, and had striven, by a more rigorous attention to her new obligations, to silence the voice that would not be stilled.

But the years had passed, and the voice had not been silenced. It had grown, indeed, more importunate with every season. Lord Ormsby was a good man, and she honoured him; but he was a man to whom the present was everything and the past a closed book, and he could not understand—perhaps it was not to be expected that he should—the passionate longing that consumed his wife for the exercise of that art which had once been the very breath of her nostrils. He had never seen her act. He had, indeed, made it a condition of their marriage that her name should never again be associated with the stage; and she, in the first flush of her gratitude and her resolve, had accepted the condition without a murmur. But the stage had been to her not merely a profession—it had been a world, a religion, a means of escape from the narrowness and the weariness of common life. In the green-room she had been free; on the boards she had been herself. And now, in the midst of her magnificence, she found herself stifled by the very completeness of her prosperity.

It was in the third week of October, when the leaves were falling in the park and the first fires were being lighted in the great drawing-room, that a letter arrived which was destined to disturb the even tenor of her existence. It was brought to her by her own maid, together with the other letters of the morning, and she recognised at once, by the bold, unformed hand upon the envelope, the writing of her old friend and manager, Mr. Joseph Craddock, of the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane. She opened it with a beating heart. Mr. Craddock wrote to inform her that the great actor, Henry Alderney, whose fame was then at its height, was about to produce a new tragedy, and that he had expressed a passionate desire to play the principal part in it with her, and her alone. The season would be short—only twelve nights—but it would be the event of the theatrical year. He implored her, in the name of their old friendship and of the art they both served, to consent. He would make every arrangement that could minister to her comfort and her dignity; her name should be placed at the head of the bill; and he would undertake that nothing should be wanting which could secure the success of the undertaking. "I know," he wrote, "that you have retired, and that you have your reasons. But I also know that you were born for the stage, and that the stage has never ceased to sigh for you. Think of it, my dear friend, before you refuse. It is not for the money, nor for the fame, but for the art that is in you, and that will die with you if you do not give it one more utterance."
