The news arrived, as all really disastrous news should, upon a silver salver, and lay between a card from the Duchess of Whitcombe and an invitation to a private view of Mr. Crane’s new portraits, so that for a moment Sir Julian Vane believed it to be merely another of those delightful demands upon his leisure which society is forever making upon a young man of fashion. When he discovered that it was from his solicitor, and that it announced, in language of the most offensive clarity, that his late uncle had seen fit to tie up the entire Vane estate upon the condition that he, Julian, should for the space of one calendar year support himself by his own honest labour, or else forfeit everything to a cousin in Worcestershire who bred pigs and voted Tory, he did what any sensible man would do: he put on his hat, walked to White’s, and ordered a very large brandy.

“My dear Julian,” said Lord Algy Merton, when the story had been told, with that admirable economy of gesture which marks the true artist in conversation, “it is the most amusing thing I have heard this season. Positively medieval. You are to be a clerk, I suppose, in a counting-house, with a high stool and a pen behind your ear. I shall come and look at you through the window.”

“I think it is monstrous,” said Mrs. Delamere, who was beautiful, and therefore permitted to be kind. “To expect a man of poor Julian’s habits to earn his bread! Why, he has never earned so much as a compliment in his life.”

“He has earned a great many,” said Lord Algy, “but he has always spent them at once. That is the difference between capital and income.”

The matter was discussed, as such matters are, in every drawing-room in Mayfair, and it was universally agreed that the condition was the finest joke of the century, and that Julian Vane must be assisted in every possible way to make a fool of his uncle’s memory. It was Lady Brackenbury—that formidable woman whose parties were so exclusive that to be excluded from them was almost a title of nobility—who conceived the happy notion of placing him with Mrs. Arabella Ffoulkes, the wife of the Right Honourable Orlando Ffoulkes, Minister without Portfolio and with a great many opinions. Mrs. Ffoulkes required a private secretary. She required, more precisely, someone to answer her letters, remember her engagements, and tell her, with perfect tact, which of her admirers were worth encouraging and which were merely persistent. Julian, who had spent nine years doing precisely this for himself and his friends, and doing it beautifully, was engaged at a salary of two hundred pounds a year, and was informed that he might begin on Monday.

The Ffoulkes establishment in Eaton Place was a triumph of that peculiar art which consists in making the very rich look as though they had always been so. There were tapestries from Flanders, and a carpet from Isfahan, and upon a little table of malachite there stood a photograph of Mr. Gladstone, inscribed with a sentiment so flattering that it could only have been written by a man who intended never to speak to the recipient again. Mrs. Ffoulkes herself was a woman of forty, who had the figure of a girl and the conscience of a connoisseur: she collected admirers as other women collect fans, and displayed them with the same charming want of discrimination.

“Sir Julian,” she said, on the first morning, “I am told you are quite useless. Is it true?”

“Madam,” he replied, “I have devoted my life to the cultivation of uselessness, and I may say without vanity that I have brought it to a fine art. I can do nothing with an elegance that has often been remarked upon.”

“Then you are exactly what I want,” said Mrs. Ffoulkes. “My husband is a man of such dreadful energy that he makes one quite tired merely to watch him. He is always doing things. It is a vice. I hope you will not encourage him.”

“I shall do my best to set him a better example,” said Julian, gravely.

His duties, it soon appeared, were of a nature so delicate that they might have baffled a diplomat and did baffle a bishop. He wrote Mrs. Ffoulkes’s letters, which were numerous and chiefly concerned with the correction of other people’s grammar. He remembered the names of her guests, which was more difficult, for she had a habit of inviting people because she liked their faces and forgetting them because she liked their absence. He arranged flowers, which she said was the highest of the arts, since it left nothing behind. And on Thursday afternoons he accompanied her to the House of Commons, where she sat in the Ladies’ Gallery and looked down upon her husband with the air of a woman who has commissioned a portrait and is not entirely pleased with the likeness.

It was on one of these afternoons that the great affair of the Bayswater tramways came to a head. Mr. Ffoulkes, who had a genius for compromise, had proposed a scheme which pleased nobody, and there was a threat of resignation. The evening papers were full of it. Mrs. Ffoulkes, in a gown of pale green satin, was in a condition of high excitement, which she concealed beneath a manner of perfect languor.

“Julian,” she said—for she had early dropped the formality of his title, on the ground that it made her feel like a schoolmistress—“Julian, you must tell me what to do. Orlando is in a fury. He says he will resign and go and live in the country, where there are no trams and no newspapers and no one to contradict him. I cannot endure the country. The flowers there smell too much. Tell me what to do.”

“Do nothing,” said Julian.
