The Kingdom of Aethelgard was carved from the side of a mountain of white stone, a realm of terraced gardens and marble palaces where the wind smelled of jasmine and cold rock. At the highest terrace stood the palace of King Vorhen, a fortress of polished columns and sun-reflecting domes. Within its labyrinthine halls lived his daughter, Princess Elara. She was as beautiful as the kingdom she inhabited, possessing a face like a carved cameo and a will as unyielding as the marble beneath her feet. By her twenty-first year, she had rejected every suitor who dared approach her. She dismissed the bronze-skinned princes of the southern isles as tedious, mocked the poetry of the northern earls as drivel, and yawned at the martial parades of the eastern warlords. They were all predictable, she declared, offering up the same recycled flatteries and inherited glories.

Infuriated by her stubbornness but unable to deny her anything, King Vorhen demanded to know what it would take for her to choose a husband. Elara, sitting beneath a canopy of woven silver, looked out over the sprawling balcony at the vast, dark expanse of the Whisperwood, the ancient forest that bordered their pristine city. "I will marry the man who can bring me something I have never seen," she proclaimed, her voice echoing off the stone walls. "Something that cannot be found in our libraries, our vaults, or our skies."

The decree spread across the continents like wildfire. Aethelgard’s gates were flooded with treasure-laden caravans and ambitious men. The first to arrive was King Thessaly of the Sun-Drowned Wastes, a man whose coffers were legendary. He laid before the princess a casket of black sand, within which rested a gemstone that burned with its own internal fire, casting dancing shadows of crimson and gold across the marble floor. Elara barely glanced at it. "A captured flame," she said, gesturing to the torches lining the hall. "I have seen fire a thousand times. Its color does not interest me." Thessaly departed in shame, leaving the gemstone in the dust.

Next came the Grand Astromancer of the floating spires, a man draped in robes woven from midnight blue. He presented a sphere of spun glass containing a trapped fragment of a comet, a swirling nebula of light that hummed with cosmic energy. It was a marvel that drew gasps from the entire court. Elara leaned forward, her eyes reflecting the starlight, but then she leaned back and waved her hand. "I have watched the heavens through my father's telescopes," she said. "Stars, even those brought to earth, are still just stars. Take it away."

Weeks turned into months. The great hall overflowed with rejected curiosities: mechanical birds that sang in twelve tongues, bottled tears of sirens, maps to lands that existed only in dreams. Elara surveyed them all with the same cool detachment. She began to believe that the world was entirely exhausted of surprises, that everything worth seeing had already been cataloged and presented to her.

It was on the morning of the hundredth day that Kael arrived. He did not come with a caravan or a retinue of guards. He walked through the iron gates of Aethelgard wearing a roughspun tunic, his feet calloused and bare, smelling of pine needles and damp earth. He was a shepherd from the deepest edges of the Whisperwood, a young man whose life was measured by the seasons and the movements of his flock. The palace guards, amused by his audacity, let him pass if only to provide the court with a moment of levity.

Kael walked the length of the grand hall, his footsteps echoing softly against the mosaic tiles. King Vorhen watched from his throne, his brow furrowed in confusion. Elara sat perfectly still, her expression an icy mask of boredom, expecting yet another tedious offering. Kael stopped at the base of the dais. He did not bow. Instead, he held out his hands, cupped together as if holding a fragile, precious treasure.

"I have brought you something you have never seen, Princess," Kael said, his voice steady and clear, carrying no trace of the nervous trembling that usually afflicted those who stood before her.

Elara raised a delicate eyebrow. "Open your hands, then," she commanded.

Kael slowly parted his fingers. The court leaned in, expecting a rare jewel or a magical artifact. But there was nothing. His palms were empty, save for a small pool of rainwater that had collected in his cupped hands during his walk through the morning mist.

Elara’s eyes flicked from his hands to his face. "Is this a joke?" she asked, her voice dropping to a dangerous whisper. "You bring me a mouthful of water?"

"Look closer, Princess," Kael said softly. "Not at the water, but at what it holds."

Elara, despite her irritation, found herself leaning forward. The hall was silent save for the crackle of the hearths. She peered into the shallow pool resting in the shepherd’s rough, dirt-stained palms. The water was perfectly still, acting as a flawless, natural mirror. And in that mirror, she saw herself. But it was not the regal, untouchable portrait she was accustomed to seeing in her polished silver looking-glasses. In the shepherd’s hands, framed by earth and calloused skin, her reflection looked startlingly human. There were faint shadows of exhaustion beneath her eyes, a subtle vulnerability in the set of her mouth, and a deep, restless sadness in her gaze that she had never noticed before. She looked not like a marble goddess, but like a lonely girl trapped in a cage of stone.

For a fleeting second, a crack appeared in her armor. Her breath hitched, and her eyes widened. She had truly never seen herself like this—unfiltered, unadorned, stripped of the royal majesty that framed her every waking moment.

Then, the vulnerability vanished, swallowed by a tidal wave of pride. How dare this peasant, this unwashed shepherd from the dark woods, mock her? He had not brought a wonder; he had brought a trick, a cheap illusion meant to embarrass her in front of her court. He had made her look weak. He had made her look ordinary.
