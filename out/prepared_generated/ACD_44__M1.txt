It was upon a raw and gusty afternoon in the late autumn that a stranger presented himself at my consulting-room in Cavendish Square. The day had been of the sort which makes London a misery to those with any fondness for open air—a persistent drizzle that gave the streets a greasy sheen and a sky of the colour of a wet slate. My practice, though growing, was not yet so robust that I could afford to turn away a patient, and yet the man who stood upon my threshold was a puzzle from the very first.

He was tall and lean, with a face the colour of old parchment and a pair of eyes so pale and washed-out that they seemed to have been bleached by some fiercer sun than our own. His clothes were of a good but ill-kept cut; his boots were caked with a clay that was certainly not the product of a London pavement. He carried a canvas kit-bag, and when he removed his hat, I saw a deep, puckered scar that ran from his hairline to the corner of his left eye.

“Dr. Alistair Vance?” he inquired, in a voice that held the dry rasp of the desert.

“The same,” said I. “Pray come in, sir. You are suffering, I take it, from some malady?”

He entered, but did not sit. He stood by the mantelpiece, his pale eyes fixed upon me with a disconcerting intensity. “A malady of the memory, Doctor. A malady of a debt unpaid. You do not know me, but I know you. You were the sawbones at the field hospital near Fort Sabine, in the summer of ’80. I was one of your patients. A gunshot wound to the shoulder, from a Ghilzai jezail. You saved my arm, and very nearly my life.”

I confess that the name of Fort Sabine sent a shiver through me. It had been a wretched, sun-baked outpost on the North-West Frontier, and the heat, the flies, and the endless procession of broken men had left their mark upon me. Yet I could place no face to this man. The casualties had been many, and in the fever of that time, one man’s agony often blurred into another’s.

“I am sorry, sir,” I said, with genuine regret. “The conditions were chaotic. I treated hundreds of men. I fear I cannot recall you.”

A flicker of something—anger, or perhaps disappointment—crossed his gaunt features. “I do not ask for gratitude. I ask for a service. There is a thing buried near the fort. A thing of great value to me. I must have it recovered. I cannot go myself; I am watched. I have not the funds for a party of men. But you, Doctor, you know the country. You know the landmarks. The old Banyan tree, the dry riverbed, the officer’s cemetery with the cracked headstone. You can find the spot. I will pay you handsomely for your trouble.”

His request was as mad as it was impossible. The idea of returning to that blighted land to dig up some buried trinket was absurd. But it was not the absurdity that troubled me most; it was the man’s manner, the feverish light in his eyes, and the vague yet insistent claim upon my past. I gave him a polite refusal, stating that my practice kept me in London and that I had no intention of returning to the frontier. He argued for a full ten minutes, his voice rising in pitch, until I was forced to ring for my landlady to show him out.

When he had gone, leaving a lingering scent of dust and carbolic soap, I felt a profound unease. The man was not merely a fantasist; there was a desperate, almost menacing purpose about him. That evening, finding my mind too agitated for the quiet company of my books, I took a hansom to Baker Street.

Sherlock Holmes was in his favourite attitude upon the hearthrug, one long, thin leg cocked over the arm of his chair, while he studied a chemical reaction in a series of test-tubes. The air in the room was thick with the fumes of some acrid reagent. He did not look up as I entered, but merely remarked, “A patient from the Empire, I see. Afghanistan, to be precise. The man’s boots were of a type issued to the Indian Army, and the dust upon them, though caked with London mud, has the distinct reddish hue of the soil near Kandahar.”

I stared at him in astonishment. “You have not even seen him! He left my rooms three hours ago.”

“Elementary, my dear Vance,” said he, with a thin smile. “You have the look of a man who has been confronted with a ghost. Your military service is a matter of record, and the only theatre of recent conflict which would produce such a specimen is the North-West Frontier. But tell me the particulars. The evidence of the eyes is always more interesting than that of the boots.”

I related the whole strange business: the man’s appearance, his claim to have been my patient at Fort Sabine, his talk of a buried object, and his insistence that I accompany him to recover it.

Holmes listened with his eyes closed, his fingers steepled beneath his chin. When I had finished, he sprang to his feet and began to pace the room with the restless energy of a panther.

“A buried treasure!” he cried. “How delightfully melodramatic! And the key to it is a memory you do not possess. A clever device, my friend. A very clever device indeed. He does not want your medical knowledge; he wants your authority. He wants a man who can vouch for him, a man who knows the ground and can lend an air of legitimacy to his expedition. This is no treasure hunt, Vance. This is a retrieval.”
