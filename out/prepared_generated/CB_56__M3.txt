The rain lashed the cobblestones of Whinbury with a fury that seemed personal, as though the heavens themselves sought to chastise the town for harboring so stubborn a sedition. Inside the parlor of Mrs. Thorne's Academy for Young Ladies, however, the fire burned with a hard, bright rigor, casting long shadows across the threadbare Turkey carpet. There, by the hearth, sat Miss Margaret Hale—no relation to the heroine of any southern tale, but a namesake of equally resolute and unyielding northern spirit. She was folding garments into a trunk. The room was close, smelling of damp wool and burning peat. Margaret watched the fire, her hands pausing over a shawl. She felt the weight of the coming voyage, a vast, hungry ocean that would swallow her whole and spit her out upon a foreign shore. New Zealand. The word itself sounded like a blow. She was twenty-five, an age by which a woman without fortune or family is expected to have accepted her sentence. She had been a teacher at Mrs. Thorne's for seven years. She had watched her own youth drain into the inkwells of indifferent scholars. She would not watch her maturity do the same. She had answered an advertisement in the Leeds Mercury. She had been accepted. She was to sail from Liverpool in three days. And now, on this wild Thursday evening, she sat in the parlor of the only home she had known since girlhood, awaiting the arrival of her old schoolmistress, Mrs. Thorne herself, who had summoned her with a note.

"Come to me at seven. We must speak before you go."

The note had been brief, unyielding. It was Mrs. Thorne's way. Margaret had not seen her former mistress in nearly a year. The old woman had retired to a cottage on the edge of the moor, leaving the academy in the hands of her niece, a woman of tepid piety and meager intellect. Margaret had heard that Mrs. Thorne's health was failing. She had heard also that the old woman's tongue had not failed, nor her eye, nor her judgment.

At half past seven the door opened, and Mrs. Thorne entered. She was thinner than Margaret remembered, her face drawn tight across the bones, her dark eyes burning with a feverish, inward light. She wore her black bombazine and her shawl pinned at the throat with a severity that defied the cold. She did not smile. She looked at the trunk, then at Margaret, then at the fire.

"So," she said. "You are going."

"I am going."

"To the antipodes. To savages and ferns and mud."

"To a school. To a position. To a life, Mrs. Thorne."

The old woman lowered herself into the chair opposite. Her movements were stiff, proud, pained. She did not ask for tea. She did not remove her bonnet. She sat like a sentinel, her gloved hands folded on her knee.

"A life," she repeated, and the word carried the weight of an indictment. "And what life is that? A life of exile. A life of banishment. You will die there, Margaret. You will die among strangers, and they will bury you in foreign earth, and no one who knew your mother will weep at your grave."

Margaret's hands tightened on the shawl she was folding. She felt the old wound open, the one she had carried since she was twelve years old, standing in this very hallway, holding a valise, watching her father walk away into the rain and never return. She swallowed against the ache.

"I will not die of boredom, at any rate," she said quietly. "I will not die of watching other girls live the lives I was denied."

Mrs. Thorne's eyes flashed. "You are bitter."

"I am honest. I have earned the right to be honest."

"You have earned nothing of the kind. You have been fed, housed, educated, employed. You have been given more than most women in this county dare dream of. And you repay it by fleeing to the other side of the earth."

"Fleeing!" Margaret stood. The fire threw her shadow tall and sharp against the wall. "I am not fleeing. I am choosing. There is a difference, and you know it, ma'am. You taught me the difference yourself, though you may regret the lesson now."

The old woman's lips thinned. For a moment, neither spoke. The wind pressed against the windowpanes. The fire shifted and settled. Then Mrs. Thorne removed her gloves, slowly, finger by finger, and laid them on the table beside her.

"I was twenty-two when I opened this school," she said. Her voice had changed. The sharpness had not gone, but something else had entered it, something raw and unguarded, like a stone turned over to reveal the damp earth beneath. "Twenty-two. I had nothing. My father was dead. My mother was in a workhouse. I had a certificate from a second-rate institution and thirty pounds saved from five years of governessing. I took this house. I taught. I starved. I paid my mother's keep until she died. I never married. I never traveled. I never once set foot outside Yorkshire. Do you understand me? Not once."

Margaret sat down. The fire hissed.

"I could have gone," Mrs. Thorne continued. "In eighteen thirty-two, there was a colony in Van Diemen's Land seeking a schoolmistress. I had the letter. I held it in my hands for three days. I burned it on the fourth."

"Why?"

The question hung between them. Mrs. Thorne looked into the fire. Her face was very still.

"Because I was afraid," she said. "Because I was afraid, and because I believed that if I held fast to what I had, however small, however wretched, God would reward my constancy. I believed that sacrifice was its own justification. I was wrong." She turned to Margaret. Her eyes were bright and wet. "I was wrong, and I have known it every day for twenty-three years."
