At twenty-three, Julian Devereux was considered an authority on beauty. He had spent his formative years cultivating an aesthetic sensibility that bordered on religion. His rooms in London were draped in sun-bleached velvets, filled with lilies, and lit by candles of the purest beeswax, all arranged to frame the various beautiful young men and women he kept as companions. Beauty, he believed, was a moral imperative. Thus, when his friend, Lord Ashford, invited him to a fortnight at Blackwood Hall to meet a woman of unparalleled intellect, Julian accepted only because the Ashford library was famous, and he assumed the woman would be an ornament of a different, but equally acceptable, kind.

He arrived at Blackwood Hall on an afternoon thick with the scent of damp earth and dying leaves. The house party was a familiar tableau of languid aristocrats draped over chintz sofas, their conversations revolving around who was wearing what, and who was sleeping with whom. Julian was profoundly bored. On the third evening, he was introduced to Miss Clara Hartwell. He felt a sharp, involuntary recoil. She was painfully plain. Her hair was a mousy brown, scraped back in a bun so severe it pulled at the corners of her eyes. Her nose was too long, her chin too pointed, and her dress was a shapeless, drab serge that hung on her frame like a sack. She wore spectacles that magnified her pale eyes to an almost amphibian degree. Julian, ever the aesthete, murmured a polite greeting and immediately turned his attention to the breathtaking Venetian glass vase on the mantelpiece.

The next morning, Julian found himself seated beside Miss Hartwell at breakfast. The other guests were sluggish, their minds clouded from the previous night's port. Julian buttered a piece of toast with delicate precision, preparing to endure a tedious meal. "I see you admiring the glass," Clara said, her voice surprisingly low and resonant. Julian glanced at her, startled that she had noticed. "It is Murano, is it not? Sixteenth century," he offered. "It is," she replied. "But I find myself more interested in the imperfection at its base. See, there is a bubble in the glass, trapped during the firing. It makes the whole structure fragile, compromises its symmetry. It is a flaw that makes it mortal." Julian stared at her. No one had ever spoken of the vase in such a way. He looked at the base of the glass, saw the tiny imprisoned sphere of air, and felt a strange thrill. "You think beauty is lessened by fragility?" he asked. "I think beauty is a tyrant," she said, slicing a pear with a decisive motion. "It demands worship and offers nothing in return but a fleeting sensation. A mind, however, offers a universe."

Over the following days, Julian discovered that Clara was the daughter of a deceased botanist who had educated herself in her father's library. While the other guests played whist and gossiped, Julian sought her out. They walked in the gardens, and she spoke of the classification of mosses, of the intricate, hidden architectures of lichen. She dissected the poetry of Browning with a surgical precision that left him breathless. She did not simper or flatter; she challenged him. When he declared that Keats was the pinnacle of human achievement, she argued that Keats was a slave to sensation, that his odes were beautiful but ultimately empty vessels, lacking the structural rigor of Milton. Julian was incensed, then captivated. He realized, with a growing horror, that he was never bored in her presence. The realization unsettled him. She was not beautiful. She was the antithesis of everything he had worshipped. Yet, when she was not near, the world seemed a dull, monochrome place, and the beautiful young men in his London rooms seemed like hollow, chattering dolls.

The crisis came during a parlor game one evening. Lord Ashford, amused by Clara's sharp tongue, had proposed a game of wits. The guests were in high spirits, the firelight casting flattering shadows on aristocratic faces. Clara, however, sat slightly apart, her plainness accentuated by the opulent surroundings. Julian watched her, feeling a peculiar tightness in his chest. After the game, Lord Ashford drew Julian aside in the gallery. "My dear boy," Ashford began, swirling his brandy, "I must warn you. You are spending an inordinate amount of time with the Hartwell woman." Julian stiffened. "She is the only person in this house capable of discussing anything beyond the cut of a coat," he said. Ashford raised an eyebrow. "Come now, Julian. She is a curiosity, I grant you. But you have a reputation. People are beginning to talk. You are seen as a connoisseur of the exquisite. To be seen favoring such a woman is to invite ridicule."

Julian returned to London in a state of acute agitation. He threw himself back into his aesthetic life, surrounding himself with beautiful things and beautiful people. He attended a soiree at the home of the Honourable Vivienne Sterling, a woman whose face was so symmetrical it had been painted by three Royal Academicians. Vivienne lounged on a chaise, her golden hair spilling over a cushion of jade silk. Her eyes were like violets, her skin like cream. She was, by any objective measure, a masterpiece. Julian sat beside her and attempted a conversation about the nature of art. Vivienne smiled, a slow, practiced smile, and said she found art terribly confusing, but that she did adore a painting of a dog she had seen at the Academy. Julian felt a wave of despair. He looked at her perfect face, her perfect skin, her perfect hair, and saw nothing. It was as if he had been staring at the sun for years, and only now, in the shade, did he realize he was blind. He excused himself and walked home through the foggy London streets, his mind racing.
