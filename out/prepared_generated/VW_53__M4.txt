The lanterns had been hung, rather crookedly, from the boughs of the old pear tree, and their light—a soft, wavering gold, the colour of honey or of candle-flame seen through a glass of wine—fell in broken patches upon the lawn, upon the white cloths of the tables, upon the shoulders of the guests who moved and murmured there, and upon the dark, glossy leaves of the laurels at the garden’s edge. Inside the house, someone was playing the piano; the notes came out to her, thin and separate, each one a little globe of sound that floated for a moment and then dissolved into the general evening. Mrs. Ashworth, standing at the foot of the stone steps, with her hand upon the cool, rough balustrade, felt the music rather than heard it, felt it as a vibration in the air, a faint, persistent trembling that seemed to be a part of the dusk itself, of the scent of the stocks and the tobacco plant, of the distant, intermittent laughter that rose and fell like the tide.

Fifty years. The words, unspoken, formed themselves somewhere in the back of her mind, and she let them stay there, not examining them, not turning them over, but merely conscious of their weight, their strange, accumulated heaviness. Fifty years since she had stood in another garden, in another county, with the smell of rain on the earth and the rooks calling in the elms, and had looked at the ring upon her finger and wondered—ah, but that was a long time ago, and the wondering had long since ceased to have any edge to it. It had become a habit, almost a comfort, like the ache in her knee that told her when the weather was about to change. She had married Edward, and they had had the children, and the children had had children of their own, and now here they all were, gathered in this garden in Hampstead, under these crooked lanterns, to celebrate a thing that had begun so quietly, so uncertainly, in that other garden, fifty years ago.

A figure detached itself from the group upon the lawn and came towards her—her daughter, Margaret, with a shawl about her shoulders and a glass in her hand.

“Mother, you’re not cold? You’ve been standing here for ages. What are you thinking about?”

“Nothing, my dear. Nothing at all. The evening, merely. It’s very beautiful, don’t you think?”

Margaret looked at her with that quick, searching glance which mothers know so well, and which daughters, in their turn, learn to use; then she smiled, and said something about the lanterns being a great success, and drifted back to her guests. The lanterns were a success. They had been Edward’s idea—Edward, who at seventy-eight still had ideas, still made plans, still believed that the world might be arranged, if one only took sufficient pains, into a pattern of comfort and order. He had spent the whole afternoon directing the gardener’s boy, who had held the ladder and looked bewildered, while Edward, with the string between his teeth and his spectacles slipping down his nose, had measured and adjusted and tied knots that would not slip. And now the lanterns hung, a little crookedly, it was true, but they hung, and their light was very beautiful.

She moved away from the steps, down the gravel path that led towards the summer-house, and as she went she passed the open window of the drawing-room, and the music came out to her more clearly now, a waltz, something sentimental and old-fashioned, and she saw, through the glass, the shapes of the dancers, the young ones chiefly, moving in a kind of slow, formal happiness, and among them, sitting in the corner with a cup of tea upon her knee, her sister-in-law, Harriet, who had never married, who had lived with them for thirty years, and who now watched the dancers with a small, fixed smile, as though she were remembering something, or perhaps regretting something, or perhaps merely thinking of the price of coal.

The summer-house was dark, and smelt of damp wood and of the clematis that had been trained over it. She sat down upon the bench—the same bench, she remembered, upon which Edward had proposed to her, not in this garden, but in another, long since sold and built over, and she had said yes, because—because why? Because he had been kind, and steady, and because her mother had said that he would make a good husband, and because, she supposed, she had been afraid of something else, of the other thing, of the wild, unreasonable, impossible thing that had seemed, for a few months, for a few weeks, to be the whole world.

His name was Stephen. Stephen Rennie. He had been a painter—not a successful one, not a man who would ever have been able to offer her a house in Hampstead, with a garden and a pear tree and lanterns hanging crookedly from the boughs—but a painter, with a studio in Chelsea and a way of looking at things, at the light upon a wall, at the curve of a woman’s arm, at the grey-green of the river at dusk, that had made her feel, for the first time in her life, that she was seeing the world, actually seeing it, and not merely passing through it. He had had a temper, and no money, and a habit of disappearing for days at a time, and once, she remembered, he had come to her with his hands shaking and his eyes bright and strange, and had said that he had sold a picture, and that now they could be married, and she had looked at him and known, with a clarity that was like a pain, that she could not do it, that she could not live in that uncertainty, that she was not brave enough, or perhaps not foolish enough, to take that particular leap.
