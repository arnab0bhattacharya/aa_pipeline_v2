It was upon the fourth day of my ascent that I first beheld the workings at San Geronimo. The mule trail had long since ended in a chaos of shattered shale, and I had proceeded on foot, clambering over the frozen scree with a vexation that the directors in London had not adequately prepared me for. I am an engineer, concerned with stresses, drainage, and the practical extraction of minerals, and I have little patience for romantic scenery. Yet even I was struck by the desolate grandeur of the place. The mountain hung immense and sterile against a sky of hard, metallic blue. There was no vegetation, no sign of life, save for the wooden derrick and the snow-smothered outbuildings that marked the entrance to the mine.

I had been sent by the board to report upon the deteriorating output of the San Geronimo copper lode. The previous manager, a man of considerable practical experience, had died some months prior under circumstances of considerable ambiguity, and the property had reverted to its owner, a Professor Silas Vane. Vane was a figure known vaguely to the scientific societies of Europe, a recluse who had purchased the derelict mine some five years previously and, contrary to all commercial advice, elected to live there. I was instructed to inspect the workings, ascertain why the copper shipments had dwindled to nothing, and recommend whether the enterprise should be abandoned.

I was met at the head of the shaft by a singularly unpleasant fellow named Gripp, a half-caste who acted as Vane’s general factotum. He led me without ceremony into the mountain. The first level was as I expected: crude but sound timbering, a stagnant seepage of water, and the ordinary disorder of an abandoned working. But as we descended the secondary shaft, I perceived a peculiar alteration in the character of the rock.

The solid grey andesite was marked by a network of deep, interlacing grooves, as if some gigantic worm had burrowed through the stone. And upon the walls, in these excavated hollows, there clung a substance that at first I took to be a mineral deposit. It was a dull, slate-coloured mould, possessing a curious, almost metallic lustre. I scraped at it with my knife. It yielded with a spongy, fleshy resistance, and from the cut there oozed a thin, greenish fluid that hissed faintly against the blade.

Gripp observed my astonishment with a sort of malicious satisfaction. “The Professor is waiting below,” he said gruffly. “You will see.”

We descended another hundred feet. Here the transformation of the mine was staggering. The rock had been wholly devoured across a vast chamber. The walls, the roof, the very floor beneath our feet, were coated in a thick, undulating mat of this grey fungus. It grew in vast, pulpy fronds, rising in ridges and cascades, and the air was thick with a stifling, sweetish odour of decay. In the glare of my safety lamp, the whole surface of the stuff seemed to palpitate, a sickening, microscopic quivering of countless spores.

In the midst of this extraordinary subterranean garden stood Professor Vane.

He was a tall, lean man, stooped and prematurely aged, with a singularly eager, nervous face. His hands and clothes were smeared with the greenish slime. He advanced to meet me with an outstretched hand, his eyes burning with a feverish intelligence.

"You are the engineer from London," he said. "I am very glad you have come. I have been expecting you. I have need of an engineer."

"I am here to inspect the copper lode," I replied, with what I hope was a suitable firmness. "I find no copper, Professor. I find this."

I gestured to the encroaching fungus. He beamed at me with the pride of a father.

"Precisely," he said. "You find the *Fomes metallivorus*. The copper is gone, my dear sir. Consumed. Digested. Transmuted into living tissue. Do you not see the magnitude of this achievement?"

I confess I did not see it. I saw a mine rendered worthless and a dangerous, unnatural growth spreading through the mountain. I told him as much, with a considerable display of irritation.

He waved his hand impatiently. "You think in terms of pounds and shillings, of copper ingots and dividends. I think in terms of the future! For twenty years I sought an organism capable of breaking down the silicates and oxides of the heavy metals. I tried radium, I tried electrolysis, I tried culture after culture in my laboratories in Zurich. And here, in the geothermal warmth of the Andes, I have succeeded. This fungus feeds upon the rock itself. It extracts the metal, renders it into a pure, organic compound. It is the ultimate miner, needing no wages, no timber, no explosives. It will render all human labour obsolete."

"It is rendering your mine obsolete," I observed drily.

"A temporary inconvenience," he said, though I thought I detected a flicker of anxiety in his eyes. "The process must be controlled. That is why I need you. I need an engineer to construct barriers, to regulate the humidity, to confine the growth to specific veins. We must keep this secret, of course. The world is not ready for such a revelation. The great mining cartels would destroy us. But together, you and I, we could refine the process. We could be masters of the earth's mineral wealth!"

I looked about the chamber. The fungus had spread across the floor to the very edge of the shaft. I noticed that the wooden supports were already softened and rotting where the stuff had touched them, the wood fibres dissolving into the same greyish pulp.

"You have let it loose," I said. "It is spreading beyond the lode."

"It is merely seeking new substrate," Vane replied, a trifle hastily. "It can be contained."

"Can it?" I said. "I observe it is eating the timbering. If it can digest lignin as readily as andesite, there is no telling where it will stop. It is a biological fire."
