The news came down from the moor-top just as the second dinner hooter was sounding, and it came not as a rumour but as a red glare in the northwest, so that the folk in the streets of Barrowden, standing with their basins in their hands, could see the smoke rolling up in a great slow curtain against the slate-coloured sky. There had been a shower of stones, they said—falling stars, some called them, though they fell in the afternoon—and the heather was alight in a dozen places, and the wind was rising.

Joshua Mowbray, who owned the Albion Mill and three hundred looms and the row of cottages in Water Lane, heard it from his foreman at the gate and did not at first believe it. He was a man of fifty-five, stout and clean-shaven, with a watch-chain that he fingered when he was doubtful, and he had a great faith in the order of things—in rates, in ledgers, in the steady turning of the wheels. A shower of stones from heaven was not in his ledger.

“Fire on the moor,” said the foreman, whose name was Pilling. “Bad, sir. It’s in the ling, and there’s a wind.”

“Have you sent the men?”

“The hands are going up now, sir. There’s nobody to lead them.”

Mowbray went out into the yard. The hands were coming from the weaving-sheds in their clogs and shawls, a straggling dark stream of them, and the women were talking all at once, and the men were quieter, looking at the glow. It was a strange light to see at three in the afternoon, and it made the tall chimney of the Albion stand out black against it like a finger raised in warning. Somebody laughed, and the laugh sounded wrong.

“Where’s Hinchcliffe?” said Mowbray.

“Hinchcliffe’s sick,” said Pilling. “And there’s none of the under-overlookers will go up. They say it’s no work of theirs, and the moor’s not mill property.”

“It’s the watershed,” said Mowbray. “If the moor burns to the edge, the beck will be foul for a month, and we’ll have no water for the boilers, and there’s the whole town’s supply in the reservoir. It’s everybody’s property tonight.”

He said it loudly, but the men were drifting away up the lane already, in twos and threes, with no more order than sheep, and the women calling after them. And then, at the corner where the lane turns up by the quarry, a young man jumped on to the low stone wall and turned about, and the stragglers stopped to hear him.

His name was Daniel Rigg, and Mowbray knew him well enough—too well. He was one of the strikers of the spring, a weaver of the new shed, twenty-eight or thereabouts, with a thin brown face and a way of looking at you as though he were measuring you for something. He had been blacklisted in the spring, and he had been blacklisted, Mowbray was aware, chiefly because Mowbray himself had said that a man who would not sign was a man who would not work, and that the trade would be better without him. He had been seen about the town since, in the reading-room and at the chapel, and there was talk that he had a following.

“Now then,” Rigg called out, and his voice carried clean and hard over the noise of the clogs. “You can go up anyhow and run about with wet sacks and get your boots burnt, or you can go up in parties with a captain to each, and fight it where it can be fought. It’s a wind from the southwest, and it’ll run the ling to the beck in an hour unless we get a break at the Delf and another at the Grey Stones. There’s forty of us here and more coming. I want ten men with me on the Delf, and ten under somebody at the Grey Stones, and the rest to beat the low edge by the quarry where the bracken’s green. Who’ll come?”

There was a moment’s silence. The men looked at one another, and then a big, slow fellow named Tattersall said, “I’ll go with thee, Dan,” and after that they went quickly enough, and in a minute the lane was emptying up the hill in two parties, and Rigg was standing on the wall with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, giving out what he wanted in short, plain words, as though he had done nothing else all his life.

Mowbray stood in the yard and watched them go, and Pilling watched him.

“He’s a blacklisted man, sir,” said Pilling, doubtfully.

“I know what he is,” said Mowbray.

“There’s some would say it’s not for him to be giving orders in a town where he’s been stood off.”

“There’s some would say a good deal,” said Mowbray. He fingered his watch-chain. “Pilling, I’m going up.”

“You’ll not go up, sir. Your chest—”

“I’m going up,” said Mowbray, “and you’ll come with me, and we’ll take the mill cart and the two dozen sacks from the lower shed and the barrels of the fire-stuff from the yard. And Pilling—”

“Sir?”

“If I’m to be of any use up there, I’m to be under him. Do you understand me? Under him. I’ll not have two captains on a burning moor.”

Pilling’s face was a picture. “Mr. Mowbray, the men—”

“The men will do what’s wanted if it’s wanted plainly,” said Mowbray. “It’s not a question of the mill now. It’s the beck and the reservoir and the town. Get the cart.”

They went up the lane in the cart, and the light grew and the smell of burning came down to them, sweet and pungent, the smell of the ling and the peat, and once a great moth of a stone went over their heads with a hiss and fell somewhere beyond the crest, and Pilling ducked.
