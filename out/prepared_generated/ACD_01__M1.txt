It was in the autumn of ’93, a season of persistent drizzle and greasy fogs, that the singular case of Mr. Silas Croft first came to my attention. I was, at that time, a junior clerk at the Cornhill & Metropolitan Bank, a position of considerable tedium but one which afforded an excellent observation post for the endless pageant of the City. My duties were largely confined to the ledgers and the paying-in counter, and it was from this latter station that I had noted, over many weeks, the peculiar habit of an elderly customer.

His name, as I learned from the pass-book he presented, was Silas Croft, and his address was given as a lodging-house in the less salubrious reaches of Clerkenwell. Each morning, at the stroke of ten when our doors opened, Mr. Croft would enter. He was a small, stooped figure, perpetually clad in a threadbare black overcoat that seemed a size too large for his shrunken frame. His face was a mask of pale, papery skin stretched over fine bones, and his eyes, of a watery blue, held a look of perpetual, anxious inquiry. In his hand, he would clutch a single gold sovereign.

The ritual was always the same. He would approach my counter, push the coin forward with a tremulous forefinger, and wait in silence while I entered the deposit in his book. “One sovereign, Mr. Croft,” I would say, to which he would only give a slight, jerky nod. Then he would depart, leaving behind him the faint scent of damp wool and camphor.

At precisely three o’clock in the afternoon, as the daylight began to fail, he would return. He would present his book, and I would count out the very same sovereign—which I could often identify by a small nick upon the Queen’s profile—and hand it back to him. He would secure it in a small leather pouch, nod again, and vanish into the crowded street. Day after day, the transaction was repeated. My superiors, men of practical and unimaginative minds, dismissed it as the harmless eccentricity of an old man with a fixed income and a strange devotion to his gold. But to me, there was something strained and desperate in the performance. It was not the act of a man indulging a whim, but of one engaged in a duty of the utmost gravity.

One Tuesday in late October, the routine was broken. Ten o’clock came and went, and Mr. Croft did not appear. I found myself watching the door with a strange sense of unease. By noon, my unease had hardened into a certainty that something was amiss. The absence of the man was more telling than his presence had ever been. That evening, I left the bank with a resolution that surprised even myself. My weekly wage was a modest one, but I possessed a small sum of savings, and I felt compelled to spend a portion of it in pursuit of an answer. I had read of the private inquiry agents who operated in the city, and the following morning, I made my way to a narrow, soot-stained court off Fleet Street.

The brass plate beside the door read simply: ‘A. J. Blackwood, Private Inquiries’. The man who answered my knock was tall and spare, with a sharp, intelligent face and eyes that seemed to take in every detail of my person and attire in a single, swift glance. He wore a neat grey suit and his hands, long-fingered and immaculate, were those of a scholar or a musician.

“Mr. Blackwood?” I inquired.

“The same,” he replied, his voice crisp and devoid of affectation. “And you are a clerk from the Cornhill & Metropolitan Bank, troubled by a missing depositor. Pray, come in.”

I was, I confess, taken aback. “How could you possibly know that?”

A faint smile touched his lips. “A simple deduction. The mud on your boots is the particular grey clay of the Bank’s courtyard. Your coat is of a quality worn by junior staff of financial institutions. Your expression is one of anxious responsibility, and you have come to a private agent at an early hour, suggesting a matter of personal concern rather than official business. The most likely cause for such concern in a man of your station is a disruption in his daily routine. The missing depositor is the most logical conclusion. Now, pray, be seated and tell me the particulars.”

I laid the whole affair before him, from the first sovereign to the final, ominous absence. He listened without interruption, his long fingers steepled before him. When I had finished, he was silent for a moment.

“Eccentricity is the cloak of many a strange business,” he mused. “A sovereign a day. It is not a large sum, yet it is a significant one for a man of evidently limited means. It was a bond, you see. A daily testament.”

“A testament to what?” I asked.

“That is the question,” said Blackwood, rising and taking his hat from a peg. “Let us not theorise in the abstract when we can observe the facts. You have his address?”

An hour later, we were standing before a grimy lodging-house in a narrow, ill-kept street in Clerkenwell. The landlady, a stout woman with a suspicious eye, informed us that Mr. Croft had not been seen since the previous morning. “Paid his rent regular, he did,” she grumbled. “Quiet as a mouse. But he’s gone, and his room is locked.”

Blackwood’s gaze fell upon the door at the head of the stairs. “Locked, you say? I think we shall require the assistance of the local constabulary.” A short while later, a burly police sergeant had forced the lock with his shoulder.

The room was small and sparsely furnished. A narrow bed, a rickety table, and a single chair. The air was cold and stale. On the table sat a half-eaten loaf of bread and a copy of the previous day’s *Times*. It was the floor, however, that commanded our attention. A section of the floorboards near the window was stained with a dark, brownish blotch. The sergeant bent to examine it. “Blood,” he said grimly.
