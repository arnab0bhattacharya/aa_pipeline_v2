If the firm of Gobble, Snatch, and Co. — a house of ancient respectability situated at the corner of Threadneedle Street, where the very counting-house stools seemed to absorb a sanctified aroma of ledgers, ink, and safe deposits — had ever entertained a design of sending a confidential clerk into foreign parts, that clerk would assuredly not have been young Mr. Timothy Quirk. He was a young man of a particularly mild and vapoury disposition, endowed with a waistcoat of modest pattern, a hat that seemed to apologize for its own existence, and a pair of eyes that looked out upon the world with a perpetual expression of gentle astonishment, as if they were perpetually expecting the ink-pots to stage a rebellion and the brass scales to fly out of the window. Yet it was Timothy Quirk, and no other, who found himself jolted and rattled and bounced upon the decaying roads of northern France in the wet autumn of the year of grace one thousand seven hundred and eighty-nine, charged with the highly delicate, not to say hopeless, errand of recovering the portable assets of the Marquis de Roche-Ambon, a nobleman of ancient lineage and lately of a very hasty departure.

The Marquis had fled, as all the world knew, and his chateau, a great sprawling pile of grey stone that loomed over the village of Pouille-en-Vexin like a sleeping giant afflicted with a nightmarish case of damp rot, was said to be occupied only by his steward. This steward, Monsieur Bouviers, had been left in charge of the estate, and Timothy, bearing in his breast-pocket certain imposing legal documents adorned with wax seals that looked immensely official and meant precisely nothing to a hungry peasant, was directed to present himself, demand the keys to the strong-boxes, and make a general accounting. The morning Timothy arrived, the sky was a repository of wet ash, drizzling upon the cracked turrets of the chateau and running down the grimy windows in tears, as if the very heavens were weeping for the state of French property. The village below was a picture of profound and watchful hunger. The villagers stood in their doorways, their faces drawn and sharp, their eyes following the stranger’s chaise with a fixed and quiet intensity that made Timothy feel as though he were a particularly plump partridge who had just wandered into a convention of foxes.

Upon alighting in the weedy courtyard, Timothy was met by Monsieur Bouviers himself. He was a man of broad shoulders, a thick neck, and a waistcoat that strained at the buttons, being clearly designed for a lesser steward and stretched to accommodate a greater. He wore the late Marquis’s second-best velvet coat, which was a trifle too long in the sleeves, and he carried a silver-headed cane that he twirled with an air of absolute and unchallengeable ownership. He looked, in short, not like a man guarding another’s property, but like a man who has just signed the deed and is daring the ink to dry.

“Monsieur!” cried Bouviers, sweeping off a plumed hat that also belonged to the Marquis. “You bring the weather of England with you, hein? But enter, enter! The Chateau de Roche-Ambon, though it weeps for its master, still knows how to offer a glass of wine to a guest!”

Timothy, who had expected to be met with furtive resistance, found himself ushered into a vast hall, up a sweeping staircase, and into a dining-room where a fire roared in a fireplace vast enough to roast an ox. The table was laid with silver candlesticks of ancient and elaborate design, and the wine which Bouviers poured was of a vintage that made Timothy’s mild eyes water with its opulence. The steward, meanwhile, talked incessantly, his voice booming off the panelled walls, his hands gesturing wildly to encompass the tapestries, the paintings, and the very rafters of the building.

“A sad business! A most sad business!” Bouviers declaimed, striking the table. “The Marquis, a great man, a noble man, but perhaps a little hasty, a little — how you say — impulsive! He goes! Pouf! In the night! And he leaves me, his humble servant, to guard the nest, to keep the wolves from the door! And such wolves, Monsieur! Such wolves!” Here he rolled his eyes dramatically toward the window, indicating the village below. “They would pull the very slates from the roof to build their hovels, if I did not stand firm! But I am here! I am the rock! Bouviers is the rock!”

Timothy, sipping the wine and feeling it was not his place to comment on the rock-like qualities of the steward, produced his legal documents and cleared his throat. “The house of Gobble, Snatch, and Co., sir,” he began, “acting as agents for the Marquis, has empowered me to — that is to say, I am to take an inventory, and to secure certain deposits —”

“Ah, the deposits!” Bouviers waved a hand as if swatting a fly. “The deposits! Yes, yes! They are safe, Monsieur! As safe as the beard on my chin! I have them under lock and key, in the strong-room below! But what is a deposit, Monsieur, when the roof is leaking and the peasants are sharpening their scythes? I have had to — how you say — to manage! To make adjustments! The silver you see upon the table, it is but a fraction of what remains. The rest, I have hidden! Yes! Hidden from the Jacobins, from the Republicans, from the very birds of the air! I am the guardian! Bouviers is the guardian!”

Timothy, whose head was beginning to spin, both from the wine and from the steward’s volubility, suggested that perhaps a viewing of the strong-room would be in order. Bouviers consented with a theatrical bow, and led the way down a damp stone corridor to a heavy oak door, which he unlocked with a key that hung from a chain about his neck.
