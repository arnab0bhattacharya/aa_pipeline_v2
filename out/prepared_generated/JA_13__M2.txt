It is a truth universally acknowledged, though perhaps not always admitted by the parties concerned, that a woman who prides herself on her indifference to vanity is often the most susceptible to its subtlest flatters. Mrs. Hartley of Elmbridge, a village in Hampshire of no particular renown save for the distinct regularity of its parish church and the unremarkable excellence of its turnips, had long cultivated the opinion that she was immune to the common follies of her sex. She was, by her own estimation, a creature of pure reason, unimpressed by finery, and wholly disinterested in the opinions of gentlemen who possessed more spirit than sense.

This self-assurance was tested, however, by the arrival of Mr. Julian Thorne. He was a man of London, visiting the country for his health, which was said to be delicate, though his complexion was as ruddy and his appetite as robust as any farmer in the parish. He was tall, well-made, and possessed of a voice that carried with an easy authority, qualities which Mrs. Hartley found distinctly agreeable, provided she did not dwell upon them too long.

For three weeks, Mr. Thorne made a practice of calling at the Hartley house. His conversations were light, his compliments measured, and his attention to Mrs. Hartley’s opinions on literature and local politics was such as to suggest a genuine intellectual interest. Mrs. Hartley, observing that he never inquired after her fortune or her brother’s estate, concluded with a smile of superior satisfaction that she had at last found a man who valued the mind above the purse. She told her sister, Mrs. Fairfax, who was a woman of less abstract virtue and more practical concern, that Mr. Thorne was “a man of unimpeachable integrity, entirely free from the mercenary spirit that infects our age.”

Mrs. Fairfax, who had observed Mr. Thorne’s eyes linger a moment too long on the new silk dress Mrs. Hartley wore to church, replied with a caution that her sister dismissed as mere jealousy. “You see too much, my dear,” said Mrs. Hartley. “Mr. Thorne is a gentleman of the old school. He does not court; he merely converses. There is a dignity in his reserve that one can only admire.”

The dignified reserve was, however, shattered one afternoon by the visit of Mr. and Mrs. Pemberton, elderly neighbors who had known the Hartley family for forty years. They arrived with a basket of apples and a story that left Mrs. Hartley’s face, which had been pale with the heat of the room, turning a shade paler still, or perhaps with a different sort of heat entirely.

“It is a piteous thing,” said Mrs. Pemberton, settling herself in the chair by the fire, “to see a young man of Mr. Thorne’s breeding so given to his wits. I heard it all, or enough of it, at the assembly in Winchester. There was a gentleman, a Mr. Galloway, who was dining with Mr. Thorne, and the conversation turned to the ladies of the neighborhood. Mr. Galloway, who is a man of very little discretion, mentioned your name, Mrs. Hartley. He said you were the most unapproachable woman in Hampshire, and that no man in his right mind would waste a season upon you. Mr. Thorne, who is always the soul of good humor, laughed and said, ‘I am prepared to stake twenty guineas that I shall have a declaration from Miss Hartley before the month is out, without saying a word that is not strictly polite.’”

Mrs. Hartley sat motionless, her hands folded tightly in her lap. The silence in the room was profound, broken only by the ticking of the mantel clock and the soft cough of Mr. Pemberton, who seemed suddenly very interested in the pattern of his own coat.

“Is this,” said Mrs. Hartley at last, her voice steady but lacking its usual warmth, “a joke, Mrs. Pemberton? I know Mr. Thorne to be a man of some levity, but I had not thought him capable of such a gross deception.”

Mrs. Pemberton shook her head gently. “It was no joke, my dear. And I fear Mr. Galloway was not the only one to overhear. The wager was taken. And I am told that Mr. Thorne has been quite successful in keeping his word, for he told Mr. Galloway this morning that the declaration was in his pocket, sealed and signed, though he had not yet shown it.”

Mrs. Hartley looked at her sister. Mrs. Fairfax had the grace to look as though she wished the floor would open and swallow her. “I am sorry, Elizabeth,” she said softly. “I should have warned you.”

“Warning!” said Mrs. Hartley, rising abruptly. “I have been warned by my own reason all along. I told you he was too smooth. I told you his compliments were too easy. But I was too proud to believe that a man would so openly trade in the feelings of another as if they were cards at a gaming table. I thought myself above such things. I thought my indifference would protect me from the need for flattery. And yet, when he spoke of my taste for poetry, I did not wonder if he had read the book. I thought he was a kindred spirit. How tedious and how ridiculous my vanity has been.”

She walked to the window and looked out at the garden, where the late autumn light was fading over the bare branches of the beech trees. It was a cold, clear light, and she found it more honest than the warmth of the drawing room.

“Will you see him again?” asked Mrs. Pemberton, with a cautious hope that the affair might be patched over.
