The birthday of Prince Ivor was kept with all the solemnity that a court, which had long since forgotten the reason for its own ceremonies, could muster. Bells were rung in the high towers of Aurelian, and the gardens were strewn with petals of the white rose, which was the flower of the royal house, though no one now could say why. The Prince, who had lived his eighteen years in the Lonely Tower, knew of the world only what his tutors had told him, and they, being men of great learning and no experience, had told him principally of the stars, which are far off, and of the virtues, which are farther still. He had never seen a beggar in the street, nor a merchant in his stall, nor any creature at all save the King his father, the ministers, the guards, and the gardeners who tended the roses below his window and never looked up.

Upon the morning of his birthday he was brought down the winding stair for the first time, and stood before the King in the great hall, and the King, who was a man of majestic presence and a most profound ignorance of everything beyond the palace walls, regarded him with a satisfaction that was almost tender.

‘My son,’ said the King, ‘thou art now eighteen years of age, and it is the custom of our house that on this day the heir shall ask of his father one thing, and it shall be granted, were it the half of the kingdom or the whole of it. Ask, therefore, and be not modest, for modesty is a virtue for those who have nothing to give.’

The Prince did not hesitate, for he had thought of nothing else for a year and a day.

‘Sire,’ said he, ‘I would see a poor man.’

There fell a silence in the hall, and the ministers looked at one another, and the King stroked his beard, which was the colour of a winter cloud, and frowned a little, not in anger but in perplexity.

‘A poor man?’ said the King. ‘Art thou certain thou dost not mean a golden cup, or a horse, or the hand of some princess? For I am told there are princesses in the world, though I have never met with one myself.’

‘I have cups enough,’ said the Prince, ‘and I have no wish for a horse, for where would I ride? And as for princesses, I am told they are like other ladies, only more so. But a poor man I have never seen, and I would see one, for my tutors tell me that poverty is the great fact of the world, and I would know what it looks like, that I may pity it properly.’

The King was pleased with this answer, for it sounded virtuous, and virtue in a son is a comfortable thing to a father who has never had occasion to test it.

‘It shall be done,’ said the King. ‘Ho, there! Let the ministers go forth into the city and bring hither a poor man, the poorest that may be found, and let him be brought before the Prince with all decency and speed.’

So the ministers went out, and they took with them the captain of the guard and twelve men, and they searched the city of Aurelian from the Street of the Silversmiths to the little square where the flower-sellers sit, and they found no poor man at all, for Aurelian was a prosperous city, and its poor were kept in a quarter beyond the river, which the ministers had never visited, as it was not on the way to anywhere they wished to go.

They returned at evening and said, ‘Sire, we have searched the city and found no poor man, but we have found a man who is somewhat shabby, and who complains of the price of candles, and him we have brought.’

And the man was brought in, and he was a chandler by trade, and he wore a coat that had been mended in three places, and he was exceedingly frightened, and he fell upon his knees and said, ‘I am a loyal subject, and I pay my taxes, and if there is any complaint against me I will mend it, for I am a chandler and I know my trade.’

The Prince looked at him with great curiosity, and then with some disappointment.

‘Is this a poor man?’ said he.

‘He is the shabbiest we could find,’ said the ministers.

‘But he is not poor,’ said the Prince. ‘He has a coat, and a trade, and he fears me, which is to say he has something to lose. A poor man should have nothing to lose, and should fear nothing, for fear is the tax that property pays. This man is merely uncomfortable.’

The King was troubled, for he had promised, and a king who does not keep his promise is a king who will soon be a subject of conversation.

‘Let the search be made again,’ said the King, ‘and let it be made in the quarter beyond the river, which I am told exists, though I have never seen it, and let no man return until he has found one who is truly poor.’

So the ministers went out again by night, and crossed the river in a boat, and came to the quarter beyond, where the streets were narrow and the lamps were few, and there they found a man sitting on a doorstep with his hands empty and his eyes fixed on nothing at all.

‘Come with us,’ said the ministers, ‘for the King commands it.’

And the man rose and went with them, for he had nothing else to do, and he was brought before the Prince in the great hall at midnight, and the candles were lit, and the court was assembled, and the King sat upon his throne with his crown upon his head, for he felt that an occasion of this sort demanded the crown.
