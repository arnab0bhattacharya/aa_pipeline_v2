The village of Pinchdean lies in a hollow of the South Downs, a place so entirely tucked away from the currents of modern life that the arrival of a bicycle is still an event of some remark. It was in the barn of the old mill, which stands above the village, that young Arthur Verrinder constructed his flying machine. He was an engineer by training, having served an apprenticeship in the works at Portsmouth, and he possessed that practical turn of mind which takes a thing apart, notes its principles, and puts it together again with improvements. He had returned to Pinchdean with a quantity of steel tubing, a light petrol engine, and a collection of canvas wings, and there he had built, with a quiet persistence that the villagers could not fathom, a machine that he said would fly.

The machine itself was a curious object. It consisted of a framework of steel tubes, upon which was stretched a canvas surface shaped something like the wings of a great bat. Below this surface hung the engine, a neat little motor, and a seat for the operator and a passenger. A propeller of wood and fabric, some six feet in diameter, was fixed behind the seat. The whole affair stood upon three wheels and looked, as Mrs. Tattle the postmistress observed, like a skeleton umbrella that had been left out in a gale.

The first flights were made in the early morning, before the village was properly awake, and for a time nobody believed the rumours. But one morning the baker's boy, delivering bread to the mill, saw the thing rise from the meadow behind the barn and sail out over the elms, turning in a wide circle above the church spire before descending. Within a day the whole village had been to the meadow to stare at the machine, and Arthur Verrinder found himself a celebrity of the most uncomfortable kind.

He was a modest young man, rather pale, with the ink-stained fingers of a draughtsman, and he had no taste for the commotion. He explained the principle of the thing a dozen times over, to farmers who nodded sagely and understood nothing, and to boys who wanted only to touch the propeller. The machine worked, he said, upon the same principle as a kite, except that it carried its own wind. The engine drove the propeller, which pushed the machine forward, and the forward motion drove air over the curved wings, which lifted it. It was, he said, extremely simple. Nobody believed him.

Old Squire Battersby was the first person of consequence to visit. He came on a grey mare, with his grooms at a discreet distance, and stood in the meadow with his hands behind his back, looking at the machine with the expression of a man examining a dubious horse. He walked round it twice, prodded a wing with his stick, and asked Arthur what he wanted for it. Arthur, who had not considered selling it, said that it was not for sale. The Squire offered fifty pounds, then a hundred, and grew red in the face when Arthur refused. He said the thing was a dangerous toy, that Arthur was a fool, and that he would have the law on him if it frightened the Battersby hunters. He rode off in a temper, and was heard that evening at the inn declaring that the machine was a humbug and would never fly.

The vicar, the Reverend Mr. Smalley, took a different line. He preached a sermon on the Sunday following, in which he compared Arthur to the builders of the Tower of Babel, and warned his congregation that the heavens were not meant to be entered by mortal man. The text was from Isaiah: "The stars in their courses fought against Sisera," which Mr. Smalley applied to the flying machine with a degree of theological ingenuity that rather overreached itself. The congregation listened with the respectful attention they gave to all sermons, and went out into the sunlight with no intention whatever of staying away from the meadow.

It was Miss Alice Durrant who first offered to fly. She was the daughter of the retired doctor, who lived in the square house by the green, and she was the person Arthur admired most in the world, though he had never told her so, having a young man's tendency to worship from a distance that is more romantic than useful. She was twenty-one, with a quick dark eye and a manner that was at once composed and mischievous. She had been to the meadow twice, had asked Arthur several questions of a kind that showed she understood the principle of the machine, and on the second visit she had said, quite simply, that she would like to go up in it.

Arthur, who had been explaining the carburettor, stopped with his mouth open. He said it was not quite ready for a passenger. She said she thought it was. He said there might be danger. She said she was not afraid, and that if he could trust the machine himself, he could trust it with her. He said he would make a trial flight first, and she said that was sensible, and she would come on Thursday.

The trial flight was made on the Wednesday evening, in a wind that was rather stronger than Arthur liked. The machine rose unevenly, lurched over the elms, and came down in the next field with a bump that buckled a wheel. Arthur replaced the wheel by candlelight, working in the barn with a lantern, and on Thursday morning he told Alice that the machine was ready.

They went up at half past ten, with the entire village assembled in the meadow. Arthur started the engine, the propeller roared, and the machine bumped across the grass and rose. The sensation, Arthur told me afterwards, was extraordinary.
