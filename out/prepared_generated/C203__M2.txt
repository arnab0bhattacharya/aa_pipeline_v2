The fog in London did not behave like the mist in his father’s village. There, the fog was a gentle, damp blanket that smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke, a thing that could be brushed aside by a sturdy boot. Here, in the heart of the city, the fog was a solid substance, a grey wall that pressed against the lungs and tasted of coal dust and rot. Arthur Penhaligon, twenty-three and armed with a leather satchel containing three pounds, a letter of introduction, and two unfinished novels, walked through it with the desperate energy of a man who believed he had left his soul in the countryside.

He had arrived that morning on the 9:15 from Shrewsbury, standing in the carriage with his chin tucked against his chest, watching the darkened windows of the suburban houses slide by like a film reel. He had told himself that London was a machine, and if he could just find the right gear, the right lever, it would turn him into a writer. Not a clerk. Not a copyist. A writer. But as he stepped off the train at Victoria, the machine felt less like a mechanism and more like a beast, indifferent and breathing.

He began his search for a room first, for without a roof, the ink would dry before it hit the page. He walked north, away from the station, into the labyrinth of Holborn and the Temple. The streets were narrow, choked with the smoke of a thousand hearths and the rumble of motorcars that sounded like angry beetles. He visited three boarding houses. The first was run by a woman with eyes like wet stones who told him he smelled of the provinces and refused him on the basis that she did not want "country air" in her parlour. The second was a room so small he had to lie diagonally to fit on the bed, and the landlady, a stout man with a moustache that twitched like a worm, wanted a month’s rent upfront. Arthur had three pounds. He had a week’s food, perhaps, if he ate bread and water.

The third room was in a building that leaned precariously over a courtyard, the windows stained with soot. The woman who opened the door was old, her face a map of wrinkles, and she looked at him not with suspicion, but with a weary recognition. She knew the look. It was the look of a boy who thought he was a man. She showed him the room. It was above a kitchen that smelled of boiled cabbage and stale beer. The wallpaper was peeling in long strips, revealing the dark bricks beneath. But it was dry, and it had a window, however small.

"Five shillings a week," she said, not turning around. "No meals. You keep the stairwell clean."

Arthur did not ask about the smell. He did not ask why the floorboards creaked so loudly. He paid her two pounds for the first month, a sum that left him with one pound and ten shillings. It was not enough. He knew it was not enough. But it was a place, and a place was a foundation.

With the room secured, he turned his attention to the job. He had a letter of introduction from a cousin to a printer in Fetter Lane. The printer was a small, precise man named Mr. Halloway who read the letter over his spectacles and looked at Arthur with the disdain of a jeweler inspecting a counterfeit stone.

"You want to be a writer?" Halloway asked, his voice dry as parchment.

"I want to be a writer," Arthur said. "But I need to eat. I can set type. I can proofread. I can do anything."

Halloway smiled, a thin, colorless expression. "We are short on hands for the night shift. Twelve hours. No conversation. You break the line, you break the bone. Can you do that?"

Arthur could do that. Arthur could do anything. He took the job. It was not a job for a writer, but it was a job. He would work the night shift, and by day, he would write. Or so he thought.

The afternoon wore on, and the fog thickened. Arthur walked the city, not with purpose, but with the aimless drift of a man trying to find a shape in the static. He went to the British Museum, standing before the Rosetta Stone, feeling the weight of history pressing down on him. He went to the river, watching the barges move slowly, their black hulls cutting through the grey water. He saw a man selling matches on the corner of Fleet Street, his fingers blue with cold. He saw a group of women laughing in a doorway, their faces bright in the gloom. He saw a newspaper boy shouting the headlines, his voice cracking in the damp air.

He saw everything, and it meant nothing. The city was a giant, but it was a silent giant. It did not speak to him. It did not offer him its secrets. He was a fly in the web, vibrating with energy, but trapped in the sticky threads. He had come to London to find his voice, but he had only found the silence.

By evening, the sun had set, though no one would have known it from the sky. The gas lamps flickered on, casting a sickly yellow light on the cobblestones. Arthur walked back to his room, his feet heavy, his satchel light. He had found a room. He had found a job. But he had not found a story.

He climbed the stairs to his room, the smell of boiled cabbage hitting him before he opened the door. He sat on the edge of the bed, the diagonal one, and took out his notebook. The paper was blank. He stared at it, waiting for the words to come. The words that would make him a writer. The words that would save him from the night shift, from the cabbage, from the fog.
