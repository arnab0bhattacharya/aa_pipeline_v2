The rain lashed against the single pane of the drawing-room window, blurring the grey expanse of the Calder valley into a smear of slate and iron. Inside, the fire had burnt low, leaving only a bed of embers that glowed with a sullen, dying heat, mirroring the state of the man who sat opposite me. Mr. Silas Thorne. He had not spoken in twenty minutes. His hands, once accustomed to the precise manipulation of looms and the counting of coins, now trembled upon his knees, the joints swollen and reddened by a chill that seemed to emanate from his very skin. He looked less like a gentleman than a husk, stripped of its grain by the wind.

I, Eliza Vane, sat rigid in the chair by the mantelpiece, my fingers wrapped tightly around the stem of a dead rose I had pulled from the vase in a moment of fidgeting. I had agreed to this meeting out of a misplaced sense of duty, a belief that my late father’s former partner might still possess some shred of dignity. I was mistaken. There was no dignity in the hollows of his cheeks, nor in the way his eyes darted toward the door as if expecting a bailiff, or perhaps a ghost, to collect the debts of his soul.

"Eliza," he said at last. His voice was a rasp, like dry leaves skittering over stone. "I have come to offer you my hand."

I did not start. I had expected the confession of bankruptcy; I had not expected the proposal. The absurdity of it struck me with a cold, sharp clarity. He looked at me with an expression of weary resignation, as if I were a ledger he was closing out at a loss.

"Your hand," I repeated, the words tasting of ash. "You propose to marry me, Mr. Thorne? You, who have not looked me in the eye since the mills burned?"

"I have come to my senses," he said, forcing a smile that twisted his face into a mask of agony. "I see now that wealth is a fleeting vapor. What remains is... character. Your character, Eliza. It is strong. It is unyielding. I need an anchor. I need a home that will not collapse under the weight of my failure."

I stared at him. The fire crackled, a sudden, violent snap of a log, sending a shower of sparks into the dark. "You need a shelter," I said, my voice low and steady. "You do not need a wife. You seek a refuge from the cold, and you mistake my warmth for love."

He flinched. "Do not be cruel. I am ruined. My name is a brand of infamy in this town. The creditors are at my door. If I do not secure a position of respectability, I shall be dragged into the gaol. You have money, Eliza. You have a name that still commands some respect. Let me be your husband, and you shall be my savior."

The word *savior* hung in the air, heavy and grotesque. I looked at his hands again. They were still trembling. I felt a surge of anger, hot and bright, rising in my chest. It was not anger at his poverty, nor at his loss. It was anger at the audacity of his presumption. He wished to buy me with his ruin. He wished to pay for his safety with my freedom.

"And if I refuse?" I asked.

His face went pale, the blood draining from his cheeks until he looked like the corpse in the red room, stripped of all vitality. "Then I am lost," he whispered. "There is no other way out. My pride is gone. I have nothing left but this... this last chance."

I stood up. The movement was slow, deliberate. I walked to the window and pushed aside the curtain. The rain had intensified, a curtain of silver wire beating against the glass. The street below was empty, the gas lamps flickering in the wind, casting long, wavering shadows across the wet cobblestones. I looked out into the darkness, searching for something in the gloom that might guide me.

"Mr. Thorne," I said, not turning around. "You say you see my character now. You say it is strong. Very well. But you forget that strength is not a commodity to be traded. It is not a shield to be placed behind you while you hide from the world. I did not marry into your family for my money, nor did I serve your father for his favor. I served him because he was my father’s friend, and I loved him. But you, Silas? You have never seen me. You have seen only my inheritance. You have seen only the Vane name, which you now wish to borrow to shield your own shame."

I turned back to him. He was staring at the floor, his head bowed.

"I will not be your anchor," I said. "An anchor holds a ship in place, but it does not steer it. I will not be the ballast that keeps you from sinking, nor the rope that drags you to shore. I am not a lifeboat, Silas. I am a person, with my own will, my own fire, my own fate to face. If you sink, you will sink alone. That is the justice of it."

He looked up, his eyes wet, but not with tears. There was a hardness in them now, a glint of something dangerous. "You are sure?" he asked. "You will let me go to prison? You will let the world see you as the woman who cast out a fallen friend?"

"I will let you face the consequences of your actions," I replied. "That is not cruelty; it is honesty. You have no right to demand that I share in your disgrace. I have done no wrong. I have not squandered, I have not lied, I have not betrayed. I will not be made guilty by association."
