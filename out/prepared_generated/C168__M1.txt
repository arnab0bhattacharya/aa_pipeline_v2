The storm had passed, leaving the sea a bruised purple and the sky a washed-out grey. Salvatore pulled his *gozzo* into the small, stone harbour of San Lorenzo, the muscles in his back and arms screaming from the effort. In the bottom of the boat, wrapped in a salt-stiffened sailcloth, lay the boy. He was pale, his lips tinged with blue, but his chest rose and fell in a shallow, steady rhythm. Salvatore’s own hands were raw, his knuckles bleeding where the rope had burned through his calluses. He had found the boy clinging to a spar, a mile from the wreck of the Baron’s pleasure yacht, the *Sirena*, which the sea had claimed without ceremony.

News travelled faster than a boat in a village like San Lorenzo. By the time Salvatore had moored, a small crowd had gathered, their faces a mixture of awe and pity. Word of the Baron’s son, Emilio, lost at sea, had reached them the day before. Now, here he was, a miracle dredged from the churning water. Two days later, a carriage with the Baron’s crest – a silver eagle clutching a trident – rattled into the village. Salvatore was brought before Baron Vittorio Conti not as a supplicant, but as a hero. The Baron, a man whose face was a roadmap of grief and relief, clasped Salvatore’s shoulders. He smelled of wine and expensive pipe tobacco. “You have returned my heart to me,” the Baron said, his voice thick. “Ask anything.”

Salvatore, a man who had only ever known the rhythm of the tides and the weight of a net, did not know what to ask for. “A new net, my lord. And rope,” he mumbled, his gaze fixed on the polished marble floor. The Baron laughed, a booming sound that filled the cavernous hall. “You shall have a thousand nets. But you will not need them. You will live here, in my house. You will be my son’s companion. You will eat at my table.” And so, Salvatore the fisherman became Salvatore of the House of Conti. He was given clean clothes that felt like a stranger’s skin, a room with a soft bed, and meals that made his head swim. He was a curiosity at first, then a fixture. The young Emilio, his saviour, adored him, and through the boy, Salvatore found his footing in this strange new world. He learned to stand straight, to speak when spoken to, to navigate the treacherous currents of a nobleman’s household.

But a palace is its own kind of sea, and it has its own predators. The chief among them was Signor Gennaro, the Baron’s steward. Gennaro had served the Conti family for thirty years. His posture was as rigid as a ship’s mast, his hair was the colour of ash, and his eyes, small and dark, missed nothing. He had built his life on order, on the precise accounting of every grain of wheat and every drop of oil. He was the palace’s memory, its ledger, its unwritten law. And Salvatore, this upstart from the docks, was a blot on his perfect page. The boy’s rise was an insult to Gennaro’s decades of quiet, unswerving loyalty. He watched Salvatore’s clumsy attempts at etiquette, his easy rapport with the Baron and his son, and a cold, hard knot of resentment tightened in his gut.

Gennaro did not act with the heat of anger, but with the cold precision of a stiletto. He began with small things. A shipment of the finest Marsala, intended for the Baron’s private cellar, was logged as having been received and stored. A week later, Gennaro, with a look of profound regret, informed the Baron that a cask was missing. He suggested, with careful phrasing, that perhaps the new man, not yet used to the castle’s ways, had been careless. Salvatore denied it, his Sicilian temper flaring, which only made him look guilty and uncouth. The Baron waved it away, but a seed of doubt was planted. Then, Gennaro misplaced the keys to the silver pantry, only to ‘discover’ them a day later in the pocket of a jacket Salvatore had worn. He spoke to the other servants in hushed tones, shaking his head sadly about the fisherman’s strange habits, his late-night wanderings, his unfamiliarity with the value of things.

Salvatore felt the change in the air of the house. The friendly nods from the maids became averted eyes. The cook, who once slipped him extra almond pastries, now served him with a stiff formality. He was an island again, but this island was gilded and hostile. He tried to speak to the Baron, but how could he explain? His complaints sounded petty, a commoner’s paranoia. Gennaro’s insinuations were woven into the very fabric of the household’s daily operations. The steward was the palace; to accuse him was to accuse the stone walls themselves. Desperate, Salvatore focused on what he knew: the boy, Emilio. He taught him how to tie sailor’s knots, how to read the clouds, how to find the North Star. In the boy’s clear, trusting eyes, he saw a reflection of the man he used to be, and it gave him strength.

The breaking point came on the Feast of San Lorenzo. The Baron was hosting a grand dinner for the neighbouring nobles. The centrepiece was to be a magnificent silver service, a family heirloom. As the guests gathered, Gennaro approached the Baron, his face a mask of utter dismay. He spoke in a low, urgent voice, but Salvatore, standing near the doorway, heard every word. The silver was gone. The pantry was empty. There was only one person, Gennaro implied with a sorrowful glance in Salvatore’s direction, who would not understand its immense value, who might see it as mere metal to be melted down. The Baron’s face darkened. The humiliation was a physical blow. He turned, his eyes finding Salvatore. The look was not of anger, but of profound, weary disappointment. It was a look that said, *I made a mistake*.
