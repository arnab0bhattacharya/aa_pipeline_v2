It was upon a raw November afternoon, when the fog had settled so thickly upon Charing Cross Road that the lamps burned yellow at three o’clock, that I received my first intimation of the singular business which was to occupy me for the better part of a fortnight. I had been seated by my fire, endeavouring to bring some order to a ledger which had long stood in need of it, when my landlady announced a visitor, and there entered a small, spare man of perhaps sixty years, wrapped to the chin in a greatcoat, with the pallor of one who lives much among books and little in the sun.

“Mr. Gideon Marrable,” said he, laying a card upon the table. “I keep a shop for rare and second-hand volumes in this street—not a hundred yards from your door. I have come upon a matter which I confess I do not understand, and which has begun to prey upon my mind. I am told that you have a faculty for such puzzles.”

I begged him to be seated and to tell his story in his own way, which he did with the methodical precision of a man accustomed to catalogues.

“For the past two years,” said he, “I have had among my customers a gentleman who signs himself in my ledger as Mr. Edward Vane. Whether that be his true name I cannot say; it is the name he gave, and the address he left was a poste restante in the Strand, which I have since learned is a device for those who do not wish to be found. He is a man of middle height, dark, with a short beard trimmed close, and he wears a ring upon his left hand—a signet, I think, though I never saw the device. He has been a good customer, paying promptly and in gold, and his taste has been for the older topographies—county histories, itineraries, books of roads and travels, the dry sort of volume that most men pass over. I was glad enough of his custom, for such stock moves slowly.”

“And what has gone wrong?”

“It is this. About six months ago he returned a book—a folio Camden’s ‘Britannia’ of 1695—saying that it was imperfect and that he would not keep it. I thought no more of it until, some weeks later, I had occasion to consult the same volume for another customer, and found that a single leaf had been cut out—cleanly, sir, with a razor, close to the binding. I was vexed, but I set it down to accident or to some former owner, and thought no more. Then he returned a second book, and a third, and in each of them, upon examination, I found the same thing—one leaf gone, and always a leaf bearing a map or a plan. Not the fine engraved plates, you understand, but the plain black-line plans that are bound in among the letterpress. And here is the queer part of it: the books he kept were never touched. It was only those he brought back.”

“Have you the volumes still?”

“I have three of them in the shop at this moment.”

“Then let us go and look at them,” said I, taking up my hat, “for I have found in my experience that a book which has been robbed is often more eloquent than one which has not.”

The shop, when we reached it, was a narrow, dusky place, smelling of leather and old paper, with shelves running to the ceiling and a single gas-jet hissing above the counter. Mr. Marrable brought out the three volumes and laid them before me. I examined the stubs where the leaves had been removed, and then, with his permission, I turned to the places where the missing leaves should have fallen, and read the letterpress upon the pages which faced them.

The first was a history of the county of Kent, and the missing plan was of the marshes about Romney. The second was a book of roads, and the plan was of the great cross-roads at a place called Amblingford, in Surrey. The third was an old itinerary of the southern counties, and the plan showed the downs above a village named Chalke Coombe. At first glance there was no connexion between them—a marsh, a crossroads, and a hill—save that all three lay within a day’s ride of London, and all three were places of no particular note.

“You have compared them?” I asked.

“I have laid them side by side a hundred times,” said Marrable, “and I can make nothing of it.”

I was still poring over the stubs when the shop-bell tinkled and a boy came in with a parcel. Marrable cut the string, and out fell a fourth volume, with a note within in a small, neat hand: *Returned with thanks—imperfect. E.V.*

“He has not been in himself for a month,” said Marrable. “He sends by messenger.”

I took up the book—a thin quarto of Sussex antiquities—and found that a leaf had been removed in the same fashion. The facing page described a tract of country near the sea, and spoke of a ruined chapel and a lane called Gallows Gate. And then, as I held the leaf-stub to the gas-jet, I saw what I had been looking for. The edge of the stub was not straight. It had been cut, not with a single stroke, but with several, and the cuts had left a faint serration, as though the razor had been guided by a rule that slipped. Upon the verso of the stub, where the paper was thick, there remained the faintest impression of the plate which had lain against it—a ghost of a line, raised in the rag paper by the pressure of the press. I held it slantwise to the light and made out a portion of a coast, a curve of a river, and, at the corner, the figure of a small cross.
