The year was 1792, and the air of Paris did not smell of the lavender water and old paper that Mr. Josiah Pumble, bookseller of Cheapside, had expected to find; it smelled, with a pungency that seemed to assault the very nostrils of the soul, of gunpowder, stale bread, and the sharp, metallic tang of impending catastrophe. Mr. Pumble, a man of such moderate dimensions and entirely unremarkable complexion that one might have mistaken him for a rather large and well-dressed button, had traveled to the French capital for the most pedestrian of purposes: to collect a debt of forty pounds sterling from a printer named Monsieur Deschamps, for a consignment of religious tracts on the virtues of patience. It was a dull business, this debt-collecting, but Mr. Pumble had never done a brave thing in his life, nor a clever one, nor a thing that did not involve the safe exchange of one thing for another, preferably money for paper.

He stood now in the shadow of a narrow, crooked street in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, his trunks at his feet, his face a mask of bewildered horror. The street was not empty; it was full, crowded, jammed, and heaving with a tide of humanity that seemed to flow uphill against the very laws of nature. They were shouting. They were not shouting in the polite, measured tones of a London coachman requesting a fare, but in a roar that shook the plaster from the walls and vibrated in the bones of the passersby. A mob, or perhaps a multitude, or perhaps simply the people, surged past, carrying with them a momentum that threatened to sweep Mr. Pumble, and his trunks, and his forty pounds of potential profit, straight into the Seine.

"Make way! Make way for the liberty!" bellowed a man with a pike, his face red and shining with sweat and blood.

"Liberty!" thought Mr. Pumble, clutching his hat with both hands as if it were a life preserver. "I have heard of liberty in the books. It is a very abstract concept. I had rather have a receipt."

It was in this state of mental and physical disarray that he was suddenly seized by a pair of small, trembling hands. He turned, expecting to be robbed, or perhaps murdered, which, in his current state of nerves, would have been a distinct relief. But it was not a knife that was thrust before him, nor a pike; it was a boy. A boy of perhaps fifteen, with eyes as large and wide as saucers, and a face so pale that it seemed to be carved from marble. He wore a smock stained with ink, a sure sign of his trade, and he clutched a bundle of papers wrapped in oilskin to his chest as if it were the heart of his mother.

"Sir! Sir, please!" the boy gasped, his voice cracking under the weight of his terror. "Monsieur! I am Pierre. I am the apprentice. My master, Monsieur Deschamps, he is gone. The men with the red caps, they took him. They took him to the place where the heads fall. They say he is a tyrant. They say he prints lies. But he prints only the truth, sir! Or at least, the truth of the accounts! Please! You are English. You are safe. The English are not yet in the list. Please, take these papers. They are his ledgers. They are his life. If I stay, I die. If I go without them, I am a thief. Please, sir!"

Mr. Pumble looked at the boy. He looked at the oilskin bundle. He looked at the surging, violent crowd that was closing in behind them like a pack of wolves. His first instinct, the instinct of a man who had never done a brave thing in his life, was to run. To flee to the nearest tavern, lock the door, and drink a bottle of wine until his heart stopped beating from fright. But something stayed him. Perhaps it was the boy’s eyes, which held a reflection of his own cowardice so perfectly that it was almost painful. Perhaps it was the sudden, absurd realization that if he left the boy here, he would have to explain it to his wife, Mrs. Pumble, who was a woman of such profound dullness that she would likely accuse him of having abandoned the debt for a romantic adventure.

"Listen to me, lad," said Mr. Pumble, his voice trembling so violently that it sounded like a rusty hinge. "I am not a hero. I am not a soldier. I am a bookseller. If I take that parcel, I may be killed. I may be hanged. I may be turned into a soup."

"The soup is better than the guillotine, sir!" the boy cried, a tear cutting a clean path through the grime on his cheek. "Please! For the memory of my master! He was a good man. He paid his debts. He never cheated a customer. He only printed the words that were given to him. Please!"

The crowd surged closer. A man with a tricolor sash waved his arms, shouting something about the sanctity of the people and the death of the king. Mr. Pumble felt a cold sweat break out on his forehead. He looked at the bundle. It was heavy, heavier than forty pounds of debt. He looked at the boy. The boy’s knees were knocking together, a metronome of pure fear.

"Right," said Mr. Pumble, the word escaping his lips before his brain could intervene. "Right. I shall take it. But I shall do it because I am a coward who cannot stand the sight of a weeping boy, not because I am brave. And if I am killed, I expect a full refund of my travel expenses."
