The cottage of the woodcutter, Brannoc, stood at the very hem of the royal forest, where the ordered gardens of the palace gave way to a wildness of briar and ancient oak. It was a small, huddled dwelling, its thatch like the tousled head of a dreamer, and from its single window, night and day, there came a faint, steady light, as though a star had been caught and tethered within. This was the workshop of his daughter, Elara, and the light was the glow of her loom, a patient, golden-eyed companion to her long and solitary labour.

Her fame, such as it was, had not been sought. It was whispered among the charcoal-burners and the herb-gatherers that Elara could weave more than thread. She wove the silver of moonlight into the borders of her cloth, they said, and the scent of rain into its very heart. Her tapestries were not pictures but windows; to look upon one was to feel the chill of the mountain stream it depicted, or to hear the distant hunting horns of a forgotten age. The truth was simpler and more magical: she possessed a sympathy so profound that her hands seemed to translate the world’s quietest secrets into wool and flax.

It was this whisper that reached the ears of Queen Isolde, a woman for whom the world was a mirror in which she sought, with a restless and unsatisfied eye, her own reflection. The Queen was beautiful, as a statue in a public square is beautiful, and she was admired, as a statue is admired, for the perfection of her form and the coldness of her material. Yet she was not beloved. Her courtiers praised her wit, which was like a diamond, hard and sharp; they lauded her taste, which was impeccable and utterly without mercy. But in the secret chambers of their hearts, they feared her, and fear is a chilly substitute for love. She had heard of the woodcutter’s daughter and was seized by a magnificent, imperious idea.

She summoned Elara to the palace. The girl came, dressed in her simple homespun, her hands calloused from her craft, and stood before the Queen in a great hall of polished marble, a small, still figure of warmth and silence in a place of chill and echo. The Queen, reclining on a divan of crimson velvet, regarded her as a connoisseur might regard a curious, unpolished stone.

‘They tell me, child,’ began the Queen, her voice a melody of exquisite boredom, ‘that your hands can weave the very soul of things into cloth. I have a task for you. You will weave for me a robe. You will spin the threads of my desire and the weft of my ambition. This robe, when I put it on, must make me the most beloved queen in the world. Not the most feared, nor the most powerful, nor even the most envied. The most beloved. You have one year. The wool from my own flocks will be delivered to your cottage. Do not fail me, for failure is a form of treason, and treason is so terribly out of fashion.’

Elara, who saw not a queen but a woman whose eyes held the terror of a child lost in a dark wood, simply bowed her head and returned to her cottage.

And so her labour began. The wool arrived in great, soft bales, white as a winter dawn. But Elara did not immediately set it upon her loom. She spent the first weeks simply holding it, feeling its texture, its warmth, its potential. She would sit by her window and watch the light change upon the forest leaves, from the pale green of spring to the deep, sombre bronze of autumn. She saw the sun’s rays, like golden needles, darning the shadows of the glade. She saw the first frost etch its delicate, silver filigree upon the glass. She listened to the wind, which was not a single sound, but a thousand: the sigh of the pines, the rustle of the birch, the deep, resonant hum of the ancient oaks.

She did not begin to weave until the first snowdrops pierced the earth. And when she did, she did not choose the brilliant colours of the rainbow, nor the rich dyes of the palace. She took no madder for crimson, no woad for blue. Instead, she gathered the soft, pale ash of her hearth-fire, the muted green of moss from the north side of the trees, the quiet grey of the lichen that clung to the stones. She dyed her wool in these humble baths, and the colours that emerged were the colours of memory, of twilight, of the world when it is seen through the eyes of love rather than the eyes of desire.

Her loom sang a different song now. It did not clatter with the harsh music of ambition. It whispered. And into the fabric she wove not the grand, the spectacular, or the awe-inspiring, but the small and the true. She wove the quiet contentment of a cat asleep before a fire. She wove the feeling of a cool hand upon a fevered brow. She wove the sound of a child’s laughter, which is the most perfect music in the world. She wove the scent of a familiar garden after rain, the comfort of an old friendship, the tenderness of a word left unspoken. Her shuttle flew, not with haste, but with a deep, deliberate affection, each pass of the thread a small act of compassion.

The year passed. The court, which had initially been amused by the Queen’s whim, grew tired of waiting. The King, a gentle and somewhat bewildered man, suggested to his wife that perhaps the girl had run away. The Queen silenced him with a look that could have frozen a waterfall. But in her private chambers, she too began to feel a strange anxiety. She found herself dreaming not of the robe, but of the girl’s quiet, knowing eyes.
