The garret at the top of Mrs. Tetchell’s lodging house in Cottle Lane possessed a singular, magnificent quality in the estimation of its sole inhabitant: it was the exact altitude from which great verse must necessarily fall. Elias Thornberry had chosen it not for the damp-stained walls, which wept brown tears whenever the weather turned, nor for the lice that seemed to materialize from the very floorboards, but because the climb to the fourth floor left him breathless, and that breathlessness, he felt certain, was the physical manifestation of aspiration. He was twenty-two, possessed of a countenance that he hoped resembled Lord Byron’s, and entirely convinced that he was destined to be remembered as the finest poet of the age. All that remained was for inspiration to strike.

It was now the autumn of 1834, and Elias had been waiting for that strike for fourteen months.

Mrs. Tetchell, a woman constructed largely of bone, gristle, and an abiding suspicion of those who did not work with their hands, did not share his confidence in his destiny. She stood in the doorway of his room on a Thursday morning, her arms folded across her apron, watching him as he sat at the rickety table, staring at a blank sheet of paper.

“It is three weeks, Mr. Thornberry,” she said. Her voice had the quality of a gate hinge that had never known oil. “Three weeks since the last shilling was spent, and yet I see you sitting there, doing nothing.”

“I am composing, Mrs. Tetchell.”

“You are staring, Mr. Thornberry. There is a difference, though I grant you the cost is the same.” She shifted her weight. “My late husband, may he rot, was a cooper, and he said that a man who does not fill the barrel must still pay for the wood. I have been patient because you are quiet and do not bring drink up the stairs, but patience is a resource, and mine is spent down to the splinters. I will need something on account by Saturday, or I shall have to let the room to someone who understands the value of labour.”

She left, and Elias turned back to his blank page. He dipped his pen — a fine instrument, the one luxury he had refused to pawn — and held it above the paper, waiting. The ink formed a small, trembling drop at the nib, caught the grey light from the window, and fell, blotting the page. He stared at the blot. It looked, he thought, rather like a beetle. He set down the pen and went to the window.

Across the narrow street, in the shop beneath the faded sign of the Glove and Cuff, stood Miss Prudence Callow. She was the daughter of the glover, a young woman of perhaps twenty, with brown hair pinned sensibly beneath a cap and hands that moved with quick, certain economy as she arranged her wares in the window. Elias had spoken to her twice. The first time, he had crossed the street in a state of high excitement, having composed what he believed to be a remarkable couplet in the night, and had recited it to her on the pavement. She had listened, her head slightly inclined, and then said, “Yes, very nice. Were you wanting to purchase gloves, sir, or were you just standing about?” The second time, he had attempted to explain the nature of the poetic vocation, and she had looked at him with an expression that he could only describe as pitying, though she was kind about it.

From his window, he could see her now, stitching at her counter. He pressed his fingertips to the glass. Surely she must sense it — the tremendous force of genius pressing against the walls of his garret, seeking egress. He felt it as a physical weight, a pressure behind his eyes. The poem was there, whole and perfect, if only he could find the door.

On Friday, Mr. Fossett called. Mr. Fossett was a creditor of the most disagreeable and persistent kind: a butcher who had extended credit on the strength of Elias’s assurances that a great work was forthcoming, and that its publication would settle all accounts with interest. He was a thick-necked man who smelled of tallow and wore a stained leather apron even when not at his trade, as though to remind the world of his solidity.

“Thornberry,” he said, from the foot of the stairs, having pushed past Mrs. Tetchell, who followed him with an expression of grim vindication. “You owe me two pounds, four shillings, and sixpence, and I have heard enough about your great poem to paper every privy in Southwark with it. Where is my money?”

“It is coming, Mr. Fossett. The work proceeds.”

“The work!” Mr. Fossett climbed the stairs, each step a pronouncement. “The work is meat going bad in my cellar, is what the work is. I am not a patron of the arts, Thornberry. I am a butcher. I want my money, or I shall take it out of your hide, and I do not mean that metaphorically.” He stood in the doorway, filling it, and looked at the bare table, the empty grate, the single chair. “You have nothing,” he said, and it was not a question.

Elias raised his chin. “I have my talent, sir, which is worth more than your entire stock.”

Mr. Fossett stared at him for a long moment, then turned and descended the stairs, muttering. Mrs. Tetchell lingered. “Saturday,” she said.

That evening, Elias sat in the dark, for he could not afford a candle. The room was cold. He pulled his coat — his only coat — about his shoulders and tried to compose by the light of the street lamp that bled through the window. He wrote a line, crossed it out, wrote another, crossed that out. The words would not behave. They came to him in the night, luminous and perfect, but by the time they reached the page they were leaden, deformed, ordinary. He crumpled the paper and threw it into the corner, where it joined a small, sad hillock of its fellows.
