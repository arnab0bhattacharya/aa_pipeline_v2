The announcement, which Mr. Hollingsworth had chosen to make upon a Sunday evening when the family party was assembled in the great drawing-room at Wrenfield Hall, was delivered with all the composure of a man who had long since settled the matter in his own mind, and who derived no small satisfaction from the contemplation of its effects upon others. He was a gentleman of seventy, of a vigorous constitution and an irascible temper, whose fortune, accumulated through three generations of prudent merchants and one of fortunate marriages, was generally understood to be considerable; and whose expectations, during the last fifteen years, had been the peculiar care of every relation within the circle of his acquaintance. He had, he said, observed with regret the assiduity of his nephews and nieces, and the frequency with which his health was enquired after by persons who had never before evinced the smallest solicitude concerning it; and he had therefore determined, while still in possession of his faculties, to bestow his property upon a charitable institution for the relief of decayed musicians, a class of men for whom he had always entertained a particular esteem, his own father having been a performer upon the violoncello.

The effect of this communication may be more easily imagined than described. Mrs. Cressingham, his eldest niece, a lady of a certain age and a very determined manner, was the first to recover herself sufficiently to observe that she trusted her dear uncle would not be precipitate; that the claims of relationship, however humble, were not to be lightly set aside; and that she was persuaded, when he came to reflect upon the matter, he would see the propriety of consulting the interests of those who were, after all, his natural heirs. Mr. Cressingham, her husband, a gentleman of small estate and large expectations, remarked with a forced smile that charity began at home, and that he had no doubt his uncle was merely pleased to exercise his wit at their expense. The younger nephews and nieces said nothing, but looked at one another with an expression which spoke volumes; and Miss Hollingsworth, a maiden lady of fifty, who had lived at the Hall for twenty years and considered herself indispensable to its comfort, was heard to declare, in a voice tremulous with emotion, that she had never expected to be treated so unkindly by a brother whom she had ever loved and honoured.

The only person in the room who received the intelligence with any appearance of composure was Miss Eleanor Vane, a young lady of nineteen, who had been left an orphan at an early age, and whose father, a clergyman of small preferment, had been the particular friend of Mr. Hollingsworth in his youth. She had been brought to Wrenfield at the age of twelve, and had resided there ever since, in a position which it would be difficult to define, being neither a daughter nor a servant, but something between the two; and she had long since learned to expect nothing from her guardian's fortune, and to be grateful for the shelter of his roof. She said nothing, therefore, but continued to ply her needle with an assiduity which might have been mistaken for indifference; and it was perhaps this very composure which first attracted the notice of Mr. Hollingsworth, who, having expected to see her as much dismayed as the rest, was pleased to observe that she alone had the good sense to accept his determination without a murmur.

The weeks which followed were not calculated to raise the family in the estimation of any impartial observer. Mrs. Cressingham, who had previously been in the habit of visiting her uncle twice a year, now came every fortnight, bringing with her such delicacies as were most likely to tempt the appetite of an invalid, and reading to him from the newspapers with an assiduity which was evidently the result of a determination to be useful. Mr. Cressingham, who had never been known to spend a morning in the house, now discovered a sudden taste for the conversation of his uncle, and would sit with him for hours, discussing the price of corn and the state of the roads, with an air of profound interest which deceived nobody. The younger nephews and nieces wrote letters of affectionate enquiry, which were read aloud at the breakfast-table with an emphasis that betrayed a hope of their being understood as sincere. Even Miss Hollingsworth, who had been in the habit of complaining of her brother's temper, now discovered that he was the best of men, and that she had always said so.

Mr. Hollingsworth received these attentions with a gravity which was not without its humour. He was not a man of much penetration, but he was not without a certain shrewdness in matters which concerned his own comfort; and he had long since learned to distinguish between the solicitude of interest and the solicitude of affection. He said nothing, however, but continued to receive his visitors with the same civility as before, and to listen to their conversation with an air of polite attention which might have been mistaken for approbation.

It was in the third week of this period that Miss Vane, who had been in the habit of walking to the village every morning for the purpose of visiting a sick woman, returned to the Hall one day in a state of some agitation. She had met with an accident, she said, upon the road; her ankle had been turned by a stone, and she had been obliged to sit down upon a stile until she could recover herself. She had not been there above ten minutes, when a carriage had stopped, and a gentleman had alighted, and had offered her his assistance. He had been very civil, she said, and had insisted upon conveying her home; but she had declined, not wishing to give trouble, and had walked back slowly, leaning upon her parasol.
