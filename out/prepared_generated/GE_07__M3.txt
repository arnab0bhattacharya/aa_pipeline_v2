When Silas Marlow first discerned the changed aspect of his father’s tanning yard, with its great pits covered over by rough-hewn planks and the familiar stench of lime and hides supplanted by the cleaner, sharper scent of sawn timber, he was brought up sharply against the unyielding nature of facts. He had travelled down from London on the outside of the coach, nursing in his breast that large, vague ambition which is the peculiar sustenance of young men apprenticed to trades that deal in ideas rather than in raw material. He had come back to Thistleton expecting to find things much as he had left them three years ago, save for the solemnity of a funeral; and behold, the very ground beneath his family’s fortunes had shifted. The yard, with its vats and its winding gear, belonged no longer to the Marlow name, but to a certain Mr. Jarvis, a timber-merchant from Nottingham, who had already begun to stack his deals where the hides had once been stretched. Silas stood for a moment in the narrow lane, looking at this tangible evidence of displacement, feeling within himself the sudden, sharp collision of two distinct tendencies: the tender, backward-drawing fibres of filial affection, and the vigorous, forward-thrusting shoots of his own individual life.

It is a common error in our estimates of human conduct to suppose that a young man’s ambition is a sort of conscious selfishness, easily thrust aside when the claims of kindred present themselves. We are accustomed to think that the sacrifice of personal prospects is a matter requiring only a sufficient degree of moral heroism, forgetting that the very substance of such prospects has woven itself into the texture of the youth’s imagination, so that to tear it away is to leave a ragged edge on the soul. Silas had been but sixteen when he went to his uncle’s bookshop in Paternoster Row, carrying with him nothing but a small bundle of clothes and a vast, unarticulated hunger for knowledge. The tanning yard had always been repugnant to him; he loathed the sight of the great, sodden hides, and the slow, mechanical round of the tanner’s life seemed to him a sort of spiritual death. In the bookshop, amid the scent of leather of a different kind, he had found his true element. He had learned to bind books, to converse with the copperplate of ancient title-pages, and, in the stolen hours of the night, to read the very volumes he was set to bind. His employer, Mr. Crisp, a man of dry intellect and punctilious habits, had perceived in Silas a rare capacity for the trade, and had even hinted that a partnership might not be beyond the reach of a young man of his diligence and sobriety. It was this vision of future mastery, of a life spent among the treasuries of human thought, that Silas carried in his mind as he turned away from the dismantled tanning yard and walked toward the narrow street where his mother now lived.

The house to which he was directed was not the substantial, stone-built dwelling he had known, but a humble cottage in a back lane, tenanted by his mother’s cousin, Mrs. Birkett. It was a decent enough abode, with its scrupulously whitened doorstep and its muslin curtains, but it wore to Silas’s eyes the aspect of a dependence. When he entered the low parlour, and saw his mother rising from a chair by the fire, a sudden compunction seized him. She looked older, feebler, and her black gown, borrowed for the funeral, seemed to weigh upon her with more than its material heaviness.

“Eh, my lad,” she said, in the broad Trentside dialect that sounded strange to Silas after his years of London speech, “thee’rt come at last. I begun to think the coach had gone down i’ the waters.”

“I made what haste I could, Mother,” said Silas, taking her hand, which was rough and reddened yet with the habits of her former housewifery. “I could not leave the shop earlier. Mr. Crisp was very particular about a job of binding that had to go out.”

Mrs. Birkett, a comfortable, well-favoured woman, with the look of one who has been accustomed to a certain ease in life, and who practises the virtues of hospitality without any very severe strain upon her own resources, bustled forward to set out some tea. She was not a woman of profound reflections; her kindness was of that practical, unexhaustive sort which finds its satisfaction in providing warm tea and buttered toast, and which is inclined to look upon the sorrows of others as things that will be mended by a good night’s rest.

“It’s a poor welcome for thee, Silas,” she observed, pouring out the tea, “but we maun e’en do the best we can. Thy mother’s had a sore time of it, what with the sickness and the settling up of things. But Jarvis paid a fair price for the yard, and the debts is mostly cleared, save for a bit of a balance here and there.”

Silas listened in silence, his eyes fixed on his mother’s face. He saw in it the traces of a struggle that had been more than physical; it was the slow, gradual surrender of a lifelong independence. The tanner’s wife, who had once ruled her own household with a kindly but unquestioned authority, was now an inmate in another woman’s house, and the consciousness of dependence was eating into the very roots of her spirit. He felt a sudden, passionate impulse to take her away from it all, to provide her with a home of her own, where she might once more sit by her own fireside and speak with the authority of a mistress. But even as the impulse was formed, there arose in his mind the spectre of his own future, the long years of patient toil that had gone to build up his position in Mr. Crisp’s shop, and the hope, growing ever more definite, that he might one day stand behind the counter as a partner, or even as a master himself.
