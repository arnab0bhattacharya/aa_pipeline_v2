It was upon the atoll of Makaatu, a mere ring of shattered coral and pandanus scrub in the mid-Pacific, that my friend Hartley made the first of his astonishing observations regarding the terrestrial crabs of that island. We had landed there to collect botanical specimens and to escape the intolerable tedium of the *S.S. Celandine*, whose captain was a dull, God-fearing man with no conversation beyond the price of copra. Hartley was a naturalist of some repute, possessed of a quiet, scientific temper, and I was his unofficial assistant, having shipped with him chiefly for the sake of my health and the novelty of the tropics.

The crabs themselves were of a species unknown to Hartley, though clearly related to the common robber crabs of the Indian Ocean. They were massive, heavy-shelled creatures, of a dull, ash-grey colour, with preposterously large and powerful pincers. What first caught Hartley’s attention was not their size, however, but their extraordinary and, so far as we knew, unnatural cooperation.

We were sitting one afternoon in the meagre shade of a pandanus tree, near the edge of the hard, white coral beach, when we observed a party of four of these creatures engaged upon a fallen coconut. Now, the ordinary robber crab, as is widely known, will tear the husk from a nut with its pincers and hammer the shell upon a stone. But these four crabs did nothing of the sort. They had arranged themselves in a precise square around the nut, and with a perfect, mechanical simultaneity, they gripped the husk with their great claws. Then, by a coordinated and perfectly timed heave, they simply tore the husk asunder, ripping it into four equal quarters in a single movement. Having done this, two of the crabs dragged the exposed shell to a sharp ridge of coral, while the other two wedged a flat stone beneath it. Then, with a combined effort that was as deliberate as any engineering operation, they cracked the shell.

Hartley was upon his feet in an instant, his pince-nez glinting. "Do you see that, Bunting?" he cried. "Did you ever see the like of that?"

I confessed I had not. It was entirely contrary to the nature of crustaceans, which are, as a rule, solitary, stupid, and aggressively selfish. The idea of four crabs cooperating in a mechanical task was as absurd as the idea of four cats building a house. Yet we watched them repeat the process twice more, with the same uncanny precision.

Hartley was deeply excited. He produced his notebook and began to scribble furiously, muttering about the origins of social instinct and the limitations of the ganglionic nervous system. "It is a fundamental breach of the laws of nature," he said. "A creature with a nervous system no larger than a pinhead has no business behaving like a gang of dockworkers."

Our companion on the expedition was a man named Jenks, the mate of the *Celandine*, who had been put ashore with us to assist in carrying our gear. Jenks was a short, broad, red-faced man of the merchant marine, entirely untroubled by scientific curiosity, and possessed of a singularly commercial turn of mind. He came plodding up the beach just as the crabs were finishing their third coconut.

"What's the game?" he asked, squinting at the creatures.

Hartley explained the phenomenon to him, dwelling upon its biological impossibility. Jenks was unimpressed.

"Big crabs," he said, thoughtfully. "Big claws. I dare say the people in Sydney would pay a bob or two for 'em. There's a market for curiosities, you know, and these are big enough to make a show of."

"My dear Jenks," said Hartley, "they are not curiosities; they are a biological impossibility. They are an anomaly. We must observe them, not sell them."

"Well, I don't know about that," said Jenks, with the obstinacy of his class. "I reckon I could get a couple of quid apiece for 'em. There's a fellow in George Street who buys all sorts of queer fish. Crabs like that, with big claws, would draw a crowd."

Hartley waved him away with the irritated impatience of the scientist, and returned to his observations. But the incident had planted a seed of discord, for Jenks, having once conceived the notion of selling the crabs, began to look upon them with a proprietary and calculating eye.

Over the following days, Hartley's observations grew more and more disquieting. The crabs, it appeared, did not merely cooperate in the opening of coconuts; they cooperated in everything. We found them building shelters of coral fragments, arranged in a manner that was, if not architectural, at least orderly and deliberate. We found them hunting in parties, surrounding and driving small lizards into the open. And, most remarkably of all, we found that they appeared to communicate.

Hartley described it as a "rhythmic percussion." The crabs would tap their claws upon the coral, in a series of measured beats, before undertaking any cooperative action. The patterns were not random. They were regular, repetitive, and, Hartley insisted, significant.

"It is a language," he said, on the third evening, as we sat by our small fire. "A primitive language, I grant you, but a language nonetheless. They are conveying information. They are coordinating."

"Rubbish," said Jenks, from the shadows. "They're just tapping. Crabs tap. I've seen crabs tap a thousand times."

"You have seen them tap," said Hartley, "but you have not seen them tap in unison, nor have you seen them tap before a specific action. The tapping precedes the action. It is a signal. It is a council of war."

Jenks snorted, and spat into the fire.

It was on the fourth day that Hartley first expressed the suspicion that was to dominate the remainder of our stay on Makaatu. We had been watching a party of crabs engaged in the construction of a large coral mound, a task of considerable engineering complexity, when Hartley suddenly lowered his binoculars and turned to me with a pale face.
