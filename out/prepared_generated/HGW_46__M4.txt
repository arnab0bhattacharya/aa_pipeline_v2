The whole business began, as such things will, with a toothache. My own. It had nagged at me for three days, a dull, persistent reminder of the perishable nature of the flesh, and so I had gone to see the island’s principal medical man, a grave and deliberate old native named Kaelo, who lived in a neat whitewashed hut set apart from the village and who treated his patients with a mixture of prayer, herbal decoctions, and an unsettling confidence in his own judgement.

I found him not at his hut but at the edge of the reef, where the lagoon water lay like a sheet of hammered glass over the coral, and there he was engaged in the most peculiar operation. An old man—I took him for seventy at least, a ruin of a man, all skin and bone and clouded eye—sat on a flat rock while Kaelo, with a small stone pestle, ground something in a shell. The old fellow was trembling, and not with cold, for the day was warm. Kaelo mixed the powder with a little water and bade him drink. I watched, expecting nothing in particular, for I had grown used to the island’s pharmacopoeia of leaves and bark and bitter roots.

What happened next I set down here without comment, for I have no explanation to offer. Within a quarter of an hour the trembling ceased. Within an hour the old man’s eyes, which had been like milky glass, had cleared to a startling brightness. By evening, when I returned to the village on some pretext of my own, he was sitting up and eating a hearty meal of fish and taro, and his skin, which had been dry as parchment, had taken on a certain ruddy tone. I questioned Kaelo. He was evasive, as these people can be when they do not wish to speak, and I did not press him, for I was a guest in his country and my position as Resident Commissioner was one of persuasion rather than command. But I watched. And over the following weeks I saw the old man—whose name was Tavi—grow visibly younger. Not in the manner of a convalescent recovering lost strength, but in the manner of a clock running backward. His hair, which had been white, darkened at the roots. The deep seams in his face smoothed. He took to fishing again, and then to dancing, and there was a young woman in the village who began to look at him in a way that would have been scandalous in a man of his former years.

I am a man of science by training, though my career has been one of administration and the dull business of customs duties and land disputes. But I had read my Darwin and my Huxley, and I knew that what I was seeing was impossible. I made discreet inquiries. The islanders were reticent, but I had learned their language well enough, and I had one friend among them, a young man named Rennie—for so he styled himself, having been to a mission school—who had a weakness for tobacco and for the sound of his own voice.

“It is the *moko*,” he told me one evening, when we sat on the verandah of my bungalow and the sun went down in a blaze of crimson over the sea. “The medicine of the old ones. It is made from a certain bark and a certain stone, and it is not for everyone.”

“Why not?”

He shrugged. “It is not for everyone. Kaelo says it is not a thing to be given to those who are not ready. It makes the body young, but it does not make the spirit wise. Some men who have taken it have gone mad, for they could not bear to be young again and yet remember all that they had learned in their long lives. And some have died.”

“But it works.”

“It works,” he said, and looked at me with an expression I could not read. “But you must not ask for it. Kaelo will not give it to you. It is not for the white man.”

I let the matter drop, but it did not let me drop. I wrote a letter. I wrote it in the privacy of my study, and I sealed it, and I sent it by the monthly steamer to a firm in London—a firm of chemical manufacturers with whom I had had some correspondence on the subject of copra and the extraction of oils. I described what I had seen. I asked, in the cautious language of a man who does not wish to appear a fool, whether such a thing might be of interest to them. I received a reply by the next steamer, and it was not cautious at all. It was enthusiastic. It offered me a sum of money that made my head swim, and it asked for the formula, the method of preparation, the source of the ingredients, and a specimen of the bark and the stone.

I sat with that letter for three days. I am not a wealthy man. My pension, when it came, would be modest. I had a wife in England whom I had not seen for four years, and a daughter whose education was costing me more than I could comfortably afford. The money would have solved everything. And yet I could not do it. I told myself that it was a matter of principle, that I was the representative of Her Majesty’s Government and that I could not betray a trust. But it was not principle. It was the memory of Tavi’s eyes, clearing from milky glass to brightness, and the way he had looked at the young woman, and the way she had looked at him. It was a thing that belonged to these people, and I had no right to it.
