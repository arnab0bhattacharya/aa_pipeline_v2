The candle, guttering in its silver stick, cast a trembling light upon the chamber’s walls, and its feeble rays seemed to me but a poor emblem of the life that flickered so faintly within the bosom of my patient. Mrs. Agatha Finch lay upon her bed, her form so wasted by the long consumption that she appeared but a shadow of the woman I had attended these twenty years. Her face, once comely with the placid beauty of a virtuous life, was now sharpened by illness, and her eyes, though sunken, held a feverish and unnatural lustre. I had come at her urgent summons, believing her to be in the final extremity, and had prepared my heart for the solemn duties of my profession; but I was not prepared for the confession that now fell from her lips, nor for the weight of sin that her quiet life had concealed.

“Doctor Ellery,” she began, her voice a frail whisper that scarce reached my ears, “you have been a kind friend to me, and I must unburden my soul to you, for I dare not speak of this to the minister. He would look upon me with horror, and I could not bear it. I have lived among these good people as a woman of charity and peace, and they have bestowed upon me a respect I have never deserved. I have been a hypocrite, sir, a vile and selfish hypocrite, and the memory of my treachery has poisoned every hour of my existence.”

I sought to calm her, to assure her that the mercy of Heaven was infinite, but she would not be stayed. Her tale, once begun, poured forth with a torrent of anguish that seemed to drain the little strength remaining to her.

“You must know,” she continued, “that in my youth I had a friend, a dear and lovely creature, by name Miss Eliza Thorne. We were as sisters, brought up together in the village of Marlow Green, and I loved her with a tenderness that I believed could never be shaken. She was all that was gentle and good, and her beauty, though not of the dazzling kind that draws the gaze of the multitude, possessed a sweetness that won every heart. Her mind was cultivated, her temper angelic, and her spirit, free from guile, looked out upon the world with a confidence that I, in my darker nature, could not share. I envied her, Doctor Ellery; I envied her the serene purity of her soul, and I coveted the admiration that followed her steps. But I did not know the depth of my own wickedness until the serpent entered our Eden.”

She paused, and a spasm of pain contorted her features. I gave her a little wine, and she revived sufficiently to proceed.

“There came to our village a gentleman of fortune, a Mr. Henry Crawford, who possessed all the accomplishments that dazzle the young and unwary. He was handsome, eloquent, and versed in the arts of pleasing; and he fixed his attentions upon my friend. I saw his design, for I was not so blinded by his flattery as she. He was a libertine, a man who had ruined more than one innocent girl, and he sought to add Eliza to the list of his conquests. I knew this, and yet—oh, the blackness of my heart!—I did not warn her. Nay, worse, I became his accomplice. He perceived my weakness, and he played upon it with a cunning that now fills me with loathing. He whispered to me that Eliza was cold and proud, that she valued her virtue above all things, and that she would never stoop to love a man of his character; but that if I, her dearest friend, would but speak a word in his favour, would but soften her heart towards him, he would be forever grateful. And I, miserable creature that I was, listened to his persuasions. I told myself that I acted for her good, that perhaps he truly loved her, that a union with so wealthy and accomplished a man would raise her above the obscurity of our provincial life. But in truth, I was jealous of her superiority, and I longed to see her brought low, to prove that she too could fall from her pedestal of virtue.”

A sob broke from her, and I waited in silence, for I knew not what to say. The rain beat against the window, and the wind moaned in the chimney, as if nature herself lamented the tale of perfidy.

“I spoke to her of him,” she went on, “with a warmth that I feigned to be the disinterested affection of a sister. I painted his virtues in glowing colours, and I dismissed his vices as the thoughtless errors of youth. She listened to me, for she trusted me utterly. And when he pressed his suit, she did not repulse him with the firmness she would otherwise have shown. She hesitated, and in that hesitation he found his opportunity. He used every art of seduction; he spoke of love, of marriage, of a future of happiness and honour; and she, poor innocent, believed him. She gave him her heart, and then—then he abandoned her. He left the village, and we heard no more of him. Eliza was left to face her ruin alone. Her father, a stern and unforgiving man, turned her from his door; her friends forsook her; and she, the gentle, the pure, the beloved, became a wanderer in the world, with nothing but her shame and her sorrow for company.”

The old woman’s voice broke, and she wept bitterly. I took her hand, and she clung to it as a drowning creature to a plank.
