The fair at Battersea Fields, on that particular afternoon in the autumn of the year 1838, presented a spectacle of such concentrated humanity, such a jostling, jostling, elbowing, shouting, laughing, weeping, and general uproar, as might have led a stranger to believe that the whole population of London had been emptied out into a single muddy meadow for the express purpose of losing their pockets and their dignity. It was a panorama of vice and virtue, of gingerbread and gin, of fortunes told and fortunes lost, all under a sky the colour of a week-old bruise. Stalls and booths, like a line of argumentative giants, bellowed their wares: the man with the marionettes, whose tiny wooden kings and queens performed their little tragedies with a frantic, clicking earnestness; the seller of brandy-balls, whose sticky wares were the terror of every mother and the delight of every child; and, in a corner of considerable consequence, the booth of Mr. Tobias Puddle, proprietor and sole exhibitor of that most remarkable of all porcine prodigies, the Learned Pig, known to the public as the Baconian Sage.

Mr. Puddle was a man built upon the same principles as a bagpipe—loud, full of air, and capable of producing an astonishing noise when squeezed by circumstance. His face was a map of past jovialities and present anxieties, and his voice, when raised in its professional capacity, could cut through the din of the fair as a hot knife cuts through butter—or, as was more often the case with Mr. Puddle’s finances, through nothing at all. His pride, his livelihood, his one and only anchor in the turbulent sea of life, was the Baconian Sage, a creature of prodigious intelligence and still more prodigious girth. The Sage could, for the consideration of a single penny, spell out your name with wooden letters, indicate the day of the week with a dainty hoof, and—his crowning achievement—select the Queen of Hearts from a fan of playing cards with an air of such profound, such thoughtful, such utterly convincing sagacity, that the audience would forget for a moment that he was, when all was said and done, merely a pig. And then came the fatal evening when Mr. Puddle, having partaken of a quantity of gin that was heroic rather than prudent, sat down to a game of cards with a rival showman, one Silas Vane, a man whose smile was a thin crack in a tombstone and whose soul was a thing of such greasy, calculating darkness that it seemed to have been rendered down from the fat of lesser men. The game was piquet; the stakes were high; and when the last card was played, the Baconian Sage, his own magnificent self, was the price of Mr. Puddle’s folly.

To describe the despair that descended upon Tobias Puddle would be to attempt to paint a hurricane with a single brushstroke. It was a despair of the bowels, of the bones, of the very soul. He wandered the fair like a ghost at his own funeral, his magnificent voice reduced to a series of heartbroken sighs, his eyes fixed upon the booth of Silas Vane, where the Baconian Sage now sat, looking less like a learned philosopher and more like a prisoner awaiting an uncertain fate. It was in this state of utter dejection that he was discovered by his three unlikely allies. The first was Mr. Bartholomew Crimp, a purveyor of hot pies, a man whose stomach was the size of a prize-winning pumpkin and whose heart was of a similar generous dimensions. The second was Miss Tibby Sparrow, a seller of ballads, a young woman with eyes as bright and sharp as a sparrow’s and a voice that could wring tears from a stone or a ha’penny from a miser. The third was little Molly Mudge, who sold watercress, a creature so pale and thin she seemed to have been fashioned from the very morning mist, but who possessed a gaze of unnerving intensity and a knowledge of the fair’s secrets that would have shamed a spymaster.

‘It is gone, Bartholomew!’ wailed Mr. Puddle, seizing the pie-man’s greasy sleeve. ‘The Sage is gone! Won and lost and gone! I am undone, a ship without a rudder, a pie without a filling!’ Mr. Crimp, whose philosophy of life was that most troubles could be solved with a hot pie and a kind word, immediately proposed a remedy involving both. But Miss Sparrow, ever the pragmatist, cut him short. ‘Pies are for eating, Bartholomew, not for winning pigs,’ she chirped. ‘What we need is a scheme. That Vane is a sharper; he won’t play fair. We must be sharper still.’ It was then that little Molly Mudge spoke, her voice a dry rustle like leaves on pavement. ‘He keeps the cards in his coat,’ she whispered. ‘And he cheats with his left thumb.’ A council of war was convened behind the pie-stall, amidst the comforting fug of baking pastry. The plan, when it emerged, was a thing of beautiful, ramshackle ingenuity, a Rube Goldberg machine of deception and derring-do. Mr. Crimp would be the distraction; his bulk and his booming, good-natured challenge to a pie-eating contest would draw a crowd and occupy Vane’s attention. Miss Sparrow, with her quick fingers and sharper wits, would be the pickpocket, tasked with swapping Vane’s tainted deck for a deck of their own. And Molly Mudge, invisible in her poverty, would be the lookout, her small hand raised to signal the all-clear.

The attempt was made as dusk began to gather, the lanterns of the fair casting long, dancing shadows that seemed to mock their desperate enterprise. Mr. Crimp, with a roar that startled a flock of pigeons, challenged Silas Vane to a contest of gluttony, a challenge Vane, ever the showman, could not refuse. As the two men faced each other over a mountain of pork pies, a crowd gathered, their faces illuminated by the flickering lights.
