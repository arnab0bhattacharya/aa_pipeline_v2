The rain lashed the tall, narrow windows of the counting-house, and the wind roared down the chimney like some unquiet spirit. I sat at my desk, the great leather-bound ledgers open before me, but the columns of figures swam before my tired eyes. I was not thinking of the debits and credits of Vanhouten & Co. I was thinking of the upper room, where, for two hours that afternoon, I had striven, with a heavy and a hopeless heart, to beat the rudiments of the English tongue into the thick skulls of my employer’s sons.

I, Arthur Pendennis, a plain man of accounts and a stranger in this damp and ancient city of Antwerp, had been called from my normal duties a month since by Mr. Vanhouten. He was a man of iron will and a heavy hand, whose wealth was built upon the patient, unyielding labor of his life. He had spoken to me in his deep, guttural English, commanding rather than requesting that I instruct his boys, Leo and Karl, in the language of commerce. "They are backward, Mr. Pendennis," he had said, his broad brow clouding. "They are idle. You are English; you are precise. Teach them."

I had consented, fool that I was, mistaking the fierce fire of his ambition for a promise of authority.

The boys were indeed idle, but it was an idleness born of absolute license, nurtured in the luxurious hothouse of their mother’s affections. Madame Vanhouten was a woman of soft, pale beauty and a will far more stubborn, because far more secret, than her husband’s. She worshipped her sons with a blind, idolatrous devotion that left no room for discipline. She saw in them only angels; in me, an unjust taskmaster.

That afternoon’s lesson had been a very purgatory. I had set them to translate a simple passage from the Book of Proverbs, choosing the scripture because its plain, forceful words might strike some spark of duty in their sluggish minds. But they had lolled in their chairs, yawning, poking one another with concealed pins, scattering the crumbs of sweetmeats over their slates. When I spoke, they feigned a dull, bovine incomprehension. When I commanded attention, Leo, the elder and the bolder, had looked at me with his father’s dark eyes, but with a sneer his father would have struck from his face.

"Mother says we are too delicate for this dry drudgery," Leo had said, his voice a falsetto mimicry of my own earnest tone. "She says the English tongue is too harsh; it makes the throat sore."

I had felt a sudden heat rise in my own throat, a fierce temptation to seize the boy by his velvet collar and shake the insolence from him. But I had restrained myself. I was only the bookkeeper, a dependent. I had spoken with what severity I could muster, and they had laughed behind their hands.

Then the door had opened softly, and Madame Vanhouten had entered. She wore a wrapper of pale silk, and her light hair fell in languid curls about her neck. She looked at her sons with a smile of such melting tenderness that it turned my very stomach, knowing the poison it concealed. She looked at me with a glance of cool, measured offense.

"Mr. Pendennis," she had said, in her slow, precise English, "the boys are fatigued. You press them too hard. See how pale Leo is!"

"He is pale, madam, from the sweetmeats he has eaten, not from the labor of his mind," I had replied, the words escaping my lips before I could weigh them.

Her eyes had flashed a sudden, hard light—a spark of the true metal beneath the silk and softness. "You are severe, Mr. Pendennis. You forget yourself. My husband employs you to keep his books, not to tyrannize over his children."

She had swept the boys away with her, leaving me standing alone in the schoolroom, the open books and the scattered slates mute witnesses to my defeat. I had returned to the counting-house, to my figures, but the figures were a mockery. I thought of Mr. Vanhouten, due to return from his business at the docks by the evening post. He would ask me for a report. He would say, "How do the boys progress, Mr. Pendennis?" And I must answer.

The hours wore on. The daylight faded, and the gas lamps were lit, casting a yellow, sickly glare over the wet streets. I heard the heavy front door open and close, heard the master’s tread in the hall, the deep voice calling for lights. My heart beat thickly. I knew that soon I would be summoned.

The bell rang. I shut the ledger with a sigh that seemed to draw all the marrow from my bones, and went into the parlor. It was a handsome room, hung with dark velvet, warmed by a great fire. Mr. Vanhouten stood before it, his hands clasped behind his back, his heavy brows drawn together. Madame Vanhouten sat on a low stool near the hearth, embroidering. She did not look up as I entered.

"Well, Mr. Pendennis," said the merchant, turning his sharp gaze upon me, "the month is up. How do the boys progress? Do they take to the English? Are they industrious?"

The question hung in the air. I looked at him, and then my glance fell upon her. She was bending over her work, but I saw the faint, scornful smile that played about her lips. She was waiting for me to lie, or to equivocate, as I had seen other dependents do in this house. She felt secure in her power. She believed me a coward.

A sudden, fierce rebellion swelled in my breast. Why should I bear the burden of their deceit? Why should I be the scapegoat for their indolence? I was a man, though a poor one. I had a soul to save, and a conscience that would not be stilled by the fear of a master’s frown.
