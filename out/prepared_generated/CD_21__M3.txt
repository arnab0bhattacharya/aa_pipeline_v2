If any man living, or dead either for that matter, had been a perfect and unapproachable master in the profound science of Parish Economy, that man was Mr. Amos Snitchem. Mr. Snitchem was the clerk of the parish of Saint Bodkin’s-in-the-Fog, and he dwelt in a narrow, damp, melancholy little street off Throgmorton Lane, where the very houses seemed to lean their shoulders against one another to keep out the cold, and largely failed. He was a tall, gaunt, angular man, with a face like a frost-bitten turnip, who carried his chin elevated in the air, not from pride of spirit, but from a settled determination to keep his spectacles out of the reach of any charitable vapor that might chance to be wandering about. He wore a greatcoat of so rusty a black that it appeared to be slowly mourning its own decay, and he carried under his arm a battered leathern ledger, which he hugged to his breast as if it contained the entire wisdom of the Solomons, reduced to shillings and pence.

It was in the hard, bitter, pinching winter of the year eighteen hundred and thirty-something—for the precise year matters not, seeing that the frost was precisely as cruel as a parish beadle in all of them—that the Overseers of Saint Bodkin’s, finding the poor clamorous and the coal-wharf abundant, resolved to distribute a certain quantity of coals to the destitute. And to whom should they entrust this distribution but to Mr. Snitchem? "For," said the Overseers, nudging each other with the air of men who had made a wonderful discovery, "Snitchem is so economical, so wonderfully, so beautifully economical, that he will make a ton of coals go further than any ten men in the parish!"

Mr. Snitchem accepted the post with a thin, frosty smile. He knew his own worth. He had long maintained that the poor were lavish in their appetites, immoderate in their demands, and utterly devoid of that nice calculation which could extract a pint of warmth from a peck of dust. He had a profound belief in the moral efficacy of small coals. He believed that a great, roaring fire was an incentive to vice; and that a scanty, smouldering, apologetic little heap, which required constant coaxing and emitted more smoke than heat, was conducive to deep reflection, humility, and parish economy.

Accordingly, on the morning of the distribution, Mr. Snitchem took his station in the bleak churchyard, beside a monstrous black heap of coals, armed with his ledger, a coal-scoop, and a pair of leather gloves. The wind howled among the old tombs, driving the sleet through the railings, and turning the faces of the assembled poor to a uniform livid blue. They came shivering forward, wrapped in tattered blankets, old shawls, and such other melancholy defenses against the weather as their poverty could muster.

Mrs. Gamp, a woman of considerable bulk and a perpetual cold in the head, presented herself first.

"Lord love your precious economy, Mr. Snitchem!" said Mrs. Gamp, with a sniff that shook her bonnet. "Give us a good heavy sack, for the frost has got into my very marrow, and my little grandchild is turning blue in the cradle."

"Half a peck, Mrs. Gamp," said Mr. Snitchem, consulting his ledger with an abstracted air, as if he were reading the burial service. "Half a peck. The parish does not provide marrow-warmers, Mrs. Gamp. The parish provides coals. Half a peck."

"But, Mr. Snitchem—"

"Half a peck, Mrs. Gamp. Next!" cried Mr. Snitchem, flicking a solitary, ragged lump into the woman's apron with the air of a prince bestowing a dukedom.

And so it went on. The poor came, and the poor went away, shivering and dismayed, carrying their little allowances in their hands, their aprons, or in old tin cans, looking back at the great black heap with a hungry despair. Mr. Snitchem dealt out the lumps with the exactness of an apothecary, praising the hardness of the coal, its lasting properties, its absolute refusal to burn quickly—a virtue, he observed, that should endear it to all thinking men. He was in the very zenith of his economical glory, dealing out a particularly small half-scoop to a shivering old crossing-sweeper, when a name was muttered by the next applicant that caused his arm to stiffen, and his rusty pen to drop a blot of ink upon the page.

"What name?" said Mr. Snitchem, sharply.

"Snitchem," was the reply. "Martha Snitchem. Bell-yard, Mint Street."

Mr. Snitchem raised his frost-bitten turnip of a face, and peered over his spectacles at the applicant. It was an old woman, very bent, very thin, very ragged. She leaned upon a stick, and her face, wrinkled and furrowed like a winter apple, was shaded by a broken, battered bonnet that had evidently seen as many winters as herself. She did not look at him; she looked only at the coals, with a wistful, eager, hollow gaze.

"Martha Snitchem," repeated Mr. Snitchem, in a voice that sounded as if the frost had suddenly got into his own throat. "Mother?"

"Eh, Amos?" said the old woman, tremulously, raising her dim eyes to his face. "Is that you, Amos? My sight is so bad now, and the cold is so cruel. I didn't know it was you, my dear."

Mr. Snitchem stood for a moment, paralyzed. He had not seen his mother for three years. He had not visited her. He had been so deeply engaged in the parish books, and in the grand work of saving the parish tallow-candles, that he had entirely forgotten the old woman in the damp room in Bell-yard. And now, here she was, a pauper, come to the churchyard to beg a few coals from the hands of her own son!

"Hem!" said Mr. Snitchem, clearing his throat, and looking fiercely at the coal-scoop, as if it were to blame. "This is a—a parish matter, Mother. The parish allows half a peck to the destitute. You are destitute, I suppose?"
