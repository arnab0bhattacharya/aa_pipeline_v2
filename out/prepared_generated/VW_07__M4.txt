The morning had a way of arriving in pieces. First the pale wash over the rooftops, then the slow kindling of the plane trees along the Mall, and finally—at precisely the hour when the park gates were unlatched and the first clerks came hurrying through with their collars turned up—the light would find the water, and the water would give it back, trembling, in a thousand broken coins. Mrs. Ellesmere had come to know these pieces as one knows the stages of a long illness. She carried her paper bag of bread crusts, saved from the week’s loaves, and she walked the same path, past the pelicans who never looked at her, past the deck chairs stacked and waiting, to the bench beneath the third plane tree where the ducks gathered in their shabby, importunate way.

It was not for the ducks, of course. She knew that. The ducks were merely the pretext, the small ceremony that gave shape to an hour which would otherwise have had no shape at all. For here—just here, where the gravel curved and the water lapped at the stone lip—here was where Arthur had paused each morning on his way to the office, in those last years before the war, when he had been a young man with a rolled umbrella and a laugh that carried across the grass. He had liked the ducks. He had liked the absurd gravity of their paddling, the way they quarrelled over nothing, the way they would not be hurried. “They’re the only creatures in London,” he had said once, “who understand that the day is long.” And she had laughed then, standing at the window, watching him stoop to scatter crumbs from a bag she had given him—for he was always forgetting to eat, always giving away his lunch to the first hungry thing he met.

The bag was lighter now. She scattered the crusts in a slow arc, and the ducks came, and the water closed over the bread, and the morning went on being morning. A gull screamed somewhere above the trees. A man in a bowler hat passed, reading his newspaper as he walked, and she thought, with the peculiar flatness that had become her habit, that Arthur would have been thirty-one this April.

It was then that she noticed the boy.

He was sitting on the next bench, perhaps ten feet away, with a book open on his knee. He was not reading it. His eyes were fixed on the water, on the ducks, on the same small circle of gravel and light that held her own gaze, and there was something in the angle of his shoulders—something patient, something a little lost—that made her look away quickly and press her hands together in her lap. He was young. Eighteen, perhaps nineteen. The age was not difficult to calculate; she had been calculating it for seven years now, every time she saw a young man in the street, every time a boy’s voice cracked on the stairs of the omnibus. They were all Arthur, and none of them were, and the arithmetic of it had worn a groove in her mind that she could not smooth out.

He came again the next morning. And the next. He came, she began to understand, at the same hour she did, and he sat on the same bench, and he opened the same book—something with a dark cover, a history perhaps, or a volume of essays—and he did not read it. He watched the ducks. Or he watched the water. Or he watched nothing at all, which was a thing she understood. Once he took a paper bag from his pocket and scattered crumbs, clumsily, as if he were not used to it, and the ducks came to him and he smiled—a quick, surprised smile that vanished almost before it had arrived—and Mrs. Ellesmere felt something turn over in her chest, something that had been lying still for a very long time.

She did not want him to speak. That was the truth of it, and she held the truth carefully, turning it this way and that in the light of her mind. If he spoke, he would become a person. He would have a name, a history, a mother perhaps, a mother who did not know where he was on these bright, cold mornings, a mother who perhaps did not care, and then she would have to feel something for him that was not simply this—this quiet, this proximity, this sitting together in the same square of sunlight without any demand. If he spoke, he would break the spell. He would say something ordinary, something about the weather or the ducks or the book he was not reading, and she would have to answer, and her voice would come out strange and rusty, and he would look at her with that polite, puzzled look that young people gave to old women who sat alone in parks, and the spell would be broken.

But if he did not speak—if they simply sat, morning after morning, in this shared silence—then he could remain what he was: a shape, a shadow, a boy who might have been Arthur, who might have been any boy, who might have been no one at all. He could remain a possibility. And possibilities, she had learned, were the only things that did not disappoint.

She began to arrive earlier. She began to linger longer. She found herself choosing her bread with care—the softer pieces, the crusts with a little butter still clinging to them—and she told herself it was for the ducks. She found herself watching the boy’s hands, the way they turned the pages of his book without reading, the way they rested on his knee, the way they were—she noticed this with a small, sharp pang—a little chapped, a little red, as if he had been out in the cold too long. She found herself wondering if he had a coat.
