The letter from Mr. Penhale had been brief and businesslike, requesting the services of a qualified mining engineer to survey the Wheal Gwendraeth mine, long closed, with a view to possible reopening. The fee offered was generous, and Thomas Rennick, who had spent the better part of his fifty years in and under the ground, saw no reason to decline. He had known the Wheal Gwendraeth in its latter days, a marginal concern even then, its lodes pinching out and its lower levels troubled by water. That it should be considered worth the investment of fresh capital seemed to him optimistic, but optimism was the currency of the mining trade, and he was not one to refuse a paid excursion into the country.

He arrived at the village of St. Perran’s Cross on a grey afternoon in late October, the sky the colour of a slate roof and a fine, persistent drizzle greasing the cobbles. The Wheal Gwendraeth lay a half-mile to the north, its engine house a broken tooth on the skyline, its stack long since robbed of its upper courses. Mr. Penhale, a stout, anxious man with the look of one who had inherited his money rather than earned it, met him at the inn and drove him out in a trap, talking all the while of his plans for modernisation—a new winding engine, perhaps, and a proper drainage adit.

“She’s been shut these twenty years and more,” Penhale said, reining in at the gate. “My father had her, and his father before him. But the old levels are sound, I’m told. It’s only the price of tin that kept her closed.”

Rennick made a noncommittal noise. He had heard such talk before. He climbed down and looked at the site: the crumbling count-house, the overgrown spoil heaps, the black mouth of the adit with its heavy timber portal, the door itself swollen and warped with age. A padlock, red with rust, held it shut.

“You have the key?”

Penhale produced a ring from his pocket. “My father’s. I’ve not had it off this ring since he died.”

The lock resisted, then gave with a groan. The door swung inward on hinges that shrieked like gulls. A cold, damp breath came out of the earth, carrying the smell of old timber and stagnant water and something else—something faintly organic, like a cellar where vegetables had rotted. Rennick took the lamp Penhale had provided, lit it, and stepped inside.

The adit ran straight for perhaps thirty yards before opening into a wider gallery where the lode had been worked. The walls were rough, the floor uneven, the timbers of the roof sagging but still holding. Rennick walked slowly, his eyes on the ground, noting the debris of abandonment: a broken wheelbarrow, a rusted pick-head, the bleached bones of a rat. The air was still and cold.

He had gone perhaps a hundred yards when he stopped.

On a ledge of rock to his left, where a miner might have set it down while he ate his crib, sat a candle. It was not old. The wax was fresh, pale yellow, and the wick was blackened at the tip, as though it had been snuffed rather than burned to nothing. Rennick touched it. The wax was firm but not hard; it had not been there for years. He looked at the floor beneath the ledge and saw, in the dust, the print of a boot—a recent print, the edges sharp, the sole pattern distinct.

He straightened and called back to Penhale, who was waiting at the entrance. “Mr. Penhale, when was the last time anyone was down here?”

“Not since the closure,” Penhale called back. “Twenty years, as I said. Why?”

Rennick did not answer at once. He held the lamp higher and looked down the gallery. The passage continued into darkness, but the floor was clearer now, as though it had been swept or trodden. He walked on.

Fifty yards further, the gallery opened into a stope where the lode had been stoped upward, a black chamber that rose beyond the reach of his light. And here, propped against the wall, was a ladder.

It was a good ladder, well made, of ash, its rungs sound and its stiles straight. It was not twenty years old. It was not even five years old. Rennick ran his hand along the wood. It was smooth, worn by use, and there was mud on the feet of it, mud that had not yet dried to the consistency of the floor. Someone had been using this ladder, and recently.

He set the lamp down and climbed. The ladder led to an upper level, a narrow gallery that ran back into the hill. At the top, he found more evidence: a coil of rope, newer than anything else in the mine; a tin mug; a candle stub, burned down to the holder. And there, on the wall, a mark—a chalk mark, a number, and beside it a date. The date was three days old.

Rennick climbed back down and stood for a moment in the dark, thinking. The mine was not abandoned. Someone was working it, and working it without the owner’s knowledge. The question was who, and how they had got in. The adit was the only entrance he knew of, and it had been locked. But there were other ways into old mines—shafts, cross-cuts, levels driven from neighbouring ground. He had heard of such things before: small men, tributers, working a forgotten corner of a mine for their own profit, selling the ore through quiet channels. It was not unknown in Cornwall.

He made his way back to the entrance, where Penhale stood wringing his hands.

“Well?” Penhale said. “Is she sound?”

“She’s sound enough,” Rennick said. “But she’s not empty.”

Penhale stared. “Not empty? What do you mean?”

Rennick told him. The candle, the ladder, the chalk mark. Penhale’s face went from anxious to angry to something else—something like fear.
