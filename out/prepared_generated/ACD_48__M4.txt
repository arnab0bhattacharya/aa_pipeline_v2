The letter reached me on a Tuesday morning, brought up with the coffee and the usual bundle of newspapers, and it was some minutes before I had done with it, for the hand was small and firm and had plainly been written in haste, and the paper smelled faintly of salt and of some sharp scent that I took at first to be camphor. It was signed “E. Marchbanks,” and at the foot of it there was a postscript which ran: “I beg that you will not communicate with me at my lodgings, as I am watched. I will be in the second pew from the door of St. Cuthbert’s, off the Vauxhall Bridge Road, on Thursday at eleven, and I will wear a grey cloak and a small gold brooch in the shape of a fern.”

“You observe, Watson,” said Holmes, when I had handed it across to him, “that the lady has been at some pains to make herself identifiable, which argues a certain practical turn of mind; and that she writes of being watched, which argues either a genuine peril or a disordered fancy. The scent upon the paper is eau de Cologne of an inferior quality, such as is sold at the chemists’ shops about the docks. The salt is not from the sea, for the letter was posted in London. It is from her own person, or from her gloves. She has come off a long voyage and has not yet settled. New Zealand, I should say, from the fern.”

“You cannot know that.”

“My dear fellow, the fern is the badge of that colony, and is worn there upon brooches and upon buttons and upon the hearts of the exiled. Add to it that the writing slopes a little to the right, as the writing of persons does who have been taught to keep their paper steady upon a table that moves, and you have a woman who has crossed the world and has brought her habits with her. But here is the more interesting point—that she writes to me at all. A woman with a solicitor does not write to a consulting detective. Therefore the solicitor is the trouble.”

St. Cuthbert’s was a soot-blackened church of the last century, standing back from the road behind a railing, and at eleven o’clock upon the Thursday it was empty save for an old woman who dusted the pews and a clergyman who was busy with the flowers at the altar. The lady in the grey cloak was in the second pew, and she rose as we came up the aisle. She was perhaps six-and-twenty, brown from the sun, with a direct and level gaze and a hand that was hard and dry from the handling of reins.

“You are Mr. Holmes,” she said. “I had expected a—forgive me—a more elderly person. But I have read of your cases, and I have no one else to whom I can turn. My name is Ellen Marchbanks. My father held a sheep station near the Rakaia, and he died in the winter, and I am come home—home, though I was born in this city and have not seen it since I was four years old—to take up what he left me. It is not a great fortune, but it is a sure one, and it is in the funds. There is a man named Pargiter, who calls himself the solicitor of the estate, and who met me at the dock at Tilbury with a cab and a bunch of hothouse flowers and a manner that I did not like. He had a paper for me to sign before I had so much as seen my trunk taken off the tender.”

“What was the paper?”

“I have it.” She drew it from beneath her cloak, folded small. “He said it was a form of receipt for certain moneys already advanced to my father. I said I would read it at my leisure. He was very pressing, but I am not a fool, and I put it in my pocket and said I would sign it in the morning. In the morning I read it. It is a power of attorney, drawn so that he may sell and convey and receive upon my behalf, and it is dated a fortnight before I landed.”

Holmes had taken the paper and was holding it to the light of a window.

“It is drawn by a clerk of some competence,” said he, “but not by a solicitor. Observe the phrase ‘I do hereby irrevocably constitute and appoint’—that is the language of a commercial traveller who has copied it from a printed form and has not known where to stop. A solicitor would have said ‘appoint’ and have been done with it. And the paper is good, but the seal is not. The seal has been made with a penny stamp and a pencil-end, and no solicitor in London would so far forget himself. Where are your lodgings?”

“In a house in Lupus Street. Mr. Pargiter found them for me. He said they were the rooms of a cousin of his, and that I should be comfortable and cheap.”

“And you have been watched?”

“There is a man who stands at the corner of the street in the evening with a barrow of oranges, and who does not sell oranges. He has sold none to me, and I have passed him nine times. And on Tuesday a letter came to me from Mr. Pargiter, enclosing a cheque for twenty pounds upon account, and asking me to call at his office in the Strand upon Friday at four, and to bring the power of attorney with me, signed. He said that he wished to complete the arrangements before the quarter-day.”

“You have not signed it?”

“I have not.”
