I had passed many months in the solitary chambers of my inheritance, a dwelling whose ancient timber had known the tread of three generations, and which now sheltered only myself and the living ruin of its master. My grandfather, the venerable Herr Albrecht von Steiger, had become a stranger to his own blood. His mind, once a fortress of learning and gentle authority, had been reduced by the slow, merciless tide of age to a barren shore, upon which the flotsam of memory no longer washed. He knew me not; he knew my mother, his own dear daughter, not; he sat in his chair by the hearth and watched the flames as a traveller might watch a foreign sunrise, with a vacant and wondering eye. This spectacle of decay, this daily effacement of a soul I so revered, was a torment that goaded my spirit to an unnatural activity. I, who had devoted my youth to the secrets of the alchemist’s art, resolved that where nature had failed, my science should triumph.

My laboratory, a high-vaulted chamber overlooking the slate roofs of the university town of Altenburg, became the theatre of my desperate ambition. The air, thick with the cloying scent of ether and the sharp tang of metallic salts, was my sole companion. For weeks I toiled, my face pale in the flickering light of the alembic, my hands stained with the very essence of my purpose. I believed—oh, the fatal hubris of the thinking man!—that memory was not a spirit, but a substance; a subtle humour of the brain that could be replenished, a faded pigment that could be restored to its original vibrancy by a potent and carefully measured tincture. I sought not merely to cure, but to reverse the very arrow of time, to call back the years that had fled and restore to my grandfather the full and perfect mirror of his own life.

At length, the elixir was complete. It lay in a phial of crystal, a liquid of such profound and abyssal darkness that it seemed to drink the very light from the air around it. My heart, a tempest of hope and dread, beat with a violence that shook my frame. I descended to the great hall where my grandfather sat, his form a shadow in the encroaching twilight. With hands that trembled, I brought the phial to his lips. He drank without question, as a child might, his vacant gaze fixed upon my own. I watched, scarcely breathing, as a change, subtle at first, then swift and terrible, began to manifest upon his countenance. The blankness in his eyes receded, replaced first by a flicker of confusion, then by the dawning light of recognition. A slow smile spread across his lips. “Emil,” he whispered, his voice a dry rustle of leaves, yet it was the voice of my childhood, the voice I had thought lost to me forever. “My boy. How you have grown.” Tears of a joy so intense it bordered on agony streamed down my face; I fell at his knees and wept, believing I had performed a miracle, that I had wrested my beloved from the very grasp of oblivion.

But the mind, once unlocked, does not open only to pleasant gardens. It is a vast and echoing mansion, and in its deepest cellars lie the things best left undisturbed. The light of recognition in my grandfather’s eyes soon hardened into a glare of dawning horror. His hands, which had been resting placidly on his lap, clenched the arms of his chair until the knuckles were white. “The birch wood,” he murmured, his voice now a hoarse and frantic thing. “The storm… the water was so black… We left him there.” I drew back, my joy turning to a cold stone in my gut. “Grandfather, what do you speak of? It is I, Emil.” He did not seem to hear me. His eyes were fixed on some dreadful scene enacted in the theatre of his restored mind. “He would not sign the deed,” he cried, his voice rising to a frantic pitch. “Your father’s hand upon his shoulder… the stone… oh, God, the sound it made upon his skull. We swore an oath, a blood oath, in this very room. Forty years… and his name was never spoken again.”

A paralysis of the soul seized me. The fire in the hearth seemed to throw no warmth; the shadows in the corners of the room deepened into monstrous shapes. The crime he confessed—a crime of avarice and kin-murder, perpetrated by my own father and covered by the silence of my house—was a poison more virulent than any I had ever distilled. The tincture, my great and glorious achievement, had not been a key to a treasure-house, but a picklock to a charnel-house. I had sought to restore a man, and had instead resurrected a ghost, and with it, the bloody secret that had been the foundation of our family’s prosperity. My grandfather, his mind now a clear and terrible mirror, stared at me, and in his eyes I saw not the love of a moment before, but the reflection of my own horror and complicity. He had remembered everything. He remembered the man he had killed, and he remembered the boy he had raised in the shadow of that silence. The suffering I had sought to cure was as nothing to the agony I had now created; for I had given him back his past, and in so doing, had given myself a inheritance not of land or gold, but of a sin I could never atone for. I was the keeper of his memory now, and its terrible weight had fallen upon my own shoulders. In the profound silence of that accursed room, with the storm of my grandfather’s recollection raging around me, I understood that there are fates far worse than oblivion.
