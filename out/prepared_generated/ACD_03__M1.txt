It was upon one of those raw, dripping mornings in the month of November that the matter was brought to my attention. The fog, a foul yellow vapor, had crept up from the river and lay thick in the streets of Covent Garden, muffling the cries of the costermongers and lending a ghostly aspect to the wagons of fruit and flowers. My companion, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, stood at the window of our lodgings in Baker Street, his thin, eager form silhouetted against the gray light, as he observed the sluggish progress of the hansoms below.

“A day for indoor pursuits, Watson,” he remarked, turning from the window with a restless energy that belied his words. “The city is a perfect laboratory of crime on such a morning. Every shadow conceals a potential clue, every hurried footfall a desperate purpose. Yet the official police are blind to it all. They see only the mud and the damp.”

His discourse was interrupted by the sharp summons of the bell, followed by the tread of Mrs. Hudson upon the stairs. A moment later she announced a Mrs. Eliza Croft, of the firm of Croft & Son, Florists, of the Covent Garden market.

Our visitor was a woman of perhaps forty-five years, with a face that spoke of early rising and hard work, yet her manner was one of quiet composure. She wore a neat black dress, and her hands, though scrubbed raw, were steady as she clasped the reticule in her lap.

“Mr. Holmes,” she began, her voice clear and low, “I am told you are a man who can solve puzzles that are beyond the reach of the ordinary constable. I find myself in the possession of a small one, and it has begun to unsettle me.”

“Pray be seated, madam, and tell us the facts,” said Holmes, steepling his fingers and fixing her with his keen, grey eyes. “I find that the most trifling of cases can often prove the most instructive.”

“For three years,” said Mrs. Croft, “my shop has supplied the morning flowers for the Grand Criterion Hotel in the Strand. It is a large and fashionable establishment, and our business is regular. For the past six months, one order has stood out from the rest. Every morning, at precisely eight o’clock, a fresh white Madonna lily is to be delivered to a specific room. Room 314.”

“A white lily,” Holmes murmured. “A flower of purity, and, in the language of the undertaker, of death. A curious choice for a daily tribute.”

“Indeed, sir. The order is placed by telephone, always by the same soft-voiced man, and the payment is made in advance to our account at the bank. The strange part is this: my errand boy, a sharp lad named Tim, reported to me that the hotel’s ledger, which he sometimes glimpses at the porter’s desk, lists Room 314 as vacant. Unoccupied. Yet the flowers are ordered, and the bell for that room is rung to summon the hall-porter for the delivery.”

Holmes leaned forward, a flicker of interest in his eyes. “You have verified this yourself?”

“Not the ledger, sir. But I have had reason to doubt the hotel’s records. Two days ago, young Tim was met at the service entrance by a new porter, a man he had never seen before. This fellow was tall and broad, with a nasty scar that pulled one eyebrow up into a permanent sneer. He took the lily, and when Tim mentioned the room number, the man told him sharply that there was no such guest, and that the order was a mistake. He turned the boy away without a word of explanation. When Tim insisted, the porter took him by the collar and fairly shoved him out into the mews. The lad was white to the lips when he returned to me.”

“A new porter, you say?” Holmes’s voice was sharp. “And this was the first time he had appeared?”

“The first time Tim had seen him. I made my own inquiries. The hall-porter on duty, a reliable man named Higgins, swears he has hired no one new. He knows nothing of this scarred fellow. It is as if the man appeared from nowhere to intercept that single delivery.”

Holmes sprang to his feet and began to pace the room. “Excellent! Watson, this is capital! A phantom guest, a daily lily, and a menacing interloper who vanishes as quickly as he came. The plot thickens, as they say in the penny dreadfuls. Mrs. Croft, you have done right to bring this to me. Tell me, what is your own theory in the matter?”

The florist hesitated. “I have no theory, sir. Only a fear. It feels wrong. Flowers are for the living, for joy, for comfort. This feels like a secret, and secrets of that kind are rarely wholesome. I do not wish my boy harmed, nor do I wish to be an unwitting party to some mischief.”

“Precisely,” said Holmes. “Your duty is to your trade and your conscience. Now, we must act with some speed. Do not send the boy tomorrow. Instead, I shall go in his place.”

The following morning, the fog had lifted, leaving behind a crisp, cold air that smelled of coal smoke and damp stone. Holmes, dressed in the plain clothes of a working florist, carried the long pasteboard box containing the single white lily. I accompanied him, posing as a friend from his trade. We took up a position across the Strand from the Grand Criterion, a vast edifice of Portland stone and plate glass.

At five minutes past eight, a man emerged from a side entrance of the hotel. He was of middle height, dressed in the dark uniform of a hotel servant, but he moved with a furtive haste that was out of keeping with his station. He scanned the street, his eyes lingering for a moment on our doorway before he turned and hurried westward.

“The scarred porter, I presume,” I whispered.
