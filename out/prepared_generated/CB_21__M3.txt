The morning had dawned in sullen sympathy with my fate. A low, leaden sky pressed upon the flat Lincolnshire wastes, and a piercing wind swept across the desolate moors, bearing the sharp, icy lash of impending rain. I stood at the iron gates of Thornfield Grange—nay, not Thornfield, but the equally grim estate of Blackwood Moors—with nothing but a small leather satchel clasped in my bare hands, and a few shillings jingling hollowly in my pocket. I had been dismissed without a character. The heavy oak door had shut behind me with a dull, final thud, as absolute and merciless as the sentence pronounced by the mistress of the house.

Mrs. Crewe’s face, pale and hard as marble, had floated before me in the gloom of the drawing-room. "You have betrayed my trust, Miss Vance," she had said, her voice vibrating with a cold, unyielding fury that barely masked her triumph. "You have encouraged the addresses of my son, seeking to entrap him into a dishonourable alliance. Gather your things. You shall leave my house at dawn."

I had not deigned to plead. To defend myself against a charge so monstrous, so entirely fabricated, would have been to grant it the dignity of a debate. I knew my own heart; it was clear of any such vulgar ambition. If Edward Crewe had cast sheep's eyes at me, if he had waylaid me in the dim corridors with his sighs and his foolish, wavering protestations, it was no doing of mine. I had kept my distance, my eyes down, my tongue strictly governed by the rigid rules of my station. But Edward was a coward. When his mother, sharp-eyed and suspicious, had confronted him, he had lacked the manhood to confess his own importunity. He had stammered, flushed a dull red, and looked at the carpet—leaving me to bear the brunt of his weakness and her wrath.

I set my face towards the north road. The nearest town was ten miles away, a long, desolate walk across a barren landscape, but I did not falter. I was entirely alone. I had no friends in this flat, damp county; my kin were dead or scattered. My only companion was the fierce, unquenchable fire of my own indignation.

The wind struck me violently as I breasted the first rise beyond the Blackwood gates. It was a tempestuous, moaning wind, sweeping over the brown, withered heath and bending the bare branches of the scattered ash-trees. The clouds drove across the sky in dark, tumultuous masses, and presently the rain began to fall—not a gentle, intermittent shower, but a heavy, driving storm that drenched me to the skin in ten minutes. My thin grey cloak clung damply to my shoulders, and my boots, thin-soled and worn, were soon sodden with the black mud of the lane.

Yet, amidst the physical discomfort, a strange, stern exaltation lifted my spirit. I was outcast, penniless, and my reputation was blackened; but I was free. I was not bound to the creeping, unmanly Edward, nor subjected any longer to the freezing, tyrannical rule of his mother. The world lay before me, bleak and hostile, perhaps, but wide. I felt within me a strength I had not known I possessed—a resilience that mocked at the fury of the elements. Let the storm rave! It was less pitiless than the human hearts I had just left behind.

I walked on for hours, my pace unconsciously quickening. The rain ceased at last, and a pale, watery sun struggled through the breaking clouds, casting long, spectral shadows across the fen. I was weary, my limbs ached, and a sharp hunger gnawed at my side; but I pressed forward, my mind a tumult of thought. Why was I thus punished? Because I was poor, and a dependent. Because I had dared to exist in a sphere where my presence was deemed an encroachment. The injustice of it burned within me like a living coal.

A carriage swept past me, its wheels splashing me with mud from the miry ruts. I caught a glimpse of a silken-lined interior, a warm foot-muff, and a face that looked out with cold, incurious eyes upon the solitary wayfarer. I did not flinch. Let them pass! The gulf between us was fixed, not by nature, but by the cruel artificialities of society. I had my own soul, my own integrity, and no wealth could purchase that inward peace which springs from a clear conscience.

As the afternoon waned, the spire of St. Botolph's church rose against the darkening sky, a beacon of hope in the desolation. I had reached the outskirts of the town. The streets were narrow and ill-paved, the houses low and forbidding, but they promised shelter, and shelter I must find. I sought out a modest inn, its signboard swinging creakingly in the wind, and pushed open the heavy door.

The common room was low-roofed and smoky, warmed by a great fire that roared up the cavernous chimney. A group of farmers and labourers were gathered about the hearth, their voices raised in boisterous conversation; but they fell silent as I entered, eyeing me with a curiosity that was not unkindly mixed with surprise. I was, I doubted not, a strange figure: a young woman, pale and drenched, with eyes that burned with an unnatural brightness, and a bearing that, despite my forlorn condition, was erect and unyielding.

I approached the hostess, a stout, bustling woman with a shrewd but good-natured face. "I seek a room for the night," I said, and my voice, though quiet, did not tremble. "And I would be grateful for some bread and tea."

She looked me over, her glance resting on my wet garments and my mud-stained boots. "You've had a rough journey, miss," she observed, in a tone that was more compassionate than I had expected. "Aye, you may have the small chamber at the back. 'Tis but a poor place, but 'tis clean and warm."
