I am a Poet. I set this down at the head of my narrative, not in a spirit of boastfulness, but as a simple statement of fact, as another man might say, ‘I am a carpenter,’ or ‘I am a barrister.’ For it is a fact, as immutable and as plain as the soot upon the windows of my lodging, that my soul is fashioned from a finer clay than that which constitutes the souls of my fellow-men, and that my destiny is not to grub for pennies in the dust of commerce, but to soar upon the wings of song. It was for this purpose, and this alone, that I had taken up my residence in the Borough, in a small back room at the house of Mrs. Prattle, a location I chose for its very obscurity and its proximity to the great, heaving, glorious, and altogether poetic heart of London.

My room was a narrow cell, furnished with a bed that creaked a lamentation whenever I so much as sighed upon it, a single chair of a rickety and despondent disposition, and a table which served as my altar, my desk, and my sole repository of worldly goods. The wallpaper, a pattern of faded ferns, seemed to droop in sympathy with my own melancholic temperament, and the window, which looked out upon a brick wall adorned with a single, stubborn vine, framed a view so picturesque in its desolation that it was a constant spur to my muse. Here, I waited. I waited for Inspiration, that celestial visitor, to descend upon me and to dictate the immortal verses that lay coiled within my breast. I had my ink, I had my paper, I had a pen of goose-quill which I felt certain had been plucked from the wing of a Pegasus in a former life. All that was wanting was the spark.

My landlady, Mrs. Prattle, was a woman of a cheerful and voluble disposition, a quality which I found, in the main, to be a great impediment to the contemplative life. She was a small, round woman, with a face like a polished apple and a cap that was forever askew. ‘A poet, Mr. Tumbledown, a poet!’ she would cry, bustling into my room without the ceremony of a knock, her arms laden with coals or a crust of bread. ‘Well, I never! And is it a good trade, sir? Does it pay the rent, is what I should like to know. Not that I wish to pry, but a widow woman, Mr. Tumbledown, with a mortgage hanging over her head like the sword of—what was his name?—a poet would know. Well, it hangs there, sir, and it’s a heavy thing to hang. Mr. Grub, the baker, was asking after you again this morning. “When is your poet going to pay for his bread?” says he. “When his muse rises,” says I. “His muse can rise and fall,” says he, “but my flour is flour, and it must be paid for.” What could I say to that, sir?’

To these practical considerations, I could only offer a sigh and a wave of my hand, as if to brush away the vulgar cobwebs of commerce. ‘My dear madam,’ I would say, ‘you might as well ask the eagle when he intends to settle his account with the wind. The spirit moves when it moves. It is a question of destiny, not of days.’

But it was not only Mrs. Prattle who failed to comprehend the sublime nature of my calling. There was a young woman, a Miss Prudence Pinch, who kept a small glove-shop directly opposite my window. From my vantage point, I could observe her at her work, her nimble fingers sorting her wares, her sensible bonnet always straight, her gaze direct and unnervingly clear. She was, I confess, a handsome girl, in a way that was more solid than ethereal, more earth than fire. Her opinion of me, I soon gathered, was of the lowest. One afternoon, as I stood contemplating the clouds for a simile, our eyes met across the street. She was holding up a stout leather gauntlet, and I fancied I saw in her expression a look of such profound and pitying disapproval that it quite knocked the metaphor out of my head.

Her views were confirmed one evening as I was returning home, my pockets empty and my head full of a truly magnificent, though unfinished, ode to the moon. I had been attempting to barter a promise of future literary fame for a cut of mutton at the butcher’s, an enterprise which had ended in nothing but a short and rather one-sided lecture on the nature of value. As I passed her shop, Miss Pinch was putting up her shutters.

‘A hard day at the anvil of the Muses, Mr. Tumbledown?’ she asked, her voice as clear and sharp as a new needle.

‘The forge of genius is never cold, Miss Pinch,’ I replied, with as much dignity as I could muster.

‘It seems to be cold enough to freeze the baker’s heart,’ she retorted, not unkindly. ‘A pair of gloves, sir, would be of more use to you than an ode to the moon. These hands,’ she said, holding up her own, which were chapped and red from her work, ‘are not made for idle fancies. They are made for stitching seams and keeping the cold out. There is a poetry in that, too, if one has eyes to see it.’ She then said a thing that struck me with the force of a blow. ‘A poet who cannot pay for his bread, sir, is merely a hungry man with a turn for phrase.’
