The smell of the workshop was a mixture of machine oil, shaved ash, and the faint, sweet tang of the linseed varnish Thomas Avery had brushed onto the canvas wings. It was a smell he had come to love more than the scent of the Downs after a rain. For two years, the old tithe barn on his father’s farm had been his sanctuary and his obsession. The villagers of Ashcombe Parva, when they thought of it at all, called it ‘young Avery’s folly,’ and they would shake their heads with a kind of fond condescension. Thomas, a gangly young man with spectacles perpetually sliding down his nose and hands permanently stained with grease, was tolerated. His machine was not.

It was a curious thing, a great dragonfly of wood, wire, and fabric. A central chassis of sturdy ash held a small, sputtering petrol engine that Thomas had salvaged from a broken-down motorcar. Above it, two tiers of wings, braced with a hundred tension wires, stretched nearly thirty feet from tip to tip. Below, a tricycle of bicycle wheels, sprung with rubber cords, offered a fragile promise of a gentle landing. Thomas had spent his every waking hour, and every penny he earned from mending farm implements, on this creature of his heart. He had tested it in pieces, run the engine until it roared with a satisfying, if deafening, fury, and towed the finished frame across the meadow to ensure its wheels ran true. But he had not yet flown. He was waiting for the perfect day: a stiff, steady breeze from the sea, and the courage to trust his own creation.

The news that he intended to offer rides to the villagers was met with a predictable storm of reaction. The first to call was Mr. Percival Groat, the squire. His motorcar, a gleaming Wolseley, had crunched to a halt outside the barn, scattering a flock of chickens. The squire, a portly man whose waxed moustache was as rigid as his opinions, surveyed the flying machine with the eye of a man appraising a prize heifer.

‘Avery,’ he boomed, his voice echoing in the vast space. ‘This is a marvel. A positive marvel. The future of transport, what?’ He walked around the machine, tapping a wing strut with his cane. ‘I must have it. I’ll give you a hundred pounds for it, here and now. Think of it, man! You could buy a proper workshop, set yourself up in business. No more mucking about with ploughshares.’

Thomas, wiping his hands on a rag, felt a flush of pride, followed immediately by a cold knot of possession. ‘She’s not for sale, Squire. I built her to fly, not to be a showpiece in your conservatory.’

Groat’s face reddened. ‘Don’t be a fool, boy. What use is it to you? You’ll kill yourself, and that’ll be the end of it. I’m offering you a future. A sensible one.’

‘I’ve got a future,’ Thomas said, his voice quieter than he’d intended. ‘It’s right here.’

The squire had harrumphed, climbed back into his Wolseley, and departed in a cloud of dust and indignation. Thomas watched him go, his heart hammering against his ribs. The hundred pounds was a fortune. But the machine was his soul.

The vicar’s visit was of a different nature entirely. The Reverend Mr. Alistair Finch was a thin, pale man with a voice like a mournful cello. He found Thomas in the meadow, adjusting the tension of the wing-warping wires. He did not look at the machine, but fixed his gaze on the young man.

‘Thomas,’ he began, his tone heavy with concern. ‘I have heard tell of your… contraption. And of your intention to take our flock into the heavens upon it.’

‘It’s just a machine, Vicar,’ Thomas replied, though his hands stilled on the wires.

‘It is a challenge to Providence,’ the vicar intoned, clasping his hands behind his back. ‘God gave us the earth to walk upon. He gave the birds the sky. To seek to usurp their domain is an act of hubris, a tempting of fate. Think of the example you set. If the Lord had meant for us to fly, He would have given us wings. This is a dangerous vanity, Thomas. I implore you, for the sake of your immortal soul and the peace of this parish, to dismantle it.’

Thomas felt a familiar stubbornness rise within him. He had sat through a thousand of the vicar’s sermons, each one a gentle but firm reminder of his place in the grand, unchanging order of things. ‘The Lord also gave us minds to reason and hands to build, Vicar. If He didn’t want us to fly, why did He give us the birds to learn from?’

The vicar sighed, a sound of profound disappointment. ‘The serpent was subtler than any beast of the field, Thomas. Be wary of the wisdom of the world.’ He turned and walked back towards the village, his black coat a stark silhouette against the green of the Downs.

The vicar’s sermon the following Sunday was a thinly veiled attack on ‘the Icarus of our parish,’ a warning against the ‘sinful pride of invention’ that sought to ‘tear the secrets from God’s own heavens.’ The congregation had shuffled and glanced sideways at the Avery family pew. Thomas’s mother had wept quietly into her handkerchief. Thomas himself had sat rigid, his jaw set, the familiar smell of the barn’s oil and varnish seeming to cling to him like a shroud of guilt.

It was this atmosphere of doubt and disapproval that made the appearance of Elsie Marlow at the barn door so extraordinary. She was the squire’s niece, a girl of nineteen with hair the colour of ripe wheat and eyes that held a spark of mischief that her uncle’s presence could never quite extinguish. Thomas had admired her from afar for years, watching her ride her pony with a fearless grace, or laugh with the other girls by the village well. She was everything he was not: confident, bright, and untroubled by the opinions of others.
