The mist that hung over the harbor of Calleva did not merely obscure the water; it seemed to choke the very air, a thick, gray shroud that smothered the breath of the living and the memory of the dead. It was a night of profound and aching stillness, broken only by the low, mournful groan of the wind as it tore at the rigging of the *Aethelgard*, a vessel that lay anchored in the deep, trembling not from the force of the gale, but from the weight of its cargo. I stood upon the shingle beach, my feet sinking into the cold, wet sand, watching the faint, sickly glow of the ship’s lanterns piercing the darkness. They were not lights of hope, but of warning; they marked the boundary between the pure and the corrupted, the living and the dying. Within that vessel, huddled in the dark and damp hold, lay the refugees of a continent that had forgotten the name of mercy. They were the exiles of a world ravaged by a pestilence so new and so terrible that it had stripped the flesh from their bones and the sanity from their minds, leaving them as ghosts of their former selves, drifting upon the tides of despair toward our shores.

The town of Calleva, with its stone walls and its proud, ancient history, had turned its face away from the suffering. The bells, which had once rung out in celebration of harvests and victories, now tolled a funeral knell for our own compassion. The citizens, driven by a terror that was both rational and insane, barred the gates and barred their hearts. They spoke of infection, of decay, of the inevitable rot that would spread from those wretched bodies to our own clean, safe streets. They called it prudence; I called it a crime against the very essence of our humanity. To see human beings, created in the image of God, shunned as if they were carrion, was a sight that turned my soul to ice. I am a physician, and my hands have been trained to heal, to bind, to comfort; yet in this moment, I felt the full, crushing weight of my impotence. I could not cure the disease of the body without first curing the disease of the spirit that had infected our town.

It was Thomas Halloway, the Mayor, who had summoned the council to the old hall on the cliff. I went to him not as a petitioner, but as a witness to the horror that was unfolding at the water's edge. The hall was dim, lit by candles that flickered and sputtered in the draft, casting long, dancing shadows on the stone walls. The council members sat in a semicircle, their faces grim, their eyes fixed upon the table before them as if the answer to their dilemma lay in the grain of the wood. Halloway, a man of iron will and rigid morality, presided over the gathering. He was a man who believed that order was the highest virtue, and that the safety of the many must always outweigh the suffering of the few.

"You speak of duty, Doctor," Halloway said, his voice cold and precise, cutting through the silence like a blade. "But what is the duty of a man to those who bring death with them? Is it to share their fate, or to preserve the life of his community? You ask us to open our doors to a plague. You ask us to invite ruin upon our homes, our children, our graves."

I stood before them, my hands trembling not from fear, but from a rage that burned in my chest, a fire that I could not quench. "I ask you," I replied, my voice rising, gaining strength as it poured from the deepest recesses of my heart, "to look upon those who suffer and to see not monsters, but men. They are not the carriers of death; they are the victims of it. They are the broken remnants of a civilization that has fallen, and they come to us not as conquerors, but as beggars for mercy. If we turn them away, we do not save ourselves; we condemn ourselves. For what is a society that abandons its most vulnerable? Is it not a society that has already perished, though its walls still stand and its markets still trade?"

A murmur went through the room. Some looked at me with pity, as one looks at a madman; others with anger, as one looks at an intruder. But Halloway’s face remained unchanged, a mask of stone. "Your eloquence is wasted on us, Doctor. We are not moved by words. We are moved by the fear of our own destruction. The ship must be turned back. It must be burned if it will not leave."

The word *burned* struck me with the force of a physical blow. To burn them—to reduce human beings to ash and smoke—was a thought so monstrous, so utterly devoid of all feeling, that it seemed to suspend time itself. I looked at the faces of the council, searching for a spark of humanity, a flicker of that innate goodness that I had always believed to be the foundation of our existence. I found only fear, cold and hard as the rocks that formed the cliffs above us. They were afraid of the unknown, of the uncontrollable, of the chaos that the refugees represented. They wanted the world to be safe, predictable, and clean. But the world is not clean. It is stained with the blood of the innocent and the tears of the forgotten.

I thought of my own youth, of the lessons I had learned from my father, who had walked the earth with a gentle step and a heavy heart. He had told me that the true test of a man is not how he treats those who are powerful, but how he treats those who are powerless.
