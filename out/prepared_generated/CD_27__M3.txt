If any wandering ghost of a departed bailiff, hovering in the cloudy rafters of the Old Bailey, desired a perfect specimen of human helplessness delivered over to the tender mercies of the law, he could not have found a more complete embodiment than in the person of Mr. Jacob Grubble. Mr. Grubble was a coal-heaver. He possessed a back broad enough to carry half a ton of Wapping’s finest soot, and hands knotted like the roots of ancient elms; yet there he stood, hemmed in by the oaken dock, looking as helplessly at the judge as a baby might look at a rattle, with great, gloomy tears tumbling down his sooty cheeks. He was charged with the heinous offence of purloining four pounds, ten shillings, and sixpence in copper and silver from the counting-house till of his employer, Mr. Silas Mudge, a wholesale coal merchant of Wapping Wall.

To declare that Mr. Grubble was innocent was a simple matter. To prove it, amidst the grinding machinery of justice, was quite another. The evidence was as plain as a plum pudding. Mr. Mudge swore to the loss of the money; Mr. Mudge’s head clerk, a thin, pale young man by the name of Mr. Viper, swore that he had counted the till at noon, and it was four pounds, ten shillings, and sixpence short; and a police constable swore that he had found a shilling in Mr. Grubble’s pocket. The shilling was, unhappily, a very old shilling, bearing the date of a bygone reign, and Mr. Mudge swore again that he had received that very shilling from a barge-master only the day before. It was a monstrous, crushing coincidence. The young gentleman who had been thrust into the arena to defend Mr. Grubble—a briefless student by the name of Mr. Edwin Pumphrey—felt much as a man might feel who is commanded to drain a river with a teaspoon. He stared at his brief, he stared at the ceiling, and he stared at the unfortunate Grubble, but inspiration sat stubbornly in the back of his head and refused to come forward.

Now, it is a truth universally unacknowledged by those who sit in high places, that the smallest cog in the great machine of human affairs is often the one that stops the entire apparatus from spinning wildly out of true. The smallest cog in the legal establishment of Mr. Pumphrey was an office boy by the name of Tobias Tilt. Tobias was a youth of thirteen, with a nose that seemed perpetually engaged in smelling out a mystery, and a pair of eyes that darted about the court like two black beetles in a hurry. He had been dispatched that very morning to deliver a summons in Wapping, and having performed that errand with extraordinary rapidity, had lingered about the steps of the Old Bailey, waiting for his master to emerge from the temple of justice.

Tobias, pressing his nose against the railing of the gallery, surveyed the scene below. He saw the judge; he saw the jury; he saw Mr. Pumphrey mopping his brow with a handkerchief that looked as if it had been used to wipe the ink from a copybook; and he saw Mr. Viper.

Mr. Viper was a young man of exceedingly genteel appearance. He wore a high stock, a coat of fashionable cut, and a waistcoat of vivid pattern. But what caught the sharp eye of Tobias Tilt was not the waistcoat, nor yet the stock, but the boots. They were of polished leather, gleaming with a lustre that reflected the gaslight, and they were, without a doubt, entirely new. They creaked when Mr. Viper crossed his legs, and they bore upon their toes a peculiar little ornament of red leather, stitched in the shape of a heart.

Tobias Tilt rubbed his nose. He had been in Wapping that morning. He had stood in the entry of the "Jolly Tar" public-house, waiting for a witness. He had seen Mr. Viper enter that establishment, and he had seen Mr. Viper emerge, wearing a pair of boots that did not creak. He had also seen a bootmaker’s boy, carrying a bundle, enter the "Jolly Tar" shortly before Mr. Viper came out. The bootmaker’s shop was that of Mr. Creakle, in Rosemary Lane, and Tobias knew that Mr. Creakle was famous for stitching little red hearts upon the toes of his customers’ boots, to denote that they were made of the very best Corinthian leather.

Here was a discovery! Here was a mystery wrapped in a riddle, and enclosed in a boot! Tobias Tilt wriggled through the crowd like an eel through a basket of oysters. He darted down the stairs, and he darted up to his master, who was sitting at the table with his head in his hands.

"If you please, sir," whispered Tobias, plucking his master’s sleeve, "the clerk’s boots is new, and they’ve got red hearts on ’em, and he bought ’em this very morning at Creakle’s, and he paid for ’em with a handful of copper and silver, and I see him, sir, I see him!"

Mr. Pumphrey started as if he had been shot. He stared at Tobias. He stared at Mr. Viper. He stared at Mr. Viper’s boots. Then he rose, and with a voice that trembled like a jelly on a plate, he addressed the court.

"My lord," said Mr. Pumphrey, "I have a question to put to the witness. Mr. Viper, you have sworn that you counted the till at noon, and found it four pounds, ten shillings, and sixpence short. Pray, sir, when did you purchase those boots?"

Mr. Viper turned pale, then red, then pale again. He stammered, he hesitated, he looked at Mr. Mudge, and he looked at the judge.

"I—I bought them this morning," said he.

"And where, sir, did you buy them?"

"At—at Creakle’s, in Rosemary Lane."

"And what, sir, did you pay for them?"

"I—I paid four pounds, ten shillings, and sixpence."
