It was precisely half-past nine on the evening of October the fourteenth when the rumour reached our quiet cathedral city of Selchester. I had been reading in the study when my brother-in-law, Horace, burst through the door with the unceremonious haste of a man who has entirely forgotten the conventions of domestic quiet. He is a manufacturer of agricultural implements, a practical, hard-headed man who ordinarily views the sensational with a sort of contemptuous irritation; but his face was flushed, and he held an evening paper in his hand as if it were a weapon.

“It is going to strike us,” he said, panting. “The meteor. They have calculated it at the Greenwich Observatory. It is to hit the earth at midnight, somewhere in the south of England.”

My wife, Eleanor, followed him into the room, her countenance pale. “Is it really true, Arthur?” she asked, with that eager, tremulous inflection which always irritated me. “Horace says the end of the world has been telegraphed.”

I took the paper from Horace and read the brief, sensational paragraph. A great luminous body, visible now in the western sky, was hurtling towards the earth with terrific velocity. The exact calculations were disputed, but the consensus of the astronomical authorities was that a collision was inevitable at midnight. It was a disagreeable, startling thing to read. I went to the window and drew back the curtain. There, in the clear, cold sky, hung a bright, greenish star, perceptibly larger than any of its fellows, and undeniably growing as one watched it. The extraordinary fact of our impending annihilation was established in my mind with a sudden, quiet conviction. The world was to be smashed to atoms in two hours and a half.

Selchester is a place of considerable ecclesiastical dignity, and I suppose it was to be expected that the news would throw its respectable society into a panic. But what surprised me was the immediate and profound alteration in the mental atmosphere of our own drawing-room. We had invited the Dean’s daughter, Miss Verrinder, and our cousin, young Philip, to spend the evening, and they had already arrived. When we communicated the news, there was a brief, breathless silence, and then an extraordinary relaxation of the ordinary social restraints.

Eleanor sat down abruptly upon the sofa. “I have never liked you, Horace,” she said, with a sudden, flat directness that was entirely foreign to her usual evasive manner. “You have been a tyrant in this house, and I have detested your meanness and your gross, noisy habits for fifteen years. I am glad we are all going to die.”

Horace stared at her, his flush deepening to a dark purple. He seemed about to speak, but the immense shadow of the meteor overrode his personal anger. “I have been mean,” he said, slowly, like a man conning over a list of facts. “I falsified the depreciation accounts in ’97. I have been living on the capital of the business for three years. And I have been carrying on an intrigue with Mrs. Blaxland, the vicar’s wife. I tell you this because it doesn’t matter now.”

Miss Verrinder, a young woman of strict evangelical principles, gave a little cry at this, but she did not leave the room. She sat down in an armchair and clasped her hands. “I stole the Cathedral communion plate,” she said, with a nervous, chattering haste. “I took the silver chalice last Easter. I pawned it in London. The Dean thought it was a sacrilegious theft by some tramp. I did it to pay my brother’s gambling debts. I have been in utter misery ever since.”

Young Philip, who was lounging by the mantelpiece, laughed, a rather disagreeable laugh. “I forged your name to that three-hundred-pound cheque, Horace,” he remarked, lighting a cigarette. “I thought you would never find it out. And I am glad I did it. You have always been a beast to me.”

I must confess that this sudden, wholesale stripping off of the masks of respectability produced in me a profound and curious reaction. We were a typical, middle-class, cathedral-city family, outwardly decorous, inwardly a seething mass of squalid secrets; and now, with the shadow of a falling star over us, we were engaged in a grotesque, indiscriminate confession. I looked at the greenish light in the sky, which was now casting sharp, black shadows of the window-frames upon the carpet, and I felt an overwhelming impulse to speak.

“I have despised you all,” I said, “for your trivial, empty lives. Eleanor, your social climbing has been an offence to me. Horace, your business dishonesty is only what I might have expected from your intellectual calibre. I have longed to leave you all and go to live in a cottage on the South Downs, entirely alone. I have also embezzled the funds of the Selchester Literary Society to pay for my collection of Roman coins. I have no regret for that. The society’s lectures were an insult to human intelligence.”

Eleanor wept, but it was a thin, ineffectual weeping, without any real passion. Horace sat staring at the carpet, his jaw hanging loosely. Miss Verrinder was murmuring prayers, but she kept opening her eyes to look at the growing light outside. Philip smoked his cigarette with an assumption of careless cynicism that was, I think, entirely assumed. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked on with a maddening, methodical persistence. It was a quarter to eleven.

The extraordinary mental exhilaration of the confession had given place now to a profound depression. We had exhausted our secrets, and there was nothing left but the bare, terrible fact of our impending destruction. The greenish star was now a large, blazing disc, and a strange, murmuring sound, like the hum of a distant dynamo, began to be audible in the sky. The whole city was in a turmoil of bells and shouting. I went to the window again and looked out. The Cathedral spire stood out black and sharp against the livid light, and I could see little figures running to and fro in the Close, looking like ants in a disturbed nest.
