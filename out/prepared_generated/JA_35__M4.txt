The invitation to Berkeley Square had been conveyed in terms of the warmest affection, and received by the Miss Lorings with a gratitude proportioned rather to the grandeur of the situation than to any very distinct expectation of pleasure. Their cousin, now Lady Ashcombe, had been a Miss Loring herself, and it was felt by the family in Devonshire that her elevation ought to be a comfort to them all; though how precisely it was to operate as a comfort had never been satisfactorily explained. The Miss Lorings were orphans of small fortune, and their visit was to be one of those long, indefinite visits which the rich confer upon the poor, and which the poor accept with a show of eagerness, because it is not for them to be nice.

The house in Berkeley Square was of a size to make the two sisters, as they entered it, draw instinctively closer together. The hall alone might have contained the whole of their late residence; and the servant who received their trunks was of a solemnity which suggested that trunks were a species of luggage with which he was but professionally acquainted. They were conducted to a handsome apartment on the third floor, where the windows commanded a view of chimneys, and where they were given to understand that they should dine at half past six, and that her ladyship would see them at dinner.

“At dinner,” said Harriet, when the servant had withdrawn. “Not before. She cannot spare us half an hour before dinner.”

“She is a great woman now,” said Eleanor, “and great women have their hours. We are very well lodged, Harriet.”

“We are very high lodged,” returned the younger sister. “So high that we are out of sight of the family, and out of mind, I dare say, until the bell rings. I wish we had never come.”

Eleanor made no reply. She was arranging their few gowns in a wardrobe large enough for the wardrobe of a princess, and thinking, not for the first time, that the disproportion between the furniture and the occupant was of a kind to try the temper of any woman who had a gown to hang.

Lady Ashcombe received them in the drawing-room with an embrace for each, and a glance that travelled over their persons with the quickness of long practice. She was still handsome; but there was a restlessness in her eye, and a something in her manner of speaking, which seemed to require of every sentence that it should do the office of two. She inquired after Devonshire, after the vicar, after the old house, and after every particular of their journey, without waiting, however, for any very particular answer; and then, with the same breath, desired them to sit down near the fire, and asked whether they had ever seen a room so furnished.

“It is very fine,” said Eleanor.

“Fine! my dear creature, it is the admiration of every body. The chairs are from France. The whole suite—I would not tell you what they cost, you would not believe me. But you must not think that we are always so lodged; we go to Ashcombe in the spring, where I am sure you must come to us. The abbey is a heap of ruins, but the modern part is very tolerable. Lord Ashcombe is so fond of it.”

Here her ladyship sighed, and it was observable that she sighed with a sort of satisfaction, as though the fondness of Lord Ashcombe for ruins were a merit she had discovered in him and was determined to make the most of.

At dinner the sisters had the honour of being placed at some distance from their host and hostess, in the society of a Major Penhallow, who talked of nothing but the roads, and of a Lady Maria Trefusis, who talked of nothing at all. Lord Ashcombe himself was a heavy, civil gentleman, who ate with great attention, and said “Indeed!” at intervals with an air of profound conviction. It was Lady Ashcombe who conducted the conversation, and she conducted it as a general conducts an army, with much noise and many feints, and no very visible object. She talked of the opera, of the Prince, of a new carriage, of her own health, and of the ingratitude of servants, in a strain which left her auditors no leisure to admire the fluency of her discourse, so much were they occupied in wondering what it could all be for.

“You are very silent, Harriet,” said her ladyship at last, across the table, with a smile which was meant to be kind, and which was only condescending. “You used to be the merriest of us all. I hope you are not dull in London already.”

“I am not dull,” said Harriet, “but I am not used to so much company.”

“Company! La! this is nothing. You will see us in our proper state next week. We shall have fifty people, and I shall be worn to a thread. Lord Ashcombe, do you hear that? We are to have fifty people, and I am to be worn to a thread.”

“Indeed!” said his lordship.

“I tell you what, my dear,” continued her ladyship, turning again to Harriet, “you must get a husband while you are with us. There is no time to be lost; you are not so young as you were, you know. We must have you married before the season is over.”

Harriet coloured, and said, with some spirit, that she had no such intention.

“No intention! Oh, that is what they all say. But you must let me manage for you. I have a great many acquaintance, and I am sure I could find somebody. There is Mr. Ellison now—a very good sort of man, with a fortune. He is to dine here on Thursday. You must be very charming, Harriet.”
