To be entirely natural is the last refuge of the commonplace, and to be perfectly proud is the highest achievement of art. This, at least, was the philosophy of Seraphina, the most exquisitely beautiful young swan upon the ornamental waters of the royal lake at Ardenthorn. She was a creature of such dazzling and immaculate whiteness that the lilies seemed to pale beside her, and her movements through the still water were marked by a rhythm so deliberate and harmonious that it put the finest Italian opera completely to shame.

The royal lake at Ardenthorn was, by the decree of a sentimental monarch, a sanctuary for all manner of waterfowl. It was a democratic society, and therefore entirely unendurable to those who possessed any genuine sense of the beautiful. The mallards, with their harsh, metallic voices and their absurdly busy little tails, spent their days paddling frantically after every stray crumb of bread tossed by the old women who sat upon the marble benches. The moorhens were worse; they were common, and what is more, they were aware of it, which is the only true tragedy of the lower classes. Then there were the geese, who possessed all the moral certainty of the middle-class and none of its occasional virtues. They honked aggressively, believing volume to be an adequate substitute for wit.

Seraphina held herself entirely apart from this bustling, squabbling multitude. She glided through the reeds as a duchess glides through a ballroom, bestowing neither glance nor greeting upon her inferiors. She was, she frequently reminded herself, a swan of lineage, and to sully one’s beak with the same muddy crusts as a common duck was a social solecism she could not contemplate without a shudder.

One crisp afternoon in late autumn, when the elms surrounding the lake were dropping their leaves like golden coins before the feet of a passing monarch, a young kitchen-maid, whose romantic education had been sadly neglected, threw a whole loaf of stale bread into the shallows. It was a coarse, unseemly loaf, and it struck the water with a heavy, graceless splash. Immediately, the surface of the lake was churned into a furious foam by the ducks. They converged upon the bread with the unseemly haste of stockbrokers clamouring for a railway share.

A small, brownish duck, who had been worsted in the initial scramble, paddled breathlessly towards Seraphina, bearing a moist and disintegrating fragment in his beak. He laid it timidly upon the water before her, a humble offering to her undeniable beauty.

‘I beg your pardon,’ said Seraphina, drawing back her neck with a curve of infinite disdain, ‘but I make it a rule never to eat anything that has been touched by the webbed feet of the proletariat. Pray take your sordid morsel back to your vulgar friends. It is quite bad enough to be obliged to inhabit the same lake as they are, without being expected to share their diet.’

The little duck, who was not accustomed to epigram and mistook her rhetoric for mere ill-temper, muttered an apology and retreated. Seraphina watched him go with a faint smile. She felt that she had said a very clever thing, and a clever thing, she had always maintained, was infinitely preferable to a kind one.

Winter, when it came to the valley of Ardenthorn, did not arrive with the gentle melancholy of a dying season, but with the sudden, ruthless severity of a creditor who has lost all patience. The frosts descended from the northern hills like an army of silver spears, and in a single night, the royal lake was locked in a crystal prison. The reeds were turned to brittle glass, and the beautiful, dark water, which had mirrored the sky so faithfully, became a hard, white mirror that reflected only the cruelty of the cold.

The ducks, who were practical and entirely devoid of aesthetic sensibility, did not despair. They had seen winter before. They gathered in a shivering, garrulous group near the stone bridge, and with much quacking and consultation, they began to break the thinner ice with their sturdy breasts, keeping a small patch of water open. They knew, too, of a hidden spring beneath the overhanging willows, where the water, warmed by some subterranean magic, never quite surrendered to the ice.

Seraphina, however, found herself in a most distressing predicament. Her elegant, narrow feet, so admirably adapted for the graceful propulsion of water, were entirely useless upon the ice. She slipped and stumbled, her movements, so poetically serene in their natural element, becoming suddenly awkward and absurd. She was very cold, and she was very hungry, for the bread-throwing maidens no longer came to the frost-bound banks. The world, she realised, was a place where beauty was entirely unappreciated unless it was accompanied by a thick layer of down and a practical knowledge of the weather.

She saw the ducks gathered in their open pool near the willows, their brown forms huddled together for warmth. They were talking loudly, as was their irritating habit, and seemed to be deriving some coarse comfort from their proximity to one another. Seraphina, with the desperate courage of a fallen aristocrat, began to slide her way across the ice towards them.

When she reached the edge of their little pool, they fell silent. The little brown duck, who had offered her the bread, paddled forward a few inches. He looked at her with his small, bright eyes, eyes that held neither malice nor pity, but merely a calm, unblinking comprehension.

‘The water is quite open here,’ he said, simply.

‘I perceive that it is,’ Seraphina replied, her voice trembling slightly, though whether from the cold or from wounded pride, she could not tell. ‘I suppose you consider yourselves very clever, having found this tepid little puddle.’

‘We do not consider ourselves clever,’ answered the duck. ‘We merely consider ourselves warm. It is a modest ambition, but a very agreeable one in this weather.’
