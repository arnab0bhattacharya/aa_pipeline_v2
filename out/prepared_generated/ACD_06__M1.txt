The morning post had brought a small, flat packet to the consulting rooms of Mr. Ambrose Quill, wrapped in brown paper and tied with a neat bow of garden twine. It contained nothing but a pressed flower—a single stem of yellow flag iris, its petals flattened to the texture of old parchment—slipped between two squares of blotting paper. The widow who presented it, Mrs. Elspeth Waverley, laid it upon the desk with the careful deliberation of a woman who has learned to handle fragile things.

“Three months it has been coming, Mr. Quill,” said she, her voice steady but for a slight tremor at the edge. “Every Tuesday, without fail. A different flower each week. My husband, Captain Waverley, was a great collector of botanical specimens. He was a man of precise habits.”

I glanced at the specimen. The iris was unremarkable to my eye, but the postmark on the accompanying envelope read *Tewkesbury*, and I recalled that Mrs. Waverley had entered with a map-case under her arm.

“Your husband died when, madam?”

“Fourteen months ago. He had been on a walking tour through the western counties the year before—Devon, Somerset, Gloucestershire, and so on. He kept a journal of every plant he found. But this—” she gestured to the flower “—this is not from his journal. This is someone sending them to *me*.”

She unrolled the map upon my desk. It was a well-thumbed Ordnance Survey sheet of the West Country, and upon it she had marked, in small pencil crosses, the towns from which the packets had come. Plymouth. Exeter. Yeovil. Bath. Tewkesbury. The crosses formed a line—a route, unmistakable, tracing west to east along the valleys and downs.

“It is his route,” said she. “The very same. Someone is walking it again.”

I studied the map a moment, then the flower, then the widow’s face, which was handsome and composed but for the deep shadow beneath her eyes that speaks of sleepless nights.

“Have you enemies, Mrs. Waverley?”

“None that I know of.”

“And your husband—was he a man who made enemies?”

“He was a soldier and a gentleman. He quarrelled with no one, save perhaps over the price of a good horse.”

“Then we are dealing with something subtler than malice.” I rose and took my hat from the stand. “I think, madam, that we had better see what the Post Office can tell us about the hand that writes these envelopes. The script, I observe, is educated but disguised—the capital letters are formed with unnecessary care, as though the writer were anxious to conceal a natural flourish. Come. We shall walk to the General Post Office in Lombard Street and speak to the sorters.”

We took a hansom through the morning traffic, the map-case balanced on Mrs. Waverley’s knees. At Lombard Street I sought out a superintendent named Grice, a brisk man with ink-stained cuffs, who agreed without fuss to examine the envelopes Mrs. Waverley had brought.

“Gloucestershire postmark,” said he, turning one over. “But the paper—see here, Mr. Quill—this is not common stationery. It is the sort of laid paper used by the Waterlow works for their fine printing. I have seen it in orders from the county museums.”

“A museum,” I repeated. “That is a point of some interest.”

I thanked him and we returned to the street. The fog was lifting, and a watery sun shone upon the wet pavements.

“Your husband’s journal,” said I. “Have you it safe?”

“At home, in Kensington. He left it with his other papers.”

“Then let us go there at once. I have a fancy that the flowers are not messages of menace, Mrs. Waverley, but of *continuation*. Someone is completing a work your husband began.”

At the Waverley house—a trim villa in a quiet street off Holland Park—Mrs. Waverley led me to a small study lined with glass cases. Within them stood the Captain’s collection: dried specimens mounted on card, each labelled in a neat copperplate with the Latin name, the locality, and the date. I ran my eye along the rows. There were gaps—several cards bore only the locality and date, with the space for the name left blank.

“Your husband died before he could identify them all,” said I.

“He had sent some away to be determined by a specialist at Kew. He was waiting for the replies when he was thrown from his horse.”

“And the specialist—did he ever write back?”

Mrs. Waverley frowned. “I believe there was a letter, but it came after the funeral. I did not open it. I put it with the other papers.”

She fetched a tin box from the desk and sorted through it. At length she drew out a letter on the same laid paper I had seen at the Post Office. The hand was the same—careful, disguised capitals.

“Dear Captain Waverley,” I read aloud, “I have determined the three specimens you sent as follows: *Orchis mascula*, *Fumaria capreolata*, and *Geranium lucidum*. I shall be in the district next month and hope to call upon you to see the remainder of the collection. Yours faithfully, Edmund Vale, Curator, County Museum, Taunton.”

“Edmund Vale,” said I. “That is our correspondent, Mrs. Waverley. He is walking your husband’s route, finding the same flowers, and sending them to you as a record of progress. He means no harm. He means, I think, to finish the work.”

“But why not write to me plainly?”

“Because he is a man of method and perhaps of diffidence. He fears to intrude upon your grief. The flowers are his way of speaking when he cannot find the words.”

I took up the map again. The next cross would fall, by the progression, near Cirencester. If Mr. Vale kept to his schedule, he would be there within the week.

“We must go down and meet him,” said I. “It is the only way to be certain.”
