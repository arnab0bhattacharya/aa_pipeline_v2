The light of the gas-lamp, struggling through the fog of a late November afternoon, fell upon the green baize of the door in sickly, jaundiced patches. It was a light that seemed less to illuminate than to reveal the depth of the gloom it failed to conquer; a light, one might say, of officialdom itself, which serves only to define the shadows of the great machine it is meant to serve. I stood before that door, my hand upon the cold brass of the knob, and felt the familiar, leaden weight of the hour upon my spirit. It was the hour when the business of the day congeals into the anxieties of the night, when the quiet hum of the great Departments becomes a monotonous drone in the ears, a sound of immense, indifferent power moving through its ordained channels.

It was at this precise moment, as I hesitated with the weariness of a man who has spent the day translating the will of one man into the language of a thousand, that I saw him. He was not so much standing in the corridor as filling a vacancy in it, a tall, gaunt figure in a coat of worn black that seemed to have absorbed all the soot and sorrow of the city. His face, which I remembered as being full of a fiery, argumentative intelligence at school, was now a mask of such profound, hungry stillness that it arrested my very breath. It was a face that had been scoured by some great internal fire, leaving behind only the hard, essential bone and a pair of eyes that burned with a cold, unwavering light. He did not smile. He did not extend a hand. He merely inclined his head, a gesture of such formal, antique courtesy that it felt like a rebuke.

“Ellery,” he said. His voice, too, was changed; the old, booming resonance was gone, replaced by a dry, metallic tone that gave no quarter. “It has been a long time. I did not think to find you here. A cog in the machine, it seems.”

“Thorpe,” I replied, my own voice sounding thin and bureaucratic in the sudden, charged silence. “My God, man. What has become of you?” The question was an absurdity, a platitude offered to the abyss, and I saw the faintest flicker of contempt in his eyes.

“Time has become of me,” he said. “And thought. And a certain clarity. I see things now as they are, not as they are described in the newspapers you help to write. I have come to see the Minister. Your Minister. Aveling.”

The request hung in the air between us, as tangible and as dangerous as a lit fuse. To see the Minister was not a simple matter; it was a transaction of immense symbolic weight, a concession of access that could be interpreted in a dozen damaging ways. I looked at this man, this relic of a shared boyhood who now spoke of our world as a fever-dream from which he had awoken, and I felt a strange, paternal impulse. I saw not an anarchist, a term which was then a vague and terrifying bogeyman, but the boy who had once defended a younger fellow from a bully with a ferocity that bordered on madness. I thought, with the naive arrogance of my position, that I could bridge the chasm. I could bring these two worlds into the same room, and by the simple fact of my presence, my mediating voice, I could force a reconciliation. I could make him understand the necessity of the machine, and perhaps, in turn, make the Minister see the humanity in the opposition. It was a noble, a foolish, a profoundly misguided thought.

“He is a very busy man, Thorpe,” I began, adopting the smooth, oblique manner of my trade. “But… for an old friend. I will see what can be done. Perhaps tomorrow. A short interview, you understand.”

Thorpe’s thin lips did not smile, but a kind of grim satisfaction settled upon his features. It was the look of a man who has just been handed the key to a cell he intends to burn down. “Tomorrow, then,” he said. “At two o’clock. You will arrange it.” It was not a request. It was a statement of fact, delivered with the absolute certainty of a man who has already foreseen the end of the story. He turned and was gone, melting back into the shadows of the corridor as silently as he had appeared, leaving me with the lingering scent of damp wool and a chill that had nothing to do with the November air.

The next day, at two o’clock precisely, the two of them stood in the Minister’s study. It was a room of mahogany and leather, of bound volumes and the quiet ticking of a long-case clock that measured out the life of the Empire in patient, inexorable seconds. Lord Aveling, a man whose placid features were a national asset, stood by the fireplace, a picture of benign authority. I stood near the door, a proud, foolish sentinel, believing myself to be the fulcrum upon which this delicate balance rested.

Thorpe had not changed his coat. He seemed to have brought the grime and despair of the outer world into this sanctuary of order. He did not wait for the pleasantries I had so carefully rehearsed in my mind. He simply looked at the Minister, and when he spoke, his voice was the same dry, cutting instrument I had heard in the corridor.

“You preside over a system of organised starvation,” he said, without preamble. “You sign your name to documents that condemn thousands to a life of squalor and hopelessness. You call this governance. I call it slow murder.”

Lord Aveling, to his credit, did not flinch. He had been assailed by far more eloquent and far less honest men. “My good fellow,” he began, his voice a soothing balm, “the complexities of policy are… immense. One must balance the needs of the many…”
