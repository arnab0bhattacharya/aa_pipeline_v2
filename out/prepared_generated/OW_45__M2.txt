The morning air in the city of Valoria was thick with the scent of rotting pears and the perfume of ambition. Princess Elara had long since ceased to believe in the sincerity of her courtiers, for she had learned, as one learns to walk, that flattery is the only language in which the great can truly speak. She had been told, with a monotone that suggested the information was as common as the cobblestones beneath her feet, that she was the most beautiful creature in the world. This, of course, was a lie of such exquisite proportions that it could only be true if one defined beauty as the precise absence of surprise. To be the most beautiful is to be the most predictable; it is to be a fixed point in the chaos of the human eye, a mirror that reflects nothing but the vanity of the beholder. Elara, who possessed a mind as sharp as a diamond and a heart as cold as the diamond’s surface, decided that she would go to the market to find the truth, or at least a lie of a more interesting variety.

She discarded her gown of emerald silk, which was cut to emphasize the waist and obscure the soul, and donned the roughspun tunic of a fishmonger’s daughter. It was a disguise of such poor taste that it was almost artistic. She walked with the deliberate slouch of one who has never known the burden of a corset, and her face, though unmasked by paint, wore an expression of profound boredom, which is the only expression that can compete with beauty. The market of Valoria was a cacophony of color and commerce. Stalls of saffron-yellow spices competed with pyramids of blood-red tomatoes; the air was alive with the haggling of merchants who sold not just goods, but the illusion of value. Elara moved through the crowd like a ghost, invisible to those who looked for a princess, and visible only to those who looked for a mystery.

She found her way, by accident or design, to a small, shadowed stall tucked between a vendor of dried figs and a man who sold clocks that did not tell the time but merely marked the passing of moments. Here sat a woman of advanced age, her skin the texture of old parchment, her eyes white and opaque, blind as the moon on a cloudless night. She was arranging a basket of violets, small, humble flowers that smelled of damp earth and forgotten promises.

“Will you buy, my child?” the blind woman asked. Her voice was low, a sound like the turning of a page in a book no one is reading.

“I am not a child,” Elara replied, keeping her head down. “I am a woman who is looking for something. I do not know what it is, but I suspect it is not flowers.”

The blind woman laughed, a dry, crackling sound. “Ah, the market is full of people looking for things they do not know. It is the only place where ignorance is profitable. But tell me, my dark-eyed stranger, what do you seek?”

“Truth,” Elara said, the word falling into the space between them like a stone into a well. “I have been told I am beautiful. I wish to know if it is so. Not in the way of mirrors, which are liars, but in the way of life, which is a judge.”

The blind woman paused in her arranging. She reached out a hand, the fingers gnarled and strong, and touched Elara’s cheek. The touch was gentle, yet it carried the weight of a diagnosis. “You are not beautiful,” the woman said.

Elara felt a strange, cold thrill run through her. This was the truth she had come for, or perhaps a lie of a different color. “And yet,” the woman continued, “when you speak, the air around you changes. It becomes lighter, as if the weight of the world has been temporarily suspended. Your voice, my child, is a song. It is not a song of melody, but of resonance. It holds a sadness that is so precise it becomes a kind of joy.”

Elara frowned. “I do not sing. I only speak.”

“Speaking is the only honest form of singing,” the blind woman replied. “The others are merely imitating the bird. You are the bird, though you do not know it. You have a voice that could make a stone weep, or a king forget his throne. It is a dangerous voice. Beauty is safe; it is static, like a painting in a gallery. But a voice that moves the soul is a force of nature. It cannot be caged. It cannot be owned. It is free, and freedom is the only thing that is truly terrifying.”

Elara stood there, the basket of violets in her hand, feeling the weight of the words. She had expected to be told she was plain, or perhaps that she was beautiful in a conventional sense. Instead, she had been told that she possessed something far more volatile than beauty. Beauty was a currency, easily spent and easily counterfeited. But a voice that could move the soul? That was a power that could not be traded, only given or taken.

“Why do you sell flowers?” Elara asked. “If you are blind, do you not see their color?”

“I see their scent,” the woman said. “And I know their shape by the way they break. The violet is the flower of secrecy. It does not shout its beauty; it whispers it. It is the only flower that understands the value of silence. I sell them to those who know that the most important things in life are the ones that cannot be seen.”

Elara bought the basket. It was a foolish purchase, for she had no use for flowers, and her disguise was already beginning to fray at the edges. The crowd pressed in around her, and the noise of the market swelled, a tide of voices and clattering carts. She walked away, the violets heavy in her hands, their scent sharp and distinct against the smell of the city.
