The wind on the moors above Rylstone Parsonage had a voice that night—a wild, sibilant cry that seemed to echo the unrest in my own breast. It was a wind that came down from the black fells, smelling of peat and wet stone, and it rattled the casement of the study where I sat, not at my own work, but at the endless, monotonous task of mending my brother’s sermons. My own ambitions, once as fierce and bright as any beacon, had long since been banked and buried, consumed by the cold ash of duty. I was Margaret Vance, the elder sister, the prop of the house, the silent guardian of our invalid, Elara.

Elara lay in the adjoining room, a frail creature whose world was bounded by the four walls of her chamber and the landscape of her imagination. She was a poet. Her verses, written in a delicate, trembling script on scraps of paper, were her life’s blood, and she showed them to no one. To me, they were a source of both pride and a strange, sharp pain. They were beautiful, I knew—lyrics of the heart as pure and piercing as the cry of a curlew on the moor—but they were also a living reproach. While I had learned to count shillings and darn stockings, Elara had kept her spirit’s fire alight, tended by a muse I had long since turned out of doors.

Our brother, Thomas, the Rector of St. Cuthbert’s, had brought a visitor to the parsonage that afternoon: a Mr. Alistair Finch, a man of some literary note from London, who had come to survey the rugged landscape for a volume of essays. He was a man whose eyes saw more than rocks and heather; they saw the poetry latent in all things. During tea, a stiff and formal affair where I presided with aching feet and a mind full of grocery bills, the conversation had turned to the nature of inspiration. Mr. Finch, with the easy authority of his kind, had spoken of the untutored genius that sometimes flourishes in remote places, a voice speaking from the wilderness.

It was then that a mischance, a catastrophe of small proportions, had occurred. A gust of wind, stronger than the rest, had snatched a loose sheet from the pile of papers I was sorting for Thomas—papers I had mistaken for his own. It fluttered across the room and landed at Mr. Finch’s feet. He picked it up, his gaze falling upon the script. I saw his expression change from casual interest to a focused, almost reverent, intensity.

“Good heavens,” he had breathed, his voice low. “What is this? ‘*The river, a vein of hammered silver, throbs through the valley’s stony heart…*’ Who wrote this? This is not mere verse; it is vision.”

A hot, terrible blush had scalded my face. I had snatched the paper from him, stammering that it was a trifle, a private thing. But the words were out. He had praised them. He had called them ‘vision’. And in that moment, watching Elara’s pale face light with a sudden, startled joy, a feeling rose in me so ugly and so potent that I thought I should choke on it. It was jealousy. A raw, black, and shameful thing. It coiled in my stomach and tightened my throat. I, who had sacrificed everything, was envious of my own sick sister, whose only gift was a talent I had no right to covet.

Now, hours later, the house was quiet save for the wind and Elara’s soft, difficult breathing. Mr. Finch’s words echoed in the silence, a judgment upon my own barren years. *Vision*. What had I to show for my life but a house kept in order and a family sustained? My own youthful dreams of writing, of creating something that would last beyond my flesh, seemed a childish vanity now.

I rose and went to the window, pressing my forehead against the cold glass. The moon had torn a ragged hole in the clouds, and its light fell upon the graveyard below, silvering the leaning headstones. A sense of the utter injustice of it all seized me. Why should her spirit soar while mine was chained? Why should she receive the praise for a life I had made possible? The thought was a serpent in my Eden of self-sacrifice. I hated myself for it, and in my self-hatred, I hated her for a moment, too.

A soft sound from her room pulled me back. A cough, followed by a sigh. The jealousy vanished as quickly as it had come, replaced by a wave of remorse so fierce it was like a physical blow. What demon had possessed me? Elara, my gentle, suffering Elara, who had never had a harsh word for anyone, who looked at me with such utter trust. To begrudge her this one small gleam of light in her shadowed world was the act of a monster.

I pushed open her door and went to her bedside. She was awake, her eyes shining in the gloom.

“Margaret?” she whispered. “Is that you?”

“Yes, dearest. I could not sleep.”

“I have been thinking of what that gentleman said,” she murmured, her voice thin as thread. “Do you think he meant it? About the poem?”

I took her hand. It was cool and light as a bird’s wing. The shame of my earlier feeling burned in me anew.

“He meant it, Elara,” I said, and my voice was steady, for I had wrestled the demon and subdued it. “He saw what I have always known. Your gift is a rare and precious thing. It is a light that should not be kept under a bushel.”

A tear slid from the corner of her eye and traced a path down her temple. “But I am so afraid, Margaret. Afraid to show it. Afraid it is not good enough. And you… you have always been the strong one. You have given up so much for me and Thomas. It seems unfair that I should have this… this one little thing, when you have nothing.”
