The parish of Little Marston elected Thomas Whitby as overseer of the poor not because he was learned, but because he was honest. It was a bitter irony that the job required him to keep accounts he could not read. Thomas was a man of the soil, his hands calloused from the plough and the scythe, his face weathered by a lifetime of squinting into the Leicestershire sun. He knew the turn of the seasons and the price of a bullock, but the letters of the alphabet remained a foreign and unwelcoming country to him. He had tried, once, as a boy, but the shapes would not settle in his mind. Now, at fifty, the shame of it was a constant, dull ache.

The previous overseer, Mr. Silas Croft, had been a different sort of man. A man of ledgers and quills, he had held the post for a decade, his neat, spidery script a testament to his learning. His sudden death from a fever had left the parish in a lurch, and in the cramped vestry meeting, with the winter coming on and the poor rolls swelling, the farmers and tradesmen had looked for a man they could trust with their money. They had looked at Thomas Whitby.

"A good, honest man," the vicar had said, clapping Thomas on the shoulder. "The accounts are simple enough, Whitby. Just record the outgoings. The paupers' bread, the coal for the workhouse, the occasional shilling for a widow's rent. Croft's ledgers are all in order; you need only continue in the same vein."

Thomas had felt the heat rise in his neck. He could not tell them. Not here, not in front of the men he’d known his whole life. So he had nodded, his throat tight, and accepted the heavy iron key to the vestry chest and the responsibility that came with it.

The ledgers sat on the vestry table, two great, leather-bound volumes. Their pages were filled with Croft's elegant, looping script, a code as impenetrable to Thomas as the markings on a Roman ruin. He would run a rough finger over the lines, trying to glean some meaning, but the words were just black marks, mocking him. He was the shepherd of the flock, but he could not count his sheep. The fear of making a mistake, of being found out, was a cold stone in his gut. He felt the weight of every hungry child and every shivering elder in the parish, and he was failing them with his ignorance.

His daughter, Elspeth, was the one who saw. She was a quiet girl of nineteen, with her mother's dark hair and a mind as sharp and clear as a winter stream. She had learned her letters from the village schoolmistress and possessed a natural aptitude for order and numbers. She would watch her father from the doorway of their cottage, his shoulders slumped over the great book, his brow furrowed in a way that had nothing to do with the price of hay.

One evening, as the fire crackled and the wind moaned around the eaves, she brought him a cup of small beer and set it beside him. "You fret over it, Father," she said softly.

Thomas grunted, not looking up. "It is the parish business, girl. Not for you."

"The marks on the page worry you," she persisted, her voice gentle. "I could help."

He finally looked at her, a flash of shame and pride in his tired eyes. "You?"

"I can read the numbers and the names," she said. "I could help you tally the figures. Keep the accounts neat."

The relief that washed over him was so profound it was almost painful. He saw in her offer not an intrusion, but a lifeline. He could not say the words aloud, but he nodded, a slow, heavy motion of consent.

The next day, Elspeth came with him to the vestry. She did not touch the new, empty ledger at first. Instead, she opened the last of Mr. Croft's books, the one covering the past year. Her small, nimble fingers traced the columns. "This is the 'Received' column," she said, pointing. "And this is 'Disbursed'. This here is the weekly allowance to Widow Hutton, and here is the payment for the coal from the Bardon pits."

Thomas watched, mesmerised. The jumble of lines resolved itself under her guidance into a story of the parish's life, its needs and its mercies. He felt a surge of immense gratitude for this clever, quiet girl of his.

Over the next week, Elspeth became his secret scribe. He would tell her the day's transactions—"Two shillings for the coffin for old Mr. Gable, a loaf and a farthing's worth of cheese for the Miller boy's family"—and she would write them down in a clear, round hand that he could almost make out. It was a good system. But in her careful work of copying and checking, Elspeth began to notice things.

The figures did not add up. The amounts disbursed seemed strangely high for so small a parish. Payments were made to names she did not recognise—'A. Thorne, for services rendered', 'J. Pike, for sundry materials'. There were large, regular sums paid out for 'special provisions' that seemed to have no detail attached. The total sum of money received from the landowners and tenants of the parish each quarter was a tidy sum, yet the vestry's cash box was often perilously low.

"Father," she said one night, her voice low and serious as she laid a column of figures before him. "Something is wrong."

"What is it? Have I made an error?" he asked, his old fear returning.

"No. It is not your work. It is Mr. Croft's." She pointed a trembling finger at the ledger. "These payments to 'A. Thorne' and 'J. Pike'. I asked Mrs. Davison at the post office. There is no one of that name in Little Marston, nor in the next three villages. And this 'special provision'… it is ten shillings a week. That is enough to feed half the workhouse."
