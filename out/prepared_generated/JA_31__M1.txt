It was an established truth within the family of Mr. Greville of Hartwell Park that his son and heir, Henry, possessed a most lamentable judgement in the choice of his companions. His sister, Miss Augusta Greville, had long made it her particular amusement to catalogue their follies, and to predict, with a cheerful lack of charity, the various and unhappy ends to which their indolence or extravagance would lead them. There was Mr. Finch, whose opinions were as changeable as his cravats; the dashing but dissolute Captain Lovelace, who had, as she foretold, fled to the continent to escape his creditors; and then, above all others, there was Mr. Bertram Croft.

Mr. Croft had been the subject of her most sustained and elegant ridicule. He was a young man of good family but no fortune, who read too much, spoke too little, and possessed a gravity of manner that Augusta found simply insufferable. “He looks,” she had once declared to her friend, Miss Eleanor Vance, as they watched the gentlemen ride away from the park, “as though he is perpetually calculating the precise depth of his own dignity. It is a failing in a young man to be so determinedly old.” Eleanor, with her gentle and forgiving nature, had merely smiled and suggested that he might be merely shy. “Shy!” Augusta had retorted, with a light laugh. “My dear Eleanor, shyness is a flutter; that man is a monument. Henry calls him ‘Croft the Conscience,’ and he is not far wrong. I dare say he would find a moral in the flight of a woodlouse.”

These opinions, delivered in the confidence of their shared bedchamber or on quiet walks through the Hartwell shrubbery, had been the currency of their friendship for years. To Eleanor, they were merely Augusta’s wit; to Augusta, they were the simple truth. It was therefore with a sense of profound and singular shock that she learnt, from the village post, of two remarkable changes in Mr. Croft’s circumstances.

The first was announced in the *Surrey Chronicle*, which her father read aloud at breakfast. Mr. Bertram Croft, having distinguished himself at the Bar, had been appointed to the Bench of Magistrates for the county. “Croft!” her brother Henry had exclaimed, dropping his fork. “Our Croft? A magistrate? Good heavens, he will have the whole village in the stocks for untidy hedgerows.” Augusta had laughed, but the sound was a trifle thin. The second piece of intelligence, however, arrived not from the public prints but from Eleanor herself, in a letter written in a hand so agitated it was barely legible. Mr. Croft, it transpired, was a frequent and welcome visitor at the Vance’s modest home, and his intentions towards Eleanor were now of a most serious and declared nature.

Augusta sat with the letter in her lap for a full quarter of an hour after she had finished it. The world, which had hitherto arranged itself in such a pleasing and predictable fashion, had tilted on its axis. Mr. Croft, the monument, the conscience, the moralising woodlouse-observer, was not only a man of consequence, but was now courting the one person whose good opinion Augusta valued above all others. She felt a disquieting mixture of astonishment, vexation, and a new and unfamiliar sensation that she could only identify as shame. How many of her witticisms had Eleanor been obliged to hear? How many casual cruelties, delivered with a smile, had her friend absorbed in patient silence?

The question of whether to confess these past follies presented itself to her with the force of a moral dilemma, a subject she would ordinarily have found tedious in the extreme. To speak was to risk Eleanor’s censure and to reveal herself as a creature of uncharitable mockery. To be silent was to live with the knowledge that her friend was entering into an engagement founded upon a partial and unflattering acquaintance with the man she was to marry. Eleanor must be imagining that Augusta would be delighted, that she would welcome Mr. Croft as a brother. The deception felt like a weight upon her spirits.

The opportunity to resolve the matter came sooner than she expected. Eleanor, in a state of fluttering happiness, called at Hartwell two days later. They walked in the garden, where the roses were heavy with scent, and Eleanor spoke of Mr. Croft with a tenderness that made Augusta’s silence feel all the more culpable.

“He is not what you once thought him, Augusta,” Eleanor said at last, her eyes fixed upon a distant bed of lavender. “You must allow that he is greatly altered.”

“Altered?” Augusta replied, forcing a lightness into her tone. “My dear, I am not sure I ever knew him well enough to judge of any alteration. He was Henry’s friend.”

A faint blush coloured Eleanor’s cheeks. “But you had an opinion. A very decided one, if I recall. You thought him proud and grave.”

Augusta’s heart began to beat uncomfortably. “I am sure I said many foolish things,” she murmured, looking away. “One speaks without thought.”

“It is of that I wish to speak,” Eleanor said, her voice soft but firm. “I know what you have said of him. You need not fear to tell me. I have guessed it all.”

Augusta turned to her, her face pale. To confess now seemed both a relief and an impossibility. “Eleanor, I…”

“No, do not,” her friend interposed gently. “It is of no consequence now. But I must tell you that when he first began to visit us, he spoke of you and your family with the greatest respect. He recalled your wit, and said it was the sharpest he had ever encountered. He thought you did not like him, and it caused him some pain, for he had long admired you from afar.”
