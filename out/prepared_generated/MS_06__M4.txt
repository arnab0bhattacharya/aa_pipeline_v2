I was born, as I have oft been told, in a hut of reeds and pumice-stone, within the sound of that same sea which has since proved at once my ruin and my exaltation. My father was a fisherman of Santa Venerina, a man of few words and many scars, who taught me to read the sky as others read a book, and to hold my breath beneath the waves until the darkness sang in my ears. My mother died when I was yet a child, and her memory survives in me only as a warmth, a scent of rosemary, a hand that smoothed my hair in the night. Thus I grew, a creature of the shore, knowing nothing of courts or of the intricate and terrible passions that fester in the hearts of men who dwell in palaces.

It was on the eve of the feast of Saint Rosalia, in the year ’93, that the storm descended upon us with a fury I had never witnessed. The sky turned the colour of a bruised plum, and the sea rose up as though some ancient god of the deep had wakened from a long and sullen sleep. The fishing boats were dragged ashore; the women wept and clutched their children; and all along the strand the old men muttered of the wrath of Heaven. I stood upon the rocks, my hair lashed across my face, and watched the madness of the waters with a strange and fearful joy, for I was young, and the terror of the elements stirred in me a wild delight.

Then I saw it—a vessel, small and frail, driven helplessly toward the jagged teeth of the reef. I heard, or fancied that I heard, a cry above the roaring of the gale. I waited for no second thought. I cast off my jacket, and plunged into the boiling surf.

The struggle that ensued I cannot recount with any clearness. I remember the salt water choking me, the black waves closing over my head, the desperate effort of my limbs against the overwhelming might of the sea. I remember, too, the face of the youth I seized—pale as marble, his dark eyes wide with the terror of death, his lips murmuring, as I thought, a prayer. I remember the cruel rocks, and the blood that streamed from my own hands as I dragged him, more dead than living, upon the sand.

When I awoke to consciousness, I lay in a great chamber hung with crimson velvet, and a physician in black stood over me, and a lady, whose beauty seemed to me that of a being from another world, bent upon me eyes suffused with tears. It was the Lady Eleonora di Montefalco, wife of the Count, and the youth I had saved was their only son, the young Signor Ludovico. The Count himself came to my bedside, a man of grave and noble presence, and taking my hand in his, he said that I had preserved the light of his eyes, the heir of his ancient name, and that henceforth I should want for nothing so long as life remained to him.

Thus was I lifted from the humble station in which I was born, and placed in a sphere whose splendours I had never dreamed of. I was given a chamber in the palace, garments befitting a gentleman, and the instruction of a tutor in letters and in music. The young Signor Ludovico, who was of a gentle and generous nature, treated me rather as a brother than as a servant; and the Count, seeing my devotion to his son, advanced me from one honour to another, until I stood, as it were, upon the very threshold of that world which had once seemed to me as distant as the stars.

But I was not long to enjoy my felicity in peace. There was in the household one Maestro Giacomo, the steward, who had served the family for thirty years. He was a man of spare and withered frame, with a face like a mask of parchment, and eyes that seemed to look through and beyond all who addressed him. In the beginning he treated me with a cold civility, which I, in the simplicity of my heart, mistook for the reserve of age. But I soon perceived that he regarded me with a hatred the more terrible because it was silent and dissembled.

He had, as I learned, a nephew, one Baldassare, whom he had long designed for the post of confidence which the Count had conferred upon me; and the ruin of this hope he attributed to my intrusion. Yet it was not merely the loss of advancement that inflamed him. He looked upon me as a creature of the mire, an upstart, a worm that had crawled from the slime of the sea into the light of his master’s favour; and his proud and ancient spirit revolted at the thought that a fisherman’s brat should stand where he had stood, and receive the smiles which he had earned by the labour of a lifetime.

He began his work with a subtlety that I, in my inexperience, could not detect. He would speak before me of the instability of fortune, of the vanity of human hopes, of the danger of trusting to the favour of the great; and these maxims, which I took for the wisdom of a pious age, were in truth the first whispers of a conspiracy against my peace. He would drop hints, too, before the other servants, of the presumption of those who, raised from nothing, forget the rock from which they were hewn; and I soon perceived that I was regarded by many in the household with envy and suspicion.
