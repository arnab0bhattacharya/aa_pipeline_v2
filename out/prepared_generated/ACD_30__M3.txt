I had remarked to him upon the singular affair of the Lingfield colt, and the strange behaviour of its trainer, Mr. Silas Blackmore, when he turned to me with that keen, alert expression which I had learned to regard as the precursor of a challenge.

"You recall the case, Watson? The animal is a phenomenon in private, yet a nonentity in public. Blackmore is an honest man, ruined by the mystery."

I nodded. The racing papers had been full of little else. Blackmore's colt, Golden Gale, had clocked faster private trials than any three-year-old in England, yet in three public starts had finished so badly that the stewards had held an inquiry. Suspicion had naturally fallen upon the jockey, young Ashton, but the lad had been exonerated, and the mystery remained.

"I have had a letter from Blackmore this morning," said my friend. "He begs me to come down to Hatherleigh Stables. The man is at his wits' end. I think, Watson, we must spend a week in Newmarket."

We arrived at Hatherleigh on a Tuesday evening, the October wind sweeping across the heath with a bitter edge. Blackmore met us at the gate — a tall, angular man, weathered by forty years on the turf, with a look of settled gloom upon his hard features. He led us through the yard, where the stable lads touched their caps, and into a spartan sitting-room that smelled of saddle soap and stale tobacco.

"It is good of you to come, sir," said he, pushing a decanter across the table. "I am at my last stake. Golden Gale is the finest animal I have ever put a bridle on. In his work he is sublime. He will gallop the course in a time that would turn the Prince of Wales pale. Yet put him in a race and he stops as though he had run into a wall."

"And the jockey?"

"Young Ashton rides him in both. The boy is as honest as the day. I have watched him myself. He rides the same race, uses the same tactics. The colt simply will not extend himself."

"And the feed?"

"Tested by a chemist in Bury St. Edmunds. Oats, bran, beans — nothing else."

"And the stable lads?"

"Loyal to a man. Most have been with me ten years or more."

My friend sat for some little time in silence, his fingers pressed together, his eyes fixed upon the fire. "I shall need a week," said he at last. "I must see the colt at his work, and I must observe the routine of your establishment after dark. Tell me, Blackmore — who has access to the stables at night?"

"Only my head lad, Griggs, and the night-watchman, Purvis. Purvis is an old soldier from the Suffolk regiment. He sleeps in the room above the harness-room and makes his rounds at midnight and three."

"And Griggs?"

"He lives in the cottage by the gate. A faithful fellow — I owe him my life, for he pulled me from under a horse in '82."

"Has he enemies?"

"None that I know of."

"And visitors? Does anyone call at the stables in the evening?"

Blackmore hesitated. "Now that you mention it, a gentleman named Colthurst has called twice this month. He represents certain bookmaking interests in London. He wished to purchase a half-share in Golden Gale. I refused."

My friend's eyes gleamed. "I see. And the price he offered?"

"Five thousand pounds for a half-share."

"For a colt who cannot win a race? Remarkably generous."

On the Wednesday morning we watched Golden Gale at his gallops. The colt was a magnificent bay, with a deep girth and a head that bespoke both quality and courage. I observed him cover four furlongs in a time that made Blackmore nod with grim pride. "There," said he. "You see what he can do. And yet on Saturday at Epsom he finished last of twelve, beaten twenty lengths."

That evening, after the stable lads had retired, my friend and I concealed ourselves in an empty box-stall at the far end of the yard. The night was cold and very dark, with a thin rain falling. We had taken up our position at eight o'clock, and for three hours we sat in patience, listening to the stamping of horses and the creak of timbers.

It was near midnight when my friend gripped my arm. A figure had emerged from the shadow of the feed-room and crossed the yard with a swift, stealthy tread. It moved to the door of Golden Gale's box and entered. We heard the colt stir, then a low murmuring voice.

"Come," whispered my friend.

We crossed the yard together. At the door of the box my friend struck a match, and the sudden flare of light revealed a man crouching beside the colt's head, one hand pressed to its nostril, the other holding a small phial. The man started back with an oath.

It was Griggs.

The head lad's face went the colour of ash. "Sir," he gasped, "I can explain —"

"I think not," said my friend, coolly. "You have been dosing this colt with a sedative, administered through the nostrils. The effect would be slight — enough to dull a horse's competitive fire without leaving a trace in the bloodstream. In private trials, where the colt runs on habit and training, the effect is negligible. In the excitement of a public race, it is decisive. Who is paying you, Griggs?"

The man sank upon the straw. "Colthurst," he muttered. "He promised me two hundred pounds for every race the colt lost. I am a ruined man, sir. My wife is ill, and I have debts —"

"Debts which will now be paid by the hangman, I fear," said Blackmore, who had appeared behind us, his face white with fury and grief.
