The morning mist still clung to the willows bordering the slow reaches of the Arrow, turning the familiar landscape into a pale version of its summer self, when Edward Leatherhead walked out to take a final survey of his inheritance. It was an inheritance that presented itself to his mind in somewhat the same ghostly and indeterminate fashion as that morning mist: there was the fair outward show of the land—the broad acres, the thriving homestead, the tenantry who touched their hats with that comfortable deference which belongs to an old-established name—but within, where the solid substance of pecuniary value should have lain, there was only a nebulous deficit, a vacuity of debt that had descended upon him with the suddenness of an autumn fog. His father, good easy man, had been one of those persons whose benevolence is exercised chiefly at the expense of their creditors; a man who could not pass a broken-winded horse in a fair without purchasing it at a price that implied a compliment to the seller’s judgment, and who lent money to needy neighbours with a punctuality that would have been highly commendable had it not been for the unfortunate circumstance that the money was not his own to lend. The elder Leatherhead had died with the character of an open-handed gentleman, and had left his son the melancholy privilege of discovering that open-handedness, when it takes the form of signing promissory notes without reckoning the interest, is a virtue that may be classed among the expensive luxuries of life.

Edward stood by the gate of the home meadow, whistling softly to himself—a habit he had when his mind was working at a problem that required a steady and deliberate resolution. He was a young man of five-and-twenty, with a face that bore the stamp of that mixed constitution which is common enough in our Midland counties: a certain squareness of jaw that argued a capacity for obstinate endurance, tempered by eyes of a hazel colour, in which a quicker sensibility might be read. He had been bred to no profession, his father having considered that a Leatherhead of Chilverton must necessarily be above the necessity of trade; and the consequence was that he found himself at an age when most men have already entered upon some calling, equipped with no weapon against adversity but a tolerably good education, a sound constitution, and a not inconsiderable share of that self-reliance which is the slow product of an English country upbringing.

He was turning over in his mind the two courses that lay open to him, and weighing them with that careful balancing of consequences which is perhaps less the result of philosophic training than of sheer necessity, when a voice broke upon his meditations from the other side of the hedge.

"Nay, Mester Edward, yo' munna go for to fret yo'rself to t' bone about they debts. I mind the day when yo'r feyther come home wi' that there gray mare—feyther to the one as foaled in the lower paddock—and paid seven-an'-twenty pound for her, an' she no more worth than fifteen, if she were that. But he were a kind maister, God rest him, an' I dunno as we'd wish it otherwise."

It was old Reuben Mott, the head ploughman, who had been with the Leatherheads for better than forty years, and who had a habit of appearing at gates and hedgerows with the punctuality of a homing bird whenever there was any matter of family concern abroad. He was a short, squarely built man, with a face of that weather-beaten complexion which seems to be the natural product of a Warwickshire soil, and his speech had the broad, deliberate movement of a man whose thoughts are slow to form but sure when they come.

"I'm not fretting, Reuben," said Edward, with a smile that was perhaps a trifle forced. "But a man must look his difficulties in the face, and mine are just now of a sort that won't be stared down. I've had the bailiff's letter, and there's the interest due at Michaelmas besides."

"Ay, ay, the bailiff," said Reuben, shaking his head slowly, as if the word itself carried a weight that required to be shifted from one side of his mind to the other before it could be properly comprehended. "But there's Mester Bartholomew, now—yo'r uncle. He's got brass enough, an' to spare. He could pay it all off wi' what he earns in a month, I'll be bound."

"There's Mester Bartholomew, certainly," said Edward, and there was a slight contraction of his brows as he spoke the name, which showed that this particular remedy was not without its own peculiar bitterness.

Bartholomew Leatherhead was his father's elder brother, a man who had made a considerable fortune in the Birmingham hardware trade, and who had retired some fifteen years since to a property in the neighbourhood of Leamington, where he maintained an establishment of a solidity and respectability that was meant to convey, without the vulgarity of explicit statement, that its owner was a person who had never permitted sentiment to interfere with the main chance. He was a man of sixty, with a hard, shrewd face, in which the small grey eyes had a trick of fixing themselves upon an object with a steadiness that was not so much penetrating as appraising, as if he were habitually estimating the value of whatever came under his notice, whether it were a piece of ironmongery or a human character. He had never forgiven his younger brother for the combination of improvidence and amiability which had led to the gradual alienation of a portion of the family property; and the fact that the elder Leatherhead had preserved, through all his embarrassments, a cheerful and confiding disposition, had only served to deepen that resentment in Bartholomew's mind, for there is nothing which a practical man finds harder to forgive than the spectacle of happiness built upon foundations which he knows to be unsound.
