# The Reformation of Mrs. Chelling

It began, as so many of these things begin, with a leaflet. Mrs. Chelling found it pushed through the letter-box one Tuesday morning, sandwiched between an advertisement for Pears' soap and a bill from the grocer. It was printed on green paper in a type that suggested urgency, and it announced the formation of the Morningside Debating Society for the Advancement of Progressive Thought. "All are welcome," it declared, and beneath this, in italics: "The future belongs to those who question the present."

Mrs. Chelling read it twice and put it in her pocket.

She was twenty-four, and had been married two years to Mr. Herbert Chelling, an estate agent of considerable success and no particular distinction. He had a good office in the High Street of Plumstead Common, a bowler hat which he set upon his head each morning with the gravity of a man performing a sacrament, and a conviction that the world was, on the whole, arranged about as sensibly as it could be. He came home each evening at six, ate his dinner, read the Evening News, and slept the sleep of the justifiably content. It had never occurred to him that his wife might be anything other than satisfied with this arrangement. Nor, indeed, had it occurred to Mrs. Chelling herself—until the leaflet.

She attended the first meeting on the following Thursday. It was held in the back room of a temperance hall, and there were perhaps twenty people present, most of them women of the sort who wore loose jackets and carried notebooks. A Miss Aldridge, who had once been to a conference in Birmingham, spoke for forty minutes on the subject of women's labour, and Mrs. Chelling sat in the second row and felt something stir in her that she could not immediately name.

She returned the next Thursday, and the one after that. By the fourth meeting she had been asked to read a paper, and she chose as her subject "The Economic Dependence of Married Women: A Consideration." She spent a week preparing it at the public library, and Herbert, observing her industry, remarked that it was nice to see her with a hobby.

"A hobby!" she said.

"Well, what would you call it?"

"I should call it an investigation."

Herbert laughed. He was a good-natured man, and he laughed at her the way one laughs at a child who has put on a large hat. "What do you want to investigate for?" he said.

"Because I want to understand things."

"What things?"

"Things," said Mrs. Chelling, with a firmness that surprised them both.

The society expanded. It acquired a more ambitious programme and a second-hand printing press. It attracted speakers of increasingly radical disposition, including a Mrs. Pelham-Finch, who had once shared a platform with a member of the London School Board and who spoke of the liberation of women's productive capacities with an eloquence that left Mrs. Chelling flushed and restless. By the spring, Mrs. Chelling had been elected to the committee, and had begun to wear her hair in a manner that Herbert described, not without alarm, as "intellectual."

It was at about this time that she announced her intention to earn money.

"Earn money?" said Herbert, laying down his paper with the careful deliberation of a man who suspects he has misheard.

"Money. An income. By my own labour."

"But what for?"

Mrs. Chelling regarded him with that new directness which he had come to find unsettling. "Because I wish to be independent," she said. "Because I have been reading about these things, and I have come to see that a woman who does not earn her own living is in a position of—well, of dependence."

"Of course you're dependent. You're my wife."

"That," said Mrs. Chelling, "is precisely what I mean."

There followed a discussion of considerable length, conducted with a civility that barely concealed the alarm on one side and a determination on the other that was all the more formidable for being quiet. Herbert pointed out that he provided a good house, a sufficiency of clothes, and a holiday at Margate every summer. Mrs. Chelling replied that these were the appurtenances of a pet, not the earnings of a person. Herbert said that she was talking nonsense. Mrs. Chelling said that she was talking sense, and that the fact he could not distinguish between the two was a symptom of the very condition she was describing.

She proposed to offer her services as a clerk-typist to a firm in the City. She had, she said, been practising for a month at the society's office, and was now tolerably proficient. She had also, she added, been offered a small salary by the society itself, to assist with its correspondence and the editing of its monthly bulletin, "The Morningside Review."

"You have been doing this without telling me?" said Herbert.

"I have been doing it without asking you," she corrected.

It was this distinction, more than anything else, that frightened him. He was not a stupid man—merely a conventional one, which in England amounts to much the same thing—and he could see that something had shifted in the architecture of his household that he could not push back into place with a firm hand. He lay awake that night and considered the matter. In the morning he did something he had not done since his courtship: he wrote to Mrs. Chelling's father.

Mr. Bunting was a retired schoolmaster who lived in Ealing and held opinions of a sturdy, unreflective kind. He believed in the Empire, the Anglican Church, and the natural authority of the male head of household, and he saw no particular reason to examine these beliefs, since they had served him well enough for sixty years. He received Herbert's letter with grave concern and came over by the next available train.
