Mr. van der Meer’s summons came at the close of a long day spent with ledgers and invoices, the damp chill of the Antwerp docks clinging to my coat. His office, panelled in dark, sweet-smelling wood, was an island of order in the bustling chaos of his merchant house. The single candle on his desk threw his face into sharp relief, carving deep lines around his mouth and eyes. He was a man who valued facts as a miser values gold, and he dealt in them with the same unyielding precision.

“Mr. Hales,” he began, his voice as crisp as the paper before him. “My accounts are in your charge, and you have kept them well. Your figures are true. Therefore, I shall place another, more delicate matter in your care.”

He paused, letting the weight of his words settle in the quiet room. “My sons, Pieter and Karel, are failing in their English. Their tutor, a man of letters from Ghent, has gone to England on some family business. The boys’ future is in the Indies, in the new territories. English is the key that will unlock commerce for them. Without it, they are ships without a rudder.”

I bowed my head. “I will do my utmost, sir.”

“Their mother, Madame van der Meer,” he continued, a slight tightening around his eyes the only sign of his disapproval, “is of the opinion that their spirits are too tender for harsh correction. She believes a gentle hand is best. I believe in results. You will teach them three evenings a week. You will report to me, and to me alone, on their progress. Is that understood?”

“Perfectly, sir.”

“Good. Do not fail me, Mr. Hales. Truth, in all things.”

The first evening, I was shown into the schoolroom, a bright, airy chamber with a view of the manicured garden. The boys, Pieter and Karel, aged twelve and ten, were not idle in their idleness; they were active in their resistance. Pieter, the elder, stared out of the window with a sullen expression, while Karel occupied himself by meticulously folding a piece of paper into ever more complex shapes. The air was thick with a sense of enforced boredom.

“Good evening, gentlemen,” I said, placing my books on the table. “We shall begin with the subjunctive mood.”

Pieter turned his head slowly. “My mother says we are to have a story in English. Not grammar. Grammar is for clerks.”

A soft rustle at the door announced Madame van der Meer. She was a woman of delicate beauty, with pale skin and hair like spun flax, perpetually swathed in silks that whispered when she moved. She smiled at me, a smile that was meant to be charming but which felt like a silken cord.

“Mr. Hales,” she cooed, gliding into the room. “I hope you will not be too severe with my little doves. They have such imaginative minds. I find that to force rules upon them is like trying to cage the lark. Let them sing, I say.”

“Madame,” I replied, keeping my voice level, “a lark that cannot sing in the common tongue may find itself unheard in the wider world. A firm grasp of the language is the cage that permits the song.”

She looked at me as if I had uttered a mild obscenity. “What a severe philosophy! But you English are so practical. Come, my boys, show Mr. Hales the pretty picture-book we have found.”

Thus the lessons were shaped. We did not decline verbs; we cooed over plates of pastoral scenes. We did not parse sentences; we listened to Madame’s anecdotes of her childhood, which she insisted I translate into simple, unchallenging English. The boys learned nothing. Their eyes, bright with their mother’s indulgence, held a glint of triumph whenever I attempted to introduce a point of grammar, only to have Madame glide in and dismiss it as ‘too dreary’.

The weeks that followed were a trial of my patience and my conscience. Each evening I left the house feeling a peculiar frustration, a sense of my own purpose being thwarted. By day, I sat at my desk, totalling columns of figures where every mark had its meaning, its consequence. The stark truth of the ledger book stood in mocking contrast to the fog of falsehood that was the schoolroom. The boys were not stupid; they were wilfully ignorant, sheltered by a mother who traded their future for a temporary peace.

The crisis arrived on a Tuesday, the day of my report. Mr. van der Meer received me in his office. The rain lashed against the tall windows, and the wind moaned in the chimney like a lost soul.

“Well, Mr. Hales?” he asked, his gaze as sharp as a hawk’s. “How do my sons progress? Can they yet conduct a simple transaction in English? Can they draft a letter of credit?”

The truth was a bitter pill on my tongue. I saw the boys’ smug faces, heard Madame’s tinkling laugh, felt the weight of the merchant’s trust. To lie would be to betray him, to condemn the boys to a future for which they were wholly unprepared. To tell the truth would be to bring a domestic storm down upon my head and theirs.

For a moment, I hesitated. The injustice of it all—the mother’s sabotage, the boys’ laziness—rose in me like a fever. I was their teacher; I had a duty not merely to them, but to the man who paid me. A duty to the truth.

“They have made little progress, sir,” I said, the words falling like stones into the quiet room.

Mr. van der Meer’s face did not change, but a stillness came over him that was more menacing than any anger. “Explain.”
