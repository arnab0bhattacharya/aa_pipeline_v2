The dust motes danced. They caught the late afternoon sun, slanting now, heavy and gold, through the tall windows of the Form Room, and he watched them—if one watched them, if one fixed the eye and did not blink, they seemed purposeful, a slow revolving constellation in the musty air, and then, if one blinked, they were merely dust, settling on the dark wood, the ink-stained grooves, the ordinary things. But it was the end of the term; it was the end of all terms; he was leaving, and so the dust seemed to him now like something preservative, a fine silt falling over the fossil beds of the afternoon.

He sat at the high master’s desk, his hands flat on the leather top, which was cracked and worn smooth, feeling the emptiness of the room. It pressed on him. Thirty years he had sat here, and the room was empty, and it was not empty; it was full to bursting. The chalk dust hung in the shafts of light, and the smell of it—dry, cold, and acrid—mixed with the smell of damp wool and boiled potatoes from the kitchens below. He breathed it in, feeling it fill him, and he thought: I am a vessel, and they have poured out of me. All of them. Down the stairs, out into the gravel, into the world, into the mud.

He looked at the desk in the front row, the third from the left. There was a name carved there. He could not read it from where he sat, but he knew it was there; they were all there, little scars in the oak, gouged out with penknives in idle minutes, in the slow dragging hours of translation. He pushed himself up, his knees stiff, and walked down the aisle between the desks. His footsteps echoed on the boards, a hollow sound, the sound of a man walking in a church, or a warehouse, somewhere built for a multitude and holding only one.

He laid his hand on the desk. The wood was cool. He traced the letters with his finger, feeling the rough edges where the boy had pressed too hard. P-E-L-H-A-M. Pelham. And there it was, rising up in him—not a thought, not a picture, but a sensation, the feel of a cricket ball hitting the meat of the bat, the smell of cut grass, and a voice, high and clear, calling out from the far side of the field, and then the voice was gone, and there was only the name, and the wood, and the sun moving on the wall.

There had been so many. They came in boys, and they left boys, in a sense, because they were always boys to him, even when they grew tall and wore moustaches and came back to visit with their wives, awkward in the drawing room, holding teacups as if they were grenades. But the ones who did not come back, the ones who were still boys, fixed and frozen in the amber of his memory—they were the ones who pressed on him now, in this empty room, in this last hour.

He moved to the next desk. The name was fainter here, worn by elbows and sleeves. He bent his head. T-A-Y-L-E-N. No, Taylour. With a U. He had corrected it, he remembered, corrected the spelling, and the boy had looked up at him with those eyes—what colour were they?—and said, “But sir, it is the old spelling, my grandfather spelt it so.” And he had felt a fool, and a pleasure, too, because the boy had a history, a lineage stretching back beyond the school, beyond Kent, into some dimmer England, and he was part of it, the old schoolmaster with his chalk and his Virgil, a small link in a long chain.

But Taylour was gone. Gone to Gallipoli, or was it Salonika? The places blurred in his mind, all heat and flies and dust, so different from this damp green county. He only knew that Taylour was gone, and that the desk remained, and that the name was fading.

He walked back to the master’s desk and sat down. The sun had moved further, throwing long shadows across the floor. The room was darkening. He thought of the boys he had forgotten. There must be many. They had sat here, thirty at a time, a new batch every year, and they had looked at him, and he had looked at them, and they had written their names on their papers, and he had marked them, and they had gone. What were their faces? He could not see them. They were a blur, a sea of faces, and he felt a sudden shame, a hot prickling in his neck, that he had forgotten them, that they had passed through him and left no trace.

He opened the desk drawer. It was empty, save for a single piece of chalk, broken in two. He took it out and turned it in his fingers. It was smooth on one side, where the thumb had pressed, and sharp on the other. He could write with it, on the board behind him, a word, a name, a date. But there was no one to read it.

He thought of the war, of the lists that came, pinned to the noticeboard in the hallway, the names in black ink, and the silence that fell over the school when the new list went up. He had read them, standing in the hallway, his hat in his hand, and the names had seemed to him not like names at all, but like holes in the air, gaps in the fabric of the world, and he had felt the draft from them, a cold wind blowing through the school.
