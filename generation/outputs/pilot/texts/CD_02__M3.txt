If any young gentleman of modest expectations and extraordinary uncles had ever reason to curse the day he first possessed a spare shilling, that young gentleman was Mr. Edwin Puffle. Mr. Puffle was a clerk—a very damp, very ink-stained, and very patient clerk—in the counting-house of Messrs. Gripe, Grasp, and Clench, ship-brokers, of Narrow Lane, in the City of London; and in the year of grace one thousand eight hundred and forty-something, he happened, by one of those unaccountable freaks of Fortune which she plays upon the insignificant, to be the holder of a winning number in a public lottery.

It was not a magnificent sum. It was not a sum to purchase a country estate, or a carriage, or a seat in Parliament. It was exactly fifty pounds. But to Edwin Puffle, who existed upon a salary of forty-five pounds per annum, and whose weekly accounts were generally a long arithmetic of halfpence, it appeared a gilded mountain of opulence. He received the golden pieces in a small canvas bag, and he resolved, with all the solemnity of a man making a will, to keep the matter an inviolable secret from his relations.

Mr. Puffle had relations. He had them in alarming profusion. He had a mother-in-law’s sister, Mrs. Sarah Squeaks, who was afflicted with a perpetual cold and a still more perpetual demand for loans. He had a cousin, Mr. Nathaniel Nipper, a man of no visible occupation, who lived by the mysterious doctrine that blood is thicker than water, and, therefore, a relative’s pocket is a common reservoir. He had an uncle, Major Stiltstock, a retired militia officer of immense stomach and no visible income, who believed the world owed him a living, and Edwin owed the world. These, and a few minor satellites, revolved about the planet of Puffle, constantly eclipsing his comfort.

On the evening of his good fortune, Mr. Puffle sat in his lodging in Thumbling’s Rents, a narrow thoroughfare near the river, where the houses leaned forward as if to whisper each other into ruin. His apartment was on the third floor, and consisted of one room, in which he cooked, ate, slept, and was occasionally dunned. He placed the canvas bag in the hollow of his only spare boot, pushed the boot under the bed, and, folding his arms, looked round with the complacency of a monarch surveying his dominions. He was safe. He was solitary. He was rich. And he would tell no one.

Within a week, every one of them had heard of it.

How the intelligence escaped is one of those mysteries which no philosophy can unravel. Perhaps the clerk at the lottery office had a brother who kept a public-house, and the publican had a customer who was a postman, and the postman had a friend who was a tailor, and the tailor had a brother-in-law who knew Mrs. Squeaks. Or perhaps Mrs. Squeaks herself, endowed with that supernatural instinct which belongs to needy relatives, smelt the gold through the brick walls of Narrow Lane. At any rate, on the seventh morning, while Mr. Puffle was consuming his modest tea and a fragment of toasted bread, he heard a heavy, asthmatic tread upon the staircase, and presently Mrs. Sarah Squeaks stood in the doorway, breathing like a pair of agricultural bellows, and shaking her umbrella as if it were a weapon of assault.

“Eh, dear, dear, Edwin!” said Mrs. Squeaks, sinking into the only chair, and pressing her hand to her side. “I am almost gone. The stairs! The cruel stairs! But I had to come. I had to come, if it was my last effort. You have heard, of course, of my affliction?”

Mr. Puffle, with the guilty air of a man who has hidden a boot, said he had not.

“Not heard!” cried Mrs. Squeaks, with a tragic inflection that seemed to imply the whole world was callous. “Not heard that my landlord has raised my rent, and that unless I can pay five pounds by tomorrow, I and my poor orphan niece will be turned into the street! Five pounds, Edwin! And such a small sum to one who has just come into fifty! For I know all, Edwin. I had it from Mrs. Grummage, who had it from the greengrocer, who had it from the man who cleans the lottery office windows. Five pounds, Edwin—and the amount is exactly fifty!”

Mr. Puffle, with that fatal weakness which distinguishes the timid in the presence of the importunate, produced five golden pounds. Mrs. Squeaks, having kissed him, wept over him, and called him her preserver, departed with a velocity that seemed inconsistent with her previous asthmatic condition.

He had scarcely recovered from this visit when there came a loud, military knock, and Mr. Nathaniel Nipper bounced into the room, with his hat on one side and his hand extended.

“Well, Ed!” said Mr. Nipper, with that jocular familiarity which is the last refuge of the borrower. “Glad to see you looking so flourishing. Prosperity becomes you. I always said you’d rise in the world. You have a head, Ed—a head. And a head, properly directed, is worth money. Speaking of money, I want ten pounds.”

Mr. Puffle feebly protested.

“Ten pounds, Ed,” repeated Mr. Nipper, with the air of a man arranging a matter of business. “I have a little affair on hand—an investment—infallible—which only requires ten pounds to set it going. I came to you, because you’re a man of sense, and because you’ve just won fifty. Come, Ed, don’t be mean. Ten pounds, and I’ll make it twenty in a month.”

Mr. Puffle, overborne by the audacity of the demand, and the hopelessness of resistance, produced ten pounds. Mr. Nipper, having slapped him on the back, called him a trump, and vanished, leaving behind him a strong smell of tobacco and a general impression of moral bankruptcy.

The third visitor was the Major. The Major came in the evening, with the slow, deliberate tread of a man who intends to make a long stay, and who carries his own resources in his waistcoat. He was a large man, with a purple face, and a manner of adjusting his shirt-collar, which seemed to imply that the world was a parade-ground, and he was reviewing it. He sat down, placed his cane between his legs, and, after a preliminary cough, began:

“Well, Edwin, my boy, I am glad to find you at home. I have been thinking of you. I have been thinking that you are a young man of promise, and that I, as your uncle, have a duty to perform. I have a duty, Edwin. A duty to myself, and a duty to you. I have, in short, a little bill—a trifle—thirty-five pounds—which falls due tomorrow, and which, if not met, will involve consequences which I shudder to contemplate. I need not say more. I know your generous nature. I know your recent good fortune. I am not the man to remind you of it, but—”

The Major paused, adjusted his collar, and looked at the ceiling.

“But,” said Mr. Puffle, faintly, “I have already—”

“Already!” interrupted the Major, with a look of pained surprise. “Surely, Edwin, you have not been so weak—so fatally weak—as to part with any of it already? To whom? To that woman Squeaks? To that scapegrace Nipper? Folly, Edwin! Utter folly! They are not relations of your standing. I am the head of your family. I am the natural guardian of your interests. Give me the thirty-five pounds, and I will take care of it, and of you.”

Mr. Puffle, with the despairing conviction that he was ruined, produced the remaining thirty-five pounds. The Major, having pocketed them, observed that he had always had a reliance on Edwin’s good sense, and that he would call again in a day or two, and talk over the family interests. He then departed, leaving Mr. Puffle sitting on the edge of his bed, with the empty boot in his hand, and a conviction that he was the most unfortunate of mortals.

The next morning, as he sat at his desk in the counting-house of Messrs. Gripe, Grasp, and Clench, with a pen behind each ear, and a blot before him, Mr. Puffle was visited by a philosophical reflection. He thought that if he had not won the fifty pounds, he would have been spared the visits of his relations, the loss of his money, and the humiliation of his present poverty. He thought that Fortune was a jade, who gave with one hand only to enable her relatives to take with the other. He thought that the best thing a man could do, when he came into a little money, was to lose it immediately, or to hide it in a place where even his relations could not find it. And he thought, finally, that the boot was a very indifferent hiding-place, and that the next time he won fifty pounds, he would put it in the fire, or throw it in the river, or do anything with it, rather than let his relations know of it.
