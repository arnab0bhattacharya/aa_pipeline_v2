It was in the heavy, rain-washed dusk of a late November evening that I found myself descending the narrow stairs from my room in Scotland Yard, carrying within my breast the weight of an order which, though worded with the bland civility of a new superior, possessed for me the somber finality of a death sentence pronounced by some blind, indifferent fate. The gaslight in the stone corridor flickered as if uncertain of its own reality, casting long, distorted shadows that seemed to mock the very notion of justice I had, until that morning, believed myself to be serving. Assistant Commissioner Voss—a man recently transferred from the Colonial Service, possessing the immaculate boots and untroubled conscience of one who has never had to look into the eyes of a man he is about to betray—had spoken to me across the polished mahogany of his desk with that air of administrative brightness which is perhaps the most terrible form of cruelty, because it is entirely unconscious of itself. He desired an arrest. The newspapers, he observed, had been troublesome of late, suggesting that the authorities were lethargic in the matter of foreign anarchists. He wished to demonstrate energy. He did not much care, I perceived, whether the individual arrested was guilty of any particular crime, so long as the individual was plausibly connected to the circles that met in the back rooms of Soho, and would furnish the morning papers with a photograph of sufficient notoriety to satisfy the public appetite for vigilance. I had listened, I believe, with an expression of attentive composure, while within me there stirred something that was not precisely indignation—for indignation presupposes a surprise I had long ceased to feel—but rather a profound, weary recognition of the manner in which the machinery of institutions grinds forward, indifferent to the flesh it crushes between its wheels.

For three years I had cultivated my network among the exiles, the refugees, the dreamers and the desperate who congregated in the shabby rooms above the eating-houses of that quarter. It is a work of patience, of moral equivocation, of a slow insinuation into confidences that leaves one’s own sense of integrity suspended in a grey twilight where the distinction between fidelity and treachery becomes, by imperceptible degrees, impossible to discern. I had informants, of course. Several. But there was only one upon whom I could rely with that absolute certainty which is the sole luxury permitted to a man in my position, and that was Ladislas Korom. He was a man of some education, a Hungarian by birth, who had fled his native country after the suppression of what the newspapers called a disturbance, but which those who lived through it knew to have been a convulsion that tore apart the very fabric of their lives. He kept a small shop where he repaired watches and clocks—a trade at which he was remarkably skilled, his long, pale fingers possessing a delicacy of touch that seemed almost incongruous with the coarseness of his circumstances. It was through him that I had learned of the meetings, the printing presses, the caches of pamphlets whose incendiary rhetoric was, in truth, more pathetic than dangerous, but which nevertheless constituted, under the letter of the law, grounds for prosecution. He had given me names. He had given me warnings. On two occasions, the information he provided had prevented acts of violence that would have resulted in loss of life, and the knowledge of this—that I had, through him, preserved certain persons from death—had sustained me through the long periods of moral unease that are inseparable from the life of a police inspector who moves among men as a deceiver.

I found him that evening, as I had expected, in the narrow room above his shop, seated at his workbench beneath a single gas-jet that cast a yellowish, unhealthy glow over the disassembled mechanism of a clock. The room smelled of brass filings and strong tobacco. He looked up as I entered, and the expression on his face—that expression of guarded welcome which I had come to know so well, in which trust and suspicion were held in a balance so delicate that a breath might disturb it—produced in me a sensation I can only describe as a kind of nausea of the soul. He offered me a chair. He offered me tobacco. I accepted both, and for some minutes we sat in that silence which, between men who are bound together by a secret, can be more eloquent than speech.

I was reflecting, with a bitterness that surprised even myself, upon the irony of my situation. The very qualities that had made Korom invaluable to me—his reliability, his centrality to the network, the fact that he was known and trusted by figures whose names appeared in police files—were now the very qualities that made him the only candidate for Voss’s demanded arrest. He was plausible. He was foreign. He was connected. And because he was my informant, I knew precisely where he would be at any given hour, and precisely what evidence could be assembled against him, for I had, in a sense, manufactured that evidence myself through the reports I had filed over the years—reports that documented his associations, his presence at meetings, his proximity to declarations that, taken out of the context of the cooperation he was providing, assumed the aspect of conspiracy.

I knocked the ash from my pipe with a deliberation that was, I suppose, a form of procrastination, and I said to him, in that oblique manner which the life compels a man to adopt, that the climate of the city was changing. That there were pressures from above. That certain persons had been named. He watched me with his dark, intelligent eyes—eyes that had seen, I knew, things far worse than anything this city could produce—and I observed in their depths a slow alteration, a darkening, as if a cloud had passed across the light of comprehension within him. He said nothing for a long time. Then he asked me, in that halting English of his, with its curious formalities and its foreign constructions, whether I was advising him to leave.

The word struck me with the force of a physical blow. To leave. The simplicity of it. The reasonableness. And yet I knew, with a certainty that lay upon my chest like a stone, that I could not permit it. If he vanished, the arrest would not be made. Voss would demand an explanation. My network would be exposed. And the consequences would fall, not upon me alone, but upon others—upon informants I had not yet identified, upon operations that were still in their delicate, nascent stages. The machinery required its sacrifice, and Korom was to be that sacrifice, because the machinery could not conceive of any other form of resolution.

I told him that I was not advising him to leave. I told him that I was advising nothing. I told him that the situation was as I had described it, and that he must draw his own conclusions. The words left my mouth with the hollow, ceremonial sound of phrases uttered at a funeral—phrases which signify nothing, which comfort no one, and which serve only to mark the passage of a soul from one state of being to another, irrevocably.

He understood. I saw it in the way his hands, which had been resting on the workbench with the stillness of a man accustomed to precision, became very slightly tremulous, and then were still again, with a stillness that was deliberate, controlled, the stillness of a man who has determined upon a course of action and will not be moved from it by any expression of feeling. He said, at last, that he had always known this moment would come. He said it without bitterness, without reproach, with only a kind of weary fatalism that I found more unbearable than any accusation could have been. He said that he had placed his trust in me, and that the trust had, in its own way, been honoured—for had I not come to warn him? Had I not, by my very presence in that room, acknowledged the debt that existed between us?

I could not look at him. I sat there, in that yellowish light, with the smell of brass and tobacco in my nostrils, and I felt upon me the full, crushing weight of that moral solitude which is the inevitable inheritance of a man who has made of deception a profession and of fidelity an impossibility. I was preserving the safety of the many by sacrificing the one who had made that safety possible. And the one, in his terrible, quiet comprehension, was offering me, in his restraint, an absolution I had not earned and could not accept.

When at last I rose to go, he extended his hand. I took it. The palm was dry, the grip firm, the clasp brief—the handshake of men who part with the understanding that they will not meet again, and who are determined that the parting shall carry no stain of theatrical emotion. I descended the stairs into the street, where the November rain fell in a fine, persistent drizzle that seemed to dissolve the very outlines of the buildings, so that the city appeared as a mirage, a phantom of brick and gaslight, trembling on the edge of dissolution. And I walked back through those streets, beneath the lampposts that haloed in the wet air, carrying with me the knowledge that I had set in motion a process which would end, as such processes always end, in a courtroom, in a sentence, in the slow, grinding obliteration of a life that had been, in its own obscure and melancholy fashion, a life of honour.
