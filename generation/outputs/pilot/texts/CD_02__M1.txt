Mr. Peregrine Pym, junior clerk in the shipping firm of Bodkins, Blunt & Fogg, was a young man whose entire worldly fortune, until a certain Tuesday in the month of November, might have been contained within a thimble, with room to spare for his modest hopes and a small collection of buttonholes. His existence was a quiet, unvarnished one, spent in the dim, ink-scented labyrinth of a counting-house in Leadenhall Street, where the scratching of pens was the only music, and the only drama was the slow, inexorable march of ledgers from one side of a desk to the other. Lodging in a single, narrow room in a house that leaned over a grimy court off Fetter Lane, Mr. Pym was a man of routine and routine’s quiet desperation, until the day a ticket in the Grand Metropolitan Lottery, purchased on a whim with a sixpence he had almost regretted spending, awarded him the sum of one hundred and seventeen pounds, fourteen shillings, and sixpence.

This was not a fortune to set the Thames on fire; it would not have purchased a single share in a respectable steam-vessel; but to Mr. Pym it was a treasure of Croesus, a golden mountain rising from the flat, grey plain of his life. It was a secret, a delicious, private hoard, and he resolved, with all the determination of a man who had never had a secret worth keeping, that it should remain so. He knew his relations, the Pym and the Cribb connection, as a botanist knows a patch of particularly tenacious weeds. They were a grasping, needy, and wonderfully imaginative family, whose misfortunes were always precisely timed and whose requirements were invariably calibrated to the exact measure of any available funds. To them, Mr. Pym’s good fortune would be not his own, but a communal resource, a pot of honey into which they would dip their sticky fingers with cheerful unanimity.

For six days, Mr. Pym kept his counsel. He banked his prize in the London and Provincial, and walked the streets with the light, buoyant step of a man who has a sovereign in his pocket and a secret in his heart. He bought a new neckerchief. He dined on a chop. He felt, for the first time, like a person of consequence. On the seventh day, as a damp November fog pressed its grimy face against his window-pane, the siege began.

It was heralded by a thunderous knocking at the door below, a knock that spoke of grievous tidings and an urgent desire for tea. Mrs. Flinders, his landlady, a woman whose face was a permanent expression of weary anticipation, announced a visitor. It was his aunt, Mrs. Cribb, a lady built on the lines of a funereal monument and dressed in the perpetual weeds of some long-forgotten sorrow. She swept into the small room, filling it with the scent of camphor and impending doom.

“Peregrine!” she began, pressing a damp glove to her brow. “Oh, my dear boy, you will scarcely credit the calamity that has befallen us! Your cousin, Bartholomew, that bright, promising boy…”

Mr. Pym’s heart sank. Bartholomew was a young man of thirty-five whose promising qualities had so far eluded discovery. “What has happened to him, Aunt?”

“He is to be married!” she cried, as if announcing a terminal illness. “To a Miss Prattle, of Peckham, whose family, I am sorry to say, has no notion of how a wedding ought to be conducted. The dress, the flowers, the breakfast at the Dog and Duck—it is a vortex of expense! Bartholomew, my poor, dear boy, finds himself short. A trifle, a mere bagatelle, but it is the little stones that cause the coach to overturn. He requires, to see the happy couple safely upon their way, the sum of precisely fifty pounds.”

Mr. Pym, flustered and cornered, stammered that he was a man of small means. But Mrs. Cribb was a force of nature. She painted a picture of Bartholomew’s ruin, of the bride’s disappointment, of the family name dragged through the mud of Peckham, with such persuasive gloom that Mr. Pym, before he knew what he was doing, was writing a cheque. As she departed, dabbing at her eyes with a triumphant air, she patted his hand. “You are a good boy, Peregrine. A true Pym. We knew we could rely on you.”

He had scarcely time to mourn the loss of fifty pounds when, an hour later, the knocking recommenced. This time it was his uncle, Mr. Silas Pym, a man who dealt in damaged goods and sorrows of a wholesale nature. He entered with a sigh that seemed to contain all the wind and rain of the preceding decade.

“Nephew,” he said, sinking into a chair. “Disaster. A catastrophe of the first water. The brig *Sea Serpent*, bound for Riga with a cargo of—well, no matter what—a fine, upstanding cargo—has gone down. All hands, and all goods, lost to the merciless deep.”

“Good heavens!” said Mr. Pym.

“My underwriter,” continued Mr. Pym, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, “is a man of stone, a veritable granite block of a man. He will not advance a penny until the paperwork is in order. But the captain’s widow, a poor, weeping creature, requires an immediate sum for sustenance and passage back to her family in Portsmouth. It is a matter of life and death, Peregrine. A temporary loan, you understand. A loan of exactly forty pounds, to be repaid the moment the insurance is settled.”

The sum was so specific, the story so tragically convenient, that Mr. Pym felt a cold dread. But the image of the weeping widow and the granite-hearted underwriter was too powerful. With a heavy sigh, he wrote another cheque. Uncle Silas clapped him on the shoulder, his grief instantly evaporating. “Sound man, Peregrine. A rock. I shall not forget this.” He departed, leaving behind the faint smell of stale brandy and impending litigation.

Mr. Pym sat in a state of profound shock. He had given away ninety pounds. His golden mountain was now a mere hillock. He calculated what remained: twenty-seven pounds, fourteen shillings, and sixpence. A respectable sum, but not the transformative one he had dreamed of. He would have to be more resolute. He must harden his heart.

He was still engaged in this difficult moral exercise when the third and final assault was mounted. It was his cousin, Arabella Cribb, a young lady whose life was a perpetual drama of thwarted ambition and inadequate bonnets. She burst into the room in a cloud of frantic energy and smelling salts.

“Oh, Peregrine! You will never guess! My triumph! My undoing!” she cried. “The celebrated Signor Fantocini has chosen *me*! Me, for the part of Ophelia at the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane! It is the chance of a lifetime!”

“Why, Arabella, that is wonderful news!” said Mr. Pym, genuinely pleased.

“It is a disaster!” she wailed. “For I have nothing to wear! The costume, the gowns, the very feathers for the mad scene must be of the finest silk! Signor Fantocini is a tyrant of taste. He has looked upon my own poor wardrobe and wept. I require a complete new set of theatrical finery. The cost, from Madame Fifi’s in Covent Garden, is a matter of grave importance. I am short, by the most cruel stroke of fate, of precisely twenty-seven pounds.”

Mr. Pym stared at her. Twenty-seven pounds. The exact amount of his remaining capital, down to the last shilling. The coincidence was so perfect, so audaciously calculated, that he almost laughed. He looked from Arabella’s pleading, desperate face to the window, where the fog swirled like a dirty spectre of his own lost fortune. He thought of Bartholomew’s wedding, of the *Sea Serpent*’s watery grave, of his own foolish, secretive pride. He had wanted to be a man of substance, but he was merely a man of substance to be extracted.

With a feeling of utter resignation, he took out his chequebook. He wrote the final cheque for twenty-seven pounds, leaving the fourteen shillings and sixpence for his own use. Arabella seized the paper, kissed him fervently on the cheek, and swept out of the room, her mission accomplished, her tragedy averted.

Mr. Pym sat alone in the silence. His secret was out; his fortune was gone. He was precisely where he had been a week ago, save for a new neckerchief and a profound education in the ways of the world. As he sat there, a strange, bubbling sound began to issue from his chest. It grew into a chuckle, then a full-throated, helpless laugh. He had been a fool, a prize goose, a veritable lottery of one for his relations to pluck. And yet, as the laughter subsided, he felt not bitterness, but a peculiar lightness. The secret had been a burden; the fortune, a temptation. He picked up his pen, dipped it in the ink, and began to draft a letter of application for a small raise in his wages, a sum so modest and so entirely his own that no one would ever think to ask him for it. Outside, the fog began to lift.
