The heat of the valley, a stagnant and oppressive weight, lay upon the town of Santa Rosa like a shroud, pressing the breath out of the very stones. It was a place of crumbling plaster and shuttered windows, where the silence of the siesta was broken only by the buzzing of flies in the stagnant air of the cantina, or the distant, hollow clang of a church bell marking the hours that seemed to have no beginning and no end. In the office of the Ministry of Public Works, a room high-ceilinged and vast, smelling of dust and damp leather, Mr. Arthur Pemberton sat before a map that represented not merely the topography of the land, but the intricate, treacherous web of his own existence.

Pemberton was a man of iron and calculation, an Englishman of the type that exports itself to the ends of the earth to impose order upon chaos, yet he found himself here, in the year of our Lord 1893, the object of a contest that had nothing to do with gradients or ballast. He was the pivot upon which the fate of the republic turned, and the knowledge of it sat heavy upon his soul, distinct from the physical heat. The government, represented by the sleek and sweating figure of the Minister, Don Rafael, demanded his loyalty, his signature, his genius for bridging chasms. They spoke of civilization, of progress, of the railway that would bind the coastal cities to the mineral wealth of the interior, a steel artery that would pump life into the dying body of the nation.

But the passes—the vital, narrow throats of the mountains where the line must go—were held by the General.

The General did not sit in offices. He existed in the mist and the rock, a shadow with a following of men who were less soldiers than brigands with a cause. He had sent a message, not written in ink but carried by a man with eyes like flint and a machete at his belt. The message was simple, devoid of the flowery rhetoric of the Ministry. The railway would be built, yes. But it would be built for the people, for the liberation of the valley, not for the coffers of the capital. The General, too, promised civilization—a civilization of the rifle and the shared loaf, perhaps, but a civilization nonetheless.

Pemberton looked at the map. The red line of the proposed survey snaked through the blue contortions of the Cordillera. It was a line of blood, he thought, or perhaps a line of ash. He picked up his pencil, the graphite tip hovering over the paper. To choose the route favored by the Ministry meant to invite the General’s wrath, to see his coolies shot in the ravines and his bridges blown to scrap metal before the first spike was driven. To favor the General’s pass, the difficult, jagged route through the *Quebrada del Diablo*, was to defy the men who paid his wages and held the title to the land.

He heard the door open behind him, the heavy, sweeping sound of a frock coat. It was Don Rafael. The Minister smelled of bay rum and anxiety.

“Mr. Pemberton,” Don Rafael said, his voice smooth as oil, yet quivering with a latent hysteria. “You have been staring at the mountains for an hour. They do not speak, do they? It is we who must give them a voice.”

“They speak loudly enough, Your Excellency,” Pemberton replied, not turning. His voice was dry, the English accent clipping the Spanish vowels into something hard and unyielding. “They speak of rockfalls and grades of one in forty. They speak of expense.”

“Expense is a matter for the treasury,” Don Rafael waved a hand, a gesture of magnificent, hollow indifference. “But the *morale* of the investor... ah, that is delicate. We must show that the Republic is master in its own house. We must go through the *Paso del Sol*. It is direct. It is... triumphant.”

“The *Paso del Sol* is held by the General’s nephew,” Pemberton said slowly. “If I send my surveyors there, I am sending them to their death. And if the line is cut there, the railway is a failure before it begins. It is a bad engineering decision, Don Rafael. It is a vanity.”

The Minister moved to the table, his shadow falling over the map, darkening the red line. “And the alternative? To skulk through the Devil’s Gorge? To concede that the bandit controls the arteries of the state? No, Mr. Pemberton. You English understand discipline. You understand that order must be imposed. You will go to the *Paso del Sol*. You will make the line go there.”

“And if the General objects?”

“The General is a fever,” Don Rafael said, his eyes darting to the window, as if expecting a bullet. “Fever passes. The railway remains. You have our protection. You have the army.”

“The army is in the barracks,” Pemberton muttered, turning at last to face the man. “The General is in the mountains. There is a difference between a map and a territory, Your Excellency.”

Don Rafael smiled, a thin stretching of the lips that did not reach his eyes. “Then make the map the territory, Mr. Pemberton. That is what we pay you for. Civilisation is not a gift, you know. It is an act of violence. It is the cutting away of the wilderness.”

He left then, leaving behind the scent of cologne and the crushing weight of the command. Pemberton sat back in his chair. The light in the room was failing, the shadows lengthening, turning the white plaster of the walls to a bruised purple. He felt a profound, weary disgust—not with the General, who was a savage with a code, nor with the Minister, who was a thief with a title, but with the iron rails themselves. He had thought them a force of logic, of geometry. Here, they were merely a rope for hanging men.

He rose and walked to the window, looking out at the darkening silhouette of the mountains. They were immense, indifferent, and terrible. Somewhere up there, in the cold air, the General was waiting. He would demand an answer soon. He would demand a signature on the ground, written in the dust of the trail.

Pemberton realized then that his loyalty was a currency that could not be spent in two markets. He had been hired to bring light to this dark place, but the light he carried was merely a lantern that illuminated the faces of the men who wanted to steal the oil. The railway would come, yes. It would snake its way through the rock, indifferent to the blood spilled to lay it. It would bring the trains, the clocks, the ledgers. But it would not bring justice. It would only bring the means to exploit the injustice more efficiently.

He picked up his heavy theodolite case, checking the brass fittings with a mechanical tenderness. It was the only honest thing in the room. It measured what was there, not what was desired. He would go out at dawn. He would not go to the *Paso del Sol*, nor to the Devil’s Gorge. He would go to the place where the earth demanded to be crossed, where the physics of the slope allowed it. He would let the mountain decide.

And if the General shot him, or the Minister dismissed him, it would be a small matter in the long, indifferent history of the Cordillera. The silence of the room pressed in again, heavy as the heat of the coming day. Pemberton lit the lamp, the small flame trembling in the glass, a fragile, solitary spark against the encroaching dark. He sat down to write his letter of resignation, or perhaps his letter of acceptance. He did not know which. He only knew that the line must be drawn, and that he was the hand holding the pencil.
