Mr. Octavius Pinchbeck, junior clerk in the counting-house of Messrs. Grumble, Hogg & Vane, Shipping Agents and General Merchants, of Candlewick Street, in the City of London, was a young gentleman of such retiring disposition, and so small a figure in the world’s eye, that the very porters at the wharf, who knew every face upon the river, were wont to salute him as “the young man as comes and goes,”—a designation which, while it did no great honour to his individuality, had at least the merit of accuracy. He was thin, he was sandy, he was given to stooping; his coat, though brushed with a fidelity that argued a good heart, had that shiny, threadbare aspect which bespeaks a garment long acquainted with its wearer’s elbows; and his whole appearance was that of a man who might be supposed to have been born at a desk, to have been weaned upon a ledger, and to be destined, in the fullness of time, to be buried in a ledger, with a pen behind his ear for a monument.

Now it chanced that Mr. Pinchbeck, being of a modest and speculative turn, and having by him a certain sum of two pounds seven shillings, the slow accumulation of many months of self-denial, did one day purchase a ticket in a public lottery—not the great State affair, be it understood, but a small and semi-respectable raffle, got up for the benefit of a charitable institution, and conducted by a committee of exceedingly benevolent gentlemen, who took care to secure their own expenses first. And it further chanced—for Fortune is a lady of the most unaccountable caprices—that the number upon Mr. Pinchbeck’s ticket was that which came up the winner of the third prize: to wit, the sum of forty pounds sterling.

Forty pounds! To Mr. Pinchbeck, whose annual salary was eighty, and who had never in his life possessed so much as ten pounds at one time, it was a fortune. He received the money in a little canvas bag, counted it thrice over in a retired corner of the office, and went home to his lodgings in Little Popham’s Rents, off the Minories, with his hat pressed down upon his brows, and his hand firmly clenched upon his breast-pocket, and his heart beating like a watchman’s rattle. And there, in the privacy of his own chamber, he resolved—with a caution which did honour to his prudence—that not a soul should know of his good fortune; for he was acquainted, by long and bitter experience, with the nature of his relatives.

Mr. Pinchbeck’s relatives were numerous, and they were terrible. There was his aunt, Mrs. Sophronia Gudge, a widow of ponderous respectability, who wore mourning for a husband deceased these fifteen years, and who spoke of “poor dear Gudge” upon every possible occasion, and upon some that were impossible. There was his uncle, Mr. Bartholomew Pinchbeck, a man of vast and mysterious schemes, who had been upon the point of making his fortune in every quarter of the globe, and had never yet made it in any. There was his cousin, Mr. Adolphus Pinchbeck, who played the flute, and had a complaint of the chest, and considered himself ill-used by Providence. There was his cousin, Miss Amelia Pinchbeck, who was of a romantic turn, and painted flowers upon velvet, and had once been disappointed in love—or said so. And there was his mother’s second cousin, Mrs. Wix, who kept a small shop for the sale of stay-laces and small haberdashery, and who had a way of sighing over her tea that was more eloquent than any words.

Within a week, every one of them had heard of it.

How the intelligence travelled, Mr. Pinchbeck never could discover. Whether the young man who swept the office had overheard him counting the sovereigns; whether the landlady, Mrs. Cribb, had peeped through the keyhole; whether the very cat upon the hearth had been in the pay of his relations—certain it is that upon the seventh day after his good fortune, and upon the very evening when he had resolved to purchase a new hat and a pair of gloves, and perhaps treat himself to a box at the theatre, there came a knock at his door, and there entered Mrs. Sophronia Gudge.

“My dear Octavius,” said that lady, sinking into the easy-chair with a rustle of black crape, and applying a handkerchief to her eyes, “I am the bearer of heavy tidings.”

Mr. Pinchbeck, who had hastily thrust his canvas bag beneath the pillow, inquired, with a quaking voice, what had happened.

“It is your poor uncle Bartholomew,” said Mrs. Gudge. “He has been arrested for debt—the sum is forty pounds—and unless it be paid by tomorrow noon, he must go to the Fleet, and there he will die, for his constitution is not what it was, and the damp of that place is dreadful, and I have no doubt it will carry him off, and then what will become of us all?”

Mr. Pinchbeck was about to observe that his uncle Bartholomew had been arrested for debt no fewer than seven times before, and had always emerged with a cheerfulness that argued a constitution of iron; but Mrs. Gudge was not to be interrupted.

“I knew,” she continued, “that you would be the first to help, for you have always been a good boy, Octavius, and I have no doubt you have saved a little something out of your salary, and forty pounds would be nothing to you, and everything to him.”

Mr. Pinchbeck, with a sinking heart, was endeavouring to frame a denial, when the door opened again, and there entered Mr. Adolphus Pinchbeck, with his flute under his arm and a cough that shook the window-panes.

“Cousin Octavius,” said Mr. Adolphus, “I am come to you in my extremity. The physician—a most eminent man, I assure you—has ordered me to the South of France, and my passage-money is forty pounds, and I have not a penny. I am a broken man—my chest, my chest!” and he coughed again, and looked at Mr. Pinchbeck with the air of a martyr.

Before Mr. Pinchbeck could reply, the door—which seemed to have acquired a fatal facility of opening—admitted Miss Amelia Pinchbeck, who rushed in with a little scream, and threw herself upon the sofa.

“Oh, Octavius!” she cried, “I am the most miserable of women! The velvet—the flowers—the frame—all is lost! My landlord demands forty pounds for rent, and if I cannot pay it, he will seize my paintings, and my heart is broken!”

“And I,” said a voice at the door, “am ruined entirely!” It was Mr. Bartholomew Pinchbeck himself, who had escaped his arrest by some miracle known only to himself, and who now stood upon the threshold, beaming with an injured innocence that was quite affecting. “I have been robbed—robbed, I say—of forty pounds by a villain in whom I trusted, and unless I can replace it, my credit is gone for ever!”

“And my stay-laces,” whimpered Mrs. Wix, who had followed him in, “are all out of fashion, and I have no money to buy new stock, and forty pounds would set me up again, and I am sure Octavius has it, for I always said he was the lucky one of the family.”

They all stood around him—the whole five of them—like a pack of hounds around a hare, and each repeated the sum of forty pounds with a unanimity that was frightful. Mr. Pinchbeck looked from one to the other; he thought of his canvas bag beneath the pillow; he thought of the new hat; he thought of the box at the theatre; and then he thought of the Fleet, and the South of France, and the velvet paintings, and the stay-laces, and the villain in whom his uncle had trusted.

“My dear relations,” said Mr. Pinchbeck, with a ghastly smile, “I have indeed been fortunate—most fortunate—and I have exactly forty pounds, which I shall be most happy to divide among you.”

He said this with the air of a man who has just swallowed a pill of extraordinary magnitude; and then he drew forth the canvas bag, and counted out the sovereigns—eight to each—and they all embraced him, and wept over him, and declared that he was the best of men; and then they departed, each with his or her forty pounds in hand, and left Mr. Pinchbeck sitting alone in his chamber, with the empty bag upon his knee, and a countenance of such profound and touching resignation that the very cat upon the hearth (if she was indeed in the pay of his relations) must have been moved to pity.

And the next morning, Mr. Pinchbeck went to his desk as usual, and the porters at the wharf saluted him as “the young man as comes and goes,” and he was, I believe, the only man in the City of London who had ever won forty pounds in a lottery and was the poorer for it.
