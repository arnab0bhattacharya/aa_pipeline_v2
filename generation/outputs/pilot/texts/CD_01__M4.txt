The shop of Mr. Abel Crummles stood in the shadow of a narrow street off Holborn—a street so pinched and crooked that the sun, in its most generous moods, could only visit it in patches, and the fog, in its least generous, took up its residence there for days together, like a lodger who has no intention of leaving. It was a pawnbroker's, though the three brass balls had long since been taken down, and the windows were shuttered so close that no curious eye could penetrate the gloom within; and above the shop, in two rooms that smelled of dust and old ledgers, lived Mr. Crummles himself, a retired man of seventy years, who had not spoken a civil word to a soul in thirty of them.

It was to this establishment that I, Thomas Wren, aged ten, was conveyed upon a raw November afternoon by a solicitor's clerk who had no more notion of my destination than I had myself. My mother was dead; my father had gone to the devil by way of the West Indies; and the only relation remaining to me in the world was this grandfather, whom I had never seen, and of whom I knew nothing, save that he had once been a pawnbroker and was now—so the clerk informed me with a sniff—"a rum old file, and no mistake."

The clerk knocked thrice upon the door and departed, and I stood upon the step in the rain, a small boy with a bundle, listening to the slow approach of footsteps within. The door opened a crack, and an eye appeared—a single eye, grey and wary, that surveyed me as a jeweller surveys a doubtful stone.

"Well?" said a voice, dry as an old leaf.

"I am Thomas Wren," said I, "your grandson, sir, and I have come to live with you."

The eye regarded me for a long moment. Then the door opened wider, and I saw a tall old man, bent at the shoulders, in a snuff-coloured coat and a wig that had seen better days—and behind him, in the dim shop, a sight that I have never forgotten: shelves upon shelves of pledges, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string, each bearing a tag with a name and date, and each, as I was afterward to learn, untouched for thirty years.

"Come in, then," said Mr. Crummles. "Wipe your feet. I'll have no dirt tracked in."

I wiped my feet, and entered.

The shop was a cavern of curiosities. There were watches by the dozen, hanging from hooks like so many metallic bats; there were rings and brooches and snuff-boxes in glass cases thick with dust; there were suits of clothes, folded and labelled, that had once belonged to gentlemen who had fallen upon hard times; and there were musical instruments, and china figures, and a stuffed parrot that regarded me with a glassy and malevolent stare. But it was the brown-paper parcels that drew my eye—for they were everywhere, stacked upon the shelves from floor to ceiling, and each one, I noticed, bore a date that was thirty years old or more.

"Grandfather," said I, "why do you never sell these things?"

Mr. Crummles turned upon me a look of such sudden and unexpected ferocity that I stepped back a pace.

"Who told you to ask that?" he demanded.

"Nobody, sir. I only wondered—"

"Wondered!" cried the old man, with a bitter laugh. "Wondering is the beginning of meddling, and meddling is the beginning of ruin. You'll keep your wonders to yourself in this house, young man, or you'll find yourself out in the street again, and no mistake about it."

And with that he showed me to a small room at the back of the shop, containing a bed, a chair, and a washing-stand, and left me to my own devices.

I was not a boy to be discouraged by a single rebuff, however; and as the days passed, I set myself to the task of winning my grandfather's affection with all the patience and ingenuity I possessed. I swept the shop without being asked; I dusted the glass cases; I learned to make his tea exactly as he liked it—strong, with a dash of rum, and no sugar. I asked him no more questions about the parcels, though I thought of them constantly; and I observed him closely, as a naturalist observes a strange specimen, noting the way he would pause before a certain shelf and stand there, motionless, for minutes together, his lips moving as if in conversation with some invisible companion.

For thirty years, I learned, he had kept every pledge that had ever come into his hands. He had never sold a single one. He had never returned one, either, though many had been redeemed; and those that were not redeemed he had simply—kept. The watches, the rings, the suits of clothes, the stuffed parrot—all had been left with him, and all had been kept, and the tags upon them bore the names of men and women long since dead or vanished.

"Why?" I asked him at last, when I had been with him three months, and had earned, I thought, the right to a question. "Why do you keep them, sir? They are worth money. You could sell them."

Mr. Crummles was silent for a long time. Then he said, in a voice quite different from his usual dry croak—a voice that trembled slightly, like a candle-flame in a draught:

"Because they might come back."

"Come back, sir?"

"The people," said he. "The people who left them. They might come back for them. And if I sold them, what should I say? What should I say to a man who came to redeem his father's watch, and found it gone?" He shook his head slowly. "No, no. I keep them. I keep them all. It is a foolishness, I dare say. But I keep them."

I looked at the shelves, at the brown-paper parcels with their faded tags, and I understood, in that moment, something of the old man's heart—the heart of a pawnbroker who had seen too much of the world's cruelty, and who had resolved, in his own small way, to be faithful to a trust that no one else remembered.

"Grandfather," said I, "I will help you keep them. I will help you keep them all."

The old man looked at me for a long moment, and then—for the first time since I had known him—he smiled. It was a small smile, and a crooked one, and it sat strangely upon his wrinkled face, like a new coat upon an old man's back; but it was a smile, and it was for me.

"Thomas," said he, "you are a good boy. I did not think to have a good boy in this house again."

And from that day forward, we kept the shop together—he and I—and the pledges upon the shelves, and the secret that had lain in the dust for thirty years; and though no one ever came back for them, we kept them still, and I believe we shall keep them to the end.
