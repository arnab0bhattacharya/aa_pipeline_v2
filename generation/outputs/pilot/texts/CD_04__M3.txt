If any mortal man, in that autumn of the year eighteen hundred and fifty-something—for the exact date is of no consequence to the moral of the matter—had been told that the Borough of Muddlecombe was not the very crown and glory of the Midland counties, he would have demanded satisfaction, or at the very least, a very public apology. But of all the glories of Muddlecombe, its indisputable pivot, its sun, its moon, and its principal constellation, was Alderman Bumbleton; a gentleman who carried his corporation upon his stomach, and his sense of civic pride in his voice, which was a deep, foggy, and melodious instrument, admirably suited to the proposing of toasts and the crushing of opposition.

It was upon a raw and drizzling morning that the said alderman stood in the grand, or rather the intended grand, apartment of the New Town Hall. The building had been erected at vast public expense, to celebrate which, and to dedicate the edifice to the palate and the pocket of the ratepayer, a grand banquet was to be held that very day. The hall was certainly capacious; but its capaciousness was of a sort that admitted not only the tables and chairs, but the entire sky. The roof—which was to have been a miracle of hammer-beam and slate—was not there. It had been expected. It had been paid for. But it was absent. The contractor, a gentleman of fluid capital and nomadic habits, had departed for the Continent in the dead of night, carrying with him the funds, the plans, and, indeed, the very moral obligation to cover the town’s head.

A great gap, a jagged and irregular aperture, yawned overhead, through which the clouds were slowly and damply assembling, like a congregation of wet blankets. Workmen’s ladders leaned against the bare walls like skeletons leaning against the ramparts of a fallen castle.

Alderman Bumbleton surveyed the void. His civic chain, a massive golden collar, lay heavy upon his chest, but not so heavy as the weight of his pride. To admit a fault was, in the alderman’s philosophy, a confession of weakness; and to confess weakness was to abdicate the throne of respectability.

"Mr. Grubble," said the Alderman to his clerk, a thin, pale man with a cough that sounded like the rustling of dry leaves.

"Your Worship?" said Mr. Grubble, with a shiver that was partly physical and partly professional.

"The—ah—the architectural effect of this ceiling," said the Alderman, waving a plump hand towards the clouds, "is striking. Striking, sir. It conveys a sense of—of amplitude."

"It conveys a draught, your Worship," said Grubble, venturing a polite but miserable whisper.

"A draught, sir?" thundered the Alderman, his voice echoing off the unfinished brickwork. "A draught is a thing of health! It is the breath of the atmosphere, sir! It is the very essence of ventilation! And ventilation is the boast of the modern age!"

Mr. Grubble said no more. He had a wife and four small children, and his courage, never a robust plant, had withered under the aldermanic eye.

The guests began to arrive. They came in carriages and in gigs, in cloaks and in greatcoats, for the rain had now begun to fall with a steady, determined, and altogether unfunny perseverance. They were the elite of Muddlecombe: the Mayor, the Magistrates, the Solicitors, the Doctors, and those neutral gentlemen of no particular profession but considerable appetite, who are to be found in every market town, clustered about the inns and the auction-rooms, like flies about a sugar-barrel.

They entered the hall, and they looked up. The rain fell upon their hats. A drop fell upon the nose of the Mayor, who was a nervous man with a watery eye, and he started as if he had been struck by a fairy.

"Bless my soul!" said the Mayor. "It is—it is precipitating!"

"It is a feature, sir," said Alderman Bumbleton, advancing with a beaming, rubicund countenance, upon which the rain was now falling as freely as upon a turnip in a field. "A feature of the design! An open-air amphitheatre, sir! A tribute to the ancients! The Greeks, sir, the Romans! Did they shelter themselves from the heavens? No, sir! They rejoiced in the dew of the gods! Besides," he added, dropping his voice to a confidential roar, "the—ah—the contractor has been called away on urgent business. A sudden indisposition of his mother-in-law, I understand. The roof will follow."

They sat down to table. The tables were long, and white, and damp. The cloth was wet, as if it had been drawn through a pond. The soup was cold. The fish was cold. The meat was cold. But the guests, being Englishmen, and being furthermore accustomed to the consumption of cold meats at picnics, bore it with a fortitude that was only slightly undermined by the occasional sneeze.

The waiters, in a frantic endeavor to preserve some semblance of dryness, had erected a series of umbrellas, of various colors and conditions, over the central table. This gave to the banquet the appearance of a bazaar, or a rag-fair, or a congregation of distressed mushrooms.

Alderman Bumbleton, seated at the center, beneath a gingham parasol of extraordinary dimensions, which he had borrowed from his landlady, beamed upon the assembly. The rain ran down his cheeks and collected in the creases of his chin, like tears in the folds of a benevolent stone image. He tapped his glass. The tinkling sound was somewhat deadened by the pattering of the rain upon the tables and the cloth, and upon the hats of the guests, and upon the soup.

"Gentlemen!" said the Alderman. "I give you the New Town Hall!"

"Hear, hear!" said the guests, with a modified enthusiasm, and a simultaneous reaching for their pocket-handkerchiefs.

"A structure, sir," continued the Alderman, addressing himself to the Mayor, who was mopping his brow with a napkin already saturated, "that will stand for centuries! A monument to the enterprise, the public spirit, and the—ah—the broad and comprehensive views of the Borough of Muddlecombe!"

"Very broad," muttered a solicitor from under a dripping umbrella. "As broad as the sky."

"The roof," pursued the Alderman, with a grand disregard of the element that was even then soaking his shirt-collar, "is a triumph of engineering. It is not yet visible to the naked eye, sir, because it is of a—a—soaring nature. It is in transit. It is coming. It will arrive. And when it does, sir, it will cover us with glory!"

"It covers us with water now," said the same solicitor, who was a man of some local reputation for plain speaking, and a considerable quantity of port.

"That, sir," said Alderman Bumbleton, turning upon him with a look that would have withered a less seasoned legal gentleman, "is the dew of prosperity! It is—ah—it is the beneficent influence of the atmosphere upon our municipal institutions! It is, if I may say so, the very rain of heaven, which falls alike upon the just and the unjust, but which, today, falls with a peculiar and distinguishing favor upon the just, who are here assembled!"

A ragged cheer arose, somewhat dampened by the simultaneous sneezing of the Mayor, who had now begun to show symptoms of a violent cold in the head.

The courses followed one another with the slow and dismal regularity of a funeral procession. The venison was sodden. The pastry was flabby. The jelly melted before it reached the mouth. The wine was diluted, not by the dishonest vintner, but by the impartial clouds. The very candles, guttering and flaring in the draught, seemed to weep, and to shed tears of tallow upon the cloth.

And yet, through it all, Alderman Bumbleton sat erect, and smiled, and ate, and drank, and talked. He talked of the future of Muddlecombe. He talked of the docks, the railways, the gas-works. He talked of the destiny of the Anglo-Saxon race. He talked of anything and everything but the roof. He did not look up. He did not allow his eye to wander to that melancholy void, through which the rain continued to descend with a monotonous and relentless industry, as if it had been hired by the hour to perform that particular function.

The band, which had been engaged at great expense, and which was stationed in a gallery that was also unfinished, and open to the sky, played "Rule Britannia" and "God Save the Queen," with a vigor that was only slightly impaired by the fact that the wind blew through their instruments, and the rain wet their music, and the trombone-player was observed to be weeping, though whether from patriotic emotion or from personal discomfort, it was impossible to determine.

The toasts were drunk. The speeches were made. The Mayor, sneezing violently, proposed the health of Alderman Bumbleton, the father of the New Town Hall. The Alderman, in replying, rose to the highest pitch of his rhetoric. He stood, a colossal figure, wet but unsubdued. The rain fell upon his bare head, for he had removed his hat in deference to the occasion, and it ran down his face and his neck and his corporation, and it even penetrated to the very marrow of his boots.

"Gentlemen," he said, "I thank you. I have labored for this town. I have spent my days and my nights in its service. I have erected this hall. It may not be—ah—quite finished. There may be a—a slight deficiency in the matter of the roof. But, gentlemen, what is a roof? What is any roof, but a—a concession to human weakness? A roof is a shelter from the storm. But we, gentlemen, do not fear the storm! We are Muddlecombe men! We are Midland men! We are Englishmen! And an Englishman, sir, is not to be deterred by a little—a little aqueous interruption, from the performance of his duty, or the enjoyment of his dinner!"

He sat down amidst a thunder of applause, which was mingled with the thunder of the clouds, which had now begun to rumble in a most unpromising and aldermanic manner.

The banquet broke up. The guests dispersed, dripping and discontented, to their homes. Alderman Bumbleton remained to the last. He stood alone in the midst of the ruined feast, the wet cloths, the sodden fragments, the guttering candles, and the open sky. He looked up at last. The rain fell full upon his face. He drew a long breath. He buttoned his coat. And then, with a slow and stately step, he marched out of the New Town Hall, carrying his corporation, his dignity, and his wet boots, into the gloom of the autumn evening, a monument to the unyielding nature of civic pride.
