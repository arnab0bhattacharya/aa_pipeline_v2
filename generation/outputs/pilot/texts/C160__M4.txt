The rain had not stopped for forty-one days. It fell in a grey, relentless sheet that turned the Thames into a swollen brown artery and the streets of London into canals of silt and refuse. From the high windows of the Ashworth townhouse in Belgravia, the city looked like a watercolour left out in a storm, its edges bleeding and its colours running to a uniform, despairing murk.

Leo Ashworth, Viscount Penryn, stood with his forehead pressed to the cold glass, watching a scrum of people fighting over a crate of something—cabbages, perhaps, or the last of the tinned goods from a looted depot—until the police drones buzzed down, their rotors whipping the rain into a frenzy. He felt a familiar, profound uselessness. He was twenty-eight years old, with a fortune built on centuries of careful marriages and industrial speculation, and a seat in the House of Lords that he had never once occupied. Politics, he had always believed, was a grubby business for men with either too much ambition or too little imagination. He preferred the clean lines of his architectural drawings, the silent order of a well-proportioned room.

His father, the Earl, had been a different breed. A man of thunderous certainties and a handshake that could crack walnuts, he had died two winters ago, leaving Leo with a title he didn't want and a legacy of public service he had no intention of fulfilling. The family’s ancient motto, *Servire, non serviri*—to serve, not to be served—was embossed on a brass plate in the study, and Leo walked past it every day with a quiet sneer.

The knock on the door was timid, almost lost in the drumming of the rain. Leo ignored it. Mrs. Gable, the housekeeper, would see to it. But the knocking came again, more insistent. Then the door to his study opened without a knock at all, and Mrs. Gable stood there, her face the colour of old parchment.

“My lord,” she said, her voice trembling. “It’s… it’s the people. From the market. They’re at the door.”

Leo turned, frowning. “The market? Tell them to go to the council. Or the police. There are relief centres.”

“They won’t go, my lord. They’re asking for you.”

He went to the window. A crowd of perhaps fifty had gathered on the steps below, a ragged collection of the soaked and the starving. They weren't shouting. They were just standing, a silent, sodden mass, their faces upturned to the house. One man, a gaunt figure in a sodden wool coat, held a placard that was little more than a piece of cardboard with two words scrawled in charcoal: *ASHWORTH. SPEAK.*

A cold dread, quite unlike the chill of the weather, settled in Leo’s stomach. He had done nothing to earn this. His father had been the one they remembered, the one who had walked among them during the floods of ‘78, who had fought for the Thames Barrier’s expansion in the Upper House. They were not calling for Leo. They were calling for a ghost.

For three days, the crowd outside his door grew. It was peaceful, but its silence was more damning than any riot. The news feeds, sensing a narrative, began to circle. They called it the “Siege of Belgravia,” a bizarre spectacle of aristocratic privilege under pressure. Leo’s isolation became a prison. He stopped looking out the windows. He stopped eating. He just sat in his study, staring at the brass motto, the words *Servire, non serviri* burning into his mind.

On the fourth day, a man was allowed through the police cordon. He was old, with the weathered face of a farmer and eyes the colour of the North Sea. His name was Silas Rooke, and he was the unofficial leader of the market traders, a man who had lost his entire harvest in the great storm that had flattened the eastern counties.

“They’re not here to hurt you, Lord Penryn,” Rooke said, accepting a cup of tea with hands that shook. “They’re here because they’re scared. The government tells us it’s a temporary disruption. They talk about ‘supply chain realignment’ and ‘strategic grain reserves.’ But we see the empty shelves. We see the soldiers at the power stations. They’re lying, my lord. And the people, they remember your father. They remember a time when a man with your name stood up and said what needed to be said.”

“I am not my father,” Leo said, his voice flat.

“No,” Rooke agreed, looking at him with a gaze that was both pitying and piercing. “But you’re what we’ve got. The Emergency Powers Act goes before Parliament tomorrow. It gives the Prime Minister’s office total control over food distribution, fuel, and… God knows what else. It’s the end of everything we know. We need a voice in that chamber. A voice that isn’t one of them. You have a seat. You have a name. Use it.”

That night, Leo did not sleep. He paced the length of his study, the ghost of his father’s disapproval a palpable presence in the room. He thought of his drawings, of the elegant, useless buildings he had designed for a world that was now drowning. He thought of the silent, rain-soaked crowd. He thought of Silas Rooke’s eyes. He was a man who had spent his life avoiding the messy, chaotic, infuriating business of other people. And now, other people were demanding he become their champion.

He was terrified.

The next morning, he called for his father’s old barber to shave him. He put on a suit that had not been worn since the funeral. It felt like a costume. He emerged from the townhouse to a gasp from the crowd. He didn't speak. He simply walked through them, flanked by two nervous police officers, to the waiting car. He saw the hope in their eyes, and it felt like an accusation.

The Palace of Westminster was a Gothic mausoleum. The air inside was thick with stale rhetoric and the hum of the environmental scrubbers. The Lords’ chamber was sparsely attended. The Emergency Powers Act was a foregone conclusion. The government benches were full, their faces smug and certain. The opposition was a scattering of the confused and the impotent. Leo took his seat on the crossbenches, a forgotten island in a sea of indifference.

He listened for hours as the debate droned on. He heard the Home Secretary speak of “unprecedented challenges” and the need for “decisive, centralised action.” He heard the opposition leader offer amendments that were politely, brutally voted down. It was a theatre of the absurd, a pantomime of governance while the country starved outside. The Lord Chancellor, a sleek, well-fed man named Pettigrew, was winding up the debate for the government. He was concluding his speech, praising the “wisdom of the executive” and the “necessary, if regrettable, suspension of certain liberties.”

Leo felt a tremor in his hands. The moment was passing. He could slip out, go home, and let the world burn. It was what he had always done.

But then he saw Silas Rooke’s face in his mind, and the silent, sodden crowd. He saw the empty shelves and the soldiers at the power stations. He saw the brass plate in his study.

He stood up.

The chamber fell silent. Every head turned. The Lord Chancellor paused mid-sentence, a flicker of annoyance on his face. The procedural clerk looked flustered, unsure of the protocol for a silent peer who had never spoken.

“My lords,” Leo began, his voice hoarse and unfamiliar in the vast space. It cracked. He cleared his throat. “I do not come to this house as a politician. I have no party, no faction, and no ambition. I am, as many of you know, a man who has been absent. A man who believed that the affairs of state were a matter for other, more capable, hands.”

He paused, his heart hammering against his ribs. He could see Pettigrew’s condescending smile.

“But I have spent the last four days looking out of my window,” Leo continued, his voice gaining strength. “I have seen a city I love brought to its knees. Not by a foreign enemy, not by a terrorist, but by rain. By a storm. And I have seen a people, my people, driven to the gates of a man they have no reason to trust, because the institutions that are meant to protect them have failed. They have failed with their jargon, with their ‘strategic realignments,’ with their talk of ‘temporary disruptions’ while the truth is that they are terrified. They are hungry. And they are being asked to surrender their freedom to the very people who have proved themselves incapable of the simple act of feeding them.”

A murmur rippled through the chamber. Pettigrew’s smile had vanished.

“This Act,” Leo said, gesturing to the bill, “is not a solution. It is an abdication. It is a declaration that our system is broken, and that we must place our faith in a single, unaccountable office. My father, who sat in this house before me, believed that the strength of this nation was not in its Prime Minister, but in its people. He believed in the messy, difficult, infuriating process of debate and dissent. He believed in service.”

He looked around the chamber, at the empty benches, at the uncomfortable faces.

“I do not know if we can feed everyone tomorrow. I do not have a grand plan. But I know that the answer is not to be found in giving one man, or one office, the power to decide who eats and who starves. The answer is to be found in trusting the people who have lost everything to help rebuild. In the market traders who know the supply lines better than any minister. In the farmers who are already clearing their flooded fields. In the communities that are already organising themselves because the state has abandoned them.”

He took a breath, the words coming faster now, fuelled by a conviction he hadn't known he possessed.

“I will not vote for this Act. And I beg of you, my lords, not to vote for it either. Not for the sake of the government, or the opposition, but for the sake of the man who stood outside my door in the rain, holding a piece of cardboard with my name on it. He is not asking for a saviour. He is asking for a voice. Let us give it to him. Let us be that voice. Let us serve.”

He sat down. The silence that followed was profound, broken only by the drumming of rain against the great windows. It was the same rain, but it sounded different now. It sounded like an opportunity.
