If any honest traveller, in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and thirty-something, had found himself wandering down the cramped and crooked thoroughfare of Grimbal's Passage, near unto Holborn, he might have supposed that the narrow street had been built by some misanthropic architect expressly for the purpose of being avoided by the cheerful sun. It was a place where the very houses seemed to have stooped their shoulders against the weather, leaning across the slippery cobblestones to whisper miserable secrets into one another's damp and crumbling ears.

At the very bottom of this gloomy ravine, pressed into the darkest corner like a spider hiding in the recesses of a cracked teacup, stood the shut-up premises of Mr. Ebenezer Grittle. The shutters were of the heaviest, knottiest, and most uncompromising oak, bolted and barred with a severity that suggested Mr. Grittle expected the entire population of London to make a concerted assault upon his front window before teatime. Above this fortress, in a set of chambers that smelt pervasively of cold dust, stale tallow, and the slow decay of forgotten things, dwelt the old man himself; and it was to this uninviting sanctuary that Master Timothy Trundle, a small boy of exactly ten years of age, was abruptly and irrevocably dispatched.

Timothy had known neither father nor mother for very long, they having departed this mortal coil with a haste that left him chiefly in the care of parish beadle, and ultimately, by some obscure genealogical twist, at the mercy of his maternal grandfather. Timothy was a small child, possessing a pair of extraordinarily large, wistful eyes, which seemed perpetually engaged in trying to understand a world that went to a great deal of trouble to be misunderstood. He stood upon the slimy threshold of Grimbal's Passage, clutching a single and somewhat battered leather trunk, and looking up at the iron-knocker upon Mr. Grittle's door as if it were the jawbone of some fabulous beast prepared to swallow him whole.

The door was opened eventually, and with a prodigious grinding of rusty hinges, by Mr. Grittle himself. He was a man of a very shrivelled and dusty aspect, possessing a nose that seemed to have been sharpened expressly for the purpose of poking itself into other people's affairs, and a pair of small, red-rimmed eyes that twinkled with a hard, metallic distrust, like the reflection of a farthing candle in a brass saucepan. He wore a waistcoat that had once been of a flowery pattern, but which age and usage had reduced to a dim and ghostly landscape, and he carried his chin so tucked into his cravat that he appeared to be perpetually attempting to swallow his own collar.

"Umph!" said Mr. Grittle, surveying the boy as if he were an unwanted parcel of damaged goods. "So you are the boy."

"Yes, Grandfather," replied Timothy, trembling slightly.

"Don't 'Grandfather' me, till you've earned the right to the word," snapped the old man, with a suspicious hitch of his sharp shoulders. "Blood is thin, boy, very thin; and water is thicker, and a good sight cheaper. Come in, come in! Don't let the damp air get into the house; I can't afford to heat the atmosphere of all Holborn!"

Timothy stepped into the gloomy hall, and the door clapped-to behind him with a sound of final and dismal conviction. The interior of the dwelling exactly matched its outward promise. It was a cavern of shadows, crowded with the ghosts of departed commerce. Old garments dangled from pegs like the flayed skins of ancient martyrs, and stacks of obsolete brass instruments seemed to have grown out of the wainscoting, spreading their tarnished mouths in silent protest against the neglect of their owner.

"Mind your step, mind your step!" cried Mr. Grittle, leading the way up a staircase that groaned at every footfall as if lamenting the burden of humanity it was forced to bear. "I have no money to repair broken shins, and less patience to hear them whined about. You'll sleep in the back room. You'll earn your keep, or out you go upon the stones, where the parish may have the hauling of you!"

"I will work very hard, sir," said Timothy, earnestly.

"Work, umph! Everybody says they will work," muttered the old man, unlocking a door at the top of the stairs and revealing a sitting-room that was almost as forbidding as the shop below. "Talk is the cheapest thing in London, cheaper even than the lies that are told in the pawnbrokers' shops. Sit down, and don't fidget! Fidgeting wears out the furniture, and furniture costs money."

The boy sat down upon the very edge of a horsehair chair, which crackled under him with a threatening and reproachful sound. He cast his large eyes about the room, and observed, pinned above the mantelpiece, a small, faded silhouette of a young woman, drawn in profile. The nose was rather sharp, and the chin rather combative, but there was a certain softness about the setting of the head which somehow contradicted the severity of the features.

Mr. Grittle, catching the direction of the boy's gaze, snorted violently, marched to the mantelpiece, and turned the silhouette with its face to the wall. "Eyes off!" he barked. "That's no concern of yours! The dead are dead, and the living must look to their own pennies. Now, you are to fetch the water, sweep the floors, black the grate, and mind that you never, upon any pretext whatsoever, set foot in the shop below. Do you hear me?"

"Yes, sir. But, if you please—"

"But me no buts, boy! The shop is locked. The shop stays locked. The pledges are mine, bought and paid for, and I keep what is mine!" cried the old man, working himself into a sudden lather of indignation. "What is pledged to Grittle, stays with Grittle! Thirty years I've kept 'em, and thirty years more I'll keep 'em, if I live to be a hundred, which I don't intend to, just to spite the doctors!"

This was a puzzle that sat very heavily upon Timothy's young mind. He knew, from the general gossip of the parish authorities, that his grandfather had been a pawnbroker of some repute, but that he had shut up his business three decades ago, retaining every single article that had been left in pledge. The lower shop was a great, shuttered tomb of unredeemed humanity, filled with the sad belongings of people who had long since vanished into the poorer gutters of London.

In the days that followed, Timothy applied himself to his tasks with a desperate diligence. He blacked the grate until it shone like a mirror reflecting a coal-mine; he swept the floors until the very broom seemed to have acquired a nervous exhaustion; and he fetched the water from the street pump with a regularity that would have done credit to a mechanical automaton. All the while, Mr. Grittle sat in his armchair, watching the boy with his hard, red eyes, and demanding to know how much the coals cost, and why the candle was burning so high, and whether Timothy thought he was made of money.

"Everything costs, boy, everything!" the old man would croak, rubbing his knobby hands together. "Affection costs! Trust costs! And when you give 'em out, they never come back. They go into the dark, like the things in my shop, and they are never redeemed!"

But Timothy, being a boy of a persevering and affectionate nature, refused to be chilled by this perpetual frost. He began to leave little wildflowers—he had found a few stubborn dandelions pushing through the stones of a nearby courtyard—upon the old man's breakfast tray. He mended Mr. Grittle's slippers with a needle and thread borrowed from a neighboring charwoman, and he learned to anticipate the old man's querulous demands before they had fully formed upon his peevish lips.

One evening, when the rain was beating against the windows with a melancholy rattle, Timothy found his grandfather sitting before a meagre fire, holding the faded silhouette in his hands. The old man's face was not hard and metallic now, but deeply lined and unutterably weary. He looked up, and seeing the boy standing in the doorway, did not immediately bid him begone.

"She was my daughter," said Mr. Grittle, in a voice that seemed to have lost its accustomed sharpness, and to have crumbled into dust at the corners. "Your mother, boy. She ran away from me. Ran away, and married a feckless, penniless scamp of a Trundle, and left me alone with my ledgers and my pledges."

Timothy did not speak, but drew nearer, laying a small, grubby hand upon the old man's sleeve.

"I shut up the shop," Mr. Grittle continued, glaring into the fire as if it were the gateway to a haunted past. "I shut it up, the very week she left. I wouldn't redeem anything. I wouldn't let anything go. Every ring, and every watch, and every poor shawl that was left in my keeping, I kept! I clutched 'em to me! They were pledges of human trust, and I'd been robbed of mine, so I robbed the world of its belongings! I would hold onto what was left, and let the world come begging to my door, as I had to beg for her to come back!"

He paused, and his breath caught in his throat. "She never came back," he whispered. "Not till she was dead, and they brought her boy to my door."

Timothy squeezed the thin arm. "I am here, Grandfather," he said, softly.

The old man looked down at the boy, and for the first time, something in the hard, red eyes seemed to melt, like a long-frozen icicle dropping into a puddle of warm rain. "You are here," he repeated, slowly. "Umph! And I suppose you'll want your supper. It's in the cupboard. Don't waste the bread, boy!"

But Timothy, knowing the old man's peculiar grammar of affection, recognized the tender concession in the gruff command, and went to fetch his supper, feeling that the great, shuttered tomb below was, perhaps, not quite so hopeless a place as it had been the day before.
