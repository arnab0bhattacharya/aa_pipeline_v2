Mr. Alderman Pumblechook—for so he was universally styled, though the christening register had it Pumblechook *cum* Puddingcome, his mother having married twice and being a woman of an economical turn that sought to bury the one name decently within the other—Mr. Alderman Pumblechook rose from his bed on the morning of the great banquet with the sun upon his counterpane and a psalm of thanksgiving upon his lips. The day was come. The new Town Hall of Cinderbury—the pride of the Midlands, the wonder of the age, the edifice that was to have a clock in its tower and a fountain before its door and a cornice that should make the neighbouring boroughs bite their nails with envy—was to be opened that very afternoon with a public dinner of one hundred and forty covers, and Mr. Alderman Pumblechook was to preside.

He shaved himself with a hand that trembled with anticipated glory. He rehearsed his speech before the looking-glass, addressing the soap-dish as the assembled company and the shaving-brush as the Mayor of Thrapston, who was known to be coming with a deputation and an appetite. He was in the very middle of a rolling period concerning the commercial greatness of Cinderbury and the imperishable fame of its founders, when there came a knocking at his door so hurried, so peremptory, so entirely out of keeping with the solemnity of the occasion, that he cut himself upon the chin and answered in a voice of thunder.

The knocker was his clerk, one Nathaniel Winks, a small man with a large conscience, who stood upon the mat in a condition of agitation that had disordered his neckcloth and his grammar together. "Sir," says he, "the roof—"

"The roof, you jackanapes?"

"The roof, sir, is not on."

Mr. Alderman Pumblechook laid down his razor with the gravity of a man laying down the seals of office. "Not on," says he. "Not on? What do you mean, sir, that the roof is not on? Roofs, sir, are on. It is the nature of a roof to be on. It is the office, the function, the very definition of a roof to be on. If it were not on, sir, it would be a floor."

Mr. Winks, who had a great respect for his master and a greater for the truth, submitted, with the humility of one who knows that the facts are against him, that the contractor, Mr. Bartholomew Gudge, had been seen at the railway station at six of the clock that morning with a carpet-bag and the funds of the Building Committee, and that the roof of the new Town Hall consisted at present of a quantity of scaffolding, a quantity of rain, and a quantity of sky, in about equal proportions.

Mr. Alderman Pumblechook sat down upon the edge of his bed, and for the space of a minute and a half was silent. Then he arose, and putting on his coat with a slow and awful dignity, observed that the banquet would proceed.

"Proceed, sir!" cried Mr. Winks. "With the roof off, and the rain coming down in—in cataracts, sir, and the Mayor of Thrapston coming with a deputation!"

"The banquet," repeated Mr. Alderman Pumblechook, "will proceed. The roof, sir, is a detail. The roof, sir, is a matter for the Building Committee, and the Building Committee is—is otherwise engaged. The dignity of Cinderbury, sir, is not to be measured by slates and plaster. The dignity of Cinderbury, sir, is imperishable. Let the banquet proceed."

Now the new Town Hall of Cinderbury stood in the market-place, and was a building of the Corinthian order, or of an order so nearly approaching the Corinthian that the difference was not to be discerned by the naked eye of Cinderbury. Its portico was supported by six columns of a whiteness that had cost the committee dear, and its great hall was capable of seating one hundred and forty persons at a long table, with the chairman at the head of it, and the Mayor of Thrapston at his right hand. And it was into this great hall, at the hour appointed, that the company came, shaking the rain from their hats and their umbrellas, and looking about them with an air of wonder that was not entirely admiration.

For the rain, which had begun at daybreak, had by four of the clock settled into a steady, methodical, uncompromising downpour, such as only the Midlands can produce, and it fell through the unfinished roof of the great hall in a manner that suggested the deliberate malice of Providence. It fell upon the long table in a hundred little pattering streams. It fell upon the napery, and the napery, being of a quality that had been warranted to the committee as damask, revealed itself in its true character as a species of painted canvas that ran like a water-colour. It fell upon the great chandelier, and the great chandelier, being of a substance that was not precisely ormolu, gave out a sound as of a hundred small kettles. It fell, in fine, upon the assembled company, and the assembled company, being of the commercial and municipal classes, and having paid for their tickets, bore it with the fortitude of men who intend to have their money's worth or to know the reason why.

Mr. Alderman Pumblechook took his seat at the head of the table with the rain upon his bald head and the Mayor of Thrapston upon his right hand. The Mayor of Thrapston, a man of a dry and waggish turn, observed that the ventilation was excellent, and that he had never seen a room so open to conviction. Mr. Alderman Pumblechook replied, with a smile of ineffable sweetness, that the committee had given particular attention to the ventilation, and that he was gratified to find it appreciated.

The soup was served. It was a soup of a brown and noble complexion, and it was served in plates, and the plates contained, in addition to the soup, a quantity of the rain of Cinderbury, which had found its way through the roof and into the tureen, and which gave to the soup a certain freshness, a certain sparkle, a certain—as the Mayor of Thrapston observed—riverine quality. Mr. Alderman Pumblechook, lifting his spoon, declared that he had never tasted a soup so—so—so suggestive of the element from which it came.

The fish was served, and the rain fell upon the fish. The entrées were served, and the rain fell upon the entrées. The Mayor of Thrapston, whose humour was of a persistent and a penetrating kind, remarked that the banquet was, in a manner of speaking, a baptism, and that he hoped the Town Hall would grow up a good and honest building. Mr. Alderman Pumblechook replied that the Town Hall was already grown up, and that it was the finest Town Hall in the Midlands, and that he would maintain it against all comers.

Then came the moment for the speech. Mr. Alderman Pumblechook rose, with the rain upon his shirt-front and the rain upon his notes, and the rain, having soaked the notes, had reduced them to a pulp, and the speech that he delivered was a speech that he improvised upon the spur of the moment, and it was a speech that he never afterwards forgot. He spoke of the commercial greatness of Cinderbury. He spoke of the imperishable fame of its founders. He spoke of the new Town Hall, which he described as an edifice that would endure for ages, and he did not say that the roof was off. He spoke of the banquet, which he described as a repast that would be remembered for years, and he did not say that the soup had been diluted by the heavens. He spoke of the rain, which he described as a gracious dispensation of Providence, and the Mayor of Thrapston, who was a man of a dry and waggish turn, observed that Providence had been very gracious indeed.

And so the banquet proceeded, and the rain proceeded, and the night came on, and the candles were lit, and the candles, being lit, were immediately extinguished by the rain, and the company sat in a darkness that was rendered more impressive by the occasional flash of lightning. And Mr. Alderman Pumblechook, sitting at the head of the table with the water running off his nose, continued to speak of the commercial greatness of Cinderbury and the imperishable fame of its founders, and the company, who had paid for their tickets, listened with a respect that was not entirely admiration, and the Mayor of Thrapston, who had paid for nothing, enjoyed himself exceedingly.

It was past midnight when the last of the company departed, and Mr. Alderman Pumblechook stood alone in the great hall, with the rain falling upon his bald head and the darkness all about him, and the scaffolding of the unfinished roof looming above him like the ribs of a stranded ship. And as he stood there, he heard a voice, which was the voice of Mr. Nathaniel Winks, saying, "Sir, the Mayor of Thrapston has taken the opportunity to observe that the banquet was the most original he ever attended, and that he would not have missed it for a hundred pounds."

And Mr. Alderman Pumblechook, drawing himself up to his full height, and shaking the rain from his coat-tails, replied, "Winks, the dignity of Cinderbury is imperishable." And with that he walked out into the market-place, and the rain fell upon him, and the clock in the tower of the old church struck one, and the new Town Hall of Cinderbury stood in the darkness with its roof off, and the Mayor of Thrapston went home to Thrapston in a gig, laughing all the way.
