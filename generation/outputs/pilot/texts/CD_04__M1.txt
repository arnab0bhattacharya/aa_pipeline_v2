Alderman Pumbleton was a man built upon the same plan as the new Town Hall of Nether Wallop—broad in the base, imposing in the front, and possessed of a certain hollow grandeur that only became apparent when one tried to find the staircase. On the morning of the Great Banquet, he stood before his cheval glass, adjusting the gold chain of office across his expansive waistcoat, and felt himself to be the very keystone of the civic arch. He was, in his own estimation, the Father of the Town, the Benefactor of the Poor (at a distance), and the Architect of Prosperity. He was also, though he did not yet know it, about to become a very wet, very pompous, and very ridiculous spectacle.

"Mrs. Plimpton!" he bellowed, his voice echoing off the mahogany wardrobe like a gavel striking for order. "My galoshes! The patent leather ones! And where is the boy with my speech? Has he transcribed the section on 'The Sublime Permanence of Masonry'?"

Mrs. Plimpton, a woman whose face was a roadmap of domestic anxieties, scurried in with the galoshes. "He has, sir. But—"

"No 'buts,' Mrs. Plimpton! In the lexicon of civic duty, there are no 'buts,' only 'therefores'! Today, Nether Wallop ascends! Today, we cast off the rustic shroud of the past and don the shining raiment of Modernity!"

Outside, the heavens, which had been threatening a tantrum since daybreak, finally decided to throw their toys out of the pram. The rain did not fall; it descended in solid, gray sheets, as if the sky were a laundry and the world its washing. It drummed upon the roof of the Alderman’s carriage with the frantic, irregular rhythm of a thousand tiny hammers trying to break in.

At the site of the New Town Hall, the scene was one of magnificent, sodden confusion. The building was indeed a marvel of the age—a great, red-brick beast with Corinthian columns that looked like rows of white sugar cubes standing in a puddle. It had a portico that could have sheltered a regiment, and a façade that screamed of permanence. It also, unfortunately, lacked a roof.

The contractor, a man named Mr. Silas Crimp, had, as is the way with such geniuses of industry, perceived a higher calling in the direction of the coast, taking with him the subscription funds, the wages of the bricklayers, and the Mayor’s best umbrella. He had vanished as effectively as a magician’s rabbit, leaving behind nothing but a skeleton of rafters and a note pinned to a girder that read: *“Gone to America. Mind the drip.”*

Alderman Pumbleton arrived to find the Mayor, the Vicar, and the members of the Town Council huddled in the vestibule, looking like a collection of damp, bewildered crows. The rain was pouring through the open roof, splashing onto the newly laid marble floor, turning the grand entrance hall into a shallow, shimmering lake.

"Gentlemen!" Pumbleton boomed, shaking the water from his hat with a gesture that seemed to say he was merely greeting the elements as equals. "A glorious day! A day of moisture, of fertility, of—"

"Alderman Pumbleton," the Mayor interrupted, his teeth chattering in a rhythm that nearly kept time with the rain. "The roof. Look at the roof. Or rather, look at the sky where the roof ought to be. And Crimp! The funds! The money is gone! We are ruined! We shall be the laughingstock of the county!"

Pumbleton drew himself up to his full height, which was considerable. He looked at the dripping rafters; he looked at the Mayor’s terrified face; he looked at the water collecting around the Vicar’s ankles. A lesser man might have confessed. A weaker man might have wept. But Pumbleton was made of sterner, and considerably denser, stuff.

"Ruined?" Pumbleton cried, throwing his arms wide as if to embrace the very rain. "Sir, I see no ruin! I see opportunity! I see a bold, a daring, a *revolutionary* architectural statement! We are not merely building a hall; we are integrating the very Firmament into our design! We are bringing the Outdoors In! It is a triumph of ventilation!"

"It is a triumph of wet feet," muttered the Vicar.

"Nonsense!" Pumbleton roared, seizing the Mayor by the lapels. "We shall proceed! The Banquet will go on! We shall dine beneath the canopy of Heaven itself! It will be spoken of for generations! Pumbleton’s Picnic! The Alderman’s Al Fresco!"

"But the food," wailed the head caterer, a stout man named Flump, who was wringing his hands in his apron. "The roast swan! The jellies! They will melt! They will drown! The soup will be soup before it is even soup!"

"Then we shall have a broth of the gods!" Pumbleton declared. "Flump, do you know what makes a man great? It is not the absence of rain, sir! It is the ability to eat a vol-au-vent while standing in a puddle! Bring the tables! Bring the chairs! Let the sky do its worst!"

And so, the Great Banquet of Nether Wallop commenced. It was a scene that would have made a cynic weep and a duck rejoice. The long trestle tables were set out in the nave of the unfinished hall. Above, the gray clouds scudded by, occasionally dropping a heavy, singular drip onto a dignitary’s bald spot, causing him to jerk as if stung by a bee. The wind howled through the missing clerestory windows, extinguishing the candles as fast as the servants could light them, and sending the napkins flying like white birds fleeing a storm.

Alderman Pumbleton sat at the head of the table, under a large, borrowed umbrella held by a terrified apprentice. He was in his element. He drank the wine (which was rapidly becoming diluted with rainwater) and ate the ham (which was acquiring a certain fresh, damp flavor).

"Gentlemen!" he shouted, rising to his feet and nearly slipping on a lettuce leaf. "Look around you! Is this not magnificent? Is this not the spirit of Nether Wallop? We are a hardy race! We do not fear the elements! We invite them to table!"

The guests, shivering and soaked, clapped feebly. They were too cold to argue and too wet to leave. The Mayor was trying to eat his soup with an umbrella over his bowl. The Vicar was praying for a miracle, or at least a towel.

"Where is the Contractor?" someone cried out from the far end, where the rain was falling heaviest. "Where is Crimp?"

Pumbleton waved his fork grandly. "Mr. Crimp," he announced, his voice ringing with absolute conviction, "has gone on a… a topographical survey! To the New World! To bring us back… trees! Yes, exotic trees to plant in the courtyard! He is a visionary! He has sacrificed his comfort for our foliage!"

It was a lie so bald, so brazen, and so utterly devoid of shame that it silenced the room. The sheer audacity of it stunned the rain into a momentary pause. The guests looked at Pumbleton—this great, wet, pompous walrus of a man standing in a puddle in a roofless building—and they believed him. Not because it was true, but because it was easier than the alternative.

"Speech!" cried the Town Clerk, who had just been hit on the head by a falling slate. "Speech for the Alderman!"

"Hear, hear!" roared the shivering assembly.

Pumbleton beamed. He spread his arms wide, catching the rain on his gold chain, his face glowing with a triumph that no storm could dampen. "To Nether Wallop!" he bellowed. "To Progress! To the Sky! And to the indomitable spirit of English masonry!"

And as the guests cheered, and the rain began to fall again, harder and heavier than ever, washing the gravy off the plates and turning the floor into a swimming pool, Alderman Pumbleton sat down, satisfied that he had, once again, successfully presided over a disaster.
