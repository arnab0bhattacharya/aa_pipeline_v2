The heat of the valley below was an oppression, but up here in the Cordilleras the air possessed a thin, biting quality that seemed to cut at the lungs, and the silence of the peaks lay upon the mind like a weight of ancient stone. I had been gazing through the heavy brass theodolite at the distant smudge of Santa Maria, the capital, a place of white walls and dark treacheries, when the sound of hooves, striking sharp and hard against the loose shale of the path, broke the profound stillness of the heights. It was a sound that carried an abruptness, a hostile interruption, as if the very crust of the earth were being struck by an impatient and remorseless fate. 

I lowered my eye from the instrument. The sun beat down upon the barren slopes with a blinding, cruel persistence. Below me, the proposed line of the railway—a scratch upon the earth that was to bind the restless provinces to the sea—wound its tentative way through a deep gorge. It was a line of progress, of iron and fire, drawn through a land of primeval shadow and sudden blood. And I, an Englishman, a man of precise calculations and honest levels, found myself standing at the very hinge of their violent illusions.

The rider pulled up in a cloud of pale dust. It was not one of President Mendoza’s gaudy lancers, with their cocked hats and absurd silver trappings, but a man in a serape of dark wool, his face shadowed by a broad, weather-beaten hat. He sat his horse with the careless, dangerous ease of one who had spent his life looking over his shoulder at death. He did not dismount. He looked down at me, at my tripod, at the chainmen standing idly by the blackened rocks, with a gaze that was heavy with a profound and sardonic melancholy.

“Senor Engineer,” he said, in a voice that was soft, almost caressing, yet carrying the intimate menace of a drawn blade, “you carry the line of the rails very high. The passes are treacherous. A man might lose his way up here, or find it, depending on the company he keeps.”

I made no answer at once. I was struck by the singular futility of his observation, and by the realization that this must be one of General Vargas’s men—the general who held the eastern valleys in a grip of terror and defiance. I wiped the sweat from my brow with a hand that was not entirely steady, the heat of the sun and the sudden chill of the altitude mingling in my blood.

“I am carrying the line where the surveys dictate,” I said, with a stiff formality that sounded absurd even to my own ears in that vast, empty wilderness. “The gradients will not allow of a lower passage. I have my instructions from the Government.”

“The Government,” he repeated, the words falling from his lips with a slow, deliberate emphasis, as if tasting the bitter irony of the phrase. “A Government of clerks and shopkeepers in Santa Maria, who believe that a strip of iron will bring them civilization. They think it will make them European. They do not understand that civilization is a terrible thing, a fire that burns the hands of those who handle it. General Vargas sends his compliments, Senor. He suggests that the line might run lower, through the valley of Rio Seco, where the people are many and the soil is rich. He promises you his protection, and his friendship. He asks only for your loyalty.”

“My loyalty,” I said, a sudden anger flaring up within me, a reaction against the brooding atmosphere of menace that seemed to emanate from the man, from the very rocks around us, “is not a commodity to be bargained for in the wilderness. I am here to build a railway, not to take part in your political squabbles. I serve the contract I signed.”

The rider smiled, a mere twitch of the lips beneath the dark mustache, a smile that held no mirth, but only a weary knowledge of the world’s futile obstinacy. “A contract, Senor, is a scrap of paper. It is the loyalty of the gun that makes the steel move. The Government in Santa Maria promises you the military, but the military is corrupt, and the soldiers are barefoot. The general promises you the mountains. You will find, perhaps, that the mountains are more enduring.” 

He turned his horse with a sudden pressure of the knee, the animal pivoting on the loose stones with a grace that seemed a mockery of human clumsiness. “Consider the valley of Rio Seco, Senor Engineer. The general will expect your answer by the time the sun touches the highest peak tomorrow.” 

He rode away, leaving me standing by my theodolite, the dust of his passage settling slowly over the brass and the white-painted staves of my surveying party. The silence closed in again, but it was no longer the silence of an empty land; it was a silence heavy with the unspoken, with the obscure and menacing possibilities of a choice I had no wish to make.

I thought of President Mendoza’s minister, a stout man with a diamond pin and a greasy smile, who had pressed a glass of aguardiente upon me in a stifling office in Santa Maria, speaking of the railway as the great artery of progress, of the light of science piercing the dark ignorance of the interior. “You are bringing us life, my dear friend,” he had said, his small eyes glistening with an enthusiasm that did not extend beyond the immediate prospect of personal gain. “You are the apostle of a new era.” 

And now, on the other side of the scale, there was General Vargas, a man I had never seen, but whose name was whispered with a shudder even by my peons, a man who also spoke of progress, but of a different kind—a progress bought with rifles, a civilization that marched hand in hand with rebellion. Both demanded the line; both promised peace; and both, I knew with a cold, sinking certainty, would not hesitate to bury me under the rubble of my own cuttings if I failed to serve their turn.

I walked slowly along the proposed route, the crunch of my boots on the loose shale sounding hollow and insignificant in the vast amphitheater of the mountains. The sun was sinking, and the shadows of the peaks stretched out across the valley like the long, dark fingers of a closing fate. The sky to the east was a deep, bruised purple, against which the jagged outlines of the Cordilleras stood out with a hard, metallic sharpness. 

What was this civilization I was bringing? A means to transport troops more swiftly to their slaughter, or to carry the spoils of a plundered province to the idle tyrants of the coast? I thought of the sleek iron rails, gleaming with a cold, industrial light, laid across a land that still worshipped the ancient, terrible gods of blood and stone. The railway would come, undoubtedly; the line would be built; the engines would puff their smoke into the pure, thin air of the passes. But would it bring peace? Or would it merely hasten the pace of destruction, turning the slow, patient cruelty of the old days into a swift and relentless modern horror?

I stopped by a deep cutting that my men had begun in the side of the hill. The raw earth, red and wet, looked like a wound in the side of the mountain. The peons had left their tools lying about—picks, shovels, a broken wheelbarrow—abandoned in a sudden access of fear or laziness. A faint, acrid smell of powder hung in the air from a recent blast. 

As I stood there, gazing down into the cutting, the full weight of my isolation pressed upon me. I was a foreigner, a man of a different breed, caught in the toils of a land I did not understand, and a people whose passions were as dark and unfathomable as the shadows that were now gathering in the gorges. The Government would not protect me; they were too busy plotting against their own shadows. The rebels would not spare me; they were too intent on their own savage justice. I was a mere instrument, a pawn in a game that was played with human lives and iron rails.

The first stars began to appear in the darkening sky, cold and remote, looking down with an ironic indifference upon the petty strivings of men. The wind rose, sighing through the loose stones with a sound like the murmur of a vast, invisible multitude. I wrapped my coat tighter about me, shivering slightly in the sudden chill. 

Tomorrow I would have to give an answer. I would have to choose between the valley of Rio Seco and the high pass, between General Vargas and President Mendoza, between two lies that both called themselves civilization. I looked again at the raw wound in the hillside, at the scattered tools, the symbols of my futile endeavor. The railway would not bring peace to this tortured land. It would only bring a swifter passage to death. And I, who had come here with such high hopes, such a clear conscience, would be the one to lay the iron track of their ruin.

I turned my back on the cutting and began the long, slow descent to the camp. The darkness closed in behind me, swallowing the mountains, the gorge, and the ghostly line of my survey, leaving nothing but the cold, pitiless stars and the heavy, brooding silence of the Cordilleras.
