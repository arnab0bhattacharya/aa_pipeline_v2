The rain, which had been falling since the grey dawn over London, had settled into a steady, oblique drizzle that made the pavements of Soho glisten like the scales of some dark, submerged creature. It was the kind of dampness that penetrates the cloth of one’s coat, not with the violence of a storm, but with the slow, insidious persistence of a moral doubt. I stood beneath the awning of a shuttered chandler’s shop in Dean Street, watching the gas lamps struggle against the encroaching gloom of the evening, and I felt the familiar, cold weight of the trade I practised—a trade which, I had long since realized, consisted less in the detection of crime than in the management of the shadows where human souls are bought and sold.

For ten years I had walked these streets, learning the rhythms of the exile, the dialect of the conspiracy, and the specific, feverish silence that precedes an act of terror. I had cultivated a garden of informers, a delicate and poisonous flora that required constant, careful tending. Among them, there was one who had grown larger than the rest, a man whose roots had entwined themselves with the very foundations of my own safety. His name was Karel Vostrek.

He was not a man of action, this Vostrek; he was a man of ink and nerve, a Bohemian who had fled the iron hand of the Emperor only to find himself trapped in the softer, more complex coils of the English law. He sat now in a small, airless room above a printer’s shop in Frith Street, surrounded by pamphlets that smelled of wet ink and sedition, and he wrote the letters that kept me informed of the movements of the International Brotherhood. He was my shield. As long as the Brotherhood trusted him, they remained visible to me; as long as I remained visible to them, I remained alive.

But the order had come down from Whitehall that afternoon, delivered by a messenger whose livery was too new and whose face was too smooth to belong to the old, weary world of Scotland Yard. The new Chief—this Major General Sir Arthur Bellingham, a man of polished boots and unblemished conscience—had summoned me to his office. The room had been vast, smelling of beeswax and authority, and the light from the tall windows had fallen upon the map of London spread across his desk as if illuminating a battlefield.

"The public, Inspector," the Major General had said, his voice possessing that clipped, military resonance which brooked no ambiguity, "requires a demonstration. The papers are full of it. These Anarchists, these Nihilists... they believe they can strike at the heart of the Empire with impunity. We must show them otherwise. We must make an arrest. Something substantial. Something that will satisfy the editors of the *Times*."

I had stood before him, my hat in my hands, feeling the dampness of my coat drying in the artificial heat of the room. I had explained, with the careful, weary precision of a man who knows he is not being heard, that the revolutionaries were fluid, that they moved like smoke, that to strike at the foot soldiers was to accomplish nothing.

"Smoke?" the Major General had repeated, leaning back in his leather chair. "Nonsense. There is always a head. There is always a man who writes the manifestos, who holds the funds, who incites the rest. Find me that man, Inspector. Find me the writer, the intellect behind the chaos. If you do not, I shall find someone who can."

He had looked at me then with the bright, uncompromising gaze of a man who has never had to compromise. He saw only the failure of the police to protect the public; he did not see the intricate web of trust and betrayal that kept the web from collapsing entirely. He did not see that the man who wrote the manifestos, the man who held the knowledge, was Karel Vostrek.

And so I walked now through the wet streets of Soho, carrying the weight of that command. The irony was a bitter taste in my mouth, metallic and cold. To please the newspapers, to satisfy the vanity of a new superior, I was ordered to destroy the only instrument that had kept the chaos at bay. It was a grotesque simplification of the world, this demand for a villain to be paraded in the dock. They wanted a symbol, a face to pin upon their fears, and the only face available was the one that looked at me with such desperate, pathetic gratitude every Tuesday evening.

I turned into the narrow alleyway that led to Vostrek’s lodgings. The silence here was different from the silence of the open street; it was a pressurized silence, heavy with the smell of stale cabbage and the damp rot of the brickwork. I climbed the stairs, my footsteps echoing with a dull, hollow thud that seemed to mock the stealth I usually employed. There was no need for stealth tonight. The prey had already been caught; he simply did not know it yet.

I pushed open the door without knocking. Vostrek was there, as I knew he would be, hunched over a small table, a single candle throwing long, dancing shadows against the peeling wallpaper. He looked up, and his face, which was pale and lined with the anxieties of a hunted man, broke into a smile of relief. He was a small man, with eyes that burned with a strange, intellectual fire—a fire that I had learned to bank and control for my own purposes.

"Inspector," he breathed, rising to his feet and extending a hand that trembled slightly. "You are late. I began to fear... I thought perhaps the police had been raiding the meeting in Whitechapel."

I did not take his hand. I stood by the door, letting the cold air from the landing mingle with the stifling atmosphere of the room. I looked at him—at the ink stains on his fingers, at the manuscripts scattered across the table, at the half-eaten crust of bread that spoke of his poverty and his dedication. He was a man who believed in the inevitable triumph of the proletariat, a man who sacrificed his comfort for an idea, and he was also a man who sold his friends to the police for a few pounds a month to pay for his printing press. The duality of him was nauseating, and yet, in that moment, he seemed merely pathetic, a pawn moved by forces he could not comprehend.

"Karel," I said, and my voice sounded strange to my own ears, flat and devoid of the usual cordiality. "The time has come for a change in our arrangements."

He looked at me, his brow furrowing. "A change? But the Brotherhood... they are planning the demonstration at the Embankment. I have the names, Inspector. I have the routes. If we strike now—"

"There will be no strike," I interrupted him. "Not by them. The authorities require a scapegoat, Karel. They require a head for the block."

The smile vanished from his face. It did not fade; it was extinguished, as if a wet cloth had been thrown over a flame. He understood. He was, after all, a man of intellect. He looked at my face, searching for the loophole, the reprieve, the hidden signal that this was merely another test of his loyalty. But there was nothing there. I had cultivated him for years, nurtured his usefulness, and now I was ordered to harvest him.

"You?" he whispered. The word was barely audible.

"I have my orders," I said, stepping forward. "The new Chief desires a conviction. He desires the author of the sedition. You have written enough, Karel. You have signed enough pamphlets. You are the most plausible man in London."

He backed away until his shoulders hit the wall. The plaster crumbled slightly under his weight. "But I did it for you," he hissed, the desperation rising in his voice. "I did it to keep them in check. I saved you from the bomb throwers. I gave you the names of the men who wanted to kill the Ministers. I am your man."

"That is precisely why you are the one," I replied, the words tasting of ash. "You know too much. You are too deep in their councils. To the public, you are a revolutionary. To the police, you are a traitor. To me... you are an expense that can no longer be afforded."

He stared at me, and in his eyes I saw the realization of the absolute, crushing indifference of the world. He saw that he was not a partner in a great game, but a tool that had worn out. The moral universe, which he had thought to reshape with his pamphlets and his conspiracies, had simply closed over him.

"You are a devil," he said softly.

"I am a policeman," I corrected him. "And the rain is getting heavier."

I moved towards him then, my hand reaching for the handcuffs at my belt. The metal was cold against my skin. Outside, the wind rattled the windowpane, and the gas lamp in the street below flickered, casting a brief, distorted shadow of the two of us against the wall—two men bound together in a room full of shadows, one destroying the other to preserve the darkness. I felt no triumph, only the weary, heavy satisfaction of a duty performed, a silence secured. The newspapers would be pleased. The Major General would be satisfied. And in the labyrinth of London, the smoke would continue to rise, unchecked and unseen, for a little while longer.
