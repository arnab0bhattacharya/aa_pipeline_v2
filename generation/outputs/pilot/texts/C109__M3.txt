The train from Dover crawled into Victoria Station under a sky the colour of wet slate, and Clara felt the familiar chill of England seep into her bones before she had even stepped onto the platform. It was a damp, insidious cold, so different from the dry, biting winds of the Dordogne, and it carried with it the scent of coal smoke and wet wool. She gathered her black coat around her, adjusting the veil of her hat, and joined the slow procession of passengers alighting onto the platform. Twenty years had passed since she had last set foot in London, and yet the station seemed both utterly unchanged and profoundly alien. The noise of it, the sheer volume of English being spoken all around her, struck her like a physical blow. She had grown accustomed to the melodic cadence of French, to the soft consonants and rolling rhythms of her husband’s native tongue, and now the clipped, staccato English of the commuters sounded harsh and foreign to her ears.

She took a hansom cab to her sister Margaret’s house in Hampstead, watching the streets of London roll past the window like a landscape from a half-remembered dream. The city seemed grimmer than she recalled, scarred by the war that had touched it in ways she had only vaguely understood from across the Channel. There were buildings with their facades torn away, revealing intimate rooms like dolls’ houses, and everywhere there were women in black, widows and mourners, a city of grief layered upon grief. Her own grief was fresh and raw, a wound that had not yet begun to heal. Her mother, Eleanor, had died three days ago, and Clara had received the telegram in the kitchen of the farmhouse in Saint-Martin-le-Vieux, where she had been kneading bread for the evening meal. She remembered staring at the words, the flour dust on her hands, the dough rising on the table, and feeling as though the world had shifted beneath her feet. Henri had been in the fields, and she had stood alone in the kitchen, reading the telegram again and again, the French address and the English words jarring against each other like two incompatible truths.

Margaret’s house was a tall, narrow terrace on a street of identical houses, each one distinguished only by the colour of its door or the particular arrangement of curtains in the windows. Clara paid the cab driver and stood for a moment on the pavement, looking up at the house. She remembered it from her last visit, years ago, when her mother had still been living there, before the decline had begun. Now the net curtains in the front window looked grey with age, and the brass knocker was tarnished. She lifted it and let it fall, and the sound echoed through the house. The door was opened by her younger sister, Beatrice, who stared at Clara for a long moment before stepping aside to let her in. Beatrice had aged in ways that Clara found startling. She was thin to the point of gauntness, her dark hair streaked with grey, and she wore an expression of perpetual weariness that seemed to have settled into the very lines of her face.

‘You came,’ Beatrice said, and the words were not warm, merely factual, an observation rather than a welcome.

‘Of course I came,’ Clara replied, and heard her own voice, the slight French inflection that had crept into her English over the years, the way she shaped her vowels differently now, and she saw Beatrice notice it too, saw the flicker of something, recognition or alienation, pass across her sister’s face.

Margaret was in the parlour, arranging flowers in a vase on the mantelpiece. She turned when Clara entered and looked at her with the same measuring gaze that Beatrice had given her. Margaret was the eldest, broad-shouldered and commanding, and she had always been the one who took charge, who organised and directed. Clara supposed that she had been the one to manage their mother’s affairs, to arrange the funeral, to deal with the solicitors and the undertakers.

‘Clara,’ Margaret said. ‘You look well.’

‘Thank you,’ Clara said, and then, because she did not know what else to say, ‘How was she? At the end.’

There was a silence. Margaret placed a sprig of white heather in the vase and stepped back to examine her work. ‘She was quiet,’ she said. ‘She didn’t suffer, if that’s what you’re asking. She just slipped away.’

Clara nodded. She wanted to ask if her mother had spoken of her, if she had asked for her, but she was afraid of the answer, and so she said nothing. She stood in the parlour of her sister’s house and looked at the furnishings, the heavy mahogany furniture, the faded watercolours on the walls, the portrait of their father above the fireplace, and she felt as though she were standing in a museum, surrounded by artefacts from a life she had once lived but could no longer fully claim.

The funeral was to be held the next morning, at St. Jude’s, the church where they had all been baptised, where their parents had been married, where their father’s funeral had been held fifteen years before. Clara spent the night in the spare room, a small, cold room at the back of the house that looked out onto a narrow garden. The bed was lumpy and the sheets smelled of camphor, and she lay awake for most of the night, listening to the unfamiliar sounds of London, the distant rumble of omnibuses, the occasional hoot of a foghorn on the river, the creak of the old house settling. She thought of Henri, asleep in the farmhouse in Saint-Martin-le-Vieux, with its thick stone walls and its shutters closed against the night, and she felt a pang of longing so intense it was almost physical. She thought of her two sons, Lucien and Étienne, grown now and working the land with their father, and she wondered if they ever thought of England, if they remembered the few visits they had made as boys, stumbling over their English phrases, bewildered by the grey skies and the strange customs.

At breakfast the next morning, the three sisters sat around the table in Margaret’s kitchen, eating toast and drinking tea, and the silence between them was heavy with all the things unsaid. Clara tried to bridge the gap, asking about Margaret’s husband, Arthur, who had been too unwell to leave his bed, and about Beatrice’s children, who were away at school. The answers were brief and polite, and Clara felt the distance between them widening with every word. It was Margaret who finally said what Clara had been sensing since her arrival.

‘You’ve become quite the Frenchwoman, Clara,’ she said, spreading marmalade on her toast with careful precision. ‘The way you dress, the way you speak. One would hardly know you were English at all.’

Clara set down her teacup. ‘I’ve lived in France for twenty years, Margaret. I married a Frenchman. My children are French. It would be strange if I hadn’t changed.’

‘Yes,’ Margaret said. ‘Strange.’

Beatrice looked down at her plate and said nothing.

They travelled to the church in a black motorcar that Margaret had hired, sitting in a row on the back seat like three children, their knees not quite touching. The church was cold and half-empty, the congregation made up mostly of elderly women and a few men in ill-fitting suits. Clara recognised almost no one, and those she did recognise looked at her with a vague, puzzled expression, as though trying to place her. She supposed she had changed beyond recognition. She was fifty-two now, her hair more silver than auburn, her face tanned by the southern sun, and her black dress, bought in a shop in Périgueux, had a cut and line that was unmistakably French. She felt the eyes of the congregation on her and knew that they saw a stranger, a foreigner who had returned to claim a connection that time and distance had severed.

The service was simple and brief. The vicar spoke of Eleanor’s life in general terms, as though he had not quite known her himself, and the hymns were sung in thin, wavering voices that echoed in the vaulted space. Clara sang the words she remembered from childhood, but they felt awkward in her mouth, the old English hymns that she had not sung in twenty years. At the graveside, she stood between her sisters and watched the coffin being lowered into the earth, and she felt the grief rise in her at last, a great, aching wave that threatened to overwhelm her. She thought of her mother as she had been when Clara was a girl, before the move to Hampstead, before the slow decline, when they had lived in a house with a garden full of roses and her mother had sung as she worked. She thought of the last time she had seen her, three years ago, when she had come to visit and found her sitting by the window in Margaret’s parlour, staring out at the street with an expression of profound absence, as though she had already begun to leave them.

After the burial, they returned to Margaret’s house for the reception, and Clara found herself standing in the corner of people she did not recognise, holding a cup of tea and a plate of sandwiches she had no appetite for. Margaret moved through the room with practised ease, greeting guests, accepting condolences, directing the flow of conversation. Beatrice sat in a chair by the window, her hands folded in her lap, looking exhausted. Clara watched them both and felt a deep and terrible sadness, not just for her mother, but for the relationships that had withered and died in her absence, the sisterhood that had once been the centre of her life and was now nothing more than a formality, a set of obligations that neither party quite knew how to fulfil.

She left the next morning, on an early train from Victoria. Margaret and Beatrice saw her off on the platform, and the goodbyes were stiff and perfunctory. Margaret pressed her hand and told her to write, though they both knew she would not. Beatrice kissed her on the cheek, a quick, dry kiss that barely touched the skin. Clara climbed into the carriage and settled by the window, and as the train pulled out of the station, she looked back at her sisters standing together on the platform, two figures in black, growing smaller and smaller until they disappeared. She turned her face to the window and watched England slide past, the grey fields and the grey sky and the grey towns, and she felt the strange, hollow ache of a woman who had lost not one country but two, who belonged nowhere now except in the memory of a life she had built for herself in a place that had never been entirely her own.
