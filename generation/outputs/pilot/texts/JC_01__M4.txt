The squall came up out of the nor’-west with that stealth which is the peculiar treachery of the Indian Ocean—a blackening of the air rather than a cloud, a breathless hush that seemed to press upon the ear-drums, and then the wind, not as a gust but as a wall, solid and suffocating, that struck the barque *Lavinia* upon her starboard quarter and laid her over until her lee rail was buried in a smother of white. I was upon the poop, as it was my watch, and I remember—God knows I have had reason enough to remember—the precise quality of the light at that instant: a livid, greenish pallor, as though the sun had been drowned in the sea and its corpse-light lingered in the waste of waters. The rain followed, not drops but a deluge, a warm and blinding cataract that shut the world away and left me standing in a little circle of my own terror, solitary as a man in a tomb.

What happened in the next few minutes I have never been able to recover entire. There is a blank in my memory, a gap through which the wind screamed and the canvas thundered and the voices of the men came to me as from a great distance, distorted and meaningless. I know that I did not move. I stood with my hands upon the rail, and I felt the ship stagger and recover and stagger again, and I watched the topgallant sails tear themselves into ribbons with a kind of frozen fascination, and I did nothing—nothing at all—while the sea made fair to swallow us and the masts strained and groaned like creatures in torment. It was not fear, precisely, that held me. It was something colder and more shameful: a paralysis of the will, a sudden and complete abdication of that faculty which distinguishes the seaman from the landsman, and which I had believed—fool that I was—to be as much a part of me as the blood in my veins.

Then a hand fell upon my shoulder, and I turned to see the captain, Mr. Elias Grieve, standing at my elbow in his shirt-sleeves, bareheaded, the rain streaming from his grey beard and his eyes fixed upon me with an expression I could not read. He said nothing. He looked at me for what seemed an eternity, and then he stepped past me to the rail and began to give his orders in that flat, unhurried voice of his which had in it the quality of iron—not loud, but carrying, cutting through the roar of the elements as a knife cuts through silk. The men, who had been running wild, found their hands and their ears at once. The wheel was eased, the yards braced, the fore-topmast staysail got down and stowed, and in a space of time that I could not measure the *Lavinia* had come up to the wind and was riding it out, wet and battered but alive, while the squall passed over us and vanished into the darkness of the eastward with a last petulant flick of rain.

I stood where he had left me. I do not know how long. The stars came out, one by one, and the sea smoothed itself into that long, oily swell which follows a blow, and the ship found her rhythm again, and still I stood, my hands upon the rail, my heart a stone within me. At last I heard his step behind me, and I turned. He was lighting his pipe, shielding the match with his broad palm, and the flame illumined for an instant the deep seams of his face, the tight-shut mouth, the eyes that had seen too much to be surprised at anything—least of all at the failure of a young man upon whom he had deigned to confer the dignity of a mate’s berth.

“You had best get below,” he said, “and change your clothes. You are wet through.”

That was all. He did not speak of what had passed. He did not speak of it that night, nor the next day, nor ever. And I—I who had stood like a figurehead while the ship was in peril and the men looked to me for the word that would not come—I crept to my cabin like a whipped dog, and lay in my bunk with my face to the bulkhead, and listened to the water gurgling in the scuppers and the creak of the timbers, and wished that the sea had taken me.

In the days that followed I went about my duties with a careful and elaborate precision, as though by the mere performance of them I might somehow undo the thing that could not be undone. I watched the men. I watched them with a sickening intensity, searching their faces for that flicker of contempt which I knew must be there, for that half-smile of understanding which is worse than any open scorn. The Dutchman, Van Der Meer, who was the boatswain—a heavy, red-faced man with pale eyes and a way of looking at one sidewise—seemed to me to be always upon the point of speaking. I would catch his glance upon me in the dog-watches, and my blood would run cold, and I would turn away and busy myself with the log-line or the sand-glass, and when I looked again he would be chewing his quid and staring at the sea as though he had never had a thought of me in his life. The young fellow, Petersen, who was the other mate, a Dane with a fair, open countenance, treated me with a kindness that was worse than any cruelty. He would offer me his tobacco, or ask me to share his watch below, and I would refuse him curtly and go apart and hate him for his charity.

But it was the captain whom I watched most closely. I watched him as a man watches a judge, seeking for some sign of the sentence that is to come. He was a silent man at the best of times, given to standing apart with his pipe and his thoughts, and there was nothing in his manner that I could lay hold upon. He gave his orders as before, he took his sights and worked out his position, he walked the poop in the evening with his hands behind his back and his eyes upon the horizon. And yet I could not believe that he had forgotten. I would see him, sometimes, standing by the taffrail, looking down at the wake as it streamed away from us into the darkness, and I would fancy that he was turning over in his mind the words he would use when we came to port—the report he would make to the owners, the quiet, damning phrases that would strip me of my certificate and my livelihood and my name. I would lie awake at night and hear his voice in imagination, flat and unhurried, saying: “The second mate, sir, proved unequal to his responsibilities. Upon the night of the twelfth I was compelled to take the deck in his place.” And then the silence, and the scratching of a pen, and the end of all things.

I have heard men say that cowardice is a thing of the body only, that the spirit can rise above it. I do not believe them. I believe that what I felt in those days was a cowardice of the soul, a creeping and corrosive shame that ate into the very roots of my being and left me hollow. I had been so sure of myself—so sure of my seamanship, my nerve, my readiness to meet whatever the sea might send. And in the space of a few minutes, in the roar of a squall, I had learned that I was nothing—a man of straw, a broken reed, a creature whose will could be blown out like a candle by the first breath of danger.

We came to Aden on the twenty-third day out, and I went ashore with the captain to report to the agents. He said nothing to me on the way, and I walked beside him in a sweat of apprehension, waiting for the blow to fall. But when we were seated in the office, and the agent had asked his questions, and the captain had answered them in his flat, unemotional voice, he said nothing of the squall, nothing of my failure, nothing of the night when he had taken the deck from my hands. He spoke of the weather, of the cargo, of the run we had made, and then he rose and shook hands with the agent and we went out into the blinding sunlight, and I knew that I was saved—saved by his silence, which was more terrible than any words.

I never sailed with him again. I never asked him why he had spared me, and he never offered to tell. But I have thought of it often, in the years since, when I have walked my own quarterdeck and watched the young men under me and seen in their eyes the same fear that was in mine. And I have come to believe that he kept silent not out of mercy, but out of a deeper and more terrible knowledge—the knowledge that every man has his squall, his moment of darkness, and that the sea, which takes all things, takes also the memory of our shame and hides it in its own unfathomable silence.
