The fog of that November was not the honest, suffocating blanket of the river, but a greasier, more insidious thing that seemed to seep from the very bricks of Soho, blurring the gas-lamps into wan, spectral smudges. I had walked through it from the Yard, my greatcoat collar turned up against a dampness that was less a rain than a pervasive, chilling exhalation of the city itself. My destination was a mean and narrow house in a court off Greek Street, a place where the air was thick with the smell of boiled cabbage and the acrid, foreign scent of political desperation. It was there that I was to meet my man, Verloc—though that was not the name he was born with, nor the one by which he was known to the dozen or so fervent and impoverished conspirators who trusted him.

For five years, Verloc had been my creature. He was a heavy, taciturn man, a seller of pornographic postcards and a purveyor of gossip, a man whose soul seemed as soiled and weary as the little shop he kept. He was not clever, but he was cautious, and he possessed a kind of dumb, animal instinct for self-preservation which made him invaluable. He was my ear in the fetid undergrowth of the revolutionary movement, the man who brought me word of their fantastical plots, their pitiful quarrels, their grandiose and empty rhetoric. In return, I paid him a small stipend and, more importantly, I paid him with my silence. I knew of the man he had killed in his youth, a shadowy affair in some Continental city, and I held that knowledge not as a threat, but as a bond—a shared secret that made us, in a sense, partners in the squalid business of survival.

On this evening, however, as I ducked into the dim, gas-lit passage of his shop, the nature of that partnership felt suddenly and profoundly altered. The order had come down from the new Assistant Commissioner, a brisk, impatient man with a genius for administrative detail and a profound ignorance of the human material with which he worked. The newspapers, he had informed me, were baying for a success, for a visible blow against the hydra of anarchy. A bomb had been found, luckily, before it could go off, in a cloakroom at a railway terminus. The public was alarmed. The government was nervous. An arrest was required. “A plausible one, Inspector,” he had said, his gaze fixed on a neat pile of papers on his desk. “The man at the centre of this. The one your people call the Professor.”

I had stood before him, a man of middle years and settled habits, and felt the ground shift beneath my feet. The Professor was a phantom, a creature of rumour and fear, a man of such extreme and unyielding purity of purpose that he was universally distrusted, even by his fellow fanatics. To arrest him was impossible. To explain this to the Assistant Commissioner, to reveal the intricate, fragile web of informants and half-truths by which I maintained my precarious intelligence, was equally impossible. He wanted a name, a body, a spectacle for the morning editions. And as I stood there, listening to the distant, impersonal hum of the great machine of state, I understood with a cold, sick certainty that the only name I could offer, the only body I could deliver, was that of the man who had kept me safe.

He was in the back room, as I knew he would be, sitting before a small fire that did little to dispel the gloom. He was reading a newspaper, a French one, his heavy shoulders hunched, his thick fingers black with printer’s ink. He looked up as I entered, and a slow, cautious recognition dawned in his dull eyes. He did not rise.

“Mr. Verloc,” I said, my voice sounding strange and loud in the small space.

He grunted in acknowledgement, folding the paper with a deliberation that was almost insolent. “You are late,” he observed. “The fog.”

“Yes,” I said. “The fog.” I stood there, my hands clasped behind my back, feeling the weight of the revolver in my pocket, a weight that seemed to have increased tenfold since I had crossed the threshold. I had not drawn it in twenty years of service. I prayed I would not have to now. “There is trouble,” I began, and then stopped. The words felt like stones in my mouth.

He watched me, his face a mask of stolid, peasant patience. “There is always trouble,” he said.

“This is different.” I took a step closer, the floorboards creaking beneath my boots. “The newspapers have found a bomb. They are making a great noise. The new man at the Yard… he wants a result. He wants a man in the cells by morning.”

A flicker of something—not fear, but a deep, weary understanding—passed across Verloc’s face. He knew the mechanics of this world as well as I did. He knew that the great, blind forces of public opinion and political ambition required their regular, bloody sacrifices. He had, in his own way, been a priest at that altar for years, offering up the names of others. Now, it seemed, his turn had come.

“And who is to be this man?” he asked, his voice flat, without inflection.

I did not answer at once. The silence in the room was absolute, broken only by the sputtering of the coals and the distant, muffled sound of a street-hawker crying his wares in the fog-bound darkness. I looked at him—at the shabby coat, the greasy collar, the face that was a monument to a lifetime of quiet, unheroic villainy. He was not a good man. But he was my man. He was the one fixed point in a world of shifting allegiances and treacherous ideals. And I was about to betray him, not with a dramatic flourish, but with the quiet, bureaucratic efficiency of a state that had no time for the complexities of the human heart.

“There is a man,” I said at last, my voice barely a whisper. “A man who has been seen at these meetings. A man of known, violent associations. A man who has, in his possession, certain… inflammatory literature.” The lies came easily, for they were the common currency of my trade. “His name is Verloc.”

He did not move. He did not start, nor cry out, nor even change his expression. He simply sat there, his eyes fixed on my face, and in them I saw a profound and terrible comprehension. He saw the whole sordid transaction: my fear, my ambition, my cowardice. He saw the Assistant Commissioner’s neat piles of paper, the newspaper editors sharpening their pens, the great, indifferent public clamouring for a villain. And he saw me, the instrument of all these things, standing before him in the gloom of his own house, a Judas in a police inspector’s coat.

“I see,” he said, after a long, agonising pause. The two words were heavy with a finality that was more damning than any curse. He rose slowly to his feet, his movements ponderous and deliberate. He looked around the room, at the cheap furniture, the piles of pamphlets, the portrait of a stern-faced woman on the mantelpiece—his wife, I knew, a woman as silent and watchful as he. He seemed to be taking his leave of it all.

“You will come quietly, then,” I said, and my voice was hoarse.

He turned back to me, and for the first time, a faint, ironic smile touched his lips. “It is my business to come quietly, Inspector,” he said. “It is what I have always done.”

He walked towards me, his hands hanging loosely at his sides. He was a big man, and in the confined space he seemed to loom over me. For a moment, I thought he might strike me, and my hand moved instinctively towards my pocket. But he did not. He simply stopped a pace away and looked at me with those dull, knowing eyes.

“You will do what you must,” he said, and his voice was not bitter, but resigned, as if he were commenting on the weather. “And I will do what I must.”

He turned his back on me and walked towards the door that led to the shop, and to the street, and to the waiting police van and the cold, certain future of a public trial and a long, degrading imprisonment. I followed him, a man walking in a dream, the fog of the city seeming to have entered my very soul. The great, ironic machine of justice was about to grind its wheels, and I, who had spent my life trying to understand its workings, was to be one of its cogs. I had saved the state from a phantom, and in doing so, I had destroyed the only man who had ever trusted me. The silence between us was more eloquent than any confession, and in it, I heard the true, terrible cost of my long and faithful service.
