If the thoroughfare of Grubber’s Passage, hard by the shadow of old Holborn, could have been said to possess a middle—and it was a point upon which the local scavengers and stray dogs hotly debated, for the alley narrowed at both ends like a poorly stuffed sausage—that middle was commanded by the deadest, blackest, most defiantly shuttered warehouse in all the County of Middlesex. The blinds were not merely drawn; they were fortified. Heavy iron slats crossed the grimy glass of the lower windows, bearing such a settled coat of soot that they seemed to have wept ink for a hundred years and finally succumbed to a melancholy despair. To this mausoleum of commerce was sent, on a damp and drizzly evening in the November of 183-something, a boy of ten, by the name of Tobias Tilt.

Tobias was a sharp boy, as sharp as the corner of a threepenny bit, though considerably more worn at the edges. He possessed a pair of watchful grey eyes that seemed perpetually engaged in taking the mental measure of things—whether the height of a garden wall, the probable velocity of a passing pie-seller’s barrow, or the exact degree of benevolence in a stranger’s eye. On this particular evening, his eyes were occupied in measuring the extraordinary architecture of his new guardian’s parlour. For Tobias had been dispatched, by an uncle who found him inconveniently present in the world, to reside with his grandfather, one Mr. Gideon Slatch.

Mr. Slatch was a pawnbroker by trade, though by strict practice he was a pawnbroker retired. He was a dry, brittle old gentleman, composed of sharp angles and dusty suspicions, who wore his grey hair cropped close to a skull that seemed to have been expressly designed by nature to deflect human affection. His countenance was the colour of a brown-paper parcel left too long in the rain, and his eyes, peering out from beneath two formidable thatches of eyebrow, twinkled not with merriment, but with a hard, metallic lustre, like polished boot-buttons. He spoke in a low, grating whisper, as if the very sound of his own voice were a coin he was unwilling to spend.

‘So,’ said Mr. Slatch, fixing the boy with a boot-button eye, ‘you are the son of my daughter, who ran away. A fine piece of work. You will sleep in the back room. You will eat what I give you, and be thankful it is not less. You will not touch the pledges. You will not ask about the pledges. I have kept them, sir, every one, for thirty year. They are mine. I bought them, fair and square, and I will not be cheated out of them by sentiment, or by you. Do you mark me?’

‘I mark you, Grandfather,’ replied Tobias, who had already marked the parlour rug, the fire-irons, and the extraordinary fact that the old man’s waistcoat appeared to be entirely constructed of patches upon patches, each of a different and equally miserable hue.

‘Don’t Grandfather me,’ whispered Mr. Slatch, with a convulsive start, as if the word were a physical blow to his ribs. ‘Call me Slatch. Or sir. I am not your grandfather; I am your creditor. You owe me for your keep, and you will pay me in labour. There is a broom. There is a shop below stairs. You will sweep it. But mind! You will not touch the pledges. Not a brass button, not a pewter pot, not a mourning ring! They are sacred!’

He said this with such a theatrical flourish of his long, bony fingers that Tobias might have thought him a madman, had not the boy’s natural shrewdness informed him that madness and extreme miserliness often share the same lodging.

The next morning, Tobias descended into the shuttered shop. It was a cavern of forgotten sorrows. The dust lay thick upon the counters, and in the dim light filtering through the iron slats, the objects upon the shelves assumed the most fantastical shapes. A row of tarnished silver teapots seemed to huddle together like miserable old women awaiting a charity dole. A rack of violins, lacking strings and bridges, stretched their necks like so many starving poultry. A case of watches, all stopped at different hours, appeared to be a parliament of idle philosophers, each stubbornly insisting upon his own private time, deaf to the ticking of the next.

But it was the pledges themselves that commanded Tobias’s attention. For every item—a battered snuffbox, a miniature portrait of a lady with a beauty spot, a pair of infant’s shoes rubbed thin at the toes—bore a small yellow ticket, inscribed in Mr. Slatch’s cramped, miserly hand. And beside the ticket, written in a faded red ink, was a date, and a sum. Tobias, sweeping the floor with the gravity of a philosopher, read these tickets. The earliest date was thirty years ago. The sums were pitifully small. A shilling for a wedding ring. Sixpence for a silver locket. Half-a-crown for a military epaulette tarnished with salt and grief.

He discovered, too, that Mr. Slatch’s idiolect was as cramped as his handwriting. The old man’s conversation was a miser’s purse, admitting only the smallest coinage of speech. ‘Hah!’ he would exclaim, when Tobias asked a question. ‘Wanting to know, wanting to know! A penny for your thoughts, and a halfpenny for your ruin! Keep your eyes on the broom, and your nose out of the ledger!’ He had a habit of rubbing his left thumb and forefinger together, as if testing the texture of an invisible coin, and would punctuate his sentences with this gesture, his lips pursed as though he had bitten into a sour plum.

Tobias, being a boy of a speculative turn, set himself to earn the old man’s affection by the only method a sharp boy of ten can employ against a miser: he made himself useful in matters of account. He discovered Mr. Slatch attempting to calculate the interest on a farthing, and, with a respectful cough, pointed out that the farthing would not, by any mathematical contrivance, become a halfpenny within the old man’s natural life. Mr. Slatch was so astonished by this barefaced robbery of his arithmetic that he actually permitted the boy to balance his household ledger—a task Tobias performed with such neatness that the old man’s thumb and forefinger rubbed together in a positive frenzy of astonished approval.

‘Hah!’ said Mr. Slatch, eyeing the neat columns. ‘You have a head for figures. It is a poor head that cannot figure. But figures are facts, and facts are solid. Unlike people. People are vapours. People are bubbles. They pledge their grandmothers’ bibles for a pot of porter, and then they weep, weep, weep! I do not weep. I keep!’

‘But why do you keep them, sir?’ asked Tobias, seizing the moment. ‘The pledges, I mean. The tickets are all out of date. You could have sold them a hundred times over.’

The old man’s face underwent a remarkable transformation. The hard, metallic lustre of his eyes seemed to soften, or perhaps it was only the dim light of the parlour playing upon his brown-paper features. He rubbed his thumb and forefinger together, slowly, as if counting the years.

‘Keep them?’ he whispered. ‘Because they are mine. Because I paid for them. Because—because they did not come back!’

‘Who did not come back, sir?’

‘The people!’ cried Mr. Slatch, with a sudden violence that made the teapots rattle. ‘The people who pledged them! A shilling for a wedding ring, and the woman says, “I will return on Friday, Mr. Slatch, with the money, and God bless you.” A Friday! A hundred Fridays have passed, and the woman does not return! The husband does not return! The son does not return! They vanish into the fog, into the gutters, into the river! And they leave their sorrows on my shelves!’

He sank into his chair, a collapsed bellows of a man, and pointed a trembling finger at the boy.

‘Your mother,’ he said, in a whisper that was barely a breath. ‘She pledged her locket to me, the night she ran away with your father. A silver locket, with a clasp broken. I gave her a shilling. She said she would return. She did not return. I have the locket. I have kept it. I have kept them all, for they are all I have of the people who went away and never came back. I am not a man, sir; I am a warehouse of other people’s broken promises!’

Tobias looked at his grandfather, and in the watchful grey eyes there sprang a tear—not for the miserable old man, nor for the mother he had never known, but for the battered snuffbox, the miniature portrait, and the infant’s shoes, which seemed, in the dim light of the parlour, to be holding their breath, waiting for a door to open and a familiar voice to say, ‘I have come for my pledge, Mr. Slatch.’

The boy took up the broom, and swept the floor in silence, and Mr. Slatch sat in his chair, rubbing his thumb and forefinger together, as if trying to rub away the memory of a silver locket with a broken clasp, which lay, yellowed ticket and all, in a drawer beneath his bed, waiting, as it had waited for thirty years, for a Friday that never came.
