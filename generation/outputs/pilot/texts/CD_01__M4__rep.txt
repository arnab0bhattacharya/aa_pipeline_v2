The shop stood in a narrow street near Holborn, where the houses leaned together like gossips and the daylight came down thin and grudging, as though it had been pawned and never redeemed. It was a tall, crooked house, three stories high and one room deep, with a face of blackened brick and windows that had not been washed since the reign of King George, and over the door hung three tarnished balls upon a bracket of iron, which creaked in the wind with a sound like a lamentation. In this house dwelt Mr. Absalom Tugg, retired pawnbroker, a gentleman of seventy years and upward, who had not spoken a civil word to any human creature since the death of his wife, and who trusted nobody—not the beadle, not the butcher’s boy, not the parson himself—and who kept his shop-shutters closed and his door barred, and his secrets closer than either.

Thither, upon a raw November afternoon, came a boy of ten, with a bundle under his arm and a label round his neck, as though he had been a parcel of goods consigned by carrier. His name was Timothy Tugg, and he had never in his life beheld his grandfather, nor had his grandfather ever expressed the smallest desire to behold him; for the boy’s father had married against the old man’s will, and had gone away to Bristol and died there of a fever, and the mother had followed him within the year, and so the child had been passed from hand to hand like a bad sixpence, until at last there was nobody left to pass him to but the pawnbroker himself.

The boy knocked, and the knock echoed in the empty street like a musket-shot. A window above rattled up, and a head appeared—a head like a withered apple, with a nightcap on it and a pair of spectacles upon the nose, and eyes behind the spectacles as sharp and cold as the points of a compass.

‘Go away,’ said the head.

‘Please, sir,’ said the boy, ‘I’m Timothy.’

‘I don’t care what you are. Go away.’

‘I’m your grandson, sir, and I’ve come to live with you, and here’s the letter.’

The head considered this for a long time, and the wind blew, and the three balls creaked, and the boy stood in the wet and shivered. At last the window shut with a bang, and there was a great noise of bolts and bars within, and the door opened six inches, and the old man’s face appeared in the gap, and the old man’s hand—a dry, yellow, clutching hand, like the foot of a bird—shot out and took the letter, and the door shut again. Then there was another long silence, and the sound of paper crackling, and a grunt, and the door opened once more.

‘You may come in,’ said Mr. Tugg, ‘but you may not touch anything, and you may not ask questions, and you may not make a noise, and if you do any of those things I shall turn you out into the street, and the parish may have you, for all I care.’

The boy came in, and the door was barred behind him, and he found himself in a dark passage that smelled of dust and old leather and something else besides—something faint and musty and mysterious, like the breath of a tomb. And beyond the passage was the shop.

It was a wonderful shop. It was crammed and heaped and choked with goods—with watches and watch-chains, with rings and brooches, with silver spoons tied up in bundles, with coats and gowns and shawls hanging from the ceiling like bats, with musical boxes and china shepherds and a stuffed parrot in a cage, and with a thousand other things besides, all ticketed and numbered and laid away in rows, and every one of them covered with the dust of years. And in the midst of it all sat Mr. Tugg in a high-backed chair, with a great ledger before him, writing in it with a pen that scratched like a mouse in a wainscot.

‘You will sleep in the attic,’ said Mr. Tugg, without looking up. ‘You will eat what I give you. You will light the fire and sweep the shop and run errands, and you will not speak to me unless I speak to you first. Do you understand?’

‘Yes, sir,’ said Timothy.

‘Then go and wash your face. You look like a drowned rat.’

Now, Timothy was a boy of spirit, and he had not been in the house a week before he began to wonder at the things he saw. For there was a room at the back of the shop, behind a door that was always locked, and through the keyhole of which, when the old man was out, the boy would peep and peep again; and there was a shelf in that room, and on the shelf a row of parcels, each tied up in brown paper and sealed with red wax and labelled with a name and a date. And the dates were all of one year—thirty years back and more—and the names were all different, and the parcels were all of different sizes, and yet there they sat, year after year, and nobody came for them, and the old man never spoke of them, and the dust gathered on them until it lay like a grey fleece upon the brown paper.

‘Grandfather,’ said Timothy one evening—for he had resolved to break the rule, being of a bold and inquiring turn—‘grandfather, what are those parcels in the back room?’

The old man’s pen stopped. He raised his head, and looked at the boy over his spectacles, and his face was very still.

‘What parcels?’ said he.

‘The ones on the shelf. The ones with the names on them.’

‘You have been peeping,’ said Mr. Tugg, in a voice like the shutting of a box. ‘You have been prying and peeping, like your father before you, and I’ll have no prying in this house. Get to bed.’

But the boy did not get to bed. He sat on the stairs in the dark, and he heard the old man go into the back room and lock the door behind him, and he heard him moving about among the parcels, and he heard him—or he thought he heard him—talking to them, low and softly, as a man talks to a child. And then he heard something else: he heard the old man crying.

Timothy sat on the stairs a long time in the dark, and he thought of the parcels, and he thought of the names and the dates, and he thought of the old man crying, and his heart was sore for him. And in the morning, when the old man came down, Timothy had swept the shop and lit the fire and made the tea, and he set the cup before his grandfather and said nothing at all. And the old man looked at him sharply, and then he looked at the tea, and then he sat down and drank it, and that was the first time he had ever drunk tea that his grandson had made for him.

Now, it happened about this time that a certain Mr. Cribb, a stout, red-faced, puffing man, who was a collector of rents and a bully by profession, came to the shop and demanded to see Mr. Tugg upon business. And the business was this: that the house next door but one was to be pulled down, and the houses beyond it, and the whole street, if Mr. Cribb had his way, and Mr. Tugg’s house was to go with the rest, and Mr. Tugg was to be turned out into the street at Lady-day, and a fine new row of shops was to be built in its place, with plate-glass windows and gas-lamps, and the old pawnbroker and his three balls were to be swept away like a cobweb.

‘And a good riddance, too,’ said Mr. Cribb, ‘for the street wants improving, and you’re a nuisance, Mr. Tugg, with your shutters and your dust and your old rubbish. You’ll take the money, and be thankful.’

‘I will not take the money,’ said Mr. Tugg, ‘and I will not be turned out, and I will not be improved, and I will not be a nuisance, and I will not be thankful. I have lived in this house fifty years, and I have kept my pledges, and I will keep them still, and you may pull down the street and build your plate-glass windows, and you may bring the gas-lamps and the fine shops, but you shall not bring me out of this house, not if you pull it down about my ears.’

And Mr. Cribb puffed and reddened and went away in a rage, and the old man sat down in his chair and trembled, and Timothy, who had heard it all from the stairs, came down and stood beside him.

‘Grandfather,’ said the boy, ‘why don’t you give them up?’

‘Give what up?’

‘The parcels. The ones in the back room. They’re only pledges, aren’t they? And if nobody comes for them, and they’re thirty years old, why, you could sell them, and then you’d have the money, and you could go and live somewhere else, and—’

‘Boy,’ said the old man, and his voice was strange, ‘do you know what a pledge is? A pledge is a thing a man leaves with you when he is in trouble, and you keep it for him, and you give him the money, and he goes away and he comes back and he pays you and he takes his thing again, and it is his, and you have kept it for him. And if he does not come back—if he is dead, or gone, or lost—why, then you keep it still, because it is his, and you have no right to it, and you must keep it until he comes or until you die. And I have kept them, and I have kept them thirty years, and I have never sold one, and I never will, and when I die they will be found, and they will be given back to the people they belong to, or to their children, and they will know that Absalom Tugg kept his pledges.’

And the boy looked at the old man, and the old man looked at the boy, and neither of them spoke for a long while.

‘But, grandfather,’ said Timothy at last, ‘what if they never come?’

‘Then I shall have kept them for nothing,’ said the old man, ‘and that is my business, and not yours, and not Mr. Cribb’s, and not the parish’s. And now get you gone and sweep the shop, and don’t let me hear another word about it.’

And Timothy swept the shop, and he thought about the parcels, and he thought about the old man, and he thought about the street, and he made up his mind that he would not let Mr. Cribb have his way, not if he could help it. And how he did it, and what came of it, and whether the parcels were ever claimed, and whether the old man ever learned to trust anybody—all this is another story, and it is not for me to tell it here. But this I will say: that the boy stayed with his grandfather, and the old man grew to love him, and the shop stayed in the narrow street near Holborn, and the three tarnished balls creaked in the wind, and the dust gathered on the parcels in the back room, and the old man kept his pledges to the end.
