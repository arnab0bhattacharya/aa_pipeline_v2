The town of Miraflores—which is to say, the handful of mud-coloured houses, the church with its broken bell, and the long, low building that called itself the Hotel Bolívar—lay in the hollow of a valley so deep that the sun reached it late and left it early, and the mountains that enclosed it stood about in a silence that was less a quality of the air than a pressure upon the spirit. It was here that I first met Mr. Charles Heatherington, C.E., a man of some fifty years, spare, grey, with the stooping shoulders of one who has spent his life over plans and instruments, and with a manner of such elaborate and painful courtesy that it seemed to cost him something to maintain it, as though politeness were a burden laid upon him by his conscience rather than a natural impulse of his heart.

He had come to this republic, he told me, at the invitation of the government—a government whose authority, he observed with a dry and careful smile, was not universally acknowledged—to survey a line of railway through the mountains to the sea. The concession was a matter of some urgency, the promises of civilisation were large, and the money, he understood, was English. He spoke of these things with a certain detachment, as though the railway were a fact of nature rather than a human enterprise, and I perceived that he was a man who had trained himself to see only the technical problem, the grade and the curve, the cutting and the tunnel, and to hold all else at arm's length. It was a discipline, I think, that had become a habit of the soul.

We sat that evening on the verandah of the hotel, in the thick, warm darkness, and he told me of the passes. There were three, he said, and the government held one, and the rebels—for there were rebels, though the word was never spoken aloud in the town—held the other two. The general who commanded them was named Ocampo, and he was, by all accounts, a man of considerable ability and no scruples, who had grasped the simple truth that a railway through the mountains must pass where the mountains permit, and that he who holds the passes holds the railway. "He has sent me messages," Mr. Heatherington said, and paused to light a cigar, the flame of the match illuminating for a moment his long, grave face and the patient, scholarly eyes. "Very courteous messages. He is anxious that the line should be surveyed through the northern pass, which he controls. He speaks of the prosperity of the country, of the benefits of communication, of the future. He is, I gather, a great believer in the future."

"And the government?"

"The government," Mr. Heatherington said, "is equally anxious that the line should go through the southern pass, which is held by their troops. They also speak of the future. They have been good enough to suggest that my services are of the first importance to the republic, and that I should do well to remember that I am, in a sense, their guest." He drew on his cigar, and the tip glowed in the darkness. "It is a delicate position for a man of my profession. I am not a politician. I have no opinions upon the government of this country, which seems to me neither better nor worse than most. I have a line to survey. The line must go where the ground permits, and the ground, unfortunately, permits of two routes. The one is shorter, the other is cheaper. The one is held by the general, the other by the government. I have spent my life in the service of the railway, and I have never before found myself in a position where the railway was expected to serve two masters."

He was silent for a while, and I heard the faint, persistent sound of the river in the valley below, and the distant barking of a dog, and then, from far off, the single note of a guitar, thin and melancholy, as though the night itself were singing. "I have written to the government," he said at last, "to explain that my survey must be impartial, that I must examine both routes, that I can give no promise until I have the facts. I have written to the general to the same effect. I have been careful to use the same words. I have been, I believe, perfectly correct." He sighed, a small, dry sound. "And yet I am aware that correctness is not a quality that is much valued in this part of the world. They do not want a surveyor. They want an ally. They want a man who will say that the railway must go through their pass, and who will say it not because the ground is favourable but because they wish it. And I am afraid that I shall disappoint them."

I asked him what he would do.

"I shall survey both routes," he said, simply. "It is my profession. It is the only thing I know. I shall take my instruments and my chainmen and I shall go up into the mountains, and I shall measure the ground, and I shall make my plans, and I shall report the truth as I find it. And then—" He stopped, and I saw him make a small gesture with his hand, as though he were brushing away a fly. "And then I shall be told that I have made a mistake, that my figures are wrong, that I have been deceived by the priests or the politicians or the general, or that I am a fool or a knave. And the railway will be built where they wish it to be built, and the civilisation will come, and the mountains will be crossed, and the passes will be held by the men who hold them now, and it will be a matter of no importance at all which route was surveyed by a certain English engineer who was too conscientious to tell a lie."

He rose and stood at the edge of the verandah, looking out into the darkness, and his figure, in its loose white clothes, seemed to me for a moment like the figure of a man standing at the edge of a great abyss, contemplating the fall. "I have a wife in England," he said, "and a daughter who is to be married in the spring. I have a small house in a suburb, and a garden that I have not seen for three years, and a pension that will be sufficient for my needs. I am not a young man. I have no ambition left, except to do my work honestly and to return to my garden. And yet I find myself in this valley, in this darkness, in this heat, between a government that flatters me and a general who threatens me, and I am asked to choose between them, and I cannot choose, because I have no opinion of either, and because the only thing I care for is the truth of the ground." He turned and looked at me, and his face was very grave. "It is a strange thing," he said, "to find that the truth is a luxury that no one can afford."

The next morning he was gone, with his instruments and his chainmen, up into the mountains, and I did not see him again for many weeks. When at last he returned, he was thinner and greyer, and there was a look in his eyes that I had not seen before, a look of a man who has been alone with the mountains and has found them indifferent. He told me that he had surveyed both routes, that the northern pass was the better, that he had so reported to the government, and that the government had thanked him and had ordered the line to be built through the southern pass. He told me that the general had sent him a letter of congratulation, in which he had been pleased to say that the English engineer was a man of honour, and that he hoped to have the pleasure of his acquaintance at some future time. He told me that he was going home. "I have done my work," he said. "I have surveyed the ground. The rest is not my affair." And he smiled his dry, careful smile, and shook my hand, and went away.

I have often thought of him since, in that valley of silence and heat, standing at the edge of the verandah and looking out into the darkness, and I have wondered whether he found his garden, and whether the truth of the ground was of any use to him, and whether the railway, when it was built, went through the pass that he had chosen or the pass that had been chosen for him. But I have never learned the answer, and I suppose it does not matter. The mountains are still there, and the passes are still held by the men who hold them, and the railway, if it was built, has become a fact of nature, like the river and the heat, and no one remembers the English engineer who surveyed it, or the general who promised him his friendship, or the government that flattered him and lied.
