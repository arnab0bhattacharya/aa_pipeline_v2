It was the hour when London, having exhausted the pleasures of the afternoon, begins to invent those of the evening, and the drawing-rooms of Mayfair were already glowing with the soft, artificial light that makes cynicism look like wisdom and wisdom look like a rather dull form of charity. Miss Consuelo Vanderbilt—no relation, as she was fond of remarking, to the family with the steamships, though she possessed enough money to have bought the entire Atlantic had it been for sale—sat in a corner of the Duchess of Devonshire’s ballroom, examining the peerage as if it were a menu.

‘He must be a duke,’ she had announced to her maid that morning, with the calm finality of one who orders a carriage. ‘A duke is the only thing in England that cannot be bought outright. Everything else is merely a matter of price.’

‘And what if the dukes are all taken, Miss?’ the maid had asked, in the tone of one who has seen the London season.

‘Then I shall take the one who is left,’ Consuelo replied. ‘I have a great respect for age. It is the only thing that improves without effort.’

She was now engaged in conversation with Lord Arthur Fenton, a young man whose waistcoat was a triumph of embroidery and whose prospects were a tragedy of arithmetic. He was the nephew of the Duke of Dorset, and heir to a title that brought with it all the burdens of nobility and none of its luxuries.

‘I am looking for your uncle, Lord Arthur,’ said Miss Vanderbilt, with a smile that was as bright as the diamonds in her ears. ‘I am told he is the most eligible man in London.’

‘He is certainly the most permanent,’ said Lord Arthur. ‘My uncle is ninety years of age, Miss Vanderbilt, and he has been deaf since the Crimean War. He believes that the world is still waiting for the return of Mr. Gladstone, and he regards the electric light as a personal insult.’

‘How delightful,’ said Consuelo. ‘I am so tired of young men who have opinions about everything and convictions about nothing. Your uncle sounds like a monument. I have always wanted to own a monument.’

‘He is a monument that requires a great deal of polishing,’ said Lord Arthur, looking at her with a sudden, desperate intensity. He had seen the way she looked at the peerage, and he knew that look.
