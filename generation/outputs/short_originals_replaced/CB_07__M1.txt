I had been three weeks in Calder Mill, and I had not yet learned to breathe its air. The smoke lay upon the valley like a shroud, and the ceaseless roar of the water-wheels filled my ears even in my sleep. It was a parish of iron and steam, where the souls of men seemed to be ground to dust as surely as the corn in the mill-hopper. The masters regarded me with a cold, appraising eye, as one regards a servant who must be kept in his place; the men looked at me with a sullen hatred, seeing in my black coat the livery of their oppressor. I was the parson—the master’s man, sent to preach patience to the starving and submission to the crushed.

It was Mrs. Ashworth who first dared to tell me the truth. She kept the parish school in a low-roofed cottage near the river-bank, a woman whose husband had died in the mill, and whose face bore the hard lines of poverty, yet whose eye was as clear and searching as a flame. She was the only person in Calder Mill who did not fear Mr. Vane, the mill-owner, and therefore she was the only person who did not fear me.
