I will confess that I had lived only for this day; that every hope of my soul, every proof I had wrung from reluctant nature, every shilling of my inheritance, had been poured out as a libation upon this single altar. For twenty years I had dwelt in the darkness of the laboratory, a hermit among retorts and crucibles, wrestling with the invisible spirits of life, and now I stood upon the brink of that glory which is the true life of the philosopher, or of that oblivion which is more bitter than the grave.

The Royal Institution was crowded to the very roof; the heat was stifling, the air heavy with the breath of the multitude, yet I felt a chill as of the tomb creeping over my limbs. There sat the sages of the Royal Society, the critics, the fashionable idlers who come to gape at the wonders of science as at the antics of a mountebank. And there, in the front rank, with a smile of youthful insolence upon his lip, sat Mr. Julian Vane—that cold, calculating anatomist, who had risen from obscurity to dispute the throne I had so painfully built.

"You are pale, Doctor," whispered my assistant, a faithful but trembling youth.

"It is the heat," I replied, though my voice sounded hollow and far off in my own ears. "Or perhaps it is the weight of the ages pressing upon me. See how they watch, like vultures waiting for the carrion of my defeat."

My theory of the *Anima Mundi*, that vital fluid which permeates all organized matter, separating the living from the dead stone, had been the torment and the rapture of my existence. I had seen it in the flash of the torpedo, in the contraction of the muscle, in the spark of the mind. But to prove it—to bottle it, to show it dancing in the jar before the eyes of the incredulous—this had ever eluded me, mocking my pursuit like a phantom light in a marsh.

Then Vane rose. He was young, handsome, with a clear, ringing voice that commanded the assembly. He spoke of galvanism, of chemistry, of the mechanical laws of the body. He spoke with a brutal clarity that cut through my dreams like a knife.

"Life," he cried, holding up a vial of clear liquid, "is but a series of chemical reactions. There is no ghost in the machine, no ethereal fluid. There is only matter, and the forces that govern it. Doctor Alistair Finch seeks a spirit where there is only a mechanism."

A murmur of assent swept the room. I felt the blood rush to my face, then recede, leaving me cold. I staggered to the demonstration table. My apparatus stood there, a complex maze of Leyden jars, copper wires, and glass spheres, polished until they shone like the eyes of demons.

"Gentlemen," I began, my voice rising to a pitch of desperate eloquence, "you are mistaken! You look upon the casket and deny the jewel. You see the clay and deny the sculptor. I have spent a life in the pursuit of this essence, and I tell you that within these vessels lies the very breath of the Creator, captured and condensed!"
