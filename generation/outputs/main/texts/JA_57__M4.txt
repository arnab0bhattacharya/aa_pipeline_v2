The affair of the fortune was no sooner settled than it became the burden of every conversation at Lansdown Crescent. Miss Honoria Vellacott, having been but three months in possession of a clear twelve thousand pounds, independent of the reversion of her uncle’s estate in Devon, found herself the object of an attention as sudden as it was universal. Her guardian, Mr. Pemberton, a gentleman whose solicitude for her welfare was equalled only by his distrust of mankind in general, had taken it upon himself to deliver a warning which he considered indispensable.

“My dear Honoria,” said he, one morning, when the breakfast things had been cleared and the two were alone in the drawing-room, “you are now, in point of income, a woman of consequence. I need not tell you that consequence attracts. There is not a young man in Bath who would not consider himself fortunate to secure your affections, and there is not one, I am persuaded, whose affection would survive the loss of your fortune. I speak plainly, for I have seen too much of the world to flatter.”

Honoria, who was two-and-twenty, and had a lively mind and a warm heart, received this counsel with the respect due to its source, though she could not forbear a smile at the solemnity with which it was delivered. “And what would you have me do, sir? Refuse every civility, and live in a garret, that no man may be tempted to admire me?”

“By no means. I would have you test them. There is a method in such matters. Let it be understood that your fortune is lost—that the estate is encumbered, the funds withdrawn—and observe which of your admirers remains constant. The experiment is severe, but it is decisive.”

She considered the proposal for a moment, and the spirit of mischief, which was never long absent from her countenance, began to lend it animation. “And if one should remain?” she asked.

“Then, my dear, you will have found a man who values you for yourself; and though I would not have you marry a fool, I would have you prefer a man of principle to a man of fashion.”

The scheme was accordingly put into execution. Mr. Pemberton, with a gravity that did him honour, gave out at the Pump Room and in the Assembly Rooms that his ward had suffered a reverse of fortune; that the Devon estate was entailed upon a distant cousin, and that Miss Vellacott’s independence was reduced to a pittance of two hundred a year. The report spread with the rapidity peculiar to disagreeable news, and the effect was immediate.

Mr. Augustus Fitzhugh, who had been her most assiduous attendant, and whose conversation had consisted chiefly of compliments upon her taste in music and her elegance in dress, was observed on the following Thursday to be engaged in close conference with Miss Corbin, whose fortune was small but whose connections were respectable. Mr. Henry Danvers, a barrister of promising abilities and an elegant person, who had twice quoted Cowper to her at the bookseller’s, found himself obliged to return to London upon business of sudden importance. And Captain Elliot, whose regiment was quartered at Bath, and who had been heard to declare that he would never marry a woman who could not ride to hounds, discovered that his affections had been transferred to a widow of independent means.

There remained, however, one gentleman whose conduct was not to be accounted for upon any principle of interest. Mr. Silas Marchetti, a man of about thirty, of grave aspect and sparing speech, who had come to Bath for his health, and whose fortune was understood to be moderate, had been the least agreeable of Honoria’s suitors. He was not handsome, he was not gay, and he had once had the audacity to contradict her upon the subject of a novel which she had praised and which he had pronounced to be improbable. He had never paid her a compliment, and she had sometimes suspected that he did not admire her at all. Yet when the news of her ruin was made public, Mr. Marchetti called at Lansdown Crescent the very next morning, and requested to see Miss Vellacott.

She received him in the drawing-room, prepared to be amused. He entered with his usual composure, and after the customary enquiries, which were made with a civility that seemed to her almost impertinent, he said, “I am come, Miss Vellacott, to assure you that the report which I have heard this morning has made no alteration in my sentiments, and to beg that you will do me the honour to accept my hand.”

Honoria was astonished. “You are very good, sir,” she replied, with a smile which she could not repress, “but you are perhaps not aware that I am now a poor woman. My fortune is gone.”

“I am aware of it,” said he, calmly. “It is for that reason that I have come. I could not with propriety have addressed you while you were surrounded by admirers, but I may now hope to be heard without presumption.”

“You are the only one of my admirers, as you call them, who has not fled,” she observed, with some irony. “I confess I did not expect it of you.”

“I am not surprised,” he replied. “I have not the art of making myself agreeable. But I have the honour to be, what I hope you will find, a man of his word.”

She was silent. The experiment had succeeded, but the success was not of a kind to gratify her. The one man who remained was the one man she liked least. He was dull, he was grave, he had contradicted her about a novel. She looked at him with a mixture of perplexity and vexation, and said, “Mr. Marchetti, I am obliged to you, but I cannot accept your offer. I do not love you.”

“I am not so presumptuous as to expect it,” he replied, without any visible emotion. “I ask only to be allowed to hope that in time I may deserve your regard. I am not a man of quick parts, but I am constant.”

“Constancy is a virtue,” she said, “but it is not the only one. I am afraid I should find you very dull.”

“I dare say you would,” said he. “I am dull. But I am not changeable.”

She could not help laughing, though she was vexed. “You are the most provoking man I ever met,” she cried. “You will not be refused, and you will not be agreeable.”

“I will be refused, if you please,” he said, “but I will not be agreeable. I am not able.”

He bowed and withdrew, leaving her in a state of irritation which she found it difficult to account for. She had expected to be triumphant; she was instead discomposed. She had imagined that the man who remained would be the man she could have loved; and she found that the man who remained was the man she had always laughed at. It was not to be borne.

The next day he called again, and the next, and the next. He made no professions, he paid no compliments, he did not even seem to expect that she would relent. He talked to her of books, of politics, of the state of the roads; and she found, to her surprise, that he had opinions, and that they were not foolish. He was not eloquent, but he was sensible; he was not entertaining, but he was sincere. She began to look for his knock at the door, and to be out of humour when it did not come.

Mr. Pemberton, who had watched the progress of the affair with a satisfaction which he did not attempt to conceal, said to her one evening, “Well, my dear, and what do you think of your experiment now?”

“I think,” she replied, with a sigh, “that it is a very dangerous thing to test one’s friends. I have lost a fortune and found a husband.”

“You have found a man of principle,” said Mr. Pemberton. “I have made enquiries. He is not rich, but he is honourable. He will make you a good husband.”

“He will make me a dull one,” she said.

“Dullness is a fault that improves upon acquaintance,” observed her guardian, “whereas wit, I have observed, is apt to wear out.”

She was married to Mr. Marchetti in the following spring, and though she never entirely forgave him for having been the only one of her suitors who had not run away, she was heard to say, some years afterwards, that she had never known a man whose constancy was so little to be doubted, and whose conversation was so much to be valued. The twelve thousand pounds, it is true, had never been lost; but she kept the secret until her dying day, and Mr. Marchetti, who was not a man of quick parts, never discovered it.
