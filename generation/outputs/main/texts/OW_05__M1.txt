The Duchess of Dunmoor had outlived three husbands, which, as she observed to her nephew one afternoon, was a proof of her vitality rather than of their fragility. Society, however, preferred to attribute it to a certain indestructible hardness of heart, a quality she cultivated as carefully as she did the camellias in her conservatory. When she announced her intention of writing her memoirs, the panic that swept through the drawing-rooms of Mayfair was as palpable as a draught from an unfastened window. It was not that she possessed secrets—everybody had secrets; indeed, a secret was as necessary to a modern soul as a pocket is to a coat—but that she possessed a memory, and, what was worse, a complete lack of reticence.

To manage this catastrophe, she summoned her nephew, Mr. Evelyn St. Clair, a young man of exquisite taste and appalling debts. He was to be the editor of the manuscript. It was a position he accepted with the desperate enthusiasm of a man who has been offered a reprieve on the condition that he perform a public execution.

“You must be firm, Evelyn,” said the Duchess, gesturing with a hand weighed down by rubies the colour of arterial blood. “I have written the truth. It is a vulgar thing to do, but I felt it was time someone did it. Society is built upon a scaffolding of lies, and I intend to pull out a few of the supports and see what tumbles. You are to correct my grammar, which I am told is a relic of the Regency, and to remove anything that is merely dull. Scandal is never dull, but anecdotes about the late Duke of Dunmoor’s gout are.”

“And the names, Aunt?” Evelyn asked, his voice slightly hoarse.

“The names are to remain,” she said, with a smile that showed her remarkably white teeth. “Anonymity is the refuge of the cowardly and the second-rate. If I have called the Marchioness of Puddleton a hypocrite, I shall stand by it. She is one. She wears a bonnet to church that is an affront to the Deity and a wig that is an affront to Nature. Such things must be recorded for posterity.”

For a week, Evelyn sat in the library of the Dunmoor town house in Belgravia, a room paneled in mahogany the colour of dried blood and filled with the scent of old leather and dried flowers. He was a man who believed that life should be a work of art, and the Duchess’s prose was a violent splash of vermillion on a canvas of grey silk. She wrote with a savage elegance, her sentences long and balanced like the arms of a chandelier, yet dripping with venom.

He worked through the early chapters—the childhood in Shropshire, the first marriage to the elderly Lord Faversham, who had died of a surfeit of port and disappointment. It was witty, it was cruel, but it was safe. Then he reached the chapters concerning the year 1865.

The manuscript was written in a spidery, violet ink on heavy cream paper. Evelyn read the first paragraph, then the second. The fire in the grate seemed to burn lower, the shadows in the room to lengthen. He recognized the name of his mother, Lady Beatrice St. Clair. She had been a celebrated beauty in her day, a woman of such fragile, porcelain loveliness that men spoke of her as if she were a rare orchid that might wither if looked at too directly.

The Duchess, however, had not looked at her with reverence.

*“Beatrice,”* the memoirs read, *“was possessed of that particular kind of innocence which is only found in women who have made a very careful study of vice. She wept beautifully, which was fortunate, as she had much to weep about, though little of it was sorrow. She wore a dress of white satin to the Duchess of Wiltshire’s ball that made her look like a lily, and indeed, she had the lily’s nature: pale, scentless, and rooted in mud.”*

Evelyn laid the pages down. His hands were trembling. He looked around the room, at the bust of Cicero, at the Sevres vases, as if seeking an ally in the silent objects. He read on.

The Duchess detailed a series of events in the summer of ’65. There was a house party at a country estate, a game of croquet on a lawn that was described as being as green as envy, and a scandal involving a certain Captain Roderick Vane, a man of no fortune but immense personal charm. The Duchess wrote of late-night meetings in the conservatory, of letters hidden in prayer books, and of a diamond necklace that vanished and reappeared in a pawnbroker’s window in Cheapside.

*“My brother-in-law,”* the Duchess wrote of Evelyn’s father, *“was a man who saw the world through a haze of claret and good nature. He did not see that his Beatrice was painting her soul as carefully as her face. It is a mistake to think that men love innocence. They love the appearance of it. They love the theatre of it. Beatrice was a superb actress, and the tragedy was that she was playing a part she did not know she had written.”*

The memoirs went on to describe a confrontation. It seemed the Duchess had discovered the truth of the necklace and the Captain. She had not blackmailed her sister-in-law—that would have been mercenary and beneath her. Instead, she had simply watched. She had recorded the dates, the times, the specific shade of the rose Beatrice wore in her hair the night she ruined herself.

Evelyn sat back, the leather chair creaking. The woman he had known as his mother—gentle, pious, obsessed with the welfare of the poor and the cut of her silks—was being dismembered on the page. It was not merely that she had been unfaithful; that was a common tragedy. It was that she had been *clever* about it. She had traded her virtue for a set of emeralds and a few months of passion with a man who, as the Duchess noted with brutal precision, had the calves of a groom and the morals of a weasel.

He heard the rustle of silk in the hallway. The Duchess entered, leaning on a cane topped with a large opal. She looked at Evelyn, then at the open manuscript.

“Ah,” she said, her voice dry as autumn leaves. “You have reached the chapter on the Green Summer. I thought the description of the croquet lawn was particularly fine. I have always felt that croquet is a game that reveals character. It requires a certain ruthlessness to drive a ball through the hoop of one’s opponent.”

“Aunt,” Evelyn said, his voice tight. “This is my mother.”

“She was my sister-in-law,” the Duchess corrected, settling herself into a chair opposite him. “And a very tiresome one. She was so dreadfully moral in public. It is the public morality that I object to, Evelyn. If one is going to be wicked, one should do it with a certain flair, not with this hypocritical draping of white lace. She ruined her life for a pair of emeralds and a man with hairy hands. It is a trade I would not have made. I would have held out for rubies.”

“You cannot publish this,” Evelyn said. “It will destroy my family. It will destroy me.”

The Duchess looked at him, her eyes like chips of flint. “My dear boy, I have outlived three husbands and the reputation of half the House of Lords. I am not interested in preserving things. I am interested in the truth. The truth is that your mother was a fraud, a lovely, weeping fraud, and that your father was a fool. It is a very common arrangement, but it makes for excellent reading.”

“And if I refuse to edit it?”

“Then I shall find someone who will,” she said smoothly. “There is a young man from the *Pall Mall Gazette* who has been asking about my memoirs. He has a taste for the sensational. But I thought it would be kinder to have the family involved. One likes to keep these things in the house.”

Evelyn looked at the manuscript. The violet ink seemed to glow in the dim light. He realized then that the Duchess was not writing history; she was writing a verdict. She was the judge, the jury, and the executioner, and she had decided that the world was a stage, and she would be the one to pull down the curtain.

“I shall correct the grammar,” Evelyn said quietly. “But I cannot change the facts.”

“I did not ask you to change the facts,” the Duchess said, smiling. “I only asked you to make them sound beautiful. Truth without style is merely gossip, and gossip without style is merely malice. But truth with style, Evelyn—that is art.”

She rose and moved toward the door, her jewels clicking softly. “Do finish the chapter by dinner. I am having the Bishop of London to dine, and I intend to read him the passage about the croquet. He has a terrible backhand, and I think he will recognize the description.”
