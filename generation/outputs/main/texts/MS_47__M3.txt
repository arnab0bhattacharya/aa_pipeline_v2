The vessel that bore me towards the Iberian shore had weathered a tempest of such savage magnificence that, even as the clouds dissevered and the billows subsided into a sullen swell, I remained upon the deck, my gaze fixed upon the horizon, as if to wrest from the retiring storm some omen of my future fortunes. I had left behind the melancholy relics of my native England, where the diminishing remnants of a once-vast populace wandered like spectres through the silent, grass-grown streets of London, abandoned by the world and bereft of hope. The pestilence that had traversed Europe with the swiftness of a desert wind had spared none, till at length a whispered rumour, borne upon the lips of dying men and fading letters, reached my solitary ear: that in Lisbon, the city of the seven hills, a remedy had been found, a sovereign balm wrested from the very vitals of the earth, or perhaps from the deep repositories of ancient science. As a physician, whose days and nights had been consumed in a fruitless warfare against the invisible foe, I was stung by an impatience that would not be quelled; I gathered my remaining resources, and, committing myself to the treacherous deep, voyaged southward with a heart vibrating between the keenest anguish and the most exalted expectation. 

It was upon a morning of surpassing loveliness, when the sun rose in cloudless majesty over the waters of the Tagus, that the city first disclosed itself to my longing eyes. It stood arrayed in a splendour that seemed to mock the desolation of the world, its white edifices gleaming with an almost unnatural purity upon the verdant slopes, its towers and domes catching the early light as if bathed in liquid gold. No smoke of funeral pyres ascended from its midst, no spectral silence reigned along its quays; rather, a singular and profound tranquillity pervaded the scene, so unlike the distracted misery to which I had grown accustomed, that I felt my eyes swell with sudden, burning tears. I leaned upon the vessel’s prow, stretching out my arms, as if to embrace the serene vision, and breathed a prayer of thanksgiving to the Providence that had guided me to this sanctuary. Yet, even in that first rapture of hope, a subtle, indefinable shadow crept over my spirit; the calm was too unbroken, the order too perfect, resembling less the blessed refuge of suffering humanity than a sealed and sleeping mausoleum, where the dead, in marble majesty, repose forever.

When I disembarked upon the broad marble quay, I was immediately struck by the singular deportment of the inhabitants. They moved about their affairs with a measured precision, a tranquil regularity, that spoke of a society governed by principles unknown to the distracted nations of the north. The women, attired in garments of a simple yet elegant design, bore themselves with a gentle dignity, their countenances, though pale, unruffled by the passionate grief that had furrowed the cheeks of my countrymen; the men walked with a calm, deliberate tread, their eyes never flashing with the feverish fire of despair, nor sinking to the earth beneath the weight of unutterable sorrow. I sought to address a passerby, a man of venerable appearance, whose aspect seemed to invite confidence; I conjured him, in the name of suffering humanity, to direct me to the sages who had discovered the remedy, to the halls where the healing art was practised. He paused, and regarded me with a look of mild, uninquiring serenity, such as one might bestow upon a wayward child; and then, in accents of melodious softness, he bade me welcome to the city, advising me to seek repose at a designated edifice, and to present myself upon the morrow before the Council of Health. He spoke not of the remedy, nor of the pestilence, nor of the world beyond the river’s mouth; and when I pressed him, with an eloquence born of agonizing need, his eyes only expressed a tranquil compassion, and he gently withdrew, leaving me to marvel at a composure that seemed less the offspring of security than of some profound, unnatural surrender.

The ensuing days were a torment of suspense, a crucible in which my hopes were alternately raised to the most soaring pitch, and dashed to the earth with a violence that threatened to shatter my reason. I was conducted, with a punctilious civility that mocked my burning impatience, through spacious halls and silent corridors, where the functionaries of the city received me with bows of studied grace. I was questioned, not concerning the ravages of the pestilence, but upon the state of the roads, the ruins of ancient cities, the wild beasts that had reclaimed the deserted countryside; and when I turned the discourse to the object of my mission, I was met with evasions so bland, so courteously impenetrable, that I felt myself a captive in a net of silk. At length, after interminable delays, I was ushered into the presence of the Chief Magistrate, a man whose noble and commanding aspect seemed to promise a soul capable of great resolves. His name was Valerius; and as he sat upon a raised seat, beneath a canopy of crimson velvet, I thought I had never beheld a countenance so expressive of intellectual power, yet so marred by an air of melancholy inflexibility. I poured forth my supplication; I painted, in the glowing colours of impassioned truth, the miseries of England, the tears of widows, the untimely graves of the young and the beautiful; I conjured him, by all that was sacred, to impart the secret which might snatch a remnant of the human race from extinction. 

He heard me to the end, his eyes fixed upon my face with a gaze of profound attention, his brow clouded by a sombre shadow; and when I had concluded, a silence fell between us, a silence so profound that I could hear the beating of my own over-wrought heart. Then, in a voice of measured, melancholy sweetness, he spoke. He spoke of the pestilence not as a transient calamity, but as a necessary visitation, a purifying flame through which the world must pass, ere it could be regenerated; he spoke of the remedy, not as a gift to be bestowed upon a dying world, but as a sacred trust, reserved for those who had been found worthy to inherit the renovated earth. He said that the nations of Europe, in their pride and tumultuous folly, had brought the destruction upon themselves, and that to extend the remedy to them would be to perpetuate the errors, the crimes, the unceasing strife that had called down the judgement of Heaven. He conjured me to remain in Lisbon, to abandon the vain hope of recalling the dead to life, and to dedicate my remaining years to the cultivation of tranquillity, to the contemplation of a world slowly settling into an ordered and perpetual repose. 

As he spoke, a cold horror crept over my frame; the vault of the hall seemed to close upon me, and the air grew thick and suffocating. I saw, with a dreadful clearness, that this calm, this order, this serenity, was but the mask of a selfish, a monstrous despair; that these people, secure in their isolation, had looked upon the agonies of a perishing world with the cold apathy of stoics, and had resolved to purchase their own survival at the price of universal ruin. I raised my voice; I denounced them, in the impassioned language of a heart bursting with indignation and grief; I called upon Heaven to witness their cruelty, their inhuman forbearance; I threatened, I implored, I wept—but all in vain. Valerius regarded me with the same melancholy, unwavering gaze; and when my strength was exhausted, and I sank back, overcome by the impotence of my own wrath, he waved his hand, and I was conducted, with the same silent civility, from his presence. I found myself once more upon the marble quay, the broad expanse of the Tagus gleaming before me, the white city rising in its beauty upon the hills; but the glory was departed from the scene. I saw only a splendid prison, a city of the dead, where life was preserved, indeed, but where the soul had perished. I turned my eyes towards the north, towards the desolate land where my brethren languished and died; and a cry of anguish, of unutterable, unavailing woe, burst from my lips, as I felt the full weight of my failure, the utter hopelessness of my solitary, unheeded mission.
