It was upon a raw November morning in the year 1894 that my friend Mr. Novak called upon me at my chambers in Ashley Street. I had known him in my Indian days, but he was now a chaplain at the Royal Naval Hospital at Haslar, and his face, usually placid and ruddy, bore the marks of some profound agitation.

"It is a most extraordinary business, Maddox," said he, sitting forward in his chair with his hat clasped tightly in his hands. "I have a puzzle which touches neither the body nor the soul, but rather the dark corners of a man's conscience."

I bade him state his case, which he did with a crispness that belied his cloth.

A sailor named Joseph Ffoulkes had been brought into Haslar a fortnight earlier, suffering from a fever contracted in the East. He was a grizzled, weather-beaten man, with a blue anchor tattooed upon his left wrist. He was dying, and knew it. In his final hours, the nurses found him clutching a small object in his right hand, which he refused to relinquish. He raved without ceasing, his mind wandering among the tropics, but one phrase he repeated with terrible clarity: "The debt to the Alderman. Tell the Alderman in England. The ivory settles it."

When Ffoulkes at last expired, the object was taken from his rigid fingers. It proved to be a flat disc of carved ivory, about the breadth of a crown piece. Upon one side was engraved the crest of a diving falcon, and beneath it, the initials "J. S." Upon the reverse was a date—"1872"—and a number, "417."

"I made what inquiries I could," Novak continued. "I traced the crest to a family of the name of Saunderson, in the Midlands. The head of that house is Alderman Sir John Saunderson, a wealthy ship-owner residing at Greythorpe Hall, near the town of Letchford. He is a man of great local standing, a magistrate, and a subscriber to many charities."

"You wrote to him?"

"I did better. I went to him. I took the ivory disc with me. I was shown into his study—a fine, oak-panelled room, hung with pictures of shipping. Sir John is a broad, heavy man, with a clean-shaven face and small, shrewd, grey eyes. I stated my errand plainly. I told him of Ffoulkes, of the dying words, and of the token. I laid the disc upon his desk."

Novak paused, and passed a hand over his brow.

"Well?" I asked.

"He looked at it, and I saw his hand tighten upon the edge of the desk. The colour left his face—quite visibly, Maddox, as though a tap had been turned. Then he recovered himself with an effort that was almost painful to witness. He said, 'I have no knowledge of this man or this trinket. There is some mistake. Pray leave it with me, and I will make inquiries.' His manner was cold, but his left hand, which rested upon the blotting-pad, was trembling. I left the token with him, for I had no claim to it, and returned to Haslar. But the thing has preyed upon my mind, Maddox. I am convinced that the Alderman lied to me, and that he is frightened."

I considered the matter for a moment. "What is your object in coming to me?"

"I wish you to go to Greythorpe. I cannot rest until I know the truth. You have a gift for observation, and a way with men. I am but a poor chaplain, and I was dismissed."

I confess that the affair intrigued me. The following morning found me alighting from the local train at Letchford, a brisk market town set upon a ridge of red sandstone. The air was sharp, and a thin rain was falling, streaking the carriage windows as I drove out through the town to Greythorpe Hall.

The house was a solid, Queen Anne building of dark brick, standing in formal gardens. I sent in my card, with a note stating that I was making inquiries on behalf of the Royal Naval Hospital. I was shown into the same oak-panelled study which Novak had described, and there, after a few minutes, Sir John Saunderson joined me.

He was all that my friend had represented: a big, florid man, dressed in a dark tweed suit, with a heavy gold watch-chain stretched across his waistcoat. His eyes were watchful, and his mouth set in a firm line.

"You are from Haslar, Mr. Maddox?" said he, in a deep, measured voice. "I fear I cannot add to what I told Mr. Novak. The matter is a mystery to me."

"I am sure it is, Sir John," said I. "But the dead man's words were very specific. He spoke of a debt. Perhaps I might ask you a question or two, if you will bear with me."

He inclined his head, but did not offer me a seat. He wished to keep the interview short.

"You were in business in the East before you returned to England in the late seventies?"

"I was. I had interests in the Straits Settlements. I returned home in 1878."

"And Joseph Ffoulkes? Does the name recall nothing?"

"Nothing whatever."

"He was a bosun, Sir John. He served on vessels trading out of Singapore." I paused, then added: "He had a very singular tattoo upon his left wrist—a blue anchor, with a serpent coiled about its stock."

I had invented the serpent. It was a trick of my trade. The effect was instantaneous and damning. Sir John's eyes dilated, and his right hand, which had been resting on the mantelpiece, gave a violent twitch.

"I know nothing of tattoos," he said, but his voice had risen half an octave, and there was a sheen of moisture upon his upper lip.

I looked past him, over his shoulder, at the row of bound shipping ledgers upon the top shelf. One of them, a thick, calf-bound volume, bore the date "1872" in faded gilt upon its spine. It was pulled out a quarter of an inch from its fellows.

"You have a fine set of ledgers there, Sir John," I remarked. "Do they cover the year 1872?"

He swung round, and I saw his gaze dart to the shelf. Then he collected himself.

"They are private accounts. They have no bearing upon your inquiry."

"Perhaps not," I said. "But I observe that the volume for 1872 has been recently disturbed. The dust upon its upper edge has been newly smudged. You have consulted it since Mr. Novak's visit, Sir John. Why?"

His face darkened. "You are exceeding your authority, sir. This is my house. If you have a charge to make, make it. Otherwise, I must ask you to leave."

"I have no charge," I answered. "But I will tell you what I observe. You are a man who has lived in the tropics, yet your right hand is perfectly white, while your left is weathered to the colour of teak. You wear a signet ring upon the left hand—a curious habit for a right-handed man. I fancy that ring once bore the Saunderson crest, but you have had it altered. The diving falcon has been filed away. You wear it now upon the left hand, so that the blank surface is hidden against the finger. The ivory disc which Ffoulkes held bore that crest. It was a token, Sir John—a token of a partnership, or a bond, struck in 1872, when you were both young men in the East."

He stepped back from me, and his face was the colour of clay.

"Ffoulkes knew something," I continued. "He carried that token to his death. He spoke of a debt. I do not think it was a debt of money, Sir John. I think it was a debt of blood."

The room was very still. The fire ticked in the grate. Sir John Saunderson sank slowly into a chair, and covered his face with his hands.

"You are a dangerous man, Mr. Maddox," he whispered, through his fingers.

"I am a patient one," I answered. "Tell me the truth, and it shall go no further than this room, unless the law requires it."

He was silent for a long minute. Then he spoke, in a low, hurried voice, as one who unburdens himself of a weight carried too long.

"Ffoulkes was my partner in a trading venture in the Straits. In 1872, there was a rising among the coolies upon our plantation at Pontian. Ffoulkes had beaten a man to death for some trifling theft. The man's kin came for him in the night. I was there. I saw Ffoulkes strike the first blow. But it was my knife that was found in the body. Ffoulkes swore he would hang us both if I did not take the blame. I fled. I bribed my way out of the colony. I left everything—my name, my trade, my partner. I carved that token of ivory from the tusk of an elephant I had shot, and I gave it to Ffoulkes. It was a bond between us. I owed him my silence, and he owed me mine."

"And the debt?" I asked.

"The debt was this. He knew I had prospered. He had not. He wrote to me, three years ago, demanding money. I paid. He demanded more. I refused. He threatened to come to England, to expose me. And now he is dead, and the token is in my desk, and I am free."

He looked up at me with the eyes of a hunted man.

"Are you?" I asked.

I left him there, sitting in his fine study, with his ledgers upon the shelf and his secret laid bare. I had no warrant, no authority, and no desire to prosecute. The dead man was beyond the law's reach, and the living was bound in chains of his own forging.

But as my carriage turned out of the gates of Greythorpe Hall, I glanced back through the rain-streaked glass. A figure stood at the study window, motionless, watching me go. And I knew that the debt of Joseph Ffoulkes was not yet settled, and that Sir John Saunderson would carry its weight to his grave.
