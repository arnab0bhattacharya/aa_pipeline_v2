Miss Clara Vernon had long enjoyed, in her own estimation, the reputation of being a remarkably candid young woman. She considered plain dealing a virtue, and as she was not deficient in quickness of observation, she found frequent opportunities for its exercise. In the small town of Plumford, Kent, where society was rather limited and rather eager to be amused, her frankness was generally received with a mixture of amusement and alarm. Her mother, a gentlewoman of more complaisant temper, often sighed and wished her daughter would cultivate a little more reserve; but Clara maintained that disguising one’s opinions was a species of deceit she could never adopt.

It was therefore with great readiness that she gave her judgement upon the recent marriage of Mr. and Mrs. Frederick Ashworth. Mr. Ashworth was a man of some property in the neighbourhood, who, having passed his fortieth year, had surprised all Plumford by selecting for his wife a Miss Latimer, a young woman of obscure connections and very moderate fortune, who had resided for the last twelvemonth with an aunt in the town. The match was generally allowed to be imprudent, and the bride was pronounced by several ladies to be decidedly inferior in manner; but as nobody liked to offend Mr. Ashworth, the subject was handled with a degree of caution which Clara found both hypocritical and tiresome.

She was not long in expressing herself with more freedom. Meeting with a small party at the house of Mrs. Browning, a lady of considerable consequence in Plumford, Clara found herself drawn into a discussion of the new couple, who had but lately returned from their wedding journey. Mrs. Browning, with that sort of cautious enquiry which passes for civility, asked whether her guests had observed Mrs. Ashworth’s dress at church the preceding Sunday.

“I observed it very particularly,” said Clara, “and I must own I was struck. She wore a pelisse of crimson velvet, which could only have been intended to convey an impression of consequence she has no other means of supporting. A woman who has been six months in the neighbourhood without forming a single desirable acquaintance, and who is now married to a man who might have looked so much higher, need not wonder if people find something absurd in her pretensions.”

Mrs. Browning looked down and gently said that she did not like to judge hastily. Miss Hayward, another young lady present, changed the subject with some awkwardness, and Clara was left to infer that she had said more than was agreeable, a conclusion which, had she been less satisfied with her own discernment, might have produced a degree of caution.

A day or two afterwards she was walking with her younger sister, Eleanor, when they encountered a gentleman upon the turnpike road whom Clara had never seen before. He was young, remarkably handsome, and possessed of an air which conveyed, without any appearance of studied elegance, the impression of a man who had seen much of the best society. He raised his hat as they passed, and Eleanor, who was better informed than her sister in some particulars of local intelligence, coloured and looked eager.

“Who is that?” Clara demanded, as soon as he was out of hearing.

“That is Mr. Edmund Grey,” replied Eleanor, with a consciousness she could not entirely suppress. “He is lately come to stay with his uncle, General Grey, at the Grange. I have heard that he is intended for Parliament, and that he has a very considerable fortune.”

Clara said nothing, but she was not the less inclined to think that here was a man whose good opinion would be worth securing. She had heard the General spoken of as a man of influence in the county, and she knew that his nephew, if he entered upon public life, must become a person of some importance. She determined, without acknowledging the determination even to herself, that she would make herself agreeable.

The opportunity presented itself sooner than she had expected. General Grey gave a dinner party the following week, and the Vernons were included in the invitation. Clara, who was really a girl of considerable understanding and no inconsiderable charm when she chose to exert it, took pains with her appearance and her conversation, and was rewarded by finding herself seated next to Mr. Grey. He conversed with an ease and good sense which she found highly agreeable. He spoke of books, of public affairs, and of the county, with a degree of moderation and knowledge which satisfied her judgement; and when he addressed a particular remark to her with an air of marked attention, she felt a pleasure which she had seldom experienced in the society of Plumford.

Emboldened by his agreeableness, and yielding to her natural propensity for free discussion, she ventured, when the conversation turned to neighbourhood news, to introduce the subject of the Ashworths. She repeated, with some embellishment and not a little of her usual emphasis, the substance of what she had said at Mrs. Browning’s. She dwelt upon the meanness of the bride’s connections, the absurdity of her pretensions, and the imprudence of Mr. Ashworth’s choice. She spoke with all the fluency of strong conviction, and concluded by declaring that she, for one, could never bring herself to pay attention to a woman who had nothing to recommend her but the circumstance of having caught a man who ought to have known better.

Mr. Grey heard her in silence. He did not smile, nor did he look offended; but there was a restraint in his manner which Clara, in the warmth of her discourse, did not immediately perceive. When she had finished, he only said, with a degree of composure which she thought rather cold, “You are, I perceive, very severe in your judgements, Miss Vernon. I have seldom found that people whose circumstances are narrow are the less entitled to consideration on that account. Indeed, my own experience has led me to think that they are frequently the most deserving of it.”

Clara felt a little abashed, but she was not silenced. She attempted some qualification of her former remarks, but the conversation had lost its ease, and Mr. Grey soon afterwards turned to address the lady on his other side.

It was Eleanor who, later in the evening, imparted the information which completed Clara’s mortification. They were standing near the window of the drawing room, and Mr. Grey was on the opposite side of the apartment, engaged in conversation with General Grey.

“Clara,” said Eleanor, in a voice of some agitation, “do you know whom you were speaking of so freely to Mr. Grey?”

“Mrs. Ashworth, to be sure. And what of it?”

“Do you know who Mrs. Ashworth is?”

“I know she was a Miss Latimer, with nothing to recommend her.”

“She was a Miss Latimer, certainly; but do you know nothing of her family?”

“Nothing at all, except that it is nobody.”

Eleanor hesitated; then, with a look of genuine concern, she said, “She is Mr. Grey’s sister.”

The colour rushed to Clara’s face. For a moment she could not speak. She turned from the window and looked across the room to where Mr. Grey was standing, and the recollection of what she had said, and of the composed coldness with which he had received it, rushed upon her with a force which left her no room for self-justification. She saw, with a clearness which she had never before possessed, that what she had called candour had been merely the indulgence of her own love of superiority; that she had spoken of a woman she did not know, in terms which she could not justify; and that she had done so before the very man whose good opinion she had most at heart. The reflection was not a comfortable one.

She said nothing more that evening. She bore her part in the remaining civilities of the party with a composure which cost her no small effort, and when they returned home she sat long in her own room, turning over in her mind the events of the evening, and endeavouring to reconcile her former confidence in her own judgement with the painful conviction which now occupied its place.

Mrs. Vernon, who had observed her daughter’s silence, attributed it to fatigue, and said no more than a mother’s kindness suggested. Eleanor alone suspected the truth, and she was too wise and too gentle to intrude upon a distress which she knew must be its own cure.

The next morning brought a note from Mrs. Browning, couched in terms of friendly enquiry, but containing a piece of information which Clara received with a degree of interest she had never before felt for any communication from that quarter. Mrs. Ashworth, it appeared, was shortly to be introduced into the General’s family, and Mr. Grey had already expressed his intention of showing his sister every attention which her new position in the neighbourhood demanded.

Clara laid down the note and was silent. She had learned, at length, that frankness without kindness is a virtue very nearly allied to folly, and that the opinions we are most ready to utter are sometimes those which it most concerns us to keep.
