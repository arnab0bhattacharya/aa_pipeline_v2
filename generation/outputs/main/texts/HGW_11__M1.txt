The mule track to the Socavón de las Ánimas was, for the last three leagues, little more than a scar upon the face of the mountain, a treacherous thread of loose scree and frozen mud that clung to the precipice with an optimism I could not share. My name is Arthur Penhaligon, and I am, or was, a mining engineer of some standing, engaged by the Valparaíso Consolidated to assess the prospects of a certain remote property in the Cordillera Blanca. The property, a silver mine of considerable antiquity and recent silence, had been purchased by a Dr. Silas Croft, a man of scientific reputation whose correspondence with the company had been both brilliant and infuriatingly vague. He spoke of ‘novel extraction techniques’ and ‘geological catalysts’, phrases which to a practical man mean little more than a clever way of losing money.

I found the mine not in the bustle of a working enterprise, but in a sepulchral quiet. The great stamping mill was still, its iron heads furred with rust. The ore chutes were choked with drifts of snow. Only the entrance to the main adit showed signs of life, a neat, timbered portal from which a steady, warm draught issued, carrying with it a strange, earthy smell, like a cellar after rain, but with a sharp, mineral undertone. It was there that I met Dr. Croft. He was not the wild-eyed prospector I had pictured, but a tall, spare man in his late fifties, with the pale, indoor complexion of a scholar and eyes that burned with a feverish, proprietary light. He wore a clean linen shirt and corduroy trousers, and his hands, though stained with some dark, ochre-like substance, were the hands of a man who worked with instruments, not a shovel.

“Mr. Penhaligon,” he said, his voice precise and without warmth. “You have come a long way. I trust the journey was not too arduous. Your company is… persistent.”

“Dr. Croft. I am here to see the mine and report on its viability,” I replied, shaking his hand. His grip was surprisingly strong. “The silence on the property is a cause for concern.”

“Silence is the mother of discovery,” he countered, with a faint, dry smile. “And conventional methods of prospecting are… inadequate here. Come. I will show you what has occupied my time.”

He led me not into the main shaft, but into a smaller, side tunnel. The air grew warmer and damper as we descended. The timbering was newer, the workmanship impeccable. But it was the rock itself that held my attention. The walls, which should have been solid quartzite and schist, were veined with a network of what I first took for a peculiar, pale lichen. It was a filigree of fine, white threads, no thicker than a horsehair, that spread across the stone in a delicate, crystalline pattern.

“Interesting formation,” I remarked, running a gloved hand over the surface. “Some kind of mineral deposit?”

“Not a deposit, Mr. Penhaligon. A process,” Croft said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “A living process. That is *Lithophaga prima*. My creation.”

He took a small, pointed steel pick from his belt and, with a few sharp taps, chipped away a piece of the tunnel wall. It came away with a dry, brittle sound, like a piece of rotten wood. He handed me the fragment. It was astonishingly light. I turned it over in my fingers. The stone was porous, riddled with the same fine white threads, and it crumbled to a gritty powder with the slightest pressure.

“Good God,” I breathed. “The rock… it’s been consumed.”

“Precisely!” Croft’s eyes shone with triumph. “It is a fungus, of a most remarkable order. It secretes an enzyme that breaks down silicates and carbonates, converting the very substance of the mountain into this friable material, which it then absorbs for its own growth. It eats the rock, Mr. Penhaligon. It eats the rock.”

I stared at him, my practical mind struggling to assimilate the sheer, monstrous scale of the idea. A fungus that ate rock. The implications were staggering, terrifying. “And the silver?”

“Ah, the silver!” he chuckled. “The fungus has no interest in metals. They are left behind, concentrated in the powder. A simple sluicing operation is all that is required. No blasting, no dangerous shafts, no costly timbering. We simply… digest the mountain and pan the residue.”

He led me deeper, into a vast cavern where his ‘process’ was on full display. The air was thick with the smell of damp earth and ozone. The walls of the cavern were not walls at all, but a soft, yielding substance, like packed soil, shot through with the white mycelial threads. In the centre of the cavern, the fungus grew in great, pale, shelf-like structures that glowed with a faint, internal luminescence. It was a sight of profound and unnatural beauty, and of profound and unnatural danger.

“It is magnificent, is it not?” Croft said, gesturing to the slow, silent consumption of the Andes. “A biological engine of unparalleled efficiency. With this, I will reshape the very foundations of industry. But it must be done with care. With secrecy.”

“Secrecy?” I said, finding my voice at last. “Dr. Croft, this is not a mining technique. This is a cataclysm. You have introduced a new principle into geology. What is to stop it from spreading? What happens when it reaches the surface? What happens when it gets into the soil, the water, the foundations of the world?”

He waved a dismissive hand. “It is a simple organism. It requires a high-silicate environment. It will not trouble the topsoil. Its spread is slow, and I have the means to check it. I have developed an inhibitor, a copper-sulphate solution. But I need help, Mr. Penhaligon. I need an engineer’s mind to help me manage the yields, to build the sluices, to contain the… the operation. The Valparaíso company will have its silver, and more than it ever dreamed of. But this discovery, this *Lithophaga*, must remain my own. Our own.”

He looked at me, his gaze intense, pleading, and utterly mad. He was offering me a share in a miracle and a catastrophe. He was offering me a fortune and a terrible complicity.

“There is no ‘our own’, Dr. Croft,” I said, my voice shaking with a horror that was slowly dawning upon me. “This is not a secret to be kept. It is a plague to be reported.”

His face, which had been alight with messianic zeal, hardened into a mask of cold fury. “You do not understand. You are a man of picks and pulleys. You cannot see the grandeur of it.”

“I see the end of the world in it,” I retorted, backing away towards the tunnel entrance. “I am leaving. And when I reach the coast, I will report everything.”

Croft did not move to stop me. He simply smiled, a thin, chilling expression. “You are mistaken, Mr. Penhaligon. You will not be leaving.”

He reached out and pressed a lever on a small, brass contraption I had not noticed before. There was a grinding sound, and from the ceiling of the cavern, a fine, white powder began to sift down, enveloping me in a choking cloud. It was the dust of consumed rock, and it was thick with the spores of his terrible creation. I fell to my knees, coughing, my lungs burning. Through the haze, I saw Croft turn and walk calmly back towards the deeper recesses of the mine, leaving me in the heart of his living tomb. The last sound I heard was the soft, sibilant whisper of the fungus, patiently, endlessly, eating the mountain around me.
