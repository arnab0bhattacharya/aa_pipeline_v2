The morning had been so remarkably mild for October, and the walk to the village so thoroughly agreeable, that Mrs. Pendleton and her three daughters were in unusually high spirits when they returned to Stockwood Lodge. They found the breakfast parlour in a state of some disorder, the tea things neglected, and Mr. Pendleton standing before the fire with a letter in his hand and a countenance of uncommon perplexity.

Mrs. Pendleton, who had been anticipating the pleasure of relating a piece of interesting village news, was obliged to suspend her narrative. She looked at her husband, then at the letter, and sat down with a degree of composure that was meant to indicate her readiness for anything.

"You are quite white, my dear," said she. "I hope it is not bad news from your sister."

"My sister has nothing to do with it," replied Mr. Pendleton, with a shortness very foreign to his usual manner. "It is from Robert."

The effect of this name upon the company was immediate. Eleanor, the eldest, who had been pouring out her father's chocolate, set down the cup with a trembling hand; Cecilia, the second, turned her whole attention from a new bonnet which she had been trimming; and Maria, the youngest, who had hitherto been more occupied with the village news than anything domestic, fixed her eyes upon her father with eager curiosity.

"Robert!" repeated Mrs. Pendleton. "Surely, Mr. Pendleton, you cannot mean your brother Robert."

"I do not know any other Robert who would write to me," returned her husband, "and I must own I am surprised at hearing from this one. He writes from Bristol, and informs me that he has lately returned from the West Indies, where he has been these fifteen years and more, and that he purposes waiting upon us next week."

"Next week!" cried Mrs. Pendleton, sinking into her chair. "Good heavens! And what are we to do with him?"

This was a question which Mr. Pendleton did not appear disposed to answer. He laid the letter on the table, walked to the window, and stood looking out at the dripping trees, as if he expected to find in the autumn landscape some solution to the difficulties of his brother's return.

The truth was, that Mr. Robert Pendleton had quarrelled with his family some sixteen years before, in a manner so decisive that all correspondence had ceased between them. What the quarrel had been about, no one at Stockwood could now say with any certainty. Mr. Pendleton himself had always been remarkably reticent on the subject, and Mrs. Pendleton, who had not been married at the time, had only the most general notion that it had involved some disagreement over money and some expressions of mutual contempt. The sisters, who had grown up in the firm belief that their uncle was a sort of wicked relation existing only to be spoken of in whispers, had each formed their own private conjectures, which they now began to impart with considerable freedom.

"I have always understood," said Eleanor, who was of a serious turn and read more improving books than her sisters, "that my uncle was a man of very dissipated habits, and that he went abroad in consequence of debts which he could not pay. If this fortune, of which he speaks, should be the result of many years of patient industry and reformed conduct, I think we ought to receive him with every mark of kindness and forgiveness."

Cecilia, who was generally allowed to be the beauty of the family, and who therefore looked at everything with reference to its effect upon appearances, had her own objections to this theory.

"My dear Eleanor, you are always supposing that people are reformed. I see no reason why my uncle should not have been always respectable, and merely unfortunate. For my part, I think it much more likely that he was treated with some injustice, and that he has come back to be reconciled. We must be very careful to show him proper attention, for if he is really rich, you know, he may be of the greatest consequence to us."

This observation, which touched upon a subject always near to Cecilia's heart, was received in silence by Eleanor, who did not entirely approve of her sister's motives, and with a laugh by Maria, who never thought of motives at all.

"I dare say he is a very agreeable old gentleman," said Maria, "and will bring us presents from the Indies. I hope he has got some shawls, at any rate. But what I want to know is, what was the quarrel about? Papa, you have never told us."

Mr. Pendleton turned from the window with an air of mingled impatience and reluctance.

"The quarrel, Maria, was about a matter which I do not choose to discuss with my daughters," said he. "It is sufficient that my brother and I differed, and that we have not spoken since. If he now wishes to renew our acquaintance, I am not the man to refuse him, but I own I did not expect it."

Mrs. Pendleton, who had been thinking all this while rather than listening, now entered upon the subject with her accustomed practical good sense.

"Well, my dear," said she, "it is no use wondering what the quarrel was about, for I am sure if you two brothers could forget it, that would be quite enough. The question is, what does he want? A man who has made a fortune abroad does not come to Somerset merely for the pleasure of seeing his relations. He must have some design. Perhaps he means to buy an estate in the neighbourhood."

"My dear," returned Mr. Pendleton, rather drily, "my brother's designs are entirely his own. He says he wishes to see me, and to renew the intercourse which has been so long interrupted. I see no reason to suppose he has anything further in view."

"But he must have something further in view," persisted Mrs. Pendleton, who was not of a temper to believe that anyone acted without a motive. "A man of forty-five, with a fortune, does not travel about the country for nothing. Perhaps he means to marry again."

This suggestion, which had not occurred to any of the sisters, was received with much interest. Maria immediately began to wonder whether their uncle would bring a wife with him, and Cecilia to consider whether a new aunt would be an acquisition or an encumbrance. Eleanor alone did not join in the speculation. She was thinking of her father's countenance when he had spoken of his brother, and of something in the letter which lay upon the table, which she could not read but which seemed to her to imply a gravity beyond a mere visit of ceremony.

The question of the uncle's intentions was still under debate when the Stockwood carriage, returning from the village, brought back the intelligence that a gentleman had been seen at the inn, who was reported to have lately arrived from the Indies and to be making inquiries about the road to Stockwood. Whether this were Mr. Robert Pendleton or not, no one could say with certainty, but the coincidence was sufficiently striking to put the whole family into a flutter.

Mrs. Pendleton immediately began to consider whether the best bed-chamber was in order, and whether the damask curtains had been properly aired. Cecilia went to change her morning dress, for she declared she would not be found in a dimity gown if her uncle should arrive before dinner. Maria ran to the window to watch the lane. Eleanor alone remained seated, and her father, catching her eye as he passed through the room, gave her a look which seemed to say that he, at least, did not expect much happiness from the meeting.

Whether the stranger at the inn were their uncle or not, the family was in too great a state of expectation to settle to anything. Mrs. Pendleton found fault with the arrangements of the morning room, discovered that the silver candlesticks wanted cleaning, and sent for the housekeeper, with whom she was closeted for half an hour. Cecilia, having dressed herself to her satisfaction, sat down to the pianoforte, but played only fragments of tunes, and kept looking towards the window. Maria, who had no patience for music, walked in the shrubbery, and started at every sound of wheels.

It was nearly four o'clock, and the autumn light was already beginning to fail, when a post-chaise was heard upon the gravel. The sound brought everyone to the hall. Mr. Pendleton, who had been sitting alone in his study, came out with a composed but grave air. Mrs. Pendleton arranged her cap, Cecilia smoothed her dress, and Maria fairly ran to the door.

The chaise stopped. The steps were let down. A gentleman of middle age, plainly but well dressed, with a brown complexion and a quiet, observant eye, alighted. He looked up at the house, then at the group assembled at the door, and advancing with a firm step, took off his hat.

"William," said he, "I believe you did not expect me so soon."

Mr. Pendleton advanced, and the brothers met with a civility so restrained that it was plain the sixteen years of silence had not been wholly forgotten. Mrs. Pendleton made her curtsy, the sisters theirs, and Mr. Robert Pendleton, having bowed to each with a precision that seemed the result of long absence from English society, was conducted into the house.

Whatever theories the sisters had formed, they were all, for the present, obliged to acknowledge that their uncle was not precisely what they had imagined. He was neither dissipated nor unfortunate nor particularly romantic. He was a plain, sensible-looking man, who appeared to have lived a great deal in hot climates, and to have contracted a habit of saying very little. Whether this reserve concealed a design of buying an estate, of marrying, or of making some handsome provision for his nieces, was a question which the evening did not resolve. He sat at dinner opposite his brother, ate moderately, drank a glass of wine, and answered all questions with brevity. When Mrs. Pendleton ventured to ask whether he found England much altered, he replied that it was not so much altered as he had expected, and that the roads were better than he remembered them.

It was left to Maria, whose curiosity was stronger than her discretion, to attempt something more direct.

"Did you bring us anything from the Indies, sir?" said she, with a frankness which made her mother shudder and her sisters blush.

Robert Pendleton looked at her for a moment with an expression which might have been amusement, and said that he had brought a few curiosities, which he should have the pleasure of showing when his trunks were opened. Whether these curiosities included shawls, he did not say; and Maria, though much disappointed, was obliged to be satisfied.

The evening passed without any further illumination. Mr. Robert Pendleton retired early, pleading the fatigues of his journey, and the family was left to its conjectures. Mrs. Pendleton, who was never sanguine, declared that she could make nothing of him. Cecilia thought him respectable but dull. Maria, whose hopes of the shawls had been revived, pronounced him a very good sort of man. Eleanor said nothing, but went to her father, who was sitting alone, and pressing his hand, inquired whether she might not hope that this visit would end in something pleasant.

Mr. Pendleton shook his head. "I cannot tell, my love," said he. "Robert and I have accounts to settle which are not of a kind to be settled in a day. But he is my brother, and he is welcome."
