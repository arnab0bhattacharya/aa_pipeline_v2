It was with a sinking of the heart that Priscilla Vane first saw the gabled roof of her uncle’s house, rising like a stern grey thought above the elms of Wraxford. At sixteen, with the world suddenly narrowed by her father’s ruin from a prospect of cheerful prosperity to a single, grim necessity, she was disposed to see the whole of her future in the unrelieved slate and stone of Mr. Pomeroy’s residence. The house, which stood a little apart from the town, in a quiet lane where the cathedral spire was a constant, distant needle pricking the sky, seemed to her the very embodiment of its owner. She had met her uncle, Mr. Silas Pomeroy, the solicitor, only twice before, and on both occasions he had presented to her imagination the figure of a man who had been drained of all human moisture by the dry dust of his legal parchments. He was thin, precise in his movements, and possessed of a silence so profound that it seemed less a habit than a constitutional condition, like the stillness of deep water.

Her expectations of misery were, in the first weeks, amply fulfilled. The house was as silent as its master, its rooms smelling of beeswax and the faint, acid odour of old paper. Her duties were simple and unvarying: to manage the one maid, a woman of few words named Mrs. Gedge; to see to the ordering of plain, substantial meals; and to render the house orderly and quiet, which was to say, to render it unchanged from the state in which she found it. Mr. Pomeroy’s routine was a fixed orbit. He breakfasted at eight, read the newspaper with a furrowed brow, and departed for his office in the shadow of the cathedral. He returned at six, dined in solitary state, and then, as Priscilla assumed, retired to his study to pore over deeds and conveyances until he went to bed. He spoke to her only of necessities—the state of the larder, the need for coal, a question about the mending of his linen—and his voice, when he used it, was as dry and rustling as the leaves of a legal tome. Priscilla felt herself to be not so much a niece as a new and efficient piece of household furniture, a functional object whose feelings were as irrelevant as the polish on the mahogany sideboard.

The slow thaw began not with a word, but with a sound. One evening in late November, as a bitter wind drove sleet against the windowpanes of the parlour where she sat with her needlework, she thought she heard a faint murmur from her uncle’s study. It was not the monotonous drone of reading a letter or a will; it had a cadence, a rise and fall that was strangely musical. Curiosity, a feeling that had been dormant in her for weeks, stirred within her. She crept to the study door, which was slightly ajar, and peered through the crack. Mr. Pomeroy was seated in his great leather chair, his back to her, a single candle casting a pool of light on the desk before him. He was not reading a legal document. The slim volume in his hand was bound not in brown calf but in a faded blue morocco. And the words that reached her, whispered in that dry voice, were not of fee simple or contingent remainder.

“*She walks in beauty, like the night / Of cloudless climes and starry skies…*”

Priscilla drew back, her heart beating with a strange, quickened rhythm. It was as if she had discovered a secret spring in the heart of a barren rock. The man who could quote Lord Byron in a whisper was not the man she had taken him to be. This discovery, once made, altered the very light in which she saw him. She began to observe him more closely, and to notice things that had before been mere facts without significance. She saw how, in the evenings, he would sometimes take a book from the shelf—not a volume of law, but a collection of essays or a history—and then, after a moment, replace it with a sigh, as if the act of reading for pleasure were a self-indulgence he could not quite permit. She noted the way his eye would sometimes rest on the portrait of a young woman in a simple white muslin dress that hung in the hall—a portrait so old and faint that Priscilla had barely registered it. The woman had a gentle, intelligent face, and a smile that seemed to hold a question.

The revelation, when it came, was quiet and cumulative, like the falling of snow. Mrs. Gedge, whose loquacity was inversely proportional to her master’s, let fall a few significant crumbs of information. The lady in the portrait was Miss Eleanor Vance, who had been engaged to Mr. Pomeroy thirty years ago. She had died of a fever in the spring of the year they were to be married. After that, Mr. Pomeroy had never spoken of her, and had lived as they saw him now. Priscilla felt a sudden, piercing sympathy that transformed her earlier resentment into something akin to tenderness. His silence was not emptiness; it was a chamber sealed off, containing a grief so profound and so long-endured that it had petrified his entire outward life. The dry lawyer, the man of parchments and precedents, was merely the shell that had formed over a wound that had never healed. His secret reading of poetry was not a hobby; it was a form of communion, a way of keeping alive a part of himself that had died with Eleanor Vance.

One snowy afternoon, finding him at his desk and seemingly unoccupied, Priscilla, emboldened by a courage she did not know she possessed, ventured a question. “Uncle,” she said, her voice sounding thin in the quiet room, “do you think the snow will lie?”

Mr. Pomeroy looked up, his pale eyes seeming to focus on her with an effort. “It is likely, child. The frost is set in.” He was about to return to his papers when Priscilla, with her heart in her mouth, added, “It puts one in mind of Mr. Wordsworth. ‘*And all the air a solemn stillness holds…*’”

A stillness, more solemn than any the snow could bring, fell upon the room. Mr. Pomeroy stared at her, his face a mask of astonishment. For a long moment, she feared she had made a terrible mistake, that she had trespassed on forbidden ground. Then, a change came over his features, a softening so slight that it was almost imperceptible, like the first crack in the ice on a winter river. He did not smile, but the deep lines of his face seemed to rearrange themselves into a pattern less severe. “You are fond of poetry, Priscilla?” he asked, and his voice, for the first time, held a note of genuine inquiry.

“Yes, Uncle,” she said simply.

He nodded slowly, his gaze returning to the window where the snowflakes were now falling in a thick, silent curtain. “It is a great comfort,” he said, so low she barely heard him, “in a world that can be very cold.” He said no more, and soon returned to his work, but the atmosphere between them was irrevocably altered. The wall of ice had been breached, not by a dramatic thaw, but by a single, shared verse. Priscilla went about her duties that evening with a lightened spirit. She understood now that she had not been sent to a prison, but to a place of quiet sorrow, and that her role was not merely to keep house, but perhaps, in some small way, to warm it. The uncle she had dreaded was a man who had loved deeply and lost, and who had carried his grief in a silence as vast and as patient as the winter sky. And in that knowledge, her own misfortunes seemed lighter, her own future a little less bleak.
