It was universally admitted in London society that the Duchess of Stourfield had outlived her three husbands with consummate artistry. Lord Amias had succumbed to a chill caught in his own shooting box, which was a thorough lack of endurance on his part; the second, a wealthy brewer elevated to the peerage, had perished in a hunting accident, thereby proving that commerce and horses should never be mixed; and the third, a distinguished diplomat, had simply faded away during a particularly tedious ambassadorial dinner in Vienna, a feat for which the Duchess was generally envied. Having achieved the absolute pinnacle of widowhood, she found herself, at the age of eighty, confronted with the terrible prospect of having nothing left to do. She had ruled the political salons of Mayfair for half a century, destroyed the reputations of three cabinet ministers, and made the arranging of orchids a competitive blood sport. To remain idle was to become merely decorative, and the Duchess had always preferred to be dangerous.

"I have decided to write my memoirs," she announced to her nephew, Lord Valerius, one rainy Tuesday afternoon in her amber-hued drawing room in Belgrave Square.

Valerius, who was a remarkably handsome young man with a profound appreciation for his own inheritance and an equally profound terror of his aunt, paused in the act of lighting a gold-tipped cigarette. "My dear aunt," he murmured, adopting that tone of languid indifference which he had practiced for hours in his dressing-glass, "memoirs are terribly middle-class. They are usually written by people who have failed at living and wish to succeed at talking."

"On the contrary, my dear boy," said the Duchess, adjusting a magnificent rope of black pearls that rested against her aging throat as comfortably as venom rests upon a flower. "They are written by those who have succeeded at living, and wish to punish those who have merely existed. Society has bored me for forty years. I intend to return the compliment."

Valerius felt a sudden, icy premonition. The Duchess’s memory was notoriously encyclopedically precise regarding the sins of others, a quality she disguised beneath a cloud of lavender and lace. Every family in London, from the haughty Marquesses of Belhaven to the newly rich American duchesses, held their breath in an agony of anticipation. For a society that thrived entirely upon secrecy, hypocrisy, and the polite withholding of truth, a truthful woman of eighty was a more terrifying spectacle than a cholera epidemic.

"I suppose," Valerius ventured carefully, watching a thin blue spiral of smoke ascend toward the painted ceiling, "that you will require someone to edit the manuscript. The public is so easily shocked. A misplaced comma can ruin a reputation just as thoroughly as a misplaced kiss."

"Precisely," the Duchess replied, her dark eyes gleaming with a predatory amusement. "I am told one must omit things for the sake of public decency. I cannot think why. Decency has never done anything for me. But you shall edit it, Valerius. You have a beautiful handwriting, a flawless taste in waistcoats, and, above all, a vested interest in ensuring that I do not libel the Queen. The royalties, naturally, shall go toward restoring the conservatory."

It was a command, not a request. Two days later, a heavy wooden dispatch box, bound in tarnished brass, was delivered to Valerius’s rooms in Mount Street. He waited until the autumn dusk had settled over the chimney pots of Mayfair, poured himself a generous measure of brandy-and-soda from a cut-glass decanter, and, with the trembling fingers of a man defusing a bomb, lifted the lid.

The manuscript was bound in crimson morocco, a colour that Valerius thought unnecessarily martial. He turned the first few pages, finding them delightfully anodyne. The Duchess wrote of her childhood in the country, of governesses who wept over Italian poetry, and of her presentation at Court, where the candlelight had caught the silver thread in her gown and the cold avarice in the eyes of the Prince Consort. Her prose was elegant, polished, and as sharp as a stiletto wrapped in a silk handkerchief. Valerius began to relax. Perhaps the memoirs were merely to be a chronicle of fashion and political small-talk. Perhaps the terror that had gripped the drawing-rooms of London was merely a symptom of collective guilt.

He turned a page of thick, cream-laid paper and stopped. The brandy-glass halted halfway to his lips. He read the paragraph once, then again, a cold sweat breaking out upon his forehead.

The chapter was titled merely *Chapter the Seventh: On the Inconstancy of Matrons*. In it, the Duchess related, with the clinical detachment of an anatomist and the relish of a gourmet, the details of an illicit liaison that had taken place in the winter of 1862. It appeared that the second husband—the brewer, whose memory was generally associated with a splendid cellar and a profound deafness—had possessed a romantic rival. The rival, a man of considerable title and negligible morality, had been a frequent guest at the Stourfield country estate in Wiltshire.

Valerius read on, his heart beating a frantic, irregular rhythm against his ribs. The Duchess described the architecture of the affair with devastating precision: the secret notes concealed in the binding of prayer books, the midnight assignations in the orangery, the scent of jasmine that clung to the gentleman’s coat as he returned to the breakfast table, protesting that he had merely been taking the night air. It was a masterpiece of scandalous revelation. But it was not the generalized immorality that struck the young lord like a physical blow. It was a single, devastating footnote, appended in the Duchess’s angular, unforgiving hand.

*The gentleman in question, whose passion for horticulture was matched only by his passion for his neighbour’s wife, was the late Duke of Ravenscroft, father to the present Marchioness of Valerius.*

Valerius stared at the name. The Marchioness of Valerius was his own mother, a woman of stainless, terrifying virtue, who kept a daily journal of her good works, attended morning service with unvarying regularity, and who considered the wearing of diamonds before dinner to be a sign of moral decay. She was a pillar of society, a monument to propriety, and, if the Duchess’s manuscript was to be believed, the illegitimate daughter of a brewer’s wife and a philandering Duke. His grandfather, the estimable, deaf brewer, had been thoroughly, comprehensively cuckolded, and the Duchess had known for thirty years and kept it, like a rare vintage, waiting to be uncorked at the precise moment that would cause the maximum amount of destruction.

Valerius sprang to his feet and began to pace the Persian carpet, his composure entirely shattered. The brandy-and-soda sat forgotten, its ice melting into a tepid, insipid pool. To publish was to destroy his mother, to drag the proud name of Valerius through the very deepest gutters of public ridicule. But to suppress the chapter was to thwart his aunt, an act of rebellion that was, in the hierarchy of London sins, considerably more unforgivable than a mere irregular birth.

He returned to the manuscript, searching desperately for a loophole, a phrase that might be reinterpreted, a fact that might be altered. But the Duchess’s prose was impervious to doubt. She had not merely insinuated; she had declared. She had laid out the evidence with the cold finality of a judge passing a death sentence.

He picked up his pen, his hand trembling. He crossed out a line, then another, but the ink seemed to bleed through the paper, mocking his attempts at censorship. He realized, with a sinking horror, that the truth was a disease that could not be cured by a mere stroke of a pen. The Duchess had trapped him. If he edited the truth, he was a party to his own family’s ruin. If he destroyed the chapter, he risked the inheritance that was his sole comfort in a world that demanded expensive waistcoats and fine claret.

He looked at the fire, which was blazing cheerfully in the grate, a warm, inviting, destructive force. He could burn the pages. He could claim they had been lost in the post, or eaten by a dog, or mislaid by a careless servant. But he knew, even as he considered it, that the Duchess would simply write them again. She possessed a memory that was entirely devoid of charity and entirely impervious to loss. The truth was out, and it was sitting in his drawing room, bound in crimson morocco, waiting to devour his future.

He sank back into his armchair, his handsome face pale with despair. The clock on the mantelpiece chimed the half-hour, a delicate, crystalline sound that seemed to mark the passing of his family’s reputation. He looked again at the fatal page, at the neat, angular handwriting that had so casually detonated a bomb beneath the foundations of his life.

"Nothing is serious nowadays," he murmured to himself, echoing a fashionable axiom he had heard at the club only the night before. "At least, nothing should be."

But as he sat there, staring into the crimson morocco binding, he knew that some things were terribly, irreversibly serious. And that his aunt, the Duchess of Stourfield, had just proven herself to be the most serious woman in England.
