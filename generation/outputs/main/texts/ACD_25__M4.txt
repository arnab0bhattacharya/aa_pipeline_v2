The rain had beaten against the windows of the smoking-room of the Merchants’ Club for the better part of an hour, and I had begun to think that my old friend Mr. Alaric Vane had forgotten our appointment, when the door opened and he came in with the brisk, dripping energy of a man who has walked rather than ridden through a storm. He shook the water from his coat, declined the waiter’s offer of a fire, and sat down opposite me with the air of one who has something on his mind and is not certain how to be rid of it.

“You are a barrister, Vane,” said I, “and I am a man of no profession but curiosity. Yet I fancy that you have come to me because your own profession has failed you.”

“You are not far wrong,” he answered, drawing a folded paper from his inner pocket. “I have a client—or rather, a client’s brother—who is in a very awkward position. He is a police constable in Surrey, a steady fellow by the name of Hollis, and his sister, Ellen, was until Tuesday last a lady’s maid in the household of Sir James and Lady Pemberton, at Ashcombe Grange, near Godalming. On Monday night Lady Pemberton’s diamond necklace—a thing of considerable value, I understand—disappeared from her dressing-table. The house was searched, the servants were questioned, and by Tuesday morning Ellen Hollis was dismissed without a character and with a very plain intimation that if she showed her face in the neighbourhood again she would be arrested.”

“And the necklace?”

“Has not been found. Nor has any trace of it been discovered. The girl swears she never touched it, and her brother believes her. He came to me because he had heard that I had some experience in these matters, and I came to you because I have none.”

I confess that the story interested me, for it had about it that quality of the apparently simple which so often conceals the deeply intricate. “Tell me all that you know,” said I, “and spare no detail, however trifling.”

Vane spread the paper upon his knee. “I have set it down as Hollis gave it to me. The household at Ashcombe Grange consists of Sir James, Lady Pemberton, their son, a young man of three-and-twenty who is something of a gambler, I gather, and a niece, Miss Agatha Pemberton, who is an orphan and dependent upon her uncle’s bounty. There are, besides, the butler, a man named Craddock, who has been with the family twenty years; a cook; two housemaids; and the lady’s maid, Ellen Hollis. On Monday evening Lady Pemberton wore the necklace at dinner. She retired at half-past ten, and placed the necklace, as was her custom, in its case upon the dressing-table. At seven the next morning the case was empty. The window was found unfastened, but there were no marks of a ladder upon the sill, and the ground beneath was a flower-bed which showed no footprint. The necklace was insured, I may add, with the Mercantile and General Office.”

“When was the policy effected?”

“That is the curious part of it. It was increased—the amount doubled, in fact—only a week before the loss.”

I sat forward in my chair. “Who effected the increase?”

“Lady Pemberton herself. She wrote to the office from the Grange, and the endorsement was sent down and signed in the presence of Craddock, the butler, as witness.”

“And the girl Hollis—what manner is she?”

“Hollis describes her as quiet, plain-featured, and devoted to her mistress. She had been in Lady Pemberton’s service three years, and had never before been accused of the slightest dishonesty.”

I rose and took my hat from the peg. “I think,” said I, “that we might do worse than run down to Godalming to-morrow morning. But first I should like to see the brother, and to have a word with the insurance office.”

The affair, as it unfolded, proved to be one of those cases in which the obvious is at once the most tempting and the most dangerous. The constable, a broad-shouldered, honest-faced fellow, met us at the station and drove us to the Grange in a dog-cart, and on the way he gave us his sister’s account in her own words.

“She says, sir, that her mistress was in high spirits that Monday evening—more than common, for she had been troubled about money matters, as Ellen thought, though she never spoke of them. And there was a gentleman called in the afternoon—a Mr. Lestock, a tall, dark man, with a limp, who had been in the house once or twice before, and who talked with Sir James in the library for above an hour. Ellen took in the tea, and she noticed that Mr. Lestock’s eyes followed her mistress in a way she did not like. And that night, when she went up to her mistress’s room to draw the curtains, Lady Pemberton was standing before the glass with the necklace in her hands, and she said, ‘Ellen, I think I shall wear it to-morrow, after all.’ And then she put it back in the case and locked it herself—which was not her custom, for generally she left the key in the lock.”

“She locked it herself,” I repeated. “And the key?”

“She kept it under her pillow, sir. Ellen says she saw her put it there.”

At the Grange we were received by Sir James, a florid, uneasy man, who seemed more anxious to have the matter done with than to have it solved, and by Lady Pemberton, a handsome, imperious woman, whose manner was that of one who is accustomed to be believed and does not much care whether she is. She repeated the story of the loss with a fluency that suggested rehearsal, and when I asked whether she had any suspicion of the maid, she said, “I have no suspicion of anyone. I only know that the necklace is gone, and that the girl was the last person in the room.”

“You locked the case yourself, I understand.”

“I did.”

“And the key was under your pillow?”

“It was.”

“Was the pillow disturbed?”

She hesitated. “I—I do not know. I did not think to look.”

I asked leave to examine the room, and she conducted me thither herself. It was a large, airy chamber, with a dressing-table between the windows, and a bed with a heavy silk coverlet. I went down upon my knees and examined the carpet, which was a plain drugget, and then the boards about the dressing-table. There was nothing to be seen but a little dust, which had been recently disturbed near the fireplace, as though a chair had been moved. I looked at the window; the fastening was a common one, and could be opened from without by a thin blade. I turned to Lady Pemberton, who stood watching me with a curious fixity.

“You have had the necklace valued lately?” I asked.

“I—I believe the insurance was increased,” she said. “My husband saw to it.”

“It was you who wrote to the office, I think.”

Her colour rose. “I do not see what that has to do with the matter.”

“It has this to do with it,” said I, “that a necklace which is insured for double its value is a very tempting thing to a woman who is pressed for money, and a very dangerous thing to a maid who is known to have been the last in the room.”

She turned away with a little gesture of contempt, but I saw her hands clench at her sides.

That evening I sent a telegram to London, and the next morning there came to the inn where we were staying a reply which confirmed my suspicion. The Mercantile and General had received, on the very morning of the loss, a letter from Lady Pemberton withdrawing her claim, upon the ground that the necklace had been mislaid and was now recovered. No such letter had been posted by her, and the handwriting was not hers. It was, however, in the hand of a man who had been in the house on the Monday afternoon—Mr. Lestock.

I laid the telegram before Vane. “The necklace was never stolen,” said I. “It was pawned, or sold, and the insurance was to cover the loss. But the girl Hollis was in the way, and so she was dismissed. The letter to the insurance office was written by Lestock, who is a confederate, and who limped because he had hurt his foot in getting over the garden wall—not to steal the necklace, but to post the letter which was to make the fraud complete. You will find the necklace in a pawnbroker’s shop in the Borough, and you will find Mr. Lestock at the address which I have written upon this card. I should recommend that you take the constable with you, and that you say nothing to Lady Pemberton until you have the necklace in your hand.”

Vane looked at me with something like admiration. “You have done in a day,” said he, “what the whole of Scotland Yard might have bungled over for a month.”

“It is not Scotland Yard’s fault,” I answered, “but the fault of the obvious. The obvious is always the last thing that a policeman thinks of, because it is the first thing that everyone else thinks of. The maid was dismissed because she was the last in the room, and the last in the room is always the guilty one—until you find that the last in the room was the one who locked the case and put the key under her pillow. Good morning, Vane. I shall be glad to hear that Ellen Hollis is reinstated, and that Mr. Lestock is enjoying the hospitality of the Surrey constabulary.”
