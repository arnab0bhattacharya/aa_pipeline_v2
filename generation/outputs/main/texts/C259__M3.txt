The rumour had arrived in Selchester not by telegram or newspaper, but on the lips of boys returning from the railway station, and by dusk it had filled every twisting lane of the cathedral city. A meteor, said to be as broad as the cathedral spire itself, would strike the earth at midnight. By nine o’clock the cathedral bells had ceased their practice, the pubs had emptied of all but the most determined drinkers, and a hush had settled over the close, as though the old city were holding its breath.

In the drawing room of Canon Eadric Holmwood’s house on Vicars’ Lane, the family had gathered with no clear idea of what to do. The Canon himself stood at the window, watching the November dark, his hands clasped behind his back. He was a man who had spent thirty years cultivating an appearance of unflappable decency, and the prospect of annihilation found him ill-equipped. His wife, Beatrix, sat by the fire with her embroidery abandoned in her lap. Their elder son, Charles, home from London for the weekend, stood by the mantelpiece with a glass of port he had not touched. Their daughter, Margaret, aged twenty-two, sat in the corner with a book she was not reading. And in the armchair nearest the door sat Aunt Vivienne, Beatrix’s elder sister, who had come for Michaelmas and never quite left.

It was Aunt Vivienne who broke the silence. She had a low, theatrical voice that carried the residue of a youth spent upon amateur stages, and she spoke with the practiced ease of a woman who had long ceased to fear embarrassment.

‘If this is our last night,’ she said, ‘I should like to die without pretence. I have been carrying on with Major Thorneby since the regatta. Everyone suspected, I know, but I should like to say it plainly.’

The fire popped. Nobody moved for a moment. Then Beatrix looked up from her embroidery with an expression that was not shock but something closer to weary recognition.

‘Vivienne,’ she said, ‘you are sixty-one years old.’

‘I am,’ said Vivienne, with a small, defiant smile. ‘And so is he.’

Charles let out a sound that might have been a laugh or a cough. He set down his port. ‘Well,’ he said, ‘since we are apparently confessing — I have not been working at the Foreign Office for the last eight months. I was dismissed in March. I have been living in a boarding house in Bloomsbury and borrowing money from my friend Alistair, which I cannot repay.’

The Canon turned from the window. His face in the gaslight was grey and old. ‘Dismissed?’ he said. ‘For what?’

‘For persistent lateness, attendance, and — they used the phrase moral instability. I have been drinking, Father. Rather a lot.’

The Canon opened his mouth, then closed it. He seemed about to speak, then instead he crossed to his armchair and sat down heavily. ‘I see,’ he said, to no one in particular.

Margaret, in her corner, had lowered her book. She looked at her brother with something that was not pity but a kind of sudden, sharp kinship. ‘I was not sent down from Girton for illness,’ she said quietly. ‘I was sent down because I wrote a series of essays arguing that the Church of England was an instrument of patriarchal control, and I read portions of them aloud at a college debate. The principal said I had brought the institution into disrepute.’

Beatrix stared at her daughter. ‘You told us it was pneumonia.’

‘I told you what I thought you wanted to hear.’

The room settled into a strange, suspended quiet. The fire burned. Outside, a dog barked somewhere in the close, and the sound was so ordinary it seemed absurd.

It was Beatrix who spoke next. She had been looking at her embroidery, and when she raised her head there was something in her face that her family had not seen before — a kind of tired, patient honesty.

‘I never wanted to marry your father,’ she said. ‘I wanted to marry a man named Edward Pell, who was a schoolteacher in Dorset. My parents would not permit it. He was not suitable. I married Eadric because I was told to, and I have tried to be a good wife, and I believe I have been, but I have never forgotten Edward, and I have written to him every Christmas for thirty years. He has written back. I keep his letters in the bottom drawer of my writing desk.’

The Canon stared at his wife. ‘Beatrix,’ he said.

‘I am sorry, Eadric. But if the world is ending, I find I cannot bear to carry it any longer.’

The Canon was silent for a long time. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked its steady, indifferent rhythm. At last he spoke, and his voice had lost its pulpit resonance. It was the voice of a tired man.

‘I have doubted God for fifteen years,’ he said. ‘Not in any dramatic fashion. I have simply woken each morning and found the faith was not there, and I have put on my surplice and gone to the altar and said the words anyway, because I did not know what else to do, and because I thought that if I continued, the belief might return. It has not returned. I have been a fraud for half my life, and I am not sure I would change it even now.’

Nobody spoke. The fire had burned low, and the room was growing cold. Charles crossed to the grate and knelt to add coal, and for a while there was only the sound of the fire catching, the scrape of the poker.

Margaret closed her book. ‘What time is it?’ she said.

The Canon looked at the clock. ‘Five past eleven,’ he said.

They waited. There was nothing else to do. Aunt Vivienne dozed in her chair. Beatrix took up her embroidery and began to stitch, though her eyes were wet and she could not seem to follow the pattern. Charles sat on the floor beside the fire, his back against the fender, and Margaret came and sat beside him, and for the first time in years they sat together like children, shoulder touching, without self-consciousness.

The Canon remained in his chair, staring at the fire.

At a quarter to midnight, the cathedral clock began to chime. They all listened. The sound rolled out across the close, deep and measured, and it seemed to each of them that they could feel it in the floorboards, in their chests. Twelve strokes. Then silence.

They waited for the end. The fire burned. The clock ticked. Outside, the November wind stirred the bare branches of the elms along Vicars’ Lane.

Nothing happened.

Minutes passed. A quarter of an hour. Half an hour. The Canon rose and went to the window. The stars were out, cold and distant, arranged in their old familiar patterns. No meteor. No fire. No end.

He turned back to the room. His family was looking at him, and he at them, and he saw that each of them wore the same expression — a strange, bewildered shame, as though they had all undressed in public and only now realised the crowd was still there.

‘Well,’ said Aunt Vivienne, waking with a start. ‘Did I miss it?’

Nobody answered. Charles stood and brushed the ash from his trousers. Margaret picked up her book. Beatrix continued her stitching with trembling hands.

They would have to go on now. They would have to wake tomorrow and sit across from one another at breakfast, and all that had been said would still be true, and they would have to find a way to live with it.

The Canon went to the door. ‘I shall ring for Mrs. Hodge to bring tea,’ he said, and his voice was steady, and very tired.
