To any observer unacquainted with the inward history of the Birkett family, the prospect of Matthew Birkett emigrating to the Canadas would have seemed not only natural but imperative. He was a man of nine-and-twenty, with a frame robust enough to wrestle with the unyielding roots of a new world, and a mind possessing that calculating patience which, in the slow arithmetic of rural life, compounds small savings into eventual independence. For Matthew had been offered employment in the establishment of a timber merchant near Toronto—a connection forged by the brother of his late employer in Ollerton—who had written of vast forests yielding their wealth to the diligent, and of a society where a man’s worth was measured not by the pedigree of his grandfather but by the calloused strength of his own hands. It was the very gateway to fortune; and fortune, to a man conscious of his own powers, wears an aspect of irresistible necessity.

But the Birkett history contained a counterweight to this rational ambition, one which, in its silent, unalterable presence, threatened to sink the whole enterprise of Matthew’s hope. It lay in the person of his younger brother, Thomas.

Thomas was now five-and-twenty, with the physical stature of a man but the bewildered, fleeting consciousness of a child of seven. He had been born with a mind that had simply ceased its formation before arriving at any substantial notion of sequence or consequence; he could feed the hens and carry water from the pump, but he could not count beyond ten, nor follow the narrative of a simple parable, nor remember from one day to the next the face of the butcher’s boy. He was not idle, for his hands were never entirely at rest; he would spend hours picking burrs from the wool of the old sheepdog, or arranging coloured stones in lines along the windowsill, finding in such repetitive orderings a tranquil, animal satisfaction. Yet he required constant oversight, lest his innocent fascination with fire lead him to the hearth, or his inability to distinguish between a shilling and a farthing leave him defrauded in the market square.

It was the memory of their mother, Mary Birkett, that formed the invisible chain around Matthew’s wrists. In the final weeks of her decline, as the autumn of 1839 drew its damp and chilling twilight over the town of Tuxford, she had extracted a promise from her elder son. It was a scene Matthew had since replayed in his mind with a fidelity that only increased his torment: the small, stifling bedroom with its smell of camphor and tallow, the gaunt, fevered hand grasping his wrist with a strength born of approaching death, and the whispered, breath-laden entreaty. “Thee’ll not leave him, Matthew,” she had said, her eyes holding the dim, terrible clarity of the dying. “He’s simple, and he’s God’s child, but the world’ll use him hard. Thee’ll look to him when I’m gone, and thee’ll not cross the seas and leave him to the parish.” Matthew had given the promise, his voice catching in his throat, because one cannot refuse a dying mother, and because the heart, in such moments, obeys an impulse stronger than any calculation of future convenience.

But the dead, who are exempt from the daily friction of existence, leave to the living the grievous task of reconciling their commands with the relentless pressure of circumstance. Matthew’s cousin, Sarah, a woman of shrewd and somewhat acid temperament, had seen in the Canadian prospect a necessary deliverance for them both. She had found a buyer for the remainder of their lease, and had even identified a place for Thomas at the county asylum in Nottingham, an institution newly built and spoken of with cautious approval by the clergy.

“He’d be well looked to, Matthew, and thee know’st it,” Sarah argued one evening, as they sat in the low-ceilinged kitchen, Thomas being safely occupied in the yard. “They’ve got gardens there, and the matron’s a decent woman—I’ve spoke to her myself. It’s no shame to put him where he’ll be safe, and thee can pay for his keep out o’ thy wages from Canada. Thee’rt tied hand and foot, and for what? For a word spoke i’ the dark, when poor Aunt Mary didna know what she was askin’.”

“It was a promise, Sarah,” said Matthew, his gaze fixed on the dying embers, his brow furrowed by that heavy, inward-looking distress which accompanies the struggle between desire and duty. “And she did know what she was asking. She knew I’d a mind to go, and she held me to my word lest I should forget him.”

“Eh, forget him!” Sarah retorted, her voice sharp with a practicality that brooked no sentimentality. “Thee’d not forget him, but thee’d be a fool to throw away such a chance. What good’ll thee do him here? Dost think the masters i’ this town ’ull raise thy wages because thee’st got a simple brother to feed? They’d as lief see thee i’ the workhouse, and him along with thee. A promise to the dead’s a solemn thing, I’ll grant, but the Lord didna make us to be buried alive.”

Her words, though they carried the harsh, unvarnished truth of economic necessity, could not entirely dissolve the moral viscosity that held Matthew back. He recognized, in his own divided state, that subtle interaction of motive which so often makes human actions a compromise rather than a clear-cut choice. He knew that his desire for emigration was not purely selfish; it was mingled with a genuine belief that, if he prospered, he could ultimately provide for Thomas in a manner impossible on the earnings of a provincial clerk. Yet he also knew that the immediate act of placing his brother in an asylum, however well-regarded, was an abrogation of the precise letter of his promise. The living must decide whether a promise binds them to the form of an action or to its underlying intent; and Matthew, whose conscience was as sensitive to the first as his intellect was to the second, found himself suspended between the two, unable to commit to either.

He went out into the yard, seeking the cool air to still the fever of his deliberation. The evening was thick with the scent of damp earth and the distant, acrid smell of coal-smoke drifting from the town. Thomas was sitting on a wooden bench beside the pump, engaged in his habitual, monotonous task of untangling a length of rope. His face, in the fading light, wore that vacant, untroubled expression which is perhaps the most profound of nature’s mercies—the absence of the very faculty which causes pain by foreseeing it.

“Matt,” said Thomas, looking up as his brother approached, his speech slow and somewhat guttural, “the rope’s all knots. I canna get ’em out.”

“I’ll help thee i’ a minute, Tom,” Matthew replied, his voice softening involuntarily, as it always did when he addressed his brother. He sat down beside him on the bench, taking the knotted rope into his own hands. He worked at the fibres mechanically, his eyes resting on Thomas’s thick, unresisting fingers.

Thomas watched him for a moment, then said, with the sudden, inconsequential gravity of the simple-minded, “Mother said I mun stay wi’ thee.”

The words fell into the still evening air with the weight of a judicial sentence. Matthew’s hands paused in their work; he looked at his brother’s face, searching for some trace of conscious appeal, but found only the placid, unreflecting surface of a mind that repeats without understanding. Thomas had merely echoed a phrase he had heard years ago, retaining it in his memory as a sound rather than a concept. Yet to Matthew, struggling in the toils of his moral dilemma, the utterance seemed to possess an almost supernatural significance, as if the dead had spoken through the lips of the living.

He knew, with the terrible clarity that sometimes illuminates the mind in moments of crisis, that he could not leave. The promise was not merely a contract entered into under the duress of death; it was the expression of a fundamental human obligation, the recognition that the strong are bound to the weak by ties which transcend the calculations of self-interest. To place Thomas in an institution, however benign, would be to transfer his burden to strangers, and to sever that natural bond of kinship which was, for all its hardship, the very condition of his brother’s continued existence. The promise to the dead, he perceived, was not a dead letter; it was a living obligation, because it was rooted in the nature of things—in the instinct of human solidarity which makes the abandonment of a helpless dependant an offence against the moral order itself.

But the sacrifice was immense, and Matthew felt its full weight as he sat in the gathering darkness. He was renouncing not merely a geographical relocation, but the entire trajectory of his future life—the prospect of advancement, of marriage perhaps, of that gradual emancipation from the narrowness of provincial poverty which is the dream of every ambitious young man. He was binding himself to a life of unceasing vigilance and self-denial, in which his own desires must always be subordinated to the needs of his helpless brother.

Sarah found them thus when she came to the door to call them in for supper. The dusk had deepened into night, and the two figures on the bench were barely distinguishable from the shadow of the pump. She looked at them for a moment in silence, her shrewd eyes reading the situation with that swift, feminine intuition which comprehends a decision before it is spoken. She saw that Matthew’s struggle was over, and that the victory had gone not to his ambition but to his conscience.

“Well,” she said, her voice betraying a reluctant softening beneath its accustomed sharpness, “thee’d best come in; the broth’s gettin’ cold. And Tom, thee’t left the yard-gate open again, and the chickens ’ull be out.”

Matthew rose, laying his hand on Thomas’s shoulder. “Come, Tom,” he said. “Let’s go in.”

Thomas stood up obediently, his face once more assuming its habitual placidity. He had forgotten his mother’s words already, and the knotted rope, and the brief, incomprehensible gravity of his brother’s gaze. He was thinking only of the supper that awaited him, and of the coloured stones on the windowsill, which he would arrange anew before he slept. Matthew followed him into the house, closing the yard-gate behind him with a quiet, deliberate finality, as if he were closing the door upon a future that had been his, and was now, by the silent, irrevocable logic of moral choice, no longer possible.
