Mr. Aubrey Vance was not, strictly speaking, a barrister in want of a brief. He was a barrister in want of a purpose, a distinction his father, a man who had made his fortune in the dry goods trade and had purchased for his son every advantage that fortune could command, would have been the first to appreciate. Aubrey had chambers in the Middle Temple, a set of rooms he had furnished with an antiquarian’s eye and a gentleman’s purse, and he went there three days a week to read the law reports and to be seen to be a man of affairs. The rest of his time was divided between his club, the opera, and the family estate in the county of Wessex, a Palladian house set in a deer park where the loudest noise was the crunch of gravel under a carriage wheel.

It was at the assizes in the county town of Marchester that he was, for the first time in his career, truly engaged. He had gone down for the spectacle of it, to dine with the High Sheriff and to observe the machinery of justice from the comfortable elevation of the magistrates’ bench. But a fellow barrister, a sickly, overworked man named Pemberton who was to prosecute a case of petty theft, had collapsed with a fever, and the judge, a testy old knight who disliked delay, had cast his eye about the court for a replacement. His gaze had landed on Aubrey, whose family name was known, whose appearance was respectable, and who was, by the grace of his father’s guineas, a member of the Bar.

The case was a simple one, and sordid. A glove-maker from the lower town, a man named Silas Rook, was accused of stealing a silver locket and a length of velvet from the shop of a mercer on the High Street. The evidence was circumstantial but damning enough for the assizes: the velvet had been found in a sack of leather scraps behind Rook’s workshop, and a boy had sworn he’d seen Rook loitering near the mercer’s back door on the night in question. Rook, a gaunt, grey-faced man with hands that were a map of callouses and needle-pricks, protested his innocence with a dull, persistent monotony that was more weary than convincing.

Aubrey, seated at the counsel’s table in his borrowed wig and gown, felt a profound and detached interest. This was the law in action, raw and unpolished. He saw it as a problem of logic, a puzzle of evidence and testimony. He cross-examined the mercer’s boy with a cool, precise manner he had cultivated from reading the great advocates, tying the lad in knots of trivial contradiction until the judge admonished him for badgering the witness. He pointed out to the jury that the sack of scraps was accessible to any man, and that the velvet was of a common enough weave. He was, he thought, doing rather well.

It was in his final address that he made his misstep. He was speaking of the character of his client, a man of no formal education, a man whose life was one of unremitting toil. “My lord, gentlemen of the jury,” he said, his voice resonant in the hushed courtroom, “we must consider the nature of this man’s world. It is a world of narrow streets and narrow means, a world in which a man’s horizons are bounded by the walls of his workshop and the price of a kid glove. Is it so strange that in such a world, a man might be tempted? Is it not our duty to consider the pressures of poverty?”

He had meant it as a plea for mercy, a rhetorical flourish. But he saw the effect on the jury, a collection of farmers and shopkeepers, and on the judge, whose lip curled. He had not pleaded for the man’s innocence; he had, in effect, pleaded for his guilt. He had painted Rook as a creature of his circumstances, and in doing so, he had stripped him of his dignity. The verdict was guilty. The sentence was six months’ hard labour.

Aubrey left the court with the hollow feeling of a game poorly played. That evening, at the Sheriff’s table, he was congratulated on his eloquence. He drank his wine and smiled, but the taste of it was ashes. He had never before felt the weight of a man’s life in his hands, and he had found it lighter than he expected, and far more fragile.

A week later, back in London, he found himself unable to shake the image of Silas Rook’s hands. He made some inquiries. The locket, he learned, had been a family piece, pawned and redeemed a dozen times. The mercer, a man named Finch, was known to be a sharp dealer. And the boy who had testified? He was an apprentice of Finch’s, a lad with a reputation for slyness.

Aubrey, for the first time in his life, felt the sting of a genuine curiosity. He took a hansom cab to the dingy purlieus of Southwark, to the address Rook had given. It was a street of tumbledown houses, the air thick with the smell of tanning pits and coal smoke. He found Rook’s wife, a small, fierce woman with eyes like chips of flint, in a single room that served as kitchen, parlour, and workshop. The walls were hung with hides and patterns, and on a table lay a half-finished pair of gloves, the stitching so fine it was almost invisible.

“You’re the lawyer,” she said, not looking up from her work. “The one who said my Silas was a thief because he was poor.”

Aubrey’s carefully prepared words died in his throat. “I… I did not mean…”

“I know what you meant,” she said, her needle flying. “You meant to be kind. You meant to say he was a good man brought low. But in that court, with those men, it was the same as saying he was a bad one. He is not a bad man, sir. He is a proud man. He would not steal a crust.”

She told him then of Finch’s grudge against her husband, a dispute over a debt. She told him of the boy’s reputation. She told him of the sack of scraps, which was kept outside for the rag-and-bone man, and which any passer-by could have reached. It was all there, a story as plain as the nose on his face, and he had not seen it. He had been too busy looking at the law, and not at the people.

He went to see Finch. The mercer was a stout, smug man who denied everything with an oily smile. But Aubrey, now alert, noticed the way his eyes flickered to a strongbox on his shelf. He noticed the boy, who would not meet his gaze. He had no power to compel them, no authority to reopen the case. He was just a young man with a conscience and a comfortable inheritance.

He returned to Marchester and sought out the judge, a man who was, beneath his bluster, not unkind. Aubrey laid out what he had found, not as a lawyer, but as a man. He admitted his own failure, his own blind and comfortable ignorance. The judge listened, his face a mask. He said nothing.

But a month later, a petition for mercy, signed by several of Marchester’s more respectable citizens who had been quietly canvassed by the Vance family solicitor, found its way to the Home Office. The sentence was commuted. Silas Rook was released.

Aubrey Vance never practised law again in the way he had before. He still went to his chambers, but he took on the cases no one else wanted, the hopeless ones, the poor ones. He learned the names of the alleys and the courts of his own county town, the ones he had never known existed. He learned that the town he had grown up in was not a backdrop for his own pleasant life, but a living, suffering thing, and that his ignorance of it had been the most comfortable thing he had ever owned.
