It was a damp and chilly evening in the month of November when I received a telegram from my friend Mr. Silas Vane, a solicitor of some standing in the City, whose letters I have always found to be as precise as his ledgers. The message was brief: *“Furniture moves. Lady insists. Come at once. 14 Lombard Street.”*

I was in my rooms at Baker Street, attempting to ignore the silence that had fallen after the last case of the month, when the telegram arrived. I knew at once that Vane’s anxiety was genuine; he was not a man to summon me for trivialities. I donned my overcoat, picked up my hat, and hailed a cab. The journey to the City was swift, the driver lashing his horse through the misty streets until we emerged into the narrow, cobblestoned thoroughfare that gave the locality its name.

Fourteen Lombard Street was one of a row of respectable terrace houses, their brickwork darkened by the soot of a century’s industry. The house stood adjacent to the imposing grey facade of the Imperial Bank, a structure that had been undergoing considerable alteration for the past six months. As we alighted, I observed that the bank’s foundations had been deepened, and the cellar of the house next door seemed to be connected, by some invisible subterranean conduit, to the vaults below.

Mr. Vane met us at the door. He was a lean, nervous man, with eyes that darted constantly to the shadows. “Mrs. Agnes Prynne is inside,” he said, his voice low. “She is quite certain, Mr. Holmes. She claims that something enters the house every night and shifts her chairs, her tables, her very rug. The family believes it is a delusion brought on by age, but I confess I have not been able to dismiss the idea entirely.”

We entered the drawing room. It was a spacious chamber, comfortably furnished but somewhat dusty, as if the occupants had recently departed. Mrs. Prynne sat in a large armchair, a shawl wrapped tightly about her shoulders. She was a small woman, with a face that was pale but possessed of a fierce intelligence. Her eyes, though dimmed by cataracts, held a sharp, accusing light.

“You are the detective?” she asked, her voice trembling not with fear, but with indignation. “I have told them all. I am not mad. I hear the scratch. I feel the vibration. And then, in the morning, the chair is moved. An inch, perhaps two. But it is enough. It is a trespass.”

I looked around the room. The furniture was arranged with a rigid symmetry. A mahogany sideboard stood against the left wall, a settee against the right, and a small round table in the center. I walked to the sideboard and ran my hand along its top. It was clean, save for a fine layer of dust. I looked at the floorboards. They were oak, thick and sturdy, but I noticed a slight discoloration near the baseboard on the north side of the room. It was not the discoloration of age, but of recent dampness, or perhaps oil.

“Mrs. Prynne,” I said, leaning down to speak to her level, “do you mind if I examine the walls? I wish to understand the nature of this intrusion.”

She nodded stiffly. “Do what you must. But know that they do not come in through the windows. I have barred them. They come from the earth.”

I turned to Mr. Vane. “Where does the main drain run, Mr. Vane?”

“The bank is building a new vault,” Vane replied. “They have broken through the cellar wall to access the sub-basement. I had no idea it extended beneath your house, Mrs. Prynne. I assumed it was merely a matter of plumbing.”

I walked to the north wall, the side adjacent to the bank. I ran my fingers along the skirting board. It was solid oak, well-finished. But near the corner, where the wall met the floor, I detected a faint, rhythmic hum. It was too low to be heard by the ear, but the vibration was distinct in the palm of my hand. I crouched down and looked closely at the baseboard. There, barely visible against the dark wood, was a hairline crack. I pulled a small tool from my pocket and gently probed the crack. It gave way.

“Mr. Vane,” I said, standing up and brushing the dust from my knees. “You mentioned the bank’s vaults. How deep do they go?”

“As far as the river bed, I am told,” Vane said, looking puzzled. “Why do you ask?”

“Because,” I said, tapping the baseboard, “this is not a crack from age. This is a seam. And there is a draft here. A cold, damp draft. Mrs. Prynne is not imagining the movement. Something is passing through this wall, or rather, through a void behind it. But it is not a person. A person could not fit through a crack in the baseboard, nor could they move a heavy sideboard with such precision without making more noise.”

I returned to the center of the room. I looked at the small round table. It had been moved slightly to the left. I placed my hand on it and pushed it back to its original position. Then I looked at the sideboard. I pushed it forward by an inch.

“Mrs. Prynne,” I said, “when you say the furniture moves, do you mean it moves on its own? Or do you mean it is shifted by an external force?”

“It moves,” she said. “I see it in the morning. It is in a different place.”

I smiled faintly. “Then we must observe it. We must catch the mover in the act. But we must be careful. If it is what I suspect, it is not a ghost, but a mechanism. And mechanisms can be dangerous.”

I spent the next two hours examining the house. I checked the windows, the doors, the chimneys. There was no evidence of forced entry. The only anomaly was that draft from the north wall. I decided that the best course of action was to wait. I asked Mrs. Prynne to stay in the room, to watch the furniture, and to signal me if she felt any vibration.

I sat in the corner, in the shadows, and waited. The night was quiet. The only sound was the distant rumble of a cab on the street and the tick of the clock. At midnight, I felt it. A low, subsonic thrum. It came from the north wall. I watched the sideboard. It did not move. But the dust on top of it began to dance. A fine cloud of grey particles rose into the air, swirling in a small vortex.

I stood up and moved to the wall. I placed my hand against the skirting board. It was warm. Not hot, but warm, as if something were generating heat behind it. I listened. There was a sound, faint but distinct. A grinding, mechanical whine. It was the sound of gears turning, of metal on metal.

I realized then what was happening. The bank’s vaults had been enlarged. They had not just dug down; they had dug in. The new vault was directly beneath the drawing room. But the furniture was not moving because of the vault. It was moving because of the heat.

I looked at the sideboard again. The wood was oak, but it was old oak, dry and brittle. The heat from the new vault, rising through the crack in the baseboard, was causing the wood to expand and contract. As the day warmed the house, the wood would swell, pushing the furniture against the wall. As the night cooled the house, the wood would shrink, pulling the furniture back. But the crack in the baseboard allowed the cold air from the vault to seep in, creating a localized cold spot. This contrast in temperature was causing the wood to warp and shift, inch by inch, over the course of the night.

It was a slow, silent war between heat and cold, played out on the grain of the wood. Mrs. Prynne had felt the vibration because the mechanical hum of the bank’s ventilation system was passing through the wall. She had seen the furniture move because she was watching it with the patience of the elderly and the clarity of the observant.

I walked to the window and looked out at the bank. The lights in the new vault were on, a yellow glow in the darkness. I turned back to Mrs. Prynne, who was watching me with wide, hopeful eyes.

“Mrs. Prynne,” I said, “there is no ghost. There is no thief. There is only heat. The bank’s new vault is warm. Your house is cold. The wood is expanding and contracting. It is a natural phenomenon, but one that is being amplified by the construction next door.”

She looked at the sideboard, then at me. “So it is not a trespass?”

“It is a nuisance,” I said. “But it is not a crime. However, I do not think the bank is aware of the effect their work is having on your home. I suggest you write to them. Tell them that your furniture is moving. They will be surprised. But I assure you, if you seal that crack in the baseboard with a good caulking, the movement will cease.”

Mrs. Prynne looked at the crack, then at me. A smile played on her lips. “You are a clever man, Mr. Holmes. I knew I was not mad. But I did not know it was the earth that was moving, not the air.”

I took my leave of them, feeling a satisfaction that was rare in my profession. It was not a case of murder or robbery, but it was a case of observation, of attention to detail, of the interplay between the human and the mechanical. As I walked back to my cab, the mist was thickening, and the street lamps cast long, blurred shadows on the pavement. I thought of Mrs. Prynne, sitting in her drawing room, watching the furniture, and I smiled. The world is full of mysteries, some of them dark, some of them light. But all of them, if you look closely enough, have a rational explanation. And it is our duty, as investigators, to find that explanation, however small, however humble.
