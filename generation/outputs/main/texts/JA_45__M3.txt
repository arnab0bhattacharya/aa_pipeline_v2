Mr. Arthur Vincent, rector of Puddleton-cum-Thorpe, was a man of very moderate fortune and very regular habits, whose journeys to London were so few and so far between that each might be considered an event of some historical importance in his own private annals. He had come to town upon business of a decidedly clerical nature: to petition the Bishop of Hertfordshire for a vacant living, a matter which he contemplated with a degree of anxiety that was, perhaps, less proportioned to the value of the living—which was but five hundred a year—than to his thorough conviction that a bishop was a creature of an order entirely separate from ordinary men, and not to be approached without something very like awe.

The petitioning, however, was not to take place until the following morning, and Mr. Vincent found himself with an evening upon his hands and a pressing invitation from his cousin, Mrs. Carrington, to dine in Harley Street. Mrs. Carrington was a woman of fashion, or at least of that species of fashion which consists in knowing everybody’s name and nobody’s business, and she entertained for her cousin something of the affection one might feel for a curious artefact: he was rustic, he was unpolished, and he was, therefore, not without his use as a conversation-piece.

“You will find us very plain, I am sure,” said Mrs. Carrington, receiving him in a drawing-room hung with crimson silk and illuminated by as many candles as might have served for a ball. “We live entirely *en famille*—a few friends, no ceremony. I dare say it will seem very dull to you, Mr. Vincent, after your lively doings in the country.”

Mr. Vincent, whose liveliest doings consisted of a parish meeting and a visit to a sick parishioner, bore this with perfect good humour and replied that he was sure he should find nothing dull in Harley Street, which was perfectly true, though not perhaps in the sense Mrs. Carrington intended.

The company was already assembled, and Mr. Vincent’s entrance produced that slight pause which always greets a newcomer whose coat is not of the newest cut. There was Mr. Fanshawe, a thin young man of considerable pretension, who was understood to be writing a tragedy; Mrs. Marchmont, a lady of forty who abhorred the country with a fervour proportionate to her having been obliged to live there for two seasons without advantage; and Colonel Letchmere, a retired officer of dragoons, whose claims to distinction rested upon a limp and a singularly well-told anecdote regarding the Peninsula.

Mrs. Carrington lost no time in presenting her cousin for what he was. “Mr. Vincent is *quite* a country gentleman,” she observed, with a graceful little laugh that seemed to invite the company to share in a very agreeable joke. “He actually resides the whole year round in his parish. You may imagine, Mrs. Marchmont, how shocking that must sound to one who knows what it is to be exiled.”

Mrs. Marchmont, who had not the smallest notion of anything shocking in the arrangement, declared herself quite delighted to meet anybody who could endure the country at all, and added that she herself had been so ill at Shropshire the previous autumn that she had positively declined to open a single window for six weeks together. Mr. Vincent received this intelligence with an expression of such unaffected concern that Mrs. Marchmont was obliged to look away, not being entirely certain whether she had been pitied or rebuked.

At dinner, Mr. Vincent was placed near Mr. Fanshawe, who immediately undertook to draw him out. “You find a great want of society in the country, I suppose, sir?” said Mr. Fanshawe, with the air of one who propounds a question in natural philosophy.

“Indeed, I find a great deal of it,” replied Mr. Vincent. “My parishioners are very sociable, and I am seldom an evening at home.”

Mr. Fanshawe looked disappointed, as one whose experiment has yielded an unexpected result, and tried another line. “But intellectual society—the exchange of ideas, the collision of minds—you must miss that?”

“I have a very good neighbour, a Dr. Phipps, who reads the *Quarterly Review* regularly, and we sometimes differ about it quite warmly.”

Whether it was the notion of two country clergymen differing warmly over the *Quarterly Review*, or merely the word “warmly” applied to any intellectual contest in the country, Mr. Fanshawe appeared to find something in this reply that exceeded his powers of comprehension, and abandoned the inquiry.

It was Colonel Letchmere, however, who proved the most unexpected convert. He had sat in silence, as was his habit until the second course, when he suddenly addressed Mr. Vincent with a directness that had in it something military. “You were saying you visit your parishioners, sir. Do you mean the poorer sort?”

“Everybody,” said Mr. Vincent. “I could not very well visit only the rich, and the poor are more often in need of a friend.”

“A friend!” repeated the Colonel, with a slight emphasis that might have been surprise. “You use that word, do you? In my experience, parsons and poor folks don’t deal much in friendship—more in instruction, and that sort of thing.”

Mr. Vincent smiled—a plain, honest smile, which did nothing to embellish his countenance but a great deal to recommend it. “I am afraid I am not a very great instructor, Colonel. I find I learn a great deal more than I teach.”

Mrs. Carrington, who had been watching this exchange with something of the anxiety of a hostess whose curiosity has begun to misbehave in company, attempted to turn the conversation. “Colonel Letchmere, you must tell Mr. Vincent about your Spanish campaign. It is always so amusing.”

But the Colonel, for the first time in Mrs. Carrington’s recollection, did not wish to tell his Spanish campaign. He fixed his attention upon Mr. Vincent and said, with an air of genuine interest, “And this living you are petitioning for—is it a large parish?”

“Moderate, sir. Some eight hundred souls, and a great many children, which is the best sign of a parish, in my opinion.”

Mrs. Marchmont, who had been languishing in her corner, here roused herself to observe that eight hundred souls sounded a vast number to be responsible for, and that she for one should be quite terrified. Mr. Vincent allowed that the responsibility was not trifling, but added, with that quiet simplicity which was beginning to have its effect upon the room, that the souls in question were remarkably patient with a clergyman’s shortcomings, which made the burden lighter than it might otherwise appear.

Mrs. Carrington began to perceive, with some uneasiness, that her cousin was not playing the part assigned to him at all as he ought. He was not ridiculous; he was not even peculiar. He was merely a sensible man, speaking sensibly upon subjects which sensible people ought to care about, and the fashionable trifles she had arranged as a foil to his simplicity were beginning to look rather thin. She made one more attempt. “Mr. Vincent is quite devoted to his parish,” she said, with a playfulness that was a shade too determined. “He quite neglects his own amusement for it.”

“I am very well amused, ma’am,” said Mr. Vincent simply; and Mrs. Marchmont, who had not expected seriousness from a man in a coat so plainly cut, was surprised to find herself thinking that she had not been so well amused herself in a very long while.

When the gentlemen rejoined the ladies, Colonel Letchmere placed himself beside Mr. Vincent with an air of settled purpose, and for the remainder of the evening conversed with him in a tone which, if Mrs. Carrington’s ears did not deceive her, bore every mark of real respect. Mr. Fanshawe, too, though he could not be brought to abandon his tragedy, condescended to ask a question or two about the poor, which Mr. Vincent answered with such plain good sense that Mr. Fanshawe was left with the uncomfortable impression that his tragedy wanted something, and that the something might possibly be truth.

Mrs. Carrington, surveying her party as they took leave, could not but acknowledge that her cousin had somehow failed to be the amusement she had intended. He had been, on the contrary, rather more than that—a circumstance which she found she did not at all know how to resent, and which she was obliged, in the privacy of her own thoughts, to class among the evening’s disappointments, though she could not have said precisely why.
