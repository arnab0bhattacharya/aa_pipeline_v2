The fatigue of a country practice is a very real and tangible thing, a sort of grey, clinging dust that settles on a man’s spirit. I know, for I was that man for three years, as assistant to Dr. Abernathy in the market town of Wychford. My days were a round of poultices, quinine, and the endless, wearying catalogue of human ailments, from the croup in infants to the gout in elderly farmers. The nights were the worst, broken by the frantic rattling of a latch and a voice crying out for the doctor in the darkness. I would drag myself from my bed, my mind thick with sleep, my body aching with a fatigue that sleep itself seemed powerless to cure. It was in this state of perpetual exhaustion that my thoughts began to turn towards a chemical solution.

I had a small laboratory of my own in a lean-to shed at the back of Abernathy’s house, where I pursued my own researches into the nature of vitality. The prevailing theory, of course, involved humours and tonics, but I suspected the truth lay deeper, in the very chemistry of the blood. Fatigue, I reasoned, was not merely an absence of energy, but the presence of a poison—a toxin produced by the muscles and nerves in the course of their work. If one could neutralise this toxin, or prevent its formation, the body might go on indefinitely. After months of work with frog tissues and a series of complex alkaloids, I arrived at a serum I called, with a certain lack of poetry, ‘Vitalin’. It was a clear, faintly amber fluid, and in the first trials I administered it to a pair of over-worked dray-horses. They pulled their loads for a week without a sign of weariness, their coats shining, their eyes unnaturally bright. I felt a thrill of triumph. Then I made the decision that has led me to this present predicament. I began to inject myself.

The effect was instantaneous and quite wonderful. The grey dust of fatigue vanished. The ache in my bones was gone. My mind, which had felt for years as if it were wrapped in cotton wool, became sharp and clear as a winter morning. I felt not merely rested, but supercharged with a vital, humming energy. That night, I did not go to bed. I read through three volumes of German chemistry, re-organised my specimens, and when the first call came at three in the morning for old Mrs. Gable’s rheumatism, I was on my feet and out of the door before the servant had finished knocking. I was brisk, cheerful, and my diagnosis was, if I say so myself, impeccable.

My practice, which had been merely adequate under the weary stewardship of Dr. Abernathy, began to flourish. Word spread through Wychford and the surrounding farms of a doctor’s assistant who never slept, who was always available, always lucid, and whose treatments seemed to have a miraculous efficacy. I took on more patients. I made calls at all hours. I would walk five miles to a remote farmstead in the middle of the night, arrive with my bag, and treat a case of pleurisy with a calm, tireless precision that astonished the family. Dr. Abernathy, who was a good man but a creature of habit and port wine, was at first grateful, then bewildered. “Gads, Thornhill,” he’d say, peering at me over his spectacles, “you have the constitution of an ox. I saw your lamp burning at four this morning.” I would simply smile and say I had found a new invigorating tonic.

But it was the patients who noticed it first, the subtle, creeping wrongness. It began with a farmer named Silas Croft, whose son I had treated for a nasty bout of fever. “It’s your eyes, sir,” he said to me one day in the market square, his own eyes narrowed with a farmer’s shrewdness. “They don’t seem to… blink. Not the way a man’s eyes ought to.” I laughed it off, but I made a point of blinking deliberately the next time I saw him. Then there was the business with the catgut. I was suturing a gash on a woodcutter’s arm, my fingers working with a speed and steadiness that was frankly inhuman. The woodcutter, a man named Jabez, watched, fascinated and horrified. “Your hands, sir,” he whispered. “They’re like a machine.” I had not felt the tremor of fatigue that usually sets in after a delicate task. My fingers were as steady as steel forceps.

The unease grew. People would speak to me in the street, but their voices were hushed, their manner deferential, as if they were addressing some new species of creature. I heard whispers of ‘the tireless doctor’ and ‘the man who never sleeps’. I saw a woman pull her child away from me as I passed. The very thing that had made my practice a success—my inhuman stamina—was now making me an object of fear. They had wanted a doctor, but not this. They wanted a man who understood their tiredness because he felt it himself, a man who shared their frailties. I had become a perfect, efficient, tireless automaton, and they recoiled from me.

The final straw came last Tuesday. A young woman, a maid from the manor, had suffered a fall and broken her wrist. I set the bone and bandaged it with my usual efficiency. Her mother, a stout, sensible woman, was there. When I had finished, she did not thank me. She simply looked at my face, at my unblinking eyes and the faint, perpetual sheen of sweat on my brow that was the only outward sign of the serum’s work. “You’ve done your job, sir,” she said, her voice flat. “But it’s not natural. You’re not like us. A doctor should be a man, not a… a clockwork thing that just goes on and on.”

Her words struck me with the force of a physical blow. I looked at my reflection in the dark window of the surgery. My face was pale, my eyes wide and bright, and there was not a line of weariness on it. I looked like a portrait of myself, done by an artist who had never seen a tired man. I have not taken the serum for two days now. The fatigue is returning, a great, grey, familiar wave. My hands tremble as I write this. My mind is becoming cloudy again. It is a terrible, heavy, human feeling. And it is the most welcome sensation I have ever known. I must go now. I hear the latch of the garden gate. Someone is coming for the doctor. And for the first time in a long time, I shall go to the door feeling the weary, aching, honest weight of being a man.
