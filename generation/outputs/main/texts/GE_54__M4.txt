The illness that came upon the Reverend Mr. Osmund Thripp at Michaelmas was of that slow, remitting character which baffles the physician while it tries the patience of the sick man’s friends; and as his living of Cuckow’s Barrow lay upon the high moorland, seven miles from any town, and his lodgings in the village were of a dampness that no fire of peat could subdue, it was thought best by the few who concerned themselves in the matter that he should be removed to the farmhouse of Gabriel Renshaw, at the Hollows, where there was a chimney that drew, and a woman who understood the uses of whey and linen. The curate, being a man of small means and much pride, resisted at first, as men do who would rather be ill in their own manner than well in another’s; but the fever took the argument out of his hands, and he was carried up the lane in a cart, with his books in a box beside him, before he had well consented.

The Renshaws were of that yeoman stock which has long been the sinew of the moorland parishes—a family that held forty acres of rough grazing, a few cows, and a name older than the churchyard wall. Gabriel Renshaw was a silent man who read the sky better than he read his Bible, and his wife, Deborah, was of the sort whose tenderness expresses itself in a vigorous ordering of other people’s comforts. But it was their daughter, Rachel, who, by a silent election no one had pronounced, became the curate’s nurse. She was twenty years old, and had never in her life been farther from the Hollows than the market town at the vale’s foot, eight miles off; her world was bounded by the stone stile above the pasture and the clough where the water chattered after rain. She could milk, churn, salt bacon, and read the Bible aloud in a slow, careful voice, but of the world of books beyond that volume she knew nothing, having been taught at the dame’s school only so much as enabled her to spell out the hymns.

When the fever abated, Mr. Thripp lay for many days in a state of that weak lucidity which makes the mind a mirror to every trifle. He watched the light creep along the whitewashed wall, and the shadow of the geranium in the window, and the hands of Rachel Renshaw as she moved about the room, and he felt a gratitude which was almost a pain, because it could find no adequate expression. He was a young man of a shy and scholarly habit, bred at a grammar school and a college, and he had come to Cuckow’s Barrow with a vague hope of doing good and a more definite hope of being left alone with his Greek. Now, lying helpless in a farmhouse chamber, he discovered that he was being nursed with a devotion that asked nothing and understood nothing of his books, and he was moved by it in a way that surprised him.

“You must let me lend you something to read,” he said one afternoon, when she had brought him his broth and was about to withdraw. “It is dull work sitting by a sick man. There is a book of travels yonder—Captain Cook’s voyages. It is not divinity, but it is very entertaining.”

Rachel coloured, and stood with her hand upon the door-latch. “I doubt I should make little of it, sir,” she said. “I am not a great scholar.”

“There is nothing to make of it but the words,” said the curate. “And the words are plain enough. Try the first chapter. If you find it tedious, you may tell me so honestly, and I shall not be offended.”

She took the book, and went down the stairs with it as if it were a thing of glass. That night, by the light of a single candle in the kitchen, after her father had gone to his bed and her mother was asleep, she opened it, and began to read; and it is not too much to say that the world opened with it. The slow sentences, the strange names, the far islands, the ships that went into seas where no keel had been—these things wrought upon her imagination with a power the more intense because she had no one to whom she could speak of them. She read the first chapter twice, and then the second, and when she came to the end of the third she found that her candle was guttering and the fire was grey ash, and she went to bed with her head full of a great wind and the crying of birds over unknown water.

Thereafter the lending of books became a custom between them, and the custom ripened into conversation, and the conversation, by imperceptible degrees, into a species of intimacy which neither of them would have dared to name. The curate, who had never been a man of many friends, found himself waiting for the hours when she would come to sit by his window with her sewing, and he would read aloud to her—first passages of the travels, then, as his strength returned, the essays of Mr. Addison, and once, with a hesitation he could not account for, a poem of Mr. Cowper’s. She listened with her head bent over her work, but he observed that her needle would sometimes stop, and that she would look out of the window with a fixed gaze, as if she saw something beyond the moor.

“You are thinking of the islands,” he said one day, smiling.

“I am thinking,” she answered, slowly, “that there is a great deal in the world, sir, that I never knew of. It makes me feel—I cannot rightly say—as if I had been asleep all my life, and were but now beginning to wake.”

Mr. Thripp was silent. He was conscious of a strange emotion, which he would have described, had he been obliged to describe it, as a sense of responsibility; but it was mingled with a pleasure that was not altogether the pleasure of a teacher. He looked at her profile, clear and grave against the light, and he thought that he had never seen a face so honest, and he was aware that his heart beat more quickly than the state of his health warranted.

It was Deborah Renshaw, whose eyes were sharper than her husband’s, who first perceived the danger. She said nothing to her daughter, but she spoke to the curate’s landlady in the village, and the landlady spoke to the vicar’s wife, and by such invisible channels as gossip travels in a moorland parish, the matter came to be understood without ever having been stated. The vicar’s wife, a lady of benevolent intentions and a firm grasp of the proprieties, drove over to the Hollows in her pony-chaise, and sat with Mr. Thripp for an hour, and in the course of that hour she remarked, with a smile that was meant to be kind, that it was a great thing for a young clergyman to have a good woman about him in his sickness, but that he must be careful not to let gratitude—a very amiable feeling, but not always a prudent one—carry him beyond the bounds of what was fitting.

Mr. Thripp received this admonition with a coldness that surprised himself. He said that he was sensible of her ladyship’s kindness, and that he hoped he knew his duty; and when she was gone he lay for a long time looking at the ceiling, and felt that he had been insulted in the person of Rachel Renshaw, and that the insult was one which he could not resent.

The days that followed were days of inward perturbation. He began to consider his position with the painful acuteness of a man who has been bred to self-examination. He was a curate with seventy pounds a year and no prospect of preferment; he was the son of a country attorney who had spent his substance on his education; he had nothing to offer any woman but the uncertain tenure of a curacy and the society of a man who had read more Greek than was good for him. And Rachel Renshaw was a farmer’s daughter, with a plain gown and a dialect that betrayed her at every turn, and a mind that had but just begun to open to the world of letters. What, then, was the nature of the feeling that had grown up between them? Was it gratitude on his side and a childish wonder on hers? Or was it that rare and terrible thing which the poets call love, and which the prudent call a folly?

He could not answer the question; but he knew that when she came into the room the light seemed to fall differently, and that when she was absent he listened for her step upon the stair. And he knew, too, with a certainty that was like a knife in his breast, that if he spoke to her of what he felt, he must either marry her—and drag her into a life of poverty and struggle for which her training had not fitted her—or he must leave the Hollows and never see her again. The one course seemed to him a betrayal of his duty to his calling; the other, a betrayal of something that was not his calling, but was, he began to suspect, his very self.

On the first day that he was able to walk abroad, he went out alone upon the moor, and stood by the stone stile, and looked down at the farmhouse among its huddled trees. The smoke went up straight into the still October air, and he could see the figure of Rachel in the yard, feeding the fowls. She did not see him. He watched her for a long time, and he felt the wind on his face, and he heard the curlew crying over the waste, and he thought that the world was very large and very lonely, and that a man’s life was a small thing to set against the happiness of two persons.

He turned away at last, and went back to the house, and when he met her in the passage he said, with a steadiness that cost him an effort, “Miss Renshaw, I have been thinking that I have trespassed too long upon your family’s kindness. I shall be well enough to return to my lodgings in a week.”

She looked at him with a quick, startled glance, and then her eyes fell, and she said, in a low voice, “As you think best, sir.”

And she went into the kitchen and shut the door, and Mr. Thripp stood in the passage, and heard the clatter of the dishes, and knew that he had made the first move in a game whose issue was hidden from him, and that the wisdom of the serpent is sometimes but another name for the cowardice of the heart.
