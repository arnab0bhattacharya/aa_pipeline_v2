The shutters at Greylees Hall had been closed for a fortnight, sealing the house in a twilight gloom that smelled of stale tallow and unlaundered linen. Clara sat in the high-backed armchair beside the dead hearth, exactly where she had been the day the letter arrived from London. The letter that held David’s confession, written in his sprawling, careless hand: a sudden crisis of the heart, an apology for his fickleness, and a final, devastating assurance that he would always remember her fondly. She had read it until the paper grew soft and torn at the creases, and then she had simply stopped moving. She wore the same grey muslin dress, its hem now frayed and muddy from dragging across the floorboards, and her dark hair fell in heavy, untended tangles down her back. The world outside could freeze over entirely, she thought, and she would not stir to warm her hands by the fire.

Her younger brother, Tobias, had no such intention of freezing. At fourteen, Tobias possessed a restless, muscular energy that the confines of the house could no longer contain. He had endured the first week of mourning in relative silence, watching his sister fade into the upholstery like a ghost, but his patience was a shallow well. It was a quarter past five on a Tuesday morning when he threw open the heavy oak door of the parlour, letting the draught from the stone-flagged hallway rush in.

"Get up, Clara," he said, his voice breaking awkwardly between boyish treble and manhood. He stood in the doorway holding a pair of her stout leather walking boots, a heavy woollen cloak thrown over his shoulder.

Clara did not turn her head. "Go away, Toby."

"I am not going away, and neither are you." He marched across the rug and dropped the boots with a heavy clatter directly onto her skirts. "You stink of despair and tallow. We are going up to Thorp Fell."

"I am not going anywhere." Her voice was a dry rasp, devoid of inflection. "Leave me to my peace."

"It is not peace, it is rot," Tobias declared, entirely undeterred by the dark, hollowed look she finally turned upon him. "You have not seen the sun since the month turned. You will catch a putrid fever and die in this chair, and I refuse to explain to Mother and Father in Bath that I let you perish over a London dandy who wasn't worth the powder to shoot him."

The mention of David’s name sent a visible tremor through her shoulders, but she pressed her lips into a thin, bloodless line. "You do not understand."

"I understand that he was a fool. I understand that you are acting like one. Put on the boots, Clara." He thrust the heavy cloak into her lap. "I will drag you by the hair if I must. You know I am strong enough."

She looked at his flushed, earnest face, framed by the unruly sandy curls that escaped his cap. He was entirely serious. The absolute, unshakeable certainty in his eyes—a boyish arrogance that refused to acknowledge the crushing weight of adult grief—was a force she could not reason with. With a sigh that seemed to pull the very air from the room, Clara rose. Her joints ached with the effort, her limbs stiff from days of disuse. She changed into the boots and wrapped the cloak around her shoulders, moving with the slow, mechanical obedience of a sleepwalker.

The morning was brutally cold. A sharp frost had settled over the Peak District valley, painting the coarse grass and loose stones in a brittle silver. The air bit at Clara’s lungs as they stepped out of the back door, crossing the frost-hardened garden and passing through the wooden gate that led onto the open fells. Tobias set a punishing pace, his long legs striding up the steep, zigzagging path that wound toward the summit of Thorp Fell. Clara followed in silence, her eyes fixed on the ground, watching the rhythmic scuff of Toby’s boots against the gravel.

As they climbed higher, the wind picked up, howling across the exposed moorland and tearing through Clara’s cloak. She pulled it tighter, shivering violently, cursing her brother under her breath for his cruelty. What was the point of this? To show her how bleak and unforgiving the world was? She already knew. She had thought she was building a life, a future rooted in love and mutual understanding, only to find it was built on sand. The wind seemed to mock her, echoing the hollow emptiness that had taken root in her chest.

"Look at the ground, Clara," Tobias commanded over his shoulder, pausing on a rocky outcrop.

She glared at his back, wanting to scream at him to leave her be, but her breath caught in her throat. She looked down. Beneath the thin layer of frost, the peat and gravel were churned and scarred. Deep, parallel grooves ran through the earth, cutting across the path and disappearing into the heather.

"What is it?" she asked, the first words she had spoken voluntarily in days.

"Old mining tracks," Tobias said, crouching down to trace the grooves with his gloved finger. "From the lead miners, maybe a hundred years ago. They drove their carts up here, pulled by donkeys, bringing the ore down to the smelting mill in the valley."

Clara stared at the marks in the earth. They were old, faded by time and weather, but unmistakably the work of human hands. Men had laboured here, day after day, extracting something valuable from the unforgiving rock. "I did not know there were mines up here," she murmured, feeling a strange, involuntary pull of curiosity.

"There are shafts all over the fell," Tobias said, standing up and pointing further up the slope. "See that depression in the heather? That is a bell pit. And there, by the outcrop, that is the remains of a windlass. They came up here because they believed there was something worth finding, even when the land gave them nothing but stone and cold." He looked back at her, his young face serious. "They did not sit in their parlours and weep because the hill was steep."

Clara felt a flush of shame warm her cheeks, chasing away some of the biting cold. She thought of David, of the future she had imagined for them, and realized how fragile it had been. A promise made in a drawing room, easily broken by a change of heart. But the marks on the ground were real. They had endured long after the men who made them were gone. They were a testament to stubborn endurance, to the refusal to yield to the harshness of the landscape.

"Come on," Tobias said, turning to resume the climb. "We are almost at the top."

They walked on in silence, but it was a different silence now. Clara lifted her head, taking in the vast expanse of the moorland stretching out around them. The sky was a pale, washed-out grey, but on the eastern horizon, a faint band of gold was beginning to bleed through the clouds. The wind still tugged at her cloak, but it felt less like a mockery and more like a bracing reminder that she was alive, that her heart still beat, even if it was bruised.

When they reached the summit of Thorp Fell, Tobias stopped and turned to face the view. Below them, the valley was still shrouded in mist, the stone walls and farmsteads reduced to dark, indistinct shapes. But above them, the sky was transforming, the grey giving way to streaks of pink and orange. The sun crested the horizon, casting a long, warm light across the frost-covered fell. The ice crystals sparkled, transforming the bleak moorland into a glittering, alien landscape.

Clara stood beside her brother, breathing deeply. The air tasted of peat and frost and wild, open space. She felt the warmth of the sun on her face, a gentle, tentative touch that seemed to thaw something deep within her. The grief was still there, a heavy ache in her chest, but it no longer felt like a bottomless void. It felt like a wound that was beginning to close.

"Thank you, Toby," she said softly, her voice barely audible above the wind.

He did not look at her, but she saw a small, satisfied smile tug at the corner of his mouth. "We will go up again tomorrow," he said. "And the day after. And the day after that. Until you remember how to breathe."

Clara nodded, pulling her cloak tighter around her shoulders. She looked out over the valley, at the mist slowly lifting to reveal the patchwork of fields and stone walls below. The world was still turning, the sun still rising, and somewhere deep within her, beneath the layers of hurt and disappointment, a tiny spark of something that might, in time, become hope began to glow. She turned to her brother, her face pale and drawn but her eyes clear, and together they began the long walk down the fell, toward the warmth of Greylees Hall and the slow, uncertain business of living.
