The young man’s name was Fergus O’Rourke, and he arrived in London with a single good coat, a sheaf of verses that he had not the courage to publish, and a letter of introduction to the Duchess of Merrow, who had known his mother at some forgotten watering-place and had long since forgotten her entirely. The coat was of a green so dark it was almost black, and he wore it with the air of a man who has decided that poverty, if it must be worn, should be worn like a plume.

The Duchess received him in a drawing-room of pale yellow silk, where the light came through the windows as though it had been filtered through honey. She was a large, handsome woman with a face like a Roman emperor’s and a manner that suggested she had never in her life been contradicted, except by her husband, whom she had outlived. She looked at Fergus O’Rourke with the mild interest one might accord to a rather curious piece of furniture that had arrived without a bill.

“Your mother,” she said, “was a Mostyn, was she not? I remember a Mostyn at Boulogne. She had red hair and a great many opinions, and she married a man who was always losing his umbrella.”

“She was my mother,” said Fergus, “and she lost the umbrella with him.”

The Duchess laughed, a low, comfortable laugh, like a cat purring in a warm room. “And what do you do, Mr. O’Rourke? Are you a poet? All Irishmen are poets, I am told, when they are not being something worse.”

“I am a poet,” said Fergus, “and I am also a beggar, but I have the good sense to beg only for what is worth having. I have brought you a letter from my mother’s cousin, who is a bishop, and who assures me that you are the kindest woman in London.”

“The bishop is a fool,” said the Duchess, “but he is a fool with excellent taste. Sit down, Mr. O’Rourke, and tell me something outrageous. I am so tired of the truth.”

So Fergus sat down, and he told her that her drawing-room was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and that the yellow silk reminded him of the inside of a peach, and that he would gladly live in it if she would have him, and that he would pay her in sonnets. The Duchess was delighted. She had not been paid in sonnets before. She had been paid in compliments, which are a debased currency, and in flattery, which is counterfeit, but never in sonnets. She asked him to dine on Thursday.

Within a month, Fergus O’Rourke was invited everywhere. He was invited to the Marchioness of Tintagel’s musical evenings, where he sat beside the piano and made remarks about Chopin that were not entirely true but were entirely delightful. He was invited to Lady Arabella Quince’s garden parties, where he stood beneath the lime trees and said that the roses were too beautiful to be moral, and that the lilies were the only honest flowers in England. He was invited to the Earl of Dunmoyle’s dinners, where he ate nothing and drank a great deal and said that the soup was a symphony and the fish was a sonnet and the roast was a tragedy, but a tragedy of the highest order. He was invited to the house of Mrs. Vandeleur, the American widow, who had a fortune made in railways and a collection of Pre-Raphaelites that she did not understand, and who told everyone that Mr. O’Rourke was the only man in London who had ever made her feel that her money was a burden she bore with grace.

But there was one house he was not invited to, and it was the one house he most wished to enter. It was the house of the Duchess of Merrow, in Grosvenor Square, where the yellow drawing-room was, and where the Duchess sat like a Roman emperor among her cushions, and where, on Thursday evenings, she gathered about her the wittiest and the wickedest and the most beautiful people in London, and gave them supper, and told them what to think.

Fergus had dined at the Duchess’s once, on the Thursday after his first visit, and had been brilliant. He had said that the only way to get rid of a temptation was to yield to it, and the Duchess had said that he was a dangerous young man, and the company had laughed. He had been asked again the following Thursday, and had been even more brilliant, and had said that the truth was rarely pure and never simple, and the Duchess had said that he was a scandal, and the company had laughed again. And then, on the third Thursday, he had not been asked. And on the fourth, he had not been asked. And on the fifth, he had walked past the house in Grosvenor Square and seen the lights in the windows and heard the sound of laughter, and had gone home to his lodgings in a street off the Strand, where the landlady was beginning to look at him with the eye of a woman who has not been paid.

He wrote to the Duchess. He wrote a letter that was a sonnet in prose, and he sent it with a bunch of white violets, which were the only flowers he could afford, and which he had stolen from a garden in Belgravia. The Duchess did not reply. He wrote again, and this time the letter was a satire on the vanity of duchesses, and he sent it with nothing at all, because he had nothing left to send. The Duchess did not reply. He called at the house in Grosvenor Square, and was told by the butler that her Grace was not at home, and the butler said it in a voice that suggested her Grace would never be at home again, not to him.

It was Mrs. Vandeleur who told him the truth, one evening at her own house, when the Pre-Raphaelites had been put away for the night and the railway fortune was sitting alone with a glass of champagne.

“My dear Mr. O’Rourke,” she said, “you have committed the one sin that London never forgives. You have been clever in the wrong house.”

“I do not understand,” said Fergus.

“You were clever at the Duchess’s,” said Mrs. Vandeleur, “and she forgave you, because she is clever herself, and she likes to be surrounded by clever people. But then you were clever at Lady Tintagel’s, and Lady Tintagel told the Duchess, and the Duchess said that you were her discovery. And then you were clever at Lady Arabella’s, and Lady Arabella told the Duchess, and the Duchess said that you were her protégé. And then you were clever at Dunmoyle’s, and Dunmoyle told the Duchess, and the Duchess said nothing at all. And then you were clever at my house, and I told the Duchess, because I am a wicked woman and I like to see what will happen, and the Duchess said that you were a young man who was being passed from hand to hand like a plate of sandwiches.”

“But that is absurd,” said Fergus. “I am not a plate of sandwiches.”

“You are an Irish poet with one good coat,” said Mrs. Vandeleur, “and you have been eaten at every table in Mayfair except hers, and now she will not have you because you are no longer hers to eat. She is a woman who likes to own things, Mr. O’Rourke, and she does not like to own things that have been owned by other people. It is a kind of virginity, but it is the virginity of the collector.”

Fergus went home and looked at his one good coat. It was beginning to fray at the cuffs, and there was a stain on the lapel where the Duchess’s butler had spilled a glass of claret, and it had been brushed and pressed and worn to so many houses that it seemed to him, in the gaslight, to be a coat that belonged to someone else. He sat down and wrote a poem, and the poem was about a nightingale who sang at the window of a duchess, and the duchess did not hear him because she was listening to a parrot, and the nightingale died of a broken heart, and the parrot was given a biscuit.

He sent the poem to the Duchess, and the Duchess read it, and she laughed, and she said to her friend Lady Tintagel, “That young man is really very clever. It is such a pity he is not a gentleman.” And she put the poem in a drawer with the letter about the white violets, and she never answered it, and she never invited him again. And Fergus O’Rourke, who had one good coat and a sheaf of verses and a genius for saying the wrong thing in the right house, went out into the London night and walked until the gaslamps went out, and then he went home and wrote a play, and the play was produced, and it was a great success, and the Duchess of Merrow went to see it, and she said it was the cleverest thing she had ever seen, and she wondered who had written it.
