It is a melancholy truth, yet one which I have had too frequent occasion to verify in the bitter apprenticeship of my own sufferings, that the world, whilst it is eager to bestow its gaudy laurels upon the shadow, recoils with instinctive horror from the living substance. When the tale, which I had penned in the silent agonies of midnight watchings, first made its appearance among the circulating libraries of our metropolis, it was lauded to the very skies. The drawing-rooms of Mayfair rung with its praise; grave divines and men of profound erudition declared it a work of singular genius, and I was told, by those who little suspected who cowered beneath their gaze, that the unknown author possessed a soul capable of fathoming the deepest abysses of the human heart. But when the seal of secrecy was broken—when it was at last bruited abroad that the hand which had drawn forth such stores of passion and reflection belonged to a woman, and that woman a wife—the scene was wondrously and terribly altered. The same lips that had pronounced the work divine now curled into smiles of derision, or parted in accents of unfeigned disgust; and the very beings who had sought to pierce the veil of my incognito, now shrank from my presence as from a contagion, deeming my intellect a usurpation, and my fame a dishonour to my sex.

I had not, in the artless enthusiasm of my spirit, foreseen this reverse. I had believed that the tribute of tears which my pages had wrung from many a gentle eye would have served as a passport to the sympathies of my kind; but I was rudely awakened from this delusion. I found myself an exile in the heart of London, surrounded by a multitude, yet utterly desolate—a mark for the pointed finger of ill-disguised contempt. But of all the pangs which this sudden and universal desertion inflicted upon me, there was one which, transcending the rest in bitterness, sank deep into my soul, and mingled its poison with every current of my thought. My husband, Mr. Vane, a man whose whole life had been devoted to the severer sciences, and who prided himself on the cold, unimpassioned nature of his investigations, felt his pride wounded to the very quick by the revelation of my authorship. He, who had moved with a measured and stately step among the philosophers and chemists of his day, could not brook the reflection that his wife had usurped a throne in the republic of letters, and had become an object of public commentary. He looked upon my success not as an honour reflected upon his house, but as a monstrous anomaly, a rebellion against the order of nature, which demanded a severe and exemplary correction.

It was on a night memorable in the calendar of my woes that he first broke silence on the subject. The weather had been sultry all day, and the atmosphere, overcharged with electricity, seemed to communicate its own oppressive influence to the human frame, rendering it restless and uneasy. I had sought my own apartment, and was gazing from the window upon the dimly lighted streets, where the lamps flickered in the gusty wind, when Vane entered. His countenance, naturally pale and expressive of a frigid self-possession, was now convulsed by the dark workings of a wounded pride; his eyes, usually so devoid of warmth, flashed with a sullen fire. He spoke not for several moments, but paced the room with a heavy tread, as if the tempest which was gathering in the heavens had found an echo in his own bosom. At length he stood before me, and his voice, though low, thrilled through my frame with a sense of impending doom.

"You have achieved your triumph, Clara," he said; "you have made yourself the talk of the town, the jest of the coffee-houses, and the pity of the wise. The name of Vane, which I had hoped to see associated with the sober honours of science, is now bandied about as the appendage of a novel-writing wife."

I would have spoken—I would have pleaded the natural impulses of my mind, the irrepressible longings which had driven me to the composition of that work; but he waved his hand with a gesture of command, and I was mute.

"I forbid you," he continued, and his tones grew harsh and imperious, "ever again to exercise a faculty which has already brought disgrace upon my house. I have borne with your midnight lucubrations, your absence of mind, your fits of abstraction, in the hope that they would pass away; but the evil has culminated, and I must apply the remedy. Your pen is to be laid aside for ever; your thoughts must be confined to the duties of your station, and if you cannot be content with the quiet obscurity of a wife, you shall at least be prevented from outraging the decencies of life."

He spoke, and departed. I remained motionless, as if some supernatural power had struck me into stone. The storm, which had been gathering all day, now burst forth with terrific fury; the thunder rolled in heavy peals above the housetops, and the lightning, glancing along the streets, seemed to reveal to my startled gaze the utter desolation of my fate. I had been stripped of the one blessing which had made life tolerable—the free exercise of that genius which, however others might despise it, was to me the voice of God within my soul. I was forbidden to create, to imagine, to pour forth the overflowings of my spirit upon paper; I was to be buried alive in the monotonous round of domestic trifles, and to stifle the deepest, holiest impulses of my nature.

They who have never felt the yearning to express the burning thoughts that consume them, may deem this prohibition a light thing; but to me it was as if the vital air were withdrawn from my lungs. I wandered through the house like a ghost, my eyes void of expression, my lips sealed in a despair which dared not utter itself. The society of London, which had already shunned me, had now no terrors for me; I was dead to the world, and the world was dead to me. But there was a fire within which could not be quenched, even by the mandate of a husband. It smouldered in my bosom, and fed upon my vitals; and in the dead of night, when all around me was hushed in slumber, I would sit and gaze upon the blank wall of my chamber, and feel that I was indeed a prisoner, bound hand and foot to the chariot-wheels of another's will. The conflict between the duty which I owed to my husband and the irrepressible demands of my own genius, was one which tore my soul asunder, and left me a prey to the most poignant misery. I dared not write, and I could not think without writing; my mind was a tempestuous ocean, which, denied its natural outlet, turned its fury inward upon itself. I had tasted of the tree of knowledge, and the fruit was bitter to my lips; I had aspired to a place among the immortals, and I was hurled down to the earth, a thing of shame and reproach. Such was the reward of my ambition; such the wages of my sin.
