When Mr. and Mrs. Thaddeus Bell took possession of Number Four, Wilmot Crescent, in the newly fashionable brick fields of Kentish Town, they arrived with little more than two trunks, a parrot in a brass cage, and an unshakeable conviction that the future would pay for the present. Thaddeus was a clerk at a legal scrivener’s office in Chancery Lane, a young man of exceeding good humour whose smile revealed teeth so white and regular that they seemed to promise virtue itself. His wife, Flora, was if anything more radiant, possessed of a complexion like strawberry cream and a manner so warmly engaging that even the most hardened tradesman felt his heart soften when she entered his shop. They were, by universal admission, the pleasantest couple who had ever settled in that damp and aspiring corner of the parish of St. Pancras.

The house itself was a modest Italianate villa, stuccoed across its front, with a small strip of garden behind where a sickly laburnum struggled against the London clay. The landlord, a Mr. Pettle, had let it on the strength of Thaddeus’s smile alone, asking no references and accepting the first quarter’s rent as a promise against the second. This set the pattern for everything that followed.

Within a fortnight of moving in, Flora had discovered the shops that lined the high road, and the shopkeepers had discovered her. Mr. Quirke, the upholsterer, was the first to extend credit. She wandered into his establishment on a Tuesday, trailing her gloved fingers across a bolt of crimson moreen, and remarked, with such genuine delight, that his window display was the finest she had seen since leaving the West End. Mr. Quirke, who had never been to the West End and had not previously cared that he had not, was so charmed that he offered to fit the entire parlour with draperies and a new suite of chairs, to be settled at Michaelmas. Flora accepted with a small, grateful inclination of her head that made Mr. Quirke feel he was doing something noble.

From the upholsterer the contagion spread. Mr. Tudge, the grocer on the corner of Lady Margaret Street, extended an account the day Flora lingered in his shop to praise the plumpness of his York hams and to ask, with real interest, after his rheumatic wife. He delivered twice weekly thereafter: Twinings tea, Westphalian bacon, Jamaican ginger, French plums in syrup, all entered in a leather ledger that grew fatter than any ham on his hooks. The butcher, a solemn Scotsman named Macandrew who had never been known to smile, found himself smiling when Flora told him his brisket was a work of art, and he began leaving extra kidneys wrapped in paper at no charge. The wine merchant, the baker, the chandler, the coal merchant on the Brecknock Road, the poulterer, the man who hired out pianofortes by the month, even the nurseryman who supplied a bay tree in a tub for the front steps, all of them opened their ledgers to the Bells, and all of them went home of an evening feeling that they had met something rare, a young couple who understood the value of kindness in a world grown coarse with calculation.

Thaddeus contributed his share. He had a way of clapping a tradesman on the shoulder and saying, “You are too generous, and I shall not forget it,” with such warmth that the tradesman felt he had been paid in something better than coin. When the piano arrived and Flora began to play, slightly out of key but with immense feeling, neighbours walking past Number Four would pause and think that here at last was a household where happiness had taken physical form. The parlour glowed with crimson draperies. The sideboard in the dining room was stocked with Bristol glass and Wedgwood, obtained from a china dealer in Hampstead Road who had been unable to resist Thaddeus’s offer to pay at the year’s end. The kitchen smelt always of something good. The parrot, whose name was Admiral, learned to whistle the first bar of a popular ballad, and visitors declared him a wonder.

None of these visitors ever mentioned the word credit, and neither did the Bells. The bills lay stacked in a drawer of the kitchen dresser, unopened, because opening them would have been an act of distrust, and the Bells were not the sort of people who distrusted others. Michaelmas came and went. Christmas approached. The accounts remained unsettled, and the tradesmen, who were by nature a patient and generous race when properly appreciated, continued to deliver.

It was in the third week of January that the reckoning began, not with a knock at the door but with the post. On a grey and dripping morning, no fewer than nine envelopes arrived together, each bearing the billhead of a different tradesman and each containing, in addition to expressions of esteem and hopes that the family was well, a polite but unmistakable request for settlement. The combined total was a sum that would have purchased the villa outright and left enough over for a second parrot. Flora opened them at the breakfast table while Thaddeus was spreading marmalade on his toast, and she turned the colour of the moreen draperies. Thaddeus, reading over her shoulder, grew pale, then paler still, and sat down rather suddenly on the pianoforte stool, which emitted a discordant note.

They spent the day in a state of paralysis, walking through their beautiful parlour as though it were a crime scene. Flora tried the pianoforte for comfort and found that it offered none. Admiral whistled his bar and was told to be quiet. They could not pay. They could not conceivably pay. Thaddeus’s salary was modest, and what was not modest had already been anticipated against next quarter. They considered flight, considered appealing to Flora’s mother in Lewisham, considered, in a moment of wild desolation, selling the parrot. None of these courses seemed feasible.

It was on the following morning, while they were sitting in a silence that Admiral had the decency not to interrupt, that a cab drew up outside Number Four and deposited upon the wet pavement a tall woman in a black bombazine dress, carrying a leather portmanteau and an umbrella of almost aggressive proportions. This was Miss Hephzibah Mordiford, Thaddeus’s maiden aunt from Chelmsford, a woman whom the Bells had avoided with the same diligence that they had applied to the opening of bills. She was not charming. She did not praise hams or compliment window displays. She had a face like a closed ledger and a manner that suggested she had never, in the whole of her sixty-three years, extended credit to anyone for anything.

She had, however, money. This was the thing about Miss Mordiford that everyone knew and that the Bells had spent two years trying not to need. She stood in the parlour, dripping onto the new Axminster carpet, and looked about her at the crimson draperies, the Bristol glass, the bay tree wilting by the front window, and the pianoforte standing in its corner like an accusation. She said nothing for a long moment. Then she removed her bonnet, placed it on the sideboard beside the Wedgwood, and announced that she had come to stay and to take charge.

The Bells were in no position to refuse. Within an hour Miss Mordiford had installed herself in the best bedroom, had demanded the key to the kitchen dresser drawer, and had spread the nine bills across the dining table with the precision of a military cartographer unfolding a map. She read each one twice. She made notations in pencil on the margins. She then summoned Thaddeus and Flora, who appeared before her like defendants, and informed them that the total was somewhat less than she had feared and considerably more than they deserved.

Over the days that followed, a transformation occurred at Number Four that was as thorough as it was unwelcome. Miss Mordiford visited each tradesman in person. She paid Mr. Quirke in full, but told him that his moreen was overpriced and his stitching uneven. She paid Mr. Tudge but cancelled all future deliveries of French plums and Jamaican ginger. She informed Macandrew that his kidneys were not a gift but an imposition upon people too polite to refuse them. She returned the hired pianoforte, despite Flora’s protests, and sold the bay tree to a publican further down the road. She installed a system of household accounts so detailed that every penny of butter was made to account for itself. Flora was forbidden to enter shops except to pay cash on the barrel, and Thaddeus was made to surrender a portion of his salary each week to a tin box that Miss Mordiford kept beneath her bed.

The tradesmen, who had loved the Bells, bore this reversal with what grace they could. Mr. Quirke confided to a neighbour that he preferred the old days but that the aunt had a point about the stitching. Mr. Tudge missed Flora terribly but could not fault the aunt’s arithmetic. Macandrew, who had never smiled before and now found he could not, simply nodded when Miss Mordiford entered his shop and accepted her coin without comment.

Miss Mordiford stayed. She stayed through February and March and into April, long enough to establish habits of economy that she declared would outlast her visit, though she privately doubted it. She found the parrot intolerable and the laburnum beyond saving, but she developed, in her austere way, a grudging respect for Flora, who bore her dispossession without complaint, and for Thaddeus, who eventually thanked his aunt, with a sincerity that surprised them both, for having saved them from a fate that his own pleasantness had done so much to bring about.

Miss Mordiford departed on a Thursday in May, leaving behind a house that was half as furnished and twice as solvent as she had found it. The Bells stood on the front steps and watched her cab carry her away up the wet road toward Chelmsford. When it was out of sight, they went back inside. The parlour seemed very bare without the draperies and the pianoforte. Flora sat down on one of the remaining chairs and looked at Thaddeus. Thaddeus looked at Flora. Admiral, from his brass cage in the corner, whistled his single bar. They both laughed, not because anything was funny, but because the house was quiet, and paid for, and theirs, and they had nowhere else to be.
