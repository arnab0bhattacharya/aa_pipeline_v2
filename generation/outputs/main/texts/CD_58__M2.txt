The fog hung over the port of St. Valéry not as a mere weather phenomenon, but as a thick, grey, conspiratorial shroud, smothering the masts of the fishing boats and blurring the line between the rotting quay and the indifferent sea. It was a damp, cold, and profoundly suspicious morning, the kind of morning in which honest men shrink into their coats and dishonest men sharpen their knives behind their backs. I, Captain Barnaby Thorne, stood upon the wet cobblestones, my boots squelching with a sound that seemed to echo the squelching of my own conscience, for I was a man of the sea, and the sea is honest in its brutality, whereas the land is a labyrinth of lies. I had come to this French shore to smuggle out a family of Huguenots, a job I had undertaken with a heavy heart and a lighter purse, for the risks were immense and the pay was paltry. But there, standing before me with the air of a woman who had just swallowed a lemon whole, was Madame Pichon.

Madame Pichon was the nurse. She was a creature of such profound and unyielding severity that one might have mistaken her for a piece of furniture that had gained sentience and decided to be difficult about it. She was small, round, and possessed of eyes that were not eyes at all, but rather two black, polished buttons set in a mask of pale, tight skin. She held in her arms a bundle of wool and a child, the child being a small, pale, and terrified creature named Jean, whose parents had been arrested by the revolutionary committee that very night. The committee, a body of men who believed that liberty was a word to be shouted until one’s throat bled, had taken the parents to the prisons, and the child to the edge of the cliff, but for the intervention of Madame Pichon, who had dragged the boy from the very jaws of tyranny with a ferocity that would have done credit to a bear.

"You are late, Captain," Madame Pichon said. Her voice was dry, like the rustling of old papers, and carried an authority that brooked no interruption. "The tide is turning. The Committee’s agents are already on the quayside, sniffing for the scent of treason."

"I am not late, Madame," I replied, trying to look as if I were not currently trembling with the chill and the fear of being hanged for my troubles. "I am merely... conducting a final inspection of the rigging. One must be thorough."

"Thoroughness is the enemy of survival," she snapped, shoving the child forward. "Give me your hand, Jean. We go. Now."

I looked at the child. He looked up at me with eyes that held no fear, only a vacant, terrible hope. I looked at Madame Pichon. She looked at me with eyes that held no hope, only a cold, calculating assessment of my worth as a vessel for her cargo. I had a suspicion, a nagging, persistent suspicion, that sat in the pit of my stomach like a stone. I had seen this woman before, or a woman very much like her, in the salons of Paris, whispering into the ears of the rich and powerful, recording their sins in a little black book. Was she a spy? Was she here to ensure that I, and perhaps the child, did not reach England? Or was she simply a woman of such intense and pathological devotion to her charge that she would burn the world to keep the boy warm?

"Here," she said, handing me the child. "Do not drop him. If you drop him, I will break your neck. If the Committee finds him, I will break my own. We are all bound by the same terrible fate, Captain. We are all doomed."

I took the child. He was light, lighter than a bird, and he smelled of milk and fear. I wrapped him in my own coat, a heavy wool thing that had seen better days, and tucked him against my chest. Madame Pichon followed, her steps quick and silent, like those of a cat that has been fed and is now hunting for sport. We moved through the fog, past the moored boats, past the customs house where a bored officer was dozing over a ledger, and down to the small, flat-bottomed boat that waited in the shadow of the pier.

The boat was rowed by a man named Gaspard, a local fisherman who asked no questions and charged a price that would have made a usurer blush. As we pushed off from the quay, the fog closed in around us, erasing the port, the city, and the world we knew. We were alone on the water, surrounded by a grey nothingness that pressed in on all sides. I could feel the child’s small heart beating against my chest, a rapid, frantic drumming that matched the beating of my own.

Madame Pichon stood at the bow, her face turned away from me, looking out into the mist. I could not see her eyes, but I could feel them. I felt a sudden, irrational urge to ask her why she had come. Why risk her life for a child whose parents were enemies of the state? But I knew the answer. It was not for the child. It was for the principle. It was for the idea that she, Madame Pichon, was right, and that the world, with all its chaos and cruelty, could still be ordered by a single, fierce will.

"We are being followed," she said, without turning around. Her voice was calm, almost pleasant, which made it all the more terrifying.

I looked back. The fog was thick, but there was a shape in the distance, a dark line on the water, moving with a speed that was too fast for a fishing boat. It was a rowboat, low and sleek, cutting through the mist like a knife.

"Gaspard!" I shouted. "Row! Row for your life!"

Gaspard, who had been lazily dipping his oars, sprang to life. The oars splashed into the water with a violence that sent spray into my face. We began to move, faster and faster, the little boat pitching and rolling in the choppy water. The shape behind us grew larger. I could see now that it was not a fishing boat, but a long, narrow skiff, with three men in it, their faces hidden by hoods.

"Is it the Committee?" I asked, my voice trembling.

"It is irrelevant," Madame Pichon said. "They will not catch us. The current is strong, and the fog is thick. They are blind, Captain. We are not."

I looked at her. She was smiling. It was a small, tight smile, the kind of smile that a cat gives a mouse just before it pounces. I realized then that she was not afraid. She was not worried about the skiff, or the Committee, or the fate of the child. She was enjoying this. She was enjoying the danger, the tension, the thrill of the chase. She was a spy, or a fanatic, or both, and she was using me, and the child, and the boat, as pieces in a game that I did not understand and could not win.

"Where are we going?" I asked.

"To safety," she said. "Or to death. It is the same thing, if one is brave enough to face it."

The skiff was closing in. I could hear the splash of their oars, the creak of the ropes, the muffled voices of the men. I looked at the child in my arms. He had stopped crying. He was looking at me with those vacant, terrible eyes, as if he knew something that I did not. I looked at Madame Pichon. She was looking at the skiff, her face illuminated by the faint glow of the setting sun, which was trying, and failing, to break through the fog.

"Give me the child," she said.

"No," I said. "Not yet. We must reach the coast."

"The coast is behind them," she said. "We are going to the island. The island of Les Écureuils. It is a place of exile, but it is a place of freedom. And it is a place where I can be trusted."

"Trusted?" I laughed. It was a bitter, hollow sound. "By whom?"

"By the truth," she said. "And by the lie. They are the same thing, Captain. They are both stories we tell to keep the cold out."

The skiff was very close now. I could see the faces of the men, hidden in shadow, their eyes bright with malice. I tightened my hold on the child. I looked at Madame Pichon. I did not know if she was a spy, or a saint, or a monster. I did not know if we would live or die. I knew only that I was in the hands of a woman who was more dangerous than the sea, more unpredictable than the fog, and more terrifying than the revolution itself. And as the oars of the skiff splashed closer, I realized that I was no longer the captain. I was merely the cargo.
