In the Midlands town of Tildmarsh, where the uneven pavements were bordered by squat houses of a greyish-yellow limestone that seemed to hold the afternoon’s dust with a tenacious and unyielding grip, the pursuit of science was generally held to be a doubtful and unprofitable exercise. It is true that the younger sons of the local gentry might dabble in a botanical sketch or the naming of a star, provided such amusements did not interfere with the more serious businesses of hunting, tenant-farming, or the calculation of tithe-rents; but a devotion to the earth’s ancient strata that required one to wander the quarries in a coat splashed with mud, carrying a hammer, and speaking of creatures that had neither horns to be hunted nor flesh to be eaten—this was a form of eccentricity that the practical mind of Tildmarsh could not easily accommodate. Mr. Arthur Fellingham, however, was the subject of this mild neighbourhood ridicule; and being a man of somewhat delicate constitution and a retired allowance from the Civil List, he bore the town’s judgments with a patience that perhaps concealed a deeper susceptibility. He had been a widower for fifteen years, and his one absorbing earthly care—if we may not call it his sole earthly joy—was his daughter Clara; a young woman whose dark, observant eyes and quiet self-command suggested a temperment perhaps too finely wrought for the ordinary provincial machinery of matches and settlements, yet who was, by the strict laws of society, entirely dependent on the small sum her father had managed to put by for her marriage portion.

Mr. Fellingham’s other great care, or rather, his intellectual passion, was his collection of fossils, gathered mostly from the surrounding Carboniferous limestone. He had a cabinet in his parlour where the delicate spirals of goniatites and the rough, ridged backs of brachiopods were arranged with a precision that would have done credit to a museum; though in Tildmarsh it was thought an oddity bordering on the unwholesome for a grown man to spend his candle-light in the examination of what were, after all, merely very old stones. Mr. Jonas Tudge, the owner of the largest quarry on the edge of the town, was one of the most pronounced in his contempt, being a man whose intellect, if robust and broad, was turned exclusively toward the extraction of marketable stone and the accumulation of shillings. Mr. Tudge had a son, young Samuel, a heavy-shouldered young man of few words but substantial prospects, whose bashful glances at Clara Fellingham during the Sunday services at St. Oswald’s were noted by several pairs of maternal eyes, though Clara herself seemed to receive them with an impassive and unresponsive regularity that might have discouraged a suitor of more delicate vanity.

It was in the autumn of 1834 that Mr. Tudge’s workmen, in blasting a lower seam of his quarry, came upon a remarkable specimen: the near-perfect outline of a large fish-like creature, its scales and fins traced in the dark bituminous shale with a startling, lifelike clarity. The workmen, knowing of Mr. Fellingham’s harmless mania, and perhaps hoping for a jug of ale for their pains, sent word to him. Mr. Fellingham, upon seeing the specimen, was struck with that peculiar thrill which the naturalist feels when the slow processes of geological time seem to yield up their secrets directly into his own hands. It was a creature of an order he had long hypothesised but never seen whole; a beautiful, terrible messenger from an age when the monstrous and the primitive held dominion. Mr. Tudge, seeing the gentleman’s excitement, was shrewd enough to perceive an opportunity. “I dunna know what it’s worth, Mester Fellingham,” he said, wiping his brow with a thick, chalk-dusted hand, “but my men do expect summat for the find, and I’m told they’re rare. I’ll not part with it under twenty pound.” Twenty pounds was exactly the amount of the first instalment of Clara’s marriage portion, which Mr. Fellingham had that very week received from his agent in London, and which lay, in golden sovereigns, in a small mahogany box in his bedroom. 

Mr. Fellingham went home in a state of agitation that was the more painful because he was accustomed to regard his scientific pursuits as the very antithesis of worldly turmoil. Here was a conflict between two forms of devotion, or rather, between an intellectual devotion and a paternal duty; and it is in the nature of such conflicts that the individual is rarely able to weigh the opposing motives with the calm arithmetic of a bystander. To purchase the fossil would be to delay, perhaps fatally, Clara’s settlement; for young Samuel Tudge, though plodding, was not likely to wait indefinitely for an answer, and the town’s gossip-mongers would not fail to attribute the withdrawal of the dowry to some hidden disgrace or financial ruin. Yet to let the specimen go, to see it perhaps sold to a London dealer or destroyed by the workmen’s careless hammers, seemed to Mr. Fellingham like a betrayal of truth itself—a yielding to the narrow, provincial spirit that had mocked him for twenty years. He sat in his parlour, the fire burning low, and looked at the empty space in his cabinet where the fossil would rest, and then at the door beyond which Clara sat sewing, her quiet breath a reproach and a claim upon his conscience.

He was still revolving these uneasy thoughts when Clara herself entered the room, and seating herself opposite him, began to speak with a directness that he had not anticipated. She had heard of the discovery from the quarry-men’s messenger, and had also, with that keen intelligence which young women often possess regarding their own future, divined the nature of her father’s dilemma. “Father,” she said, laying her hand on his arm, “I know of the fossil, and I know of the twenty pounds. You must buy it.” Mr. Fellingham protested, with a tremor in his voice, that her future was not to be sacrificed to an old man’s fancy; that young Tudge might be lost to her, and that he could not, in conscience, deprive her of the means to a respectable establishment. Clara, however, replied with a gentle firmness that surprised him: “I have no wish to marry Samuel Tudge, father, nor any man who would have me only for twenty pounds. If my settlement is to be the price of a stone, let the stone be a treasure to you. I would rather see you happy in your work than be made secure in a house where your science is held in contempt.” 

It is a common error among those who observe human nature from a distance to suppose that the young are incapable of a disinterested sacrifice, or that a woman’s notion of her own welfare is invariably bound up with the acquisition of a husband and a home. Clara Fellingham, in renouncing the dowry, was not actuated by mere romantic self-abnegation, but by a clear perception that a marriage contracted under such conditions would be a bondage of the spirit; and she had, moreover, an affection for her father’s intellectual life that made his joy a part of her own. Mr. Fellingham, thus relieved of the burden of choice by the very person for whom he had been anxious, yielded with a gratitude that was not unmingled with shame. The next day he carried the twenty pounds to Mr. Tudge, and the great fossil was borne in triumph to the parlour in Mill Lane.

The town, of course, did not alter its opinion. If anything, the purchase confirmed the general verdict that Mr. Fellingham was a foolish old man, and that his daughter was unlucky to have such a guardian. Mr. Tudge, receiving his money with a grunt of satisfaction, observed to the landlord of the Greyhound that “the old scholar had gotten a lump of rock for his daughter’s keep, and sarve him right.” Young Samuel, on being told that Miss Fellingham’s portion had been spent on a stone, transferred his bashful attentions to the daughter of a respectable grazier, and Clara was left to her quiet life of reading, walking, and caring for her father’s cabinet. But the human soul is not always proportioned to the world’s judgments; and in the slow, unobserved growth of their mutual understanding, the father and daughter found a richness of companionship that the practical matrons of Tildmarsh, with their calculable notions of happiness, could hardly have conceived. The fossil rested in its cabinet, a dark and silent witness to an ancient world; but in the small parlour, the light of an affectionate and unselfish sacrifice illuminated two human lives with a warmth that no mere calculation of shillings and settlements could ever bestow.
