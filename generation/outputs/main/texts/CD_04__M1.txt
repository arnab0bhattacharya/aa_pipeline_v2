It was the morning of the Great Day—the day for which the town of Muddleford had been scrubbed, and painted, and swept, and sworn at, for the better part of a twelvemonth; the day, in short, upon which Alderman Marmaduke Bumble, the most pompous man in the county of Warwickshire, and a gentleman whose corporation was a monument to the virtues of turtle-soup and civic pride, was to preside over the Banquet of Inauguration.

The new Town Hall of Muddleford—erected at the enormous expense of the rate-payers, and the still more enormous expense of their peace of mind—stood upon the market-place like a great, grey, unfinished tooth. It was to be a masterpiece of the Perpendicular style, a glory to the Midlands, a finger-post to Progress. But on this fatal morning, as the rain came down in slanting, spiteful lines, turning the cobbles of the market-place into a slippery mirror of the heavens’ disapproval, a terrible discovery was made.

The roof was not on.

Or rather, to speak with that precision which the subject demands, the roof was *partly* on—that is to say, the rafters were there, staring up at the sky like the ribs of a starved ox, but the slates, the lead, the ridge-tiles, the finials, and the gargoyles which were to have sneered at the populace for centuries to come, were nowhere to be found. And Mr. Phineas Crimp, the contractor, a man of red whiskers and roving eyes, had vanished, together with the balance of the subscription fund, leaving behind him nothing but a half-eaten pork pie and a bill for lime.

A murmur arose—a faint, ominous murmur, like the sound of a wasp in a bottle—among the assembled committee-men. But Alderman Bumble, who was at that moment admiring his reflection in the window of the Saracen’s Head, and adjusting the gold chain of office upon his expansive waistcoat, would hear of no disaster.

“Gloom, gentlemen, is the enemy of Progress!” he bellowed, turning upon the trembling sub-committee with a smile that was all teeth and no heart. “A little moisture! A little atmospheric humidity! It is the breath of the Almighty upon the cheek of Enterprise! We shall not be defeated by a shower, nor by a rogue, nor by a deficiency of slates! To the tables! To the turtle!”

And to the tables they went.

The scene which presented itself within the shell of the new Town Hall was one which Hogarth might have painted had he been in a particularly melancholy mood. The banqueting table, a mile of white damask, stretched from the Mayor’s chair to the very door, groaning under the weight of silver, crystal, and pyramids of pastry. But above this festive board, the sky—grey, weeping, and infinite—looked down with a calm indifference. The rain, no longer content to fall outside, began to fall *inside*, dripping from the naked beams, splashing into the soup tureens, and turning the plum pudding into a species of aquatic plant.

The guests, brave souls of the Muddleford gentry, huddled in their evening dress, their white neckcloths wilting like dying lilies. The ladies shivered in their silks; the gentlemen coughed into their hands; and the waiters, moving with the grim resignation of men marching to the scaffold, served the fish course under a steady drizzle.

Alderman Bumble rose to speak. He stood at the head of the table, a great, red-faced planet around which the lesser stars of the town council revolved. He was wet. His wig was wet. His satin knee-breeches were soaked through. But he was an Alderman of Muddleford, and he would not admit it.

“Citizens!” he cried, his voice booming with a false resonance that competed bravely with the drumming of the rain on the uncovered floor. “We are gathered here—in this—this Temple of Liberty!—to celebrate the triumph of the human will over the elements! Some might say,” he continued, glancing at a large drip that had just extinguished a candelabra, “that the weather is inclement. I say, it is *bracing*! It is the tears of joy which the heavens themselves shed in envy of our prosperity!”

A shudder ran through the assembly, partly from the rhetoric, but mostly from a gust of wind that swept through the missing western wall, carrying with it a dead leaf and a wandering rook.

“And as for the—ahem—the aesthetic peculiarities of our new edifice,” the Alderman went on, mopping his brow with a handkerchief that was already wringing wet, “let us say that it is *modern*! It is *ventilated*! It is a rejection of the dark, stuffy prejudices of the past! We breathe the air of the future! We dine beneath the canopy of Providence!”

He raised his glass. His hand shook, whether from cold or indignation it was impossible to say.

“To Muddleford!” he shouted. “To Progress! And to the—ah—*indefatigable* energy of our public works!”

“To Muddleford!” echoed the guests, their voices thin and watery.

They drank. The wine was cold. The soup was colder. And above them, the sky opened its floodgates with a vengeance. The rain came down in sheets, in buckets, in cataracts. It washed the rouge from the cheeks of the Mayoress; it dissolved the jelly in the trifle; it turned the floor into a shallow lake.

Through this deluge, Alderman Bumble continued to eat. He carved the roast beef with a determination that was almost heroic. He passed the potatoes with a smile that defied the laws of physics. He spoke of the new drainage system (which was, of course, the cause of the flood) as a triumph of engineering.

“It is merely a temporary overflow!” he declared, as a small wave washed his napkin off his knee. “A sign of abundance! A blessing! Let us not be downcast! Let us be *jubilant*!”

And the people of Muddleford, being English, and therefore possessed of a stubbornness that bordered on insanity, tried to be jubilant. They toasted the Queen. They toasted the Alderman. They toasted the rogue Crimp (though with less enthusiasm), and they toasted the roof that was not there.

As the evening wore on, and the candles guttered in their pools of rainwater, the scene became one of grotesque comedy. The guests sat up to their ankles in water, eating their trifle with spoons, while the rain fell upon their heads with a steady, rhythmic beat. Alderman Bumble, now soaked to the skin, his gold chain tarnished, his wig askew, stood like a great, sodden monument to denial.

“A triumph!” he bellowed, as the thunder rolled overhead. “A magnificent, glorious, *wet* triumph!”

And when at last the company dispersed, creeping away into the muddy night like drowned rats leaving a sinking ship, the Alderman stood alone in the ruin of his banquet. He looked up at the rafters, at the weeping sky, at the puddle that had once been the soup tureen.

“Crimp!” he muttered, shaking his fist at the darkness. “You shall be brought to book! You shall be indicted! You shall be—”

But the rain, indifferent to the threats of man, continued to fall, washing away the words, washing away the crumbs, and washing away the last vestiges of the Alderman’s dignity, until there was nothing left but the sound of the water, and the empty, echoing shell of the Town Hall of Muddleford.
