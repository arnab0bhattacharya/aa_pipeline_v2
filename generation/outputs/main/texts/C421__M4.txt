The rain in Zurich had a way of making everything feel provisional, as if the city itself were a sketch that might be revised by morning. Nadia Volkova had learned to carry her umbrella everywhere, though she often forgot it, and she would arrive at the university damp and distracted, her dark hair escaping its pins. She was twenty-three, a long way from the birch forests of her childhood, and she was studying medicine because her father had believed in the healing arts and because she had wanted, once, to prove that she could endure something difficult. The endurance had turned out to be the easy part. It was the loneliness that kept her awake.

She met him at a gathering in a narrow house off the Rämistrasse, in a room crowded with émigrés and students and men who smoked pipes and argued about the future of their country as if it were a patient on an operating table. His name was Lev Aronovich Shteyn, and he spoke with a low, unhurried voice that made the others lean in. He was not the loudest man in the room. He was the one the loudest men fell silent for. Nadia stood near the door with a glass of tea she did not want, and when he began to speak about the duty of the educated to the suffering masses, she felt something shift in her chest, a loosening, as though a window had opened in a stuffy room.

He found her afterward, when the arguments had broken into smaller arguments and the samovar had gone cold. He asked her name, her studies, her impressions of the city. He listened with his head tilted slightly, as if she were telling him something of great importance. When she confessed that she sometimes felt invisible here, he smiled and said that the invisible ones were the ones who saw most clearly. It was a foolish thing to say, she knew, the sort of thing a man said to a woman he wished to charm. But she had been invisible for so long that being seen, even falsely, was a kind of warmth.

She began to attend his circle. They met on Thursday evenings in the back room of a café near the Limmat, where the proprietor tolerated them because they bought coffee and argued quietly. There was a thin woman named Raya who translated pamphlets, and a former student of law called Grisha who had a nervous habit of cracking his knuckles, and a few others whose names Nadia learned and then forgot. Shteyn presided over them all with a gentle authority, assigning readings, asking questions, drawing out the shy ones. He remembered small details about Nadia—that she had a brother in Kiev, that she could not abide the taste of anise—and he would mention them casually, as if they were shared secrets. She found herself dressing more carefully on Thursdays.

It was Piotr who warned her. Piotr Zaleski was a Polish student in her anatomy course, a quiet young man with a long face and hands that were always stained with ink. He sat beside her in lectures and never spoke unless spoken to, and she had assumed he was merely shy, or perhaps dull. One afternoon, as they were leaving the dissection hall together, he said, without preamble, "You should be careful with Shteyn."

She stopped on the steps. The rain had paused, and the sky was a pale, uncertain grey. "What do you mean?"

"I mean what I said." He did not look at her. He was pulling on his gloves, his movements precise and deliberate. "He collects people. He collects their loyalty. It is not the same as friendship."

"You hardly know him," she said, and heard the sharpness in her own voice.

"I know his type." Piotr finally met her eyes. His gaze was steady and unreadable. "I have seen it before. In Warsaw. In Kraków. Men who speak beautifully and mean only themselves."

She laughed, a short, dismissive sound. "You are jealous."

He did not deny it. He only inclined his head, as if she had confirmed something he already suspected, and walked away into the mist. She watched him go and felt a flicker of triumph, which she later recognized as the beginning of her mistake.

The weeks that followed were the happiest she had known in Zurich. Shteyn invited her to help with a translation project, and they worked together in his rented room, which was small and neat and smelled of tobacco and paper. He told her about his childhood in Odessa, about the prison where he had spent two years for printing forbidden literature, about the wife who had left him for a man in Geneva. He spoke of these things without self-pity, as if they were merely facts, and she admired him for it. She began to imagine a future in which she was not alone, in which her life had a shape and a purpose beyond the next examination.

It was Raya who finally told her the truth, though not on purpose. They were walking home together after a Thursday meeting, and Raya, who was tired and had drunk too much coffee, said something about Shteyn's "little collection." Nadia asked what she meant. Raya looked at her with sudden alarm, as if she had said too much, and then, because she was not a cruel woman, she said it anyway. There had been others. There were always others. Young women, mostly, who were lonely and far from home and flattered by his attention. He did not seduce them, exactly. He simply absorbed them, and then, when they had given him everything he wanted, he lost interest. The translation project had been done by three different women before Nadia. None of them were in the circle anymore.

Nadia did not cry. She walked home alone through the wet streets, and she thought about Piotr's steady, unreadable gaze, and she understood that she had mistaken his honesty for jealousy because honesty was harder to bear. She thought about the way Shteyn had remembered her brother's name and her dislike of anise, and she saw now that these were not gifts but investments. She had been a patient he was studying, not a person he loved.

She did not go back to the café. She did not answer Shteyn's note, which arrived two days later and was written in his elegant, sloping hand. She saw him once more, from a distance, on the Bahnhofstrasse, walking with a new girl, a thin blonde with a serious face and a stack of books under her arm. He was leaning toward her, listening with his head tilted slightly, as if she were telling him something of great importance.

Piotr was in the library the next afternoon, as he always was, his long face bent over a volume of anatomy. She sat down across from him without asking permission. He looked up, surprised, and then, slowly, he smiled. It was a small smile, cautious and a little sad, and it was the most honest thing she had seen in months.

"I am sorry," she said. "You were not jealous."

"No," he said. "I was not."

They sat in silence for a while, and outside the tall windows the rain began again, soft and persistent, washing the city clean.
