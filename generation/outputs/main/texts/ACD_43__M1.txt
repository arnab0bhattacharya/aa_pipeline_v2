It was upon a raw and cheerless morning in the November of '87 that the singular business of the empty house in Kennington Road was brought to our notice. A fine, persistent drizzle had been falling since dawn, blurring the grimy windows of the city and turning the thoroughfares into a dull mosaic of wet cobblestones and blackened puddles. My friend, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, had been in a state of morose inactivity for the better part of a week, and the arrival of a telegram from Inspector Lestrade of Scotland Yard served as a welcome spark to his tinder.

“A death in Lambeth, Watson,” he announced, tossing the flimsy message across the breakfast table. “Lestrade is baffled, which is his natural state, but he professes to find the circumstances unusually obscure. The game, I perceive, is afoot. Fetch your coat and hat.”

A short cab drive deposited us before a tall, narrow house of soot-stained brick, its windows blind and shuttered. A constable stood at the door, his cape glistening with rain, and within we found Lestrade hunched in the empty front parlour, his hands thrust deep into the pockets of his overcoat. The room was bare, save for the body of a man lying upon the bare floorboards.

The deceased was a gentleman of perhaps fifty years, dressed in a well-cut but travel-worn suit of grey tweed. His face, turned towards the cold grate, was thin and aquiline, with a neatly trimmed dark beard flecked with grey. There was no sign of violence upon him; his clothes were not disarranged, and his hands, resting palms down upon the dusty boards, were quite relaxed. His eyes were closed as if in sleep.

“Heart failure, by the look of it,” said Lestrade, with the weary air of a man stating the obvious. “No marks, no struggle. A doctor is on his way, but I’ll wager my pension it’s natural causes. The puzzle is not how he died, but who in blazes he is and what he was doing in this derelict shell of a house.”

Holmes knelt, his sharp features intent. He examined the dead man’s hands, the crease of his trousers, the scuffs upon his shoes. “You are wrong on every count, Lestrade,” he said, his voice quiet but carrying an edge of certainty. “The death was not natural, the man was of some means, and he was here to meet someone.”

“Indeed?” Lestrade snorted. “And what tells you all that, Mr. Holmes?”

“His hands are those of a man who has done no manual labour, yet the right forefinger shows a slight callus, the sort acquired by a man who handles a pen for many hours a day. A clerk, perhaps, or an author. His shoes, though dusty, are of excellent quality, and the wear is even. He was not a vagrant seeking shelter. As for the meeting, observe the floor.” Holmes pointed to a place near the hearth. “Two sets of footprints in the thick dust. The deceased’s, and another’s—a smaller, more pointed shoe. Our visitor has since departed.”

Lestrade grunted, unimpressed. “Very well, a meeting of two. But where is the other man? And what of this?” He gestured to a single object of interest in the room: a folded newspaper lying beside the body. “An American paper. The *St. Joseph Gazette*, of all things. And look here.” He pointed to a date printed at the top of the page. October the 12th. It was ringed about with a firm, black circle of ink.

I picked up the paper. It was thin and brittle, the print faded. St. Joseph, I knew, was a town in the American West, a jumping-off point for the frontier territories. The ringed date was of no immediate significance to me.

Holmes took the paper from me, his eyes scanning the columns with that rapid, bird-like glance of his. He paid no heed to the circled date, but seemed to absorb the texture of the newsprint, the advertisements, the very flavour of the place. For a full minute, the only sound was the drip of water from Lestrade’s coat onto the floor.

“St. Joseph, Missouri,” Holmes murmured, almost to himself. “The date… October the 12th, 1867.” He looked up, and there was a strange, bright light in his grey eyes. “Good heavens, Lestrade, you have stumbled upon a ghost.”

“A ghost?” Lestrade scoffed. “I’ve stumbled upon a dead man in an empty house. I don’t deal in spirits, Mr. Holmes.”

“Then you will never solve this case,” Holmes retorted crisply. “Do you recall the affair of the Merchant’s Bank of St. Joseph? A robbery of some two hundred thousand dollars in gold coin. The work of a gang led by a man named Ezra Kinnear. The robbery was a success, but the gang was betrayed from within. Kinnear was shot dead during the ambush by the Pinkertons. The gold vanished. It was October the 12th, 1867.”

A silence fell upon the room. Even Lestrade was impressed into a momentary quiet. I remembered the name Kinnear now; it had been a sensation in its day, a tale of the wild American frontier that had reached even the quiet corners of England.

“This man,” Holmes continued, gesturing to the body, “has come to London to meet someone connected to that twenty-year-old crime. The ringed date is not a date of death, but a date of remembrance. A date of vengeance, perhaps. Or of payment.”

“But who is he?” Lestrade insisted.

“His identity is less important than his purpose,” Holmes said, rising to his feet. “But we may find it in his pockets. The coroner will be here shortly, Lestrade. I suggest you secure the house and allow no one else to enter. Come, Watson, our work lies elsewhere.”

Back in the familiar confines of our rooms at Baker Street, the fire was lit and the scent of shag tobacco filled the air. Holmes sat for some time in his favourite chair, his fingers steepled, staring into the flames. The newspaper lay on the table between us.

“A ghost, indeed,” he said at last. “The ghost of Kinnear’s betrayed partner, a man named Silas Croft. It was always rumoured that Croft was the one who sold Kinnear out to the law, and that he took the lion’s share of the gold for himself. He disappeared completely after the affair. And here, twenty years later, a man is found dead with a newspaper from the scene of the crime.”

“You believe the dead man is Silas Croft?” I asked.

“No, Watson. The dead man is the *other* ghost. The one who has been hunting Croft all these years. A relative of Kinnear’s, perhaps? A son seeking justice? Or a fellow gang member cheated of his share? It matters not. What matters is that he found Croft.”

“And Croft killed him?”

“Precisely. The meeting was arranged. Croft, now living under an assumed name in London, was confronted by this man from his past. There was a quarrel. The death was made to look like a seizure. Croft, a man of some cunning, would know how to do it. A little poison, perhaps, that mimics a heart attack. The doctor will find it, if he is thorough.”

“But how do we find Croft?” I asked. “London is a vast haystack.”

Holmes smiled, a thin, predatory expression. “We have the needle, my dear fellow. The newspaper. Croft will not expect a man in London to know the significance of St. Joseph and the year 1867. He believes he is safe. But this paper tells us he is a man of a certain age, American, and possessed of a secret. I shall make a few discreet enquiries in the American expatriate community. There are a few old-timers in the City who trade in secrets and memories. They will know of a man who arrived in London twenty years ago, a man with money but no past. A man who fits the description of Silas Croft.”

He rose and took his coat from the rack. “The chase has begun, Watson. The fox is a cunning one, but he has been running for twenty years. His legs are tired.” He paused at the door. “Expect me before midnight. I fancy I shall have a name for our Inspector Lestrade.” And with that, he was gone, leaving me alone with the silent newspaper and the chilling thought of a long-delayed reckoning finally come home to roost in a cold, empty house in Lambeth.
