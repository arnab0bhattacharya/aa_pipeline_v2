The rain had not let up since the Isle of Wight, and by the time the packet train sighed into Southampton, the gas lamps along the platform were smeared into yellow bruises on the wet glass. Captain Elias Thorne stepped down with his valise in one hand and, in the other, a letter. It was a small thing, really—a rectangle of cheap paper gone soft at the corners from a dead man's grip—but it had crossed an ocean in his breast pocket, and he had begun to feel toward it the way a man feels toward a stone in his boot: not heavy, precisely, but impossible to forget.

The dying passenger had been a Mr. Halloway, a quiet fellow who had come aboard at Buenos Aires with a cough and a trunk and little else. He had lasted eleven days. On the last, when the ship's surgeon had gone below to see to the morphine, Halloway had caught Thorne's sleeve and pressed the letter into his hand. "Blythe House," he had said, in a voice like a file drawn over wood. "Near Lyndhurst. Give it to the family. Only to the family. Not to the post." And then, because dying men are permitted their dramatics, he had added: "They will know what it means." He had not said what it meant himself, and Thorne, who had seen men die in the ordinary way of fevers and bad water, had not thought to ask.

The house was a half-hour's drive from the station, a squarish Georgian affair of gray stone with a drive of wet gravel and a brass knocker in the shape of a fox's head. A parlor maid took his card. A butler, gray and correct, took his hat. And then he was shown into a drawing room where a fire burned and three people waited: a woman in black, a young man with his father's heavy jaw, and a girl of perhaps sixteen who sat very straight and did not look at him at all.

"My name is Thorne. I'm master of the *Corinthian*, out of Buenos Aires. A passenger of ours died on the passage. Mr. Halloway. He asked that this be delivered to you personally." He held out the letter.

The woman took it. She was not old, but her face had the closed quality of a house after a funeral. She turned the letter over once, read the name on the front—her own name, Thorne presumed—and then, without a word, she crossed to the fire and laid it on the coals.

Thorne was so startled that he took a step forward. "Madam—forgive me—he was most particular—"

"Mr. Halloway," said the woman, watching the paper curl and blacken, "was most particular about a great many things. He was particular for twenty years. He is particular no longer." She turned back to him with a smile that had nothing in it. "You have done your duty, Captain. You need not do more. My son will see you out."

The young man did, with a shilling for the cab and an apology that was worse than nothing. The girl had still not looked at him.

Thorne took lodgings near the docks, a pair of rooms above a chandler's shop kept by a widow named Mrs. Pike, and it was there, two nights later, that he came home to find his door ajar and his life turned out like a pocket. His sea chest had been upended, his shirts scattered, his logbooks fanned across the floor. The mattress had been slit. The little japanned box where he kept his mother's rings and his certificate of master lay open and empty on the bed—though, and this was the curious thing, the rings were still there, winking in the lamplight, and the certificate, too. Whoever had come had not wanted what a thief wants. They had wanted paper. Every letter he owned—his sister's, his agent's, a bill from a chandler in Gravesend—had been opened and left where it fell. Only one thing was gone, and it was not a thing he had known he possessed: the envelope. The envelope the letter had come in, with the name of Blythe House on it in Halloway's cramped hand, which Thorne had tossed into the drawer of the nightstand and forgotten.

He sat down on the ruined mattress with his heart going like a pump and thought, with the peculiar clarity of fear, that he had carried something across the Atlantic without knowing what it was, and that someone had now come to take it back.

The investigator's office was in London, up a stair in a court off the Strand, and smelled of coal smoke and cold tea. His name was Mr. Ambrose Vellacott, and he was a small, neat man with a waxed mustache and the air of a solicitor who had seen too much of human nature to be surprised by it. He listened to Thorne's account without interrupting, his pen moving now and then in a fine, economical hand.

"You did not read the letter," he said at last.

"It was sealed. And it wasn't mine."

"Admirable. And inconvenient." Vellacott laid down his pen. "I know the name Halloway. I know it the way one knows the name of a disease one has had. There was a matter, some twenty years ago, in the Argentine—a land scheme, a great deal of other people's money, and a young man who was blamed for it and vanished. His name was Halloway. The family here disowned him, changed their name to something quieter, and set about pretending he had never existed. That is the ordinary way of such things. But there was a second matter, a year or two later, a killing in Rosario—a creditor, they said, who had followed him out—and the man who was killed had a brother, and the brother was the sort who does not forget. I have heard it said that the brother has spent twenty years looking for Halloway, and that he is a patient man, and that he is in England."

Thorne's mouth had gone dry. "The letter—"

"Was the thing the brother wanted. Halloway knew he was dying. He knew the brother would come for him, and he knew what the brother would do to him if he found him alive. So he made a bargain with the only people who could hurt him more than the brother could: his own family. The letter was a confession, I should think. Signed, witnessed, and sworn. Something that would put the whole Argentine business in its true light—and perhaps something that would put the family in it too, for they knew more than they ever admitted. He wanted them to have it. He wanted them to be afraid of it, and of him, for the rest of their lives. A dying man's last vanity." Vellacott shrugged. "They burned it. I cannot say I blame them. But the brother did not know it was burned. He only knew a letter had come. And now he knows a ship's captain brought it, and he will wonder what else the captain knows, and he will wonder it in the way of a man who has waited twenty years."

Thorne sat very still. Outside, the Strand was a low rumble of wheels and voices, ordinary and enormous. "What do I do?" he said.

Vellacott picked up his pen again and drew a sheet of paper toward him. "You do what you have done, Captain. You go to sea. And if you are wise, you do not come home for a while." He began to write. "I will give you the name of a man in Liverpool who can find you a berth by Friday. And I will tell you one thing more, for which you may thank your stars: you never read the letter. Whatever it said, it died with the fire. You are only a man who carried a stone in his pocket and did not know its weight." He sanded the page and folded it. "That is the safest thing in the world to be, just now. A man who knows nothing."

Thorne took the paper. It was raining again, and the gas lamps along the Strand were smeared into yellow bruises, and somewhere in the city a patient man was asking questions that had no answers. He went down the stair and out into it, and he did not look behind him.
