The house of Ashcombe stood amid the gentle undulations of the Hampshire downs, a seat of ancient peace and prosperity, whose grey stone walls and ivy-mantled towers seemed to breathe the very spirit of settled and beneficent power. It was here, in the autumn of the year 1831, that I first knew what it was to be loved, and, in the same hour, to learn the bitter lesson of dependence. I was an orphan, the daughter of a scholar whose learning had been his only wealth, and whose death had left me to the cold charity of the world. Mr. Ellwood, the master of Ashcombe, had been his friend in youth; and when he learned of his old companion’s ruin and death, he sought me out, and brought me, a trembling and desolate child, to this stately home. Mrs. Ellwood, a woman of proud and commanding spirit, received me with a graciousness that I then believed to be the very soul of kindness. She called me her dear child, and bade me look upon her as a mother; and in the warmth of that welcome, the cold and lonely feeling which had possessed my heart melted away like frost before the morning sun.

For many years I was happy. I was treated in all things as a daughter of the house; my education was tended with a care that equalled that bestowed upon the young Eleanor, the only child of the Ellwoods; and if there were moments when I felt a certain difference in the manner of the servants, or when Mrs. Ellwood’s eye would rest upon me with a thoughtful and measuring gaze, I attributed it to the natural reserve of a mind occupied with affairs too vast for my comprehension. My gratitude was boundless. I loved Mrs. Ellwood as a daughter loves a mother; I looked up to her as to a being of a superior order, and I strove to repay her goodness by an affection that knew no bounds, and a devotion that was the very breath of my existence.

But the time came when the scene was changed. The young Charles Ellwood, who had been absent at the university, returned to Ashcombe, a man in the bloom of youth and the pride of intellect. I need not say how my heart acknowledged him as its lord. The love that had grown up in my soul from childhood, pure and unselfish as the light of heaven, now burst into a flame of passionate adoration. He saw it, and returned it with a fervour that equalled my own. We walked together in the twilight groves of Ashcombe, and I listened to his vows of eternal constancy with a rapture that seemed to lift me above the common lot of mortals. I was no longer the dependent orphan; I was the chosen bride of him who was to be the master of that fair domain. The world was all brightness and joy; and I thanked God in my heart for the trials that had led me to such a haven of bliss.

But the storm was gathering in the clear sky. Mrs. Ellwood, who had observed our growing attachment with a watchfulness that I had not suspected, now began to change her manner towards me. Her kindness did not cease, but it assumed a different character. It became a thing of words rather than of deeds, of hints rather than of embraces. She would speak to me of my father’s misfortunes, of the charity that had taken me in, of the debt I owed to the house of Ashcombe, and of the necessity of proving my gratitude by a submission that should be entire and unquestioning. These allusions, at first rare and delicate, became by degrees frequent and pointed. She would dwell upon the grandeur of the family, upon the noble alliances that were expected for Charles, upon the duty of a young man in his position to look for a wife among the daughters of the great and the powerful. And then she would turn to me with a smile that was meant to be kind, and say that I must not be surprised if Charles’s fancy should change, and that a girl of my good sense would not dream of encouraging a passion that could only end in disappointment.

I listened to these words with a heart that was torn by conflicting emotions. I could not doubt the truth of her assertions, for I was but too conscious of my own unworthiness. I was a beggar, a pensioner upon their bounty, and I had dared to lift my eyes to the son of the house. The thought was madness; and yet the love I bore him was the life of my soul, and I could not tear it from my breast without tearing my heart asunder. I began to watch Mrs. Ellwood’s countenance, to tremble at her approach, and to interpret every word she spoke as a fresh reminder of my degradation. The very kindness of her tone seemed to me a reproach; and when she called me her dear girl, and kissed my forehead, I felt that her lips were cold, and that her heart was not with her words.

The climax came one evening in the library. Charles had spoken to his mother of his love for me, and of his resolution to make me his wife. Mrs. Ellwood heard him with a calmness that was more terrible than anger. She did not reproach him; she did not even mention my name. She spoke of the duties of his station, of the expectations of his family, of the folly of a boyish passion, of the necessity of prudence and of a suitable alliance. She spoke of me only as a dependent, a child of charity, whom it was his duty to protect, not to ruin. And then she turned to me, and with a smile that I can never forget, she said, “My dear girl, you must not be so selfish as to think of your own happiness at the expense of his. You owe us a debt that can never be repaid; and the best proof of your gratitude will be to retire from a position which you can never hope to fill with credit to yourself or to us.”

These words fell upon my heart like a thunderbolt. I stood for a moment speechless and motionless, while the room seemed to swim around me. Then I turned to Charles, and saw the conflict of love and duty in his countenance. He was silent; and in that silence I read my doom. I withdrew from the library, and shut myself up in my chamber, where I gave way to the agony that consumed me. I wept until I could weep no more; and then I rose, and looked at myself in the glass. I saw a face that was pale and haggard, and eyes that were red with weeping. I thought of the gulf that lay between me and the man I loved; I thought of the kindness that had been shown me, and of the debt that I could never repay; and I resolved to fly from a place where I could only bring sorrow and disgrace upon those who had been my benefactors.

The next morning I rose early, and, leaving a letter upon the table, I quitted Ashcombe for ever. I did not know whither I should go; I only knew that I must go. I walked through the park, and looked back upon the house, and the tears streamed down my cheeks as I thought of the happy hours I had passed beneath its roof. The sun was rising over the downs, and the birds were singing in the trees, and the world was all beauty and peace; but my heart was desolate, and I felt that I was an outcast from the only home I had ever known. I hurried on, and the trees closed behind me, and the house was hidden from my sight; and I went forth into the wide world, a wanderer and a beggar, but with a heart that was not wholly broken, for it still cherished the memory of a love that was pure and unselfish, and a gratitude that no unkindness could destroy.
