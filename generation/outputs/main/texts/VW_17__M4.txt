The gas lamps in Lincoln’s Inn were not yet lit, and the dusk had the quality of a held breath, a bluish hush falling over the quadrangles where the stone urns and the solemn windows seemed to be listening for something that never came. Agnes Rayner, walking with her hand resting lightly on the arm of Humphrey Gaskell, felt the pressure of his sleeve beneath her fingers, the good, dark, dependable stuff of it, and thought—or rather the thought drifted through her, unbidden, like a leaf on the surface of a pond—that she had walked this way a hundred times and never before noticed how the shadows of the chimney stacks lengthened and lay across the path like bars. Bars. She almost said the word aloud. Humphrey was speaking of a case, a point of equity, some tangled matter of trusts and testamentary intention, and his voice went on, steady, reasonable, a little monotonous, like water running over stones; and she, murmuring now and then, yes, indeed, quite so, was thinking how strange it was that one could be engaged to be married and feel nothing more than this—this mild approval, this sense of a door closing softly, without violence, in a distant part of the house.

He was a good man. That was the thing one said, and it was true; he was good, in the way that a well-made chair is good, or a solid roof, or a clock that keeps accurate time. Her father had said it, rubbing his hands before the fire in the study at Kensington, his voice rich with satisfaction: the most promising of my pupils, Agnes, the most promising by far; a man who will go far, mark my words, and a man, my dear, who will be kind to you. Kind. She had looked at the fire, at the little caverns of red and gold that opened and closed in the coals, and had said yes, Papa, and had meant it, for there was no reason to say anything else. There was no reason not to. That was the difficulty, if difficulty it could be called; that was the thing that nagged at her in the small hours, when the house in Kensington settled into its creaks and sighs and the gaslight in the street threw a pale rectangle on the ceiling. There was no reason. One could not refuse a good man simply because—because what? Because his hand, resting now on hers, was a little damp? Because he cleared his throat before beginning a sentence, as though to clear a path for it? Because when he looked at her there was in his eyes something so complete, so unquestioning, that she felt herself dwindling, becoming a picture, a portrait of a woman, rather than the woman herself?

The fog was rising from the river, creeping up the lanes, softening the edges of things. A clerk hurried past with his collar turned up, his boots making a hollow sound on the cobbles; a door opened somewhere and let out a wedge of yellow light and the murmur of voices, and then the door shut and the murmur ceased and the darkness closed again. Humphrey was saying something about the wedding—about the honeymoon, about a house he had in mind, in Bloomsbury, near the Square, with a garden; and she heard herself answer, that would be lovely, Humphrey, a garden would be lovely, and she thought of the garden at Kensington, of the laurel bush that had grown so tall and dark, of the way the rain gathered and fell from its leaves in heavy drops, one after another, after the shower had passed. She had stood beneath it once as a child, letting the drops fall on her upturned face, and had felt something—a shiver, a delight, a sense of being utterly alive and utterly alone—that she had never since recaptured. Was that what she was looking for? A drop of cold water on the face? She almost laughed, and Humphrey, feeling the tremor of her arm, looked down at her with concern.

“Are you cold, my dear?”

“No,” she said. “No, not at all. I was thinking of—of the garden at home. The laurel. It is nothing.”

He pressed her hand, and she felt the pressure as one feels a weight laid upon one, a benediction, a claim. He was so sure of her. That was the thing. He was so sure, and she was not sure of anything, and the not-sureness was like a stone in her shoe, a small, hard, constant irritation that she could not stoop to remove. She looked at his profile, at the firm chin, the nose a little too straight, the hair already thinning at the temples though he was barely thirty; and she thought, I shall look at that profile for forty years, and I shall grow accustomed to it, and perhaps in time I shall not see it at all, as one does not see the furniture in a room one has lived in too long. And then she thought, with a sudden, inexplicable pang, of a man she had seen once on a steamer crossing to Dieppe, a man with a brown face and a scar above his eyebrow, who had stood at the rail and smoked and said nothing, and whom she had never spoken to, and who had disappeared into the crowd at the harbour, and who was more real to her, more vivid, more troubling, than Humphrey Gaskell would ever be.

“You are very silent,” he said.

“I am listening,” she said. “I am listening to the evening.”

And indeed there was something to listen to: the distant rumble of a cart, the cry of a newsboy, the faint, persistent dripping of water from some gutter or eaves, and beneath it all the great, slow, breathing silence of the Inn, the silence of centuries, of wigs and gowns and dusty precedents, of men who had argued and judged and gone to their graves, and whose voices seemed to linger in the stones, faint, indistinguishable, like the murmur of the sea in a shell. She thought of her father, of his study, of the rows and rows of law reports bound in calf, and of the way he would sit among them, a small god in his own heaven, dispensing justice, or what passed for justice, in the certainties of his age. She was to marry into that world. She was to become a part of it, a hostess, a wife, a mother perhaps, and the door would close behind her, softly, without violence, and she would be safe, and she would be good, and she would be—what? She did not know. She could not see.

They had come to the gate, and beyond it the street, and the lamplighter was going his rounds, touching his pole to the wicks, and one by one the lamps bloomed into pale, hissing flowers of light. Humphrey stopped and turned to her, and took her other hand, so that both her hands were held, and she felt the warmth of his palms, the slight roughness of his skin, and she looked up at him and saw, in the new lamplight, his face, earnest, patient, full of a devotion that asked nothing but to be allowed to serve.

“Agnes,” he said. “You are happy?”

She opened her lips to say yes. It was the answer that was expected, the answer that was true in all the ways that could be counted and measured and written down. But the word did not come. Instead there came to her, with a clarity that was almost painful, the memory of the laurel bush, of the cold drops falling on her face, of the child she had been, standing alone in the wet garden, shivering and exultant. And she thought, I am not that child any more. I am here, in this street, in this fog, holding this man’s hands, and the lamps are being lit, and the evening is coming on, and I have said yes, and I shall say yes again, and again, and the door will close, and I shall be happy—I shall be happy—and she heard her own voice, as though from a great distance, saying:

“Yes, Humphrey. I am happy.”

And the lamplighter passed on, and the fog thickened, and the two figures stood for a moment in the pool of yellow light, and then moved on, arm in arm, towards the waiting house, and the dark, and the long, long years.
