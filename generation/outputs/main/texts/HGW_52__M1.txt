The trouble began, as such things generally do, with a rumour, and the rumour, like all the best of them, contained a grain of solid, inconvenient fact. It was whispered in the taprooms and the banks of the Trent that the supply of coal from the North had ceased. Not dwindled, mind you, but stopped dead, like a tap turned off at the mains. Within a week, the rumour had hardened into a dreadful certainty. The great furnaces of the Potteries had cooled to a sullen grey. The trains on the London and North Western stood silent and cold upon their tracks. In our own city of Wednesbury, a place built on the twin pillars of iron and coal, the very air, usually thick with the soot of industry, became unnervingly clear and quiet. A great and terrible silence had fallen upon the Midlands.

Mr. Silas Croft, my father, was a man of substance, a coal merchant of considerable standing. He had a yard down by the canal, a fleet of six fine dray-horses, and a nose for profit that was as sharp and as reliable as a bloodhound's. When the first whispers of the shortage had reached him, he had done what any sensible man of business would do. He had bought. He had bought up every last sack and every last ton from the smaller pits and private dealers, paying good money for stock that was about to become worth its weight in gold. His yard, which was usually a mountain range of black, glittering fuel, was now a veritable continent of it. Behind a high brick wall and a stout oak gate, Silas Croft sat upon the largest private stock of coal in Staffordshire.

The city, meanwhile, began to freeze. Autumn was giving way to the sharp teeth of winter, and the temperature dropped with a grim, daily regularity. The first to feel it were the poor, in their little brick back-to-backs where the only luxury was a single grate. Then the middle classes, in their solid villas, began to ration their fuel, huddling in one room and watching their breath cloud in the unheated parlours. The hospitals were in a panic. The workhouses were overflowing not with the idle, but with the frozen. And all the while, the smoke-stacks of Wednesbury stood naked against the grey sky, and the silence of the engines was the loudest sound in the land.

It was on a Tuesday, I think, that the motor-car came. A great, black, official-looking machine that puffed and snorted its way to our front door, scattering chickens and attracting a crowd of curious, cold faces. Out of it stepped a gentleman from the Ministry of Supply, a man with a weary face and the sort of London tweed that looks utterly out of place in the Black Country. My father received him in his study, a room that was, by the grace of a small, cunningly-tended fire, the only warm place in the house.

The offer was, as my father described it afterwards, “handsome.” The government, in its desperation, would purchase his entire stock at a price that would make a king blush. They would send the army to manage the transport. It was a matter of national importance, the gentleman said. A matter of life and death.

My father, who was no fool, listened patiently. He steepled his fingers, a gesture he used when calculating the precise angle of an advantage. “A matter of national importance, you say?” he began, his voice mild and reasonable. “And it is a national emergency that has, by a curious coincidence, made my coal worth ten times what it was a month ago. It seems to me, sir, that the nation is willing to pay for its emergencies. My price, therefore, is not the handsome one you offer, but the proper one. The market price.”

The gentleman from London grew pale. He spoke of patriotism, of the suffering of the people, of the duties of a citizen. My father, in return, spoke of supply and demand, of the risks of business, of the sanctity of a contract. It was a collision of two entirely different worlds. The gentleman left in a state of high agitation, promising to return with a better offer and, if that failed, with a legal authority that would not be so easily dismissed.

The news of the government's visit, and my father's refusal, spread through the town with the speed of a fire in a dry field. The first sign of the new sentiment was not a petition, but a stone. It came through the window of the front parlour on Wednesday evening, followed by a coarse shout that was swallowed by the wind. On Thursday, a small, silent crowd gathered at the gate of the yard. They were not a mob, not yet. They were neighbours: Mr. Gable from the ironworks, his face black with impotent fury; Mrs. Pritchard from number fourteen, whose youngest had the croup; old Mr. Hemlock, the retired schoolmaster, who simply stood and stared at the wall as if he could will it to fall.

It was in the midst of this simmering quiet that my daughter, Eleanor, came down to the study. She is a clever girl, my Eleanor, too clever by half. She had been at the University in Manchester and had come back with her head full of books and theories and a distressing sympathy for the sort of people who write manifestos. She stood before my father's desk, her hands clasped behind her back, her expression one of grave disappointment.

“Papa,” she said, her voice steady and clear. “You must sell the coal.”

My father looked up from his ledger, his eyes like flint. “Must I, indeed? And who is to compel me? The rabble at the gate? The King's ministers?”

“I am not speaking of compulsion. I am speaking of what is right,” she said. “There are people out there who cannot warm their children. Mr. Hemlock’s wife is ill with bronchitis. The fever hospital has fuel for two more days. This is not a question of profit and loss, Papa. It is a question of human decency.”

“Decency!” my father snorted, rising to his feet. “Decency does not pay for the roof over your head, girl! Decency does not feed the six horses in my stable. I am a businessman. I saw an opportunity and I took it. That is the way of the world. Your books and your socialist pamphlets may tell you otherwise, but that is the hard, cold truth of it.”

“The world can be changed, Papa,” she insisted, her voice rising with a passion that was entirely her own. “The workers, the people out there, they create all the wealth. They dig the coal and they smelt the iron. And when a crisis comes, a man like you, who does nothing but own and sell, holds the whole of society to ransom for his own enrichment. It is monstrous. It is theft.”

I have seen my father face down union agitators and hostile magistrates without turning a hair, but the righteous fury in his own daughter's eyes seemed to unsettle him. “Theft?” he roared. “It is my property! Bought with my money! What is this nonsense you are talking? Property is the very foundation of everything!”

“It is a foundation built on the backs of the people you are now freezing!” Eleanor shot back. “You speak of the market as if it were a law of nature, like gravity. But it is a system, Papa, a system designed by and for men like you. And it is a system that is now eating itself. You are sitting on a mountain of warmth while the city starves for it. They will not ask much longer. They will take it.”

As if to punctuate her words, a great, rhythmic chant began to rise from the crowd at the gate. It was not the angry, chaotic shouting of a riot, but something far more ominous. A single, repeated word, spoken by a hundred cold and weary voices.

“Coal! Coal! Coal!”

My father stood rigid, his face pale and set. He looked from his daughter to the window, where the shadows of the crowd were darkening against the evening light. He had built his life on the certainty of ledgers and the predictable logic of commerce. Now, a new and terrifying arithmetic was being shouted at him from the darkness, an arithmetic of cold, and hunger, and the collective will of a people pushed to their limit. The fortune he had hoarded behind his brick wall felt, for the first time, less like a treasure and more like a bomb.
