It was upon a raw November morning, with a yellow fog coiling through the streets, that Mrs. Hannay was shown into my friend's consulting-room. She was a woman of perhaps thirty-five, handsome but worn, with the pallid look of one long resident in tropical climes, and her hands, gloved in threadbare black, trembled against her umbrella.

"I fear I intrude, sir," she began, with a nervous catch in her breath. "But my husband, the Reverend James Hannay, is in great perplexity, and I dare not go to the police."

My friend, who had been standing at the window examining a clay fragment, turned sharply. "Pray be seated, madam. You intrude upon nothing. If you will state the facts plainly, and in the order in which they occurred, I shall be better able to judge whether I can be of service."

Our visitor composed herself with an effort. "My husband has spent fourteen years at the mission station of Li-Cheng, in the province of Sze-Chuan. He returned to England a fortnight since, on medical advice, and we have taken a house in Pinner. Three days ago his luggage arrived from the docks. It had been sent in advance by the shipping company."

"And there was something amiss with it?"

"Everything, sir, and nothing. The boxes were unbroken, the locks untouched. But my husband is a very precise man. He had packed his own trunks in China, arranging each article in a manner familiar to him. When he opened the largest trunk, he found that every garment, every book, had been removed and replaced. The order was different. The packing had been done by another hand, and with extraordinary care. Even his under-linen had been refolded."

My friend's eyes narrowed. "He is certain of this?"

"As certain as a man can be of his own belongings. His Chinese New Testament, which he always wraps in oilskin at the bottom, had been placed near the top. His riding-boots had been stuffed with paper, a thing he never does."

"A thorough search," my friend murmured, "and a patient one. The searcher did not merely rummage. He examined, and he restored. That is significant. What was he looking for, and what did he find? Tell me, madam, does your husband keep anything of value among his possessions?"

"Nothing of monetary value. His books, his sermons, a few curios. There is a carved ivory figure, a gift from a convert, which might be worth a few pounds."

"And papers?"

She hesitated. It was but a moment, yet my friend, whose eye missed nothing, leaned forward.

"Come, madam. The truth, if you please."

"There are his journals, sir. He kept a daily record of his work at Li-Cheng, covering all fourteen years. They are bound notebooks, fifteen in number. They were in the trunk."

"Are they still?"

"They are. But my husband believes that the binding of the ninth volume has been disturbed. The thread is broken at the spine, and he is certain it was intact when he packed it."

My friend rubbed his hands together. "A specific volume! The ninth, covering, I take it, his fifth and sixth years at the station. We progress. And now, the man who follows your husband. Tell me of him."

"It began a week ago, when we arrived in London for a few days. My husband walks every morning to the British Museum, where he consults the Oriental manuscripts. He first noticed the man upon the third day. He was standing across the street from our hotel, watching the entrance. He made no attempt to approach."

"His appearance?"

"He is short and thickset, with a sallow complexion and very dark eyes. He wears a grey ulster and a soft cap. My husband, who has lived long among the Chinese, says his features are partly Oriental, though he passes for a European."

"That is a common type in the treaty ports. Has the man spoken to your husband?"

"Never, sir. He merely follows. When my husband enters the Museum, the man waits outside. When my husband returns, the man is behind him, at a distance of perhaps thirty yards. He never closes that distance."

My friend rose and took down a file from his shelves. He turned the pages rapidly, paused at one, and read for a moment in silence. Then he closed the book and rang the bell.

"Mrs. Hannay," said he, "I shall need to see your husband's journals, particularly the ninth. Can you bring them to me this afternoon?"

"I can send them by the afternoon train, sir."

"Do better. Bring them yourself. I shall wish to ask your husband a few questions. But first I must visit the docks. You spoke of the shipping company?"

"The Blythe and Mersey Line. The trunks came upon the steamer _Merope_, from Shanghai."

My friend nodded and scribbled a note. "You may leave us now, madam. I shall expect you at four o'clock. And pray tell your husband to vary his route to the Museum tomorrow. Let him go by way of Holborn, and not by the direct road. It would be as well to confound our shadow for one day longer."

After she had gone, my friend sat for some minutes in silence, tapping the clay fragment against his palm.

"You see the shape of it?" he asked at length.

"I confess I do not."

"Consider. A missionary returns from the interior of China. His luggage is searched with consummate care, not for gold, but for information. One journal alone is examined. A man, half Chinese, follows him through London but does not approach. If he wished murder or robbery, he would have acted. He does not wish these. He watches because he is uncertain. The search of the luggage has not given him what he sought, or has given it only in part. He follows your friend to learn whether he has yet communicated with anyone, whether he has spoken of what he knows."

"But what does he know?"

"That, my dear fellow, is precisely what the ninth journal will tell us. I have here a record of disturbances at Li-Cheng. In the autumn of 1887, a French consul named Drouet disappeared in that district and was never found. The local mandarins reported his death by bandits. But there were rumours of a more sinister sort. If Hannay was at the station then, and if he saw what he was not meant to see, his journal becomes a dangerous document."

"And the man who follows him?"

"Is almost certainly an agent of the Viceroy of Sze-Chuan, or of certain interested parties in Shanghai who have profited by Drouet's removal. The search of the luggage was done by a confederate at the docks. The surveillance is the second line of inquiry. They are not yet certain that Hannay possesses evidence against them, and they dare not act until they are sure."

By four o'clock Mrs. Hannay had returned, bringing with her the Reverend James Hannay himself, a tall, gaunt man with a stoop and a gentle, puzzled face.

My friend wasted no time. "Sir, I believe your life may be in danger. I shall be plain with you. In 1887, when you were at Li-Cheng, a European disappeared in that district. What did you see?"

Hannay's face went grey. He clutched the arm of his chair. "Good God, sir! I have spoken of it to no one. I was returning from a village to the south, by a path through the hills. I saw a party of soldiers, and with them a man in European dress, bound. I did not know him then. I learned afterward that it was the Frenchman, Drouet. I saw the officer in command receive a purse of silver from a Chinese civilian. I did not understand the language, but I saw the exchange."

"And the officer's name?"

"Colonel Wen. He commanded the garrison at Chen-Yuan."

"And you recorded this in your journal?"

"I did, sir. I recorded everything. I was afraid, and I wrote it down that night, with the date and the hour."

My friend took the ninth journal, turned to the entry, and read it. Then he closed the book and placed it in his desk.

"Sir," said he, "this journal must not return to Pinner. It will be safer with me. I shall communicate tonight with the Foreign Office, and also with the French Embassy, for this matter concerns them deeply. As for the man in the grey ulster, I think we shall have no further trouble from him. When he learns that the journal has passed from your hands, his purpose is at an end. But you will do well to remain in London for the present, and to keep within doors after dark."

Hannay bowed his head. "I am in your hands, sir. I thank God I came to you."

My friend smiled and waved his hand. "Thank the fog, Doctor," said he, turning to me. "It is upon such days as these that the Englishman, deprived of his exercise, is driven to seek advice. And the criminal, deprived of his visibility, is driven to make mistakes. I think our friend in the grey ulster will find that he has followed one path too many."
