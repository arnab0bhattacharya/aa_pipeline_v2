The house in Hill Street was one of those quiet, severe Mayfair houses that seem to have been built for the express purpose of concealing a scandal, and old Lord Ambermere, who had retired from the Diplomatic Service with the rank of Ambassador and a reputation for saying the most shocking things in the most faultless French, had chosen it precisely because it looked as though nothing could possibly happen in it. He was a small, dry, exquisite old gentleman, with a face like a crumpled rose-leaf and eyes that had outlived the necessity of being sincere, and he passed his evenings—for he had long since given up the tedious business of dining out—in a high-backed chair by the fire, with a glass of Tokay at his elbow and his great-nephew, the Honourable Lucian Vane, upon the hearthrug.

Lucian was nineteen, and had lately come up to London with the intention of being a success, and with no other equipment for that career than a handsome face, a small allowance, and a nature so transparently guileless that it had already begun to be spoken of in certain houses as simplicity, and in others as something very near to folly. His mother had commended him to Lord Ambermere’s care in a letter of which the ink was hardly dry, and Lord Ambermere had received him with the air of a man accepting a parcel he had not ordered.

“The first thing to understand about society,” said Lord Ambermere, on the third evening of their acquaintance, “is that it is composed entirely of people who are afraid of one another, and that its whole art consists in concealing this fear beneath a manner of perfect indifference. You will find, Lucian, that a man who says exactly what he means is regarded as a public danger, and a man who says exactly what he does not mean is elected to the best clubs. Sincerity is the last refuge of the man who has nothing else to offer. I recommend it to you only when you have exhausted every other resource.”

“But, sir,” said Lucian, “I had always been taught that a gentleman’s word—”

“A gentleman’s word,” interrupted Lord Ambermere, “is a very beautiful thing, and, like all beautiful things, it should be kept in a glass case and never used. Consider the case of my old friend the Duke of Crayle. He was the most truthful man in England, and he died universally respected and entirely forgotten. Whereas Lord Peveril, who never told the truth in his life except by accident—and then always apologised for it—was mourned by three hundred people who had never met him, and a whole column in the *Times* which he would have enjoyed far more. No, my boy, if you wish to be a success, you must learn the great art of saying the opposite of what you mean, and saying it with an air of such profound conviction that nobody will dare to contradict you.”

“The opposite of what I mean?” said Lucian, whose brow had assumed the solemnity of a young man receiving a religious instruction.

“Precisely. When you meet a woman who is plain, tell her she is beautiful, and she will believe you, because she has been telling herself so for years. When you meet a man who is a fool, tell him he is a genius, and he will make you his executor. When you are asked your opinion of a book you have not read, praise it for its style, and when you are asked about a picture you have not seen, praise it for its feeling. It is not deceit, Lucian—it is diplomacy. The diplomat is simply a man who has discovered that the truth is a weapon which should never be drawn in public.”

“And does everyone do this?” asked Lucian, in a voice of some dismay.

“Everyone who succeeds. The rest write letters to the newspapers and are known as characters, which is the polite word for failures. There are, of course, a few persons who are clever enough to say what they mean and make it sound like the opposite, but they are the aristocracy of the profession, and you are not yet ready for them.”

Lucian said nothing, but he looked at his great-uncle with the expression of a young disciple who has received the first lesson of a difficult and rather dubious faith, and Lord Ambermere, observing him over the rim of his glass, permitted himself a smile of a very dry and private character. He had expected the boy to be shocked, or at least to be puzzled, and he had looked forward to the amusement of watching him flounder. What he had not expected was that Lucian would go away and take him at his word.

The occasion for the experiment came a fortnight later, at a dinner at the house of Mrs. Athelstan Grieve, a lady of large fortune and small discrimination, whose drawing-room was the chosen battlefield of that season’s most determined mothers. Lucian, who had been placed next to the Honourable Miss Christabel Grieve, a young woman of decided opinions and a profile of the kind that is described as distinguished when it is not described as something else, found himself at a loss for conversation until he remembered his great-uncle’s instruction.

“Miss Grieve,” he said, with an air of profound conviction, “I must tell you that I have never in my life seen a complexion so radiant, or eyes so perfectly calculated to drive a man to distraction.”

Miss Grieve, who had been informed by her mother that morning that she was looking decidedly yellow, and who had spent the afternoon in a state of silent fury against the mirror, turned to him with a start. “Do you really think so, Mr. Vane?” she said, and her voice had lost something of its usual asperity.

“I think so?” cried Lucian, warming to his theme. “I am convinced of it. Indeed, I was saying to Lord Ambermere only yesterday that the season would be entirely insupportable without you.”

Miss Grieve blushed—an operation which she had not performed for some years, and which she found, upon the whole, rather agreeable—and proceeded to tell him, at considerable length, of her views upon the education of the poor, which were, as it happened, both sensible and dull. Lucian listened with an appearance of the most profound attention, and at the end of her discourse informed her that he had never heard the subject treated with such brilliance. Miss Grieve, who had never before been called brilliant by anybody, decided on the spot that Mr. Vane was a young man of remarkable discernment, and told her mother so before the evening was out.

Lord Ambermere, who had watched the whole performance from the other end of the table, was at first merely amused. But when, three days later, he learned that Lucian had informed Lady Marchmont—a woman of formidable intellect and no beauty whatsoever—that she was the most beautiful creature in London, and that Lady Marchmont had thereupon invited him to her box at the Opera; and when he further learned that Lucian had assured Mr. Justice Harkness, a gentleman of the most rigid and unyielding conservatism, that his views upon the reform of the divorce laws were those of a man of genius, and that the Judge had been heard to say that the young fellow was the only person in London who understood the question; and when, finally, he discovered that Lucian had told the Duchess of Wexcombe, whose parties were the dullest in England, that her Wednesday evenings were the one intellectual pleasure of his existence, and that the Duchess had immediately placed him at her right hand for the remainder of the season—Lord Ambermere began to be aware of a sensation which he had not experienced for thirty years, and which he was obliged to recognise, after some reflection, as astonishment.

“Lucian,” he said, when the young man next presented himself in Hill Street, “I am told that you have become the most popular young man in London.”

“I have followed your advice, sir,” said Lucian, with the modest air of a scholar who has mastered his lesson. “I have said the opposite of what I meant on every occasion, and I find that it answers perfectly. Lady Marchmont believes I am in love with her, Mr. Justice Harkness believes I am a Tory of the old school, and the Duchess believes I am a genius. I have never been so much sought after in my life.”

“And what do you mean,” asked Lord Ambermere, with a certain curiosity, “when you tell Lady Marchmont she is beautiful?”

“I mean that she is the plainest woman I have ever seen, sir, and that her conversation is entirely about her health.”

“And when you praise the Judge’s views?”

“I mean that they are absurd, sir, and that he is a man of no intelligence whatever.”

“And the Duchess?”

“Her parties are the dullest in London, sir, and her mind is a perfect wilderness.”

Lord Ambermere was silent for a moment. “And do you never,” he said at last, “say anything that is true?”

“I have discovered,” said Lucian, “that truth is a very dangerous commodity, sir, and that the man who deals in it is likely to be thought a fool. I have therefore taken your advice, and I have said the opposite of what I mean on every occasion. I find that I am universally respected, and that I have not made a single enemy. It is the most wonderful thing in the world.”

Lord Ambermere looked at his great-nephew for a long time, and it seemed to him that the boy’s face had lost something of its openness, and had acquired instead a certain polish, like a stone that has been too long in the water. “And are you happy?” he asked.

“Happy?” said Lucian. “I have succeeded, sir. I am invited everywhere, and I am talked about, and I am told that I shall certainly be made a private secretary to some Cabinet Minister before I am five-and-twenty. I have discovered that the world is exactly as you described it, and that the art of pleasing is the art of saying what is not. I am very much obliged to you, sir, for the lesson.”

Lord Ambermere turned his glass slowly between his fingers, and for the first time in many years he did not know what to say. He had intended to make a cynic, and he had made a success; and it occurred to him, with a pang that was almost remorse, that the difference between the two is one that the world has never been able to distinguish, and that he had perhaps done the boy an injury which no amount of Tokay could repair.

“Lucian,” he said at last, “I have been a diplomat for forty years, and I have learned that the man who says the opposite of what he means is a very clever fellow, and that the man who says what he means is a very brave one. But the man who does not know which he is doing—that man, my boy, is a diplomat indeed. I congratulate you. You have surpassed your master.”

And Lucian, who did not understand him in the least, bowed and smiled, and went away to dine with the Duchess of Wexcombe, where he was very brilliant, and said a great many things that he did not mean, and was universally admired.
