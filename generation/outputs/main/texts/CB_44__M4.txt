The rain had beaten against the window-panes of the little house in Ardwick all the afternoon, and now, as the dusk gathered, it slackened into a steady, sullen drizzle that blurred the gas-lamps in the street below and turned the cobbles to a sheet of black glass. Within, the fire burned low in the grate, for coals were dear, and Mrs. Ellen Ashworth, seated at her work-table with the last of the light upon her needle, was stitching a mourning-hem for a customer in Chorlton who had buried her husband in the spring and was even now, Ellen suspected, beginning to think of colours less sombre. The child, little Thomas, lay asleep in the truckle-bed in the corner, his breath soft and even, one arm flung out upon the coverlet as though he would grasp at some dream-companion.

She had been three years a widow. Three years since the fever had taken Edmund Ashworth from her side in the space of a single week, leaving her with a house half-furnished, a name that his family considered besmirched by the very fact of her existence, and a boy of two summers who had his father’s stubborn chin and his mother’s dark, questioning eyes. The Ashworths of Bowdon—Edmund’s elder brother, Mr. Humphrey Ashworth, and his maiden sister, Miss Caroline—had made it plain from the first that they considered the marriage a mésalliance. Ellen had been a governess; her father had been a clerk; her dowry had been a small annuity and a chest of linen. They had not attended the wedding. They had, however, attended the funeral, and at the funeral Humphrey had spoken to her for the first time in two years, and had said, with the air of a man performing a painful duty, that the family would be willing to overlook the past and to provide for the child’s education, if she would consent to place him in their care.

She had refused. She had refused with a quietness that had surprised even herself, and Humphrey had gone away in a cold fury, and Miss Caroline had written her a letter of six pages, full of scripture and reproach, which Ellen had read once and then burned, watching the flames eat the words as though they were the accusations of an enemy.

Now, on this wet November evening, there came a knock at the door. Ellen rose, and looked first at the child, then at the window, and then at the clock upon the mantel. It was half-past five. She smoothed her apron, tucked a stray wisp of hair behind her ear, and went to open.

The man upon the step was tall, and stooped a little, as though the rain had beaten him down. He wore a great-coat of good cloth, but the collar was turned up against the weather and the hat pulled low, so that his face was in shadow. He held a leathern case beneath his arm, and when he spoke his voice was low and careful, as though he feared to be overheard.

“Mrs. Ashworth? I am Mr. Josiah Pennyman, of the firm of Pennyman and Hoyle, solicitors. I believe you have received my letters.”

She had. Two of them, in the past month, each couched in the language of disinterested concern, each requesting the favour of an interview. She had not answered either. She had hoped, by silence, to make it plain that there was nothing to discuss. But here he was, upon her doorstep, with the rain dripping from his hat-brim, and the child stirring in his sleep behind her, and the fire burning low.

“You had better come in,” she said.

He came in, and stood in the little parlour with his hat in his hand, and looked about him at the plain furniture, the worn rug, the sewing-machine upon the table, the row of books upon the shelf—her father’s books, and Edmund’s, and a few of her own. He looked at the child, and his face changed, though he said nothing. Then he sat down in the chair she indicated, and placed his case upon his knee, and said:

“I am here at the request of Mr. Humphrey Ashworth. He is anxious—deeply anxious—about the welfare of his nephew.”

“He has a strange way of showing it,” said Ellen. She had resumed her seat, and her needle, and she did not look at him. “He has not seen the boy these three years.”

“He believes that to be your doing, madam.”

“He may believe what he pleases. I have never forbidden him the house. I have never refused him a sight of his nephew. He has chosen to stay away, because I would not give up my son to be brought up in his image.”

Mr. Pennyman was silent for a moment. He was a man of perhaps fifty, with grey hair and a thin, intelligent face, and eyes that seemed to see more than he chose to say. He had the manner of one who has spent his life among the quarrels of families, and has learned to hold his peace.

“Mrs. Ashworth,” he said at last, “I am not here to argue with you. I am here to lay before you, plainly, the terms which my client is prepared to offer. He will settle upon the boy an income of two hundred pounds a year, to be paid to you, so long as you reside in a house of his choosing, and under the supervision of a person whom he shall appoint. He will pay for the boy’s education at a school of his selection. He will, in short, provide for him as though he were his own son—upon one condition.”

“And that condition?”

“That the boy shall be removed from your influence. That he shall be brought up by Miss Caroline and himself, in the principles of the family. That you shall see him only at such times as they shall permit.”

Ellen’s needle paused. She looked up, and her eyes were bright, but not with tears.

“And if I refuse?”

“Then, madam, I am instructed to say that my client will take such steps as the law allows to prove you an unfit guardian. He will not succeed—I tell you plainly, he will not succeed—but the attempt will be made. There will be scandal. There will be expense. There will be the dragging of your name through the courts, and the exposure of your circumstances, and the questioning of your character, and the boy himself will be the centre of it all. Is that what you wish?”

She set down her needle. She rose from her chair, and stood before the fire, and looked down into the embers, and for a moment she was silent. Then she said:

“You are a lawyer, Mr. Pennyman. You deal in facts. Let me give you one. When my husband lay dying, he took my hand—this hand—and he said to me, ‘Ellen, whatever they offer you, do not give them the boy. Promise me.’ And I promised him. I promised him in the sight of God, and I have kept that promise, and I will keep it. You may tell Mr. Humphrey Ashworth that he may do his worst. He may take me to court. He may drag my name through the mire. He may prove me poor—I am poor. He may prove me friendless—I am friendless. But he cannot prove me false to my husband, and he cannot make me give up my son.”

Mr. Pennyman looked at her for a long moment. Then he closed his case, and rose, and said:

“I will tell him, madam. But I must warn you—he will not be moved by sentiment. He is a hard man.”

“So am I,” said Ellen. “I have had to be.”

He went to the door, and paused, and looked back at the child upon the bed. “He has his father’s face,” he said, and his voice was gentler than before. “I knew Edmund Ashworth when he was a boy. He was a stubborn lad, too.”

“He was,” said Ellen. “And he was worth ten of his brother.”

Mr. Pennyman smiled, a little sadly, and put on his hat, and went out into the rain. She watched him go from the window, and then she turned back to the fire, and stirred it, and put on a little coal—more than she could afford—and sat down again at her work. The child slept on. The rain beat against the panes. And Ellen Ashworth, widow, seamstress, mother, took up her needle once more, and stitched, and stitched, and set her face like flint against the world.
