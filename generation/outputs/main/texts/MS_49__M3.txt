It was in the deepening twilight of an English autumn that Clara sat alone in her chamber, the casement thrown open to admit the bleak, sighing wind, which swept across the desolate heath and rattled the bare branches of the elms, as if Nature herself mourned in unison with that anguish which had of late become the unwelcome inmate of her bosom. Before her, upon the table, lay a packet of letters, directed in the well-known, hurried hand of her brother, Arthur; letters which she had once welcomed with a transport of enthusiastic joy, but which she now feared to open, dreading, with a prophetic shudder, the dark and melancholy tales they unfolded. She had been the one, she remembered with a pang sharper than the wintry blast, who had first kindled in his ardent mind the flame of liberty; she had painted the glorious struggle of the Greeks as a sacred and sublime crusade, a theatre where heroic spirits might win imperishable laurels; and he, with the impassioned generosity that formed the leading feature of his character, had yielded to her persuasions, and torn himself from the peaceful shades of his native village to encounter the perils of a distant land. Alas! how bitterly had her bright visions faded, and how terrible was the retribution which her own heart was now called upon to endure!

She took up the latest scroll, which had that morning arrived from the shores of the Morea, and, by the dim light of her solitary taper, read again those passages which had already pierced her soul with terror and remorse. "I came to this land, my beloved Clara," Arthur had written, "with my heart full of high and exalted hopes; I came to offer my life upon the altar of freedom, and to mingle my blood with that of heroes. heroes. But, oh, what a sad and terrible reality has broken upon my spirit! I find not the united bands of patriots, contending with noble self-devotion against a foreign tyrant; I see only distracted factions, torn by jealousies and sordid strife, where Greek betrays Greek, and the sacred cause of liberty is made a cloak for the basest and most selfish passions. The camp is a scene of squalid misery and disorder; the leaders, instead of devoting themselves to the salvation of their country, are engaged in ceaseless cabals to advance their own aggrandizement; and the soldiery, destitute of discipline and moral principle, too often turn their arms against each other. I have witnessed scenes of such brutal depravity, such cold-blooded cruelty, as would make the very stones cry out against humanity; and I have felt my own heart sink within me, and my faith in man, ay, and in the virtue of the cause itself, wither and decay. I am alone, Clara, alone amid a howling wilderness of vice and misery; the glorious dreams which you and I indulged are fled forever, and I am left to struggle against despair. I cannot turn back, for my honour is pledged; but I feel that I am sacrificing myself to a phantom, and that my best energies are wasted in a hopeless and degrading contest."

Such were the mournful effusions which had succeeded to those glowing descriptions and enthusiastic anticipations that once breathed from every line of his correspondence; and as Clara read and re-read them, she seemed to behold her brother, pale and haggard, standing on some desolate rock, gazing over the dark and stormy sea, a prey to disappointment and anguish, while she, the unconscious cause of his wretchedness, sat afar off in luxurious peace, a self-condemned criminal. The wind moaned around the ancient turret, and she started as its wailing voice seemed to whisper in her ear a terrible accusation. "I sent him forth," she cried, clasping her hands in an agony of grief; "I, with my vain and babbling enthusiasm, urged him to leave his happy home, his tranquil pursuits, and all the endearments of kindred and friendship, to mingle in a strife where all is foul and terrible. Oh, Arthur, my brother, my more than brother! if you perish, if your noble spirit sinks under the weight of these accumulated horrors, your blood will be upon my head! I have been the blind and reckless instrument of destruction to the being I loved best on earth; and my remorse will be more enduring than the marble of his tomb."

But the darkest and most harrowing of these communications was yet to come. It was a letter written in the wild and incoherent style of a mind laboring under the pressure of some overpowering calamity, and dated from a ruined monastery near the walls of Missolonghi, where Arthur had sought a temporary retreat from the horrors of the camp. He described himself as worn down by sickness and fatigue, his frame emaciated, and his spirits broken by the continual spectacle of cruelty, treachery, and woe. He spoke of a recent event which had filled him with horror and despair: a massacre of the innocent inhabitants of a neighbouring village by a band of lawless soldiers, who called themselves the champions of liberty, but whose deeds were more fiendish than those of the barbarians they professed to oppose. He had interposed his authority to save the victims, but in vain; he had been mocked and insulted, and threatened with violence; and he had fled from the scene, sick at heart, and disgusted with the whole race of man. "I am weary of my life," he exclaimed; "I am weary of the name of liberty, which has been so profaned and degraded; I am weary of the sight of blood and tears, and of the groans of the dying and the captive. Oh, that I could once more behold the green fields and the quiet shores of England! But that joy is denied me; I am bound by a chain of iron to this land of desolation; I must endure to the end, whatever that end may be; and when it comes, it will be a release from torments such as I could not have believed it possible for human nature to endure."

Clara's tears flowed fast as she read these words; she wept, not only for her brother's sufferings, but for the blighted hopes and the shattered illusions of her own soul. She had believed that the world was growing better, that the spirit of freedom was spreading, and that the dawn of a brighter day was at hand for oppressed humanity; but this sad correspondence had opened her eyes to the melancholy truth, that the passions and the vices of men are the same in every age and in every clime, and that the fairest and holiest causes are often made the instruments of the basest and most selfish purposes. She felt as if she had been walking in a vain and deceitful dream, and as if the ground had suddenly given way beneath her feet, leaving her suspended over a gulf of darkness and despair. She could not, she would not, believe that Arthur would perish; she clung to the hope that his strong sense of duty and his inherent nobility of character would sustain him through his trials, and that he would yet return to his home, purified and ennobled by suffering. But the suspense, the fearful suspense, was almost intolerable; and she looked forward with a mixture of hope and dread to the arrival of the next courier from the East, which might bring her tidings of his fate, either of life or death.

Weeks passed away, and no tidings came. The winter set in with unusual severity; the snow lay deep upon the ground, and the winds howled around the old mansion with a fury that seemed to echo the tumult of Clara's mind. She wandered through the desolate rooms like a restless ghost, pale, silent, and absorbed in her own melancholy reflections. She avoided the society of her friends, for she could not bear to hear them speak of the war, or of the glorious cause for which her brother was suffering; she shrank from their congratulations and their sympathy, for she felt that she had no right to their praises, and that their condolence would only add to her self-reproach. She sat alone in her chamber, poring over Arthur's letters, and tracing in the faded lines the history of his declining hopes and his growing despair; and she prayed, with a fervency that amounted almost to inspiration, that Heaven would preserve him, and restore him to her arms. But her prayers were mingled with dark forebodings; she felt as if a heavy doom was hanging over her head, and as if the punishment of her rashness was about to overtake her in the loss of the being she loved best on earth. And thus the winter wore away, and the spring came, bringing no alleviation of her suspense, and no tidings from the land of sorrow and of blood.
