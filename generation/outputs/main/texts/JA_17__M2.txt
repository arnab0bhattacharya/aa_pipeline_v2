The morning air in Gloucestershire was sharp with the promise of frost, yet it carried a particular chill to the parsonage of Mr. Arthur Penhaligon that had nothing to do with the weather. It was a chill born of expectation, a rigid tension that had settled over the household like a heavy quilt, suffocating the usual warmth of domesticity. Arthur stood before the glass in the drawing-room, adjusting his cravat with a hand that trembled less from the cold than from the magnitude of the day’s impending decision. He was a man of twenty-eight, possessed of a mind well-read in theology but poorly equipped for the intricate arithmetic of social advantage. His mother, Mrs. Penhaligon, watched him from her seat by the fire, her eyes sharp and calculating, missing nothing of his fidgeting.

"You look ill, Arthur," she said, her voice carrying that peculiar blend of maternal affection and maternal command that Arthur had learned, over twenty-eight years, to interpret as a warning. "Is it the nerves? I told you, when I first heard of the Bishop’s interest, that it was a matter requiring cool heads, not hot ones."

"The nerves," Arthur replied, turning from the glass, "are a secondary concern, Mother. The primary concern is that I am to be offered a living that would secure my family’s comfort for a generation, provided I can agree to a condition which I find... morally perplexing."

"Morally perplexing," Mrs. Penhaligon repeated, raising an eyebrow. "Or perhaps financially prudent? There is a difference, my dear, though you have yet to learn it. When did the patron, Mr. Hargrave, intend to call?"

"At ten o’clock. It is now nine-forty-five."

"Then you have fifteen minutes to compose your features. Do not let him see your anxiety. A clergyman who cannot master his own countenance before a bishop is not fit to master the consciences of his flock. And do remember, Arthur, that Miss Hargrave is not a girl of no means. Her fortune is modest, yes, but her connection is not. To refuse her is to refuse the living. To refuse the living is to remain in this small parsonage, dependent on your father’s pension, which is already insufficient for your maintenance."

Arthur sighed, the sound escaping him before he could check it. "And what of Eleanor?"

Mrs. Penhaligon’s face tightened. It was the only name in the household that seemed to cause her physical pain, a name that represented a threat to her own carefully constructed order. "Eleanor is a sweet girl, Arthur. Her father is a man of good character, but of no fortune. To marry her is to choose poverty, or at best, a precarious independence. To marry Miss Hargrave is to choose stability. Is that not what a man of sense should desire? Is it not the duty of a provider to secure the welfare of his household?"

"It is the duty of a husband to love his wife," Arthur said, his voice quiet but firm. "And I fear I have a prior understanding with Eleanor. It is not a formal engagement, but it is an understanding of the heart. She has no money, Mother, but she has a disposition that suits mine. We share the same views on literature, on duty, on life. Miss Hargrave, as I recall from my last visit, is a woman of considerable plainness, both in feature and in conversation. She speaks of investments and stocks, and finds little interest in the finer workings of the human spirit."

"Plainer is often wiser," Mrs. Penhaligon retorted. "And stocks are safer than spirits. You are romanticizing, Arthur. You are seeing through the mist of youthful infatuation. Once the wedding is over, the romance fades, and what remains is the ledger. Do you wish to keep a ledger in red ink for the rest of your life? I do not think so. I have seen too many men of your age make such mistakes. They marry for love and live for regret. I urge you to marry for sense and live for peace."

Before Arthur could respond, the sound of a carriage wheel on the gravel driveway shattered the quiet. He moved to the window, his heart pounding a frantic rhythm against his ribs. There, in the garden below, stood a black carriage, from which emerged a man of robust build and a woman of even more substantial presence. Miss Hargrave walked with a briskness that suggested she viewed the world as a problem to be solved, rather than a mystery to be admired.

"Go," Mrs. Penhaligon said, standing up. "Remember, Arthur: the living is in the diocese of Worcester. It is a cure of souls with a rectory that has three bedrooms and a garden. Eleanor’s father can offer you a room in his cottage and a share of the harvest. Which will you choose?"

Arthur looked at his mother, his face pale. He looked at the window, where the figures were now approaching the house. He thought of Eleanor, who had once read him a sonnet by Keats in the rain, her eyes bright with a joy that money could not buy. He thought of Miss Hargrave, who had once asked him if he knew the yield on the new government bonds. The contrast was stark, and the choice, though framed by his mother as one of prudence, felt to him like a choice between life and death.

He straightened his waistcoat, a final, futile attempt at dignity. "I must go, Mother."

"Do not be a fool," she whispered, her voice losing its sternness as she saw his distress. "Think of your father. He is old. He cannot support you."

Arthur did not answer. He walked out of the room, his steps heavy, each one feeling like a step into a different life. As he descended the stairs, he passed the mirror again. The face looking back at him was not the face of the young man who had loved Eleanor, but the face of a man about to sell his soul for a comfortable living. He wondered, with a sudden, sharp clarity, whether he had ever truly known Eleanor, or if he had only known the idea of her, the safe, familiar idea that contrasted with the terrifying unknown of the world beyond the parsonage walls.

He opened the front door to find the morning light blinding. Mr. Hargrave was already speaking, his voice booming across the threshold. "Mr. Penhaligon! I trust you are well? We have much to discuss, and I hope you are prepared to listen to reason. My niece, Miss Hargrave, is eager to make your acquaintance properly."

Arthur closed the door behind him, cutting off the sound of his mother’s footsteps on the stairs above. He walked toward the garden, toward the black carriage, toward the life that was being offered to him. He did not look back. To look back would be to admit that he was already lost, and he was not yet ready to accept that he had chosen the living over the girl, the security over the heart. He simply walked, his face a mask of polite indifference, as the two Hargraves approached, ready to negotiate the price of his silence.
