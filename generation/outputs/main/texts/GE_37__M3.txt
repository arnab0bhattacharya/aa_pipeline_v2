Mrs. Arabella Thorold, widow of the late respected surgeon of Whitbridge, was a woman whose mental constitution demanded that her observances should justify themselves by some appeal to the general fitness of things. She had been left, by the provident care of her husband, in the enjoyment of a clear three hundred a year—a competence which, in the Midlands town of Whitbridge during the autumn of 1837, permitted a genteel economy without the vulgarity of positive privation. Her house in the High Street, a tall, narrow-bricked edifice with a handsome mahogany door and a knocker of exceptional prominence, was a monument to that precise and unimpassioned orderliness which, in the absence of strong joys, constitutes for many worthy persons the chief bulwark against despair. It was, therefore, with a considerable trepidation of spirit that she had admitted, as a lodger, a foreign gentleman of whose antecedents she knew nothing beyond his own brief and slightly foreign statement that his name was Monsieur Laval, that he was a native of Brussels, and that his commercial affairs required a temporary residence in the neighborhood of Whitbridge. 

The commercial affairs of Monsieur Laval were, however, of a nature so profoundly nocturnal as to awaken in the mind of Mrs. Thorold’s single servant, Bessy, a suspicion that the gentleman’s trade was conducted chiefly with the powers of darkness. He occupied the two best rooms on the second floor, paid his rent with a punctuality that was almost aggressive in its disregard of the customary fortnightly delays, and kept hours that seemed to have no relation to the solar economy of Whitbridge. He was seen abroad at dawn, when respectable householders were merely turning in their beds; he was invisible throughout the entire diurnal stretch, when the sun lit up the gray stone of the parish church and the market carts jostled in the square below; and his candles, Bessy reported with a bated breath that savored of the marvellous, were frequently burning at two o’clock in the morning. 

To a mind constituted like Mrs. Thorold’s—a mind in which the strictly provincial love of known biographies was curiously blended with a fatalistic submission to the pressure of monetary circumstance—this irregularity was a source of profound uneasiness. But the rent was high, and the alternative had been the letting of the rooms to a family from Birmingham-ward, whose children and all. So she stifled the misgivings that naturally sprang from an unknown tongue and a locked portmanteau, reflecting that Providence sometimes sends its blessings in strange grammatical cases. 

But the social economy of a provincial town is an engine of exquisite sensitiveness; it registers the slightest variation in the routine of its recognized units, and it translates that variation into a moral hypothesis with an alacrity that puts the inductive sciences to shame. It was not long before the whispering gallery of Whitbridge had taken hold of Monsieur Laval. At the shop of Mr. Goby, the draper—a man whose intellect moved in a perpetual atmosphere of surmise, and who found in theFailures of his neighbors a rich compensation for the monotony of measuring cambric—the foreign gentleman’s nocturnal habits were subjected to a rigorous interpretation. Mr. Goby was of the opinion, freely communicated over the counter to any lady of mature years who did not cut him short, that the stranger was a Jesuit in disguise, sent to reconnoiter the Protestant Midlands for a Papal invasion. Mrs. Patten, the rector’s wife, thought it more probable that he was an exiled French aristocrat, a supposition which had the merit of romantic charity but lacked the robust evidence of probability. The lower orders, represented chiefly by the grooms and stable-boys who congregated at the Dragon Inn, held a simpler view: they believed him to be a smuggler, or, at the very least, a horse-thief of continental cunning. 

But of all the minds upon which the presence of Monsieur Laval cast its disturbing shadow, none was more curiously affected than that of Mrs. Thorold’s daughter, Letitia. Letitia was twenty-three, and she possessed that most dangerous of all intellectual endowments in a young woman of narrow means and narrower opportunities—a mind conscious of its own emptiness, and athirst for the dramatic. Her days were passed in a round of small domestic duties, in the practice of a not very brilliant pianoforte, and in the perusal of circulating-library novels, which, while they nourished her imagination, gave her no clue to the conduct of a life so essentially devoid of incident as hers. She was a girl of dark, heavy features, with a complexion that spoke of suppressed energy, and a pair of gray eyes that had a disquieting habit of fixing themselves upon objects as if they were attempting to penetrate their molecular constitution. 

To Letitia, the lodger was not merely a source of social scandal; he was an event, a phenomenon, a problem presented to her faculties for solution. And the problem began to exercise that fascination which the unknown always possesses for the inactive. She found herself, in the long blank afternoons, listening for his footfall on the stairs; she invented pretexts for passing through the hall when his door was shut, in the hope of catching some sound that would reveal the nature of his occupations. She observed, with a minuteness that would have done credit to a naturalist, that his boots were always polished to a mirror-like brilliance, that his greatcoat was of a fashion unknown in Whitbridge, and that he carried a cane with a head of carved ivory which she suspected to be of Oriental workmanship. 

But it was one thing to observe these exterior facts, and another to penetrate the secret of that locked room, of those nocturnal absences, of that persistent silence concerning his past. Letitia’s curiosity, at first a mere fluttering of the imagination, began to take the form of a purpose. She reasoned with herself—and the reasoning was not without a certain logical force—that her mother had taken the stranger in on trust, and that it was the duty of someone in the household to verify the trust. It was possible, she argued, that Monsieur Laval was a person of dangerous character; it was even possible, as Mr. Goby had darkly hinted, that he was an agent of a foreign power, or a conspirator against the peace of the realm. The fact that Whitbridge was a town of no political importance did not seem to Letitia a sufficient reason for his presence there; for she had read enough of history to know that conspirators often choose the most insignificant spots for their machinations. 

One evening, when the autumn dusk was falling early over the High Street, and Mrs. Thorold had gone to spend the night with a married sister in the neighboring village of Little Marcham, Letitia found herself alone in the house with Bessy, who had retired to the kitchen with a headache. The silence of the house was unbroken save by the ticking of the clock and the distant murmur of the market-town’s evening traffic. Letitia sat in the parlor, pretending to read, but in reality listening with a strained attention to every sound. Presently she heard the front door open and shut softly, and then the slow, measured tread of Monsieur Laval upon the stairs. He passed her door, and she caught a glimpse of him through the chink—a tall figure in a dark cloak, with his hat pulled over his eyes, and the ivory-headed cane in his hand. She heard him enter his room and turn the key in the lock. 

Then, with a suddenness that surprised herself, Letitia rose from her chair. She went into the hall, and stood at the foot of the stairs, looking up into the darkness. She had no plan; she had no distinct intention; she was merely obeying an impulse which she could not have formulated in words, but which was strong enough to override the dictates of prudence and the habits of a lifetime. She went up the stairs, her footsteps muffled by the drugget, and stood before Monsieur Laval’s door. She could hear him moving about within, and the sound of water being poured into a basin. She stood there for a full minute, her heart beating with a violence that she could feel in her throat, and then, as silently as she had come, she crept down again. 

But the next morning, when Monsieur Laval appeared at breakfast, looking pale and worn, and with a slight cut on his right hand which he accounted for by saying that he had stumbled in the dark, Letitia’s curiosity was transformed into a conviction. She was sure now that he was engaged in some dangerous pursuit, and she determined to discover what it was. She began to watch his windows from the garden, and to note the hours at which his candles appeared and disappeared. She even went so far as to ascend to the attic, which commanded a view of his room, and from that point of vantage she could see, through a gap in the curtains, that the room was furnished with a large table covered with papers and instruments which she could not identify. 

It happened, on a Tuesday in November, that Monsieur Laval, departing earlier than his wont, left his door ajar. Letitia, passing through the upper hall, observed this circumstance, and paused, with a beating heart, upon the threshold. She knew that to enter was a breach of that tacit hospitality which her mother had extended; she knew, too, that it was a surrender to that very inquisitiveness which her reason condemned. But the impulse which had driven her to the stairs on that autumn evening was not to be withstood by the considerations of a morality which seemed, in the presence of the actual locked door, to belong to a different order of things. She pushed the door open and went in. 

The room was in a state of disorder which contrasted strangely with the precise arrangement of the rest of the house. The large table, which she had seen from the attic, was covered with maps, charts, and sheets of manuscript covered with figures and symbols. There was a small microscope, a pair of scales, and several cases of instruments which Letitia, with her limited knowledge of the sciences, could not name. On the mantelpiece were some fragments of rock and mineral, and a small phial of dark liquid. The bed was untouched, and the only sign of recent occupation was a cup of half-drunk coffee and a plate of broken bread. 

Letitia stood for a moment, bewildered, looking at the maps and the instruments. She was not learned enough to guess the nature of the pursuits to which they pointed; but she could see that they were not the apparatus of a smuggler or a conspirator. They suggested, rather, some scientific or commercial investigation of a recondite character. She was about to withdraw, when her eye was caught by a letter lying open on the table. It was written in English, and the handwriting was bold and clear. She read the first few lines, and her cheek flushed with a shame which was all the deeper because she could not immediately classify its cause. The letter ran: “My dear Laval,—I am glad to hear that you have found a quiet retreat in Whitbridge, and I trust that the geological specimens of the neighborhood will repay your researches. The survey of the coal measures is of the highest importance, and I need not remind you that the results will be of great value to the Society. I hope to join you in a fortnight, when my duties here will permit.” 

Letitia laid down the letter, and crept out of the room, closing the door softly behind her.
