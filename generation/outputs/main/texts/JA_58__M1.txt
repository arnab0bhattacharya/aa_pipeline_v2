It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a young man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife; but it is a truth less generally admitted, that his sisters may have a very decided opinion on the matter, and that such an opinion, once formed, is not easily relinquished. Miss Eleanor Vance had formed hers within a week of her brother’s engagement being made known to her. The young lady, a Miss Sophia Beaumont, was described by their aunt as possessing a fortune of ten thousand pounds, a charming countenance, and a disposition of the most lively and amiable kind. To Eleanor, this catalogue of virtues was itself the most damning evidence. A fortune of ten thousand pounds was, she reasoned, a mere trifle to a man of her brother’s standing; a charming countenance was a commodity of which the world possessed an inexhaustible supply; and a lively disposition, when unaccompanied by the steadiness of good sense, was but another name for frivolity.

She had resolved, therefore, upon a course of dignified resistance. She would not travel to Surrey to meet Miss Beaumont, nor would she receive her at her own small establishment in Bath. Her brother, Frederick, a man of amiable temper and little penetration, had pleaded with her in his letters, but Eleanor was firm. She had seen, in the marriages of her acquaintance, what became of men who were won by a pretty face and a ready laugh. They were led a merry dance, to be sure, but it was a dance that ended in want of conversation, want of principle, and want of fortune. She would not be a party to her brother’s undoing. It was not, she told herself, a matter of prejudice, but of principle. She had formed her judgement on impartial conviction, and was not to be swayed by the affection of a brother who, in matters of the heart, had ever been more governed by feeling than by reason.

This resolution was, however, put to a trial for which she had not been prepared. A letter arrived from Surrey, not from Frederick, but from Miss Beaumont herself. The hand was elegant, the language correct, and the intelligence alarming. Frederick had been seized by a putrid fever; the physicians were grave, and his constitution, never strong, was much weakened. Miss Beaumont wrote not to complain, nor to elicit pity, but to state the situation with a clarity that Eleanor, despite herself, could not help but admire. She concluded by saying that she believed Frederick would find great comfort in his sister’s presence, and that she, Miss Beaumont, would be honoured to make her acquaintance under circumstances so trying.

To refuse was impossible. To go was to concede a point of some delicacy. Eleanor packed her trunk with a feeling of grim necessity, and arrived at Ramworth House, her brother’s property in Surrey, with her opinion of Miss Beaumont not one whit altered. The young lady who met her in the hall was, as expected, exceedingly pretty. She was smaller than Eleanor had imagined, with dark, anxious eyes and a complexion that seemed to have lost its colour in the sick-room. She curtseyed with a grace that was innate rather than practised, and her first words were not of her own distress, but of Frederick’s condition.

“He is sleeping at last,” she said, her voice low. “The fever is not so high as it was. I think he will be the better for seeing you.”

Eleanor, prepared for effusion and tears, found herself met with a quiet competence that was disarming. She was conducted to her brother’s room, and for the next several days, the two women were thrown together in the shared labour of nursing. The house was silent but for the ticking of the clock in the hall and the low murmur of the apothecary. The servants moved on tiptoe. It was a trial of patience and endurance, and it was in this quiet crucible that Eleanor began to see Miss Beaumont in a light altogether different from the one cast by report.

The young lady’s disposition was, indeed, lively, but it was a liveliness of the spirits, not of the mind. She possessed a fund of quiet cheerfulness that was a balm in the sick-room, and a patience with Frederick’s fretfulness that Eleanor, with her own brisk and somewhat impatient temper, could not equal. She never spoke of her own fatigue, though her pallor and the shadows beneath her eyes told their own story. She read to Frederick when he was able to bear it, chose passages of a soothing and improving nature, and never once did Eleanor hear a complaint escape her lips. Her conversation, when they sat together in the evenings, was not of balls or bonnets, but of the books she had read, the country they were in, and, with a touching earnestness, of her hopes for Frederick’s future.

One evening, as they sat by the fire, the apothecary having given a more favourable report, Miss Beaumont said, “You must think me very strange, Miss Vance, to have written to you as I did. But I felt that he needed his family. And I… I wished to know you.”

There was an artlessness in the confession that struck Eleanor more forcibly than any display of cleverness could have done. She saw, in that moment, the folly of her own judgement. She had condemned this young woman for a set of qualities—fortune, beauty, and spirits—which were in themselves no evils, and had refused to see the good sense and strength of character that lay beneath. Her prejudice had been as strong, and as unreasonable, as any she had ever condemned in others. She had prided herself on her discernment, but it was a discernment that had seen only the surface, and had mistaken a lively temper for a flighty one.

“I was wrong,” Eleanor said, the words coming with a difficulty that surprised her. “I formed an opinion of you from report, and I have found it to be a very mistaken one. I must beg your pardon.”

Miss Beaumont looked at her, her eyes wide. “There is nothing to pardon. You did not know me. How could you?” She paused, then added with a gentle smile, “Frederick has told me that you are a woman of very strong principles. I have found it to be true.”

It was a compliment, and yet it carried with it a subtle and deserved reproof. Her principles had been strong, but they had been applied without charity. She had been so certain of her own rightness that she had closed the door to a good and amiable person, and had it not been for the accident of illness, she might have gone on in her error for ever. The discovery was humbling, and the gratitude she felt towards Miss Beaumont—for her care of Frederick, for her patience, and for her own unassuming goodness—was profound.

When Frederick was at last declared out of danger, a new and more cheerful atmosphere settled upon Ramworth House. Eleanor saw her brother’s fiancée not as a rival for his affection, but as its worthy object. She saw in their quiet companionship a meeting of minds and a harmony of temper that promised a great deal of future happiness. Her own conduct, she reflected with some chagrin, had been the only discordant note. She had set herself up as the guardian of her brother’s peace, when in truth she had been an obstacle to it. It is a painful thing to discover that one’s own judgement, so long and so confidently held, is but a mask for prejudice; but it is a necessary lesson, and one which, in the quiet of a sick-room in Surrey, Miss Eleanor Vance was at last content to learn.
