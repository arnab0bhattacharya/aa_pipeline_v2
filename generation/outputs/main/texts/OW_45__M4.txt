The Princess Aurelia had been assured, from the cradle upwards, by a chorus of nurses, courtiers, and minor poets, that she was the most beautiful girl in the world; and as she had never had any reason to doubt the word of persons so eminently respectable, she had accepted the statement with the serene complacency of one who accepts the law of gravity. But there comes a moment in the life of every well-regulated princess when the mirror seems to say something less than the truth, and the flattery of equerries begins to taste of the same insipidity as the sugared violets one finds in a bonbonnière. So it was with Aurelia. On the morning of her eighteenth birthday she looked into the great Venetian glass in her boudoir and said to herself, with a little frown that would have driven a painter to despair, ‘I am beautiful, of course, but I should like to hear it from someone who has no interest in saying so.’

The notion was a dangerous one, for it is the first step towards that dreadful modern heresy which holds that truth is a thing to be sought rather than a thing to be arranged. Nevertheless, she acted upon it. She laid aside her cloth of silver and her pearls, and put on the russet gown and the woollen hood of a country girl; and having thus, as she imagined, effectually concealed the most famous profile in the kingdom of Illyria, she slipped out through a garden gate and went down into the market-place of the royal city.

The market was a brave confusion of colours and cries. There were pyramids of oranges that glowed like little suns; there were silks from the East hanging in soft cascades from the booths; there were fishmongers who swore like poets and poets who swore like fishmongers. Aurelia walked through it all with the air of a connoisseur in a gallery, and found it delightful, for the simple reason that nobody looked at her twice. The fishwife who sold her a bunch of radishes told her she had a saucy eye; the boy who sold her a ribbon told her she had a pretty ankle; and the old man who sold her a pie told her, with a wink, that she had the look of a girl who would go far. None of these compliments was precisely the one she sought, but they were all, in their way, gratifying, and she was beginning to think that truth, when one came to examine it, was a much more agreeable commodity than she had been led to expect.

At last she came to a little booth at the corner of the square, where an old woman sat among heaps of flowers—roses that had been gathered that morning, lilies that were still heavy with the dew, and a great many modest violets that seemed to be hiding their faces from the sun. The old woman was blind. Her eyes were as white as the petals of a white rose, and her fingers moved among the stems with the delicate certainty of long practice. Aurelia stopped before her, and the old woman, hearing the rustle of a skirt, lifted her head and smiled.

‘Buy a flower, my pretty,’ she said. ‘I have roses for a lady, and lilies for a saint, and violets for a girl who is in love and doesn’t know it yet.’

‘I am not in love,’ said Aurelia, with the promptitude of one who has never been contradicted, ‘and I am not a saint, and I am not sure that I am a lady. But I will buy a rose, if you will tell me something.’

‘I will tell you anything you like,’ said the old woman, ‘for I have nothing to do but listen, and nothing to see but the inside of my own head, which is a very dull place, I assure you, for I have been there so often.’

‘Then tell me,’ said Aurelia, leaning over the counter and speaking in a low voice, ‘am I beautiful?’

The old woman laughed, a little dry laugh that was like the rustling of leaves. ‘Ah, my dear,’ she said, ‘that is a question that only the blind can answer, for the blind do not see what is before them, but what is behind them. I cannot tell you whether you are beautiful, for I have never seen you. But I can tell you that you have a voice like a silver bell, and that is better than beauty, for beauty fades, and a voice only grows sweeter with use.’

Aurelia drew back, and the rose she had been holding fell unheeded to the ground. She did not know whether to be pleased or offended. To be told that one has a voice like a silver bell is, of course, a very pretty thing; but to be told it in answer to a question about one’s beauty is to be put in the position of a person who has asked for a diamond and been given a pearl. It is not the same thing, however much the jeweller may protest.

‘You do not answer my question,’ she said, with a little flush of annoyance.

‘I answer it as well as I can,’ said the old woman, placidly. ‘You asked me if you were beautiful, and I told you that you had a beautiful voice. If you were ugly, my dear, you would have asked me if you were clever. It is only the beautiful who ask if they are beautiful, and it is only the clever who ask if they are clever. The ugly ask if they are good, and the good ask nothing at all, for they are too busy being good to bother about it.’

‘That is a very strange philosophy,’ said Aurelia.

‘It is the only philosophy,’ said the old woman. ‘Beauty is a thing that other people see; goodness is a thing that other people feel; but a voice is a thing that you yourself can hear, and that is why it is the most precious of the three. The nightingale is not beautiful, my dear, and I am told she is not particularly good; but she sings, and the whole world stops to listen. You have a voice like a silver bell, and if you are wise you will use it, and not spend your time asking blind women questions that they cannot answer.’

Aurelia stood for a moment in silence. She was conscious of a curious sensation, which she afterwards described to herself as the feeling of having been put in her place by a person who had no idea where her place was. It was not an unpleasant sensation, but it was a novel one, and novelty was the one thing that her royal education had not provided for.

‘I will take the violets,’ she said at last, ‘and I will not ask you any more questions.’

‘That is the first wise thing you have said,’ said the old woman, ‘for questions are like flowers—they are only beautiful when they are not picked. But I will tell you one thing more, because you have a kind heart, and I can hear it in your voice. You are beautiful, my dear, or you would not have been so angry when I did not say so. But you are not the most beautiful girl in the world, for there is no such person. There is only the girl who is beautiful to someone, and that is a very different matter. Now run along, and mind you don’t let the fishmonger cheat you, for he is a dreadful old rascal, and he has been standing there this half-hour listening to us, and I daresay he has made up his mind to sell you a mackerel that is not so fresh as it might be.’

Aurelia turned and saw the fishmonger, who was indeed standing at the next booth with an expression of the most profound interest. She blushed, and the fishmonger, who was a philosopher in his way, took off his cap and said, ‘Your Royal Highness need not be afraid of me. I have known you all along, for I shaved the Duke your father when he was a young man, and you have his nose. But I will not tell anyone, for a secret is like a fish—it is only good when it is kept.’

And Aurelia went home with her violets, and she was not quite sure whether she had discovered the truth or not. But she had discovered something, which is, after all, the most that any of us can hope for; and she wore the violets at dinner that night, and when the court poet told her that she was the most beautiful girl in the world, she smiled and said, ‘I know, but I have a voice like a silver bell, and that is better.’ And the court poet, who was a very young man, and had not the least idea what she meant, said that it was, and went home and wrote a sonnet about it, which was excessively bad, and which she kept in her jewel-case to the end of her days.
