It was upon a raw and gusty evening in the November of '92 that a matter of some delicacy was brought to my notice. My friend, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, and I were seated in our familiar rooms at 221B Baker Street, the gas-lamps casting long shadows upon the cluttered landscape of our sitting-room. The day's papers had yielded little of interest, and a sense of ennui, that old and familiar foe, was settling upon the apartment when Mrs. Hudson announced a visitor.

The lady who entered was of middle age, her plain, dark dress and the determined set of her jaw speaking of a life of honest toil and quiet resolve. She was Miss Eleanor Vance, and her brother, a man named Thomas, had for the past three years been the personal valet to Baron von Hessler, the minister from the small but influential Duchy of Strelau.

“Mr. Holmes,” she began, her voice steady despite the grief that shadowed her eyes, “my brother is dead. The doctors say it was his heart, a failure that seized him in a hansom cab last Tuesday night. But I do not believe it.”

Holmes, who had been observing her with the keen, analytical gaze I knew so well, steepled his long, thin fingers. “Pray be seated, Miss Vance. The grounds for your disbelief are of the first importance.”

“Thomas was as strong as an ox, sir. He had never known a day’s illness in his life. More than that, he was a man of simple habits and few confidences. But last week he came to see me, and he was not himself. He was agitated. He said only that he had stumbled upon something ‘in the Baron’s study’—something that was not meant for his eyes. He said the Baron trusted him implicitly, but that this discovery made him fear for his position, and perhaps for more.”

“And the nature of this discovery?” Holmes asked.

“That he would not say. He was a loyal man, sir, and spoke of his duty. He merely said that the Baron was a good master, but that his politics were a tangled and dangerous web. He was to meet me again to tell me more. He never came.”

I felt a pang of sympathy for the woman. Holmes, however, was already assembling the facts in his mind. “The cabman,” he said. “What of him?”

“A good man named Perkins. He told the police he picked my brother up from the German Embassy in Belgravia. Thomas seemed perfectly well when he entered, but within a few minutes he cried out and slumped over. By the time the cab reached my lodgings in Lambeth, he was gone.”

Holmes’s eyes gleamed. “The German Embassy. A place of considerable intrigue, I should imagine. And the Baron’s reaction to this sad event?”

“He expressed great sorrow, and sent a handsome sum for the funeral expenses. But when I, in my grief, mentioned my brother’s last words to me, his manner changed at once. He became curt, dismissive. He said it was the wild talk of a man whose heart was failing, and that I should forget it.”

“Very good, Miss Vance,” said Holmes, rising. “You have given me the thread. I shall pull upon it and see what unravels. Watson, your coat, if you please. The air of Belgravia may prove more bracing than that of Baker Street.”

Our first port of call was the modest lodging-house where Miss Vance resided. There, in a small box, lay the few possessions of the late Thomas Vance. Holmes examined them with meticulous care: a silver watch, a Bible with a faded inscription, a small sum of money, and a single, curious object—a smooth, grey river stone, about the size of a pigeon’s egg. It was entirely unremarkable, save for the fact that it was quite out of place among the man’s effects.

“A paperweight, perhaps?” I suggested.

“Or a memento, Watson. Or a signal,” Holmes replied, his long fingers turning the stone over and over. “Let us proceed.”

Our next destination was the mews behind the great house in Belgravia that served as the Duchy of Strelau’s embassy. We found the cabman, Perkins, polishing the brasswork on his vehicle. He was a stout, ruddy-faced man with a memory as clear as his varnish.

“Aye, I remember the poor gent well,” he confirmed. “Picked him up at the embassy’s side entrance just after ten. A quiet fellow. Sat back in the dark, said not a word. Then, as we were passing the Royal Exchange, he gave a sort of gasp. ‘The grey stone,’ he muttered, or something like it. Then he was gone. Never a word of complaint, mind you. Just… stopped.”

Holmes pressed a coin into the man’s palm. “‘The grey stone.’ You are certain of the words?”

“As certain as I am that this cab is black, sir.”

We left the mews and stood for a moment beneath the gas-lamp, the great embassy looming before us. A light burned in an upper window, where a figure passed and repassed, silhouetted against the blind.

“‘The grey stone,’” Holmes murmured, more to himself than to me. “A curious dying utterance. It is not a confession, nor a name. It is a clue, Watson, but a clue to what? The man knew he was dying. His heart was failing. Was it a natural failure, assisted by some subtle agent? Or was it fear, a terror so profound it stopped the heart as surely as a bullet? He knew what he had seen. He knew it was dangerous. And he left us this.” He tapped his pocket, where the river stone now rested. “It is a cipher, Watson, and the embassy is its key.”

The following afternoon, the newspapers gave us our next piece of the puzzle. A short notice reported a chimney fire at the German Embassy. The blaze, it seemed, had been confined to a small study and was quickly extinguished, but a quantity of papers had been consigned to the flames before the fire brigade arrived. It was, the report concluded, a minor domestic accident.

Holmes read the account with a thin, cold smile. “A domestic accident! Watson, the game is afoot. They have panicked. The valet’s death did not close the matter as they had hoped. His sister’s suspicions, or perhaps our inquiries in the mews, have put them on their guard. They have destroyed the evidence.”

“But what evidence?” I asked, bewildered. “What could a valet have seen that would cause a diplomatic minister to commit murder and then burn his own correspondence?”

Holmes leaned back in his chair, his eyes fixed upon the ceiling. “Let us reason from the facts. A valet, trusted and discreet, sees something in his master’s study. Not a simple indiscretion, but something of political import. His heart, we are told, fails him. But before it does, he speaks of a ‘grey stone.’ Then, within a day, the study where he made his discovery is purged by fire. The connections are too strong for coincidence.”

He sprang to his feet and began to pace the room. “The Baron is a diplomat. His currency is information. The valet, in the course of his duties, might have found a list of names—a roster of agents, perhaps, or a record of payments. Such a document would be worth a fortune, and worth a life to protect. But how would a man like Thomas Vance communicate its hiding place? Not with a word, which could be misunderstood. He had no time to write a note. He had only a single object in his pocket.”

He stopped before me, his expression alight with the fire of discovery. “A grey stone, Watson! Not the stone itself, but what it represents. It is a marker! A signifier! Think of the common phrases. ‘Set in stone.’ ‘A cornerstone.’ ‘Grave as a stone.’ No, no… it is more literal. He was a man of simple habits. Where would a man of simple habits hide a paper of immense value? In the most obvious place imaginable, a place so mundane it would be overlooked by any searcher.”

He snatched his hat and stick. “Come, Watson! To the embassy! I believe I know where to find the Baron’s little secret, and the proof of poor Thomas Vance’s murder.”

As we rattled towards Belgravia in a cab, Holmes explained his reasoning. “The fire was a feint, or rather, a clean-up operation for the things they *expected* to be found. They would search the desk, the safe, the books. But they would not think to look at the very foundation of the room. Thomas Vance, the valet, would know every inch of the house. He knew where the loose floorboard was, I’ll wager, right beside the hearth in the Baron’s study—the very room that ‘burned.’ The ‘grey stone’ was his message. Not the object itself, but its function. It was a weight, a marker. He told us where to look: under the stone, beneath the floor.”

We were shown into the Baron’s study by a stiff-backed footman. The room smelled faintly of smoke and chemicals. Baron von Hessler, a man with a magnificent moustache and eyes as hard as flint, rose to greet us with an expression of profound irritation.

“Mr. Holmes. This is an intrusion. The matter of my poor valet is closed.”

“On the contrary, Baron,” said Holmes, his voice dangerously soft. “It is just beginning. I have come to recover a document. A list, I believe. It concerns a certain Mr. Davies of the Foreign Office, and a Mr. Albright of the Treasury.”

The Baron’s face went white. “I do not know what you mean.”

Holmes ignored him. He walked directly to the hearth, knelt, and with the handle of his cane, prised up a floorboard that sat, almost invisibly, flush with the rest. From the cavity beneath, he drew a small oilskin packet. He did not open it, but held it in his hand.

“Thomas Vance was an honest man who discovered your treason,” Holmes stated, his voice ringing with cold certainty. “You could not allow him to speak. A rare poison, perhaps, administered in his evening glass—a toxin that mimics heart failure and leaves no trace? A clever man like yourself would know of such things. But you made one mistake. You underestimated the loyalty of a servant to his sister, and the simple clarity of his dying message.”

The Baron made a sudden lunge for the bell-pull, but I was quicker, placing myself between him and the door. Holmes had already turned, the oilskin packet held aloft like a trophy.

“It is over, Baron. Scotland Yard will be interested to see these names, and to hear the testimony of a cabman who remembers a dying man’s last words.”

As we left the embassy, the grey November sky seemed to lighten a fraction. The case was solved, the villain unmasked. Yet, as Holmes sat back in the cab, a familiar look of introspection was upon his face.

“A sordid business, Watson,” he said at last. “And yet, in its way, a perfect one. A single, unassuming object, a man’s last breath, and a fire in a chimney. The threads were all there, for anyone with the eyes to see them. It is the triumph of the commonplace over the extraordinary. And now, I find myself quite fatigued by it all. I believe a pipe of the coarsest shag tobacco is called for, and perhaps an evening of blessed, uneventful silence.”
