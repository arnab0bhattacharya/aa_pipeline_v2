There are moments in the life of a man when the whole framework of his existence, the very scaffolding of his duties and his pride, is struck as if by a physical blow, leaving him suspended in a void of his own making. I speak of Renouf, a youth barely twenty-two, who had come to us on the _Cypromonte_ bearing a newly acquired second mate’s certificate and a head full of those solemn, untried conceptions of the sea that are more dangerous than pure ignorance. He was the son of a deceased minor official from some sun-baked, sleepy administrative town in the south of France, and he carried his new authority with a rigid, fragile dignity, like a man balancing a glass brimful of water across a crowded room. We were beating across the desolate eastern stretches of the Indian Ocean, driving the old barque northward through a heat that lay upon the decks like a suffocating, invisible weight, when the sky to leeward began to gather its forces. 

I was below, dozing in my berth, when the squall struck. It did not come with the gradual, moaning preludes one learns to watch for, but descended upon the ship with the abrupt, violent swoop of a predatory bird. It was a brutal, sudden fling of the elements. I was thrown from my bunk by the sheer shock of the impact, and scrambling into my oilskins, I made my way to the deck. The night was a chaotic smother of black water and driving rain, the rigging screaming a frantic, high-pitched protest above the deep, thunderous roar of the wind. I saw the dim, confused shadow of men at the braces, pulling like desperate animals, and there, on the weather side of the lee poop, stood Renouf.

He was planted at the break of the deck, gripping the brass rail of the companionway with both hands. The rain lashed his face, and the green water was sluicing over the boards about his ankles, but he did not move. He was staring forward into the smother of spray and tearing canvas with the wide, unseeing eyes of a man watching a nightmare unfold from which he has been paralyzed. He had given no order. The helmsman, a man of great experience from the Nicobar islands, had thrown the wheel down of his own accord to keep the vessel from broaching to, but the braces remained two-blocked, the yards braced sharp against the blast, and the tall masts were groaning under a leverage that threatened to tear the very heart out of the old ship. 

I saw the Captain emerge from the dark, a stocky, formidable shadow wrapped in a ancient, glistening coat. Captain Groves was a man who had shaped the destinies of ships for thirty years, his soul as hard and as polished as the teak of his quarterdeck. He did not pause to question. He brushed past Renouf as one might brush past a piece of dead timber. He seized the knitted rail beside the wheel, roared an order to the native crew that cut through the shrieking of the wind like a blade of fire, and with his own hands laid hold of the weather brace. The men, galvanized by his voice, hurled themselves upon the rope. The yards swung with a reluctant, heavy creak; the ship righted herself, shaking the water from her flanks like a wounded beast, and the frantic, suspended menace of the moment passed into the grumbling, sullen roll of a vessel brought safely before the wind.

It was over in a matter of minutes, yet in those minutes a whole lifetime of moral reality had been laid bare. And Renouf had not moved. He stood there, his hands still white upon the rail, staring at the spot where Captain Groves had thrust his shoulder into the brace. The Captain, panting, his face streaming with rain, turned and looked at the youth. It was a look of absolute, devastating comprehension. In the flickering, uncertain light of the binnacle, I saw Groves’s lips part, but he said nothing. He merely stared, as one stares at an inexplicable ruin, before he turned his back and walked to the windward side, standing there with his hands in his pockets, gazing out into the black, chaotic night. 

Nobody spoke of it. The crew, a mixture of Levantines and dark-skinned islanders, went about the work of securing the gear with a profound, uncommunicative silence. They had seen. They knew. They were men of the sea, and they possessed that fatalistic, unspoken acceptance of human frailty which is more damning than any shouted accusation. They had seen the white officer freeze, and they had seen the old man take the deck. They did not mock; they merely ignored, and in their ignoring was the cold finality of a tomb being sealed.

For the remainder of the passage, the _Cypromonte_ became for Renouf a floating purgatory of his own construction. He moved among us like a ghost, his youthful face grown old and haggard with a secret, consuming terror. He watched the crew. He watched them as they went about their duties, scanning their impassive faces for a sneer, a whisper, a knowing glance that would betray their contempt. He sought in their downcast eyes the confirmation of his own degradation, but he found nothing. They were polite, obedient, and utterly indifferent. Their silence was a vast, impenetrable wall against which he threw himself in vain. He was a man buried alive, listening for the sound of footsteps above his grave.

And he watched Captain Groves. He watched him with the intense, trembling vigilance of a man awaiting execution. Every time the Captain spoke to him, Renouf’s face would pale, and his voice would take on a strained, unnatural eagerness, as if he were trying to atone for his paralysis by a frantic display of zeal. He was waiting for the report. He knew that Groves had only to write a single line in the official log, a single word of censure, and the fragile structure of his maritime career would collapse forever. He would be ruined, dismissed, sent back to the shore with the brand of cowardice upon his forehead. 

But Captain Groves did not report it. The days passed, the heat diminished, and the ship drew nearer to her port. The Captain treated him with a formal, distant courtesy, giving him his orders with the same blunt precision he had always used, never alluding to that night of wind and rain. It was as if the event had never occurred, as if Renouf had always been a competent, unremarkable second mate. But this very silence was a torture more exquisite than any public reprimand. It was not the silence of forgiveness. It was the silence of a man who has looked into the abyss of another man’s soul and found it wanting, and who has turned away in a grim, weary disgust, leaving the sinner to his own devices.

I would see Renouf standing alone at the break of the poop in the dog-watches, staring out over the dark, heaving water, his hands clenching and unclenching at his sides. He was a man possessed by a demon of his own making, a demon of self-knowledge. He had seen himself as he truly was, stripped of all pretension, revealed in a moment of supreme crisis as a creature of flesh and blood, paralyzed by the very fear that he had sworn to defy. And the knowledge of himself, that sudden, awful glimpse into the depths of his own weakness, was a burden he would carry for the rest of his life. 

For my part, I do not know what was in Captain Groves’s mind. Perhaps he felt that the youth had suffered enough. Perhaps he was simply tired, tired of the sea and its relentless demands, tired of the endless parade of human frailty that passed before his eyes. Or perhaps he understood, with the cynical wisdom of age, that the punishment of the logbook would be as nothing compared to the punishment of memory. He had left Renouf to the inflexible and cold-eyed taskmaster of his own conscience, and that was a court from which there is no appeal. The ship sailed on, cutting her way through the dark, silent waters, carrying her crew toward their destination, but Renouf was already a man apart, a man marooned upon a desert island of his own shame, watching the world go by with the haunted eyes of one who has looked upon the truth and found it unbearable.
