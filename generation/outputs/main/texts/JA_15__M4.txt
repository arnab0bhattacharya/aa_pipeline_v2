The house in Wimpole Street was reckoned, by those who had not the honour of being acquainted with its mistress, to be one of the most elegant in that quarter of the town; and by those who had, to be one of the most instructive, for there was scarcely an article in it which did not afford Mrs. Marchmont an opportunity of explaining its history and its price. It was to this lady that Mr. Edmund Ferrers, a young man of very decent expectations and a very modest independence, had been commended by his father, with an injunction to profit by the connection and to conduct himself with propriety. He had arrived on a Tuesday, and by Friday he was in love.

The object of his affection was a Miss Delacour, a young lady of such beauty and such composure as to seem, at first acquaintance, the very pattern of a heroine in a circulating library. She was staying with Mrs. Marchmont for the season, and it was universally understood that she was to make a very advantageous marriage. Her father was reported to be in the West Indies; her mother was dead; and her fortune, though never mentioned by herself, was mentioned by every one else, and always with an increase. Mr. Ferrers, who had come to town with a wardrobe rather better than his income warranted, and a manner which he flattered himself was perfectly easy, found in Miss Delacour's society a gratification so complete, that he began to consider how he might best appear to her as a man of substance. He spoke of his father's estate with a vagueness that was intended to be impressive, and of a small property in Devonshire which he had never seen, in terms which suggested a larger one in the same county which he had. Miss Delacour listened with an air of gentle amusement, and said very little, which he took for admiration.

Miss Delacour, for her part, had her own reasons for silence. She was not an heiress. Her father was a merchant in Antigua, it is true, but a merchant of very moderate success, and the fortune which the world had assigned her existed only in the world's imagination, and in the letters of an aunt who had a talent for figures and a greater talent for hope. She had been sent to town by that aunt, with a small allowance and a large wardrobe, upon the principle that a girl of beauty should be seen; and she had been received by Mrs. Marchmont upon the principle that a girl of beauty, if she were also an heiress, would reflect credit upon her chaperone. Neither lady had thought it necessary to undeceive the other. Miss Delacour, who was proud, and not without a certain irony, found it easier to let the world believe her rich than to explain that she was poor; and she had discovered, in the course of two seasons, that a woman with a fortune is treated with a deference which a woman without one may wait for long. She had not expected to be in love. She was now, and with a young man whom she believed to be possessed of a very pretty estate in Devonshire, and who had, besides, a manner of looking at her which was worth more than the estate.

They were thrown together, as Mrs. Marchmont was fond of saying, by the happiest chance. They walked in the Park; they stood together at the opera; they sat at the same end of Mrs. Marchmont's drawing-room, while that lady discoursed to the other end upon the impropriety of waltzing. Mr. Ferrers, emboldened by what he took to be encouragement, began to speak of a house he intended to take in the country, and of the improvements he meant to make; and Miss Delacour, who had a nice ear for a false note, heard in his descriptions a certain anxiety which she attributed to modesty, and admired. She, on her side, spoke of her father's plantation with a reserve which he attributed to feeling, and revered. Neither had the smallest intention of deceiving the other; each was deceived, and each was too well-bred to ask a question which might have set the matter right.

It was Mrs. Marchmont who at last brought the comedy to its crisis, and she did it with the best intentions in the world. She had invited to dinner a certain Sir Thomas Gower, a widower of large fortune and small understanding, who had been heard to say that he admired Miss Delacour's figure; and she had placed Mr. Ferrers at the other end of the table, between two ladies who talked of nothing but their children. It was her design that Sir Thomas should be struck, and that Mr. Ferrers should be instructed. The dinner answered her design but too well. Sir Thomas, who had no idea of concealing his sentiments, spoke to Miss Delacour of her father's estate in the West Indies with an easy familiarity which made her colour; and Mr. Ferrers, hearing the name of Delacour coupled with a fortune which he had himself exaggerated in his own mind, felt all the pangs of a man who discovers that he has been aspiring above his reach.

He was silent during the whole of the second course. Miss Delacour, who watched him, supposed that he was offended by Sir Thomas's attentions, and was pleased. But when the ladies had retired, and Sir Thomas had fallen asleep over his wine, Mr. Ferrers, who had no taste for the society of a sleeping baronet, followed them into the drawing-room, and, seating himself near Miss Delacour, said, with an effort at composure which was visible to her alone,—

"I understand, Miss Delacour, that Sir Thomas Gower is an old acquaintance of your family."

"He is no acquaintance at all," she replied, with a smile. "He is a gentleman whom Mrs. Marchmont has been so good as to introduce to me, and who has been so good as to suppose that I am richer than I am."

There was a lightness in her tone which he mistook for coquetry. "You are pleased to be mysterious," said he. "But I assure you, I have no design of intruding upon your confidence. I am aware that a woman of fortune must be cautious."

Miss Delacour looked at him with some surprise. "A woman of fortune!" she repeated. "Mr. Ferrers, you have been misinformed. I have no fortune. My father is a merchant, and not a very prosperous one; and if I have been received in Wimpole Street, it is because Mrs. Marchmont is pleased to think that my face may do her credit. I had thought you knew it."

The colour rose to Mr. Ferrers's cheeks. "I knew it!" he cried. "I knew nothing of the kind. I believed—I was told—I have been led to suppose—" He stopped, and then, with a candour which did him honour, he added, "I believed you to be an heiress, Miss Delacour; and I was the more ready to believe it, because I am not a rich man myself, and I was ashamed to appear before you as I am."

Miss Delacour was silent for a moment. Then she said, with a gravity which was not unmixed with a trembling, "And I believed you to be the owner of a very fine estate in Devonshire, Mr. Ferrers. I was the more ready to believe it, because I am not a rich woman, and I was ashamed to appear before you as I am."

They looked at each other, and there was a pause, in which the clock upon the chimney-piece seemed to tick with a peculiar distinctness. Then Mr. Ferrers began to laugh, and Miss Delacour, after a struggle, laughed too; and Mrs. Marchmont, who had been watching them from the other end of the room, and who had not the least idea of what had passed, was pleased to observe that they were getting on very well.

It was some weeks before the affair was settled, and it was settled, as such affairs are, without any very romantic declarations. Mr. Ferrers discovered that a small property in Devonshire, properly managed, might be made to support a wife; and Miss Delacour discovered that a woman without a fortune may yet be loved for herself. They were married in the autumn, and Mrs. Marchmont, who was heard to say that she had always foreseen it, was the first to congratulate them. Sir Thomas Gower, who had never understood the matter, sent them a piece of plate, and observed that he had always thought Miss Delacour a very sensible young woman. Whether he meant it as a compliment, or as a reproof, was never ascertained.
