It was upon a raw November afternoon, when the fog had settled so thickly upon Bloomsbury that the gas-lamps in Southampton Row burned like dim, yellow smudges in a wool-grey world, that I received a visitor whose errand was as singular as her appearance was commonplace. She was a stout, breathless woman of fifty, clad in decent black, with a bonnet that had known better days and a pair of cotton gloves which she twisted ceaselessly in her lap. My friend, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, was upon the sofa with his violin across his knee, and he regarded her from beneath half-closed lids with that air of languid attention which I had long since learned to recognise as the mask of the keenest interest.

“You are Mrs. Tredgold, I understand,” said he, as she was shown in. “The proprietress of the lodging-house in Marchmont Place. Pray be seated, and tell us your trouble from the beginning.”

Our visitor started. “Lord, sir, how did you know my name and where I keep?”

“The brass plate upon your gate is polished each morning by your own hands, for the smudge of the cloth is still upon your right glove. The address is upon the label of your basket, which you have not thought to remove. And your trouble, madam, if I may hazard a guess, concerns a lodger.”

“That is God’s truth, sir. A lodger by the name of Mr. Henry Vane, who came to me in August last, and who has this morning vanished as clean as if the earth had opened and swallowed him, leaving his trunk behind him and three weeks’ rent unpaid upon a quarter that he had settled in advance.”

Holmes laid down his violin and sat forward, his thin fingers pressed together. “You interest me exceedingly. Be so good as to give me the particulars, and spare nothing, however trifling it may appear.”

“He was a quiet gentleman,” said she, “and that is what troubles me, for quiet gentlemen are not in the habit of my house. He was perhaps forty years of age, spare and dark, with a face that had seen the sun in foreign parts, and a manner so soft that you would scarce hear him come down the stairs. He took my first-floor front, and he paid me for three months in advance in golden sovereigns, which I thought queer at the time, for gentlemen of means usually pay by cheque. He received no letters, sir—not one in all those weeks—and he had no visitors, saving a man in a seaman’s jersey who came once in September and stood upon my doorstep not five minutes. And every night, sir, he would set a candle in his window and let it burn until two of the clock, though he never sat at the window himself, nor seemed to look for anyone. I spoke to him about the waste of wax, and he said only that it was a habit he had from abroad, and paid me for the candles out of his own pocket.”

“A candle in the window,” murmured Holmes. “And burning until two. You are certain of the hour?”

“Certain as I stand here, sir. For my own room is at the back, and I am a poor sleeper, and many’s the time I have seen the light go out as the church clock struck the second hour.”

“Good. Continue.”

“There is little more to tell, sir, save that he was a great one for his meals, and would have his dinner sent up at seven and his breakfast at nine, and never a word of complaint in all the time. But this morning the girl went up with his tea at nine, and the door was open, and the bed had not been slept in, and there was the candle burnt down to the socket in the window, and the trunk standing at the foot of the bed with his clothes folded in it as neat as a woman’s work, and no sign of Mr. Vane at all.”

“And the trunk—you have not opened it?”

“That I have not, sir. I would not touch a gentleman’s property without witness, and I thought it best to come to you before I went to the police, for I have heard of your cleverness from my cousin, who is in service at Scotland Yard.”

Holmes rose and took down his hat and coat from the peg. “You did wisely, madam. Watson, I fancy the fog will not prevent our walking. Mrs. Tredgold, we shall accompany you to Marchmont Place at once, and I would be obliged if you would say nothing of our coming to your servants.”

The house in Marchmont Place was one of those tall, narrow edifices of the last century, with a fanlight over the door and a railing of iron before the area. The room upon the first floor was neat to the point of austerity: a bed, a washstand, a horsehair chair, and the trunk, a heavy affair of black leather bound with brass, standing open at the foot of the bed. Holmes went at once to the window, and there he stood for some minutes, examining the sill and the sash with a small lens which he had drawn from his pocket.

“You observe, Watson,” said he at last, “that the candle has been placed in the centre of the sill, and not at the side, where a man would naturally set it for his own convenience. It was not lit for the occupant of this room to see by. It was lit to be seen. And here, upon the outer sill, there is a stain of wax, and beside it a faint scoring of the paint, as though a thin wire had been drawn across it and made fast. Our quiet lodger was signalling, and he was signalled to in return.”

“To whom?” I asked.

“That is the question. Let us see what the trunk has to tell us.” He knelt beside it and lifted out the garments one by one—a dark suit, two linen shirts, a pair of boots much worn and stained with red earth, and at the bottom, wrapped in a piece of oiled silk, a small brass instrument of curious workmanship, being a cylinder fitted with a glass lens and a dial marked with the points of the compass. Holmes held it up to the light and his eyes glittered.

“A pocket transit, Watson. The instrument of a surveyor, or of a man who has need to take bearings in a wilderness. And observe the boots—the red earth is not the clay of London, but a ferruginous soil found in but few places in England, and most notably upon the moors of the north. Our friend Vane was not the idle gentleman he appeared.”

At that moment there came a heavy tread upon the stairs, and a constable presented himself at the door, with a sergeant behind him. “Beg pardon, ma’am,” said the sergeant, touching his helmet, “but we’ve had a report from the river police. A body was taken up at Blackfriars half an hour ago, and there was a card in the waistcoat pocket with this address upon it.”

Mrs. Tredgold uttered a cry and sank into the horsehair chair. Holmes was upon his feet in an instant. “The body is at the mortuary? Lead on, sergeant. Watson, bring your bag.”

The dead man lay upon a slab in the dim light of the Blackfriars mortuary, a spare, dark figure with the water running from his garments. Holmes examined him with a swift, silent thoroughness, turning back the sleeves and the collar, and at last he drew from the waistcoat a small pocket-book, which he opened. Within was a single folded paper, and upon it, in a fine, firm hand, these words: *The third beacon is lit at two. I go to the marsh at the ebb.*

“It is not murder, Watson,” said Holmes, as we stood in the fog without. “There is no mark of violence, and the man was a strong swimmer who had taken a cramp in the cold water. But he was engaged in some desperate business, and he was to meet his confederates at two of the clock, and he has gone to his account with his secret. The candle in the window was his signal that he was watched, and the wire upon the sill was the answer of those who watched him. Whether they meant him to drown, or whether he meant to swim to some vessel in the river, we shall perhaps never know. But the trunk and the transit and the red earth upon his boots tell me that the affair is not ended, and that somewhere upon the northern moors there is a man who waits for a signal that will never come.”

He turned up his collar against the fog. “Come, Watson. There is nothing more to be done tonight. But I shall keep the little brass instrument, and I shall think upon the third beacon.”
