The autumnal gales had swept the valley of the Severn with an unremitting fury, stripping the ancient oaks of their lingering verdure, and scattering the dead leaves in eddying heaps against the broken walls of the deserted habitations of mankind. It was a scene of profound and solemn desolation. The waters of the swollen river rushed turbid and violent between its forsaken banks, bearing upon its dark bosom the melancholy wrecks of a civilization that had departed forever. The pestilence, that unseen and remorseless foe, had emptied the cities, silencing the hum of industry and the cheerful voices of children, until the surviving remnants of the English race were scattered, like the torn foliage of the forest, over the face of their ravaged country. In this wide and woeful solitude, a small band of survivors had gathered themselves together in the shadow of the Malvern hills, seeking refuge from the horrors they had witnessed, and endeavoring, by the ties of common suffering, to rebuild some shattered fabric of human society.

Among these wanderers was a young woman named Aveline. She was of a lineage unmarked by worldly ambition, her heart having been ever devoted to the quiet contemplation of nature and the gentle offices of domestic affection. She had been a solitary mourner in the time of the great dying, having watched, with unavailing tears, the fading of the last beloved eyes that had beamed upon her in infancy; and the memory of that unspeakable anguish had cast a permanent shadow over her spirit, investing her manner with a gravity and a soft melancholy that spoke at once of deep suffering and of elevated purity. She had never sought power; the very thought of dominion over her fellow-creatures was alien to her gentle nature; yet, in the utter wreck of all established order, when the strong and the aged alike shrank from the responsibility of guiding the destinies of the remnant, the eyes of the whole community had turned, by a spontaneous and irresistible impulse, toward her. They had elected her their leader, not for any rugged force of will or imposing hardihood of character, but because her unblemished integrity, her calm courage in the face of despair, and her tender sympathy with the afflicted, had impressed upon their minds a conviction that she alone could be trusted to pilot them through the gathering darkness of their fate.

On the evening of which I speak, Aveline stood alone upon the summit of a low eminence that overlooked the valley, her gaze fixed upon the western horizon, where the departing storm had left a track of lurid and portentous splendor. The clouds, rent asunder by the expiring winds, disclosed a vast and solemn canopy of deepening violet, edged with a fiery gold that seemed to mock the desolation of the earth beneath. The sublimity of the scene spoke to her soul; she felt, in that high and breathless moment, how insignificant were the schemes and the sufferings of mortal men when compared with the unchanging majesty of the heavens and the eternal laws of the universe. Her thoughts, however, were soon recalled from these contemplative heights to the heavy burden of her actual position. A council had been held that day among the elders of the band, and a division of sentiment had manifested itself, which filled her with an anxious and foreboding sorrow. Some, whose hearts had been broken by the loss of their former homes, and who looked with terror upon the unknown regions beyond the horizon, implored her to keep them in the valley they knew, where the waters of the Severn still flowed, and where the soil, though overgrown, might yet be made to yield a scanty subsistence. Others, of a more adventurous spirit, or driven by a deeper desperation, urged that they should abandon the accursed district, and journey northward to the coast, where, they fondly imagined, the sea-breezes might purify the tainted air, and where some vestige of human society might yet be found upon the distant shores.

Aveline stood listening to the wind as it moaned through the leafless branches, and she felt the full weight of the responsibility that had been imposed upon her. To remain was to expose them, perhaps, to the return of the pestilence, or to the slow but certain encroachment of famine and despair; to depart was to lead them forth into a wilderness, where they might perish by the way, or find, at the end of their toilsome pilgrimage, only a more ghastly solitude. Her heart bled for them; she saw around her the pale faces of the weary and the bereaved, the trusting eyes of little children, the trembling hands of the aged; and she asked herself, with an anguish that no language could adequately express, by what right she had been chosen to decide the issue of their lives. She had not sought this dreadful pre-eminence; she had desired only to be a comforter, a fellow-sufferer, a humble sharer in their common grief; and now she found herself invested with a power which she loathed, and called upon to exercise a judgment upon which the fate of all depended. "Heaven," she murmured, in the overflowing bitterness of her spirit, "why hast Thou laid this burden upon me? I am but a weak and sorrowful woman, unfit to guide the destinies of my fellow-creatures; my own heart is broken, my own steps are uncertain; how then shall I lead others through the darkness that encompasses us? Oh, that some stronger arm, some wiser head, were here to direct us in this hour of supreme necessity!"

As she thus apostrophized the heavens, a figure approached her from the shadow of the trees. It was Elias, a man of mature years and formerly a scholar of some eminence, whose hair had whitened prematurely beneath the touch of sorrow, and whose countenance, though pale and worn, bore the impress of a serene and elevated intellect. He had been the first to propose her elevation to the leadership, and he had ever since regarded her with a reverence that was almost filial. He drew near, and, touching her arm with a gentle and respectful hand, he said, "My daughter, the night dews are falling heavily, and you have stood long in this keen wind. Come to the shelter of our roof; the assembly awaits your decision, and they will not be hasty to press their claims upon one who has already endured so much." Aveline turned toward him, and he saw that her eyes were filled with tears. "Elias," she replied, in a voice whose musical and plaintive tones were heightened by emotion, "I cannot resolve. I am torn asunder by conflicting fears. If we remain, I behold before us only a slow decay, a lingering death in the midst of these deserted fields, where every ruined dwelling will recall the memory of those who have perished, and every wind will seem to murmur a reproach against our rash security. If we go forth, I see before us a toilsome and perilous journey through a land strewn with the unburied dead, and at its close, what certainty have we that the coast will offer a refuge? The sea may be as solitary and as inhospitable as the land; the plague may have swept the shores as it has swept the valleys; and we may perish, far from the homes of our fathers, without a hand to close our eyes."

Elias looked upon her with deep compassion. "Yet, Aveline," he said, "to remain in inaction is to court despair. The human spirit was not made to repose in the shadow of death; it must strive, it must seek, it must go forward, even though the goal be uncertain. The valley is tainted with memories; the river flows over the graves of our kindred; the very air we breathe is heavy with the emanations of corruption. To stay is to die; to go is, at least, to live in hope. I have spoken with the younger men; they are ready to follow you to the end of the earth. They ask only that you will lead; they ask only that you will not abandon them to the lethargy that is creeping upon our diminished race."

His words sunk deeply into her mind; she felt their truth, and yet she shrank from the responsibility of acting upon them. She returned in silence to the rude habitation where the survivors were assembled. A flickering fire of dried wood cast a dim and uncertain light upon their anxious faces; the wind howled around the walls, and penetrated through the crevices with a chilling blast that seemed to prophesy the coming winter. They looked up as she entered, and a deep and breathless silence pervaded the room. She took her seat among them, and for a long time no one spoke. At length an old man, whose trembling frame and dim eyes bespoke the near approach of dissolution, raised his voice, and, in trembling accents, implored her not to drag them from the spot where they had spent their peaceful days. "Here," he said, "are the bones of my children; here I have lived, and here I wish to die. Do not take me from the valley; I have not strength to endure the journey; I shall perish by the way, and my bones will bleach upon the road, without a stone to mark my resting-place." A murmur of sympathy ran through the assembly; many of the women wept; and Aveline felt her heart sink within her as she listened to this appeal.

But then a young man, whose frame was robust and whose eye flashed with the fire of unsubdued courage, stood forward and replied. "We cannot stay here to die," he exclaimed. "The old man speaks for himself, not for us. We are young; we have our lives before us; we will not sit down and fold our hands in the midst of this desolation. Lead us to the coast, Aveline; lead us where we may breathe the pure air of the sea, and where we may build a new home, free from the associations of death and misery. If you refuse, we must go without you; but we would rather go with you, for we trust in your wisdom and your goodness."

Aveline rose; her tall and graceful figure was drawn up to its full height; her pale cheek flushed; her eye kindled with a sudden and unwonted fire. She felt that the moment had arrived when she must speak, and speak decisively. "My friends," she said, and her voice, though low, penetrated to every corner of the room, "I have listened to your counsels, and I have weighed them in the balance of my own judgment and my own fears. I know the attachment of some to this valley; I know the longing of others to seek a distant and unknown refuge. I will not decide hastily; I will not impose my will upon you by any arbitrary exercise of authority. But this I say: we cannot remain here without certainty of destruction. The plague may return; the winter will be severe; our provisions are scanty. We must go forth; we must seek a new abode. I will lead you to the coast. I know the perils of the journey; I know that we may encounter suffering and death by the way; but I believe that in motion alone there is safety, and that in seeking a new home we are fulfilling the designs of Providence, which has preserved us amid the general wreck that we might rebuild the fabric of human society. Let us depart, then, with courage and with trust in the mercy of Heaven. Let us leave this valley of the shadow of death, and turn our faces toward the sea, which, if it be not a refuge, will at least be a boundary to our wanderings, and will assure us that we have done all that human effort can do to preserve our lives and the lives of those committed to our charge."
