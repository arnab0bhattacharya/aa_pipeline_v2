The heat in the Grand Hall of the Palace of San Aurelio was not merely a physical weight but a moral substance, a thick, amber stagnation that seemed to suspend the very notion of time. It hung in the air, heavy with the scent of wax, old wood, and the faint, sweet rot of tropical flowers arranged in vases that had long since lost their freshness. The light, filtered through high, arched windows, fell in dusty shafts, illuminating the suspended motes like the ghosts of former, more virtuous regimes, while the rest of the room remained submerged in a shadowy, oppressive twilight. It was a place designed for ceremony, for the projection of stability, yet to Lieutenant Marcos Vaz, standing with a silver tray balanced on his steady hand, it felt less like a seat of power and more like a tomb where the dead had not yet quite settled into their rest.

General Augusto Rivas stood at the head of the long mahogany table, his uniform a study in aggressive cleanliness, the brass buttons catching the dim light with a sharpness that seemed almost violent. He had seized the presidency three weeks prior, not with the grandeur of a revolution, but with the quiet, efficient brutality of a man closing a ledger. The foreign merchants, men of various nationalities whose languages blended into a murmur of anxious commerce, sat in stiff, upright rows. They were there to be reassured, to be told that the order of things, however violently disrupted, would remain profitable. They ate little. Their eyes, however, moved constantly, cataloging the faces, the weapons, the silence. They were men who dealt in risk, but this particular risk had a taste of ash in their mouths.

Vaz moved between them, a ghost in the background, pouring wine from a decanter that had belonged to the previous president, a man who had been removed with a efficiency that still chilled the blood. The wine was dark, heavy, and old. As he poured for the German textile importer, Herr Kessler, Vaz watched the liquid swirl in the crystal glass. He thought of the province of El Cid, where he had spent the last two years before being recalled to the capital. He remembered the way the dust had settled on the faces of the starving, how the silence of the fields was not peaceful but pregnant with a terrible, waiting violence. He had seen the men Rivas’s men had left behind, not dead, but broken, their eyes hollowed out by a despair that no amount of order could fill. He had seen the looting, not of gold, but of dignity. And now, here, in the cool, perfumed air of the palace, Rivas spoke of "stability" and "economic continuity" as if these were concrete things one could build with brick and mortar, rather than fragile illusions woven from fear and force.

"General," said one of the merchants, a Frenchman with a face like a carved apple, his voice thin and reedy in the vast space. "We look forward to the new... arrangements. The security of our assets is... paramount."

Rivas smiled. It was a thin smile, barely touching his eyes, which remained cold and hard as river stones. "Security is the foundation of prosperity, Monsieur," he replied, his voice low, carrying a weight that seemed to press down on the tablecloth. "I have restored order. The chaos of the previous administration is a thing of the past. We are a nation of law, of tradition, of... continuity."

The word *continuity* hung in the air, absurd and heavy. Vaz, passing behind the Frenchman, felt a sudden, sharp pang of nausea. He thought of the boy in El Cid, the one who had stolen a loaf of bread and been shot for it by a corporal who was drunk on aguardiente and hate. The boy’s blood had been bright and hot in the dust, a stark contrast to the pale, dusty light of the morning. It was a memory that did not fit into the polished narrative of the General. It was a splinter in the smooth wood of the regime.

Vaz poured more wine. His hands did not tremble. He had learned to control the tremor, to keep the body as a separate entity from the mind, a vessel for duty that could be emptied and refilled. He watched Rivas’s face. The General was a large man, broad-shouldered, with a beard that hid a mouth that rarely smiled with genuine warmth. He was a soldier of the old school, a man who believed that power was a right, not a responsibility. He had no understanding of the people he ruled; to him, they were merely numbers on a map, resources to be managed, threats to be neutralized.

The banquet continued, a ritual of false intimacy. Toasts were made, clinked glasses, the sound sharp and brittle. The merchants laughed, a nervous, high-pitched sound that did not reach their eyes. They were buying time, bargaining with the devil for the survival of their businesses. Vaz saw the fear in them, the same fear he had seen in the eyes of the men in the provinces, the fear of the unknown, of the arbitrary violence that could strike at any moment. But here, the fear was dressed in silk and perfume; there, it was naked and bleeding.

He moved to the end of the table, where the light was dimmest. In the corner, half-hidden by the shadow of a pillar, sat a man who was not a merchant. He was a journalist, an outsider, whose presence was a secret among the guests. He watched Rivas with a intensity that was almost predatory. Vaz met his eyes for a fraction of a second. There was no recognition, no exchange of meaning, only a shared awareness of the hollowness of the moment. The journalist was writing, or perhaps just observing, capturing the scene in his mind, preparing to turn it into words that would fly across the ocean, stripping the General of his dignity, exposing the rot beneath the polish.

Vaz turned away, his tray now empty. The heat pressed against him, a physical weight. He thought of the river, the wide, muddy expanse that separated the capital from the provinces. It was a boundary, not just of geography, but of reality. On one side, the palace, the wine, the lies. On the other, the dust, the blood, the truth. He was a man caught between them, a bridge that served neither bank. He walked back to the service entrance, his footsteps silent on the polished floor.

The door opened, and the night air rushed in, cool and damp, carrying the scent of rain and distant thunder. It was a relief, a brief respite from the suffocating atmosphere of the hall. He stepped out onto the balcony, looking down at the garden, where the shadows were deepening. The city lights were beginning to flicker on in the distance, a constellation of human ambition and misery. He stood there, listening to the silence of the night, which was not empty, but full of the sounds of the world turning, indifferent to the will of any single man. The General was still speaking, his voice faint now, a whisper in the distance, a sound that had lost its power, its meaning. It was just a voice, a vibration in the air, fading into the dark. Vaz closed his eyes, letting the memory of the blood in the dust wash over him, a cleansing, terrible purity that the wine and the lies could never touch.
