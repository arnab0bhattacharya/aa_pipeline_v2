The rain had not stopped for forty-one days. It fell in a grey, granular sheet that turned the Thames into a swollen, turbid artery and made the glass of the Houses of Parliament weep with condensation. From his seat in the upper gallery, Caspian Thorne watched a rivulet of water trace a jagged path down the pane, mirroring the fissures he felt cracking open the world below.

He was a Thorne, which in the new England meant something, though he had spent his life pretending it didn't. His family’s wealth was old, rooted in the pre-Collapse land-holdings of the North, and its power was now expressed in the silent, algorithmic hum of agri-conglomerates and water-rights. Caspian had been raised in a world of quiet, insulated comfort, his cynicism a well-tailored suit he wore to keep the messy, sweating reality of politics at arm’s length. He had found it all so terribly, terribly tedious. The theatrics of the Chamber, the performative outrage of the Populist bloc, the tired, patrician murmurings of his own Heritage Party—it was a puppet show for a nation that, until recently, had been content to be distracted by its screens and its synthetic bread.

The storm changed that. They were calling it the Great Blight. It had begun in the North Atlantic, a hyper-cane that had stalled over the country, unleashing a deluge that washed the topsoil from East Anglia and turned the Midlands into a shallow, brown sea. The harvest didn't just fail; it was erased. The vast, climate-controlled agri-domes, the pride of the nation’s technocrats, had been battered into submission, their solar arrays shattered, their internal climates fouled by the relentless humidity. The food queues began three weeks ago. The riots, two weeks after that. The word ‘famine’ was no longer a whisper in the corridors of power; it was a hammering fist on the door.

Caspian’s father, Lord Alistair Thorne, had been summoned to a dozen emergency meetings. He would return to their Kensington townhouse, his face ashen, and pour himself a generous measure of single malt. “It’s the end of reason, Caspian,” he’d say, the ice clinking against the crystal. “The mob doesn’t want solutions. They want a scapegoat. They want us.”

And now, here he was. He wasn't supposed to be here. His father, taken ill with a stress-induced cardiac event, had been ordered by his physicians to rest. The party whip, a sleek and frightened man named Piers Farrow, had come to Caspian in a panic. "Your father's seat, his vote on the Emergency Powers Bill... it's crucial. The Prime Minister needs every hand. You must go, take his proxy."

Caspian had laughed, a dry, hollow sound. "I know nothing of your bill, Farrow. I am not a politician."

"You're a Thorne," Farrow had hissed, his eyes wide. "Right now, that's all that matters."

So he had come, a proxy in a suit that cost more than most people's monthly food credit. He sat in the gallery, a ghost at the feast, listening to the debate. The Prime Minister, a woman named Eleanor Vance with a voice like polished steel, was trying to project calm. Her plans were all about rationing, about international aid, about a "phased recovery." The leader of the opposition, a fiery man from the industrial North named Rhys Morgan, was having none of it. His voice echoed through the chamber, thick with fury.

"Phased recovery? The people of Leeds are eating protein paste made from sawdust! The children of Norfolk are drinking water that tastes of the sea! Your 'phased recovery' is a death sentence for a million of our citizens, and it is delivered from the comfort of a bunker!"

The chamber erupted. The Speaker, a weary-looking woman in a stiff collar, called for order, her gavel a frantic, useless heartbeat against the roar. Caspian watched the faces below. He saw the fear in the eyes of the Heritage Party members, the righteous anger of the Populists, the cold calculation of the Technocrats. It was all so predictable, so utterly, damnably human. A nation was starving, and they were arguing over procedure. He felt a familiar wave of revulsion. He should leave. He should go back to the townhouse, check on his father, and wait for this all to burn itself out.

Then it happened. A young woman in the public gallery, her face pale and streaked with tears, stood up and screamed. "You're all the same! You sit here in your velvet chairs while we starve! You're not our government! You're our executioners!"

Security moved to remove her, but the damage was done. Her cry had been raw, honest, and it had cut through the chamber's stale air like a blade. The debate stopped. The politicians fell silent, their faces masks of shock and, in some cases, genuine shame. In the sudden, ringing quiet, Piers Farrow looked up at the gallery, his eyes locking onto Caspian. He gave a small, desperate nod.

It was an accident of timing, a twist of fate. The Speaker, seeking to restore some semblance of dignity, recognised the proxy for the Northern Counties. "The Right Honourable Member for the Northern Counties has the floor."

All eyes turned to the gallery. Caspian felt the weight of their gaze, a physical pressure on his chest. He was not a speaker. He was a dilettante, a cynic, a man who had spent his life avoiding the very thing he was now being asked to do. He stood, his legs feeling strangely detached from his body. He descended the winding stone stairs to the floor of the Chamber, his footsteps echoing in the silence. He took his father's seat on the green leather benches, the smell of old wood and polish filling his nostrils.

He looked at the faces opposite him, at Rhys Morgan's defiant scowl, at Eleanor Vance's weary, calculating eyes. He had no speech prepared. He had no policy. He had only the image of that woman's face, her raw, unvarnished pain.

He cleared his throat. "My father," he began, his voice quieter than he intended, "could not be here today. He is unwell. Like many of you, I suspect, he is finding the current climate… taxing." A few nervous chuckles. "I am not a politician. I have no interest in your parties, your factions, your endless, circular arguments. I am, as you all know, a Thorne. I have spent my life in the comfortable, quiet places of this country, places that the storm has not yet touched."

He paused, letting the admission hang in the air. He saw confusion on their faces. This was not the expected script.

"But I am also a man who can see. And what I see is a country that has been broken. Not by the storm, though the storm was the hammer. It was broken by a failure of imagination. We built a nation on a foundation of sand, a nation that believed the food would always come, that the power would always flow, that the comfortable order of things was an immutable law of nature. We were wrong."

He looked directly at the Prime Minister. "You speak of phased recovery, Prime Minister. But you cannot recover a phantom limb. The England we knew, the one of endless plenty and placid indifference, is gone. It was washed away with the topsoil."

He turned to Rhys Morgan. "And you, Mr. Morgan. You speak of anger, and your anger is justified. But anger is a fire. It can warm a house, or it can burn it to the ground. What will you do when the last of the protein paste is gone? Will you shout at the empty warehouses?"

The chamber was utterly silent. He was speaking to them not as a politician, but as a man who had just woken up from a long, luxurious sleep to find his house on fire.

"My family's fortune was built on the land. We understood, once, that the land is not a resource to be exploited, but a partner to be respected. We forgot that. You all forgot that. We became a nation of consumers, not citizens. We outsourced our resilience for the sake of convenience."

He leaned forward, his voice gaining a strength he didn't know he possessed. "We cannot go back. We can only go forward. But we must go forward together. Not as parties, not as factions, but as a people. The bill before you is not enough. It is a patch on a mortal wound. We need a new vision. We need to tear up the old maps and draw new ones. We need to put the people in the fields, not just the queues. We need to teach our children how to grow food, not just how to use a screen. We need to remember what it is to be a nation, not an economy."

He stopped. He had no more words. He had spoken from a place he didn't know existed, a place beyond his carefully cultivated cynicism. He sat down, his heart hammering against his ribs.

For a long moment, no one moved. Then, a single, startling sound. A clap. It came from the Labour benches, from Rhys Morgan. He was looking at Caspian not with anger, but with a grudging, surprised respect. Another clap, then another, until the entire chamber, Heritage and Populist and Technocrat alike, was on its feet, the applause a wave of sound that was part relief, part hope, and part terror. They were not applauding him. They were applauding the truth he had been forced to speak. They were applauding the end of the old world, and the terrifying, uncertain dawn of the new. Caspian Thorne, the man who had despised politics, had just become one.
