In the city of Valeriana, where the domes were as pale as egg-shells and the river wore the colour of old silver, there lived a clockmaker named Lucius Bellamy. His shop was a little jewel-box of a place, smelling of brass and oil and the dry, sweet scent of cedar shavings, and it was filled with the patient, pensive ticking of a hundred clocks, each one counting the moments in its own particular voice. Lucius himself was a man of slender fingers and a mind of intricate design, for he believed that Time was not a tyrant to be obeyed, but a fabric to be woven, and that every soul was allotted but a single, precious thread.

His masterpiece, which he had spent a decade creating, was not a clock at all, but rather a clock’s soul made manifest. It was a great orrery of polished mahogany and inlaid lapis lazuli, with arms of spun gold and spheres of blown crystal. In its centre, where the sun of its small universe should have been, hung a single, flawless diamond, the size of a pigeon’s egg, that caught the light and held it captive. This was the Chronosynclastic Infundibulum, as Lucius had named it, and it did not mark the passage of time so much as it commanded it. By turning a small, silver key, one could cause the great diamond to cease its vibration, and in that instant, the world outside the workshop would grow utterly still. The birds would hang arrested in the air, the rain would become a curtain of suspended pearls, and the very thoughts of men would pause, like guests caught mid-sentence at a dinner party.

The fame of this marvel, of course, reached the ears of King Theron the Unhurried, a monarch whose chief terror was the swiftness of his own approaching dissolution. He was a man who loved life with a greedy, possessive passion, and the thought of leaving his silken cushions and his flatterers and his golden plate was an insufferable indignity. He summoned Lucius to the palace, not with a request, but with a command that fell as heavy and cold as a headsman’s axe.

The King received him in a chamber of crimson velvet and tarnished gold, where the air was thick with the scent of wilting roses. “Master Bellamy,” he began, his voice a soft, wheedling drawl. “I am told you have conquered the hours. I wish to conquer the years. You will bring your device here, and you will use it to grant me eternity. I wish to be, as the poets say, forever in my prime.”

Lucius Bellamy, who was more comfortable with the honest ticking of a escapement than the slippery pronouncements of a king, bowed his low. “Your Majesty, the instrument is one of precision, not of power. It is a tool for observing, not for possessing. The price of stopping the clock is not one I would advise any man to pay, least of all a king.”

“Price?” Theron laughed, a dry, rustling sound. “Everything has a price, and I am a man who can afford to pay it. Name your sum. A duchy? A mountain of jewels? The hand of my prettiest niece?”

“The price is not in gold, sire,” Lucius said, his voice quiet but firm. “The Infundibulum does not add to life; it merely holds a single moment in suspension. To cease the clock is to cease the world within that moment. You will not live forever; you will simply *be* forever, in one unchanging instant. You will see no more dawns, hear no more music, feel no new joy. Your life will become a perfect, motionless portrait of itself. The world will move on without you, and you will be a statue in the gallery of your own making, forever admired by no one, not even yourself.”

The King waved a languid, bejewelled hand. “Poetic nonsense. You speak of a cage; I call it a sanctuary. A single, perfect moment is all I desire. Bring the device.”

Lucius knew then that argument was as useless as trying to reason with a hurricane. He returned to his workshop, his heart heavy as a lead weight, and with the help of his apprentices, he moved the great machine to the palace. It was installed in the King’s solar, its golden arms gleaming in the afternoon light. The courtiers gathered, their faces a mixture of awe and avarice, whispering of the immortality that was to be theirs as well, for surely the King would share such a gift.

Lucius stood before the machine one last time, the silver key cool in his palm. “Think again, Your Majesty,” he pleaded. “To freeze the moment is to kill the soul of it. A rose is beautiful because it withers. A song is sweet because it ends.”

“Silence, you fool of a mechanic!” the King snapped, his patience finally exhausted. “Turn the key! Grant me my eternity!”

With a sigh that seemed to carry the weight of all the clocks he had ever made, Lucius Bellamy inserted the key into the lock. He turned it. There was a soft, final click, like the last note of a forgotten lullaby.

In the centre of the great orrery, the diamond ceased to shimmer. The dust motes that had been dancing in the sunbeam froze in mid-air, a galaxy of tiny, golden stars. The King, who had been in the act of raising a goblet of wine to his lips, was caught with a look of triumphant greed on his face, his fingers just touching the crystal. A courtier, leaning forward to whisper some new flattery, was fixed in a posture of fawning adoration. A fly, buzzing near the tapestry, hung motionless, its iridescent wings a smear of emerald and sapphire.

Lucius Bellamy stood alone in the sudden, profound silence. The ticking of the palace clocks had ceased. The only sound was the frantic beating of his own heart. He looked at the King, a perfect, horrible jewel of a moment, forever preserved. He had his eternity. He was a king in a kingdom of one, a monarch of a silent, sunlit afternoon. The price, as Lucius had warned, was everything. The clockmaker turned and walked out of the chamber, leaving the King to his perfect, unending, and utterly dead forever.
