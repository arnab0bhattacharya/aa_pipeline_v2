The rain had not ceased for three days when Elias Thorne entered my office in Bow Lane, bringing with him the damp, fermenting smell of a brewery. He was a man of perhaps thirty years, pale and well-dressed, but his hands shook as he laid three yellow telegraph forms on my desk. I noted the insignia of the South Eastern Railway on each, and the dates of transmission, the most recent being that very morning.

"I come to you, Mr. Callow, because the police think me mad," he said, "and because I am afraid I shall be dead by Sunday."

I bade him sit and pour himself a drink from the decanter on the sideboard. He ignored the decanter but sat, gripping his knees as though to keep himself from flying apart. I read the telegrams in order. Each contained only a name, a date, and a time of day. The first: *Josiah Varley, 14th inst., 9:40 a.m.* The second: *Martin Hooke, 16th inst., 6:15 p.m.* The third: *Elias Thorne, 21st inst., 11:00 a.m.* There was no sender identified on any of them.

"You knew these men," I said.

"Varley was my father's senior clerk at the brewery in Malling Magna. Hooke was the malt master. Both had worked for Thornes Ales for twenty years and more." He swallowed. "Varley was struck by a hansom cab on the Strand on the fourteenth, at half past nine in the morning. I saw the body. Hoke fell from the gantry above the kiln floor on the sixteenth and broke his neck. The time on the factory bell was quarter past six."

I set the telegrams down and looked at him steadily. "And you received each telegram the morning of the day in question."

"The morning of, Mr. Callow. Before the news could have reached London by any ordinary means. Varley's came on the fourteenth. Hooke's on the sixteenth. And this morning, the twenty-first, I received my own name."

I asked him about enemies, about disputes at the brewery, about the contents of his father's will, which he had inherited upon old Cornelius Thorne's death the previous autumn. He answered readily. The brewery was prosperous. There had been no quarrels of note. His father had died of a decline, peacefully, in his own bed. There was a cousin, one Godfrey Thorne, who had expected a share in the business and received nothing, but Godfrey was a harmless creature living in reduced circumstances in Lambeth and had not, to Elias's knowledge, ever threatened anyone.

I told him I would accompany him to Malling Magna on the morning train. He protested that this would leave him with only two days, but I said that if the telegrams were the work of a murderer, then the murderer was in Kent, and that was where we must go.

The train took us through a sodden countryside to the little station at Malling Magna, a market town whose chief industry was the brewery that bore Elias's name. The works sat on a rise above the River Beult, a complex of brick buildings and iron roofs from which steam rose even in the rain. We arrived in the early afternoon and went directly to the brewery offices, where Elias introduced me to the remaining senior staff. I watched them carefully. The head brewer, a Mr. Selby, was a large, phlegmatic man who seemed genuinely grieved by the deaths. The bookkeeper, Mrs. Ada Fenn, was sharp-eyed and precise, and she told me that both Varley and Hooke had been in good spirits and good health before their deaths.

I asked whether anything unusual had occurred in the months since Cornelius Thorne's death. Mrs. Fenn glanced at Elias, then said that Mr. Varley had been troubled by something in the accounts, a discrepancy he had mentioned to her only once and then refused to discuss further. She had assumed it was a minor matter, now lost.

I asked to see Varley's office. It had been left untouched since his death, as the police had requested. It was a small room with a high window looking out over the loading yard. I searched the desk methodically. In the bottom drawer, beneath a stack of delivery ledgers, I found a bundle of correspondence tied with string. Most of it was routine. But at the bottom of the bundle was a letter, written in a hand I did not recognise, dated the previous July.

The letter was addressed to Cornelius Thorne. It was brief and ugly in its content. The writer accused Thorne of having, thirty years prior, falsified the deed of partnership that established the brewery, thereby defrauding the writer's father of his rightful half-share. The letter demanded restitution and threatened exposure. It was signed *Godfrey Thorne*.

I showed this to Elias. He went white. "Godfrey is my father's cousin," he said. "He was always bitter about the brewery. But this — I had no notion he made such claims. My father never spoke of it."

I asked Mrs. Fenn whether Godfrey Thorne had visited the brewery in recent months. She said he had called once in November, asking to see the partnership deed, and had been turned away by Mr. Varley on Elias's instructions. She remembered the date: it was the day after old Cornelius's funeral.

We took a hansom to the telegraph office near the station. I showed the clerk the three telegrams and asked from which office they had been sent. He examined them and said they had all been dispatched from the Lambeth office. I asked whether he could identify the sender. He could not, but he remembered that a man had come in early on the mornings of the fourteenth and sixteenth and had paid cash. The man had been thin and poorly dressed and had a cast in his left eye.

I sent a wire to a contact at Scotland Yard, requesting that a man be sent to Godfrey Thorne's address in Lambeth. Then I told Elias that we would spend the night at the brewery. He objected, but I told him that if the telegram was genuine, the attempt would come in Malling Magna, not in London, and that the brewery was the place where the deed — if deed there was — had its roots.

We slept in the office, taking turns. Nothing disturbed the night except the settling of the buildings and the rain on the roof. In the morning, the twenty-first, I rose early and walked the premises. The kiln floor where Hooke had fallen was a high, open gallery above the malt-drying ovens, with a low rail. I noted that a section of the rail was loose, the bolts recently worked. I pointed this out to Elias, who paled further.

At half past ten, I positioned myself in the loading yard with a clear view of the kiln gallery and the main staircase. Elias I kept beside me. The brewery hands went about their work. At a quarter to eleven, Mrs. Fenn came out to say that a man had called at the gate asking for Mr. Thorne. I told her to send him through.

He came walking across the yard, a thin man in a damp coat, and I saw the cast in his left eye. He carried a telegram envelope in his hand. He stopped when he saw Elias and me together, and something in his face changed — a tightening, a recognition that the pattern had been broken.

"Godfrey Thorne, I presume," I said.

He looked at me and then at Elias. "I came to warn him," he said. "I came to warn him, as I warned the others."

I asked him what he had warned them of. He said that he had learned, through a solicitor's clerk in Maidstone who owed him a debt, that the partnership deed was indeed a forgery — that his father had been cheated, and that he had proof. He had written to Cornelius and been ignored. He had gone to the brewery after the funeral and been refused. In his bitterness, he had sent the telegrams to Varley and Hooke, not as threats but as warnings, because he had overheard, in a public house in Lambeth, two men discussing a plan to kill them. He had gone to the police and been dismissed. He had sent the third telegram to Elias for the same reason, and had come himself to Malling Magna to tell him in person.

"What men?" I asked. "And what was the plan?"

He gave me the names. They were brewery hands, and one of them was the man who had loosened the rail on the kiln gallery. Within the hour, all three were in custody. The matter of the deed was settled the following spring in Chancery, where it was found that Cornelius Thorne had indeed altered the document, and Godfrey Thorne was awarded a share that, by then, he was content to take in quarterly payments and a quiet corner office in the building that bore his family's name.
