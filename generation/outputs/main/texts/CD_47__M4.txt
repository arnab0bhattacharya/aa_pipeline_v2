The fair at Cinderhallow, held upon that wide and miry expanse of common which lies to the north-east of London, was a spectacle to make the sober citizen clutch his pocket-book and the philosopher despair of his species. Here were swings that went perilously high and roundabouts that went perilously low; here were gingerbread stalls, and oyster stalls, and stalls where a man with a painted face sold bottles of physic warranted to cure the gout, the measles, and the melancholy; here were giants, dwarfs, learned geese, and a mermaid constructed, I am credibly informed, from the tail of a codfish and the countenance of a monkey. The air was thick with the smell of fried onions, the cries of hawkers, the squeaking of fiddles, and the honest thunder of the drum; and the mud beneath one’s feet was of that rich, adhesive, tenacious character which seems peculiar to English fairs, and which has, I am persuaded, swallowed up more boots than the sea has swallowed ships.

It was upon the eastern margin of this pleasant chaos, hard by a booth where a man in a paper hat invited all and sundry to “witness the astonishing feats of the Invisible Lady,” that there stood, upon a little platform of boards, a man named Mr. Absalom Cringe. He was a small, anxious, threadbare man, with a face like a crumpled pocket-handkerchief, and a coat that had been black in the reign of George the Third, and had been growing more and more philosophical ever since. He wore a tall hat, much battered, and he held in his hand a slender wand, and he addressed the crowd in a voice of melancholy politeness.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” said Mr. Cringe, “with your kind permission, and the favour of a moment’s attention, I will now have the honour to introduce to you the most astonishing of all the productions of nature and of art—the Learned Pig! The pig, ladies and gentlemen, that reads the newspapers, that plays at cards, that tells the time by the clock, and that knows the difference between a gentleman and a blackguard, which last is more than some of us can do. Observe him, ladies and gentlemen—observe him well—the pride of my existence, the comfort of my declining years, the only friend, I may say, that Absalom Cringe possesses in this wide and wicked world—the Learned Pig!”

At this, the Learned Pig himself, who had been standing upon the boards in an attitude of profound meditation, with his snout towards the earth, raised his head and looked about him with an expression of such mild and melancholy intelligence, that the crowd, which had been disposed to jeer, became suddenly silent. He was a pig of no common order. His skin was of a delicate pink, his ears were large and expressive, and his eye—that small, bright, sagacious eye—seemed to take in the whole company at a glance, and to estimate each individual at his proper value. He wore a collar of red leather, with a little bell attached to it, and he had about him an air of conscious superiority which was quite remarkable in a quadruped.

“Now, ladies and gentlemen,” continued Mr. Cringe, “I will ask the pig to favour us with a game at cards. He will play with any gentleman present who is not afraid to match his wits against a porker’s. Is there any gentleman present who will oblige me? Is there any gentleman who will condescend to take a hand with the Learned Pig?”

There was a stir in the crowd, and a man stepped forward. He was a large, florid man, with a white hat, a blue coat, and a waistcoat of yellow plush, and he wore a heavy gold chain across it, and he smiled a broad, self-satisfied smile, as who should say, “I am the man for you.” He carried in his hand a pack of cards, which he shuffled with a dexterity that showed him to be no novice.

“I’ll play the pig,” said he, “for a guinea a game, and I’ll give him the first deal into the bargain.”

Mr. Cringe looked at the stranger with some distrust. “Your name, sir, if I may be so bold?”

“My name is Mr. Silas Grubb,” returned the other, “and I am a gentleman at large, and I never yet met the pig that could beat me at cards, and I don’t expect to meet him now.”

The crowd laughed, and Mr. Cringe, who was a poor man and could not afford to lose a guinea, hesitated. But the Learned Pig, who had been listening to the conversation with an air of the deepest attention, now stepped forward and laid his snout upon the pack of cards, as much as to say, “I accept the challenge.” The crowd cheered, and Mr. Cringe, with a sigh, consented.

The game was played upon a little table which Mr. Cringe produced from beneath the platform. The Learned Pig sat upon his haunches upon one side, and Mr. Silas Grubb sat upon the other, and the crowd pressed close around, and the game began. The pig played with a gravity and a precision that were quite wonderful. He dealt the cards with his snout, he turned them over with his hoof, he indicated his play by a little tap upon the table, and he never made a mistake. Mr. Grubb, on the other hand, played with a reckless confidence which was not justified by the event. He lost the first game, and he lost the second, and he lost the third, and at each loss his face grew redder and his smile grew broader, and he declared that the pig was the most astonishing creature that ever was seen.

“I’ll play one more game,” said he, “for double or quits, and I’ll stake my watch against the pig.”

Mr. Cringe, who was now quite elated by his success, consented; but the Learned Pig, who had been looking at Mr. Grubb with an expression of profound distrust, now laid his snout upon Mr. Cringe’s arm, and grunted a warning. Mr. Cringe, however, was too much flushed with victory to heed it. The game was played, and Mr. Grubb won. He won the next game, and the next, and the next, and at each win his smile grew broader and his face grew redder, and the crowd began to murmur. At last Mr. Grubb laid down his cards and rose from his seat.

“The pig is mine,” said he, “according to the terms of the wager, and I’ll trouble you to hand him over.”

Mr. Cringe turned pale. “The wager,” said he, “was for the watch, not for the pig.”

“The wager was for double or quits,” returned Mr. Grubb, “and the pig was the stake. I’ll thank you to hand him over, or I’ll call a constable.”

The crowd, which had been with Mr. Cringe, now began to veer round to Mr. Grubb, for the English public is always upon the side of the winner, and Mr. Cringe, who was a timid man, saw that resistance was useless. With tears in his eyes he handed the string of the Learned Pig to Mr. Grubb, and Mr. Grubb, with a broad smile, led the pig away, and the crowd dispersed, and Mr. Cringe sat down upon his platform and covered his face with his hands.

He was still sitting thus, in an attitude of the deepest dejection, when he was accosted by a man of a very different appearance from Mr. Grubb. This was a short, stout, rubicund man, with a face like a hot pie, and a white apron, and a tray of pies upon his head, and he was known to all the fair as Mr. Bartholomew Bung, the hot-pie man.

“Cheer up, Mr. Cringe,” said Mr. Bung, “and have a pie. It’s a pork pie, and I don’t mean any offence.”

Mr. Cringe raised his head and took the pie mechanically.

“I’m a ruined man, Mr. Bung,” said he. “The pig was my all. I have nothing left but this hat and this wand, and I don’t know which is the more worthless.”

“Stuff and nonsense,” returned Mr. Bung. “The pig was won by foul play, and foul play may be beat by foul play. I saw that Grubb shuffle, and I’ll take my oath he had a card up his sleeve. What you want is a friend.”

“I have no friends,” said Mr. Cringe.

“You have two,” said Mr. Bung, “and here comes the second.”

The second was a tall, lean, melancholy man, with a face like a half-paid bill, and a bundle of ballads under his arm, and he was known as Mr. Nathaniel Whine, the ballad-seller. He had a voice of extraordinary power, and he could sing a comic song with a face of such profound melancholy that it was impossible to hear him without laughter.

“I have heard of your misfortune, Mr. Cringe,” said Mr. Whine, “and I am come to offer my services. I have a plan.”

“What plan?” asked Mr. Cringe.

“A plan,” said Mr. Whine, “that requires the assistance of a third party—a party of youth and innocence and cunning. And here she comes.”

The third party was a girl of about twelve years of age, with a basket of watercress upon her arm, and a face that was half impudence and half good-nature, and she was known as Miss Polly Prue. She had a sharp eye, a sharp tongue, and a sharper wit, and she was the terror of the fair.

“I’m your girl,” said Polly, “and I know a thing or two. That Grubb is a rogue, and I’ll be even with him. He’s stopping at the ‘Pig and Whistle’ at the corner of the common, and he’s got the pig in the stable, and he’s going to sell him to-morrow to a showman from Bartholomew Fair. But I’ve got a key to the stable, and I’ve got a plan.”

The plan was this: Mr. Whine was to engage Mr. Grubb in conversation at the front of the inn, and Mr. Bung was to engage the landlord in a dispute about the price of pies, and Polly was to slip round to the stable and unlock the door, and Mr. Cringe was to wait at the back with a rope and a lantern, and the pig was to be led away in triumph.

The plan was carried out that very night. Mr. Whine sang Mr. Grubb a comic song of such extraordinary melancholy that the tears ran down that gentleman’s cheeks, and Mr. Bung created a diversion by dropping a tray of hot pies upon the landlord’s toes, and Polly, with the key which she had abstracted from the landlord’s pocket, unlocked the stable door, and Mr. Cringe, with a trembling hand, led forth the Learned Pig, who grunted with joy at the sight of his master, and licked his hand with his rough tongue, and trotted after him as obedient as a dog.
