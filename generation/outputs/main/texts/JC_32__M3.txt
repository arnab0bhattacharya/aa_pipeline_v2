The steamer’s wake had barely faded upon the brown, swirling face of the Mokoro when the silence of the vast, over-arching forest closed in again upon Lukundu, like a heavy, invisible tide. The brief, disturbing visitation of the white man’s smoke and splashing paddles had passed, leaving behind only a few crumpled leaves of printed paper and a profound unease in the heart of Hollis. He stood on the uneven, rotting planks of the station’s landing-stage, staring not at the water, but at the dense, motionless wall of the woods, which seemed to breathe a hot, damp exhalation of decay and uncomprehending patience. The morning had been clouded with a peculiar yellow heat that weighed upon the brain; now, as the afternoon lengthened, the light began to fail, and the shadows lengthened, taking on the shapes of stealthy, crouching things.

Hollis held the letter from the coast in his hand, a flimsy, smudged document that carried across a thousand miles of river and forest the ultimate, irrevocable mandate of a commercial enterprise. It was a mandate of abandonment. The chief agent at the mouth of the Mokoro, a man of ledgers and precise, unfeeling syntax, had written that the Lukundu station was no longer a paying concern. The expenses of maintaining it, the risks of the river rapids, the dilapidation of the buildings, and the uncertain yield of the ivory trade in this upper reach had rendered it an anomaly. The orders were explicit: secure the remaining tusks, leave the local chief to his own devices, and return with the next company steamer. It was a matter of business logic, of course. But Hollis, gazing at the impenetrable thicket, felt the profound irony of such logic in the face of the immense, breathing reality of the land. It was like a man deciding to empty the ocean with a cup, and then, finding the task unprofitable, tossing the cup away.

He had been three years at Lukundu. Three years of a solitude so deep it had become a second skin. He had arrived a mere company servant, a dispassionate collector of ivory, but the land had worked upon him with its silence, its fevers, its slow, inevitable rhythm of decay and rebirth. He had come to know the local people, not as units of trade, but as souls enmeshed in the mysterious, often terrifying, web of existence that the forest represented. And he had come to know Chief Makewe.

A footstep on the loose planking caused Hollis to turn. Makewe stood a few paces away, his tall, lean form draped in a faded, once-crimson cloth that had been a gift from the company. His face was an ebon mask of grave, composed severity, marked with the cicatrices of his rank. He leaned upon his long, iron-headed staff, and his eyes, deep-set and luminous, held the steady, unblinking gaze of a man accustomed to read the secrets of the river and the woods.

“You have received a word,” Makewe said, his voice low and measured, carrying the halting, formal cadence of a man speaking a language not his own, but speaking it with a weight that gave every syllable the gravity of an oath. “The smoke-canoe has spoken. It is a bad word.”

Hollis folded the letter slowly, his fingers feeling the cheap, foreign paper. “It is a word from my chief, Makewe. He says the trade is poor. He says I must go down to the great water.”

Makewe did not move. The heat shimmered around them, and the drone of insects sounded like a persistent, maddening vibration in the skull. The chief’s silence was more eloquent than any protest. It was the silence of a man who sees a long-held alliance crumbling, who perceives the approach of a darkness far more malignant than any night. For three years Hollis’s presence, however insignificant it might have seemed in the ledgers of the coast, had been a bulwark. It was not the physical protection of the station’s ancient, rusty muskets, but the moral fact of the white man’s presence, which had kept at bay the marauding, half-starved bands from the eastern territories, those wandering, predatory remnants of broken tribes that drifted through the forest like jackals. To them, the station was a thing of power, a token of the strange, invincible beings who came from beyond the great water. With Hollis gone, the spell would be broken.

“The trade is poor,” Makewe repeated, a faint, bitter irony touching the corners of his mouth. “The elephant has gone far. But the men of the forest have not gone far. They wait. They are like the grass that springs up again after the fire. When the white man’s house is empty, they will come. They do not fear the old guns. They fear the word of the white man.”

Hollis felt a sudden, acute pang of shame. The letter’s logic was undeniable, perhaps. The company existed for profit; Makewe’s safety was an incidental byproduct of commerce, never its aim. Yet, standing here, with the profound, ancient silence of the forest pressing upon him, the commercial logic seemed a flimsy, contemptible thing. He thought of the chief’s people, the women working the plantations, the children playing by the river’s edge, the very dust of their lives inextricably mingled with the dust of this place. He thought of the marauders, their desperate, feral eyes, their ruthless axes. The abandonment would not be a mere withdrawal; it would be a betrayal, a breaking of a bond that, though unspoken and unacknowledged by any treaty, was as real as the earth beneath their feet.

“You know the ways of my people, Makewe,” Hollis said, his voice rough, the words coming haltingly. “I am a servant. I do not own myself. I must go where I am sent. The word comes from very far, from men who do not know this place, who have never seen the river, who sit in rooms where the sun does not enter.”

Makewe’s eyes narrowed, and he leaned forward upon his staff. “They sit in rooms where the sun does not enter, and they send a word that is a death word. Is this just? Is this the wisdom of your people? You have been here. You have eaten our food. You have spoken truth to us. You have said, ‘I am here.’ And now you say, ‘I go.’ The men of the forest will say, ‘The white man is a liar. His word is water. It runs away.’ And they will come with their axes.”

Hollis looked away, his gaze falling upon the dilapidated station house, its whitewashed walls stained with long streaks of rust from the corrugated iron roof. It was an ugly, flimsy structure, a monument to commercial ambition and impermanence. Yet, it had become his home, the only home he had. He had no family waiting for him at the coast, no prospects beyond another assignment, another remote station, another round of the same dreary, soul-numbing cycle. The company had given him nothing but a livelihood; Lukundu had given him a purpose, a fragile, perhaps illusory, but deeply felt sense of belonging. To leave was to obey; to stay was to rebel, to sever himself from the only world he had ever known, to become a permanent exile.

“I do not say I go willingly, Makewe,” Hollis murmured. “The word is heavy. But the word is the word. My chief—”

“Your chief is far,” Makewe interrupted, his voice suddenly sharp, carrying the authority of a ruler speaking to a subject. “He is far, and he does not see. You are here. You see. The men of the forest are not far. They are in the deep bush. They watch. They wait for the smoke to go away. I have spoken.”

He turned abruptly and walked back toward the village, his tall figure moving with a slow, dignified grace that seemed to defy the oppressive heat. Hollis watched him go, a profound, aching uncertainty tearing at his soul. The light was fading fast now, and the forest, which had seemed a wall of green, dissolved into a mass of black, shifting shadows. The silence was absolute, broken only by the harsh, sudden cry of a water-bird, a sound that seemed to mock the pretensions of human endeavor.

He walked back to the station house and sat at his table, the letter from the coast lying before him like an indictment. He lit a lamp, and the small, yellow flame cast a flickering, uncertain glow upon the whitewashed walls. He thought of the company, its vast, impersonal machinery, its inexorable logic of profit and loss. He thought of his own life, a small, insignificant cog in that machinery. He thought of Makewe’s people, their trust, their vulnerability. The two worlds, so utterly incompatible, clashed within him, and he felt the full, crushing weight of the moral solitude that is the white man’s burden in the dark places of the earth. The night closed in, thick, hot, and breathless, and Hollis sat motionless, staring at the lamp’s feeble glow, knowing that whatever he decided, a part of him would die in this place, and that the river, indifferent and eternal, would continue to flow.
