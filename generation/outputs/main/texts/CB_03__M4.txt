The study of Madame Vervloet was a place of cold order, and I stood within it as a stone stands in a stream—fixed, while the current of accusation rushed past me. The rain lashed the tall windows with a fury that found an echo in my breast; the wind, coming off the canal, moaned in the chimney like a soul in torment. Before me, behind the great desk of polished oak, sat the headmistress herself, her small, neat hands folded upon a ledger, her eyes—grey and dispassionate as a winter sky—fixed upon my face. And beside her, trembling with a fine, manufactured sensibility, stood Mademoiselle Dufour, her charge, the girl who had brought me to this pass.

“You persist in your denial, Miss Grey?” Madame Vervloet’s English was precise, unaccented, and utterly without warmth. “Mademoiselle Dufour is most distressed. She tells me she saw you, with her own eyes, take the letter from her desk and read it.”

My tongue felt thick, a clumsy instrument in a mouth that could not shape the slippery syllables of French with any grace. “Madame,” I began, and the word sounded hollow in my own ears, “I did not. I was in the schoolroom the whole of that afternoon. I never entered the dormitory.”

“But you were seen,” she insisted, her voice a flat statement of fact. “Mademoiselle Dufour is a truthful girl. Why should she invent such a story?”

Why, indeed? I looked at the girl. Her lashes were downcast, her lip quivered, and she clutched a handkerchief as though it were the sole barrier between her and a sea of grief. She was pretty, in a pale, insipid way, and she knew the power of her own fragility. I had marked her before for a creature of small malice and large vanity, but I had not thought her capable of this. This was a calculated ruin.

“I cannot say, Madame,” I replied, and my voice, to my own shame, trembled. “I only know that I am innocent. I have never read a private letter in my life. It is a question of honour.”

“Honour,” Madame Vervloet repeated, and the word dropped from her lips like a stone into still water. “Honour is a fine thing, Miss Grey, but it is a fragile one. It is easily tarnished in a foreign land, where one’s conduct is not understood. You are alone here. You have no friends, no family to speak for you. Your situation is—precarious.”

The blood surged to my cheeks. It was a threat, delicately veiled, and it stung me to the quick. She was right. I was alone. I was a governess, a dependent, a creature of no consequence, far from the grey skies of my native Yorkshire, adrift in this city of slender spires and silent canals. The other mistresses, the Mademoiselles and Mesdemoiselles, held themselves aloof; they saw me as a strange, intense Englishwoman, too plain in my dress, too direct in my manner, too poor in my command of their language to be worth their notice. My only allies were the books in my little chamber, and the God who saw my heart.

“My situation may be precarious, Madame,” I said, and I felt a fierce, hot spark kindle within me, “but it does not make me a thief. It does not make me a liar. I would rather beg my bread in the streets of this city than purchase my post with a falsehood. I did not read the letter.”

My voice had risen; I could not help it. The restraint I had so painfully cultivated since my arrival was crumbling. I saw a flicker of something—surprise? irritation?—in the headmistress’s cool eye. But it was Mademoiselle Dufour who spoke, her voice a soft, wounded pipe.

“She is so strange, Madame. She is always watching us. She has such a way of looking at one, as if she could see into one’s very soul. It frightens me. I am sure she meant to do me a mischief. The letter was from my cousin in Ghent. It was—it was of a private nature.”

The girl’s eyes, for a fleeting instant, met mine, and in their pale blue depths I saw not fear, but a cold and triumphant glee. It was a look that said, *I have you, and you know it.* The injustice of it was a physical pain, a constriction in my throat. I thought of the letter itself, which I had indeed seen, not in the dormitory, but where it had fallen from her own careless hand in the garden two days prior. I had picked it up, seen the seal was broken, and had returned it to her without a word. I had not read a line. My soul revolted at the very idea.

“Madame,” I said, turning my back on the girl as on a viper, “I will tell you what I know. Two days ago, in the garden, Mademoiselle Dufour dropped a letter. I retrieved it and returned it to her. It was already open. I did not read it. If I had, I would have known its contents. Ask her—ask her if I spoke a single word of what it contained. Ask her if I have ever, by look or gesture, hinted at a knowledge of her private affairs.”

Madame Vervloet’s gaze shifted to her pupil. The girl’s composure wavered. She had not expected this. She had not expected the mouse to turn and show its teeth.

“Is this true, Mademoiselle?” the headmistress asked, her voice dangerously quiet.

“I—I do not recall,” the girl stammered, her eyes darting. “She is very sly. She may have read it then. She is always prying. She has no position, no money—what else has she to do but meddle in the affairs of her betters?”

The words were a lash. *Her betters.* The old, familiar scorn, the ancient, crushing reproach of dependence, spoken in a foreign tongue but understood perfectly by my soul. It was the same voice that had echoed in my childhood, the voice of the Reeds, the voice of all who equate poverty with baseness. A great wave of feeling rose in me, a tide of righteous anger that I could barely contain. My hands clenched at my sides. I felt I could have seized the girl and shaken the truth from her, as one shakes fruit from a tree.

But I was not a child in a red-room any longer. I was a woman, and my weapon was not my fists, but my will. I forced my breathing to slow. I fixed my eyes not on the girl, but on the headmistress.

“Madame,” I said, and my voice was low, but it did not falter, “I see how it is. I am a stranger, and she is your charge. My word is worth nothing against her tears. You have already judged me. You wish to be rid of me, but you wish to do it with a clear conscience. You wish me to confess, so that my dismissal will be an act of justice, not of convenience.”

I saw the truth of my words strike home. A faint flush stained Madame Vervloet’s sallow cheeks.

“You are impertinent,” she said, but the fire had gone out of her.

“I am honest,” I replied. “And I will not be dishonest to save my post. I will not say I did a thing I did not do. I would sooner you turned me out this instant, into the rain and the dark, than I would purchase my character with a lie. Do what you must. But know this: you are not punishing a guilty woman. You are sacrificing an innocent one to the whims of a spoiled and wicked girl.”

A silence fell, profound and terrible. The rain beat against the glass; the wind sighed in the eaves. Mademoiselle Dufour had gone very white. Madame Vervloet looked at me for a long, long moment, and in her grey eyes I saw a struggle—a flicker of doubt, a cold calculation. She was weighing the cost: the scandal of a dismissed mistress, the displeasure of a wealthy parent, the neatness of a quiet resolution. I knew, in that instant, that I had lost. Truth was a luxury she could not afford.

“You will leave us, Miss Grey,” she said at last, her voice once more cold and even. “You will pack your trunk. I will give you a month’s wages in lieu of notice. You will quit this house by six o’clock tomorrow morning.”

I inclined my head. The battle was over. I felt a strange, hollow calm. I had lost my post, my livelihood, my small foothold in the world, but I had kept my soul. I had not bent. I had not lied.

As I turned to go, my gaze crossed that of Mademoiselle Dufour. Her triumph was complete; her lip curled in a faint, victorious sneer. But I did not flinch. I looked at her as I had once looked at a cruel cousin in a far-off English nursery—with a pity so profound it was almost contempt. For I knew a truth she did not: that a lie is a chain that binds the liar, and that the soul which has bartered its honour for a moment’s victory has sold its birthright for a mess of pottage. I walked from the room with my head held high, though my heart was like lead within me, and the rain outside seemed to weep for the bitter, bitter injustice of the world.
