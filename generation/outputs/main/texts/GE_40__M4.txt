The letter from Lord Ashcombe lay upon the table in the little study at Thornleigh, its paper of a thickness and its seal of a lustre that seemed to assert a species of superiority over the plain furnishings and the humble, though neat, appointments of a country parsonage. The Reverend Ambrose Milverton had read it three times, and at each reading the words appeared to him to take a more definite and, as it were, more fleshly shape, pressing upon his conscience with the weight of a tangible thing. It was an offer of the living of St. Botolph’s, Wrenbury, in the West End of London: a living of which the emoluments were such as would have enabled a man to marry, to furnish a house with the solid comfort becoming a clergyman of the Establishment, to educate his sons at a public school, and to lay by a provision for the age which, however hopefully we may look forward to a better world, we are yet apt to contemplate with an anxious eye in this. The patron was a man of great wealth and of a certain influence in the religious world—a man who, as he himself expressed it, “liked to see a good man in his pulpit, a man who would preach the Gospel in a way to do credit to the parish, and not set people’s backs up by a lot of new-fangled notions.”

Against this glittering prospect, which had come to him with all the authority of a divine leading, stood the letter from the north. It was written in a cramped and laborious hand by the chairman of a committee of the small, scattered, and poverty-stricken parish of Netherby-in-Cleveland, a place lying among the bleak moors and the lead-mines, where the population was chiefly composed of miners and their families, and where the incumbent, if he were to live at all, must live on a stipend which to Lord Ashcombe’s mind would have appeared scarcely sufficient for the maintenance of a curate’s horse. The letter spoke of a “plain, earnest, gospel-preaching ministry” as the one thing needful; it spoke of a people “destitute of the ordinances of religion,” of a church “falling into decay,” and of a “great work to be done for the Lord in a dark place.” It was signed by one Ezra Thwaite, a name suggestive of the sturdy and unpolished honesty of the northern dales, and it was accompanied by a private note from a certain Mr. Holdsworth, a man of some standing in the county, who wrote with the frankness of a friend: “You will find the people rough, but they are in earnest, and they will love you if you love them. The living is poor, but the work is great.”

Ambrose Milverton was a young man of a sanguine and enthusiastic temperament, whose mind had been early imbued with those ideas of social and religious reform which, in the middle years of the present century, were stirring the hearts of many earnest men both within and without the pale of the Church. He had read with avidity the writings of the Christian Socialists; he had dreamed of a purified and renovated Church, which should go down into the dark places of the earth and lift up the fallen and the ignorant; he had, in short, that noble and dangerous gift of an imagination which dwells upon the future rather than the present, and which is apt to see in the actual world rather the materials for a possible paradise than the stubborn and intractable stuff of which human life is ordinarily made. To such a man the offer of a rich London living, with its comfortable sermons and its fashionable congregation, was a temptation of a subtle and insidious kind. It came to him in the guise of a prudent provision for his future usefulness; it whispered to him that a man may do more good in a position of influence than in a position of obscurity; it suggested that the Gospel may be preached with acceptance in a drawing-room as well as in a cottage, and that the rich have souls to be saved as well as the poor. And yet, at the bottom of his heart, he knew that the voice which spoke to him from the north was the voice of his true vocation.

The conflict in his mind was not to be decided by any process of logical reasoning. It was a conflict between two ideals of life, each of which had its own claims upon his conscience, and each of which was supported by a different order of motives. On the one side stood the desire for culture, for refinement, for the society of cultivated and thoughtful people, for the opportunity of developing his own powers and of exercising an influence which should be wide and conspicuous. On the other side stood the desire for self-sacrifice, for the direct and personal ministration to the ignorant and the suffering, for the consciousness of being engaged in a work which, however obscure, was yet the very work which the Master had come to do. It is a conflict which has been fought out in many a young and earnest heart, and it is not always the apparently nobler motive which prevails. For the love of ease and the love of approbation are subtle enemies, and they often disguise themselves in the garb of prudence and of a wise regard for the interests of the Church.

Ambrose walked out into the garden, and paced the little gravel path which bordered the lawn, with his hands clasped behind him and his eyes fixed upon the ground. The evening was calm and sweet; the scent of the mignonette came to him with a mournful distinctness, and the distant murmur of the village children at play seemed to belong to a world from which he was already half severed. He thought of the London church, with its cushioned seats and its painted windows, and of the well-dressed congregation who would listen to him with a critical appreciation of his eloquence; he thought of the Bishop, and of the possibility of preferment, and of the wide field of usefulness which would open before him. Then he thought of the northern moors, of the bleak winds that would howl round the little parsonage, of the rough miners and their rough speech, of the long, lonely winters, of the scanty stipend, and of the obscurity which would be his portion. And yet, as he thought, there came to him a vision of a different kind: he saw himself standing in the midst of a crowd of eager, upturned faces, he heard the simple, fervent words of prayer and praise, he felt the warmth of a love which was not to be measured by the conventionalities of society, and he knew that in that vision lay the true happiness of his life.

It is a curious fact, and one which has often been observed by those who have studied the workings of the human mind, that the decisions which we make in moments of strong emotion are often more rational than those which we make after long and laborious deliberation. The reason is not far to seek. In the moment of strong emotion we see the objects of our choice in their true proportions; we are not misled by the sophistries of self-interest, nor by the illusions of a false prudence. When Ambrose Milverton, after a long and sleepless night, sat down to write his answer to Lord Ashcombe, he did not attempt to justify his decision by any elaborate reasoning. He simply wrote that he felt himself called to a different sphere of labor, and that he could not accept the living of St. Botolph’s. And when he had written it, and had sealed it, and had given it to the servant to post, he felt a sense of relief which was almost a joy. The struggle was over; the decision was made; and he was free.

The next morning he wrote to Ezra Thwaite, accepting the living of Netherby-in-Cleveland. He wrote in a strain of high enthusiasm, speaking of the work which lay before them, and of his hope that he might be found a faithful laborer in the Lord’s vineyard. He did not know, and perhaps it was well for him that he did not know, what that work would really be: how many disappointments he would have to encounter, how many of his fair ideals would be shattered against the hard realities of human nature, how many of his own cherished plans would be thwarted by the obstinacy of prejudice and the sluggishness of custom. He did not know that the people among whom he was to labor, though in earnest, were yet suspicious of strangers, and tenacious of their own ways; that the poverty of the living would be a constant source of anxiety; that the loneliness of the moors would sometimes weigh upon his spirits like a nightmare. But neither did he know the deep and enduring satisfaction which comes from the consciousness of work done faithfully in a obscure corner, nor the love which grows up between a pastor and a people who have learned to trust him. And perhaps, after all, the knowledge of these things is better than the knowledge of the disappointments which lie in wait for every generous enthusiasm. For the heart of man is so constituted that it can bear the knowledge of its own failures better than the knowledge of the vanity of its hopes.
