The deck chairs had been lashed and stowed, and the last of the baggage carried down, so that the deck lay bare and pale as a scrubbed table, and Agnes, leaning at the rail, thought how the sea had changed its colour. At Bombay it had been a blue so solid you might have walked on it, a blue that beat against the hull like a gong struck at noon; and now it was a thin, cold green, wrinkled and uncertain, with the wind coming off it in little shudders, and beyond, in the mist, the coast of England drawn in charcoal—a line, a smudge, a darker smudge where the cliffs were—no more than that.

“You’ll catch your death,” said her mother, appearing at her elbow in the grey travelling cloak, the one with the moth-hole at the collar that no one was to mention. “Come down and have some tea. There’s a bun.”

“In a minute.”

“You’ve been saying in a minute since Ushant,” said her mother, and went, her heels going tap-tap-tap on the boards, a sound like the ticking of a clock that has decided, after long argument, to go on.

One year. Agnes said it to herself the way one says a word over and over until it empties: one year, one year. There had been the house at Cawnpore with the punkah swinging its great slow wing all night, and the dust that got into the ink, into the rice, into the creases of one’s neck; there had been the mango tree and the parrots in it, screaming like a party of English ladies at a wedding; there had been the evening when the Colonel’s wife—dear Mrs. Prendergast, with her face like a kindly, worried horse—had said, fanning herself, “Of course, my dear, we none of us quite know what we mean by India,” and the others had laughed, and the ice had tinkled in the glasses, and the sun had gone down all at once, as if a hand had pulled a blind.

What would she tell them? There would be the drawing-room at Aunt Cecilia’s—the fern in the pot, the photograph of Uncle Hubert on the mantelpiece with his beard arranged upon his chest like something out of a museum, the fire hissing, the tea coming round. “And how was India, dear?” Aunt Cecilia would say, and the whole room would turn its face to her, expectant, the way faces do turn, pale and round, like plates waiting for the joint.

Well. What?

There was the smell of it—that was one thing. Not the smell of spices and sandalwood they expected; no, the smell of wet earth after the rains, thick and sweet and somehow private, like the smell of one’s own hair when one takes out the pins; and the smell of the native quarter, which one did not mention; and the smell of the verandah in the morning, of dew on the cane chairs, of the dog’s coat, of the lemon tree in flower. She could say that. “It smells different,” she might say. And they would wait, and she would feel the words going out of her like air out of a bellows, and Aunt Cecilia would say, “How interesting,” and reach for the muffins.

There had been the night of the storm—the real storm, the one in September, when the sky had turned the colour of a bruise and the rain had come down not in drops but in sheets, in whole rivers of it, and the servants had run about with lanterns, and old Ram Dass, who was ninety and had been a boy at Lucknow, had stood in the doorway with his white beard streaming and said something in Hindustani that she had not understood and had never forgotten. She had stood at the window in her nightdress, and the lightning had showed her the garden, the whole garden, black and white, as if it had been printed on a plate, and then taken it away again. She had wanted—what had she wanted? To be inside it. To be the rain. To be let in to the enormous, careless, terrible life of the thing, which did not know her and did not want to, and which was more alive than anything at home had ever been.

Could she say that? “I stood at the window and wished to be the rain.” No. They would look at her; Aunt Cecilia would say, “You always were a fanciful child”; and someone would ask after the Prendergasts.

And there was the boy. There was always, in the end, the boy, though he had not been a boy exactly—Mr. Anstruther, who had come out to inspect the irrigation works, and who had stayed three weeks, and who had read poetry aloud on the verandah in a voice that went through her like a draught under a door. He had read from a little green book, and once, when their hands met over the page, he had gone on reading as if nothing had happened, and she had felt the whole world tilt, the stars swing round, the mango tree lean over, and then right itself, and the punkah go on swinging, and her mother say, “Do put the lamp out, Agnes, the moths are simply frightful.”

Nothing had happened. That was the thing. Nothing had happened at all, and yet everything had happened, and it was all there, in the green book, which she had brought home in her trunk, and which she would put on the shelf at home between the Shakespeare and the Baedeker, and no one would ever open it, and no one would ever know.

“You’ll tell me everything,” her friend Edith had written. “Every scrap. I shall be green with envy.” Dear Edith, with her hair coming down and her good, blunt, honest face. Edith would want the tigers and the jewels and the heathen. Edith would want to know about the Colonel’s wife and the boy—for she would have heard about the boy; there was always somebody’s cousin who knew. And Agnes would sit in Edith’s little bedroom with the chintz and the china dogs, and she would open her mouth, and there would be—nothing. Not because it was too sacred; not because it was too dreadful; but because it was too much. Because the thing had no edges. Because you could not hand it over, like a bun on a plate; because the moment you put it into words it was a different thing altogether, a flattened, dead, dried thing, a flower pressed in a book that no longer smells of anything.

The ship’s bell struck. The mist was lifting, and there was the coast, really there now, the cliffs standing up out of the grey like a wall, and a lighthouse blinking, and gulls—English gulls, smaller and crosser than the ones at Bombay—wheeling and screaming over the wake. A man came along the deck with a bucket, whistling something from a music-hall; a child in a red coat ran past and was called back; a woman shook out a shawl and folded it, and folded it again, with the patient, final movements of one who is nearly home.

And Agnes, watching, felt the whole year fold up inside her, quietly, like that shawl—the heat, the dust, the rain, the green book, the boy reading, her mother saying put out the lamp—folded and put away, and she would carry it, and it would be hers, and it would be nothing to say. The wind struck cold through her coat, and she thought of tea, and the bun, and the gangway going down, and her father’s voice booming from the quay; and she turned from the rail, and her heels went tap-tap-tap on the boards, and the cliffs came on, and the gulls screamed, and the light from the lighthouse, though it was still day, went round and round, patient, methodical, sweeping the grey water with its pale, indifferent hand.
