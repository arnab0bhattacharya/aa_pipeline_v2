The rain had fallen without ceasing since dawn, and the windows of the Royal Seamen’s Hospital were blinded with it, so that the ward lay in a grey twilight though the clock had not yet struck four. I had come down from London at the request of my friend Sherlock Holmes, who was detained upon another matter, to take a deposition from a dying man; and it was in the performance of that melancholy duty that I first heard the name of Alderman Josiah Crane.

The patient was a harpooner off a Greenland whaler, one Nils Petersen by his papers, though he had shipped under half a dozen names in as many seas. He was a huge, gaunt Dane, burnt to the colour of mahogany, and the hand that lay outside the counterpane was scarred and knotted like the root of an old oak. He had been carried in three days before, raving, from a boarding-house in Landport, and the surgeon had told me plainly that he had not twelve hours to live.

“He will speak to no one,” said the chaplain, a mild, spectacled man named Ellwood, as he conducted me up the stair. “He lies and mutters of a debt, and now and then he clutches at a bit of carved ivory that he will not let out of his hand, no, not for the matron herself. But when I read the prayers over him this morning he caught my sleeve and asked whether there was a parson in England who could be trusted to carry a message.”

“And you have sent for me?”

“I have sent for you as a man of science, Doctor, and one who has seen strange things. For the message, when at last he gave it, was not of the kind that a chaplain cares to carry alone.”

We had come to the bedside. The sailor’s eyes were open, and though the fever was bright in them there was a terrible lucidity also, as though the nearness of death had cleared his sight. He looked at me for a long time.

“You are the doctor from London,” he said at last, in a hoarse, deliberate English. “Then write this down, and write it true, and when I am dead take it to the man it names. He owes me twelve pounds, and he owes it these twenty years, and I will not go into the dark with the account unsettled.”

“I will write it,” said I, taking out my notebook.

“Josiah Crane. Alderman Josiah Crane, of the town of Marchett in the county of Warwick.” He pronounced the name with a slow precision that was more terrible than any oath. “Twelve pounds, and the price of a man’s life besides. He was mate of the *Perseverance* in the year ’seventy-four, and I was before the mast. There was a business in the Bight of Benin that is not written in any log. He knows what he owes me. He has known it these twenty years, and he has grown fat upon it.”

“What was the business?” I asked.

But the lucidity had gone out of him as swiftly as it came. He fell back to his muttering, and his hand closed upon the ivory token at his breast—a little disc, rudely carved with a bird, that might have been the work of a sailor in some idle watch. Towards midnight he died, without another word that any man could construe, and I was left with my notebook and a problem that I was very willing to lay before my friend.

Holmes listened to my account in Baker Street with his fingers at his lips, and when I had finished he asked for the token. I had obtained it from the matron, who was glad to be rid of it, and he turned it over for a long while in the lamplight.

“Carved with a knife, Watson, and not by a clever hand. The bird is a frigate-bird—you see the forked tail—and the bird is the badge of the line of packets that ran to the Bight in the old days. It is a tally, I fancy, such as the mate of a cargo might give for a bale received. But the carving is not what interests me.” He held it close. “There is a name scratched upon the reverse, very faint and very small—‘J.C.’, and beneath it a date, ‘4 June, ’74.’ Our dying man did not part with this token for twenty years because it was his evidence, and he meant to produce it when the reckoning came.”

“Then you believe his story?”

“I believe that he believed it, which is the more important thing. And I believe that if we go down to Marchett we shall find an alderman who has spent twenty years in the fear of a knock at his door. A prosperous man does not deny all knowledge of a dead sailor’s charge with the violence that the chaplain describes, unless the denial costs him something. We will take the early train.”

The town of Marchett lies in a green valley of the Midlands, and the alderman’s house stood upon the rise beyond the church—a solid, respectable residence of red brick, with a carriage-drive and a garden that bespoke the care of a gardener and the purse of a rich man. We were shown into a library that smelled of leather and tobacco, and presently the alderman came in to us: a heavy, florid man of sixty, with a great chain across his waistcoat and a manner that was all geniality until Holmes spoke the dead man’s name.

Then the change was instant and dreadful. The colour went out of his cheeks as though a sluice had been opened, and he put out one hand to the edge of the table.

“I know nothing of any sailor,” he said, and his voice was thick. “I have never been in the Bight of Benin. I was never mate of any *Perseverance*. It is a lie—a foul, blackmailing lie. I am an alderman of this town, sir, and I will have you know—”

“I have not accused you of anything,” said Holmes, mildly. “I have merely brought you a message from a dead man, and a token which he desired you to see. If you do not know the token, you will oblige me by looking at it and saying so.”

He laid the ivory disc upon the table. The alderman looked at it as a man looks at a snake. He did not touch it. His lips moved, but no sound came.

“You will observe, Watson,” said Holmes, in the easy tone of a lecturer, “that the alderman’s right hand has gone to his heart, and that his breath is short. It is not the token that has done this, but the memory of the night of the fourth of June, ’74, when a bale was jettisoned in the Bight and a man was left to drown upon it. The survivor has carried the tally for twenty years, and now he has carried it to the grave. I think, sir, that you had better sit down and tell us the truth, for the truth is a lighter burden than the one you have been carrying.”

The alderman sank into a chair, and for a long moment there was no sound but the ticking of the clock and the rain upon the window. Then he began to speak, in a low, dry voice, and the whole miserable story came out: the fraud, the jettisoned cargo, the mate who had saved himself and left his accomplice to the sea, and the twelve pounds that had been promised and never paid, because to pay it would have been to acknowledge the debt of blood.

“I have not had a quiet night these twenty years,” he said at last. “I have heard his step on the stair in every house I have lived in. And now he is dead, and I am to be hanged for a murder that was half the sea’s doing.”

“You will not be hanged,” said Holmes, rising. “The man died in a hospital bed at Portsmouth, of a fever taken in a boarding-house, and his deposition, which my friend has written and signed, says nothing of murder. He asked only for his twelve pounds. I would advise you, sir, to send it to the Seamen’s Hospital before the week is out, and to make your peace with God, for you have been a great deal nearer the gallows than ever you knew.”

We came away in the rain, and Holmes was silent until the train had cleared the town.

“It is a strange thing, Watson,” said he at last, “that a man will cheat his conscience for twenty years and be conquered at the last by a bit of carved ivory and a dying sailor’s arithmetic. But I have observed that the debt which is never paid is the debt which is never forgotten, and that the ledger of the sea is kept in a harder hand than any alderman’s.”
