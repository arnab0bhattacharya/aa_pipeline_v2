The taproom of the Feathers and Flagon in Camden Town held the chill of a tomb. No fire burned in the grate, for the scuttle was empty, and the wooden partition that separated the public bar from the snug had been wrenched apart, leaving jagged splinters of pine jutting into the gloom. A single tallow candle, secured to the counter by a puddle of its own grease, threw a wavering yellow light across the faces of the fifteen souls gathered there, casting their shadows high up on the smoke-stained ceiling. It was Christmas Eve, and the goose club had met to collect its poultry. Instead, they had found the doors unlocked, the spirits locked away, and Silas Gudgeon, the publican, vanished like a wisp of smoke.

Mrs. Nellie Trent wiped her reddened nose with the edge of her shawl and stared at the empty iron hooks above the bar where the Christmas birds should have been hanging. "Eighteen weeks," she said, her voice trembling with a mixture of grief and rage. "Eighteen weeks I have paid my fourpence. Six shillings, to the penny. And my Tommy promised the children a goose this year. A proper goose, he said, with bread sauce and onions. What am I to tell them now? What am I to tell four little ones who have been dreaming of fat and crackling since September?"

A murmur of bitter assent moved through the room. Jacob Pringle, a gaunt carter with a face like weathered leather, slammed his fist against the counter. "He's bolted, that's plain. The cowardly dog has taken every subscription and legged it to the coast. I've sent my boy to look at the railway yard, but I'll wager my last farthing Gudgeon is halfway to Dover by now with our money in his coat." He leaned forward, his breath clouding in the cold air. "Sixteen shillings from me. My wife has been putting aside the scraps for weeks, saving the dripping, hoarding the flour. For what? For a thief's pocket."

The others muttered their own tallies of loss. The goose club had forty-three members in all, drawn from the streets and alleys around the canal basin: labourers and costermongers, washerwomen and lamp-lighters, all of them poor, all of them counting on the small miracle of a bird on the table to make the season something more than another day of bread and scrape. Most had paid weekly into Gudgeon's hands, trusting the publican's leather-bound ledger as though it were a holy book. Now the ledger was gone too, taken along with the tin lockbox that had sat beneath the counter since October.

Only fifteen had answered the bell that young Alf Pringle had rung through the streets after discovering the theft, running from house to house in the freezing rain with the news. The rest were either at work, or too ill, or simply unwilling to leave what warmth they could muster in their own rooms. Those who had come stood or sat in the ruined taproom, their faces drawn and grey, their hands thrust deep into pockets that held nothing worth counting.

"We could go to the magistrate," said Ephraim Biddle, a small man who mended clocks and spoke always in a tone of cautious deliberation, as though each sentence were a mechanism he was afraid of overwinding. "A theft is a theft, after all. The parish beadle might act, or the police. There is that new force in the borough. They might pursue him."

Jacob Pringle scoffed. "And what good would that do us tomorrow? Even if they caught him, they'd keep the money for evidence, or he'd have spent it already. I want no police in my affairs. I want a goose."

"You want a goose," said Mrs. Trent, her voice cracking. "We all want a goose. But there are no geese to be had for nothing, and not one of us here has a spare shilling to buy one. Gudgeon has seen to that."

The room fell into a silence broken only by the sound of the December wind moaning through a broken pane at the back of the room, where someone had thrown a stone through the glass in frustration. The candle guttered, and for a moment the darkness pressed close, as though it too were listening.

It was then that a voice came from the corner near the door, so quiet that several of them started, having forgotten the old man was there at all. He had been sitting on a low stool throughout the proceedings, his back against the wall, his long legs drawn up and his hands folded across his knees. His name was Tobias Furneal, and he was a lamplighter, one of the last in the borough who still walked his round with a pole and a flame, touching fire to the wicks of the oil lamps along the canal path and the bridge. He was past seventy, thin as a reed, with a face so deeply lined that it seemed to have been folded many times, like an old letter kept too long in a pocket. His eyes, though, were bright, and they moved now from face to face with a steady, unhurried attention.

"I can get you your geese," he said.

The room turned to him. Jacob Pringle stared. "What's that, Furneal?"

"I can get you your geese," the old man repeated, without raising his voice. "Not from a shop, and not from a market. But there are geese to be had, if you know where to look, and if you've the nerve for it."

Ephraim Biddle frowned. "You're not suggesting we steal, Furneal. That's a hanging matter, or a transportee's."

"I am not suggesting you steal," said Tobias Furneal. He unfolded himself from the stool and stood, and though he was a slight man, there was something in his bearing that made the room grow still. "I am suggesting you take what is already yours. Every one of you paid into that club. Every one of you is owed a bird. Gudgeon did not take the money and run before he had made his purchases. He is too cunning for that. He would have known that the smell of cooking geese would lead the parish straight to him if he stayed. No. He bought the birds, stored them somewhere, and then ran when he had sold them on to a wholesaler for ready cash. That is his way. I have watched him for years, and I know his habits."

"How do you know he stored them?" asked Mrs. Trent.

"Because I lit the lamp outside his storehouse on Plumber's Mews every night this fortnight past," said Furneal. "And every night I saw him go in with a sack and come out with a ledger. The place belongs to a chandler named Crewe who owes Gudgeon for drink. It has a yard and a lean-to, and it is not locked, because Crewe is a careless man and Gudgeon has the key."

Jacob Pringle's eyes narrowed. "If Gudgeon sold the birds on, the storehouse is empty."

"He did not sell them on," said Furneal. "Not all of them. He kept a dozen back, because he is a greedy creature and could not bear to part with everything at once. He meant to sell the last dozen to the gentry on the Eve at triple the price. But he ran before he could. Something frightened him. Perhaps a creditor, perhaps the law. The birds are still there, hanging in the lean-to, and Crewe does not know they exist."

The room buzzed. Mrs. Trent clutched her shawl. "But if we take them, even if they are ours by right, it will look like theft. We have no ledger to prove what we paid."

Furneal turned to her. "The ledger is in the lean-to, Mrs. Trent. Gudgeon would not leave it in the bar, where the first searcher would find it. He kept it with the birds, because he was still tallying his profits. I saw him carry it in. If you take the birds and the ledger, you take your proof with you."

Ephraim Biddle began to protest, speaking of the dangers and the law, but Jacob Pringle cut him short. "Show us," the carter said.

Furneal nodded. He reached into the pocket of his greatcoat and produced a key, blackened with age. "Crewe's spare. I found it in the gutter three nights gone, dropped by Gudgeon himself when he stumbled out in the dark. I am a lamplighter. I find many things in the dark."

He moved toward the door, and one by one they followed, pulling their collars up against the sleet that had begun to fall. Mrs. Trent was the last to leave. She paused at the threshold and looked back at the empty taproom, at the guttering candle and the bare hooks, and then she turned her face to the street and followed the old lamplighter's tall, thin figure into the Christmas night, her steps quickening as she walked, as though the promise of a goose were a flame she could already feel warming her hands.
