The house stood in a quiet crescent off the Richmond Road, a red-brick villa with white facings and a small, trim garden behind a hedge of clipped privet. It was there that I was summoned upon a Tuesday afternoon in the October of '94, the note being brief and urgent, and signed by a lady who described herself as the wife of Mr. Josiah Vane, lately returned from the silver mines of Zacatecas.

The servant who admitted me was grey-faced with anxiety, and she conducted me without a word to the morning-room, where Mrs. Vane awaited me. She was a tall woman, handsome in a severe fashion, with dark hair drawn back and a face that had known little sleep of late. Her hands, I observed, were clasped so tightly that the knuckles showed white.

“You are the investigator of whom Dr. Harlow spoke?” she asked. “He said you had a name for reading what is hidden. I have great need of such a faculty, sir, for there is something in this house that is hidden from me, and it is breaking my husband's health and reason.”

“Pray tell me the circumstances from the beginning, madam,” said I, taking the chair to which she motioned me. “Omit nothing, however trifling it may appear.”

She composed herself with an effort. “My husband, Josiah Vane, went out to Mexico eleven years ago, a poor man. He prospered there—prospered greatly, as you may see from this house and all that is in it. He returned in August last, a different man in many ways, silent and given to long brooding, but affectionate to me and to our boy, and seemingly content. Then, three weeks ago, he received a letter from Mexico. He read it at breakfast, and I saw his face change as I had never seen it change before. He said nothing, but folded it and put it in his pocket, and went to his study and locked the door. He remained there all day. Toward evening I heard a heavy fall.”

“You went to him?”

“I went at once. I found him upon the floor beside his desk, his face livid and his limbs rigid. He could not speak. He has not spoken since. The doctor says it is a seizure brought on by some violent shock of the mind, and that his life is not in danger, but that his speech may return or may not. He lies now in the room above, and looks at me with such an agony in his eyes, sir, that I can scarcely bear it. He is trying to tell me something, and cannot.”

“And the letter?”

“It was still upon the desk, together with a paper upon which he had been drawing. I have preserved both.” She rose and took from a drawer a single sheet of foreign notepaper and a half-sheet of foolscap. “I cannot read the Spanish, and the drawing means nothing to me.”

I examined the letter first. It was written in a firm, educated hand, and ran as follows:—

*Estimado señor,—Tengo el sentimiento de comunicarle que el Padre Anselmo murió el día tres de este mes. Antes de morir me encargó que le escribiera a usted, y me dictó estas palabras: “Dile a Vane que la campana de la iglesia de San Miguel de las Peñas guarda todavía lo que él sabe. Que venga o que envíe, porque el tiempo se acaba y el agua sube.”—Su seguro servidor,*

*BENITO SALAS.*

“It is from a man named Salas,” said I, “and it speaks of the death of one Padre Anselmo, who before dying dictated a message to your husband. The message is this: ‘Tell Vane that the bell of the church of San Miguel de las Peñas still guards what he knows. Let him come or send, for time is short and the water rises.’ ”

She stared at me. “The bell of a church? What could my husband have to do with a church bell in Mexico?”

“That,” said I, “is what we must discover. And the drawing?”

It was a rough but not unskilful sketch in pencil of a church of the Spanish colonial pattern—a low, wide façade with two squat towers, and between them a bell-gable. Beneath it were written three words in my client's own hand: *San Miguel de las Peñas.*

“Have you any knowledge of this place?” I asked.

“None whatever. My husband never spoke of it. He spoke of Zacatecas, of the mines, of the men he worked with—but never of any village or any church.”

I sat for a while in thought. “Mrs. Vane,” said I at last, “I must ask you a question which may seem impertinent. Was your husband ever a man of violence?”

She flushed, then paled. “He has told me that in the early days, in the mines, there were things done that he would not speak of. He was not always his own master. But he has been a good husband to me, sir, and a good father.”

“I do not doubt it. But I must go where the facts lead. Have you among his papers any map, any document, any relic of his Mexican years?”

“There is a box in his study,” she said, after a moment's hesitation. “He kept it locked, but since his illness I have forced it open, hoping to find something that might explain. There are letters, and a few small articles. I will bring it.”

She returned presently with a japanned tin box, much battered, and set it before me. Within were bundles of letters in Spanish, a few English newspapers of old date, a silver crucifix, and a small, flat object wrapped in wash-leather. I unwrapped it, and found a bell—a handbell of bronze, green with age, and bearing upon its lip a rude engraving of a cross.

“This is no common bell,” said I. “It is a sanctuary bell, such as is hung in the missions of the old padres. And here is a paper that was beneath it.”

It was a single leaf torn from a notebook, and upon it, in Vane's hand, were these words:—

*“I, J. V., being of sound mind, do set down that the treasure of San Miguel is not in the altar but in the bell, and that I repented before God and before Padre Anselmo, and that I gave my word to restore it. If I am dead, let him who finds this do what I swore to do.”*

I laid the paper down. “Your husband, madam,” said I, “was one of those who, in the wilder days of the Mexican mines, rifled a church. The treasure—whether of gold or of silver or of jewels, I cannot say—was hidden by him and his companions in the bell of San Miguel de las Peñas. The good padre learned of it, and your husband repented and swore to restore it. He came home, and in the ease of his prosperity he put the matter from his mind. Then came this letter, telling him that the padre was dead, and that the water was rising—that is, that the church was being flooded, or the ground about it sinking, and that the bell and its secret would be lost forever. The shock of it struck him down.”

“Then there is no dishonour in it?” she cried. “He repented—he meant to restore it?”

“So it would appear. But we must act, and act quickly. The letter is three weeks old. This Salas is plainly a friend. I will send him a telegram at once, in your husband's name, bidding him save the bell at any cost, and I will myself see to the matter of the treasure's restoration to the Church. Meanwhile, madam, you will show your husband this paper and this bell, and tell him that what he swore to do shall be done. It may be that the relief will loosen the bonds of his affliction.”

And so it proved. Three days later I received a note from Mrs. Vane, written in a shaking hand, to say that her husband had spoken his first words—*“The bell, the bell”—*and that he was mending. And in the spring of the following year I had the satisfaction of reading in a Mexican journal that the treasure of San Miguel de las Peñas, being a great silver pyx and a set of altar vessels, had been restored to the Bishop of Zacatecas by a Señor Salas, at the instance of an English gentleman whose name was not given.
