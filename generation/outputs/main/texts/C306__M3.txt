The rain had not ceased for three days. It drummed against the tall windows of the Salle de Lecture with a monotonous fury that seemed to seep into the very plaster of the pensionnat, turning the grey walls greyer, chilling the marrow of every girl who sat shawled at her desk. Mademoiselle Élise Vane, instructor of French grammar and English composition to the daughters of Flemish merchants and minor aristocracy, stood at the front of the room, a volume of Racine resting unopened in her hands. She watched her pupils with the detached vigilance of a hawk perched upon a cathedral spire, her gaze moving from one bent head to the next. 

It was during this silent survey that she noticed Cécile Vermeulen. The girl sat in the third row, her fair head bent not over her translation, but toward the shadowed cavity of her desk. Her shoulders were hunched in a posture of acute, breathless concentration. As Élise watched, Cécile’s hand darted inside her pinafore, secreting something small and white into the bosom of her school frock before snapping back to the desktop. The motion was quick, practiced, and utterly damning.

Élise felt a cold trickle of apprehension slide down her own spine. She knew the architecture of that gesture. She knew the weight of folded paper hidden against a beating heart. 

"Cécile Vermeulen," Élise said, her voice cutting cleanly through the damp air and the scratching of quills. "Approchez."

The silence that followed was sudden and profound. Twenty-three pairs of eyes lifted. Cécile stood slowly, her face drained of its youthful bloom, leaving it the colour of unrendered wax. She walked down the aisle between the desks, her sensible leather boots sounding heavy and final against the wooden floorboards. When she stood before the teacher’s lectern, she kept her chin tucked, her hands clasped tightly at her waist.

"Open your pinafore pocket, Cécile," Élise commanded, keeping her tone strictly professional, though a tremor threatened the edges of her words.

"Mademoiselle, I—"

"Empty your pocket onto the desk."

With trembling fingers, the girl drew out a folded piece of paper, sealed with a crude dab of red wax. It had not come through the official post; the pensionnat’s directress, Madame Roos, rigorously vetted all incoming and outgoing correspondence. This was a smuggled letter. Élise picked it up. The seal was unbroken, but she did not need to read the contents to know the nature of the missive. The faint, earthy scent of damp wool and pipe tobacco clung to the parchment. It smelled of the canal banks, of the world outside the iron gates, of young men.

"Return to your seat," Élise said softly.

Cécile obeyed, leaving the letter on the polished oak. Élise did not look at it. She stared instead at the rain lashing the glass, feeling the weight of the institution pressing down upon her. The rules of Madame Roos’s establishment were carved into the very foundation stones: no unauthorized correspondence, no clandestine attachments. Any breach was to be reported immediately to the directress. Punishment was invariably severe—confinement to the attic dormitory, public censure before the assembled pupils, and, ultimately, expulsion. For a girl of seventeen to be sent home in disgrace could ruin her prospects entirely, rendering her unmarriageable, a stain upon her family’s mercantile honour. And the teacher who failed to report such an infraction? She, too, would be cast out, her reputation destroyed, left to fend for herself in a city that had little patience for women without references.

Élise knew the stakes intimately. She was twenty-eight, an Englishwoman stranded in Bruges by the caprice of a father who had died bankrupt, leaving her to earn her bread in a foreign tongue. She had carved out a small, austere life for herself here. She was respected, if not liked. She was secure. To protect Cécile was to gamble that security.

Yet, as she stood before the class, the present seemed to thin, growing translucent. The damp Flemish classroom dissolved, replaced by the sweeping, windswept moors of her own youth, where a girl named Élise had once stood freezing in a doorway, clutching a letter from a boy named Thomas. 

Thomas had been a clerk in her father’s failing counting house. He had possessed dark eyes and a quiet, fierce intellect that saw past her shabby gowns and her father’s mounting debts. They had exchanged letters for a year, pouring their hopes and despairs onto paper. When her father had discovered the correspondence, he had not merely confiscated the letters; he had beaten Thomas and dismissed him, then shipped Élise off to a severe aunt in the north. She had never seen Thomas again. She had never forgotten the agony of that severance, the crushing loneliness of being seventeen and entirely without ally or advocate. Her father had acted according to the dictates of propriety and survival, but the girl Élise had been left to wither in the cold.

Élise looked down at the sealed letter on her desk. She thought of Madame Roos, a woman built of iron and dogma, who viewed her pupils not as children navigating a treacherous world, but as vessels of family capital that must be kept pristine until they could be bartered in marriage. To hand this letter to the directress was to feed Cécile to the gears of the machine, to crush a small, desperate reaching for human connection.

The bell chimed, a sharp brass sound that announced the end of the morning session. The girls began to gather their books, the low murmur of their chatter filling the room. 

"Silence," Élise said. "You are dismissed to the refectory. Cécile, you will remain."

The room emptied quickly, leaving the two of them alone with the sound of the rain. Cécile remained at her desk, rigid with terror. Élise walked slowly down the aisle, the letter held lightly between her fingers. She stopped beside the girl’s desk.

"Who is he?" Élise asked, her voice low.

Cécile looked up, her blue eyes swimming with unshed tears. "His name is Jan. He is a journeyman at the lace works on the Sint-Jansstraat. Please, Mademoiselle. Do not give it to Madame Roos. She will send me away."

Élise looked at the girl’s trembling hands. She saw the desperation, the foolish, beautiful audacity of youth. She knew that Jan was likely a poor match, that the romance was probably doomed by the rigid class structures of Bruges. But she also knew that to be crushed now, to be humiliated and exiled, would break something in Cécile that might never mend.

Élise held the letter over the flame of the brass oil lamp that sat on Cécile’s desk. The paper caught quickly, curling and browning, the red wax hissing as it melted. The flames consumed the words, turning the illicit declaration to ash. Cécile let out a strangled gasp, watching her secret burn.

"Tell Jan," Élise said, dropping the last glowing corner into a brass spittoon, "that if he truly cares for your future, he will not write to you again. If he is patient, if he is successful, he may present himself to your father in two years' time. Clandestine letters will only doom you both."

Cécile stared at the ashes, then up at her teacher. "You are not going to report me?"

"I am not," Élise said, though the words felt like stepping off a cliff. "You will return to your translation of Racine, and you will not fail me. If I discover another letter, I will not burn it. I will hand it directly to Madame Roos. Do you understand?"

"Oui, Mademoiselle. Yes. I understand." Cécile’s voice was breathless with relief, her eyes shining with a gratitude that Élise knew was dangerous.

"Go to the refectory. You will be late."

The girl fled the room, leaving Élise alone with the smell of burning paper and the ceaseless rain. Élise walked slowly back to the lectern. She picked up the Racine, her hands entirely steady. She had made her choice. She had stepped out of the safe, shadowed path of obedience and thrown her lot in with the desperate, the lonely, and the young. She did not know if she had saved Cécile or merely delayed her ruin, nor did she know if she had just orchestrated her own downfall. But as she stood in the quiet room, the cold that had lived in her bones since she was seventeen seemed, for the first time in a decade, to thaw.
