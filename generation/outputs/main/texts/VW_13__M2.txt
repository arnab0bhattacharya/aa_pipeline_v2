The light in Norfolk did not fall so much as it permeated, a pale, milky substance that seemed to dissolve the hard edges of things, blurring the boundary between the shingle and the sky until one could no longer say where the earth ended and the infinite began. It was the third day, or perhaps the fourth; time had lost its rigid architecture here, crumbling into a series of identical, sun-drenched moments that stacked upon one another like wet sand.

Elspeth lay on the chaise longue, the canvas ticking against her shoulders, watching the grey-green water lap against the shore. It was a monotonous sound, a rhythmic exhalation of the sea, yet it held within it a strange, vibrating silence. Across the garden, behind the screen of rustling hollyhocks, the children were playing. She could hear the distant, sharp crack of a bat striking a ball, followed by a burst of laughter, high-pitched and unselfconscious. Then a shout, a stumble, and the laughter again. They were her niece and nephew, Arthur and Molly, small figures moving with the frantic, purposeless energy of youth, oblivious to the weight of the afternoon.

Elspeth’s eyes drifted to the house, where her sister, Clara, was visible through the window of the kitchen. Clara was on her knees, scrubbing a stain from the floor, her back hunched, her arms moving in a circular, repetitive motion. The image was domestic, mundane, yet it struck Elspeth with a peculiar force. It was not pity she felt, nor even a sharp pang of envy for the domesticity itself, but rather a sense of detachment, a coolness that settled in her chest like a stone dropped into still water. She looked at Clara’s bent form and thought: *She is doing what she must. I am doing what I can. And between these two states, there is no bridge.*

She did not envy Clara the children. To have them was to be tethered, to be pulled in a dozen directions, to be fragmented into a hundred small, attentive pieces. Elspeth had her own life, or what passed for one, a life of reading, of walking, of the long, uninterrupted stretches of thought that moved like slow currents through her mind. She had her solitude, her freedom to be still, to watch the light change on the water without needing to wipe a nose or chase a runaway ball. She felt a strange, detached admiration for her sister’s endurance, but no desire to trade places. The idea of being bound by such obligations felt not like a loss of freedom, but like a different kind of existence, one she had chosen to avoid. She was content in her own emptiness, her own spaciousness.

But Clara, kneeling on the cold tiles, felt the stain beneath her brush as a presence. It was a smear of ink, perhaps, or a mark of mud from the garden, but it seemed to grow, to spread, defying her efforts. She scrubbed harder, her breath coming in short gasps, and as she looked up, her gaze caught on the chaise longue where Elspeth lay.

Elspeth was still. She was not sleeping, but she was in that state of suspended animation that Clara had longed for all her life. Her face was pale, serene, unreadable. She looked like a figure in a painting, preserved in amber, untouched by the chaos of the day. Clara’s hand stopped moving. The brush lay in her palm, bristles splayed. She looked at her sister and felt a surge of something hot and sharp, a jealousy so acute it bordered on pain.

It was not that Clara wanted Elspeth’s books or her walks. It was that she wanted her stillness. Clara’s life was a constant motion, a series of demands, interruptions, and small deaths of the self. She had to be there for the children, for the house, for the husband who was away on business, for the neighbors who came with their questions and their gifts. She had no time to be still, no time to let the mind wander into the vast, uncharted territories of its own thoughts. Elspeth had that time. Elspeth had the luxury of being nothing, of being no one in particular, of existing in a state of pure, unencumbered perception.

Clara looked at the stain on the floor. It was still there. She looked at Elspeth. Elspeth was watching the sea. The sea did not care if it was scrubbed clean or left to rot. The sea simply was. Clara felt a sudden, violent desire to drop the brush, to crawl out of the kitchen, to cross the garden, and to lie down beside her sister. She wanted to feel the canvas on her shoulders, to hear the monotonous lap of the water, to be free of the stain, free of the children, free of the self that was always asking, always needing, always failing.

But she did not move. She remained on her knees, the brush in her hand, the stain before her. The sun moved across the floor, a long, golden rectangle that crept towards her, warming the tiles, warming her skin. She felt the heat on her face, a physical sensation that contrasted with the coldness of the water in the bucket. She looked at her sister again. Elspeth had turned her head slightly, following the flight of a gull that skimed the surface of the water.

There was a moment, a fraction of a second, where their eyes might have met, had Clara looked up. But she did not. She looked down at the floor, at the small, dark mark that defied her. She thought of the week to come, of the meals to be prepared, of the clothes to be washed, of the endless, circular motion of her days. She thought of Elspeth, lying there, in her silence, in her light.

Elspeth, meanwhile, felt a strange sensation, a tremor in the air, as if the atmosphere had shifted. She did not know why, but she felt a sudden sense of loss, a void that was not her own. She looked at the house, at the window where her sister was working. She saw the movement, the hunching of the back, the repetitive motion. And for the first time, she felt a flicker of unease. It was not pity, for pity implied a superiority she did not possess. It was something else, a recognition of a different kind of tragedy. Clara was trapped in the moment, bound to the immediate, the tangible, the real. Elspeth was free, but her freedom was a kind of exile. She was outside the circle of life, watching it from the shore.

She closed her eyes. The sound of the sea continued, a low, rhythmic murmur. She thought of the word *everything*. It was a heavy word, too large to contain. She had envied nothing, she told herself, and yet she felt a hollow space within her, a silence that was not peaceful, but empty. She opened her eyes and looked at the sky. The clouds were thinning, revealing patches of blue, a bright, sharp blue that seemed to cut into the pale grey.

Clara, in the kitchen, felt the sun on her face and a tear, unbidden, slide down her cheek. She wiped it away with the back of her hand, leaving a streak of dust. She picked up the brush and began to scrub again. The stain was fading, but it was still there. It would always be there.

Elspeth watched the gull circle overhead, a white speck against the blue. She felt a sudden, sharp pang, a memory of a time when she had been young, when she had thought that freedom was the only thing that mattered. Now, lying in the sun, she felt the weight of her own freedom, the burden of being unattached, of having no anchor, no tether. She looked at her sister, still on her knees, still scrubbing. And for the first time, she realized that she did not envy Clara nothing. She envied her the stain, the struggle, the effort. She envied her the fact that she was needed, that she was part of a pattern, a design, a whole.

The afternoon stretched out before them, long and golden, indifferent to their thoughts. The children’s laughter faded as they moved away from the garden, their voices becoming indistinct, swallowed by the distance. The sea continued to lap at the shore, a constant, unchanging rhythm. Elspeth lay still, her eyes closed, feeling the heat on her skin, the sound of the water in her ears. Clara scrubbed the floor, her arms moving in a circle, a circle that seemed to go on forever, a circle that contained everything and nothing, a circle that was both a prison and a home.

And in the space between them, in the silence of the garden, in the light of the afternoon, the two sisters existed, separate, distinct, yet bound by the invisible threads of their shared blood, their shared history, their shared, unspoken understanding of the different ways in which life could be lived, and the different ways in which it could be lost.
