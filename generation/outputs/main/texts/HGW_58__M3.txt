It was the second Tuesday of August, and the air in the Hampshire lanes hung thick with the scent of warmed dust and wild honeysuckle. I was proceeding on my bicycle towards Petersfield, thoroughly enjoying the second day of my annual fortnight’s holiday from the counting-house. I am a mere clerk, a machine for addition and subtraction during the other fifty weeks of the year, but on this bright morning I felt very much the independent gentleman, propelling myself through the landscape with a satisfying hiss of pneumatic tyres.

It was near a crossroads, where a particularly fine elm cast a pool of shadow across the white road, that I first observed the young woman. She was sitting on a stile, looking decidedly hot, dusty, and out of patience with the world. She wore a dark skirt, quite unsuited to the weather, and a straw hat that had suffered some violence to its brim. Beside her on the grass rested a small Gladstone bag. As I slowed my pace, preparatory to passing, she looked up with an expression of such forlorn irritation that I felt compelled to stop.

"Good morning," I said, resting my foot on the pedal. "You seem to be somewhat at a loss."

She regarded me with the frank suspicion that women adopt towards strangers in lonely places, but her evident distress quickly overcame her caution. "I am," she admitted. "I am trying to reach Thruxton Grange, but I have missed the way. The milestones are utterly useless, and the local people seem entirely ignorant of geography."

"Why, Thruxton is a good eight miles from here," I told her. "You have walked from the station, I suppose?"

"From Blandford station, yes. I walked because I had not the money for a cab, and I was foolish enough to think the lanes would be straightforward." She paused, weighing some private decision, and then added, "I have run away from my guardian. I am going to my aunt's house."

This was a statement of a sort that immediately piqued my interest. A runaway guardian-ward situation has a distinctly romantic flavour, quite foreign to the dull routine of a counting-house. "In that case," I said, "you must allow me to escort you. I am heading in the general direction of Petersfield, and I can easily push my machine along the lanes as far as Thruxton. It is my holiday, and I have nothing but time."

She hesitated for a moment, looking me up and down with a shrewd, practical eye. I dare say my flannel suit and sensible straw boater presented a harmless, middle-class aspect. "You are very kind," she said at last. "I am Miss Elton. My guardian, a Mr. Voss, is a man of the most insufferable tyranny. I could not bear his restrictions a moment longer."

"My name is Pringle," I replied. "John Pringle, of the Universal Assurance Company. If you will take the Gladstone bag, I will wheel the bicycle. We shall make excellent progress."

Thus began our curious partnership. We proceeded along the winding, sunken lanes, talking with the easy familiarity of strangers thrown together by circumstance. Miss Elton was a young woman of decided opinions. She spoke of Mr. Voss with a bitterness that was quite understandable. "He treats me as a piece of furniture," she declared, "a thing to be moved and managed according to his convenience. He would have me marry a man twice my age, simply because the man has a thriving tannery. A tannery! Imagine it, Mr. Pringle!"

"It sounds exceedingly unpleasant," I agreed. "I have no doubt the smell alone would be a sufficient objection."

She laughed at that, a bright, ordinary laugh that made her seem much less like a heroine in distress and more like a sensible person. We discussed the state of the roads, the recent exhibition at the Academy, and the relative merits of the new safety bicycles versus the old ordinaries. She was surprisingly well-informed on mechanical matters, a trait she attributed to a deceased uncle who had been an engineer.

We had perhaps covered three miles, and were descending into a shaded hollow where a brook crossed the road by means of a brick culvert, when the sound of rapidly trotting hooves reached our ears. I stepped to the side of the road, pulling my bicycle into the hedge, and Miss Elton moved to stand beside me. A smart dog-cart, drawn by a fine bay mare, came swinging round the bend. The driver, a stout, red-faced gentleman with side-whiskers, pulled up sharply upon seeing us.

"Ha!" he cried, glaring down from his elevated seat with an expression of mingled fury and triumph. "So I have found you at last, you undutiful girl!"

Miss Elton stiffened, and her face went quite pale. "Mr. Voss," she said, in a tone of considerable coldness.

"And with a man, too!" he continued, his eyes swiveling towards me with a look of undisguised suspicion. "A common fellow on a bicycle! This is a pretty state of affairs, upon my word!"

The injustice of the remark stung me. I am a clerk, it is true, but I am not common in the sense he implied. I stepped forward, adjusting my spectacles. "I must protest, sir," I said, with as much dignity as I could muster. "I encountered this young lady in some distress on the road, and I am merely escorting her to her aunt's residence at Thruxton."

"Escort her!" he snorted. "A likely story! I suppose you are one of those artful scoundrels who prey upon foolish girls. I know your sort. I have half a mind to horsewhip you."

"Pray do nothing of the kind," I said, feeling a trifle alarmed but determined not to show it. "I am a respectable person, employed by the Universal Assurance Company. I am on my annual holiday. Your ward was lost, and I offered my assistance. That is the entire extent of the matter."

"She is no ward of mine in the sense you imply," retorted Mr. Voss, growing redder. "She is my niece by marriage, and she is under my lawful care until she is one-and-twenty. She has absconded with money and jewels, and I have been driving over half the county in search of her."

Miss Elton spoke up, her voice trembling with indignation. "I took only what was mine, Mr. Voss. The money was left to me by my father, and you know it. You have withheld it from me for two years."

"Details! Legal details!" he barked. "The matter is entirely in my hands. And as for you, sir," he turned to me again, "I demand that you explain your presence here more fully."

I reflected for a moment that there was very little I could say to satisfy a man of his temperament. He was clearly possessed by that peculiar rage which afflicts those who feel their authority has been slighted. "I have explained it," I said. "If you will take the young lady up in your cart, I will continue on my way. We are within five miles of Thruxton."

"Do you think I am a fool?" he demanded. "I believe you have arranged this entire escapade. You have been corresponding with her, I make no doubt. I shall take your name and address, and I shall lay the matter before a magistrate."

This was a prospect I found extremely disagreeable. To be dragged into a family dispute, to be obliged to explain matters to a magistrate, to perhaps be obliged to take time off from the counting-house—it was a horrible contingency. "My name is John Pringle," I said, with forced calm. "I reside at 14, Alma Terrace, Camberwell. You will find my statement verified if you write to the offices of the Universal Assurance Company in London."

He produced a notebook and wrote down the particulars with a stubby pencil, his lips compressed in a manner that suggested he was thoroughly enjoying the drama of the situation. "I shall certainly write," he said. "And I shall certainly consult my solicitor. In the meantime, you may go about your business, and I will thank you not to interfere with young women on the public highway in future."

I was about to make a sharp reply, but Miss Elton caught my eye. She gave me a look of quiet gratitude, and a little nod, as if to say that she understood and appreciated my efforts. I thought it best to leave the matter there. I mounted my bicycle, touched my hat to her, and proceeded on my way towards Petersfield, leaving Mr. Voss to maneuver his dog-cart round in the narrow lane.

As I rode on, I reflected upon the singular nature of the encounter. It was a curious illustration of the way in which the conventions of society conspire to make simple actions appear suspicious. I had merely offered a direction and a companionable walk to a lost young woman, and had been immediately accused of abduction. I supposed Mr. Voss would write to the Assurance Company, and that my chief would look grave, and that there would be a good deal of tiresome explanation. It was the penalty of assisting a fellow creature in a world where every man is presumed to be acting from the worst motives. I rather hoped, however, that Miss Elton would reach her aunt's house in safety, and that the tannery suitor would be soundly rejected. The smell of a tannery, as I had observed, is in itself a sufficient argument against such a marriage.
