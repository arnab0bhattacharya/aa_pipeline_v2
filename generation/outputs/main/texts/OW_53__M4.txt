The dinner-party at the Marchmonts’ house in Belgravia had been assembled, as such things are, with the care of a general disposing his troops, and with a not dissimilar hope of victory. Lady Marchmont, a woman in whom the instinct for social manoeuvre had been refined by long practice into something like genius, had placed the celebrated Mr. Lucian Quill upon her right, and had arranged the remainder of her guests in a descending scale of wit and consequence, so that the table resembled nothing so much as a well-ordered bouquet, in which the rarest orchid was expected to lend its perfume to the humbler blooms about it. Mr. Quill was a talker of renown. He had said brilliant things at breakfast, and people repeated them at dinner; he had murmured epigrams into the ears of duchesses, and the duchesses had gone home and pretended they had thought of them themselves. To have Mr. Quill at one’s table was to be assured of an evening that would be discussed for a fortnight, and envied for a month.

He arrived precisely at the hour appointed, which in itself was a small triumph, for the truly fashionable are never punctual, and the truly clever are never on time. He was a slender, elegant person, with hair the colour of pale sherry and eyes that had the fatigued, luminous look of one who has seen too many drawing-rooms and found them all the same. He bowed over Lady Marchmont’s hand with the grace of a man conferring a favour, and permitted himself to be conducted to his place beside her with the air of a monarch taking his throne.

The soup was served. Lady Marchmont turned to her guest of honour with the smile of one who has prepared a stage and now awaits the play.

“I have been so looking forward to your thoughts upon the modern novel, Mr. Quill,” she said. “I hear you have been quite shocking the reviewers.”

Mr. Quill regarded his spoon for a moment with an expression of profound interest.

“Yes,” he said, at length. “The soup is very good.”

Lady Marchmont waited. The soup was indeed very good, but it was not, she felt, the stuff of which memorable evenings are made. She essayed again.

“And do you not find the season excessively dull? Everyone I meet is complaining of it.”

“The season,” said Mr. Quill, “is a period during which one is obliged to be seen in places where one does not wish to be seen, by people whom one does not wish to see, in clothes which one has been assured are fashionable, and which one suspects are merely expensive. It is, in short, a prolonged and elaborate form of penance, from which the only absolution is the twelfth of August.”

Lady Marchmont breathed again. Here, at last, was the authentic note. She glanced about the table, prepared to see the ripple of amusement spread from guest to guest. But Mr. Quill had already returned to his soup, and showed no disposition to repeat the performance. The ripple died stillborn. The guests, who had paused with their spoons suspended, resumed their eating with an air of slight disappointment.

The fish was served. Lady Marchmont, with the courage of desperation, tried once more.

“I am told you have been to Paris, Mr. Quill. Is it not the most delightful place in the world?”

“It is,” said Mr. Quill, “a city where the women dress as though they were going to a fancy-dress ball, and the men as though they were coming away from a funeral. The result is a certain picturesqueness, but one would not care to live in a picture.”

Again the flash of wit, and again its immediate extinction. Mr. Quill applied himself to his fish with the concentration of a man who has found in it a subject more worthy of his attention than any that his hostess could supply. Lady Marchmont began to feel a sensation unfamiliar to her experience—a sensation of being, for the first time in her life, at a loss.

The entrée was served. Mr. Quill remained silent. The silence spread from him like a stain, creeping down the table, infecting the guests one by one. The bishop’s wife, who had come prepared to be shocked, found nothing to be shocked at, and looked vaguely disappointed. The young poet, who had hoped to be encouraged, found no encouragement, and grew sulky. The barrister, who had intended to be witty, found his wit unaccountably deserting him, and took refuge in the wine.

Lady Marchmont looked down the table at her husband, who was making heavy weather of the conversation with the lady on his left, and who returned her glance with the helpless look of a man who has been asked to swim and has discovered that the water is deeper than he thought. She looked at the bishop, who was smiling at nothing in particular with the air of a man who has decided that the best policy is to pretend that everything is for the best. She looked at Mr. Quill, who was eating his entrée with an expression of the most serene and maddening contentment.

And then, quite suddenly, Lady Marchmont understood. The man had nothing to say. The celebrated Mr. Quill, the wit of a hundred drawing-rooms, the oracle of a thousand dinner-tables, had come to her house with no more conversation than a stuffed bird in a glass case. He had spent his epigrams, exhausted his paradoxes, fired his last brilliant rocket, and was now as empty as a drum and as silent as a tomb. He was, in short, a fraud—a charming fraud, an elegant fraud, but a fraud nevertheless.

Lady Marchmont felt a moment of pure panic. Then, with the instinct of a great general who sees his centre crumbling and throws in his last reserves, she took a deep breath and began to talk.

She talked of the weather, and made it sound like a conspiracy. She talked of her neighbours, and made them sound like characters in a play. She talked of her husband, and made him sound like a man she had invented for her own amusement. She talked of the bishop, and made him sound like a saint who had missed his vocation. She talked of the young poet, and made him sound like a genius who had not yet discovered it. She talked of the barrister, and made him sound like a man who had missed his profession and might have been a comedian. She talked of Mr. Quill, and made him sound like a man who had once said something clever, but who had forgotten what it was.

The table began to laugh. The bishop’s wife began to be shocked. The young poet began to be encouraged. The barrister began to be witty. The silence that had threatened to engulf them was rolled back like a tide, and in its place came a flood of talk, of laughter, of that peculiar and delightful excitement that comes when a dinner-party suddenly discovers that it is a success.

Mr. Quill ate his dinner. He ate it with the composure of a man who has been paid to be brilliant and has decided that the money is sufficient. He offered no further observations. He did not need to. Lady Marchmont had taken his place, and was filling it with an ease and a brilliance that surprised no one more than herself.

When the ladies retired, the bishop’s wife said to Lady Marchmont, in a tone of respectful wonder, “My dear, you were quite wonderful this evening. I had no idea you were so amusing.”

“Neither had I,” said Lady Marchmont, with perfect truth.

And in the smoking-room, the barrister said to Mr. Quill, “You were very silent tonight, Quill. Saving your wit for a better occasion?”

Mr. Quill looked at him with those fatigued, luminous eyes.

“My dear fellow,” he said, “I have discovered that the greatest luxury in the world is to listen to someone else being brilliant, and to know that you need not interrupt.”

And he lit a cigarette, and smiled the smile of a man who has, for once, got the better of a bargain.
