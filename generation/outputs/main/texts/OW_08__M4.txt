The season was at its height, and London, that august and venerable hostess, had put on her finest jewels for the occasion. In drawing-rooms from Belgravia to Mayfair, the air was thick with the scent of hothouse lilies and the delicate clatter of teacups, a symphony of porcelain and gossip. It was into this glittering arena that Miss Cordelia Van der Pelt, of the Philadelphia Van der Pelts, made her entrance, and a very decided entrance it was.

She was a girl who possessed the peculiar and rather alarming beauty of her nation: a keen, bright eye, a complexion like a new rose, and a chin that spoke of resolutions firmly fixed. She had come to London with a single, splendid purpose, which she announced with a frankness that sent a shiver of delighted horror through the more sensitive of her hostesses. She was going to marry a duke.

“A duke, my dear Cordelia?” Lady Agatha Winchcombe had enquired, her eyebrows ascending to a height that suggested they might never come down again. “How very ambitious. And which duke, pray, have you selected for the sacrifice?”

“I am told,” Miss Van der Pelt replied, with the air of one discussing the weather, “that the Duke of Cumbernauld is the only one available. The others are either married, mad, or far too clever. I have no use for a clever husband. A man of intellect is a positive menace in the home.”

“But my dear,” Lady Agatha protested, toying with a diamond serpent that coiled about her wrist, “the Duke of Cumbernauld is ninety if he is a day. He is also, I am given to understand, as deaf as a post. He lives at Cumbernauld House with his dogs and his memories, and has not been seen in society since the Jubilee.”

“Excellent,” said Miss Van der Pelt, with a smile of pure, unalloyed satisfaction. “A man of ninety is not likely to be troublesome. And deafness, Lady Agatha, is a distinct advantage in a husband. One may say precisely what one thinks without fear of contradiction. It is the very foundation of a happy marriage.”

The news travelled, as news will, with the speed of a scandal, and soon all London was agog. The American heiress was to marry the decrepit Duke of Cumbernauld, and his heir, a nephew by the name of Lord Julian Asquith, was to be left out in the cold. This Lord Julian was a young man of considerable charm and no fortune whatsoever, a combination which had made him a great favourite in the ballrooms and a source of constant anxiety to his tailor. He was, in short, a professional nephew, and the imminent marriage of his uncle to a robust and determined American girl promised to put a very sudden and disagreeable end to his profession.

It was at a garden party at Kew that Lord Julian first laid eyes upon his nemesis. She was standing by the great glasshouse, a vision in a gown of pale primrose silk, her parasol a veritable cloud of lace, and she was holding forth to a small circle of admirers with the confidence of a general addressing his troops.

“The trouble with Englishmen,” she was saying, in a voice that carried with the clear, sweet tone of a bell, “is that they are so dreadfully modest. They have no idea of their own worth. Now, an American knows his worth to the last cent, and he is not afraid to present the bill.”

Lord Julian, who was never modest and whose bills were the terror of his existence, felt a sudden, sharp pang of kinship. He approached, and was introduced.

“Miss Van der Pelt,” he said, with his most winning smile, “I hear you are to be congratulated. My uncle, the Duke, is a most fortunate man.”

“Lord Julian,” she replied, giving him her hand with a firm, cool pressure, “I am told you are the loser by my engagement. You have my sympathies. It is always painful to see one’s prospects vanish like a mirage.”

“On the contrary,” he said, with an airy wave of his hand, “I feel nothing but relief. My uncle’s title is a burden I am only too happy to see transferred to younger and more capable shoulders. I am a man of simple tastes. A good cigar, a fine wine, and the conversation of a clever woman are all the wealth I require.”

“You are to be pitied, then,” she said, her eyes sparkling with a malice he found entirely delightful, “for you are likely to remain a pauper. I intend to be a very rich duchess, and I shall not be giving any of my money away to impecunious nephews.”

“But what of love, Miss Van der Pelt?” he asked, lowering his voice to a conspiratorial whisper. “Have you no room for that in your magnificent scheme?”

“Love,” she declared, “is a luxury, Lord Julian, and I am a practical woman. I am not marrying a man; I am marrying a title. It is a business arrangement, and a very sound one. The Duke gets a nurse, and I get a coronet. It is the most honest transaction in London.”

“And what of the heir?” he persisted, his eyes fixed on hers. “What of the man who must stand by and watch you take his place?”

“He must learn to be philosophical,” she said, and turned away to greet a passing marchioness.

Lord Julian was not a man to be easily discouraged, especially by a woman who was so very stimulating. He resolved upon a campaign. He would not allow his uncle’s title to be snatched away by a foreign invader without a fight. And so, he began to court Miss Van der Pelt with all the considerable art at his disposal.

He sent her orchids, not the common kind, but rare specimens from the Himalayas, with petals like the wings of pale moths. He sent her a first edition of Mr. Swinburne’s poems, bound in crimson morocco. He discovered her favourite flowers, her favourite composer, her favourite colour, and he wove them all into a tapestry of adoration so rich and so flattering that she found herself, for the first time in her life, at a loss for a suitably practical reply.

He would call upon her at her hotel, and they would sit in the conservatory, surrounded by the scent of gardenias and the soft splash of a fountain. He would tell her outrageous stories of London society, and she would laugh, a sound, he decided, like champagne being poured into a crystal glass.

“You are a terrible influence, Lord Julian,” she said one afternoon, as he was describing the latest scandal involving a bishop and a ballet dancer. “You are trying to make me frivolous.”

“On the contrary,” he replied, “I am trying to make you happy. Frivolity is the only thing worth being serious about. A life without a little nonsense in it is like a rose without a scent—very correct, no doubt, but how dull.”

She looked at him, and for a moment her bright, practical eyes softened. “You are a paradox, Lord Julian. You have no money, no prospects, and yet you are the most contented man I have ever met.”

“It is my only extravagance,” he confessed. “I cannot afford to be unhappy. It requires too much energy, and I have a great deal of sitting about to do.”

She laughed again, and he felt a curious, unfamiliar sensation, as if he had just won a very large sum at cards. He was, he realised with a start, beginning to fall in love with the woman who was trying to ruin him. It was a complication he had not foreseen.

The climax came at a ball given by the Duchess of Belgrave. Miss Van der Pelt, resplendent in a gown of silver tissue that made her look like a mermaid who had just stepped from the sea, was standing with a group of ladies, discussing the forthcoming wedding. Lord Julian, seeing his opportunity, approached.

“I have just come from Cumbernauld House,” he announced, in a voice loud enough to carry. “My uncle is in excellent spirits. He has, I am happy to report, just acquired a new mastiff puppy, and is teaching it to fetch his slippers. He is also, I regret to say, somewhat confused as to the day of the week. He believes it is still Tuesday. He has been believing it is Tuesday for three days now. It is a charming delusion, and he seems very happy in it.”

Miss Van der Pelt’s smile faltered, but only for an instant. “A man who is content with the day he is in is a man to be envied,” she said, coolly. “I shall be very happy to join him in his Tuesday.”

“But my dear Miss Van der Pelt,” Lord Julian continued, with an air of great solicitude, “have you considered the conversation? My uncle’s principal topics are his gout, the state of the weather in 1854, and the price of coal. I fear you will find him a trifle lacking in the sparkle you so evidently require.”

“I do not require sparkle,” she said, a faint flush rising to her cheeks. “I require a title.”

“And what of laughter?” he asked, softly, so that only she could hear. “What of wit? What of the pleasure of being understood? Can a coronet keep you warm at night, Cordelia? Can it talk to you of Swinburne, or laugh at your jokes, or tell you that you are the most beautiful and maddening woman in London?”

She stared at him, her mask of practicality momentarily gone, revealing a girl of flesh and blood, with a heart that was beating very fast.

“You are impertinent, Lord Julian,” she whispered.

“I am in love,” he corrected her. “It is a condition that excuses a great deal of impertinence. I have no title to offer you, and no fortune. I have nothing but a great and overwhelming desire to spend the rest of my life making you laugh. It is a poor bargain, I know. But it is the only one I have.”

For a long moment, she was silent. Then, a slow, radiant smile spread across her face, a smile that had nothing practical about it whatsoever.

“Lord Julian,” she said, “you are the most charming pauper I have ever met. And I find, to my very great astonishment, that I have a positive weakness for paupers. They are so much more interesting than dukes.”

And so it was that London was treated to a new sensation. The American heiress did not marry the Duke of Cumbernauld. She married his penniless nephew instead, and the Duke, who was not quite sure what had happened, gave them a very handsome piece of Wedgwood as a wedding present and went back to his dogs. The happy couple are, I am told, quite scandalously poor, and quite scandalously happy, which, in the end, is the only kind of happiness worth having.
