It was upon a raw November morning in the year 1894 that Miss Eleanor Vane presented herself at my rooms in Glendale Terrace, Bayswater. She was a young woman of remarkable composure, dressed in the severe mourning which then befitted her station, though her pale face and the restless movement of her gloved fingers upon the bosom of her jacket betrayed the anxiety which underlay her outward calm.

I had been engaged of late in a series of inquiries concerning the importation of substandard machinery into the London docks, a tedious affair of forged manifests and missing clerks, and I was not ill-pleased to turn my attention to a matter which promised something less prosaic. I bade my visitor be seated and requested that she lay the facts before me in the precise order of their occurrence, a habit which I have found saves no end of confusion in the later stages of an investigation.

"You are acquainted, perhaps," she began, "with the name of my father, Silas Vane."

I acknowledged that I was. The return of Silas Vane from the Ballarat goldfields in the summer of 1893, with a fortune variously estimated at between eighty and a hundred thousand pounds, had been a matter of some remark in the financial columns. He had taken a large house in Westbourne Park, and had lived there in a retirement which, if not austere, was at least decidedly private.

"For six months after his return," Miss Vane continued, "all was well. My father was reserved regarding his experiences in the colony, as indeed he was regarding all things, but he appeared to be in good health and spirits, and he spoke occasionally of establishing my brother in a suitable profession. Then, in the first week of June of this year, there came a parcel."

She paused, and I observed that her hands tightened upon one another.

"It was a small wooden box, crudely fashioned, with no inscription save the address, which was cut from a newspaper and pasted upon the lid. It had been posted, as the stamps showed, from Southampton. My father opened it in his study. I was not present, but my brother Arthur, who was passing the door, heard a sudden exclamation and entered to find my father standing white-faced at his desk. Upon the blotter lay a nugget of gold, somewhat larger than a man's thumb, and beside it a scrap of canvas, such as might have been torn from a tent or a sail, bearing four letters painted in a crude hand: J. D. K. R."

"And your father declined to explain?"

"He refused utterly. He gathered up the nugget and the canvas and locked them in his bureau. When my mother pressed him, he said only that the matter was his own, and that we were to ask no questions. From that day forward he carried a loaded revolver in the breast pocket of his coat."

"A Webley, I presume, or a Adams?"

"A Bulldog, I believe. I have seen it in his possession when he dressed for dinner."

"You have the address of the sender? The handwriting of the cut letters?"

"I have the box itself, if it would be of service."

I said that it would, and directed her to continue.

"Through the summer and autumn my father grew withdrawn and watchful. He would not walk abroad after dusk, and he dismissed two of the indoor servants, retaining only the cook and the parlourmaid, both of whom had been long in the family. He took to walking in the mornings instead of the afternoons, and always by a varied route. Yesterday, however, he went out at eleven o'clock and did not return."

"You have informed the police?"

"My brother wished to. My father had expressly forbidden any communication with the authorities in connexion with his affairs, and I feared that if he had met with some accident of a private nature, the intervention of the police might only worsen his predicament. I therefore determined to consult you."

I considered the matter for a moment. The disappearance of a wealthy man in broad daylight in a respectable quarter of London was a serious thing, yet I was struck less by the absence itself than by the chain of events which had preceded it. The nugget, the painted canvas, the four initials, the revolver—these were the materials of a narrative which had begun in the Australian diggings and had followed the old man across the world to the streets of Bayswater.

I requested that Miss Vane return home and place the wooden box at my disposal, and that she permit me to examine her father's study before any other person entered it. She agreed, and within the hour I was standing in the hall of the house in Westbourne Park, a solid, dark-fronted villa of the sort favoured by retired merchants of the comfortable class.

The study was a small room at the rear, looking upon a strip of garden. I examined the bureau first. It was locked, but the lock was of a common pattern and yielded to a pick which I carry for professional purposes. The nugget and the scrap of canvas were in the top drawer, exactly as Miss Vane had described. I turned the canvas over. Upon the reverse side, which had been pressed against the wood, were faint marks in pencil, almost obliterated by handling, but still legible under a strong glass: "3 claims. Mudge's Gully. Feb 17."

Here, then, was a date and a place. I had spent some months in the colony in my younger days and knew the geography of the goldfields tolerably well. Mudge's Gully was a minor workings some twelve miles south of Ballarat, worked chiefly by small parties of men who had banded together to share the cost of equipment and the labour of sinking.

I next examined the writing-desk. Among the papers I found a bundle of letters, tied with a bootlace, which had evidently been kept apart from the rest. They were written in several hands and bore postmarks ranging from 1872 to 1885. All were addressed to Silas Vane at various diggings, and three of them were signed with the initials J. D. K. R.—John D. K. Rawlins, as the subscription of the earliest letter gave in full. The tone of these letters was at first cordial, then progressively strained, and the last of them, dated from Melbourne in March of 1885, contained a passage which I transcribed into my notebook: "You have the gold and I have the silence. Whether the one will weigh heavier than the other in the final reckoning is a matter we shall settle when we meet again, which God knows may be sooner than you think."

I had now the outlines of the affair. Three men had worked three claims at Mudge's Gully. Vane had emerged with the fortune; Rawlins had emerged with a grievance. The nugget was a token, the canvas a reminder, and the four initials a signature. The question was whether Rawlins, having traced Vane to London, had waylaid him upon his morning walk, or whether Vane, upon catching sight of his old associate, had gone voluntarily with him to settle the reckoning of which the letter spoke.

I turned my attention to the wooden box. It was of Australian cedar, light in colour and coarsely nailed. The address had been cut from the London edition of the *Shipping Gazette*, a paper which, as I knew, was to be found in the reading-rooms of the principal hotels frequented by colonial visitors. The stamps were affixed carelessly, but the postmark was clear: Southampton, June 3rd.

I inquired of the parlourmaid whether any caller had sought Mr. Vane in the weeks preceding his disappearance. She was a sharp-eyed woman of middle age, and she replied without hesitation that a gentleman had called twice, once in the second week of October and again a fortnight later, and had been turned from the door both times by her master's express orders.

"Describe him," said I.

"Tall, sir, and very thin. Sunburnt, as if he had lived in hot countries. He wore a grey ulster and a soft felt hat, and he walked with a stick. His left hand was crooked, as if it had been broken and set badly."

I had my clue. I directed Miss Vane to remain at the house and to send for me by telegram if her father returned, and I set off at once for the offices of the principal shipping line serving the Southampton route. There, upon the passenger lists of the vessels arriving in the first days of June, I found what I sought: among the names disembarking at Southampton from the *Orient* on the first of that month was that of J. D. K. Rawlins.

The matter was now sufficiently plain. Rawlins had come to England to confront his former partner. He had called twice at the house and been rebuffed. He had then, in all probability, watched the house, observed Vane's habit of walking in the morning, and intercepted him upon the street. Whether the meeting had ended in violence or in negotiation I could not yet determine, but I was confident that the tall, sunburnt man with the crooked hand was the key to the whole business.

I spent the remainder of the day upon inquiries among the hotels of the Southampton and Paddington districts, and it was not until the evening, at the seventh house upon my list, that I found the record I sought. A gentleman answering the description had taken a room at a modest establishment in Gloucester Terrace under the name of J. Rawlins, and had departed only that morning, leaving no forwarding address.

I was standing in the lodging-house passage, considering my next step, when the landlady, a garrulous woman with a sharp eye to profit, remarked that the gentleman had left behind a quantity of Australian newspapers, which she was about to dispose of.

I examined them. Among the sheets I found a copy of the *Ballarat Courier* of the previous March, in which a paragraph was marked in pencil. It reported the death, at the Benevolent Asylum, of one Thomas H. Keppel, formerly a miner, who had been, the notice stated, the last surviving member of the party of three which had worked the claims at Mudge's Gully in the early seventies.

I read the paragraph twice. The party of three had consisted of Silas Vane, the deceased Keppel, and one John Rawlins. Keppel had died in poverty. Rawlins, it seemed, had taken ship for England within a month of the notice.

The landlady, prompted by a small consideration, produced a further item: a telegram, crumpled and thrust behind the wash-stand, which Rawlins had evidently received and discarded. It was dated the previous day and read: "Will meet. Come to the Criterion at two. Bring the paper. V."

I glanced at my watch. It was half-past six. The Criterion was a public-house in the Edgware Road, not a quarter of a mile distant. I walked there briskly.

The bar was moderately crowded, but I had no difficulty in identifying the man I sought. He sat alone at a corner table, a tall, gaunt figure in a grey ulster, with a face deeply lined and burnt to the colour of old leather. His left hand, clasped about a glass of spirits, was unmistakably deformed. Opposite him, his back to the wall, sat Silas Vane, alive, unharmed, and apparently in the full possession of his faculties.

I crossed the room and stood before them. Rawlins looked up with a start; Vane with an expression of irritation which gave way, upon my introducing myself, to reluctant resignation.
