Miss Harriet Vance had been educated to believe that a woman's happiness depended upon two things: the steadiness of her husband's temper and the respectability of his situation. In Mr. Edmund Lisle she had found both, secured by a proposal which she had accepted with a gratitude that was not entirely separate from relief. Mr. Lisle was twenty years her senior, and his estate of Bellhurst, though not vast, was well-cultivated and free from the encumbrances that so often attended a gentleman's property. More importantly, Mr. Lisle was a man of such uniform kindness that to be near him was to be sheltered from the rougher elements of the world. Harriet was not insensible to the advantages of this arrangement; she had seen too many young women make matches of passion only to discover that passion is a poor companion in the management of a household. She told herself, and with perfect sincerity, that she was content.

The eve of the wedding brought a visitor to Bellhurst whom Harriet had heard of but never seen: Mr. Lisle's nephew, Captain Arthur Lisle, lately returned from the Peninsula. He arrived in the late afternoon, having ridden from Worcester, and was shown into the drawing-room where Harriet sat at her embroidery. He was perhaps eight-and-twenty, with the complexion of a man who had spent considerable time out of doors, and an ease of manner that suggested he had never in his life been at a loss for what to say. Harriet rose to receive him with a composure that cost her more than she would afterwards have liked to acknowledge. Captain Lisle bowed, expressed his pleasure at meeting her, and inquired after his uncle's health with a filial warmth that seemed entirely unforced. When Mr. Lisle entered the room a moment later, the nephew's face lit up with an affection so genuine that Harriet felt, for the first time, a small and unwelcome tremor of doubt—not as to her choice, but as to the completeness of her understanding of what a choice might contain.

They dined together, the three of them, and the conversation turned upon the war, upon the country, and upon the improvements Mr. Lisle had made to the Bellhurst farm buildings. Captain Lisle spoke well; he had the gift of making whatever he described seem vivid and immediate, yet he never once sought to dominate the discourse or to draw attention to himself at the expense of others. When Harriet ventured a remark about the management of the estate's woodlands, he listened with an attention that was not mere politeness, and replied in a manner that showed he had considered her opinion worth weighing. Mr. Lisle, observing this exchange, smiled with the quiet satisfaction of a man who sees his family getting on as he had hoped. "Arthur has a good head for such matters," he said, "though the army has kept him from putting it to much use. But I daresay you will find him a very tolerable companion, Miss Vance. He has his mother's sense, and I hope he has escaped his father's temper." Captain Lisle laughed at this, and Harriet laughed too, though she was conscious that the room had grown warmer than the fire could account for.

After dinner, Mr. Lisle retired early, being subject to a mild indisposition that made him wish for rest before the morrow's exertions. Harriet and Captain Lisle were left together, and for a few moments neither spoke. The silence was not uncomfortable, but it was full of a significance that Harriet found herself unable to ignore. At length Captain Lisle said, "My uncle tells me you are fond of reading. Have you a particular author you favour?" Harriet named Cowper, and they fell into a discussion of poetry that lasted the better part of an hour. She learned that he had carried a volume of Cowper's letters in his knapsack through Spain and Portugal, and that he had read them by firelight on nights when the prospect of the next day's march made sleep impossible. There was nothing in this that was calculated to move her; it was simply a fact, offered without artifice, and yet it seemed to open a door into a country she had never visited and had not known she wished to see.

It was nearly eleven o'clock when Harriet became aware that the candles had burned low, and that Captain Lisle was looking at her with an expression she could not quite interpret. She rose, meaning to bid him good-night, and he rose with her. "Miss Vance," he said, and then paused, as if reconsidering. "I hope—I am sure—that my uncle will be very happy. He deserves every happiness, and I believe you are well fitted to give it to him." Harriet thanked him, and her voice was steady, though her heart was beating rather faster than was convenient. She understood perfectly what he had not said, and she understood too that he would not say it. The restraint of his manner was not indifference; it was a kind of honour, and honour, she reflected, was a quality she had always admired but had never before encountered in a form so painful. She wished him a good-night, and went upstairs to her room, where she sat for a long time before the small fire and thought about the difference between contentment and happiness.

In the morning she rose early and found the house still quiet. She went down to the breakfast-room and was surprised to find Captain Lisle already there, standing at the window and looking out at the park. He turned when she entered, and for a moment they regarded each other in the grey light that fell through the glass. "I am leaving directly after the ceremony," he said. "I have business in Shrewsbury that will not wait." Harriet nodded, and said that she was sorry to lose his company so soon. It was a civil remark, and it was also, she knew, entirely inadequate. But there was nothing else to be said, or rather there was everything else to be said, and that was precisely why they must be silent. Captain Lisle poured her a cup of tea with a hand that was perfectly steady, and they sat together and drank it, and spoke of the weather, and of the likelihood of rain before evening.

When the time came for the wedding, Harriet put on her gown and went down to the little church at the edge of Bellhurst village with a mind that was calm and a heart that was not. Mr. Lisle was waiting for her at the altar, his face alight with a happiness so simple and so complete that she felt a surge of genuine affection for him, and was glad—truly glad—that she was doing this thing. She had made her promise, and she would keep it, not because she feared to break it, but because she understood at last what keeping a promise meant. It meant choosing, with open eyes, the life one had consented to live. Captain Lisle stood at the back of the church, and when the ceremony was over and Harriet turned as Mrs. Lisle, she saw him raise his hand in a gesture of farewell that was at once casual and final. She inclined her head in return, and then took her husband's arm, and walked out into the sunshine of a Herefordshire morning, where the birds were singing and the world went on exactly as it had done the day before.
