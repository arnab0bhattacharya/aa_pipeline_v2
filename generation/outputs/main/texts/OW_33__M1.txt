In the gardens of the Villa Ambra, where the cypresses stood like thin, black thoughts against the sky, there was a pool so still that it seemed less a body of water than a flaw in the marble of the world. It was lined with lapis lazuli, and in its depths floated the goldfish of the Prince Ambrogio—creatures of a fabulous orange, like swimming embers. And upon its surface rested one water lily, whose name was Selene, a name of ill omen and perfect appropriateness. She was the colour of pale ivory, or of the inside of a pearl, and she had decided that she was in love.

The object of her affection was, of course, the moon. This was, as the frogs pointed out with a certain brutal pragmatism, a singularly poor choice. “My dear Selene,” croaked a fat bullfrog named Barnaby, who had the voice of a ruined politician, “you are pursuing a romance with a celestial body. The logistics alone are prohibitive. He is, to put it mildly, out of your league. He is out of everyone’s league. He is, in fact, in the heavens.”

“It is the nature of love,” replied the lily, with a sigh that caused a ripple to disturb the perfect reflection of a star, “to aspire. One does not love what is easily possessed.”

“One does not eat what is easily possessed,” corrected Barnaby. “Love is a different matter entirely. It is a form of indigestion of the soul. My advice to you is to find a nice, damp stone and settle down.”

The goldfish, who were far more aesthetic in their sensibilities, if no less cynical, swam in agitated circles. Their leader, a magnificent creature with trailing fins of spun gold, was named Ignatius. “You are confusing the object with the image,” he declared, his voice a silvery bubble. “The moon you adore is merely a reflection. It is a pretty trick of the light, a smear of silver on our water. You are in love with a cosmetic effect. It is like falling in love with a blush instead of the cheek that wears it.”

“But it is the most beautiful cosmetic effect in the world,” Selene insisted. “And it is a reflection of the most beautiful thing in the world. To love the reflection is merely to love the original by proxy. It is a more refined, a more intellectual, form of devotion.”

“Intellectual!” Ignatius scoffed, flicking his tail. “It is the most sentimental piece of nonsense I have ever heard. You are a flower. Your business is to be beautiful and to smell pleasant. Leave the philosophy to the philosophers and the heartbreak to the poets. It is not our trade.”

But Selene would not listen. Every night, as the moon rose, a perfect, cool disc of silver hung in the purple velvet of the sky, she would open her petals to it. She would unfurl her ivory silk, her heart of gold stamens exposed to the cold, distant light. She felt the moon’s gaze upon her, a gaze that was both intimate and infinitely remote. It was a caress that did not touch, a communion that required no words. She felt herself transformed by it, no longer a mere flower in a palace pool, but a devotee on a celestial shore. She was a chalice for the moonlight, and she believed the moon filled her with a love that was as pure and as cold as his own.

Then the dawn would come, bleeding rose and saffron across the sky. The moon would fade, a ghost of himself, and Selene would close her petals in a sorrow so profound it felt like a physical weight. She would droop upon her stem, her beauty spent, her secret passion turned to ashes. The frogs would begin their tiresome chorus about the weather and the flies, and the goldfish would resume their glittering, pointless ballet, and she would be merely a water lily again.

One evening, the Prince Ambrogio himself came to the edge of the pool. He was a man whose face was a mask of weary boredom and whose hands were as white as the marble he leaned upon. He watched the lily open, a slow and sacred unveiling. “How perfect,” he murmured to his companion, a cynical courtier named Lord Valerius. “It opens for the moon and closes for the sun. It knows its audience.”

“Indeed, Your Highness,” said Valerius. “It is a creature of exquisite taste. It has chosen the most fashionable of lovers, one who is never seen in daylight and is the subject of endless poetry. It is the very model of a modern romance.”

The Prince sighed. “I wonder if it is happy.”

“Happiness, Your Highness,” Valerius replied, adjusting the lace at his cuff, “is a bourgeois sentiment. The lily is not concerned with happiness. It is concerned with beauty. And its devotion to the moon is the most beautiful thing in your garden. To be happy would be to ruin the effect entirely. One must suffer to be truly decorative.”

Selene heard them, and their words, meant to be detached and clever, pierced her heart. Was her love merely an effect? Was her sorrow a kind of ornament? The thought was unbearable. That night, as the moon climbed the heavens, she opened her petals with a new and desperate resolve. She would not be a passive vessel. She would speak.

“O, King of the Night,” she whispered, her voice the rustle of silk on silk. “O, silent shepherd of the stars. I love you. I have loved you from the moment I first saw your reflection trembling in the water beside me. I open myself to you, and you give me nothing but this cold, beautiful light. Is that all there is? Is that all there will ever be?”

The moon, of course, said nothing. He simply continued his serene, indifferent arc across the sky. His silence was not a refusal; it was a statement of fact. He was a body of rock and dust, three hundred thousand miles away, a prisoner of gravity and light. He could not love. He could not speak. He could only shine.

And in that moment of perfect, unadulterated silence, Selene understood. The frogs were right about the logistics. The goldfish were right about the illusion. The Prince and his courtier were right about the aesthetic. But they were all wrong about the love. Her love was not for the moon himself, a cold and barren rock. It was for the moonlight. It was for the act of loving. The moon was merely the excuse, the catalyst for the exquisite, self-contained drama of her own devotion. The beauty was not in the object, but in the adoration. The sorrow of the morning was not a failure of the romance, but its necessary and most poignant conclusion.

The next morning, as the sun rose, she closed her petals not in sorrow, but in a kind of satisfied repose. Barnaby the frog hopped onto a lily pad. “Well?” he croaked. “Did he propose?”

“He offered me the world,” Selene replied serenely.

“And?” pressed Ignatius the goldfish, circling her stem.

“And I realized,” said the lily, “that I have no use for it. My world is this pool, this light, this beautiful, foolish, and entirely sufficient adoration. It is a far more elegant arrangement.”

The frogs and the fish, finding her answer quite incomprehensible, went back to their business of catching flies and chasing reflections. And Selene, the water lily, rested in the morning light, waiting for the night, when she could once again perform the only tragedy that truly mattered: her own.
