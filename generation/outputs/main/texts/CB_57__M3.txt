The winds that swept across the bleak moors nearest York seemed to gather a peculiar malice as they struck the dark, gabled front of Thornwood Hall. I had been installed there as governess to little Miss Evelyn scarcely a fortnight, yet in that brief span, the house had already impressed upon me its heavy, unyielding character—a character of damp stone, shuttered windows, and profound, uncheerful silence. My pupil was a fragile, anxious child of eight, possessing a pale, translucent complexion and eyes that held the haunted watchfulness of a creature much older. Her father, Captain Radcliffe, was a phantom presence, absent upon some unspecified military duty, and his wife, Mrs. Radcliffe, was a woman worn to a sharp, restless edge by solitude and suspicion.

It was she who summoned me to her sitting-room on that bleak November afternoon. The fire in the grate burned low, casting a smoldering, crimson glare across the heavy, dark-wood furniture. She sat by it, her thin hands clasped in her black silk lap, her countenance bearing the severe, haggard beauty of a woman who has wept much, and has determined to weep no more.

"I desire, Miss Arden," she began, her voice low and guarded, "that you will keep a written account of Evelyn’s daily progress. A diary, if you will. Her mind, her temper, her little ailments—you must record everything with exactitude."

I considered this. "It shall be done, madam. But might I ask the purpose? Is the child so delicate that her physical fluctuations must be tracked by the physician?"

"No," Mrs. Radcliffe replied, a sudden, restless light shifting in her dark eyes. "The physician need not see it. It is for me. I am her mother, and I would observe her growth, since I am denied the privilege of observing her father’s. He is much away. When he does return to us, it is but for a night, slipping into the nursery at the midnight hour to gaze upon his sleeping child, then vanishing before the dawn. I thought him in London; the servants whisper of Portsmouth. I know not which to believe. I ask you, what wife is left to such uncertainty, and told to be content?"

Her voice had risen, not in a shriek, but in that cold, vibrating tone which speaks of a deeply cankered spirit. I felt a sudden, involuntary pang of sympathy. Yet I knew my place. I bowed my head and said only, "I will keep the diary, madam, as you wish."

I procured a thick, blank volume the very next day, and began my entries with the strictest fidelity to my charge. I wrote of Evelyn’s lessons, of her aptitude for French, of her trembling reluctance to approach the wing of the house that housed the old picture-gallery. I wrote of the weather, of the sleet that lashed the windows, of the deep, damp chill that no fire could wholly penetrate. I wrote plainly, for I have ever thought plainness the sister of truth.

It was perhaps a week later that I left the volume in my usual place upon my writing-desk, having been called abruptly away to soothe Evelyn, who had awakened from a night-terror. When I returned, the book lay where I had left it, but the ribbon marker was displaced by a fraction of an inch. A faint, evanescent perfume of dried lavender—Mrs. Radcliffe’s peculiar scent—lingered in the air. 

I stood motionless, the realization striking me with a cold, sudden shock. She had been reading the diary. But why? I had written nothing of the Captain. I had confined myself strictly to the child. Then, like a thunderbolt, the truth broke upon my mind. She did not care for Evelyn’s French verbs. She was searching the pages for any mention of the absent master. She thought me a blind instrument, a scribe who might unwittingly chronicle his clandestine movements, his letters to the servants, his midnight visits to the nursery.

A fierce, resentful fire kindled in my breast. I was a dependent, it was true, forced by the harsh dictates of circumstance to earn my bread in a stranger’s house; but I was not a spy. To be so used, to have my simple, honest records twisted into threads for her jealous loom, was an indignity I could not tamely swallow. I paced my narrow chamber, the wind howling in the casement, mirroring the tumult in my soul. I felt the humiliation of my position acutely. I was a woman, solitary and poor, set amidst a family whose miseries were a dark, hidden sore.

Yet, even as my pride revolted, a strange, sorrowful pity began to temper my anger. I thought of Mrs. Radcliffe, left to drag out her days in this melancholy, wind-beaten house, her mind a prey to suspicions she dared not voice openly. She was a prisoner, bound by chains far heavier than my own. Scripture tells us that the heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked; but it also tells us to bear one another's burdens. I could not expose her secret reading, for a confrontation would only entrench her in her misery and cost me my livelihood. 

I sat down at my desk, opened the diary, and took up my pen. If I could not be merely the governess, I must be the governess and the confessor. I began to write for two readers.

*November 14th.* *Evelyn’s spirits were exceedingly fragile this morning. She wept over her arithmetic, and I could not chide her, for the house felt oppressive, weighed down by an absence that casts a shadow over us all. The wind blows from the east, bringing a freezing rain that rattles the shutters. I observed to the housekeeper, Mrs. Flockhart, that the Master’s study remains unlit and cold, as though no gentleman had stepped foot across its threshold these many months. She looked at me strangely, but offered no remark. The child asked for her father, and I told her he was away on the King’s business. She accepted this with the resigned sadness of one accustomed to neglect. I wonder that a father’s heart can be so hardened to his own hearth.*

I laid down my pen, my heart beating with a strange, defiant thrill. I had not lied. I had not gossiped. But I had written knowing that those words would fall upon Mrs. Radcliffe’s eyes, knowing they would pierce the armor of her stoicism. I was offering her a mirror, reflecting her own desolation through the guise of a child’s lesson-book.

The next morning, Evelyn was sent down to me with a new, warm pelisse of dark blue cloth, and a message from her mother: "Evelyn is to take a longer walk today, Miss Arden, whatever the weather. The east wind is bracing, and she must not mope indoors over her books."

I looked at the child, wrapped in her new garment, and then at the diary resting on my desk. The game was begun. I had thrown down the gauntlet, not in anger, but in a fierce, agonized desire for truth. I was no longer merely the keeper of a child; I was the chronicler of a house’s silent anguish, writing a double script—on the surface, the plain, unvarnished tale of a governess and her pupil; beneath, a passionate, probing testament to the hidden wounds of a woman’s heart. I did not know what the coming days would bring, whether my boldness would draw her fire or her confidence. I only knew that the diary had become a battlefield, and upon its blank pages, the souls of two women, both bereft and both fighting for their breath, had begun to clash.
