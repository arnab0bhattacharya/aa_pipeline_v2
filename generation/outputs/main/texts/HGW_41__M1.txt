There is a peculiar sort of silence that settles upon the Outer Hebrides after the last boat has gone—a silence that is not merely an absence of sound, but a positive pressure against the ears. Angus Macleod had lived within that pressure for eleven years. He was a man of forty-five, with a face the colour of old leather and a temperament that had been weathered by the Atlantic gales into a state of philosophical resignation. His duties were simple: to keep the light of Sgeir Mòr burning, to wind the clockwork mechanism that turned the great lens, and to record the weather in a ledger that was sent to the Commissioners once a month.

It was on the night of October the 14th, during a lull in the autumnal storms, that the monotony was shattered. Angus was seated by the stove, reading a three-month-old copy of the *Scotsman*, when a shadow passed across the beam of the lantern above. He did not hear a sound, which was the first oddity; the sea was calm, but the creature that rose from the kelp beds was of such a size that it should have displaced a thousand tons of water with a roar.

He went out onto the gallery, the wind biting at his neck. There, illuminated in the rhythmic sweep of the white light, was a shape that defied the catalogues of the Board of Trade. It was a long, serpentine thing, yet it possessed fluted fins like the sails of a ship and a head that resembled a flattened, prehistoric horse. It rose high above the water, dripping phosphorescence, and regarded the lighthouse with an eye that Angus could only describe as inquisitive. It was not the mindless glare of a shark or a whale; it was a look of intelligence.

Angus did not scream. He was a practical man. He went down to the wireless room—a recent, crackling addition to the station—and tapped out a message to the mainland. "Large marine organism. Sgeir Mòr. Alive. Distinct features. Awaiting instructions."

The response came two days later, not in code, but in the form of a boat tossing on the swell, carrying a man who looked entirely unsuited for the Hebrides. This was Mr. Silas Vane, a naturalist from the British Museum, who arrived with a rifle in one hand and a heavy tripod in the other. He was a man of high nervous energy, with a monocle that seemed screwed into his skull and a vocabulary that Angus found exhausting.

"Remarkable, Macleod! Absolutely remarkable!" Vane cried, shaking the keeper’s hand with a grip that was surprisingly strong. "To think the genus survived! I must have him. I must have the specimen. The camera first, of course. Documentation is the soul of science. But the rifle... well, we must be prepared for eventualities."

Angus led the man up the spiral stairs. "I dinna think you'll need the gun, Mr. Vane. It seemed... gentle."

"Gentle?" Vane scoffed, adjusting his lens. "It is a reptile, Macleod. A survivor of the Jurassic deeps. It has no more 'gentleness' than a steam engine. It has instincts. Hunger, fear, aggression. We must observe it in its natural state before we intervene."

They waited three nights. Vane paced the floor, muttering about exposure times and the curvature of the earth. Angus brewed tea and felt a growing unease. He watched the naturalist cleaning his rifle, oiling the bolt with a lover’s care. It occurred to Angus that to Vane, the creature was merely a collection of skin and bone—a prize to be stuffed and mounted in a glass case in London, to be stared at by schoolchildren and dusty academics.

On the fourth night, the creature returned. It came closer to the shore this time, its great fluted fins dragging on the wet sand of the cove. The light swept over it, illuminating the iridescent greens and purples of its hide. It looked fragile now, out of its element, gasping slightly in the cold air.

"Steady..." Vane whispered, his eye glued to the viewfinder. The magnesium flash powder was ready. "Hold... hold..."

The camera clicked and exploded in a blinding white flash. The effect on the creature was instantaneous and terrible. It recoiled, thrashing against the rocks, letting out a sound like the tearing of canvas—a high, keening whistle of distress. It began to drag itself back toward the surf.

"Damnation!" Vane shouted, dropping the camera. "It's bolting! Quick, the rifle! I need a leg, at least! A flipper!"

Vane raised the gun, his cheek pressed to the stock. Angus saw the barrel align with the soft, exposed throat of the beast. The creature turned its head back toward the lighthouse, and in the sweeping beam, Angus saw that eye again. It was wide, wet, and terrified.

Something shifted in Angus Macleod’s soul. He thought of the years of loneliness, of the way the islanders on the mainland looked at him as if he were half-mad himself. He thought of the creature, a lonely thing in a vast, cold world, just as he was.

He moved without thinking. He stepped forward and brought his heavy boot down hard on Vane’s ankle.

The naturalist roared in pain, stumbling sideways. The rifle discharged with a deafening crack, but the bullet went wide, smashing into the granite face of the lighthouse and ricocheting into the night.

"You fool!" Vane screamed, clutching his leg. "Do you know what you've done? That is a missing link! It is the find of the century!"

Angus stood over him, his chest heaving. "It is a living thing, Mr. Vane. And you are a guest on my island."

"It is a specimen!" Vane shrieked, trying to rise. "I’ll have you dismissed! I’ll have you prosecuted! The Commissioners will hear of this obstruction!"

"The Commissioners," Angus said calmly, picking up the rifle and breaking the barrel to eject the spent shell, "are in Edinburgh. The creature is here. And I think it is time you went back to your boat."

"You are mad," Vane whispered, backing away toward the stairs. "You are sentimental and mad."

"Perhaps," Angus replied, looking out at the dark water where the ripples were slowly fading. "But I know the difference between a monster and a neighbour."

Vane limped down the stairs, muttering threats about the scientific community and the laws of property. Angus heard the boat’s engine sputter to life an hour later. He went back to the gallery. The wind had picked up, carrying the smell of salt and iodine. Far out, just at the edge of the light’s reach, a dark shape broke the surface. It did not flee. It rose, dipped its great head in a gesture that looked uncannily like a bow, and then slipped beneath the waves, leaving Angus Macleod alone with his light and his secret.
