Mr. Edmund Ashworth, on succeeding to the estate of Hartwell in the county of Derbyshire, found himself possessed of a very handsome inheritance, and a very disagreeable incumbrance. The house, the land, and the family name were his; the debts were, unhappily, his also, and of so ancient and respectable a lineage that they might almost have been considered a part of the entail. His father, a gentleman of great taste and no management, had died leaving to his son the full glory of a library and the full consequence of a mortgaged park. It was a situation requiring either a fortune or a wife, and as the first was entirely wanting, Mr. Ashworth was compelled to address himself to the second.

His feelings upon this necessity were those of a man whose pride and whose circumstances are at perpetual odds. To marry for money was a degradation he could not contemplate without a certain internal revulsion; to sell the home of his ancestors was a calamity he could not contemplate without a more practical shudder. Between these two evils, he chose the one that promised to preserve his situation in life, if not his dignity, and resolved to seek a bride whose dowry would be sufficient to silence the creditors of three generations. His search was not long. In the neighbouring town of Matlock, a Mr. Josiah Croft had lately made a considerable fortune in the manufacture of silk, and was now engaged in the very different business of launching his only daughter, Miss Eleanor, into society.

The introduction was managed with an ease that suggested the designs of the young lady’s father were as well-defined as his own. Miss Croft was a young woman of a pleasing, if not dazzling, countenance, and of a fortune so magnificent as to make the former consideration of very little consequence. Mr. Ashworth found her conversation to be of a quality he had not anticipated; she was sensible, well-read, and possessed a quiet composure that was far more agreeable than the vivacious affectation of many a country miss. He spoke of his estate, of the old oaks in the park and the fine prospect from the south front, and she listened with an intelligent appreciation. She spoke of her father’s new enterprise in bleaching, and he listened with an interest he did not entirely have to feign, for it was clear that in understanding the one, he was understanding the key to the other.

After a fortnight of dinners, walks, and supervised meetings, Mr. Ashworth found his admiration for Miss Croft to be sincere, but his object to be unchanged. He had come to see her as a partner in a financial arrangement, and was now obliged to see her as a person with whom he might arrange a partnership for life. The distinction was subtle, but it weighed upon him. The moment of explanation, however, could no longer be deferred. He had been led to believe, through Mrs. Croft’s artful hints and Mr. Croft’s broad smiles, that his proposal would be received with the warmest encouragement. He therefore requested a private audience in the Crofts’ drawing-room, a room so filled with new and fashionable furniture that he felt himself to be an antique relic.

He began his address with all the formality the occasion demanded, speaking of his esteem, his admiration, and his hopes for a union of their families. He was proceeding to a delicate allusion to the harmony of their circumstances when Miss Croft, with a look of gentle frankness, interrupted him.

“Mr. Ashworth,” she said, “you do me great honour. I believe I understand you very well. You have an estate that requires an income, and I have an income that requires a position. It is a very practical arrangement, and I see no reason to cloak it in unnecessary sentiment.”

Mr. Ashworth, who had prepared a speech of some length, was struck silent. He had expected to persuade, to overcome objections, perhaps even to inspire a little affection. He had not expected to be so thoroughly understood. “You are… remarkably candid, Miss Croft.”

“I find it is the most efficient method of communication,” she replied, her colour rising only a little. “My father has raised me to be a person of business, not of coyness. I know my own value, sir, and I know yours. You bring the name of Ashworth and a seat in Derbyshire; I bring a settlement that will make the seat habitable. It is a fair exchange. But an exchange, Mr. Ashworth, requires terms.”

He felt as though he were not proposing marriage but negotiating a contract for the supply of timber. “Terms, Miss Croft?”

“Certainly. I am not a silly girl to be swept away by a handsome ruin and a title. I require that a portion of my fortune be settled upon me entirely, for my own disposal and the provision of any children we may have. I shall not be a mere instrument for the payment of your father’s debts. Furthermore, I should wish to have a voice in the management of the estate, particularly in those matters which relate to the comfort of the tenants. A prosperous estate, Mr. Ashworth, is not merely one that is free of debt, but one where the people upon it are not in want. Their prosperity and ours are, as my father would say, a single balance sheet.”

Mr. Ashworth listened to these extraordinary terms with a mixture of astonishment and dawning respect. He had imagined a wife who would bring him a fortune and ask little in return, or perhaps one who would bring him a fortune and demand endless balls and new carriages. He had not imagined a wife who would ask for a partnership. Her conditions were shrewd, just, and utterly unforeseen. They did not speak of love, of course—that was a currency too volatile for such a transaction—but they spoke of a mutual respect and a shared purpose that he found, to his surprise, he valued more highly than the vague romantic notions he had once entertained.

He saw, in that moment, that she was not merely the key to his salvation from debt, but a far better steward for Hartwell than he was likely to be himself. Her good sense was more valuable than her fortune. He bowed his head in acquiescence. “Your terms, Miss Croft, are not only reasonable, they are wise. I accept them without reservation.”

A faint smile touched her lips. “Then we are agreed. I trust our partnership will be a profitable one for us both.”

“It will be,” said Mr. Ashworth, with a sincerity that surprised him, “the making of me.” He took her hand, and found in its firm clasp not the promise of passionate adoration, but something he had not known he was searching for: the prospect of a well-managed and prosperous future.
