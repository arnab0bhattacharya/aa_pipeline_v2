The sound came on the wind, a ghost of a memory carried over the fells. At first, Elara thought it was the wind itself, playing tricks in the skeletal branches of the sycamore outside her cottage. But then it came again, a low, resonant hum that vibrated not in her ears, but deep in her chest. A bell. A single, iron tongue, calling across the valley.

Her hands, raw and chapped from a morning spent clearing the frost-killed potatoes from the plot, stilled over the tin basin. The water, drawn from the old well and heated on the wood-fired stove, had gone cold against her skin. She hadn't heard that sound in over a year. Not since the last funeral in Little Bisley, when Old Man Hemlock was put into the earth, the bell tolling for a soul that was, by then, the only one left to mourn. After that, there had been only the wind, the crows, and the groan of her own empty house.

The ringing stopped. Silence rushed back in, thicker and heavier than before. Elara stood frozen, her breath a plume of white in the cold kitchen air. It was impossible. It had to be. The wind, or a loose timber somewhere, or the fever coming back for her, finally, to finish the job. But the sound had been too pure, too deliberate. It was an invitation.

Fear, cold and sharp, lanced through her. Fear of what she might find. A settlement of strangers, their intentions unknown. Or worse, a trap set by the desperate and the mad. But then another fear, deeper and more primal, rose to meet it. The fear of the silence. The fear of another year, then another, of talking to the birds, of reading the same water-stained books until the words blurred and lost all meaning. The fear of growing old and grey and utterly, completely alone. The fear of not going was a physical thing, a hollowness in her stomach that the cold potatoes could never fill.

She moved with a speed that surprised her, her body remembering a purpose it had forgotten. She pulled on her worn leather boots, thick wool socks, and the green canvas coat that had been her father’s. She checked the small rucksack by the door. The knife, sharp and reliable. The flask of water. A block of hard cheese and a heel of bread from yesterday. Her hand hesitated over the old bolt-action rifle leaning against the wall, then took it. She slung the pack on her shoulders, the familiar weight a small comfort.

The cottage door groaned open onto the village of Little Bisley. It was a perfect Cotswolds postcard, frozen in time and decay. Honey-coloured stone cottages with roofs of mossy slate, their windows dark and vacant as eye sockets. The lane was a river of weeds, brambles already clawing at the doorsteps of houses where families had once lived and laughed and died. The silence was her constant companion here, a presence as real as the stone. Now, the memory of that single bell had cracked its shell.

She knew where the sound had come from. The next valley over, where the village of Oakhaven sat nestled in the crook of the hill. It was a good three-hour walk, over the high ridge of the Cotswold Way. A journey she had avoided, not out of laziness, but out of a superstitious dread. To leave the known quiet of her valley felt like stepping off the edge of the world.

The climb was hard. The winter air was thin and sharp in her lungs. The path was overgrown, and she had to push through gorse and tangled hawthorn, the thorns snagging at her coat and trousers. With every step, the silence of Little Bisley fell away behind her, replaced by the sound of her own ragged breathing and the crunch of her boots on the frozen ground. The bell had not rung again. Doubt began to creep in, a cold mist in her mind. Had she imagined it? Was this a fool’s errand, a pilgrimage to a ghost?

As she crested the ridge, the wind hit her, a physical force that stole her breath. But it also carried a scent, faint and impossible. Woodsmoke. Not the damp, rotting smell of a long-dead hearth, but the clean, sharp scent of burning ash. A living fire. The hope that had propelled her up the hill surged, fierce and painful in her chest.

She descended into the Oakhaven valley. The village appeared below her, looking much like her own. The same silent stone, the same encroaching wilderness. But then she saw it. A thin, wavering thread of grey smoke rising from the chimney of the old vicarage, a grander house set apart from the rest, its windows glinting in the weak afternoon sun.

Her pace quickened, her fear now a thrumming, nervous energy. She moved from the cover of the trees into the open, her rifle held loosely but ready in her hands. As she approached the vicarage, she saw the churchyard. The gate hung open on one hinge. And in the centre of the yard, leaning against the great yew tree, was a man.

He was old, with a long, white beard and a face as weathered as the stone walls around him. He was wrapped in layers of mismatched wool, and he was whittling a piece of wood, the small curls of shavings falling onto the frosted grass at his feet. He didn’t look up as she approached, as if he had been expecting her.

Elara stopped a few yards away, her heart hammering against her ribs. She didn’t speak. The silence between them was different from the one she knew. It was not empty. It was full of unspoken questions.

Finally, the man spoke, his voice a low rasp, like stones grinding together. “Took you long enough.”

Elara’s throat was dry. “The bell,” she managed to say. “I heard the bell.”

“Aye,” the old man said, setting down his knife and the half-formed bird he had been carving. He looked at her then, his eyes a pale, startling blue. “It’s good for the soul to hear it. Even if you’re the only one listening.” He paused. “My name is Silas.”

“Elara.” She took a step closer. “I thought… I thought everyone was gone.”

“Most are,” Silas said, his gaze drifting back to the church. “But not everyone. I came here after the fever. I was the bell-ringer’s apprentice, fifty years ago. Seems my hands still remember the rope.” He gestured with a gnarled hand towards the church tower. “I ring it every Sunday. A habit. A promise. I just never knew if anyone was left to hear it.”

A wave of relief so profound it made her knees weak washed over Elara. She was not the last. The world was not just silence and ghosts. Here was a man, a voice, a fire. Here was a promise kept against all odds.

Silas pushed himself to his feet, his joints cracking. “You look half-starved, girl. I’ve got a pot of rabbit stew on the stove. And I’ve a great deal of news to tell, if you’ve a mind to listen.”

He turned and began the slow walk towards the vicarage. Elara followed, her rifle now feeling clumsy and unnecessary. She looked back for a moment, towards the high ridge that separated her valley from this one. The silence of Little Bisley was still there, waiting for her. But it was just a place now, not a prison. The sound of the bell had broken the spell. She was no longer alone. She walked towards the smoke and the promise of a voice, and for the first time in a year, she did not feel afraid.
