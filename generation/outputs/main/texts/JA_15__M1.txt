Mr. Edward Harcourt had been in town for a fortnight, and was already convinced that London was the only place in the world where a man of sense could properly exist, provided he had the means to enjoy it. This last condition was, in Edward’s case, a matter of some delicacy. His fortune was of that respectable mediocrity which allows for a gentleman’s education and his tailor’s bills, but little else; and he had come to stay with his cousin, the wealthy and fashionable Mr. Davenport, in the hope that a season in town might somehow improve his prospects. He had not, however, anticipated how rapidly a man’s principles could be eroded by the atmosphere of Mayfair.

It was at a ball given by a Lady Pembrooke, in a house so grand it seemed to apologise for the modesty of its neighbours, that he first saw Miss Augusta Fairweather. She was standing near a window, engaged in conversation with a dowager of formidable aspect, and Edward, who had been observing the assembly with the critical eye of a man who could afford to admire but not to participate, found his attention irresistibly fixed upon her. She was not, perhaps, strictly handsome; her features lacked the fashionable hauteur of the other young ladies, and her smile was a little too ready, a little too generous for the measured civilities of the room. Yet there was an air of intelligence and good-humour about her that was infinitely more captivating than the practiced indifference of her companions.

Davenport, following his cousin’s gaze, supplied the necessary intelligence. “Ah, the Fairweather girl,” he said, with the air of a man revealing a secret of state. “Granddaughter of old Sir Giles. A vast fortune, I am told. The old man was in trade, you know, but of the most respectable kind. She will have twenty thousand pounds, at the very least, and an estate in Hampshire. A prize, Edward, if ever there was one.”

Edward felt a peculiar warmth spread through his chest. A prize, indeed. He was not a mercenary man, but neither was he a fool; he knew the world in which he lived. A man in his position could not afford to marry for love alone, however pleasant the notion might be in the abstract. Here, then, was a girl who was both charming and an heiress. It seemed a combination too fortunate to be real.

A formal introduction was soon effected, and Edward found Miss Fairweather’s conversation to be as agreeable as her appearance. She spoke with a lively sense of humour and a quiet good sense that seemed at odds with her youth. When he asked her opinion of the London season, she replied with a smile, “It is a great deal of trouble taken in pursuit of a great deal of pleasure, is it not? I confess I find the country much more restful.”

“Then you must find this all very fatiguing,” said Edward, who was privately of the same opinion.

“Oh, not at all,” she replied, her eyes sparkling. “It is amusing to observe. One learns a great deal about people in a ballroom that one would never discover in a drawing-room.” She said this with such a knowing little look that Edward felt she had seen right through the polished surfaces of the company to the follies beneath.

He was, from that moment, quite undone. For Augusta’s part, her own assessment had been made with similar swiftness. Mr. Harcourt was handsome, well-mannered, and possessed of an agreeable, thoughtful air. He was a cousin of Mr. Davenport, and it was universally understood that the Davenports were immensely rich. It was therefore a natural, if not entirely logical, assumption that a relation of such a man must also be in comfortable circumstances. She saw in him a prospect of security and companionship, a gentle escape from the mercenary calculations of her own family, who were determined to see her married to the highest bidder. That Mr. Harcourt seemed to value her for herself, and not for the twenty thousand pounds she was supposed to possess, was a sentiment she found deeply touching.

Their acquaintance ripened with the rapidity of a London spring. They met at a concert, where they found themselves in agreement that the soprano’s voice was more powerful than beautiful; at an exhibition of paintings, where they shared a quiet laugh over a portrait of a pug dog in a wig; and at a series of dinners where they were conveniently seated near one another. In all this time, the subject of fortune was, by a mutual and unspoken consent, carefully avoided. Edward spoke of his modest life in the country and his hopes of a small government appointment, framing his prospects in the most favourable light he could manage without resorting to actual falsehood. Augusta spoke of her family’s estate, but never of its value, describing her home with an affection that Edward mistook for the fondness of a wealthy proprietor.

The moment of crisis arrived one afternoon in Hyde Park. They were walking together, a little apart from Davenport and Augusta’s formidable aunt, Mrs. Croft, when the conversation turned to the future.

“I find I shall be very sorry to leave town,” Edward said, with a sudden, painful sincerity. “I have never been so happy.”

Augusta’s heart gave a little flutter. “And I,” she admitted, her gaze fixed on the distant Serpentine. “My aunt says we shall return to Hampshire very soon. She finds the season excessively costly.”

Edward seized upon this opening, believing it to be a gentle hint for him to state his own position. “I can well believe it,” he said, with a nervous laugh. “London is a place designed to ruin a man. I confess, my own finances are of the most straitened kind. I must be careful, indeed.” He looked at her earnestly, hoping she would see the humility of his situation and esteem him for his honesty.

Augusta, however, heard only a lover’s modesty. He was a man of means, of course, but he was too gentlemanly to boast of it. “I am sure you are too sensible to be extravagant,” she said softly. “My family, I am afraid, has not always been so prudent. My grandfather made his money, you see, and my father has been very determined to spend it. I am often told that I must marry well.” She looked up at him, her eyes full of a hopeful anxiety.

Edward’s heart sank. Marry well. The phrase echoed in his mind, a clear and unmistakable reference to the necessity of a large fortune. He felt a cold dread descend upon him. He, with his five hundred a year, was no such prospect. To declare his love now would be an act of supreme presumption, an attempt to secure a rich wife under false pretences. His honour, he felt, would not permit it.

He became suddenly distant and formal. “Your family is right to be concerned for your welfare,” he said, his voice stiff. “A settlement in life is a matter of the greatest importance.”

Augusta, misinterpreting his reserve as a sign that he was about to make a formal proposal and was merely gathering his courage, felt a wave of relief. “Indeed,” she murmured, “it is. But I believe a mutual affection is the truest foundation.”

Edward could bear no more. He mumbled an apology about a prior engagement and took his leave, leaving Augusta in a state of bewildered distress. She had thought they had an understanding; now he was fleeing from her as if she were a creditor.

For the next three days, he avoided all the places where she might be found. Davenport, observing his cousin’s miserable countenance, at last cornered him in the library. “What the devil is the matter with you, Edward? You look as though you’ve lost your last friend.”

“I have lost something far more precious,” Edward replied, staring into the fire. “I have made a fool of myself. I have formed an attachment to a woman I cannot possibly marry.”

“Miss Fairweather? Why ever not? Her grandfather was a merchant, I grant you, but the money is as good as any.”

“Precisely!” Edward exclaimed. “The money is the whole point! She is an heiress, Davenport, and I am a pauper. She requires a man who can ‘marry well,’ and I am not he. To have spoken would have been to behave like a fortune-hunter.”

Davenport stared at him for a long moment before a slow smile spread across his face. “Good God, Edward. You are a fool, but not for the reason you suppose. Did you never think to ask who old Sir Giles Fairweather was?”

“He was in trade,” said Edward, confused.

“He was in trade in Birmingham,” Davenport said, with rising amusement. “He made his fortune in buttons. A very grand fortune, to be sure, and he spent most of it on a house he could not afford and a wife with expensive tastes. Augusta has perhaps five thousand pounds to her name, and not a shilling more. The rest is all talk and a mountain of debt.”

The world seemed to tilt on its axis. Edward stared at his cousin, the full, ridiculous truth dawning upon him. He had refused to speak of love to a woman of modest means because he had believed her to be rich, and she had believed him to be rich because he was a cousin of Davenport’s. They had both been too proud and too embarrassed to speak the plain truth of their own circumstances.

“And Augusta,” he began, a wild hope taking root in his chest, “does she know…?”

“She knows she is not an heiress, certainly. But she has been led to believe you are a man of consequence. She thinks your modesty is a charming affectation.”

Edward did not wait to hear more. He all but ran from the house, hailing the first hackney carriage he saw and directing it to the Fairweather’s residence in Grosvenor Square. He found Augusta in the drawing-room, looking pale and wan. Without preamble, he blurted out the whole absurd story.

When he had finished, she did not speak for a long moment. Then, a small, incredulous laugh escaped her lips. “Five thousand pounds,” she said. “And I thought you a man of fortune.”

“And I thought you an heiress,” Edward confessed, taking her hand. “We are both a pair of deceivers, it seems.”

“But a pair of happy ones, I hope,” she whispered, her eyes shining with unshed tears.

He knelt before her, not as a suitor for a prize, but as a man offering his whole heart to the woman he loved. And Augusta, seeing him not as a solution to her family’s anxieties, but as the one person who had valued her for herself, accepted him with a joy that was all the more profound for having been so nearly lost. They had begun their acquaintance on a foundation of mutual error, but they found, to their great satisfaction, that a marriage built upon such a comical misunderstanding could not fail to be a source of endless amusement for the rest of their lives.
