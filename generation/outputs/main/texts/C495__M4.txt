The cab from Richmond station deposited her at the corner of Marchmont Road, and Sylvia stood for a moment on the pavement, her trunk at her feet, looking at the house she had not seen in three years. It was a tall, narrow brick house, one of a terrace of identical sisters, with a bay window on the ground floor and a small front garden where the laurels grew with the same depressing vigour she remembered. The gate, she noticed, still sagged on its hinges, and the brass knocker—her father’s pride—was still polished to a hostile gleam. Nothing had changed. The very air, smelling of damp earth and coal smoke, seemed to have been bottled and stoppered since she had left it.

Her mother opened the door before she had reached the top step, her hands already fluttering to her apron strings. “Sylvia, dear. You’re earlier than we expected.” She offered her cheek, cool and powdery, and Sylvia kissed it, smelling lavender water and the faint, familiar undertow of the kitchen.

“The train was quick,” Sylvia said.

“Well. Come in, come in, you’ll catch your death.”

The hall was dim and narrow, the same grandfather clock ticking in the same corner, the same barometer hanging by the door, the same umbrella stand with the same three umbrellas. In the drawing room, her father was in his chair by the fire, the newspaper folded precisely on his knee. He lowered it and regarded her over his spectacles.

“Sylvia. You’ve got thin.”

“It’s the fashion, Papa.”

“Fashion,” he said, and returned to his paper. That was the whole of the welcome, and she had expected nothing more. She stood in the centre of the room, still wearing her hat, and felt the silence settle on her like a coat too heavy for the weather. At Cambridge, there had been talk at every meal, argument, laughter, the clatter of ideas. Here, the loudest sound was the clock.

The days that followed were a slow drowning. Her mother had a list of tasks for her—the mending, the preserving, the calls upon Mrs. Pemberton at number fourteen and old Mrs. Havers at number nine. Sylvia did them all, and each one felt like a small stone added to a pocket. She had come down from Girton with a degree in history and a head full of the suffrage meetings she had attended, the debates in the Union, the fierce, exhilarating certainty that the world was a place to be argued with. She had come home to a house where the only argument was whether to have the fire lit before six.

“You’ll be wanting to find a young man now,” her mother said one afternoon, over the ironing. “There’s the new curate at St. Luke’s. Very well spoken.”

“I don’t want a curate, Mama.”

“Well, what do you want, then?”

Sylvia did not answer. She did not know how to explain that she wanted to be useful in a way that could be measured, that she wanted to write, perhaps, or to teach, or to stand on a platform and say things that mattered. She wanted a life that was not this—this endless, soft, suffocating round of small duties and smaller talk.

It was on the Thursday that she met Arthur. He was the son of her father’s partner, and he came to dinner, as he had come to dinner every few months since she was a girl. He was thirty, with a round face and a careful manner, and he spoke to her of the weather and the price of coal and the new motor-car his cousin had bought. After dinner, when her parents had retired to the drawing room, he walked with her in the garden, among the laurels, and told her that he had always admired her.

“You’re different from the other girls,” he said. “You’ve got a mind.”

“Have I?” she said.

“I’ve a good practice now,” he went on. “A house in Kew. You wouldn’t want for anything.”

She looked at him—at his kind, dull face, his neatly trimmed moustache, his hands that had never done a day’s hard work—and felt a wave of something that was not quite contempt, but close to it. He was not a bad man. He was simply a man who had never once, in his whole life, been required to think a new thought. And she understood, in that moment, that if she stayed, she would marry him. She would become the mistress of a house in Kew, and she would have children, and she would spend her days ordering meals and arranging flowers and listening to the clock tick. The world would go on outside, and she would be inside, safe and small and slowly disappearing.

That night, she could not sleep. She sat at the window of her old bedroom, the one with the view of the laurels, and looked out at the street lamp hissing in the dark. She thought of her friends at Girton—Margaret, who was going to London to work for a publisher; Eleanor, who was training as a doctor; Hattie, who was already speaking at meetings, her voice ringing out in halls where women were not supposed to speak. They were out there, doing things, changing things. And she was here, in Richmond, in a house that smelled of lavender and coal smoke, where nothing had changed in twenty years.

The decision came to her not as a sudden revelation but as a slow, steady certainty, like water finding its level. She would leave. She would go to London. She had a little money saved from her allowance, and Margaret had written to say there was a room in her lodgings, and there was work—proofreading, teaching, something. It would be hard. It would be frightening. But it would be hers.

In the morning, she told her mother. Mrs. Ashby was at the kitchen table, shelling peas, and she did not look up at first. When she did, her face was not angry but puzzled, as though Sylvia had announced she intended to fly to the moon.

“But what will you do?” she said. “Where will you go?”

“To London. To work.”

“Work,” her mother repeated, as if the word were a foreign coin. “But you’re a lady, Sylvia. You’ve been to university. You don’t need to work.”

“I need to do something, Mama. I can’t stay here.”

“Your father will be very upset.”

“I know.”

And he was. He stood in the hall, his face red, and told her she was ungrateful, that she had been given every advantage, that she was throwing herself away on a foolish whim. She listened, and she did not argue, because she knew that he could not hear her. He was not speaking to her, really; he was speaking to the world he had built, the world where daughters stayed and married curates and kept their thoughts to themselves. She was a crack in the wall of that world, and he could not bear to look at her.

She packed her trunk that afternoon. Her mother stood in the doorway, her hands twisting in her apron, and said nothing. Sylvia kissed her goodbye, and her mother held her for a moment longer than usual, and then let her go.

The cab came at four. Sylvia stood on the pavement, her trunk at her feet, and looked back at the house. The laurels rustled in the wind. The brass knocker gleamed. The clock inside was ticking, she knew, ticking away the same seconds it had always ticked, and would go on ticking long after she was gone. She felt no sorrow, only a strange, light emptiness, as though she had set down a weight she had not known she was carrying.

“Where to, miss?” the cabman said.

“Richmond station,” she said, and climbed in. The cab pulled away, and the house grew smaller, and she did not look back again.
