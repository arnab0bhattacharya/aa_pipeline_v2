It was a winter such as I trust never to see again. The frost lay upon the moor like a white shroud, and the wind, coming down from the high fells, carried the bite of the grave. In the valley of Netherfold, where the great mill of Mr. Silas Croft stood silent as a tomb, the very air seemed frozen with hunger. The looms, which had once clattered with a life of their own, were still; and in the cottages that clung to the hillside, the silence was a heavier thing—the silence of empty bellies and waiting death.

My brother, Mr. Croft, viewed this suffering from his study window with a grim, unyielding eye. He was a man of iron principle, and his heart, if he possessed one, was encased in the armour of commerce and war. “It is the blockade, Elspeth,” he would say to me, his voice as hard as the mill-stones in the yard. “Bonaparte has closed the ports to our cotton. How can I weave what I cannot get? How can I pay for what I cannot sell? These men must bear it. We all must bear it.”

But the men, it seemed, could bear it no longer. One afternoon, when the sky was the colour of a bruise and the first flakes of a new storm began to fall, they came. Not as a mob, but as a procession of ghosts, their faces gaunt and their thin coats inadequate against the cold. They stood in the yard, a silent, shivering multitude, and their spokesman, a weaver named Jedediah Holt, knocked upon the great door.

I watched from the landing as my brother received them in the hall. He did not invite them in from the biting wind.

“What is it you want?” he demanded, his voice ringing with impatience.

“Work, Mr. Croft,” said Holt, his own voice hoarse. “We are starving. Our children cry for bread. Give us work, and we will ask for nothing more.”

“Work?” My brother laughed, a short, bitter sound. “Look around you, man. The looms are idle for want of cotton. Do you think I conjure it from the air? The Emperor’s ships stand between us and our supply. It is a fact of war, and no man can alter it.”

“Then give us credit,” another man cried out from the back. “A little meal, a little coal to see us through!”

“Credit!” my brother roared, his temper flaring. “I have extended credit until my own accounts are in peril. I am a manufacturer, not a parish almoner. Charity is the business of the church, not of trade. Now be gone from my property before I have the constable fetch you.”

He slammed the door upon them, the sound echoing in the cold hall like a pistol shot. I stood motionless on the stairs, my hands clenched, a hot shame for my own flesh and blood warring with a pity so fierce it felt like a fire in my breast. He came back into the hall, his face flushed.

“Pray do not look at me so, Elspeth,” he snapped. “It is not my doing. It is the will of Providence and the folly of the French.”

“Is it the will of Providence that a child should starve when there is bread in this house?” I asked, my voice quieter than I had intended, yet sharp with a feeling I could not suppress.

He stared at me, his eyes narrowing. “Do not be a fool. You know nothing of the world. If I were to feed every idle hand in Netherfold, I should be a beggar myself within a month. The strong must endure, and the weak… the weak must fall by the way. It is the natural order.”

He turned and went into his study, shutting the door with a decisive click. The natural order. The words echoed in the silent house. I looked at the great, cold fireplace in the hall, at the portraits of our stern ancestors on the walls. This, then, was the order they had built: a world of plenty and of want, divided by a single locked door.

That night, the storm broke in earnest. Snow fell thick and fast, burying the world in a soundless white. I could not sleep. I saw in my mind’s eye the faces of the weavers, and I heard the imagined cries of their children. My brother’s logic was a fortress of ice, but my heart was a thing of flesh and blood, and it would not be frozen.

When the house was deep in slumber, I rose. I took a large basket and went down to the larder. There were loaves of fine white bread, a great cheese, a crock of butter, and a half-ham. I wrapped the food in a clean cloth, my hands trembling, not with cold, but with the audacity of my purpose. This was a theft, a betrayal of my brother’s will. Yet, as I worked, I felt not guilt, but a strange and righteous calm. It was written, I thought, that man shall not live by bread alone; but I was certain he could not live without it.

I wrapped myself in my thickest shawl and cloak, lifted the heavy bar on the kitchen door, and stepped out into the tempest. The wind tore at me, and the snow blinded me, but I pressed on, my feet sinking deep with every step. I knew the cottages of the weavers, huddled together against the churchyard wall. The first one I reached was that of Jedediah Holt. A faint glimmer of light showed from a single tallow candle within.

I did not knock. I lifted the latch, which yielded with no sound, and pushed the door open. Inside, the cold was almost as great as without. A man and a woman sat huddled by a grate where only a few embers glowed. Their eyes, wide with shock and disbelief, fixed upon me. On a pallet in the corner, a small child coughed in its sleep.

I said not a word. I walked to the bare table, unwrapped my bundle, and laid the bread and the cheese upon it. The woman let out a small, broken sound, part sob, part prayer. The man, Holt, rose to his feet, his face a mask of astonishment.

“Miss Croft…” he began.

“Hush,” I whispered, my own voice thick with emotion. “Feed your child.”

I turned and fled back into the storm, leaving the door ajar. I went to another cottage, and another, repeating my silent errand. It was a sacrament of the night, a holy act performed in the heart of the blizzard. I was an angel of mercy, a thief in the dark—I knew not what I was, save that I was doing what was right.

When I returned to the great house, numb with cold but strangely warm within, I secured the door and crept back to my room. The snow was already covering my tracks. No one would ever know. My brother would continue to speak of the blockade and the natural order, and I would listen and serve him his tea. But I would know. And in that knowledge, in the memory of the bread on that bare table and the light in that poor woman’s eyes, I had found a truth more powerful than any of his principles. The storm outside was nothing to the one that had passed within me, and I felt, for the first time in my life, that I had found my proper element.
