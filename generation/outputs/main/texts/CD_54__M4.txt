The town of Cinderhaugh, in the county of Lancashire, presents to the traveller who enters it by the northern road a spectacle at once so striking and so monotonous, so black and yet so fiery, that the mind is divided between admiration of its industry and a certain inclination to hold its nose. Tall chimneys, of a lean and spectral aspect, rise in every quarter, like a company of giants assembled for some gloomy consultation, and from their summits issue perpetual columns of smoke, which the wind, in its caprice, twists into wreaths, bows, and coronets, or trails across the sky in long funereal pennons. The air is filled with a fine soot, which settles upon the washing, upon the window-sills, and upon the countenances of the inhabitants, giving to the youngest child the complexion of a veteran collier; and the river, which winds through the lower part of the town, is of that rich and varied hue which the dyer’s art imparts, being sometimes purple, sometimes a deep indigo, and occasionally, when the works are idle, of a pale and sickly yellow, like the face of a man who has been long in want of his dinner.

It was in this town, at the sign of the Jolly Founders, kept by one Mrs. Prudence Gubbins, a widow of considerable presence and a voice of extraordinary power, that there alighted, upon a certain Saturday afternoon in the autumn of the year, a travelling theatrical company, whose fortunes were at that moment at so low an ebb that the very property-man had been heard to declare he would sell his thunder for a loaf of bread, and would throw in the lightning into the bargain. The company consisted of four persons: Mr. Horatio Bellweather, the manager, a gentleman of a florid countenance and a rolling eye, who had been, in his time, everything by turns and nothing long, and who spoke of his engagements at Covent Garden in a manner that would have deceived anybody but his wife; Mrs. Bellweather, the leading lady, who had once been pretty, and who kept her tragedy queen’s dagger in the same bandbox with her curling-tongs; Miss Amelia Bellweather, their daughter, a pale, thin girl of sixteen, with a voice of singular sweetness and a habit of looking at the ground when spoken to; and Mr. Timothy Ticklepenny, the low comedian, a small man with a large face, who had a joke for every occasion, and who, being the only member of the company who could drive a nail or mend a harness, was regarded by his associates as a species of universal genius.

The company had come to Cinderhaugh in a cart, drawn by a horse of a philosophical turn of mind, who had long since ceased to be surprised at anything, and who now stood in the yard of the Jolly Founders with his head sunk upon his breast, as if meditating upon the vanity of human wishes. The cart, in turning into the yard, had broken its axle; and Mr. Ticklepenny, having examined the fracture with the air of a surgeon pronouncing upon a mortal case, had declared that it could not be mended for three days, and that the cost of the mending would be no less than fifteen shillings.

‘Fifteen shillings!’ cried Mr. Bellweather, striking his forehead. ‘Fifteen shillings, and our treasury at this moment contains—Ticklepenny, what does our treasury contain?’

‘A button, sir,’ replied Mr. Ticklepenny, ‘and a piece of gingerbread, which I believe to be the property of Miss Amelia.’

‘Then,’ said Mr. Bellweather, with the dignity of a man who has made up his mind to a great effort, ‘we must act. We must appeal to the town. We must put forth our best. We must play—what shall we play?’

A consultation ensued, in which the resources of the company were passed in review. The Castle Spectre was suggested, but the ghost was wanting; The Miller’s Daughter was proposed, but the mill was wanting; and it was at length resolved, after much deliberation, that the company should produce a new and original drama, in three acts, entitled The Tyrant of the Loom; or, The Weaver’s Wrongs, written by Mr. Bellweather himself, and founded upon a story which he had read in a newspaper, of a certain mill owner who had ground the faces of the poor, and had been visited by a retribution so terrible that the very reporter had been unable to describe it without a shudder.

Now, it so happened that the principal mill in Cinderhaugh, a vast and gloomy pile, situated at the lower end of the town, and known as the Arkwright Arms, or, more familiarly, the Bastille, was the property of one Mr. Josiah Grindley, a man of great wealth and of a disposition so hard that it was currently reported he had once refused a subscription of a shilling to a fund for providing the poor with blankets, on the ground that blankets encouraged habits of luxury. Mr. Grindley had heard of the intended performance, and, being a man of a suspicious and tyrannical turn, had caused a notice to be posted at the gates of his mill, which ran as follows:—

‘NOTICE. The hands employed in these works are hereby informed that any person or persons attending the performance advertised to take place at the Jolly Founders, in this town, will be dismissed from their employment, without wages, upon the following morning. By order, J. GRINDLEY.’

The effect of this notice was prodigious. The town was in a ferment. The hands, who had been looking forward to the play with the eagerness of people to whom any amusement is a godsend, were divided between a longing for the drama and a fear of the sack. The more timid stayed away; the more adventurous, headed by one Nicholas Nibble, a weaver of a speculative turn, who had been heard to say that a man might as well be hanged for a sheep as a lamb, resolved to attend; and the result was that when the evening came, the room at the Jolly Founders was filled with a dense and dusky audience, whose faces, in the light of the tallow candles that served for footlights, looked like a collection of copper coins.

The play went off with great spirit. Mr. Bellweather, as the Tyrant of the Loom, was awful in his cruelty; Mrs. Bellweather, as the injured weaver’s wife, wept and wrung her hands with a pathos that drew tears from the eyes of the most hardened; and Miss Amelia, as the weaver’s daughter, sang a song of such melting sweetness that the very candles seemed to flicker with emotion. But the crowning triumph of the evening was Mr. Ticklepenny, who, having undertaken the part of a comic mill-hand, introduced into the piece a variety of jokes and songs and dances that had nothing whatever to do with the story, but which kept the audience in a roar of laughter from the first act to the last.

It was in the third act, when the Tyrant, having been visited by a succession of misfortunes, was about to be struck by lightning, that a singular circumstance occurred. Mr. Bellweather, standing in the centre of the stage, with his arms folded and his eyes rolling, was in the very height of his declamation, when a voice from the back of the room cried out, ‘That’s Grindley! That’s Grindley to the life!’ and instantly the whole audience, as if moved by a common impulse, rose to their feet, and, pointing at the stage, shouted, ‘Grindley! Grindley! The Tyrant of the Loom!’

Mr. Bellweather, who had no notion of what was meant, was at first inclined to be angry; but, catching the eye of Mr. Ticklepenny, who was making frantic signs to him to take the hint, he bowed, and said, with great presence of mind, ‘Ladies and gentlemen, I thank you. The character is drawn from life.’ This speech was received with thunders of applause; and the play concluded amid a scene of enthusiasm that the oldest inhabitant declared he had never witnessed.

But the triumph of the evening was not yet complete. As the audience were dispersing, a messenger arrived in breathless haste from the Bastille, with the intelligence that Mr. Grindley, who had been sitting up in his counting-house, had been seized with a sudden and alarming illness, and that the doctor had been sent for in all directions. The news spread like wildfire; and it was currently reported, and believed, that the Tyrant of the Loom had been struck down by the very lightning that Mr. Bellweather had invoked upon the stage.

Whether Mr. Grindley recovered, and whether the hands were dismissed, and whether the axle was mended, and whether the company departed in triumph or in debt, I am unable to state with certainty; for the annals of Cinderhaugh are silent upon these points. But I have been told that Mr. Ticklepenny, in after years, was accustomed to say that he had never played to a better house, and that Miss Amelia’s song was encored three times; and that Mr. Bellweather, whenever he told the story, which he did upon every possible occasion, would add, with a wave of his hand, ‘It was the greatest triumph of my professional career; and I attribute it entirely to the fact that we played the piece with a broken axle.’
