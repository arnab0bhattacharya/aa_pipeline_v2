Lord Augustus Dornton had retired from the Diplomatic Service with a reputation for having unravelled the knottiest intrigues of the European courts. Society asserted that he had done so by profound statesmanship; his valet knew he had done so by listening at keyholes. He now inhabited a narrow house in Half Moon Street, a house whose silken walls and dim, elaborate bronzes seemed to exhale the very atmosphere of a well-bred secret. He was seventy years old, and had concluded that life was merely a poorly written French farce, performed by Englishmen who had entirely forgotten their lines.

Into this sanctuary of cultivated weariness came his great-nephew, young Cyril Avalon. Cyril was twenty-one, possessed of a distressing income, an entirely unblemished character, and a complexion that reminded Lord Augustus of a sunlit apricot. He was, in short, the sort of youth who is inevitably murdered in Italian operas, or married in English drawing-rooms.

“My dear boy,” said Lord Augustus, adjusting a paste buckle upon his velvet slipper, “you have come to ask me how to succeed in London. I can see the terrible question trembling on your lips. It is the one question a gentleman should never ask, primarily because the answer is entirely unfit for repetition.”

“But I am so tired of being nobody,” cried Cyril, dropping into a chair with the fluid grace of the very young. “I go to balls, and I stand against the wall. I make remarks, and they are received with the sort of silence usually reserved for the reading of a will. I want to be sought after. I want to be a power.”

“You wish to be talked about,” murmured Lord Augustus, peering through his lorgnette at a small stain upon his Persian rug. “To be talked about is to be compromised. To be compromised is to be interesting. The English public is fiercely moral, and it forgives everything but stupidity. Therefore, if you wish to conquer society, you must say exactly the opposite of what you mean, at all times, and to all people.”

Cyril looked bewildered. “The opposite, sir?”

“It is the first principle of diplomacy,” continued Lord Augustus, his thin, pallid fingers tapping a slow rhythm upon his ebony cane. “If you find a woman beautiful, tell her that she resembles a wet Wednesday in November. Women are accustomed to crude adulation; they are entirely defenceless against elegant insult. If you are bored at a dinner party, remark to your hostess that you are having the time of your life. People are far more interested in the manner in which a lie is told than in the truth of the statement itself. Truth is entirely without style. It is raw, and rough, and invariably in bad taste. But a paradox! A paradox is a well-cut coat; it conceals the commonplace nature of the body beneath it. Try it, my boy. Go into the world and be systematically insincere.”

Cyril, having nothing in his head but a few mistaken classical allusions and a profound desire for social eminence, took the advice with the awful seriousness of the very young. He departed from Half Moon Street a missionary of inversion.

The following Tuesday, at the Duchess of Harley’s crushing reception, Cyril found himself standing before Lady Agatha Carstairs. Lady Agatha was the reigning beauty of the season, a girl with eyes like sapphires and a heart like a very small, very hard pebble. Men usually told her that her smile was a sunrise. She was thoroughly tired of hearing it.

Cyril approached her, his heart beating wildly, and remembered Lord Augustus’s precept. “I suppose you know,” he said, with a composure that astonished him, “that your gown is the exact colour of a bruise, and that your conversational manner is as exhausting as a wet afternoon in Margate.”

Lady Agatha stared at him. She had been bracing herself for a poetic compliment. The shock was so violent that she blushed a deep, furious crimson. Then, suddenly, she laughed—a clear, ringing laugh that caused three elderly peers to drop their glasses of warm champagne.

“How perfectly extraordinary of you,” she cried. “You are the rudest person I have met this year. I am excessively glad to meet you.” She gave him her fan to hold, a recognized token of favor, and smiled upon him with an approval that was quite unprecedented.

Emboldened by this terrifying success, Cyril proceeded to apply the theory to the wider world. When the elderly and immensely wealthy Marquis of Fleetwood asked Cyril’s opinion on a notoriously dull political speech, Cyril replied, “I found it so thrilling, my lord, that I was obliged to be carried to my carriage in a state of suspended animation. It was the finest piece of utter nonsense I have ever had the privilege of surviving.” The Marquis, who had slept through the speech himself, was so delighted by the youth’s audacity that he invited Cyril to his country seat for the shooting.

If Cyril heard a celebrated tenor sing abominably out of tune, he would seek out the singer and assure him that his voice was a miracle of acoustic architecture, though perhaps lacking in actual melody. If he was introduced to a hostess whose house was notoriously gloomy, he congratulated her on creating an atmosphere of profound despair, which was far more fashionable than mere comfort. Society, which is always eager to be told anything provided it is said with an air of finality, began to look upon him as a wit of the first water.

Lord Augustus, hearing of these triumphs from the safety of his velvet chair, permitted himself a thin, sardonic smile. “The boy is doing very well,” he murmured to his valet. “He is proving that society is a mirror, and reflects only the grotesque. It is delightful to see one’s theories put into practice by an instrument so entirely devoid of intellect.”

By the end of the season, Cyril Avalon was the fashion. He was invited everywhere. Women confided their secrets to him, precisely because he assured them he had no intention of keeping them. Men sought his advice on their marital troubles, which he gave by advising them to immediately purchase a muzzle and a one-way ticket to the colonies. His very absurdities were quoted in the evening papers as profound epigrams. He was, in short, a complete success.

He returned to Half Moon Street one damp November afternoon, wrapped in a fur coat that was slightly too large for him, looking precisely like a young Roman emperor who had strayed into the wrong empire.

“Well,” said Lord Augustus, from the depths of his armchair, “and how does the great social conqueror find his empire? I am told you are the most sought-after young man in London. I hear that Lady Agatha Carstairs is practically engaged to you, and that you have offended the Prime Minister so brilliantly that he is offering you a seat in the Cabinet. You have followed my advice to the letter, I presume?”

“I have, sir,” said Cyril, a trifle wearily. “I have said the opposite of what I meant to every person I have met. I have told the truth only to you. And I must confess, the results have been astonishing. But I am beginning to find it a little exhausting.”

“Exhaustion is merely the body’s protest against the soul’s affectations,” said Lord Augustus. “But tell me, how did you manage Lady Agatha? She is notoriously unassailable. Her heart is made of excellent British ice.”

Cyril smiled, a slow, terrible smile that suddenly reminded Lord Augustus of someone he could not quite place. “When she told me she loved me,” said Cyril, “I replied that I found her entirely repulsive, and that my one ambition was to flee the country and never see her face again. She wept, declared I was the only man who understood her, and accepted me on the spot. We are to be married in the spring. But I must tell you something else, sir. Something rather odd.”

“Oddity is the only excuse for existence,” murmured Lord Augustus. “Pray continue.”

“When I began,” said Cyril, crossing to the fireplace and looking down at the glowing coals, “I was, as you know, a very honest young man. I had the most tedious, genuine feelings. But to succeed, I had to express their exact opposite. I told Lady Agatha I despised her, when I actually admired her. I told the Marquis his speeches were nonsense, when I actually thought them rather clever. But lately, sir, a strange thing has happened. I have been saying the opposite of what I mean for so long that I have quite forgotten what it was I originally meant. When I told Lady Agatha I found her repulsive, I was merely reciting a formula. But last night, when I looked at her, I realized I actually do find her repulsive. I told the Marquis his speech was nonsense, and I now think it is. I have become my own paradox, sir. The mask has eaten the face.”

Lord Augustus stared at his great-nephew. The youth’s complexion was no longer that of a sunlit apricot; it was paler, sharper, infinitely more sophisticated. There was a cold glitter in his eyes that had not been there in the spring. Lord Augustus suddenly recognized the face he reminded him of. It was his own.

“You have succeeded, my boy,” said Lord Augustus, a faint tremor in his voice. “You have conquered society. You are entirely insincere.”

“Yes,” said Cyril, sitting down opposite him and drawing on a pair of lemon-coloured gloves. “But the tragedy is, I no longer know whether I am being insincere or not. I said the opposite of the truth, and the truth has obligingly altered itself to fit my lies. I am exactly what I pretended to be.”

Lord Augustus was silent for a long time. The fire hissed softly in the grate. Outside, a hansom cab rattled over the wet cobblestones of Mayfair.

“It is a terrible thing,” said Lord Augustus at last, “to create a masterpiece. One always feels a certain responsibility for the damage it does. But you must not be alarmed, Cyril. Society is a bubble, and you have learned to float upon it by being entirely hollow. The only thing left for you now is to be perfectly happy, which you can do by never examining your own motives. Motives are terribly middle-class. A gentleman should only have manners.”

“I suppose so,” said Cyril, rising to leave. “But I shall be glad when I am married. Lady Agatha is very beautiful, and I am sure I shall be able to tell her every day of my life how much I detest her.”

He paused at the door, looking back at the old diplomat with a faint, enigmatic smile. “By the way, sir,” he added, “I told the Duchess of Harley this morning that your house in Half Moon Street was the most delightful place in London, and that you were the wisest and most sincere man I had ever met.”

Lord Augustus shuddered slightly. “And what did she say?” he asked.

“She said she entirely agreed with me,” said Cyril, and vanished into the fog.

Lord Augustus sat quite still, staring at the empty doorway. He had spent fifty years saying the opposite of what he meant, and nobody had ever believed him. Now, his great-nephew had said the opposite of what he meant, and everybody believed him. The difference, he supposed, was that Cyril had actually meant it.

He reached for his lorgnette, examined the small stain upon his Persian rug once more, and sighed. “It is always the young who take one seriously,” he murmured to the empty room. “And it is always the serious who are taken in by the young.”
