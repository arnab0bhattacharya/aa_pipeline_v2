It is in the nature of human affairs that a man’s reputation for incorruptible honesty, while procuring him the general esteem of his fellows, not infrequently serves to entangle him in the very web of consequences he would most zealously avoid. For the villagers of Chilvers Coton, in the year eighteen hundred and thirty-two, there was no more fixed moral astronomical point than Josiah Bunnett, the wheelwright. His workshop, situated at the convergence of the lane leading to Asthall Manor and the main thoroughfare, stood as a testament to a thoroughgoing rectitude that left no room for the comfortable equivocations of commerce. Josiah was a man whose broad, earth-coloured hands were more accustomed to the resistance of seasoned ash and elm than to the delicate balancing of social claims; and his mind, moving with the same deliberate, unyielding pace as his lathe, perceived duty not as a complex algebra of competing sympathies, but as a plain, unvarnished fact, much like the grain of the timber he daily shaped.

It was into this uncomplicated moral atmosphere that a sudden chill was introduced on a damp October morning, when Mr. Gresham, the squire’s steward, entered the workshop with an errand that served to disturb the entire parish. Mr. Gresham was a man of sharp legalities, whose respect for the squire’s rent-roll far exceeded his interest in the domestic economies of the village poor. He had come to require Josiah’s professional judgment on a matter of distraint: a valuation of the cart and associated implements belonging to Simon Folds, a small tenant farmer who had fallen into arrears that were as deep and as fatal as the winter mud.

“You will go over to Folds’ place this afternoon, Bunnett,” Mr. Gresham said, in the clipped, superior tone that marked his dealings with the village artisans. “The squire is within his rights, and we must have a fair and proper valuation for the seizure. I need not tell you that the squire relies on your judgment. You are known to be a man who would not shave the truth by a farthing to save either friend or foe.”

Josiah, wiping the shavings from his leather apron, looked steadily at the steward. “I’ll go, Mr. Gresham. But I mun tell ye now, I’ll value the cart as I see it, wi’ no mind to who’s bid me come. I’ll not put a finger on the scale.”

“Ay, ay, that is precisely why I am here,” the steward returned, impatiently. “Only see that you are not overly generous. The law requires an accurate assessment, not a parson’s charity.” With that, Mr. Gresham departed, leaving behind him a scent of damp broadcloth and an atmosphere of impending catastrophe.

The news that Josiah Bunnett was to value Simon Folds’ cart for a debt seizure spread through Chilvers Coton with the rapid, invisible diffusion of a miasmatic vapor. By noon, the village was in a state of suspended animation. At the Plough Inn, the blacksmith and the harness-maker debated the outcome over their ale, their voices lowered to a serious murmur. Every soul in the parish knew the precise arithmetic of Simon Folds’ ruin. The cart was his last remaining asset of any substantial worth; if Josiah valued it at its true market price—a sum that would barely cover the squire’s arrears when the auctioneer’s fees were deducted—the seizure would leave Folds and his ailing wife destitute. If, however, Josiah were to undervalue it, the sale would not satisfy the debt, and the squire would be provoked, perhaps turning Folds out of his cottage entirely, and bringing down upon Josiah the wrath of Mr. Gresham. But to value it honestly was to pronounce a sentence of utter desolation. The village waited, weighing its respect for Josiah’s probity against its sympathy for Simon’s plight, feeling acutely the tragic insufficiency of human goodness when confronted with the rigid machinery of economic law.

Josiah walked the half-mile to Folds’ smallholding in a state of mental turmoil that was entirely foreign to his usual methodical disposition. He was a man accustomed to dealing with the stubborn, tangible facts of matter—wood that split, iron that held, wheels that ran true—and he found this sudden demand to navigate the treacherous currents of human misery profoundly disquieting. He thought of Simon Folds, a man whose unprosperous struggle with a thin soil was written in the deep lines of his face, and whose lame wife depended on the cart to fetch her meager comforts from the market. To value the cart honestly seemed, in this light, less an act of justice than a cruelty; yet to lie, to falsify his professional judgment, would be to introduce a fatal rot into the very centre of his being. He was not unaware that a merciful falsehood might be justified in the eyes of a sentimental morality; but his mind, constrained by the severe architecture of principle, could find no loophole through which such a falsehood might pass without bringing down the entire edifice of his character. He perceived, with the agonizing clarity of a man who sees too far into the consequences of things, that the choice between truth and mercy was, in this instance, a choice between two distinct modes of ruin.

When he arrived at the Folds’ yard, Simon was standing by the cart, a man whose dejected posture was a more eloquent plea than any rhetorical entreaty could have framed. The cart itself was a sound piece of workmanship, built by Josiah’s own hands five years prior, its iron tires firm, its axle true. It was worth, at a fair estimate, twelve pounds—a sum that, in the ruthless logic of distraint, would strip Simon of his final means of livelihood.

“Thou’st come, then, Josiah,” Simon said, his voice carrying the flat, heavy cadence of the Warwickshire peasant, stripped of all resistance. “I reckon I know what it means. But I’d ask thee to look at the nigh fore-wheel. It’s a bit shaken, loike. Happen it’s not what it was.”

Josiah went to the wheel, kneeling in the mud to examine it. He felt the spokes, tested the iron tire with his heavy hammer, and found, as he had expected, that the wheel was entirely sound. The slight shake Simon spoke of was nothing more than the ordinary wear of a well-used vehicle, easily remedied by a competent wheelwright. He remained kneeling for a long moment, the damp earth soaking through his corduroys, staring at the hub as if it might yield some solution to his moral perplexity. He was acutely conscious of Simon’s gaze upon him, and of the silent, watching presence of the village, which seemed to press in upon the yard from all sides.

“Simon,” Josiah said at last, rising slowly and brushing the mud from his knees, “the wheel’s sound. I built this cart, and I know her worth. I’ll not say she’s faulty when she’s not.”

Simon’s face, already pale, seemed to congeal into a mask of resigned despair. “Ay,” he murmured. “I thought as much. It’s no fault o’ thine, Josiah. The Lord’s will be done.”

“But I’ll tell thee what I’ll do,” Josiah continued, his voice dropping to a gruff, constrained register, as if the words were being forced from him against a great internal pressure. “I’ll value her at eight pound. That’s a low figure, Simon—lower than the market, and Mr. Gresham will know it. But it’s what I’ll write on the paper. And if the squire’s not satisfied, he can send another man to look at it. I’ll not put down twelve pound and see thee stripped to the bone.”

Simon stared at him, a sudden, bewildered hope dawning in his eyes. “But Gresham’ll say thou’st lied, Josiah. He’ll say thou’st gone against the squire.”

“Let him say it,” Josiah replied, a stubborn, almost defiant light entering his steady brown eyes. “I’ve valued the cart as I see fit, for the distress and the wear. If he wants a higher price, let him sell it himself. I’ll not be the man to put the rope round thy neck.”

With that, Josiah turned and walked back towards his workshop, leaving Simon Folds standing alone in the yard, gazing at his cart as if it had been miraculously restored to him. But Josiah himself felt no lightness of heart. He knew that he had, for the first time in his life, deliberately falsified a professional judgment, and that this act, however motivated by compassion, had introduced a fissure in his own moral foundation. He had chosen mercy over truth, and in doing so, had accepted the burden of a lie. The village, which had waited for his verdict, received the news with a quiet, complex satisfaction—a satisfaction that was deeply tinged with an unspoken recognition of the cost at which it had been purchased. For in the intricate, often painful web of provincial life, the preservation of one man’s livelihood had been achieved only at the expense of another man’s conscience; and the villagers, in their obscure, instinctive wisdom, understood that such sacrifices, however necessary, leave a shadow on the soul that no subsequent righteousness can entirely efface.
