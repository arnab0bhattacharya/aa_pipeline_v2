The wind came off the moors, driving a sleet that scoured the cobbles of Whinmoor. It was a wind to strip the flesh from the bone, yet I stood in the very teeth of it, outside the great wooden doors of the Piece Hall, with my ledger clasped to my breast. I was clad in my widow’s black, a stubborn patch of mourning against the grey stone and the greyer sky. I had walked two miles through the mire, for I would not spend the pence on a carrier’s cart. Every farthing was become a solemn trust. The dead rely upon the living to exact their justice.

Within those doors, the air was thick with the reek of damp wool and the acrid smoke of pit-fires. The noise was a strange, discordant hum of barter, where the rough voices of clothiers clashed against the sharper, haggling tones of the merchants. The hall was a vast quadrangle of stone, lined with stalls where the fruits of the loom were spread. I descended the steps into that arena of commerce. I was an alien there. A woman in a mart of men is an anomaly, a creature to be jested at or trodden underfoot. I felt their eyes upon me—hard, inquisitive, contemptuous. I lifted my chin. I had endured the pulpit and the sickbed; I could endure the market-cross.

I sought out Mr. Jonas Ketch, the man who had been my husband’s partner, the architect of his ruin. He stood by a pillar, a broad, heavy-featured man, wrapped in a greatcoat of fine Kersey. He was laughing with a buyer from Leeds, his coarse red face glowing with a coarse contentment. I approached and waited until his eye fell upon me. The laugh died on his lip, replaced by a scowl that drew his heavy brows together.

“Mrs. Thorne,” he said, the greeting falling from his mouth like a stone. “What brings you here? This is no place for a woman’s skirts.”

“I come to inspect my property, Mr. Ketch. I am here to see the cloth sold.”

He gave a short, hard laugh. “Your property! A fourth share in a mill that groans under debt. I told you what to do. Sign the deed of sale and let me take the burden. I will give you fifty pound for your portion, and you may go back to your prayers.”

“You mean, you will give me fifty pound for a share worth three hundred. You cheated my husband out of his profits while he lived, and now you seek to rob his orphan boy. I will not sell. I will learn the business.”

“Learn it!” he sneered. “You, a parson’s widow! Do you think to measure a piece of broadcloth by the yardstick of the Gospel? Go home, woman.”

“I shall not go home. Give me the accounts. I will keep the books.”

He turned away with a muttered curse. But I did not turn. I went to the stall that bore the mill’s mark and stood beside the pieces of cloth, feeling the nap between my thumb and finger. I had spent the long winter nights learning the double-entry, the debit and the credit, the cold arithmetic of commerce. I knew the weight of a pound of wool, and the count of the threads in the warp. I knew the exact measure of his villainy.

A merchant approached, a thin-lipped man from Halifax. He looked at me, then at the cloth.

“What is your price for the twenty pieces?” he asked.

Before I could speak, Ketch was beside me. “Sixteen shillings a piece, Mr. Sowler. The widow is ignorant of the trade.”

“It is eighteen shillings,” I said, my voice low but steady. “The wool is of the finest Spanish sort, and the weave is close.”

Sowler raised his eyebrows. “Eighteen is steep. The cloth is but middling.”

“Look at it,” I said. I unfolded the bolt. “There is no shoddy here. You know the quality, Mr. Sowler.”

He examined it, pulling and stretching. Ketch glared at me. “You will spoil the trade,” he hissed in my ear. “You will ruin us both.”

“I will not be defrauded,” I returned. “You have sold his cloth for a song to your own underlings long enough. That game is played out.”

Sowler looked from one to the other. “I will give seventeen and sixpence,” he said.

I hesitated. It was a fair price. I felt Ketch’s breath, hot and angry, upon my cheek. I thought of my boy, of the bread he must eat, of the school fees I must pay. I thought of my husband, dying in the damp parsonage, worn out by care and Ketch’s treachery. A fire rose in my heart, a flame of fierce, righteous anger. It was a fire that had been kindled in the furnace of affliction, and it burned with a steady, white heat.

“Seventeen and sixpence,” I said. “It is agreed.”

Sowler nodded and moved on. Ketch seized my arm. “You are a fool,” he said.

“I am a fool who has saved her children’s bread from the jaws of the wolf.”

I spent the day in that harsh, unyielding place. I endured the stares, the whispered jests, the open mockery. I was the “she-merchant,” the “parson’s drab.” I heard it all, and I heeded none of it. I sold what cloth we had, driving my bargains with a quiet, unflinching persistence that surprised even myself. I had found a strength I did not know I possessed. It was not the strength of the world, which is loud and boastful, but the strength of the spirit, which endures in silence.

When evening came, the hall emptied. The merchants and buyers departed, leaving behind the smell of wool and the dust of the day. I sat on a wooden bench, my ledger open on my knee. I entered the sales, the sums, the names. The figures marched across the page, a small army of order against the chaos of my life.

The hall grew dark and cold. The great windows above were squares of deep blue, pricked with the early stars. I was weary, my limbs ached, and my hands were black with grime. But in my breast, the fire still burned. I was no longer the helpless widow. I was a woman who had looked injustice in the face and had not quailed.

I closed the book. I stood up. The hall was silent, vast and solemn, like a cathedral of commerce. The pillars rose up into the gloom, and the shadows lay thick upon the floor. I felt a strange, solitary peace. I had fought my battle, and I had not been defeated.

I walked out into the night. The wind had fallen, and the sky was clear. The stars shone down upon the moors, vast and indifferent. I thought of the text I had read that morning: “She girdeth her loins with strength, and strengtheneth her arms.” It was a word given to me, a promise fulfilled. I had girded my loins. I had strengthened my arms. And though the road ahead was long and steep, and the way dark, I knew that I should walk it to the end.
