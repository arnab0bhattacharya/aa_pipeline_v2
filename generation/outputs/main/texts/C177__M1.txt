The light in the drawing-room at Blackwood Hall was the colour of weak tea, filtering through the tall windows to fall upon Eleanor. She was perfect in that light. Her hair, the colour of spun honey, was coiled in a soft chignon at the nape of her neck, and the pale blue of her dress matched the cornflowers she was arranging in a crystal vase. She was a watercolour painting, a composition of gentle curves and soft hues, and Arthur had been taught his whole life to want exactly this.

He stood by the mantelpiece, a glass of sherry untouched in his hand, and watched her. She was humming a tune from a popular operetta, her movements graceful, economical. She was everything a man of his station could hope for in a wife: beautiful, placid, and possessed of a quiet, untroubled mind. They were to be married in the autumn. The thought should have been a source of comfort, a settled and pleasant fact like the solid weight of the silver in his hand.

Then the door opened and the room’s composition was shattered.

Miss Cora Finch entered not so much as a person but as an event. She was Eleanor’s oldest friend, a fact that had always struck Arthur as a profound mystery. Where Eleanor was porcelain, Cora was clay. Her dress was a sensible, dark grey wool, her hair pinned up with more haste than artistry, leaving a few stubborn strands to escape about her temples. She was not beautiful. Her features were too sharp, her mouth too wide, her eyes a restless, intelligent grey that seemed to catalogue the room and its occupants in a single, sweeping glance.

“Forgive my lateness,” she said, her voice clear and unapologetic. “The omnibus was held up by a stray dog with a truly heroic sense of its own importance.”

Eleanor smiled. “Cora, you are always an adventure. Arthur was just saying how quiet the afternoon was.”

Cora’s gaze landed on him. “Was he? How dreadful for him.” A faint smile touched her lips, but it was not a warm one. It was a flicker of amusement, as if he were a slightly absurd piece of furniture she had just noticed.

Arthur felt a flush creep up his neck. He was not accustomed to being found amusing. “Miss Finch. I trust your work at the typewriting office was not too arduous today?”

He meant it as a polite, if slightly condescending, inquiry. It was a world away from her own, a world of ledgers and ink and gentlemen’s tedious correspondence.

Cora raised an eyebrow. “It is always arduous, Mr. Blackwood. But it is also honest. We had a gentleman today who believed ‘accommodate’ had only one ‘c’ and one ‘m’. The world is a richer place for such men, do you not think?”

Eleanor giggled, a sound like tiny bells. Arthur found himself staring at Cora. There was a vitality in her, a fierce and crackling energy that Eleanor, in her serene beauty, simply did not possess. Cora worked. She took a rattling omnibus. She had opinions about spelling. She laughed at him.

That night, in his room, the image of Eleanor was overlaid with the memory of Cora’s grey eyes. He felt a profound and unsettling disloyalty. He was betrothed to an angel, and yet he was thinking of a sparrow. A plain, sharp-tongued sparrow who worked for her living and found him ridiculous.

A week later, they went for a walk through the estate. Eleanor, concerned for her complexion, had pleaded a headache and remained indoors with a novel. So it was Arthur and Cora who walked the lane towards the village. The air was thick with the scent of honeysuckle and damp earth.

They walked in silence for a while, the crunch of their boots on the gravel the only sound. Arthur felt an absurd need to fill the quiet.

“It is a fine day,” he offered.

Cora looked up at the sky, a vast, pale canvas. “It is. The clouds are particularly well-arranged today. One might almost think they had a purpose.”

He smiled, despite himself. “You do not think much of my attempts at conversation, do you, Miss Finch?”

She stopped and turned to face him. The sun caught the flecks of gold in her grey eyes. “I think conversation should have a point, Mr. Blackwood. Not merely to fill a silence. Some silences are more interesting than what is said in them.”

She held his gaze, and he felt stripped bare. With Eleanor, conversation was a gentle, meandering stream. With Cora, it was a challenge, a duel of wits he was not sure he was equipped for. And he wanted, with a sudden, desperate intensity, to be equipped for it.

“What is the point of this silence, then?” he asked, his voice lower than he intended.

“The point,” she said, her expression softening for a fraction of a second, “is that we are walking, and the birds are singing, and you are trying to work out why you are not with Eleanor.” She turned and began walking again, leaving him standing there, his heart hammering against his ribs.

She was right. She was infuriatingly, brilliantly right. He could not stop. He could not stop thinking of the way she had pronounced ‘accommodate’, or the way a stray lock of hair curled against her temple, or the way she looked at the world as if it were a puzzle she was determined to solve. He loved Eleanor. He knew he loved her. It was a calm, respectable, and entirely appropriate love. What he felt for Cora was a fever. It was a madness. It was the terrifying, exhilarating feeling of falling.

One evening, he found her alone in the library. Eleanor had retired early. Cora was curled in a large leather armchair, a book on her lap, her spectacles perched on the end of her nose. She looked up as he entered, and for once, there was no mockery in her eyes, only a quiet question.

“I do not know what is happening to me,” he said, the words tumbling out before he could stop them. He did not approach her, but stood by the door, a prisoner of his own confession.

Cora closed her book, her finger marking her page. She did not speak.

“I am to marry Eleanor,” he continued, his voice tight with anguish. “She is… she is everything I am supposed to want. She is kind, and good, and beautiful.”

“She is,” Cora agreed, her voice soft.

“But I cannot…” He trailed off, running a hand through his hair. “I cannot stop thinking about you. I cannot stop wanting to hear you laugh at me. It is the most damnable thing.”

A long moment passed. The only sound was the ticking of the grandfather clock in the hall. Cora took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, a slow, deliberate gesture.

“You are confusing novelty with affection, Arthur,” she said at last, using his first name for the first time. It was a small, sharp blow. “Eleanor is your world. It is a beautiful, comfortable world. I am merely a visitor from a different country. You are intrigued by the accent. It will pass.”

“You do not understand,” he said, taking a step forward. “It is not the accent. It is the country itself. It is everything.”

She stood up and walked towards him, stopping just out of reach. Her face was pale in the lamplight. “And what would you do? Give up Blackwood Hall? Your inheritance? Your standing? To be with a woman who works in an office and knows the price of bread? We are not in a novel, Arthur. This is 1912. People like us do not do such things.”

“People like us,” he repeated bitterly.

“Yes,” she said, her voice clear and steady, though he could see the tremor in her hands. “And the most sensible thing you can do is to forget this conversation. Forget me. Go to Eleanor. Be happy. That is the world you were made for.”

She looked at him then, and for the first time, he saw a flicker of something other than amusement or intelligence in her eyes. It was a deep and abiding sadness. And he knew, with a certainty that chilled him to the bone, that she was right. He was trapped. He loved a watercolour, but he was drawn to a fire, and he did not have the courage to burn his world down. He could only stand there, caught between the life he had and the life he could never have, knowing that the silence she had spoken of was no longer peaceful, but a vast and empty space that stretched out before him, forever.
