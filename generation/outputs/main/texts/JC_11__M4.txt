It was in the nature of my establishment—the Hotel of the Two Crowns, though the crowns in question had long since tarnished and the gilt had flaked from the ironwork above the door—that it should have known every manner of man who had ever presumed to govern, or to attempt the governing, of this unhappy republic. The generals, the colonels, the mere captains of a morning’s insurrection—they had all, at one time or another, ascended my staircase with its worn balustrade, had slept beneath my soiled canopies, had eaten at my table with the gravity of men who believed themselves to be shaping the destiny of nations. I had served them all, and I had learned to regard their proclamations and their pronunciamentos with the same weary equanimity with which I regarded the rain that fell each afternoon upon the plaza, or the mosquitoes that rose each evening from the stagnant waters of the harbour.

But I confess that on the night of the twelfth of October, in the year ’93, I experienced a sensation which I can only describe as a profound and creeping dread, a sense of impending catastrophe such as I had not known since the great earthquake of my childhood, when the bells of the cathedral had rung of their own accord and the tiles had slid from the roofs like the scales of some monstrous reptile.

General Ignacio Valdivia had arrived at four o’clock, accompanied by a brace of officers whose faces bore the peculiar expression of men who have not slept for a week and who expect at any moment to be shot. He had taken the entire second floor, the rooms facing the plaza, and had installed himself in the corner suite with the balcony, from which he could observe, he informed me, the movements of the enemy—though which enemy he did not specify, and I did not inquire. He was a small man, dried and brown as a strip of jerked beef, with eyes that glittered like mica in a rock face and a manner of absolute, unquestioning authority that made my servants tremble and myself bow with an alacrity I did not feel.

At half past seven, when the lamps had been lit and the evening meal was in preparation, there came a knocking at my door of such peremptory character that I knew at once, with a sinking of the heart, that fortune had turned her wheel against me. General Rufino Bracamonte stood upon my threshold, his great bulk filling the doorway, his moustaches wet with rain, his voice—a voice like the grinding of millstones—demanding the best rooms in the house, immediately, without delay, for himself and his staff.

I informed him, with many apologies and much spreading of the hands, that the second floor was unfortunately occupied.

He looked at me then with an expression I shall not forget—a look of such cold and speculative menace that I felt my bowels turn to water—and observed that he had heard, perhaps, that the second floor was occupied, and that this was precisely why he desired it. He was a man, he said, who liked to know what was above him. He would take the rooms on the third floor, the rooms directly above those which were denied him, and he would take them now.

I have often asked myself, in the years since, what impulse moved me to comply. It was not courage—of that I can assure you I possessed not a particle. It was rather the instinct of the hotel keeper, which is the instinct of the diplomatist and the coward combined: to yield, to temporize, to preserve the appearance of amiability while secretly constructing barriers and devising stratagems. I gave him the third floor. I gave him the rooms directly above General Valdivia’s suite. And then I descended to my office, where I sat for a long time in the darkness with my head in my hands, contemplating the abyss that had opened beneath my feet.

The arrangement of my hotel was such that the two floors communicated by a single staircase at the rear, and by a service stair of iron, narrow and precipitous, which descended from the kitchen to the courtyard. There was also, I remembered with a start of hope, a door at the end of the second-floor corridor which opened upon a small balcony, from which one might reach the third floor by means of a ladder used by the gardeners. It was a precarious route, but it was a route.

I summoned my head waiter, a man named Pereda, whose discretion I had tested through twenty years of revolution and counter-revolution, and in whom I had confided many secrets that would have hanged us both had they been known. I explained to him the situation. He listened in silence, his long face expressionless, his eyes fixed upon the floor. When I had finished, he crossed himself and observed that we were in the hands of God, but that he would do what he could.

The evening passed in a manner I can only describe as agonizing. I moved between the two floors like a ghost, like a spy, like a man walking on the edge of a precipice. On the second floor, General Valdivia sat at a table strewn with maps and papers, his officers gathered about him in attitudes of tense expectancy, their voices low and rapid. On the third floor, General Bracamonte paced his room with heavy strides, pausing now and then to stare out of the window at the darkened plaza, his staff officers seated in a row against the wall like schoolboys in disgrace. Twice I intercepted a servant bound for the third floor with a message from the second, and twice I turned him back with a lie. Once I heard footsteps on the service stair and rushed to interpose myself, discovering to my relief that it was only Pereda, carrying a tray of coffee to the upper regions.

At midnight, I stood in the courtyard, looking up at the two rows of lighted windows, and I thought of the generations of men who had passed through this house, of their ambitions and their vanities, their cruelties and their follies, and of how little it had all mattered in the end. The rain had ceased. The sky was clear, and the stars shone with that cold indifference which is the only true philosophy. I heard, from somewhere above, the sound of a guitar, faint and melancholy, and I knew that Pereda had found some means of soothing the generals with music, with wine, with the eternal consolations of the senses.

It was near dawn when the crisis came. I was dozing in my chair in the office when I heard a door open on the second floor, and then footsteps—heavy, deliberate footsteps—descending the main staircase. I was on my feet in an instant, my heart pounding, and I reached the foot of the stairs just as General Valdivia, wrapped in a military cloak, his face haggard in the light of the single lamp, set his foot upon the last step.

He wished, he said, to take the air. He wished to walk in the plaza. He wished to see, with his own eyes, whether the reports of his scouts were to be trusted.

I assured him that the plaza was quiet, that the night was cold, that his health—so precious to the nation—demanded that he remain within doors. I offered him coffee, brandy, a cigar, the morning papers. I spoke with an eloquence that surprised me, and I saw, in his weary eyes, the moment when he decided that the effort of going out was not worth the trouble.

He turned and mounted the stairs again, and I stood in the darkness, trembling, and listened to the sound of his door closing.

An hour later, as the first grey light of morning touched the towers of the cathedral, General Bracamonte descended the service stair, his face set in lines of grim satisfaction, and passed through the courtyard to the street without a word. He had, I learned afterwards, received intelligence that the enemy had fled the city, and he was going to take possession of the government palace. General Valdivia, meanwhile, slept soundly in his room on the second floor, dreaming perhaps of victories that would never be his.

I never told either of them. I never told anyone. The Hotel of the Two Crowns has seen many strange things, but it has kept its own counsel, as I have kept mine. And when, some years later, I read in the newspapers that General Valdivia had been shot by a firing squad in the courtyard of the very palace he had once hoped to rule, and that General Bracamonte had died in exile, in a foreign city, of a disease of the liver, I thought of that night, of the two rows of lighted windows, of the guitar in the darkness, and of the terrible, the inscrutable, the merciful indifference of Providence, which had permitted me, for one night, to hold the destinies of a nation in my trembling hands, and to keep them, by the grace of God and the discretion of Pereda, from destroying one another before the morning.
