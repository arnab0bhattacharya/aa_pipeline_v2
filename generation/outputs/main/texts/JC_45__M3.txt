The pale, hesitant sun of the Genevan winter had withdrawn behind the leaden crest of the Jura, leaving the Rue de Carouge to sink into a damp twilight that seemed to exhale from the cold stones of the lake. I had been staring from the window of my lodging for a long time, watching the grey mist crawl up from the water, creeping over the quays, swallowing the bare poplars, until the world outside resembled a vast, wet ash smeared over the face of the earth. In the dim, untidy room behind me, Vassily Sergievitch was coughing with a measured, hollow sound that had the effect of a muffled drum beating a retreat in some forgotten, stagnant swamp. He sat by the stove, his long, yellow face resting in a thin hand, his eyes fixed upon the glowing iron door as though he could read there the unrolling of our destiny, the inevitable and sordid end of all our plotting.

The concierge had brought up the mail an hour before, depositing it on the bare table with the silent, stolid malice of the Swiss lower classes, who despise us while they take our money. Amongst the pamphlets and the dull, conspiratorial circulars printed on smudged paper, there was one envelope that bore the stamps of the Russian Empire. It was addressed to me in a handwriting that seemed to waver, as if the hand that traced those characters had trembled with age or fear, or perhaps with the cunning of a practiced forger in some distant police department. I had left it lying on the edge of the table, a solitary white object in the dusk, while the shadows gathered in the corners of the room.

Vassily stopped coughing. He turned his head slowly, his glance shifting to the table, and then to me. He had the gift of saying nothing with a profound and terrible eloquence.

"It has arrived, then," he muttered, in that voice of his, which was like the rustling of dry leaves, hollow and without resonance. "You have read it?"

I had not. I had merely held it, feeling the cheap, foreign paper, struck by a subtle, heavy scent that might have been the odor of some provincial orthodox church, of wax and cypress and old age, or might have been merely the artful device of an agent provocateur. The revolutionaries of our circle in Geneva regarded such letters as police tricks, the bait of a vast, invisible net cast across the frontier to drag us back into the silence of the Siberian wastes. We had spoken of it often, in low voices, over the stale tobacco smoke, with the fierce and bitter conviction of men who have cut themselves off from the past to dwell in the shadow of an unforgiving future.

I took up the letter at last, and broke the seal. The faint, scratching sound of the tearing paper was startlingly loud in the room. Vassily did not move, but his eyes, dark and lusterless, followed my hands with a brooding intensity.

The letter was brief. It begged me to return. It spoke of illness, of a house that was empty, of a forgiveness that had been obtained from high places, of a pardon granted by the grace of the Tsar. The words were simple, the phrases broken by the clumsy, unlettered style of a provincial woman. "Your mother," it read, "is old, and the winters are hard. The authorities have promised. They are merciful. Come back, my son. Come back while there is yet time."

I looked at the signature. It was a name I had not heard spoken aloud for five years, a name that belonged to a time when I was another man, a man who had not yet learned the futility of sacrifice and the profound, unutterable loneliness of exile.

"Well?" Vassily said, from the depths of his chair.

I laid the paper down. "It is from her," I said. "Or it is not."

He grunted, a sound that conveyed neither belief nor doubt, but only the weary acceptance of a world in which such distinctions are meaningless. He had been a student in Petersburg, a maker of bombs, a man who had lived so long in the atmosphere of suspicion that he had forgotten how to trust even the evidence of his own senses. To him, every letter was a trap, every word a lie, every human gesture a mask for the basest self-interest.

"They are clever," he said, after a silence, during which the mist outside had thickened, blurring the lights on the opposite quay into long, wavering streaks of yellow that trembled on the wet cobbles like reflections in a disturbed pool. "They have their agents everywhere. They know your weaknesses, your sentiments. They know how to weave the web. A touch of sentiment, a tear, a mother's plea—it is the oldest trick of the Okhrana. It is the way they bring us back, not in chains, but in shame, with our own hands turning the key of our prison."

I listened, and his words fell upon my ear with the monotonous, hollow sound of a truth that does not convince. For while he spoke, I was seeing, not the grey room in Geneva, but a distant, flat landscape under a vast, pale sky, a low wooden house with a porch overgrown with wild hops, and a woman with a thin, lined face standing in the doorway, looking down the road that led to the town. It was an impression that came upon me with the force of a sudden light, and for a moment the whole miserable, sordid reality of my exile seemed to fall away, leaving only the clear, sharp image of that past, that irrecoverable past, which is the true and only country of the exile.

But the impression faded. The room closed in again, with its smell of stale tobacco and damp plaster, its bare walls, its meager furniture. And with the fading of the vision came the reaction, the cold, cynical doubt that is the exile's only armor against despair.

"If it is she," I said, slowly, "then she does not know what she asks. To return is to accept, to submit, to acknowledge that all we have done, all we have suffered, has been without meaning."

Vassily turned his face again to the stove. The glow of the iron had faded, and the room was growing colder. He drew his threadbare coat closer about his thin shoulders.

"She is old," he said, without looking at me. "Old people do not understand meaning. They understand only the empty chair by the fire, and the silence in the house."

His voice had changed. There was in it no longer the ring of the conspirator, the harsh, dogmatic note of the revolutionary, but something softer, more human, more broken. It was as if the mention of that distant landscape, that empty house, had touched some hidden spring in his own memory, had opened some door that he had believed sealed forever.

I took up the letter again, and read it through, slowly, as if the words might yield up some secret meaning, some hidden sign that would tell me whether they were the outpouring of a mother's heart or the cold, calculated device of a police agent. But the words remained stubbornly what they were: simple, unlettered, pathetic. And in their very simplicity there was a depth of cunning that I could not fathom, a possibility of artifice so profound that it merged into sincerity and became indistinguishable from it.

The night had closed in. The lights of the town were blurred by the mist, and the lake, invisible beyond the quays, breathed up a chill that settled on the window-pane like a cold, damp hand. Vassily had fallen asleep in his chair, his head nodding on his breast, his breathing stertorous and uneven. He looked old and shrunken, a mere bundle of rags and bones, a thing that the morning would find cold and still.

I folded the letter and put it in my pocket. The paper crackled faintly against the lining. It seemed to me that I held there, in that small rectangle of cheap foreign paper, the whole of my life, the past and the future, the irrecoverable and the inescapable, bound together by a few wavering words that might or might not be a lie.

I went to the window and pressed my forehead against the cold glass. Below, in the street, a solitary figure passed under a lamp, its shadow following it like a dark, faithful dog. The figure paused, looked up at the house, and then moved on, swallowed by the mist. It might have been anyone—a student, a worker, a beggar—or it might have been, in the ironic fancy of the moment, a messenger from that other world, that world of snow and silence and vast, patient cruelty, come to watch and wait, knowing that in the end the exile returns, whether he will or no, to the place where his bones were shaped and his soul was broken.

I turned away. Vassily slept on. The room was very still, filled only with the sound of his breathing and the faint, far-off murmur of the lake, breathing in the night like a great, weary beast.
