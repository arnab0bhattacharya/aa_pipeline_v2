The office of Mr. Ebenezer Gride, money-lender and general man of business, stood in a narrow court off Fetter Lane, where the light of day came grudgingly and the gas was kept burning at noon; and it was here, upon a certain raw November morning, that his clerk, Mr. Tobias Pinch, received his instructions. Mr. Pinch was a small, spare man of fifty, with a coat the colour of weak coffee, a pen behind his ear as though it grew there, and a manner of perpetual apology which he had worn so long that it had become, as it were, his second skin. He had been in Mr. Gride’s service seven-and-twenty years, and in that time had carried away the chairs and tables of half the parish; yet he had never once, in all those years, been able to look a distressed family in the face without a sinking at the heart which he attributed, quite wrongly, to a weakness of the stomach.

‘Pinch,’ said Mr. Gride, without looking up from a ledger in which he was recording, in a hand as dry as the paper, the misfortunes of a widow in Clerkenwell, ‘the Larkinses. Saffron Hill. Number nine, back room, second floor. Seven weeks and four days. Distraint this morning. Take the man Batty with you, and the van; and mind you have the furniture out by noon, for I have a party for the room at one.’

Mr. Pinch said, ‘Certainly, sir,’ and ‘Directly, sir,’ and made a little bow compounded equally of obedience and dyspepsia; and having gathered up the necessary papers, and the key, and his hat, and his conscience, which was a very small parcel and easily mislaid, he went out into the November fog.

The fog was of that particular London complexion which seems to have been strained through a dirty blanket; it tasted of soot and small coal, and it muffled the cries of the street so that a man might have supposed himself walking at the bottom of a well. Mr. Pinch, with Batty the van-man lumbering at his heels like a tame bear, made his way through the crowded, reeking thoroughfares of Saffron Hill, where the houses leaned together as if for mutual support, and the gutters ran with a liquid that defied analysis, and the air was thick with the smell of fried fish, wet straw, and unwashed humanity. Here were children who had never known a green field, and dogs who had never known a kind word, and old women who sat on doorsteps in the rain as though they had been part of the architecture. Mr. Pinch, who had seen it a thousand times, saw it again this morning with a peculiar heaviness, for he carried in his pocket the paper that was to turn a family out of doors, and he knew, as only a man in his calling can know, what that paper meant.

Number nine was a tall, grim house, whose staircase climbed upward in a darkness that could be felt. Mr. Pinch went up first, feeling his way by the wall, and Batty followed, breathing hard and knocking against the banisters. On the first landing there was a smell of boiled cabbage; on the second, a smell of damp plaster and something else—something like a sick-room. And here, in the gloom, a little figure rose up before him and stood still.

It was a girl of perhaps twelve years, in a frock that had been patched so often that the patches had patches, and with her hair, which was of a pale, pretty brown, strained back from a face that was thin and white and old beyond its years. She had been sitting on the top step, it seemed, waiting; and now she stood with her hands behind her, looking up at Mr. Pinch with a pair of large grey eyes that held neither fear nor defiance, but only a kind of patient inquiry, as though she had long since learned that the world was a hard place and had ceased to be surprised by it.

‘Is it you, sir?’ she said. ‘Is it you that’s come for the rent?’

Mr. Pinch, who had a great many answers ready for a great many occasions, found none for this. He cleared his throat, and shuffled his papers, and said that he was afraid—he was very much afraid—that he was the person.

‘Mother’s ill,’ said the girl. ‘She’s been ill this fortnight, and the baby’s ill too, and father—father’s gone for work, but there isn’t any work, sir, not anywhere, and he’s been to the docks and to the building and to the gas-works, and they won’t have him because of his cough. So I’ve been minding the little ones, sir, and I’ve kept them quiet, and I’ve not let them cry, because of mother’s head. And I’ve got something for you, sir.’

She brought her hands from behind her back, and in the palm of one of them, wrapped in a scrap of paper that had once been white, lay a single coin—a sixpence, worn smooth and thin with much passing from hand to hand, and polished, it seemed, by the pressure of her own small fingers.

‘It’s all I’ve got, sir,’ she said, holding it up to him. ‘It’s not much, I know, but it’s something. Will you take it, sir, and not turn us out? For mother’s sake, sir, and the baby’s. I’ll get more—I will indeed. I can run errands, and I can hold horses, and I can—I can sing, sir, a little; I’ve sung in the court for the neighbours, and they gave me a halfpenny once. I’ll bring you more, sir, if you’ll only wait.’

Mr. Pinch stood stock still upon the dark stairs, with the fog pressing against the window behind him and the child’s sixpence glimmering in the gloom, and something happened to him that had not happened in seven-and-twenty years. It was not a large thing, as the world measures things; it was only that the little parcel of his conscience, which he had thought lost, was suddenly found again, and proved to be a great deal heavier than he had supposed. He thought of the ledger in Fetter Lane, and the dry hand that wrote in it, and the party for the room at one o’clock; and then he thought of the child’s eyes, and the sick mother, and the baby that was ill, and the sixpence that was all.

‘My dear,’ he said—and his voice was not at all the voice of Mr. Gride’s clerk, but the voice of a man who had once been a boy himself, in a village where there were green fields—‘my dear, you keep your sixpence. You keep it, and buy something for the baby.’

The girl looked at him, and her lip trembled, for she had been very brave and was near the end of her bravery.

‘But the rent, sir,’ she said. ‘The rent.’

‘I will speak to Mr. Gride,’ said Mr. Pinch, and he said it with a firmness that astonished himself. ‘I will speak to Mr. Gride this very morning. And I don’t think—I don’t think, my dear, that he will turn you out.’

He turned, and went down the stairs again, past Batty, who stared at him open-mouthed; and out into the fog, which had never seemed so heavy, and back through the reeking streets to Fetter Lane, where the gas was burning at noon and Mr. Gride was still writing in his ledger. And there Mr. Pinch laid down upon the desk a certain paper, and laid down with it, not the sixpence, but something of his own which he had never laid down before; and he said what he had to say in a voice that shook, but did not falter.

Mr. Gride heard him out, and looked at him over the top of the ledger with a pair of eyes like two chips of flint.

‘You are a fool, Pinch,’ he said.

‘I hope so, sir,’ said Mr. Pinch.

‘And you will lose your situation.’

‘I hope not, sir,’ said Mr. Pinch; ‘but I am in hopes, sir, that you will give me another chance, and the Larkinses another week.’

Whether it was the fog, or the sixpence, or seven-and-twenty years of small dishonesties that had suddenly become intolerable, I cannot say; but Mr. Gride grunted, and dipped his pen, and wrote something in the margin of the ledger which Mr. Pinch did not see, and said no more. And Mr. Pinch went back to his stool, and took the pen from behind his ear, and went on with his work; and if his hand trembled a little over the figures, it was only the cold, or the fog, or the weakness of the stomach, and not at all the thought of a pale girl on a dark stair, holding out her sixpence, and saying, ‘Will you take it, sir, and not turn us out?’
