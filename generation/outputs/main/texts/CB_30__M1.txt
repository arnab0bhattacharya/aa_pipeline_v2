He found me in the school-room, which was also my own small parlour, at the hour when the boys’ lesson-books lay closed and the gas-lamps had not yet begun to hiss in the street. The rain, which had been falling since noon, struck the windows in a fine, chill drizzle, blurring the outlines of the opposite houses and making the room seem a fortress of solitude.

Mr. Arthur Vane stood upon the hearth-rug, holding his gloves as if he were about to depart, yet lingering with that restless energy which characterizes men who have no fixed purpose in the world. He was a tall man, with a countenance handsome enough to please the eye, but weary, as though the pleasure of the world had been tasted too early and found insipid.

“You look pale, Miss Grey,” he said, his voice low and vibratory, possessing that rich resonance which can make the most common words sound like a confession. “Does this land of mist and stone oppress you? You have the air of a prisoner.”

“I am not a prisoner, Mr. Vane,” I replied, keeping my eyes upon the work-basket before me, though my hands were still. “I am a teacher. There is a difference. One is held by force; the other by necessity.”

“And necessity is a harder jailer than the law,” he retorted, taking a step closer. The firelight threw his shadow large and dark against the wall behind him. “I watched you today, you know, from the window of the Hôtel d’Angleterre. I saw you walking with the little Misses Dupont. You were explaining the clouds to them—I could see your hand raised—and yet you seemed to be looking for something beyond the clouds. What do you seek in this dreary capital of rain and revolution?”

I looked up then. His eyes were fixed upon me with a piercing intentness that made the blood rush to my cheeks. I had known him but three weeks—a chance acquaintance made in the reading-room of the English hotel, where the London papers arrived a fortnight late. He had come to the Continent to kill time; I had come to earn bread.

“I seek nothing that cannot be found here,” I said, though my heart contradicted me with a heavy throb. “I seek my daily bread, and the satisfaction of a duty done.”

“Duty!” He spoke the word as if it were a curse. “How you women martyr yourselves to that phantom. Duty! You are wasting your youth in this gloom, Miss Grey. Your eyes are made for the light of London drawing-rooms, not for the flickering candle of a schoolroom.”

“London drawing-rooms have their own shadows,” I answered, striving for a calmness I did not feel. “And I have no wish to be a decoration there.”

He laughed, a short, sharp sound, and flung his gloves upon the table. “You are fierce. You are always fierce. Why cannot you admit that you are lonely? I see it in the very set of your shoulders. You are proud, and you will not weep, but you are lonely.”

The truth of his observation struck me like a physical blow. It was unjust that he should see so deeply into the quietude of my soul. “And if I were?” I demanded, my voice rising despite my efforts to restrain it. “Is loneliness a sin? Must I fly to the first stranger who offers to amuse me to cure it? If I am lonely, it is my own affair. It is not a commodity to be bought or sold.”

He moved swiftly, seizing the back of the chair opposite me, his knuckles white. “I do not wish to buy it. I wish to share it. Listen to me. I am going back to England on the packet-boat this day week. Come with me.”

The silence that followed was profound. The rain seemed to stop, the fire to cease its crackling. I looked at him—this man of leisure, of wealth, of wandering—and I felt a sudden, cold clarity.

“As your wife?” I asked, my tone hard.

“As my wife. As the mistress of my house. Why not? You are a gentlewoman. You are educated. You are beautiful, in a wild, proud way that pleases me. And I—I am rich enough to shield you from this drudgery. I offer you a home, Miss Grey. A home in the green fields of Sussex, with no boys to teach, no bells to ring.”

He spoke as one offering a benevolence, a rescue. But I heard the note of ennui in his voice. I heard the promptings of a man who was bored with the Grand Tour, who sought a new sensation, a new toy to carry back to his gilded cage.

“And what do you offer, Mr. Vane?” I asked, rising to face him. “You offer me a house. But what of the heart? You speak of my loneliness. Do you think to cure it with a title and a carriage? You look at me and see a novelty. You think, ‘Here is a spirited creature, tamed by poverty; I will take her home and see how she looks against the tapestry.’ But I am not a piece of furniture to be moved to suit your convenience.”

“By heaven, you misjudge me!” he cried, his face flushing darkly. “I offer you affection. I offer you safety.”

“You offer me a gilded cage,” I said, my voice trembling with the violence of my emotion. “And I tell you, I would rather starve in freedom than feast in servitude. You are lonely too, Mr. Vane. You are bored. You think that by taking me to England you will cure the emptiness of your days. But you cannot fill a void with a human soul. If I went with you, and the novelty wore off—as it would, for you are a man who requires constant stimulus—what then? Should I not find myself in a larger, more comfortable prison than this? No, sir. I will not be the plaything of your ennui.”

He stared at me, his chest heaving. The mask of the careless traveller had fallen; beneath it, I saw a man stung to the quick, a man unaccustomed to refusal.

“You are hard,” he whispered. “You are as hard as the stones of this city.”

“I am honest,” I replied. “And honesty is a hard thing to bear. Go back to England, Mr. Vane. Go back to your green fields. But go alone. Do not drag a fellow-creature into your restlessness. I have my work here. It is humble, it is hard, but it is real. I will not exchange the reality of my struggle for the illusion of your kindness.”

He stood for a moment longer, his hand outstretched as if to seize me, then let it fall. The firelight caught the gold chain at his waist, glinting mockingly.

“You will regret this,” he said, his voice low and ominous. “One day you will look out at the rain and wish you had accepted the shelter I offered.”

“Perhaps,” I said, turning away to the window, where the street-lamps were now beginning to glow like dull eyes in the gloom. “But it will be my own regret. And that, I think, is a burden I can carry.”

He took up his gloves and his hat. I heard the door open and close with a decisive click. I was alone again. The room seemed darker than before, and the rain fell with a renewed, mournful persistence against the glass. I pressed my forehead to the cool pane. I was lonely. God knew I was lonely. But I was free. And as the sound of his footsteps faded into the wet street, I whispered a prayer to the silence, asking for strength to endure the solitude I had chosen.
