When Mrs. Ellinor Vane, a widow of six-and-twenty, with a jointure of nine hundred a year and a small estate in the west, first came to the little spa of Ashbourne Wells, it was generally allowed by the resident company that she was a woman whom nature had endowed with more than her share of the gifts that make for social consequence. Her complexion was of that delicate sort which seems to owe its bloom to a fine sensibility rather than to health; her eyes, of a grey so clear as to borrow a momentary colour from every mood; her figure, slight and sufficiently tall; and her manner, a compound of reserve and candour, which made men of the world pronounce her “interesting,” and women of the world pronounce her “odd.” She had been married at nineteen to a man of fifty, a scholar and a recluse, who had left her, at his death, the jointure, a library of somewhat dusty books, and a habit of thinking for herself which was not likely to make her path smooth among those who mistake the confidence of society for the confidence of wisdom.

It was at the Assembly Rooms of Ashbourne Wells, where the invalids drank their waters and the idle their gossip, that she first encountered Mr. Orlando Pennant. He was a man of about five-and-thirty, with a fine open countenance, a ready laugh, and a manner which seemed to say that the world was a pleasant place and that he was on the best of terms with it. He danced well, rode well, talked well, and—what is more dangerous—listened well. He had a way of leaning towards a lady’s chair as if her smallest observation were the most important matter in the world; and he possessed, in an eminent degree, that art of seeming to be always at leisure, which is the peculiar charm of those who have nothing to do.

Mrs. Vane, accustomed to the dry precision of her late husband’s conversation, found this new manner refreshing as a draught of spring water after the dust of the library. She was not a woman to be caught by mere flattery; but she was a woman who had been long starved of that pleasant sense that her opinions were of value in the world of living men. Mr. Pennant’s attentions, therefore, fell upon soil not ill-prepared. He was assiduous without being importunate; he was gay without levity; he had, or appeared to have, a reverence for her judgement which was the most subtle flattery of all.

Her friends—for she had friends, though they were of that sober sort which is apt to be overlooked in the glare of a more brilliant acquaintance—were not slow to perceive the direction of her inclination, nor to express their uneasiness. Chief among them was a certain Mr. Theophilus Grey, a lawyer of Lincoln’s Inn, who had known her from a child, and who loved her with the affection of a man who has no daughter of his own. He wrote to her, in his plain, upright hand, a letter which he laboured over for a whole evening, and which he flattered himself was a masterpiece of tact.

“My dear Ellinor,” he wrote, “I hear from my sister that you are much in the company of a Mr. Pennant, of whom I know nothing but what report says—and report, as you know, is often a liar. I am told he is a younger son of a good family, but that his means are slender and his habits expensive. I do not say that this is true; I only say that a woman of your fortune and your position cannot be too careful in the choice of a second husband. You will pardon an old friend for speaking plainly. If you have any serious intention, let me beg of you to make inquiries. I am at your service at any moment.”

Mrs. Vane read this letter with a curl of the lip which was not altogether amiable. She had a high spirit, and the suspicion of being guided was gall to it. She had been guided—or rather, she had been managed—by her first husband, who had loved her in his dry way, but who had made her feel that her opinions were the fancies of a child. She had promised herself, when she put off her widow’s weeds, that she would never again submit her judgement to another. And here was Mr. Grey, a lawyer, a bachelor, a man who knew nothing of the world but its statutes, presuming to dictate to her in the matter of her own happiness!

She answered him with a coolness which she intended to be crushing, but which only succeeded in being a little rude. She thanked him for his interest, but assured him that she was capable of managing her own affairs; that Mr. Pennant’s character was known to her; that she was not a girl to be imposed upon; and that she trusted he would not think her wanting in gratitude if she declined to make him her father-confessor.

Mr. Grey read this reply with a sigh, and put it in his pocket-book, and said no more. He was a man who knew that advice, like medicine, is only effectual when it is taken voluntarily.

The marriage took place in the autumn, at St. Pancras, in London, with the bride’s cousin as bridesmaid and Mr. Pennant’s friend, a Captain Something, as groomsman. It was a gay wedding, and the bride was radiant. She had asserted her independence; she had proved that she was not to be led by the nose by a pack of prudent old women and prosy lawyers. She had chosen for herself, and she was happy.

Within a year, the truth began to make itself felt—not in any sudden catastrophe, but in that slow, creeping way in which disillusionment commonly comes to those who have married against the advice of their friends. Mr. Pennant was not a bad man; he was simply a man who had never learned to deny himself anything. He had married her for her money, and he had not the art to conceal it. He was extravagant, careless, fond of company, and, when his wife ventured to remonstrate, he met her with a laugh and a kiss, and the remark that she must not be a little goose. He had a way of saying “my dear” which was meant to be soothing, but which sounded to her now like the clink of a coin.

The jointure, which she had imagined would be sufficient for all their wants, was found to be insufficient for his. He had debts—not large, but numerous—and he had a genius for contracting new ones. He borrowed her carriage to go to the races; he gave dinners which she could not afford; he bought a horse which he could not ride. And when she spoke to him seriously, he was hurt—genuinely hurt—and said that she did not trust him, that she was cold, that she was always thinking of money, that she was a little hard, a little too much given to calculation. And she, who had married him to prove that she was not cold, not calculating, not the slave of prudence, found herself silenced by this reproach.

It was in the spring, when the London season was beginning, that she sat one morning in her little drawing-room in Mayfair, and looked at the accounts which her husband had left upon the table. The fire was burning low; the room was chilly; she had a headache. She heard his step in the hall, and the sound of his voice, gay and careless, as he asked the servant if Mrs. Pennant was at home. And then she heard him go into his own room, whistling an air from the new opera.

She sat quite still, and a great wave of desolation passed over her. She thought of Mr. Grey’s letter, which she had answered so proudly; she thought of the old library in the west, with its dusty books and its quiet; she thought of the years she had spent in learning to think for herself, and of how she had thrown away the first fruits of that learning upon a man who had never thought at all. She was not a woman to deceive herself long. She saw now, with that terrible clearness which comes to the honest mind when the first passion of self-assertion is over, that her friends had been right. She had married, not because she loved Mr. Pennant, but because she was angry with Mr. Grey. She had mistaken the desire to be free for the power to choose. She had been a fool—not the first, nor the last, of her sex, but none the less a fool.

The question now was—what was she to do? She was a woman of fortune, and she might, if she chose, leave him. She might go back to the west, and live alone with her books, and be a wiser woman, if a sadder one. She might write to Mr. Grey, and confess that he had been right, and ask his pardon. But she knew that the confession would cost her more than the marriage had cost her. And she knew, too, that there was a certain loyalty in her nature which would not let her desert a man who, whatever his faults, was her husband, and who, in his careless way, was fond of her.

She rose from her chair, and went to the window, and looked out upon the grey London street. The sky was overcast; the trees in the square were black and bare. She heard her husband’s step upon the stair, and she knew that he was coming to ask her to drive with him in the park, and that she would go, and that she would smile, and that she would say nothing of the accounts. And she knew, too, that in the depths of her heart she had already forgiven him—not because he deserved it, but because she had need of forgiveness herself. For she had learned, in that bitter spring, that the pride of independence is a barren thing, and that the wisdom of the world is not always the wisdom of the heart.
