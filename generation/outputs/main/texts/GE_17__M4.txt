The news—if news it could be called which was rather a slow, creeping conviction, gathering like damp in a wall—first took firm hold of the town through the medium of Mr. Bedgeworth, the solicitor, whose offices in a crooked lane behind the cathedral had for forty years been the repository of half the secrets and all the mortgages of the neighbourhood. It was Mr. Bedgeworth who, in the strictest confidence, imparted to the Vicar, and the Vicar to his wife, and the Vicar’s wife to the whole of the Close, that Miss Dorcas Pell, of the Elms, had been discovered to have sunk the greater part of her fortune in the lamentable speculation of the Bletchley Canal Company, a bubble now burst so utterly that its very name was a hiss and a byword among sober men. There remained to her, it was understood, the house she lived in and a pittance so small that the word “ruin” was on every tongue that presumed to speak of it, though many who spoke it spoke with averted face, as if the ruin were a contagion that might be caught by naming it too boldly.

Now Miss Pell was a woman of whom the town had long been accustomed to speak with a certain complacent respect, as of a clock that had never been known to lose time. She was not loved—for she had a dry manner and a way of looking at you over her spectacles that seemed to audit your very soul—but she was trusted, and trust, in matters of money, is a more durable sentiment than love. For five-and-twenty years she had been the providence of the town’s small necessities. It was Miss Pell who had advanced the hundred pounds that set up Mr. Thurlow the draper in his first shop; Miss Pell who had tided the widow Gaskin over the winter of the bad harvest; Miss Pell who had lent the young curate, Mr. Ainsley, the money to buy his horse, and had never once, in the seven years since, so much as hinted at the debt in company. She had kept a little book—a plain, morocco-bound book, much worn at the corners—in which these small charities were entered with a precision that was almost a form of modesty; for by writing down the exact sum she made it a matter of business, and by making it a matter of business she spared the debtor the humiliation of feeling himself an object of charity.

It was Mr. Thurlow, the draper, who first broke the silence. He was a man of a sanguine complexion and a soft, persuasive voice, who had risen by his own exertions and was not ashamed of them, and who, having prospered, was apt to attribute his prosperity to a system of rigorous honesty which he recommended to others with an earnestness that sometimes fatigued them. He came into his shop one morning, after a sleepless night, and stood looking at the row of coloured ribbons behind the counter, and said to his wife, who was arranging them, “Martha, I am going to pay Miss Pell.”

Mrs. Thurlow, a comfortable woman with a face like a ripe apple, paused with a ribbon in her hand. “Pay her, John? But she has never asked for it.”

“That is the very reason,” said Mr. Thurlow, with a little thump of his fist upon the counter. “Because she has never asked for it, and now she cannot ask for it, I am going to pay it. It is the hundred pounds she lent me when I had not a shilling to my name but what the shop brought in, and it is my duty, and I will not be kept from my duty by any false shame.”

“But, John,” said his wife, who was of a practical turn, “if she is ruined, as they say, she will be glad of the money. But if you pay her, and others do not, will she not be the more sensible of the others’ neglect?”

“That is her affair,” said Mr. Thurlow, buttoning his coat. “It is mine to pay my debt.”

And so he went, and the town, which watches its neighbours with an eye more vigilant than any policeman’s, saw him go, and there was a stirring in many a parlour that evening. For the town had been in a manner relieved. When the news of Miss Pell’s disaster first went abroad, there had been a general, unspoken, half-shamefaced sense that the little book was closed, that the obligations it recorded were cancelled by a higher insolvency, and that no man was bound to pay a debt which the creditor could no longer exact. It was a comfortable doctrine, and it had spread with the rapidity of a pleasant rumour. But Mr. Thurlow’s visit to the Elms put an awkward face upon it. It was as if a man had risen in the middle of a cheerful company and reminded them of a funeral.

The effect was various. Mrs. Gaskin, the widow, who had a small annuity and a large family of daughters, wept and said she would go that very day; and she did go, with the eight pounds ten she had saved, wrapped in a piece of paper, and she came away with her eyes redder than before, because Miss Pell had looked at her over her spectacles and said, “My good woman, you owe me nothing; it was a gift, and I have forgotten it.” But Mrs. Gaskin, who knew the book, and knew that the entry was there in the neat, small hand, went home and cried again, and could not tell whether she was more ashamed or more comforted.

Mr. Ainsley, the curate, was longer in making up his mind. He was a young man of a metaphysical turn, much given to the analysis of his own motives, and he spent a whole week in a state of indecision that was the more distressing because it was entirely invisible to his parishioners, who saw only a young clergyman unusually absent in his manner and more than commonly eloquent on the subject of the sin of covetousness. He argued with himself that to pay the money now would be to imply that he had not paid it before out of a want of honesty; that it would be to make a parade of a virtue which he did not possess; that it would be to insult Miss Pell by suggesting that she was in need of his charity. And then he argued on the other side, that to withhold it was to take advantage of a misfortune; that the money was not his; that the horse was dead, and the debt remained. And at last he went, and he stammered, and Miss Pell said, “I thank you, Mr. Ainsley, I had not expected it,” in a voice so dry that he went away feeling that he had been rebuked, and yet with a lighter heart than he had known for a fortnight.

But there were others who did not go. There was Mr. Bedgeworth himself, who had lent Miss Pell a hundred and fifty pounds on a bond, and who now reflected, with the professional composure of a man who has seen many fortunes made and lost, that a bond is a bond, and that a creditor who has become a bankrupt is in no position to demand payment; and he said nothing, and did nothing, and was heard to observe at the dinner-table of the Dean that the late unhappy affair of the Bletchley Canal was a warning to all persons of a sanguine temperament, and that Miss Pell, he feared, had been too sanguine. And there was the Rector, Dr. Mowbray, who owed her forty pounds for a subscription to a book of sermons, and who preached on the Sunday following a sermon upon the text, “The wicked borroweth and payeth not again,” with a force and a particularity that made his congregation look at one another, and made Mr. Thurlow, who was in his pew, smile grimly at his wife.

The town, in short, was divided, as towns are, into those who paid and those who did not, and the division did not always follow the line of wealth or of piety. It followed, rather, the line of a man’s private and unacknowledged estimate of himself; for the man who paid did so because he could not bear to think of himself as a man who had not paid, and the man who did not pay had found a reason that was sufficient to his own mind, and was, in the main, a very good reason, and was, in the main, a lie.

Miss Pell, meanwhile, sat in her parlour at the Elms, with the little morocco book before her, and she did not open it. She had a fire, for it was October, and she had her tea, and she had the cathedral bells to listen to, and she was not, to look at her, a woman who had been ruined. She was, indeed, a woman who had been relieved of a great burden; for the book had been a burden, though she had never known it, and now that the book was closed she found that she could look out of her window at the rooks in the elms with a quietness that she had not felt for years. She had lent her money because she had been afraid of poverty, and now that poverty had come she found that it was not so terrible as the fear of it. She had a little, and she wanted little, and she had the curious, dry satisfaction of a woman who has been proved right in her estimate of human nature; for she had known, all along, that some would pay and some would not, and she had known, too, that the ones who paid would be the ones who had the least to pay with. And she was not disappointed.

It was Mr. Thurlow who, some months later, proposed at a vestry meeting that a subscription should be raised for Miss Pell, “not as a charity,” he said, with his persuasive voice, “but as a tribute to a lady who has been the friend of every man in this town who has ever needed a friend, and who has never asked a favour of any one of us.” And the subscription was raised, and it was more than sufficient, and Miss Pell accepted it—for she was too wise to refuse—and she bought a new tea-urn, and a new pair of spectacles, and she lent the rest of the money out again, at a low interest, to a young surgeon who was setting up in the town, and who paid her back within the year. And the little morocco book was put away in a drawer, and was not forgotten, but was not spoken of; for the town had learned a lesson, though it was not the lesson that Mr. Thurlow had intended to teach, and the lesson was this: that a debt is not cancelled by the misfortune of the creditor, but by the honesty of the debtor; and that the honesty of the debtor is a thing that no man can know until he is tried, and that when he is tried he will find, in the depths of his own heart, a reason for the thing he wishes to do, and will call it a principle, and will be very much surprised if any one calls it by another name.
