It was upon a raw, grey morning in the latter days of November, with a thick yellow fog rolling up from the river, that my friend Hargreaves was pacing the floor of our sitting-room in Verrinder Mansions. His brow was deeply furrowed, and between his fingers he held the morning’s paper, which he had already read and re-read a dozen times. I sat by the fire with my pipe, watching him with that quiet amusement which his moods of intense concentration never failed to arouse in me.

Presently he stopped, struck the paper against his knee, and turned to me. "You observe, Carstairs," said he, "that the police have at last made an arrest in the matter of the Pimlico poisoning. They have apprehended a cabman named Jacob Shires, who, it appears, is charged with making contradictory statements regarding the disposal of his fare. It is a singular tangle, and I fear the authorities have laid hold of the wrong end of the stick."

I confessed that I had not followed the case with any closeness. It was, indeed, a matter of some notoriety. A retired merchant, Mr. Silas Dundas, had been discovered dead in the library of his residence in Pimlico, having been poisoned, it was said, by means of some alkaloid introduced into a glass of port wine which stood beside him. The death had occurred, according to the medical evidence, at about ten o'clock in the evening. And yet, at that very hour, a cabman named Shires had set down a fare answering to Dundas's description at the corner of Halford Street in Bloomsbury, a good three miles away. The police, scenting a fabrication, had arrested the cabman as an accessory, if not as the actual murderer.

"I have here a telegram from Shires's wife," Hargreaves continued, unfolding a slip of paper. "She implores me to clear her husband, who, she declares, told the exact truth to the police, and is now in a fair way to be hanged for it. I have a mind to look into it, for I never knew a woman's instinct to be wholly at fault where a man's liberty was concerned."

It was not long before our visitor arrived. Mrs. Shires was a tall, gaunt woman, with the stamp of honest poverty upon her face. She was plainly flurried and distressed, and poured out her story with a volubility which Hargreaves, by a few soothing words, soon checked. The upshot of it was that her husband, who plied for hire at the Bell Inn in Kennington, had been arrested on the preceding afternoon, and that the cab itself, with its horse, had been taken by the police to the yard at Wapping, there to await further inquiry.

"And your husband, when he set down this fare, observed nothing remarkable?" asked Hargreaves.

"Nothing, sir, save that the gentleman was very anxious to be in time for an appointment, and that he paid his fare with a half-sovereign, from which my husband deducted sixpence, the gentleman refusing to wait for change."

Hargreaves rose and drew on his overcoat. "Come, Carstairs," said he, "we shall have a cab to Kennington, and thence to Wapping. There is nothing like examining the scene of operations at first hand."

It was a long and dreary drive through the fog to the police yard at Wapping, where Shires's four-wheeler had been impounded. The inspector in charge, a stout, phlegmatic man named Bullen, was inclined to be somewhat contemptuous of Hargreaves's inquiries, but he gave us permission to examine the vehicle.

It was an old-fashioned, weather-beaten growler, with tarnished lamps and faded brown cloth cushions. Hargreaves looked at it with that quick, alert glance which I knew so well. He walked slowly round it, examined the wheels, and then, producing a powerful lens, scrutinized the step and the door.

"You will observe, Carstairs," said he, "that the off hind wheel has recently been greased. The grease is still moist, and has caught up a quantity of yellowish dust. That dust is not the black mud of Pimlico, nor yet the gravel of Kennington; it is the peculiar yellow clay of Bloomsbury. So far, the cabman's story holds good."

He then turned his attention to the interior. The cushions were worn and somewhat threadbare, but Hargreaves examined them with the most minute care, passing his hand over the velvet and bringing the lens to bear upon every stain. Presently he gave a little grunt of satisfaction, and, drawing a small pair of tweezers from his pocket, picked something from the corner of the right-hand seat. It was a short, grey hair, somewhat curled at the end.

"This is excellent," he murmured, placing it in a small envelope. "And now, Inspector Bullen, I should like to see the horse."

The horse, a sturdy, well-kept bay, was standing in a stall. Hargreaves examined its hoofs, its coat, and its mane, with the same minute attention. He then asked to see the harness, which was hanging on a peg.

"You keep your harness clean, Shires," he remarked, glancing at the inspector. "But I perceive that the off side of the breastplate has been recently mended with a piece of new leather. The stitches are coarse, and have been made by a man, not a woman. The needle has been thrust through the old leather in such a way as to leave a little ridge on the inner side, which would be likely to gall the horse."

The inspector stared. "Well, sir," said he, "and what of that?"

"Only this," returned Hargreaves, "that the man who mended that harness was no saddler. He was a person accustomed to handle coarse materials, and he used twine, not thread. I should say he was a sailor."

"But what has all this to do with the murder?" I asked, as we left the yard.

"Nothing directly," said Hargreaves, "but it is a link in the chain. The man who mended that harness was the same man who paid the fare with a half-sovereign and alighted at Halford Street. He was a sailor, or had been one, and he wore a grey beard, which was false."

"A false beard!" I exclaimed.

"Undoubtedly. The hair I found in the cab is from a beard, not from the head. You will notice that it is somewhat curled at the end, which is never the case with a natural beard, but is always the result of artificial curling. Moreover, it is grey, whereas the hair of Mr. Silas Dundas, as I observe from the portrait in the illustrated paper, was black."

I was amazed. "Then the man who alighted at Halford Street was not Dundas at all?"

"Precisely. It was some one disguised as Dundas. And the purpose of the disguise is not far to seek. The murderer, having poisoned Dundas in Pimlico, wished to create an alibi, or rather to confuse the time of death. He therefore engaged Shires's cab, disguised himself as Dundas, and drove to Bloomsbury, where he alighted, leaving the impression that Dundas was alive and well at half-past ten. The police, finding Dundas dead in Pimlico at ten, and having Shires's evidence that he was alive in Bloomsbury at half-past, naturally concluded that Shires was lying."

"But how did the murderer get from Bloomsbury to Pimlico, or vice versa?" I asked.

"That, I think, is clear enough. He walked from Halford Street to the nearest underground station, and thence returned to Pimlico, where he had left his own clothes. But he made one mistake. He mended the breastplate of Shires's horse, which had broken on the way, and in doing so he used twine and a sailor's stitch, which is a thing no landsman would do."

"But who, then, is the murderer?" I asked.

Hargreaves smiled. "That," said he, "I have yet to ascertain. But I shall be much mistaken if the real criminal is not at this moment residing at 14, Tavistock Square, under the name of Captain Penruddock. He is a half-pay naval officer, a distant relative of the deceased, and the only man who benefits under Dundas's will. I learned these facts from the morning's paper, and the telegram which I dispatched from Kennington was to ascertain whether Captain Penruddock had been seen in the neighborhood of Pimlico on the night of the murder. The answer, which I received while you were examining the cab, was in the affirmative."

It was indeed as Hargreaves had surmised. Captain Penruddock, confronted with the evidence of the false beard and the twine, broke down completely and made a full confession. He had, it appeared, gone to Pimlico on the pretext of consulting his relative on a matter of business, had poisoned him, and had then disguised himself and engaged Shires's cab in order to throw the police off the scent. He was tried at the Old Bailey in the following January, and, after a trial lasting three days, was found guilty and sentenced to death. Shires was released, and his wife, in the fulness of her gratitude, presented Hargreaves with a pair of knitted slippers, which he wore for many a day with as much satisfaction as if they had been the order of the Bath.
