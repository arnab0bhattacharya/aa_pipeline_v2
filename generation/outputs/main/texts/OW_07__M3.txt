Brendan O’Hara arrived in London with one perfectly tailored coat, a single volume of unreadable verse, and a letter of introduction to the Duchess of Harley. The coat was a masterpiece of understated deception, possessing the kind of lapels that suggest a dormant title and a rent-roll currently asleep in the funds. The verse, he was fond of telling people, was entirely ignored in Ireland, which he considered the highest compliment ever paid to his intellect. The letter had been written by his grandmother to the Duchess’s father, detailing a brief and entirely platonic affection they had shared in the famine years, a connection so distant that it possessed all the pristine chill of a glacier.

Brendan possessed the true Irish genius for conversation, which is to say he could talk brilliantly upon any subject, provided it was entirely trivial. He understood London society perfectly. Society is a circle of people pretending to be bored by those they secretly wish to imitate, and he saw no reason why he should not supply the boredom. To be entirely without money is a great inconvenience, but to be entirely without an audience is a tragedy. Brendan had no intention of being tragic. Tragedy, after all, is merely comedy that has forgotten its lines and begun to take itself seriously.

The Duchess of Harley lived in a house in Park Lane that was famous for its tapestries and its silences. The tapestries were Italian, depicting various mythological figures in the midst of being thoroughly immoral, which the Duchess liked because it proved the ancients had no notion of proper upholstery. The silences were her own, carefully cultivated to make her guests feel that whatever they had just said was both original and slightly improper.

Upon entering the room, Brendan did not wait for the Duchess to remember his family. To rely on memory in Mayfair is to rely on a faculty that society has long since traded for a good chef. He merely bowed, complimented the arrangement of her yellow roses, and observed that while the English were famous for their gardens, it was only because they had absolutely no idea what to do with their drawing-rooms. The Duchess, who had spent a fortune on a decidedly uncomfortable chintz, was immediately enchanted. She introduced him to Lady Agatha Lavenham.

Lady Agatha was beautiful in the manner of a very expensive porcelain vase; she was clearly meant to be placed upon a high shelf and admired from a distance, far away from the clumsy hands of men who might wish to put flowers in her. Her hair was the colour of old brass that has been polished by a devoted housemaid, and her eyes were as changeable and as shallow as a puddle in a Mayfair gutter. Brendan fell in love with her immediately, which was very foolish of him, and quite in keeping with the traditions of poetry.

Within a month, Brendan was invited everywhere. He was to be found discoursing on the moral duty of being entirely selfish in the drawing-room of the Marchioness of Staines, and he was heard playing a Chopin nocturne with all the wrong notes in the correct order at the Earl of Mortimer’s musical evening. He was the fashion, and the fashion, as he often remarked, is merely the temporary triumph of the ugly over the beautiful. Yet there was one house he could not enter. Lord Henry Bracknell, Lady Agatha’s brother, guarded the portals of Lavenham House with a cold eye and a frigid butler. Lord Henry was a man who had spent so long cultivating his dignity that it had entirely overgrown his humanity. He did not like Irishmen, he did not like poets, and he particularly disliked anyone who possessed neither a rental property nor a sense of their own absolute insignificance.

“You must not mind Henry,” the Duchess told Brendan one afternoon, as they watched Lady Agatha talking to a foreign prince who looked as though he had been carved out of very superior suet. “He believes that conversation should be reserved for those occasions when one has nothing to say. He thinks you are a charlatan.”

“A charlatan is merely an artist who has not yet been caught in the act of sincerity,” Brendan replied, adjusting his cuffs. They were the only part of his attire that was irreproachable, for a man’s cuffs are the windows to his vanity. “And what does Lady Agatha believe?”

“Agatha believes whatever the last person told her,” said the Duchess. “It is the secret of her extraordinary youth. She has never had an original thought, and therefore she has never been troubled by the necessity of changing her mind. She is looking remarkably well today, is she not? That particular shade of lilac she is wearing makes her look as though she has just stepped out of a very expensive funeral.”

“She looks like a moonbeam trapped in a prism,” said the poet, with a heavy sigh. “But I fear her brother’s drawing-room is a fortress built entirely of ice. I have sent three sonnets to his house, and they were returned with a note stating that the butler did not read in the evenings.”

“You cannot enter through the front door, my dear boy,” said the Duchess, adjusting her spectacles. “In London, the front door is merely an architectural device designed to keep out the interesting. You must enter through the garden. Henry is giving a garden party next Tuesday for the Foreign Secretary, who is a man of no discernible talent and therefore highly esteemed in political circles. I shall smuggle you in as a musician. You play the piano appallingly, I believe?”

“I play it with a devotion that completely overshadows my technique,” Brendan admitted. “But to play at Lady Agatha is to throw pearls before a swan. She does not hear the music; she only sees the pianist.”

“That is because she is a woman of great good sense,” the Duchess replied. “Music is a completely unnecessary art. It merely makes one feel things one has no intention of acting upon. However, it is the only way you shall cross Henry’s threshold. Once inside, I leave the morality of your conduct entirely to your own conscience, which I assume you left in Dublin along with your laundry.”

On the afternoon of the garden party, Brendan climbed over the garden wall of Lavenham House, tearing his one good coat on a particularly moralistic rose-bush. The roses were red, as red as the blush of a girl who has just been caught reading a French novel, and they seemed to regard him with a kind of botanical snobbery. He brushed himself down, picked up the sheet music he had brought as a prop, and walked onto the lawn.

The party was exactly like every other party in London; it consisted of a number of people standing on the grass, pretending they were not cold, and eating cucumber sandwiches with the solemnity of men attending a funeral. Lord Henry was holding court near a marble statue of Venus, who looked as though she wished she had been carved with more clothing and less ambition. Lady Agatha was sitting under a striped awning, fanning herself with a copy of the *Morning Post*.

Brendan approached her. He had walked across the lawn with the careful tread of a man who suspects the grass is about to betray him to the gardener.

“Lady Agatha,” he said, “I have come to tell you that your brother’s roses are entirely the wrong shade of red. They clash with your dress. It is a crime against aesthetics.”

Lady Agatha looked up. Her eyes were as shallow as a puddle in a Mayfair gutter, but they sparkled with a sudden interest. “I was just saying so to the Foreign Secretary,” she replied, “but he says that politics must always take precedence over horticulture. I think that is very foolish of him. Do you know a great deal about roses, Mr. O’Hara?”

“I know that they are the only flowers that look better when they are dying,” said Brendan. “They teach us that decay is merely the final, most perfect form of beauty. I have written a poem about it, if you would care to hear it.”

“I never listen to poetry,” said Lady Agatha, smiling. “It is far too exhausting. But you may read it to me tomorrow, if you can manage to get past the butler. I shall tell him to expect you at three o’clock. Henry is always out at three o’clock. He goes to his club to be bored by people who are his social equals.”

Brendan bowed. He had finally been invited to the one house he most wished to enter, and he realised, with a pang of something very like despair, that he had entirely forgotten his grandmother’s letter. It was still in the pocket of his one good coat, torn and stained with the mud of a Mayfair garden, and he knew, with the certainty of a true artist, that he would never need it again. In society, as in art, the best introductions are always the ones you forge yourself.
