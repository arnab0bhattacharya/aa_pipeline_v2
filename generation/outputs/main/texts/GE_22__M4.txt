The stall which Hannah Thorne kept at the Saturday market of Elmsleigh was not the most conspicuous in the open square—being but a trestle table spread with a clean linen cloth, upon which were arranged in comely order the new-laid eggs in their rush baskets and the pale yellow cheeses wrapped in vine-leaves—yet it had come to be regarded by the more discerning of the housewives as a thing worth seeking out, for Hannah’s butter was sweet and her cheeses were honest, and her manner, though reserved, had that quiet civility which is the flower of a nature too proud for servility and too just for insolence. She was now in her twenty-fourth year, a woman of a compact and wholesome person, with a face rather remarkable for the clear steadiness of its grey eyes than for any softer charm of feature, and with hands that bore the honest stains of the dairy rather than the idle whiteness of the parlour.

It was known in Elmsleigh—for in such a place nothing that concerns the heart long remains hid—that two men of very different condition had set their affections upon her. The first was Mr. Abel Renshaw, a grazier of Broadfield, a widower of some five-and-forty years, whose pastures were the richest in the valley, whose beasts took prizes at the September fair, and whose name stood for a solid and increasing substance. He was a man of a ruddy, prosperous countenance, given to a hearty laugh and a free hand at the ordinary of the Bull, and he had made no secret of his intention to marry again, and to marry, if he could compass it, the dairy-woman of Elmsleigh market. The second was a young stone-mason, one Joseph Meakin, who had come into the neighbourhood some three years before to work upon the new tower of the parish church, and who had remained to find such employment as the district afforded; a man of slender build and thoughtful, almost melancholy, countenance, whose clothes, though decent, showed the wear of constant labour, and whose hands were roughened and seamed by the chisel and the mallet. He was reputed to be clever beyond his station—a reader of books, a maker of verses, a man who could tell you the names of the stars and the reason of the tides—and there were those who said he was a better scholar than the schoolmaster, and others who hinted that such learning was but a poor provision for a wife and children.

Now Mrs. Thorne, the mother of Hannah, was a woman in whom the maternal instinct had taken the form of a prudent and unremitting regard for the main chance. She was not ill-natured; she would have been shocked at the imputation of hardness of heart; but she had known in her youth what it was to be poor, and she had conceived a profound and almost superstitious reverence for the security which is founded upon land and stock. To her mind, the proposal of Mr. Renshaw was a thing to be accepted with thanksgiving, as a mariner accepts a fair wind; whereas the attentions of Joseph Meakin were a species of imprudence which it was her duty, as a mother, to discountenance with all the firmness she could command. She had said as much to Hannah, not once nor twice, but many times, and with that iteration which is the peculiar eloquence of anxious affection. “You may think your own way best, child,” she would say, “and I dare say you know your own mind; but I am older than you, and I have seen what comes of marrying for love when there is nothing to keep the pot a-boiling. Mr. Renshaw has a house fit for a Christian to live in, and a servant to do the heavy work, and a bit of money in the bank, I’ll be bound; and if you say him nay, you may live to repent it when your hands are hard and your back aches, and the children crying for bread that you haven’t got to give them.”

Hannah listened, as daughters do, with a silent and respectful air, but with a mind that was not thereby convinced. She was not a girl of many words, and she had that habit of patient reflection which often accompanies a strong and self-reliant nature. She had promised Joseph Meakin—and the promise was not the less binding because it had been given in a moment of emotion rather than of deliberate choice—that she would wait for him until he should have saved enough to furnish a cottage and to provide for the beginnings of a household. It was a promise made in the spring-time, when the hawthorn was in blossom and the birds were busy, and when the young man, with a tremulous eagerness that was more eloquent than any words, had told her of his hopes and his plans, and had shown her a little hoard of money, painfully accumulated, which he designed for the purchase of a leasehold and the setting up of a workshop of his own. Hannah had believed in him then, and she believed in him still; but the months had passed, and the hoard had grown but slowly, and the grazier had come with his broad acres and his comfortable offer, and the mother had grown more urgent, and the neighbours had begun to talk, and Hannah’s own heart had begun to be troubled with a doubt whether, after all, the promise of a poor man was not a thing too lightly given, and whether the path of prudence were not the path of duty.

It was on a Saturday in October, when the market was full and the air was sharp with the first frost, that the crisis came. Mr. Renshaw, having disposed of his business at the ordinary, came to the stall with a face of unwonted gravity, and, leaning over the table, addressed Hannah in a low and earnest voice. He told her that he had spoken to her mother, and that her mother was favourable; that he was not a young man, and would not pretend to be one; that he wanted a wife who would be a helpmeet to him, and that he believed she was that woman; that he would settle upon her, by deed, a jointure of forty pounds a year, and would make a will leaving her all he had, if she would consent to be his wife before Christmas. Hannah heard him to the end with a face that was pale but composed, and then she answered, with a steadiness that surprised herself, that she was honoured by his offer, but that she could not accept it, because she was already bound by a promise to another.

The grazier’s countenance changed. He was not a man accustomed to rebuff, and the refusal, coming from a stall-keeper, touched him in a tender place. “I have heard,” said he, with a curl of the lip, “that you have been keeping company with that mason fellow—a man who has neither house nor land, nor, for aught I can see, the means of getting either. You will pardon me, Mistress Thorne, if I say that a woman of your sense should know better than to throw herself away upon a beggar.”

The words were not many, but they were of a kind that rankles. Hannah’s grey eyes flashed, and her cheek flushed, and she was about to make a reply which might have been sharper than her wont, when her mother, who had been listening at no great distance, came forward with a hurried step and a deprecating gesture. “Indeed, Mr. Renshaw,” she cried, “you must not think the girl means to be uncivil; she is young and foolish, and does not know her own mind. I am sure I have told her often enough that a good offer is not to be slighted; and if she has any silly notions in her head, they will soon be got rid of, I’ll warrant, when she comes to think seriously of the matter.”

But Hannah had risen from her seat, and was standing erect behind her table, with a quiet dignity that silenced her mother and made the grazier, for all his substance, feel somewhat small. “Mother,” she said, “I have promised Joseph Meakin, and I will not break my word. If he is poor, he is honest, and he is clever, and he loves me; and I would rather live in a cottage with him, and work with my own hands, than sit in a fine parlour with a man I do not love, and be beholden to him for every morsel I eat. I am sorry to offend Mr. Renshaw, but I cannot help it. I have spoken, and I shall not alter.”

There was a silence in that corner of the market, for the bystanders had gathered, and the story of the refusal was already passing from mouth to mouth. Mr. Renshaw, with a muttered word that was not altogether a blessing, turned upon his heel and strode away; and Mrs. Thorne, with tears of vexation in her eyes, began to upbraid her daughter in a shrill and agitated voice. But Hannah, having made her declaration, went quietly on with the wrapping of her cheeses, and there was in her face that look of settled purpose which is the seal of a nature that has chosen its path and will not be turned aside.

It was some two hours later, when the market was nearly over and the shadows were lengthening across the square, that Joseph Meakin came, with his tools upon his shoulder and his cap in his hand, to the stall. He had heard the news—for such news travels fast—and his face was pale and his lips were trembling. “Hannah,” he said, “they tell me you have refused Mr. Renshaw for my sake. Is it true? And will you not repent it when you have thought a while?”

Hannah looked at him with a smile that was half tears and half triumph. “I have thought a while already, Joseph,” she said, “and I have thought too long. I promised you, and I will keep my promise, if you will keep yours. But you must not think it is all for your sake; it is for mine too, for I could not be happy with a man I did not love, and I love you, Joseph, and that is the truth, whether it be wise or foolish.”

The young man took her hand, and his eyes were wet, and for a moment he could not speak. Then he said, in a low voice, “I will not fail you, Hannah. I have a bit of money saved, and I have a plan for a workshop, and if you will trust me a little longer, I will make a home for you that shall be worthy of you, though it be never so humble.”

And so the matter was settled, in despite of the mother’s lamentations and the neighbours’ predictions of ruin; and if the cottage was small and the furniture scanty, and if the winter was cold and the work hard, there was that in the hearts of the two that made the little house a place of light and warmth, and the promise that had been made in the spring-time was redeemed in the autumn of the following year, when the mason’s workshop was opened, and the first stone was laid of a fortune that was to be built, not upon the broad acres of the grazier, but upon the skill and the patience and the love of a poor man and a faithful woman.
