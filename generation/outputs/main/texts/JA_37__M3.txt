It was a circumstance of some notoriety in the neighbourhood of Danby Park, and one which its inhabitants were far too well-bred to discuss openly, that Mr. Heywood’s only son had returned from Oxford with accomplishments vastly exceeding his allowance, and debts nearly equalling his father’s mortgage. Mr. Heywood was a man of a very regular income and a very irregular temper; he had long prided himself on a strict and methodical economy, and to be thus called upon to meet the extravagancies of a youth who was barely one-and-twenty, was a trial to his philosophy which he was by no means prepared to endure with equanimity. Mrs. Heywood, a woman of less method and more imagination, received the intelligence with a composure that surprised her acquaintance, and a presence of mind that rather alarmed her husband.

Mr. Frederick Heywood was, in truth, a young man of very pleasing address, and of a disposition which, if it had been under the direction of a stronger judgment, might have led him to happiness and independence. He had a sweetness of temper which made him universally welcome in company, and a thoughtlessness of disposition which made him equally unwelcome to his creditors. He had been sent to Oxford with the view of his acquiring a degree and a proper share of polish, and he had returned with a very elegant wardrobe, a pair of match bays, and a thousand pounds’ worth of obligations to a set of friends whom he would be very happy never to see again.

Mr. Heywood, who had been a widower for fifteen years, and had therefore had no one to check his own prudent habits, or to encourage his son’s imprudent ones, felt the disappointment severely. He had calculated on Frederick’s entering the Church, and settling quietly into a living in his gift, in the neighbourhood of Danby, where he might be respectable on a small income, and useful in a small parish. But Frederick had no inclination for the Church. He had no inclination for any thing that required exertion, or debarred him from the society of London. His father represented the necessity of some profession; Frederick agreed to it all, declared the Church to be a very excellent thing for those who liked it, and went out shooting.

It was on the morning after one of these shooting excursions, when Frederick had returned with a very creditable bag of game and a very perfect unconsciousness of having given any trouble, that Mr. Heywood summoned him to the library. The old gentleman was sitting by his writing-table, on which were spread several accounts and a letter from a creditor of his son’s, when Frederick entered the room with an air of easy indifference, which was not at all calculated to soften the paternal heart.

"Frederick," said his father, "I have been looking over these accounts. They are more considerable than I had any idea of. You have been living at a rate which would be too high for a man of six thousand a-year, and you have not six hundred. I must know how you propose to discharge these debts, for I am not in a condition to do it for you. I have my own encumbrances, and the estate has been suffering for want of attention. The timber, in particular, has been much neglected."

Frederick changed colour, but it was rather from surprise than shame. He had not supposed that his father would be greatly pleased, but he had by no means expected so decided a displeasure. He stammered out something about the expenses of Oxford being very heavy, and the necessity of keeping up a certain appearance.

"Necessity!" repeated his father. "There is no necessity for a young man to live beyond his income. There is no necessity for him to keep horses, and give dinners, and lose money at billiards. But I am not come to read you a lecture, Frederick. I am come to propose a remedy. I have been thinking of selling the Home Wood. It is the finest timber in the county, and it will bring a considerable sum. It will pay your debts, and leave you something over. But it will be a material diminution to the estate, and one which I shall feel as long as I live."

Frederick was silenced. The Home Wood was the pride of Danby, and he had a real regard for the place, though he had never taken any pains to improve it. He felt the sacrifice, and for a moment he was almost ashamed. But the shame was transient; he thought his father rather hard, and he was glad when the conversation was over, and he could escape to his dogs and his gun.

He had not been gone many minutes, before Mrs. Heywood made her appearance. She was a woman of a very active mind, and a very decided turn for management. She had been married to Mr. Heywood for three years, and had not yet succeeded in gaining any very great influence over him, but she had succeeded in gaining a great deal over Frederick, who found her society much more agreeable than his father’s, and who was very glad to have some one to take his part.

"I am afraid you have been vexing yourself about Frederick," said she, as she entered the room. "Mr. Heywood is too severe. The boy has been imprudent, to be sure, but it is what every young man does. I am sure my own brother, Sir John, was quite as bad at his time of life, and he has done very well after all. But I have been thinking of a plan, my dear, which I think will set every thing to rights. I have a niece, Maria Wentworth, who will have ten thousand pounds on her majority, which is to be in the course of a few months. She is a very pretty girl, and very accomplished, and I am sure Frederick would like her vastly. If we could bring about a match between them, it would be the very thing. Frederick would be provided for, and Mr. Heywood would be spared the pain of cutting his timber."

Mr. Heywood listened to this proposal with a patience which surprised his wife. He had a great respect for her understanding, and he was willing to hear any thing that could be proposed. But he could not help thinking that the remedy was almost as bad as the disease. To sell his timber was a great evil, but to marry his son to a girl of fortune, for the sake of her fortune, was an evil of a different kind, and one which he was not at all prepared to encounter. He had a high notion of the marriage state, and he could not bear the idea of its being made a matter of bargain and sale. He replied, therefore, that he did not see the propriety of throwing a young man and a young woman together, with the express view of their falling in love with each other. He thought it a very dangerous experiment, and one which was very likely to fail. He would rather sell the Home Wood, and have done with it.

Mrs. Heywood was a good deal disappointed, but she was not discouraged. She knew that Mr. Heywood was a man of a very slow decision, and that he generally came round at last to her way of thinking. She therefore contented herself with saying that she would think no more about it at present, and that she would leave the matter to take its own course. She retired to her own apartment, and wrote a very long letter to her niece, in which she described Danby Park in the most flattering terms, and mentioned Frederick in a manner which could leave no doubt of her intentions. She did not absolutely say that she wished Maria to marry him, but she said every thing that could make Maria wish it herself.

In the mean time, Frederick, who knew nothing of these plans, and would have been very much astonished if he had, was enjoying his morning’s sport with a degree of satisfaction which was not at all diminished by the consciousness of his father’s displeasure. He was a young man of a very happy temper, and he could forget a disagreeable conversation much more easily than he could remember a disagreeable fact. He had a very good eye, and a very steady hand, and he brought down his birds with a skill which did credit to his practice. He thought his father rather unreasonable, and his step-mother very kind, and he had no doubt that every thing would be settled to his satisfaction in the end. He was therefore in excellent spirits when he returned to the house, and sat down to dinner with as good an appetite as if he had no debts, and no fears, and no father to reproach him.

Mrs. Heywood, who was a very acute observer, saw his good humour with pleasure. She thought it a very favourable symptom, and she determined to lose no time in bringing her plan to perfection. She began to talk of her niece Maria, and to expatiate on her accomplishments and her fortune. Frederick listened with his usual indifference, and made no observation. He was not at all interested in the subject, but he was too well-bred to say so, and he was very glad when the conversation turned upon something else. He had no idea of marrying any body; he had not money enough, and he had no inclination. He thought it a very foolish thing for a young man to marry before he had made his fortune, and he was by no means in a hurry to begin. He was very well as he was, and he hoped his father would not be in a hurry to sell the Home Wood, for he was very fond of shooting over it, and he should be sorry to lose it.
