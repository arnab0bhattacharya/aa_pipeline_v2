The rain lashed the windows of the post-chaise with a fury that seemed personal, as if the heavens themselves were indignant at the intrusion of two human souls into their gloom. I sat opposite Mr. Harker, my hands folded tightly in my lap, my heart beating a frantic, irregular rhythm against my ribs. It was not the storm outside that disturbed me, but the silence within. For three days, Mr. Harker had spoken to me not as a servant, nor as a mere custodian of his daughter, but as an equal. We had debated, we had argued, we had spoken of the nature of pride and the weight of duty with a candor that had stolen my breath. Now, on the return journey, that candor had vanished, replaced by a coldness so sharp it cut deeper than the wind.

He sat in the corner, his back against the door, his eyes fixed upon the dark, swirling road ahead. He did not look at me. He did not acknowledge my presence. I had tried to speak, to offer a comment on the wet moors passing outside, but the words had died on my tongue, choked by the sudden, chilling distance between us. Why? I searched his face, seeking a clue in the set of his jaw or the line of his brow, but he wore a mask of indifference so perfect it was terrifying. Was I to blame? Had I said something wrong in London? Had I been too bold? Too familiar? Or had he simply remembered his station, and I mine, and the chasm between us had snapped shut again?

The coach rattled over a bridge, the wheels shrieking against the stone. I felt a surge of anger rise within me, hot and bright, like a spark struck against flint. It was an injustice to be treated as a ghost in one’s own life. I was not a ghost. I had feelings, I had thoughts, I had a spirit that could not be tamed by a shift in mood. I looked at him, really looked at him, for the first time since we had left the city. He was a handsome man, with a face that was usually open and warm, but now it was drawn and severe. His hands were clenched on the strap of the carriage, his knuckles white.

"Mr. Harker," I said, my voice steady, though my hands trembled. "May I ask why you have become so distant?"

He did not turn. "You may ask," he said, his voice low and rough. "But you will not get an answer you will like."

"I do not care for liking it," I replied. "I care for the truth. I have no right to be confused, and I have no patience for riddles. You spoke to me in London as if we were friends. Now you speak to me as if I were a piece of furniture. Which is it? Which are you?"

He turned then, slowly. His eyes were dark, deep wells of shadow. "Both," he said. "And neither. You are a governess, Miss Elms. You are a professional. And I am a master. In London, I was lonely. I was tired of the lies of society, the smiles that did not reach the eyes, the words that meant nothing. I wanted to speak to someone who was real. You are real, Miss Elms. You are sharp, and you are brave, and you see through me. That is dangerous. It is dangerous for me, and it is dangerous for you."

"I am not dangerous," I said, my voice rising. "I am human. I have a heart, Mr. Harker. It beats, it bleeds, it hopes. You cannot use me for your convenience and then discard me when the moment passes. That is not justice. That is not even decency."

He looked at me, and for a moment, I saw something flicker in his eyes. A pain, perhaps, or a regret. But it was gone as quickly as it had come, replaced by a cold, hard resolve. "Decency," he repeated, the word sounding foreign in his mouth. "You speak of decency as if it were a law of nature. It is not. It is a convention. And conventions are broken when they become inconvenient. In London, I forgot who you were. I forgot that you are paid to watch over my child, not to share my soul. It was a mistake. A foolish, human mistake. But it is over."

"And if I refuse to accept that it is over?" I asked, my voice shaking now, not with fear, but with a fierce, desperate hope. "If I refuse to go back to being a shadow?"

He leaned forward, his face inches from mine. The smell of rain and horse sweat and tobacco filled the small space between us. "Then you will find yourself dismissed," he said, his voice quiet but absolute. "And you will have no money, no home, no protection. You will be alone in a world that has no use for women who ask too many questions. Is that what you want? To be free? Or to be ruined?"

I stared at him, my chest heaving. The rain outside seemed to intensify, drumming a relentless beat against the glass. I felt a profound sadness settle in my stomach, heavy and cold. It was not the fear of poverty that hurt most, but the realization that he did not see me. Not truly. He saw a mirror, a confidant, a tool to alleviate his loneliness. But when the loneliness returned, when the weight of his own life pressed down on him, he pushed me away. I was not part of his life. I was an interruption.

"I want to be seen," I whispered. "I want to be known. Not for what I can do for you, but for who I am. But if that is too much for you, then perhaps we are not meant to travel together."

He sat back, the tension in his body easing slightly, but the coldness remaining. "That is the wise answer," he said. "The safe answer. You are a sensible woman, Miss Elms. It is a rare quality."

"Sensible," I repeated, the word tasting like ash. "Yes. I am sensible. I am quiet. I am useful. I am everything you need, except a person. And that is why you can discard me so easily."

He said nothing. He turned back to the window, watching the rain-streaked dark. The silence that followed was not the comfortable silence of shared understanding, but the hollow, echoing silence of a chasm. I looked at my hands, folded in my lap, and felt the blood draining from my face. The journey was still long, and the night was coming, and I was alone in this carriage, trapped with a man who had opened his door to me only to slam it shut in my face. I did not cry. I had no tears left to spare for his indifference. I sat upright, my spine straight, my eyes fixed on the darkness ahead, and I waited for the dawn, knowing that when we arrived, I would be dismissed, and I would be free. And I would be alone. And I would have to find my own way in a world that offered no shelter, no warmth, no truth. But I would be true to myself. And that, I decided, was all I had.
