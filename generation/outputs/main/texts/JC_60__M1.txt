Captain Galloway had spent forty years of his life in the communion of the wind and the cordage, in that silent, patient wrestling with the elements which demands a man’s whole soul and leaves nothing for the mechanical. To him, the sea was a living, capricious woman to be humoured and obeyed; the ship, a faithful partner in that struggle. But the *Fulmar*, a steamer of the new breed, was no partner. She was a beast of burden, a captive beast, whose power resided not in the temper of the sails but in the iron belly below the waterline, in the mysterious, sweating heart of her engines. It was a power he did not understand, and what a man cannot understand, he must distrust.

His distrust settled, with a particular and personal loathing, upon the chief engineer, a Mr. Macleod, and his tribe of grimy, black-fingered men who lived in the ship’s lower world of noise and heat. They were not sailors. They knew nothing of the feel of a vessel in a following sea, of the tell-tale shudder of a spar under strain. Their world was one of gauges, valves, and the stench of hot oil. Galloway would stand on the bridge, his face to the wind, and feel the steady, insistent throb of the propeller up through the soles of his feet—a vibration like a fever, a constant, insolent assertion of a force that was not his. When Macleod would appear on deck, a short, solid man with eyes accustomed to the gloom of the engine-room, Galloway would offer him the barest civility, a nod that acknowledged a function, not a man. Macleod, for his part, would meet this with a quiet, unreadable watchfulness, his lips pressed together, as if guarding a secret he had no wish to share.

The Bay of Biscay, that vast and treacherous amphitheatre, had received them three days out of Liverpool. For a time it had been merely sullen, a grey, heaving expanse under a low ceiling of cloud. But by the second night, its mood had curdled into outright malice. The wind, a screaming, invisible presence, tore the crests from the waves and drove the spume in horizontal sheets. The *Fulmar*, with no canvas to shorten, drove straight into the teeth of it, her iron plates groaning, her whole fabric racked by the savage assault. Galloway, on the bridge for thirty-six hours without relief, felt a grim, familiar satisfaction. This was the sea he knew. This was the true test of a ship and a man. He could read the storm’s language in the water, could anticipate its blows. The steady, mechanical lunging of the steamer seemed to him a stupid, brutish defiance, a fool’s charge where a wiser vessel would have lain to or run before it.

Then, without warning, the heart of the beast ceased to beat.

The cessation of the engine’s throb was not a sound but a sudden, shocking absence of one. For a moment, the world was filled only with the shriek of the wind and the crash of water over the bow. Then a new vibration began, a sick, unbalanced shuddering that ran through the keel, followed by a grinding, metallic scream from below decks that was torn away by the gale. The *Fulmar*, deprived of her driving power, fell off the wind, her head swinging wildly, and was immediately at the mercy of the sea. She became a log, a piece of helpless wreckage, rolled and battered by the immense, indifferent waves.

Galloway’s first sensation was one of profound, almost religious vindication. *There!* he thought, the word a bitter stone in his mind. *There it is! The treachery of it!* He had known. He had always known. This mechanical heart was a fickle, faithless thing. He shouted orders to the helmsman to get the head of the ship back into the wind, his voice a thin thread against the storm’s roar. But the wheel spun uselessly. They were adrift.

He sent for Macleod. The engineer appeared on the bridge not with the frantic haste of a subordinate, but with a deliberate, heavy tread, as if he were merely answering a routine summons. His face, illuminated by a sudden, greenish flash of lightning, was streaked with grime and sweat, but his eyes were calm, direct. He had to shout to be heard.

“The main shaft has sheared, Captain! And the forward stokehold is taking water—somewhere below the waterline. My men are trying to find it.”

Galloway stared at him, his mind grappling with a reality that was utterly foreign to him. Sheared. A shaft of solid steel, sheared. It was like a man’s spine snapping. The abstraction was monstrous. “Can you not… repair it?” he bellowed, the question itself a form of accusation.

Macleod shook his head, a short, sharp gesture. “Not in this! Not in a month of Sundays! We’re dead in the water, sir. And if we don’t stop that leak, we’ll be worse than that.”

In that moment, the entire structure of Galloway’s world tilted. The storm, the sea, the ship—they were all secondary now. The primary reality was this grimy man and the incomprehensible catastrophe in his iron world. Galloway, the master of wind and sail, was utterly, pathetically impotent. His knowledge was a dead language here. He was the captain of a ship he could no longer move. He was a figurehead.

He had to rely on Macleod. The thought was a bitter draught. He had to trust the very men he had despised, to accept the judgment of a world he had dismissed as little better than the work of stokers and blacksmiths. He found himself following Macleod down the swaying, treacherous ladder into the ship’s hot, dark belly. The air was thick with the smell of brine and hot oil, the only light coming from the swaying yellow flame of a few lanterns. The noise was a deafening chaos of pounding seas against the hull and the frantic shouts of men. Water, black and oily, surged across the plates, swirling around their ankles. Macleod was everywhere, pointing, giving quiet, precise orders that were obeyed instantly. He was no longer a subordinate; he was the master of this particular hell.

Galloway stood there, useless, a tall, sea-cloaked shadow in that inferno of steam and steel. He watched the engineer’s hands, stained and sure, as he directed the men shoring up a bulkhead with timber and wedges. He saw the trust in their eyes as they looked to Macleod, a trust that was never offered to him on this ship. He was an outsider. The captain’s authority, his four decades of experience, were meaningless here. All that mattered was the knowledge in this one man’s head, the strength in these grimy men’s arms.

For hours they fought their battle in the dark, wet heart of the ship, while the storm raged above. When at last Macleod straightened up, wiping his brow with the back of a filthy hand, and declared the leak contained, there was no cheer, only a collective, exhausted sigh. Galloway felt a strange, hollow sensation. The immediate crisis was over, but the world had not been set right. It had been irrevocably altered.

He followed Macleod back up to the bridge. The grey, tumultuous dawn was breaking. The *Fulmar* was still a wreck, wallowing in the troughs of the great waves, but she was no longer sinking. Galloway looked at the engineer, who was gazing out at the raging sea with the same quiet watchfulness as before. The captain tried to form the words of thanks, of acknowledgement, but they caught in his throat. They would sound like a surrender.

Finally, he managed a few words, formal and stiff, his voice hoarse. “You and your men… you have done well, Mr. Macleod.”

Macleod turned his gaze from the sea to the captain’s face. He held it for a long moment. There was no triumph in his eyes, no mockery. Only a profound, weary understanding. He gave a single, slow nod.

“A ship’s a ship, Captain,” he said, his voice low but carrying clearly in a lull of the wind. “One way or another, we all have to keep her afloat.”

And in that simple, unadorned statement, Galloway heard the whole truth of his own inadequacy. He turned away and looked out at the grey, heaving water, a man who had mastered the ancient sea only to be shipwrecked in the new age of iron. The *Fulmar* was lost, and so was he.
