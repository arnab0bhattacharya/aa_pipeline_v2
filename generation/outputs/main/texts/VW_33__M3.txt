The soup was a pale green, and in it the little islands of cream spun round, dissolving, and she thought how odd it was that she had written the letter only that morning, and here she was now, dipping her spoon into the shallow bowl as if nothing had happened; as if the world were still the same shape it had been yesterday, or the day before, when she had walked back from the postbox in the rain and seen the brass knocker on the door and thought, That is still my life, that knocker, that door, and yet it isn’t, because I have written the letter; and now here was the soup, and the heavy silver, and the aunts leaning forward with their beads clicking against the edge of the table, and Mrs. Farrow saying something about the drains in Cheltenham.

“Cecil,” said her mother, turning slightly in her chair so that the black jet sequins on her collar caught the lamplight and threw out a little spray of light across the cloth, “Cecil will have some more, I know, he always likes the green soup.”

Cecil. The name came down the table towards her like a small, polite bell. Cecil, who had taken her arm in the park last Sunday and said, “We must think about the curtains,” and she had looked at the bare November trees and thought, Curtains; and then, I cannot; and then, looking at his good, plain, earnest face turned towards her under the brim of his hat, she had thought, He does not know that I cannot, and he will never know, because there is no door in him that opens onto the room where I am standing; and she had said, “Yes, the curtains,” and felt the earth tilt very slightly, as a ship tilts when the cargo shifts, and nothing is where it was, though the sea looks the same.

“Yes, thank you, Mrs. Thorne,” said Cecil, and held out his plate, and his hand was steady, and his cuff was clean, and she thought, He will be hurt; and then she thought, No, he will be surprised, and then he will be kind about it, and then he will go home and eat a boiled egg and read the evening paper and not understand, and that will be the worst part, not the hurt but the not understanding, because one cannot explain that a room with the wrong person in it is a prison, and a room with no one in it is at least one’s own.

“I was saying to Violet,” Aunt Mabel went on, her voice rising and falling like the wind in the trees outside, “I was saying, the drains in Cheltenham are not what they were. My friend Mrs. Arbuthnot—you remember her, Eleanor, the one with the limp—she says the whole street has been upset. They came and dug up the pavement and left it, and there it is, a great trench, and the children playing in it.”

“How very trying,” said Eleanor’s father, from the far end of the table, where he sat behind the tall silver candlesticks like a captain behind his instruments. He had a way of speaking from a great distance, as if the dinner table were a body of water he was addressing from the shore. He did not look at Eleanor. He was looking at the salt cellar, or through it, into some region where drains and trenches and the doings of Mrs. Arbuthnot were the only news, and nothing else had happened, no letter had been written, no word had been set down on paper that morning in her bedroom with the rain on the window and the little French clock ticking on the mantelpiece, ticking and ticking, and her hand going across the page as if it belonged to someone else.

The clock. She could hear it now, faintly, through the wall from the morning room. It had been her grandmother’s. It had a thin, precise voice, like a woman who has never doubted anything. She had written: *I find I cannot marry you. I am sorry. I have been wrong.* And then she had folded the paper and put it in the envelope and addressed it to his lodgings in Onslow Gardens and walked out and posted it, and the rain had come down on her face and she had thought, Now it is done; and then, But he does not know yet, and will not know until he goes home and finds the letter on his hall table, and reads it, perhaps standing up, perhaps sitting down on the little oak bench with the leather seat where he puts his boots; and she had seen him so clearly, reading it, his face changing, not dramatically, not as a face changes in passion or in grief, but slowly, as a field changes when a cloud passes over it, the light going out of it by degrees.

“Eleanor,” said her Aunt Violet, leaning forward with her necklaces swinging, “you are very quiet tonight. Are you quite well, dear? You look pale. I always say the London air in November is enough to kill anyone. Cecil, you must take her out of town. The country is what she needs. A good long walk in the wind.”

“She walks a great deal already,” said Cecil, turning to Eleanor with his kind, untroubled eyes, and she saw that he did not know, that he was sitting here in this warm, lamp-lit room with the green soup and the silver and the aunts, and he did not know, and she was the only one who knew, and the knowledge sat in her like a stone at the bottom of a clear pool, visible but unmoving, while everything else drifted and circled above it.

“Yes,” she said. “I walk.”

Her voice sounded strange to her, thin and high, like a voice heard through a wall. She looked down at her plate. The pattern on the china was a willow tree, and a small blue bridge, and two figures on the bridge, and she thought, They are crossing the bridge, and they will go on crossing it forever, because they are painted there and cannot move; and she thought, That is what marriage would be, to be painted on a plate and unable to move, and to have someone say, Look how happy they are, crossing the bridge, when all the time one is screaming, Let me off, let me off, but no sound comes out because one is only paint, only a pattern, only a woman with a parasol standing on a blue bridge forever.

“I have been reading a very interesting article,” said her father, “in the *Spectator*. About the situation in the Balkans. It seems to me that we are not paying sufficient attention to the Balkans.”

“No one ever pays sufficient attention to the Balkans,” said Aunt Mabel, with the air of one who has long suspected this.

The talk moved on, flowing around the table like water around a stone, and Eleanor sat in it, and the lamplight shone on the silver and on the aunts’ beads and on Cecil’s clean cuffs and on her mother’s jet sequins, and she thought, Tomorrow he will know; and then, correcting herself, No, tonight; he will go home and find the letter; and then, Perhaps he has found it already; perhaps he is reading it now, at this very moment, while we sit here and talk about drains and the Balkans, and the world is turning under us all and none of us knows it.

The pudding came. It was a trifle, and the sponge fingers lay in the cream like drowned things, and she looked at it and thought of the sea, and of how, when one is drowning, the world above the water goes on, people talking and laughing and eating dinner, and one is below, and the sound comes down muffled and strange, and one cannot call out.

“Eleanor,” said her mother, “you are not eating.”

“I am not very hungry,” she said.

“She is not very hungry,” said Aunt Mabel, turning to Aunt Violet, and the two of them looked at her with the tender, faintly alarmed attention of women who have seen a cloud and are wondering whether it will rain.

Cecil looked at her too, and in his eyes she saw the question, the kind, careful, patient question that was always in his eyes, and that she could not answer, because the answer was not in his language, was not in any language he spoke, and she thought, He will read the letter tonight and he will come here tomorrow, and he will knock on the door, and I will see him standing on the step, and his face will have changed, the cloud will have passed over it, and I will have done that, and yet even now, even sitting here knowing what I have done, I cannot wish it undone; for there is something in me that will not be put into a box and nailed down, something that says, I am, I am, I am, even if no one hears it, even if it is only a voice crying in a room with the curtains drawn.

“Yes,” she said, and smiled at him across the trifle, across the silver and the lamplight, “I am quite well. Only a little tired.”

And the dinner went on, and the candles burned down, and the aunts talked, and her father talked, and her mother’s jet sequins flashed, and Eleanor sat at the table and was silent, and the letter was on its way, or had arrived, or was being read at this moment, and the room held them all, held them together in the yellow light like flies in amber, and outside the rain had stopped, and the streetlamps shone on the wet pavement of Kensington, and the night was very dark, and somewhere a clock was ticking.
