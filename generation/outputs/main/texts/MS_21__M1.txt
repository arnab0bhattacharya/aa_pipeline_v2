It was in the month of November, when a damp and leaden vapour hung over the city of Bristol, that the visitation occurred which I must here relate. Twenty years had passed since the great crisis of my life, the hour in which I had secured my fortune upon the ruins of another’s, and in that long interval I had laboured to build such a fortress of wealth and reputation about me, that the past might never more find an entrance. My house, situated upon the lofty ground of Clifton, commanded a view of the bustling harbour below, a prospect I often contemplated with a strange and mournful satisfaction; for from that distant wharf, two decades since, the vessel had sailed that carried Edward Vance to the penal colonies, a man whose good name I had purchased with a lie, and whose trust I had violated to its very core. The years had treated me kindly, or so the world believed; my commerce had prospered, my name was spoken with respect in the exchange, and I lived in a solitude which the envious called magnificence. Yet, within the silent chambers of my soul, a worm was ever gnawing, and the memory of Vance—his open countenance, his generous laugh, the terrible bewilderment in his eyes when the magistrate pronounced his doom—haunted my waking hours like a spectre that would not be laid.

Upon the evening in question, the wind moaned about the casements with a voice of lamentation, and the rain beat in fitful gusts against the glass, as if some restless spirit sought admittance to my hearth. I sat before the dying embers, endeavouring to lose myself in the columns of a financial ledger, when the bell rang with a startling shrillness, shattering the profound stillness of the apartment. My housekeeper, a woman of few words and staid demeanour, entered to announce that a young lady waited below, seeking an audience upon a matter of employment. I was about to refuse, for I had long since ceased to require a secretary, preferring the heavy silence of my own company to the prattle of strangers; yet, a caprice, or perhaps a premonition of the doom that awaited me, moved me to bid her be shown in.

She entered with a step that was firm yet light, and as she advanced into the circle of the lamp-light, I felt a sudden, sharp contraction at my heart, as though a hand of ice had seized it. She was young, perhaps twenty years of age, with a form of singular grace and a countenance that bore the stamp of a noble lineage fallen upon evil days. Her features were pale and delicate, but there burned in her dark eyes a fire of resolution that reminded me—O God! how vividly!—of the man I had wronged. She wore a dress of sombre black, the fabric worn and thin, yet arranged with a touching care that spoke of innate dignity preserved amidst poverty. She did not know me; to her, I was but a prosperous merchant, a Mr. Armitage, whose advertisement she had answered in the hope of earning her bread. But I knew her; I knew that curve of the lip, that lofty brow; she was the living image of Edward Vance, standing before me like the embodied spirit of my crime.

"Sir," she began, and her voice, though low, was melodious and steady, "I am come to solicit the post of amanuensis, which I understand you seek to fill. My name is Clara Vance."

The name fell upon the silence of the room like a thunderclap. I could not speak; the blood rushed to my temples and then receded, leaving me cold and trembling. I rose from my chair and turned away, that she might not read the confession written in my face. Vance! The very sound of the word was an accusation. I looked out into the dark, tempestuous night, and the rain seemed to weep for the innocence I had destroyed. For a moment, I was tempted to dismiss her with harsh words, to drive her from the house that her presence might no longer torture me; but when I turned back and saw her standing there, so young, so defenceless, yet so proud, my resolution crumbled into dust. The daughter of the man I had sent to chains stood before me, asking only for the means to live, and I—wretch that I am—had grown rich upon her father’s agony.

"Vance," I repeated, the word choking in my throat. "It is a name I have not heard for many years. You are—?"

"The daughter of Edward Vance, sir," she replied, and a shadow of sorrow passed over her face, "who was, as you may know, a merchant of this city, until misfortune and the malice of others cast him down. He is dead now, sir, these five years; died in that far-off land of exile, leaving me alone in the world."

Dead! The word struck me with the force of a physical blow. Dead! The man whose forgiveness I might have craved, whose restitution I might have made, was gone beyond the reach of my repentance. The grave had closed over him, and I was left to face his orphan child. I felt as though the ground were opening beneath my feet, and that I was sinking into a pit of my own digging. I gazed at her with a fascination that was almost madness; she stood there, a monument of my guilt, asking for a pittance from the coffers I had filled with stolen gold.

"And you," I stammered, struggling to maintain my composure, "you have no friends, no protector?"

"None, sir," she answered simply. "My mother died long ago. I have been educated as a governess, but that resource failed me. I am skilled in languages, in penmanship, in accounts. I ask only for a modest wage and a roof over my head."

O, the sublime terror of that moment! Here was Providence, or perhaps the fiend, offering me a chance to atone, or to sink deeper into the abyss. To see her daily, to hear her voice, to look upon that face which was the mirror of my victim—was this penance sent from heaven, or a fresh torment invented by hell? I knew not; I only knew that I could not send her away. The impulse to protect her, to shelter her from the storms of life as I had failed to shelter her father, overwhelmed every other thought. It was a strange, wild desire of reparation, mingled with the selfish fear that she might discover the truth and denounce me.

"You shall stay," I said, my voice hoarse and unnatural. "You shall stay, Miss Vance. I will not turn you out into the world. You shall be my secretary, and—nay, do not look so startled—I will be your friend. I knew your father," I continued, the lie rising to my lips with terrible ease, "and I respected him. It is but justice that his daughter should find a home with me."

She looked at me with a sudden lightening of her expression, a gratitude that pierced my soul like a dagger.

"Oh, sir," she cried, "you are kind! You are more than kind. To hear my father spoken of with respect, after the cruel slanders that pursued him—it is a balm to my spirit. I had feared that all the world had forgotten his worth."

Forgotten! If she had known that I was the architect of those slanders, that I stood before her covered in the very dust of the ruin I had made, she would have recoiled in horror. But she saw only the benevolent merchant, the friend of her lost parent. The irony was more than human nature could bear. I felt the tears start to my eyes, and I turned away again to hide them.

"We will not speak of the past," I said, striving for calmness. "It is a painful theme. Let us look to the future. You shall take up your abode here. My house is large and silent; it needs the presence of youth to brighten its gloom. Tomorrow we shall arrange the details of your duties."

She curtsied gracefully, her face glowing with renewed hope, and retired, leaving me alone with the tempest. I sank back into my chair, and the full weight of my situation descended upon me. The years of peace were shattered. The avenger had come, not with the sword of justice, but with the innocent smile of a trusting girl. I was no longer the master of my house; I was a slave to the memory of the dead, bound to the service of the living. The wind howled louder, rattling the sash, and I felt as though the voice of Edward Vance were crying out from the grave, demanding an accounting of my soul. I sat there through the long watches of the night, while the lamp flickered and died, contemplating the strange and inscrutable destiny that had brought the daughter of my victim to my door, to sit at my table, and to look upon me with eyes of love and gratitude. I had built my house upon the sand, and now the flood was rising to sweep me away.
