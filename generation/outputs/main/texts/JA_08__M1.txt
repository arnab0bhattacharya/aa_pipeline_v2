Miss Julia Hartley, on the death of a great-uncle she had never met, found herself in possession of a fortune of some four thousand pounds, and in the care of an elder sister, Mrs. Priscilla Vance, who considered the inheritance as much her own affair as it was Julia’s. Mrs. Vance, a woman of unimpeachable principle and settled opinions, had married a gentleman of property in the next parish, and thus acquired a sphere of influence which she exercised with the greatest diligence. To her, the sudden elevation of her sister from a state of genteel dependency to one of independent consequence was not a happy event to be enjoyed, but a problem to be managed.

“The first thing, my dear Julia,” she announced, the very day the news was confirmed, “is that you must have a new wardrobe. Your mourning, though perfectly respectable, will not do for the season. You must be seen to advantage. And you must be seen with the *right* people. We shall pay our respects to Mrs. Elton at the Rectory. She is a woman of great discernment, and her approval will go a very long way.”

Julia, who was of a quiet and obliging disposition, saw the wisdom in this. She had no more wish to make a mistake than her sister had to see her make one, and she therefore submitted to the whole scheme with a patience that Mrs. Vance found highly commendable. Her days became a series of appointments with the milliner, the mantua-maker, and the shoemaker, all of whom were instructed by Mrs. Vance on the precise degree of elegance required—not so fine as to be vulgar, nor so plain as to be overlooked.

Her company was selected with equal care. She was introduced to the families of consequence in the neighbourhood: the Berrys of Ashcombe, whose carriage was the smartest in the county; the quiet but ancient family of the Penningtons; and above all, the aforementioned Mrs. Elton, a lady whose opinion was less a judgement than a decree. Julia was taught to speak of the weather, of the new turnpike, and of the latest novel from the circulating library in tones of intelligent interest. She was advised on which topics to avoid—the price of corn, the follies of London, and any expression of feeling too strong for the drawing-room.

For the whole of the spring and early summer, Julia was a model of propriety. She was universally approved. Mrs. Elton declared her “a very sweet girl, with not a particle of nonsense about her.” Mr. Vance, her brother-in-law, remarked that she was “a sensible young woman, who knew how to behave.” The general opinion was that Miss Hartley would make an excellent wife for some fortunate gentleman, and that her sister, Mrs. Vance, deserved great credit for her guidance. Julia smiled, accepted their compliments, and felt a quiet, creeping chill of absolute boredom.

Her life had become a perfectly polished mirror, reflecting back to the world exactly what it wished to see, but offering no glimpse of the girl within. Every conversation was a performance, every choice of ribbon a calculation. She had followed the rules so diligently that she had forgotten there had ever been any other way to live. The pleasure of her fortune was entirely eclipsed by the labour of its display.

The crisis, as she afterwards considered it, arrived on a Tuesday. Mrs. Vance came to her in a state of considerable agitation, a letter in her hand. “Julia, I have had the most wonderful news! Mr. Berryl of Ashcombe, the eldest son, has been asking after you. His mother considers you a most suitable match. She has invited you to their card party on Friday. You must wear the blue silk. It is the very thing to make an impression.”

A year ago, Julia would have acquiesced at once. She would have worn the blue silk, admired Mrs. Berryl’s new curtains, and spoken to young Mr. Berryl of the turnpike with intelligent interest. But on this particular morning, the thought of the blue silk, of Mrs. Berryl’s curtains, and of Mr. Berryl’s conversation, produced in her a feeling of such profound and sudden rebellion that it quite took her breath away.

“I believe,” said Julia, in a voice that did not seem to be her own, “that I shall not go.”

Mrs. Vance stared. “Not go? My dear, you cannot be serious. This is an invitation of the first consequence! To refuse would be an insult.”

“I do not mean to insult them,” Julia said, feeling for the first time the solid ground of her own resolve. “I mean only to stay at home. I find I have a great desire to walk to the village this afternoon, and I have not yet finished my book.”

“A book!” cried Mrs. Vance, as if her sister had proposed a visit to Bedlam. “You would give up a party at Ashcombe House for a book and a walk? Julia, you are not yourself. What can you be thinking of?”

This was a question Julia had long wished to be asked, and she answered it with perfect calm. “I am thinking, Priscilla, that for six months I have done nothing but what I was advised to do. I have worn what was chosen for me, and visited where I was sent. And I find I am excessively tired of it. I mean to make one decision entirely my own.”

The declaration, so quietly made, produced a greater shock than any tantrum could have done. Mrs. Vance was silenced, not by anger, but by a genuine and bewildered concern. Her sister, her docile, manageable Julia, was speaking of self-determination. It was a concept for which Mrs. Vance had no practical application.

Julia did not wait for her sister to recover. She put on her plainest bonnet and her walking shoes, and set off towards the village of Little Hetherington. She had no plan, no purpose, other than to be the sole arbitress of her own movements. The air felt different on her face; the birdsong was no longer a mere pleasant accompaniment to her thoughts, but a sound she was actively hearing. She walked past the Rectory, where she might have paid a call, and continued on, until she found herself on a path leading to a small, neglected churchyard on the edge of the common.

There, tending to a plot of overgrown graves, was Mr. Thomas Elton, the Rector’s younger brother. He was a man of quiet habits and a small independent income, considered by Mrs. Vance to be of no consequence whatsoever. He looked up as she approached, and his expression was one of simple, unadorned pleasure.

“Miss Hartley,” he said, wiping his hands on his handkerchief. “This is an unexpected honour. I did not think to see you here. I understood you were to be at Ashcombe.”

“I found I preferred my own company to that of the Berryls,” Julia replied, and was surprised to find the statement was not only true, but felt wonderfully liberating.

They fell into conversation, and for the first time in months, Julia spoke without a script. She did not talk of the turnpike, but of the book she was reading. She did not admire his gardening, but asked him the names of the wildflowers at her feet. Mr. Elton, who had always thought her a beautiful but remote figure, like a portrait in a fine house, was delighted to find her possessed of a lively mind and a warm heart. He spoke of his work among the poor of the parish, and of his hopes for a small school, and Julia listened with a genuine interest that surprised them both.

When at last she returned home, the sun was beginning to set. Mrs. Vance was waiting for her in the drawing-room, her face a mixture of relief and reproach. “Well,” she said, “I trust your walk has brought you to your senses. You have caused a great deal of talk, I am afraid.”

“I have done nothing wrong, Priscilla,” Julia said, taking off her bonnet. “I have only done what I wished.”

“And what,” asked Mrs. Vance, with a sigh of weary resignation, “did you wish to do?”

Julia considered the question. She thought of the quiet churchyard, and of Mr. Elton’s earnest face. She thought of the feeling of the sun on her shoulders and the freedom of an unchosen path. She had not set out to find a husband, or to defy her sister, or to make any grand statement. She had simply wanted to make one decision for herself. And in doing so, she had found, quite by accident, the first real happiness of her new life.

“I wished,” she said, with a small, private smile, “to see what the world looked like when I was not being advised how to look at it.” And though Mrs. Vance could not understand it, she saw in her sister’s eyes a settled contentment that no party at Ashcombe House could ever have provided.
