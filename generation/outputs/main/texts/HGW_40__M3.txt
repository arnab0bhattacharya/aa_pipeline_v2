I had taken the rooms at Mrs. Gribble’s in March of last year, chiefly for the gas bracket and the distance from the College. Mrs. Gribble was a stout, widowed woman with a perpetual grievance concerning the state of her linoleum, and she regarded my scientific apparatus with a hostility that only deepened as the months wore on. I had brought a lathe, a small dynamo, and an array of glass vacuum tubes that cluttered the back parlour. She complained bitterly of the smell of paraffin and the humming of my engine, which she declared put her off her starch. 

It was in November, during a particularly bleak Wednesday, that I resolved the final difficulty of my temporal apparatus. I need not detail the exact nature of the ethereal vibration I employed; it is enough to say that by interposing a localized field of rhythmic magnetic induction between two charged plates of a novel alloy, I succeeded in retarding the rate of physical occurrence within a strictly bounded area. To be plain: I had constructed a chamber wherein time itself flowed at a fraction of its ordinary speed.

I was exhausted, and my funds were low. Mrs. Gribble had knocked twice that morning to demand her rent, and the sight of her red, chapped hands as she gripped the doorframe gave me an idea. It was an impudent, entirely practical idea, and I acted upon it immediately.

I went to the stairs and called her down. She came reluctantly, wiping her hands on her apron and eyeing my room with undisguised suspicion. 

“Mrs. Gribble,” I said, “I perceive you are behind with your washing. The weather is abominable, and you are distressed. I have an apparatus here which will enable you to complete a week’s washing in a minute.”

She stared at me with that dull, heavy skepticism peculiar to the lower middle classes. “You’re having me on, Mr. Penrith,” she said.

“I am entirely serious. If you will bring your copper and your mangle into this room, I will prove it.”

She hesitated, clearly weighing the prospect of a scientific swindle against the undeniable backlog of her linen. The linen won. She went upstairs and returned with a basket of damp clothes, a scrub-board, and a general air of martyred endurance. I had already cleared the centre of the room, leaving only my two large metal plates, which I had arranged to form a cube, six feet square, in the corner.

“Put the basket inside,” I said. She did so, grumbling at the inconvenience. “Now, step in yourself. You will find the air perfectly breathable. You may wash and mangle exactly as you usually do. When I switch on the current, you will perceive nothing unusual, but to me, standing outside, your movements will appear vastly slowed. You may work for an hour by your own watch, and I shall only have waited a minute by mine.”

“And my Monday’s washing will be done?” she asked, squinting at the plates.

“Utterly done, dried, and folded, before the kitchen clock has ticked away sixty seconds.”

“Well,” she said, stepping into the cube and squaring her shoulders. “It’s a great nonsense, I’m sure. But if it saves my back, I’ll try it.”

I closed the switch. The dynamo hummed, the gas engine coughed into life, and the familiar violet glow sprang up between the plates. Mrs. Gribble began to scrub. To her, no doubt, the action was as vigorous and ordinary as ever. To me, standing outside, she was a figure trapped in a viscous, slow-motion nightmare. Her hands moved over the board like the hands of a clock viewed through treacle. The drops of water flung from her garments hung in the air, descending with a lethargic grace that was almost beautiful.

I stood for perhaps thirty seconds, watching this grotesque, slow-motion battle with the linen. Then, with the curiosity that makes the scientist what he is, I turned my attention to the apparatus itself. I had achieved the temporal retardation, but the strain on the dynamos was immense. I took up a screwdriver and began to adjust the commutator, bringing the engine up to a higher speed to compensate for the drag.

It was then I made a singularly unpleasant discovery. The temporal field was not perfectly contained. The magnetic resistance of the human body, it seemed, created a slight overlap. Mrs. Gribble was not merely slowed; she was, in a sense, suspended. The water on her clothes was not evaporating. The friction of the board was not generating its expected heat. The chemical processes of her body were correspondingly retarded. 

I did not immediately grasp the full horror of this. I merely noted it, and went to my workbench to fetch a voltmeter. I was gone perhaps twenty seconds. When I returned, I looked at Mrs. Gribble. She was still scrubbing, her face frozen in an expression of mild exertion. But I suddenly saw her not as a landlady, but as an organism. The blood in her veins was moving at a crawl. The oxygen in her lungs was being absorbed at a rate that would sustain a dormouse, not a working woman. 

I stood there, a shabby, poverty-struck demonstrator, staring at this woman who had trusted me. The freedom of the experiment had blinded me to the elementary facts of biology. I had thought only of the time-saving, the practical, the brilliant triumph over the drudgery of domestic life. I had not considered that to slow time was to slow life itself.

I sprang to the switch. But my hand hesitated. If I turned off the current abruptly, the sudden resumption of temporal flow might be catastrophic. The shock to her system could be immense. I had to bleed the field off gradually. I went to the engine and began to reduce the pressure, watching the voltmeter with a feverish intensity. 

The dynamo whined. The violet glow faded. Mrs. Gribble’s movements began to quicken. The drops of water fell faster. Her hands blurred, then resumed their ordinary, violent rhythm. She scrubbed on for a moment, then stopped, looking about her in a dazed fashion. 

“Well,” she said, her voice a trifle unsteady. “That’s a rum go. I’ve done a power of work, but I feel as cold as a church.”

“You have overexerted yourself, Mrs. Gribble,” I said, my voice shaking. “The air in here is chill. You must go and sit by the kitchen fire.”

She looked at her hands, which were bluer than I liked. “I don’t half feel queer,” she said. “And the washing isn’t dry.”

“No,” I admitted. “The process is imperfect. It retards work, but it does not accelerate drying. I must refine it.”

She went out, grumbling, and I heard her heavy tread on the stairs. I sat down on my truckle-bed and stared at the apparatus. The room was silent but for the ticking of the clock. I had done it. I had bent time to my will. But I had very nearly killed my landlady.

The next day she was confined to her bed with a severe cold, and I was left to my own devices. The incident, instead of deterring me, only sharpened my resolve. I had seen the danger; I must eliminate it. The field must be adjusted to exclude the human element, or the human element must be insulated against the field. 

I threw myself into the work with a savagery that surprised even myself. I rewound the coils, I altered the alloy of the plates, I devised a complex system of supplementary currents to counteract the biological drag. Mrs. Gribble’s cold lingered, and she sent up her daughter to scold me about the noise of my engine. I ignored the girl. I was on the scent of a greater freedom.

For three weeks I worked, eating little, sleeping less. I had no thought of Mrs. Gribble or her washing. I thought only of the perfect, isolated temporal field. And at last, one evening, I succeeded. I stepped into the cube myself, and for an hour by my watch I read and wrote, while the world outside raced by in a blur. I emerged to find that only a minute had passed in the room. I was not cold. I was not breathless. I was merely… alone.

I stood in the quiet room, listening to the distant rumble of a hansom cab. The apparatus hummed gently. I had created a sanctuary, a place where I could work, think, and live, free from the interruptions of the world. But as I looked at the plates, I felt a sudden, chilling premonition. 

I had solved the problem of the washing. But I had also solved the problem of my own life. I could now retreat into this room, and the world would pass me by. I could accumulate knowledge, write books, and perfect my theories, while the years outside were but days. I could become a god in my own parlour.

I switched off the current. The room was still. I went to the window and looked out at the dreary street, the gas lamps, the wet pavement. I had no desire to go out. I had no desire to speak to Mrs. Gribble or her daughter. I wanted only to sit in my temporal cube and watch the world spin away.

I turned back to the apparatus. It was a beautiful thing, in its way. But I knew, with a certainty that chilled me to the marrow, that I would never be able to leave it alone.
