The rain had fallen without cessation for three days upon the dale, and the whole valley of Marsden was sodden and steaming when I stepped down from the train at a little wayside station, where the porter took my bag with the air of a man who wondered what business any stranger could have in so forsaken a corner of Yorkshire. I had come at the urgent summons of my old friend Mr. Lucius Thorne, an engineer of considerable reputation, whose letter had been brief and agitated in the extreme. He met me upon the platform—a spare, grey-whiskered man of fifty, with the stoop of one who spends his days over plans and levels—and I saw at once that some heavy anxiety was preying upon him.

“You are very good to come, Mr. Vane,” said he, gripping my hand. “I am at my wits’ end, and that is the plain truth. A man in my position cannot afford to be made a fool of, and I am being made a fool of most systematically.”

“You shall tell me everything,” I answered, “and we shall see what is to be seen.”

We drove in a hired trap up a steep and miry lane, the horse labouring, and the wet fells rising upon either hand like great green walls. Presently the valley opened, and I saw below us the broad brown river, and the long line of the new viaduct already half constructed, its piers standing in the water like the legs of some monstrous creature. Beyond it, upon the farther slope, stood a low, grey farmhouse, with a cluster of outbuildings about it and a few ragged firs.

“That is Hollow Bank,” said Thorne, with a jerk of his thumb. “It belongs to a man named Reuben Hartley, and if I were a superstitious man I should say that Reuben Hartley had sold his soul to the devil to keep my line off his land. But I am not superstitious, Mr. Vane, and I do not believe in the devil. I believe in a rascal with a lantern and a pair of thick boots.”

He went on to explain. The line of the new railway had been surveyed some eighteen months before, and the viaduct was the chief work upon it. The course of the line, as finally approved, ran within a hundred yards of the Hollow Bank farmhouse, and cut off a corner of Hartley’s best pasture. Hartley had fought the bill in Parliament and lost; he had protested, threatened, and even, it was said, wept; but the company was inexorable, and the work had gone forward. Then, some three weeks since, a singular thing had begun to happen. Each morning the surveying stakes which marked the centre line of the embankment approach were found to have been moved in the night—always in the same direction, always with the same object. They had been shifted a few feet to the westward, then a few more, until it became plain that the line, if persisted in, would curve away from the farm altogether and carry the viaduct a full quarter-mile down the valley.

“I have had them reset a dozen times,” cried Thorne, “and every morning they are moved again. I have sat up at night myself, and seen nothing. I have had men watching, and they have seen nothing. And now the directors are asking questions, and there is a talk of my being superseded. They hint that I have lost my nerve, or that I am in league with the fellow. In league! Good heavens, Mr. Vane, I would give a year of my life to catch the rogue.”

“And the farmer—what does he say?”

“He says he knows nothing of it, and he says it with a face as long as a wet Sunday. I have accused him to his beard, and he has sworn by everything he holds sacred that he has never set foot near the stakes. He is either the most consummate actor or the most injured man in Yorkshire.”

We had come by this time to the scene of the trouble, and I got down and examined the ground with some care. The stakes were plain, stout ash poles, driven deep into the peaty soil, and each had been drawn out and re-driven, for the old holes were visible beside the new ones. The ground about was trampled and miry, and the rain had obliterated any footprints that might have told a tale. But I observed one thing which set me thinking. The new holes were always upon the western side of the old ones, and the distance between them was curiously regular—never more than a foot, never less than nine inches. It was as though the mover had measured his distance by some standard, and I could not but reflect that a man who moves stakes in the dark, and moves them by measure, is not a man who acts upon impulse.

I asked to see the farmer. We found him in his kitchen, a great, broad-shouldered fellow with a slow eye and a heavy jaw, who listened to Thorne’s accusations with a kind of stolid patience that might have been innocence or might have been the most profound conceit.

“I tell you again, sir,” said he, “I know naught of your stakes. I have my own work to do, and it is work enough without traipsing over the valley of a night. If your men cannot keep their own stakes in the ground, it is no fault of mine.”

“You will not object to my examining your premises?” said I.

He looked at me with a flicker of something—was it fear or was it contempt?—and then shrugged his heavy shoulders.

“Examine and welcome,” said he. “You will find nothing but cows and muck.”

I did examine them, and I found nothing. But as I came away I noticed a small thing which Thorne had not observed. In the farmyard, beside the door of a shed, there stood a pile of old railway sleepers, and upon one of them, half worn away by weather, I saw the broad arrow of the Government survey. It was a small matter, but it set a train of thought in motion which was to lead me far.

That night I did not sit up with Thorne. I had my own theory, and it required solitude. I took my position in a little clump of firs upon the hillside, some fifty yards from the line of stakes, and I waited. The rain had ceased, and a thin moon was struggling through the clouds, so that the valley lay in a grey half-light, and I could see the stakes like a row of thin black fingers against the pale water. For two hours nothing stirred. Then, a little after one o’clock, I saw a figure come stealing down the slope from the direction of the farm—a figure so small and so noiseless that at first I took it for a dog. It came to the first stake, and I saw it stoop, and I heard the soft grating of iron upon wood. Then it rose, and moved to the second, and the third, and at each it paused and stooped and rose again.

I rose from my hiding-place and walked quietly towards it. The figure was so intent upon its work that it did not hear me until I was within ten paces, and then it started up with a little cry, and I saw that it was a boy—a lad of twelve or thirteen, thin and ragged, with a pale, sharp face and a pair of great dark eyes that looked at me with a terror that was almost piteous.

“Do not be afraid,” said I, gently. “I am not going to hurt you. What is your name?”

He was silent for a moment, trembling in every limb. Then he said, in a low, husky voice:

“Tom Hartley, sir. I am Reuben Hartley’s son.”

“And why do you move the stakes, Tom?”

The boy’s lips set tight, and for a moment I thought he would refuse to answer. Then something in my face seemed to reassure him, and he said, with a kind of desperate frankness:

“Because of father, sir. He has been that put about by the railway coming so close. He says it will ruin the farm—the smoke and the noise and the cutting off of the pasture. He has not slept for weeks. And I thought—I thought if the stakes were moved, maybe the line would go another way, and father would be happy again. I have done it every night for three weeks, sir, and I have measured the distance with a bit of string, so that it should look regular and no one would suspect.”

“And does your father know?”

“No, sir, upon my soul he does not. He would leather me if he knew. He is an honest man, sir, and he would never do such a thing himself. It is all my own doing.”

I looked at the thin, white face, and I confess that my heart was touched. Here was no midnight conspirator, but a child, driven by a mistaken love to a foolish and futile piece of mischief. I took him by the hand and led him back to the farm, and there, in the grey dawn, I roused Reuben Hartley from his bed and told him the whole story. The man’s face was a study. He was angry, then astonished, then, as the boy sobbed out his confession, something else came into it—a softer thing that I had not looked for in so heavy a countenance. He put his great hand upon the boy’s head and said nothing at all.

I saw Thorne in the morning, and I told him what I had found. He was inclined at first to be very wroth, and talked of the law and of damages; but he was a just man at bottom, and when I pointed out to him that the boy had acted from no evil motive, and that a prosecution would ruin the father and make an enemy of every man in the dale, he came round.

“I will say nothing about it,” said he, “but the stakes must stay where they are, and the boy must give me his word not to touch them again.”

And so it was settled. The viaduct was finished in the following autumn, and I am told that it stands to this day, a credit to its builder. And if ever you pass that way, and see a tall young man in a porter’s uniform upon the platform at Marsden, you may know that it is Tom Hartley, who has long since learned that a railway may be a blessing to a valley, and that the way to serve a father is not to move his neighbour’s landmarks in the dark.
