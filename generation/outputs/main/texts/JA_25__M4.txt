The invitation to Mrs. Ashcombe’s assembly had been extended with such warmth, and accepted with such gratitude, that Miss Eleanor Vance had felt it impossible to confess the true state of her feelings upon the subject. To be sure, a London season was everything that a clergyman’s daughter from a small and distant parish could reasonably desire; and Mrs. Ashcombe, her former schoolfellow, was all kindness in insisting that Eleanor should accompany her. Yet the prospect of a crowded room in which she knew not a soul, and where her own modest attire must appear to the greatest disadvantage beside the elegance of Mrs. Ashcombe’s acquaintance, was sufficient to depress spirits which had already been lowered by circumstances of a more private and painful nature.

The circumstance was this: that she had come to London with the hope—carefully concealed even from herself, and certainly never to be confided to Mrs. Ashcombe—of avoiding Mr. Henry Ellison. That gentleman, the son of a wealthy and respected baronet in Eleanor’s own county, had lately begun to pay her attentions of a sort which, while they flattered her vanity, had the unfortunate effect of engaging her heart to a degree that her judgement could not approve. He was agreeable, handsome, and possessed of manners so peculiarly engaging that it was impossible to be in his company without feeling the force of them; but he was also, she had reason to believe, of a temper somewhat too volatile for constancy, and his father, she had heard, had other views for him. When Mrs. Ashcombe’s invitation arrived, therefore, it had seemed a most fortunate intervention; and Eleanor had quitted her home with the comfortable persuasion that distance and dissipation would effect a cure which proximity had only aggravated.

The assembly was held at the house of a Lady Mowbray, in a square of new and handsome buildings, and the rooms were already thronged when Mrs. Ashcombe and Eleanor arrived. Mrs. Ashcombe, who was a young woman of fashion and of a lively, unthinking disposition, was soon carried off by a party of friends, leaving Eleanor to the enjoyment of that species of solitude which is most acutely felt in a crowd. She stationed herself near a window, endeavoured to appear as if she were perfectly at her ease, and was beginning to calculate how soon she might with decency request the carriage, when she was addressed by an elderly gentleman whose appearance, though singular, was not unpleasing.

He was a stout, short man, with a face of much good humour, and a manner that seemed to set ceremony entirely at defiance. “I perceive, madam,” said he, “that you are as much a stranger to this assembly as I am myself; and as we are both evidently persons of too much sense to be amused by the noise and nonsense around us, I propose that we form an alliance for the evening. My name is Mr. Ellison—Sir John Ellison, of Ravensworth, in——shire—and I flatter myself that I am the most agreeable old fellow in the room, if the truth were known.”

Eleanor started at the name, and it was with some difficulty that she composed her features sufficiently to return his civility. That she should have been thrown into the company of Sir John Ellison—the very father of the man she had come to London to forget—appeared to her a coincidence so cruel as to be almost beyond belief. Yet she had never met him; he had no idea who she was; and she was mistress enough of herself to conceal her agitation under an appearance of polite attention.

“You are very good, sir,” she replied, “to take pity on my solitude. I confess I was beginning to think that I had never in my life seen so many people with so little to say to one another.”

“Ha! You have hit it exactly,” cried Sir John, with great satisfaction. “Nothing so dulls the understanding as a London assembly. I have been here these two hours, and I have not heard one remark that was worth the trouble of remembering. Lady Mowbray means well, but she will invite everybody she knows, and the consequence is that nobody knows anybody. For my part, I would rather sit by my own fireside with a good book and a dish of tea, than be jostled by a hundred people who care no more for me than I care for them.”

“You are a man of domestic tastes, then, sir,” said Eleanor, with a smile which she could not entirely suppress.

“Domestic! I should think so. I have a house in the country that I would not exchange for all the finery in London. But my son—my son Henry—he is for ever dragging me hither and thither. He is a young fellow of spirit, madam, and thinks his father ought to be as fond of dissipation as himself. I tell him that at my time of life a man has a right to be quiet; but he will not hear of it. He is a good boy, though—a very good boy—and I am sure I should be the last man in the world to say a word against him.”

Eleanor’s heart beat with a violence that she feared must be visible. “Your son is in London, then, sir?” she asked, with an indifference that cost her an effort.

“He is, madam—and the very cause of my being here tonight. He was to have accompanied me, but he sent me word at the last moment that he was engaged elsewhere. Engaged elsewhere! I dare say he is at some card-party or other, losing his money with as much good humour as if he were winning it. But there—young men will be young men; and Henry, I must confess, has a way with him that makes one forgive him anything.”

There was something in the old gentleman’s manner of speaking of his son—a mixture of fondness and indulgence, with a lurking sense of the young man’s failings—which struck Eleanor forcibly. She had never before considered Mr. Henry Ellison as the object of a father’s anxious affection; but here was the father himself, evidently proud of him, and as evidently blind to his defects. The discovery gave her a new and not altogether pleasant view of the situation.

“You are fortunate, sir,” she said, “in having so agreeable a companion in your son.”

“Agreeable! He is the most agreeable fellow in the world, when he chooses to be. But he is a sad rogue for all that. He will make love to every pretty girl he meets, and then wonder why the fathers and mothers look coldly upon him. I tell him he will get himself into a scrape one of these days; but he only laughs, and says that he is not the first young man who has been forgiven for being too charming.”

Eleanor felt her colour rise, and was glad that the window-curtain afforded her some shelter. “And do you think, sir,” she asked, “that a young man of such a disposition is likely to make a good husband?”

“Why, as to that, madam,” returned Sir John, with a shake of the head, “I am not so sure. He is a good-hearted boy, and he means no harm; but he is thoughtless, and thoughtlessness in a husband is a sad thing. I have told him so a hundred times; but he will not listen to me. He says he shall settle down when he is married, and that his wife will cure him of all his follies. But I have lived long enough to know that a wife is not a medicine, and that a man who cannot govern himself before marriage is not very likely to learn the art afterwards.”

This was said with an air of good-humoured sagacity which Eleanor could not but admire. She had expected to find in Sir John Ellison a fond and foolish parent, blind to the faults of his son; and she found instead a man of sense and observation, who saw clearly enough what his son was, and loved him not the less for it. The discovery was in some sort a relief, and in some sort a mortification; for it seemed to show that Mr. Henry Ellison’s failings were not the mere errors of youth, but a part of his character, which those who knew him best had no expectation of seeing amended.

“You speak very wisely, sir,” she said, “and I dare say your son is fortunate in having so judicious a father.”

“Judicious! Nay, madam, I am not so sure of that. I have been too indulgent, I am afraid. But it is a hard thing to be severe with a boy who has never given one any great cause of complaint. He is not vicious, you know; he is only wild. And wildness, they tell me, is a thing that wears off with time.”

Eleanor made no reply; and Sir John, after a pause, went on to speak of other matters—of the country, of the pleasures of a retired life, and of the follies of the fashionable world—in a strain so entertaining that she found herself insensibly forgetting the singularity of her situation. He was, indeed, a man of excellent understanding, and of a temper so sociable that it was impossible to be long in his company without being at one’s ease. He had a story for every occasion, and a comment for every story, which, if it sometimes bordered upon the impertinent, was always delivered with so much good humour that it was impossible to take offence.

At length, however, a movement in the crowd announced the arrival of some new guests, and Sir John, looking up, exclaimed, “Ah! here comes my son at last. I was beginning to think he had forgotten me.”

Eleanor’s heart sank within her. She turned her head, and there, sure enough, was Mr. Henry Ellison, making his way through the crowd with an air of easy confidence, and looking, she was obliged to confess, as handsome and as agreeable as ever. He had not yet seen her; and she had just time to resolve that she would not betray the least symptom of confusion, when he approached his father, and, catching sight of her, stopped short in evident astonishment.

“Miss Vance!” he exclaimed. “I had no idea—I did not know—my father, I believe, has not the honour of your acquaintance?”

“Your father, sir,” said Eleanor, with as much composure as she could command, “has been so kind as to relieve the dulness of my evening. We have been talking of you, Mr. Ellison, for the last half-hour.”

“Of me!” cried he, colouring. “I hope, sir,” turning to his father, “that you have not been saying anything to my disadvantage.”

“I have been saying, Henry,” returned Sir John, with a look of much meaning, “that you are a young man of very good parts, but that you want a little more steadiness. Miss Vance, I am sure, will agree with me.”

Eleanor smiled, and said something civil; but she was conscious that the evening had taken a turn which she had not anticipated, and that she was now standing in the very presence of the danger she had come to London to avoid. The only consolation was that Sir John Ellison, whatever he might think of his son, had spoken of him with a candour which showed that he was not likely to be deceived; and that if Mr. Henry Ellison were ever to win the good opinion of a woman of sense, he must first learn to deserve it.
