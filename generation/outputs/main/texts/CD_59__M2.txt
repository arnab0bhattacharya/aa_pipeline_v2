The firm of Grubb, Halloway, and Skins was situated in a street so narrow that two carriages could not pass without the coachmen engaging in a combat of elbows and tempers, yet it was a place of such profound and dusty gravity that a man might die of a broken heart within its walls and not be missed until the quarterly accounts were balanced. I was a clerk there, of the junior order, possessed of a pen that never ran dry and a spirit that was frequently kept in check by the formidable presence of Mr. Halloway. Mr. Halloway was a man of considerable girth and indefinite countenance, who wore his dignity like a heavy cloak he had found in a closet and never quite got the feel of. It was Mr. Halloway who, on a rainy Tuesday in the autumn of 1841, summoned me to his desk with a look of such alarming solemnity that I expected him to announce either the death of a client or the collapse of the Bank of England.

“Mr. Pumble,” he rumbled, his voice sounding as if it were produced by a large, rusted hinge turning in a dark doorway. “You will undertake a little errand. A small packet, sealed with wax the color of a bruised plum. You will take it to the house of Lady Vane in Essex. You will deliver it into her own hand, and you will return for a reply. Do not open it. Do not look at it. Do not let it drop. Do not let it be seen by anyone but the lady. Is that plain?”

It was as plain as a nose on a man’s face, yet I felt a peculiar chill creep down my spine, for Mr. Halloway rarely performed tasks that did not involve the sorting of ledgers or the polishing of spectacles. I took the packet, which was no larger than a large book, and heavy as a stone of the same dimensions. I was sent off by coach, and the journey into the country was a long, grey, and melancholy affair, the rain beating against the glass with a persistence that suggested personal animosity toward the passenger.

Lady Vane’s house, Thornfield Hall, was a structure of such immense and Gothic pretension that it seemed less a residence than a fortress built by a giant with a fondness for gargoyles and a lack of common sense. The grounds were vast, overgrown, and silent, the trees standing like sentinels who had forgotten their orders but remained in position out of sheer habit. I was admitted to the drawing room, a space so large and so dimly lit that one might have mistaken it for a cathedral at midnight, had it not been for the single, trembling candle on the mantelpiece.

Lady Vane sat in a high-backed chair, wrapped in shawls of such quantity that she resembled a bird nesting in a thicket of silk. She was a tall woman, with a face that was beautiful in the way that a storm is beautiful—impassive, cold, and dangerous. She took the packet from my hands with fingers that were long, pale, and remarkably steady. She did not open it. She did not even look at it closely. She simply held it, weighed it in her hand, and then placed it upon the table with a soft, final click.

“Leave it here,” she said. Her voice was low, devoid of inflection, like the sound of water flowing over smooth stones. “You will come again in a week’s time. Bring nothing. Bring no message. Come alone. You will wait until I call you. If I do not call you, you will leave.”

I departed, bewildered and shivering, having paid the coachman in advance and being left to walk back to the village inn in the mud. But the strange compulsion of the order held me. I returned the following week, and the week after that, and the week after that. For a full twelve months, I made the journey to Thornfield Hall. I waited in the same dim drawing room, sitting on the same stiff chair, watching the same candle flicker and die, only to be replaced by another. I never saw the packet opened. I never saw it moved. It sat on the table, a dark, silent sentinel, as if it had been there since the foundation of the house.

I began to imagine that the packet contained a secret of state, or perhaps a deed of ownership to the very land on which the house stood. I imagined that Lady Vane was a spy, or a widow guarding a treasure, or a woman of such profound melancholy that she required the physical presence of a sealed object to anchor her to the world. I grew to anticipate the journey. The grey skies, the muddy roads, the silent halls, and the cold, beautiful face of Lady Vane became the regular features of my life. I stopped feeling fear and began to feel a strange, quiet companionship with the mystery.

One afternoon in the autumn of the second year, as I sat waiting, I noticed that the candle had burned low. The shadows in the room had grown longer and more distinct. The packet on the table seemed to pulse with a faint, rhythmic light, or perhaps it was only the reflection of the flame in the wax. I leaned forward, my curiosity now a physical ache, a hook in my chest. I wanted to know. I wanted to see. But I remembered the order. *Bring nothing. Bring no message.*

Then, Lady Vane spoke. It was not the usual, cold command. It was a whisper, barely audible, yet it cut through the silence like a blade.

“Mr. Pumble,” she said.

I jumped, my heart hammering against my ribs. I stood up, my knees knocking together.

“Leave,” she said.

I turned and walked out, my feet heavy, my mind a whirlwind of unanswered questions. I walked to the gate, where the coach was waiting. I did not look back. I did not want to see her face, for I feared that if I did, I would see not the cold, beautiful woman I knew, but something else entirely—something broken, or something free.

I returned to London, to the narrow street, to the dust and the silence of Grubb, Halloway, and Skins. I never asked Mr. Halloway what the packet contained. I never asked Lady Vane again. But for the rest of my life, I carried the weight of that sealed object in my memory, a heavy, dark stone in my pocket, reminding me that some doors are best left closed, and some secrets are safest when they are never spoken. The world went on, as it always does, with its own noisy, bustling indifference, but I knew that in the quiet dark of that drawing room, something had been kept, and something had been let go, and I had been the witness to both, though I had seen neither.
