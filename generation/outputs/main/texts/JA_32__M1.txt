The arrival of the post at Fenwick Hall was an event of some regularity and very little consequence; but the arrival of the post on a Tuesday in March, bringing with it a letter of three pages in a hand that no one recognised, occasioned a consternation that spread through the drawing-room with the rapidity of a dropped inkwell. Mrs. Ashworth, who had been engaged in the agreeable task of lamenting the price of candles, fell silent, and the five Miss Ashworths, in various attitudes of work and idleness, looked up as one.

The letter was for their father, but Mr. Ashworth, a gentleman whose mind was better suited to the management of his fields than his correspondence, held it at arm’s length as if it were a specimen of uncertain species. "It is from my brother," he said at last, his voice holding a note of profound surprise. "It is from Edward."

The name, so long unspoken, fell upon the room like a stone into a still pond. Edward Ashworth, the uncle who had sailed for the East Indies twenty years before after a quarrel whose particulars had been lost in the mists of family history, and whose existence was now chiefly a subject for speculation. There were theories, of course. Harriet, the eldest, whose temperament was of a practical and somewhat severe cast, believed the quarrel had been over the estate itself, a matter of enclosures and expectations. The second, Amelia, whose head was full of novels, was convinced it was a tragic misunderstanding over a lady, perhaps even a secret engagement that had been cruelly thwarted. The younger girls, Maria and Catherine, had a less romantic notion, supposing it to have been a dispute over a horse, or a lost wager at cards, while the youngest, Sophie, who was barely out, thought it was simply that Uncle Edward had been dreadfully rude and had been sent away as a punishment.

"It appears," said Mr. Ashworth, adjusting his spectacles, "that Edward has been very fortunate. He has made a considerable fortune in the tea trade and is now returning to England."

"Good heavens!" cried Mrs. Ashworth, a sentiment which was less a prayer than a calculation. "A fortune! And he is coming here? To Fenwick?"

"He is, in fact, already in Bath, and proposes to pay us a visit within the fortnight."

The news, thus delivered, created an immediate and palpable alteration in the atmosphere of the house. The question of the past quarrel was instantly superseded by the more pressing question of the future. What could be the motive of such a visit, after so long an estrangement?

"He is coming to make amends," declared Amelia, her eyes shining with the prospect of a dramatic reconciliation. "He has lived to regret his youthful pride, and wishes to be restored to the bosom of his family."

"Nonsense," said Harriet, with a sisterly frankness that often bordered on the unfeeling. "A man does not cross an ocean to apologise. He is coming to see what has become of the estate. He wishes to assure himself that my father has not squandered the inheritance, and perhaps to make some proposal for its future management."

Maria and Catherine giggled. "Perhaps he has no children of his own and means to choose one of us for his heiress!" they whispered, a notion which, while improbable, was infinitely more pleasing than Harriet's.

"I think," said Sophie quietly, "that he must simply be lonely."

Her observation was received with the gentle contempt usually reserved for the youngest child's opinions, but it was, perhaps, the closest to the truth. The sisters, however, were too occupied with their own conjectures to pay it much heed. The fortnight that followed was a period of intense and varied preparation. Harriet applied herself to a rigorous study of the household accounts, determined to present a picture of flawless economy. Amelia read over the more affecting passages of her favourite novels, hoping to cultivate an air of sensibility that might touch a reformed heart. The younger girls were chiefly concerned with the renewal of their ribbons, a matter upon which their mother's purse, suddenly feeling the influence of a prospective fortune, was opened with uncharacteristic liberality.

Mr. Ashworth alone seemed unmoved by the general agitation. He spent his days as he always had, walking his grounds and discussing the turnips with his steward. When pressed by his wife for his opinion on his brother's intentions, he would only say, "Edward was always a man of business. He will state his purpose when he is ready, and not before."

The day of Uncle Edward's arrival was one of pale sunshine and a sharp east wind. The carriage, a handsome equipage that spoke of a prosperity far exceeding their own, was seen from the drawing-room windows a full ten minutes before it reached the gate. Mrs. Ashworth, who had been in a state of fluttering anxiety all morning, declared she felt quite faint and must have her salts. The sisters arranged themselves in attitudes of studied ease that deceived no one, least of all themselves.

Mr. Edward Ashworth was a man of perhaps fifty, with a countenance that had been browned by a foreign sun but whose features bore a distinct resemblance to his brother's. He was dressed with a quiet elegance that made their own country finery look rather provincial. He entered the room with a composure that was at once impressive and infuriating.

"Thomas," he said, extending a hand to his brother. The greeting was cordial, but it lacked the effusive emotion Amelia had anticipated.

After the introductions were made, and Mrs. Ashworth had recovered sufficiently to offer refreshment, a silence fell upon the room. It was a silence fraught with expectation, each person waiting for another to break it, to touch upon the great and unspoken subject of the past. Harriet cleared her throat, preparing to steer the conversation towards the management of the estate, but she was forestalled.

Uncle Edward looked from one niece to the next, his gaze lingering on each for a moment. "You have a fine family, Thomas," he said. "I am glad to see it." He then turned to Amelia. "My sister-in-law tells me you are a great reader, Miss Ashworth. I hope you have found some diversion in the country."

Amelia, flustered, could only murmur something about the pleasures of a well-stocked library.

"And you, Miss Ashworth," he said to Harriet, "I understand you have a talent for organisation. It is a valuable quality in a household."

The conversation proceeded in this manner for some time, each sister being addressed with a civility that was perfectly correct but gave no hint of his larger purpose. He spoke of the climate in Calcutta, of the price of tea in London, of the improvements in road-building, but of the quarrel, of his fortune, and of his intentions, he said nothing. The sisters grew increasingly frustrated. Here was the resolution to all their theories, the man who held the key to their family's mystery and their own potential futures, and he was discussing turnpikes.

It was Sophie, who had been watching him with a quiet curiosity, who finally ventured a question. "Uncle," she said, her voice clear and steady, "was it very different there? In India, I mean. Was it very far from home?"

For the first time, a genuine warmth entered Edward Ashworth's eyes. He looked at his youngest niece with a new attention. "It was very far, Miss Sophie," he said softly. "Far in every sense. One thinks of home a great deal in such a place, and often with more fondness than it perhaps deserves."

The remark, so gently delivered, hung in the air. It was the first and only allusion to the years of estrangement. And in that moment, the sisters understood that the quarrel, whatever it had been, was a thing of the past to him. He had not come to settle an old score, nor to inspect their inheritance, nor to choose an heiress. He had simply come home, a man grown rich and perhaps a little weary, seeking the connection of family. His purpose was not a grand design, but a simple, human wish for a place to belong after twenty years of wandering. The consternation of the past fortnight suddenly seemed rather foolish, a drama they had created entirely for themselves. Mr. Ashworth caught his brother's eye and gave a small, understanding nod. The tea was poured, and the conversation, at last, became easy.
