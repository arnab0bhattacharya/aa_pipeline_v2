It was a raw November evening when the case of Mrs. Agatha Pemberton was brought to our attention, a case which my friend, Mr. Julian St. Clair, was inclined to treat with a certain weary amusement until he observed the singular character of the lady’s distress. We found her in a handsome, if somewhat faded, drawing-room in Basinghall Street, a stone’s throw from the great financial houses of Lombard Street. Mrs. Pemberton was a lady of some eighty years, small and erect in her black silk gown, her hands, thin and blue-veined, resting upon a carved walking-stick. Her eyes, though clouded with age, held a desperate, unwavering light.

“It is no delusion, Mr. St. Clair,” she said, her voice as clear and firm as a bell. “My son, a good man but a busy one, believes my mind is going. My daughter, Mrs. Finch, thinks it is my nerves. But I know what I know. Each morning, things are not as I left them.”

St. Clair, who had been examining the pattern of the Turkey rug with a detached air, now looked up. “What sort of things, madam?”

“Small things. A thing of an inch, perhaps. The leg of that settee, for instance. I mark its position by the threads in the carpet. This morning it stood a full inch further from the wall than it did last night. The escritoire by the window, the heavy oak chair by the hearth—all have shifted. Just a little. Just enough to tell me I am not mad.”

Her son, a florid, impatient man named Mr. Cecil Pemberton, sighed heavily. “Mother, we have been over this. The house is old. The floors settle. It is a trick of the light.”

“A trick of the light does not move a mahogany table an inch to the left,” Mrs. Pemberton retorted with spirit. “I am old, sir, but my sight is still my own.”

St. Clair held up a hand. “Mr. Pemberton, I beg you. Mrs. Pemberton, you say this has been going on for some weeks?”

“Since the middle of September,” she replied promptly. “Since the noise began.”

“The noise?”

“A rumbling. A deep, grinding sound, like a great mill turning in the earth. It comes in the night, and in the morning my furniture has been moved.”

At this, Mr. Pemberton threw his hands up in despair, but St. Clair was suddenly all attention. The languid air fell from him, and his grey eyes took on the keen, bright look I knew so well. “A grinding sound. From the earth. And you are certain of the date?”

“Absolutely certain, sir. It began on the night of the fourteenth.”

St. Clair rose and walked to the window, drawing aside the heavy velvet curtain. The view was of a narrow lane, and beyond it, the blank, imposing wall of a great stone building. The wall was of granite, save for the lower courses, which were of a newer, lighter-coloured stone. At its base, a few workmen’s tools lay scattered near a large wooden hoarding.

“And what is that building?” St. Clair asked, though I fancy he already knew.

“That is the Capital and Counties Bank,” said Mr. Cecil Pemberton. “They have been enlarging their vaults for the past three months. A great nuisance, with the noise and the dust.”

St. Clair made no reply. He stood in thought for a full minute, his long, nervous fingers tapping a silent rhythm upon the window-pane. Then he turned back to the room, his face alight with that quiet energy which always preceded a solution.

“Madam,” said he, “you have been of the greatest assistance. I believe I understand the nature of your visitor. It is not a man at all.”

Mrs. Pemberton stared at him. “Not a man? But what, then? A ghost?”

“A ghost of a different sort, perhaps. One of iron and steam. Mr. Pemberton, I must ask you to procure a coil of stout rope and a crowbar. And, if it is not too much trouble, a large sheet of brown paper and some paste.”

Within the hour we were in the cellar of the Pemberton house. It was a low, brick-arched chamber, damp and smelling of earth. St. Clair, with a candle in his hand, moved slowly along the wall that adjoined the bank’s property. He tapped the bricks with the handle of the crowbar. For the most part, the sound was a solid thud, but in one particular spot, near the back wall, the tap produced a hollow, booming echo.

“Here,” he said softly. “This is our portal. The rest is simple conjecture. The bank is digging. They are using a steam-driven pile-driver or some such engine to sink their new foundations. The vibration travels through the London clay. It is a slow, steady, relentless force. It is not enough to crack a wall, but it is quite sufficient to walk a heavy piece of furniture across a polished floor, inch by inch, night after night. The deep rumble Mrs. Pemberton heard was the engine at work. Her house has not been invaded; it has been shaken.”

Mr. Pemberton looked utterly bewildered. “But the furniture moves only at night. The men work in the day.”

“Do they?” St. Clair countered crisply. “It is often the case that the most critical work—securing a foundation, for instance—is done when the traffic in the street is at its lowest. The hours between midnight and four in the morning would be ideal. Your mother, madam, is not failing. She is, in fact, a most precise and scientific observer.”

He took the crowbar and, with a few sharp blows, knocked away a loose course of bricks. A small aperture was revealed. Through it, we could see a sliver of the bank’s new cellar wall, and, more importantly, the heavy wooden scaffolding that supported the earth beyond. Even as we watched, a faint, rhythmic tremor ran through the floor beneath our feet, and a low, grinding hum vibrated in the air. It was the sound Mrs. Pemberton had described.

The following morning, St. Clair and I accompanied Mr. Cecil Pemberton to the offices of the Capital and Counties Bank. The manager, a stout, self-important man named Mr. Grimsby, received us with some impatience.

“Vibrations?” he scoffed, leaning back in his leather chair. “My dear sir, our contractors, Messrs. Pike & Son, are the most reputable firm in London. They would not be party to such a thing.”

“It is not a matter of party to it,” St. Clair said smoothly. “It is a matter of physics. I have here a diagram.” He laid a sheet of paper on the desk. “Mrs. Pemberton’s drawing-room is here. Your new vault is here. The intervening earth transmits the force of your pile-driver in a most efficient manner. The lady’s escritoire has been moved some seven inches in the last two months. I have measured it.”

Mr. Grimsby’s professional pride was stung. He sent for the foreman of the works, a burly man with brick dust in his hair. The foreman, confronted with St. Clair’s calm logic and the evidence of the hollow wall, was forced to concede the point.

“We do sink the main supports after dark, sir,” he admitted gruffly. “Less noise for the street. We never thought it would carry that far.”

The matter was settled with surprising speed. The bank, anxious to avoid any legal unpleasantness with a neighbour, agreed at once to suspend the night work and to underwrite the cost of a structural survey of the Pemberton house. Mr. Grimsby even offered a handsome sum in compensation for the lady’s distress.

As we walked back through the crisp morning air towards Lombard Street, the sun glinting on the brass plates of the counting-houses, my friend allowed himself a thin smile.

“A curious case, is it not, Julian?” I remarked. “A crime without a criminal.”

“Precisely, my dear fellow,” said St. Clair. “We are so accustomed to looking for the villain with the jemmy and the dark lantern that we are blind to the villainy of inanimate objects. The old lady’s mind is as sound as the Bank of England. It was the world around her that was shifting, not her perception of it. And the lesson is a simple one: when a witness is dismissed as fanciful, it is always wise to ask what she has seen, and to consider the possibility that she has seen it all too well.”
