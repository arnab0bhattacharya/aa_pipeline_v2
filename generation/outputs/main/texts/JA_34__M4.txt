It was a truth universally acknowledged in the parish of St. Botolph’s that Mr. Erasmus Whitfield, the vicar, was a man of profound learning and of an absence of mind so complete as to render him, in the ordinary concerns of life, little better than a child. His daughter Catherine, now in her three-and-twentieth year, had long since accustomed herself to the management of a household which his forgetfulness would otherwise have plunged into chaos; and if she had acquired a composure beyond her years, and a habit of quiet circumspection which some mistook for coldness, it was owing to the frequency with which she had been called upon to supply, at a moment’s notice, the deficiencies of her father’s memory.

On a Tuesday morning in October, Mr. Whitfield looked up from his folio of ecclesiastical history, and observed, with an air of pleasant discovery, “I have invited Mr. Ashcombe to dine with us today.”

Catherine, who was engaged in the agreeable labour of mending the parlour curtain, laid down her needle. “Mr. Ashcombe, papa?”

“Yes, my dear. The new incumbent at Harlow Green. A most sensible young man, and well read in the Fathers. I met him at the bookseller’s in Oxford, and we fell into conversation upon the Arian controversy, and I confess I was so taken with his remarks that I begged he would favour us with his company. I told him we dined at four.”

“And did you,” said Catherine, with that gentle patience which she had learned to substitute for remonstrance, “mention the circumstance to mamma?”

Mr. Whitfield’s countenance fell. He looked towards the door of the study, as though he might there discern the magnitude of his omission. “I believe,” he said, “that I had intended to do so.”

Catherine required no further explanation. Her mother, Mrs. Whitfield, was a woman of excellent intentions and a delicate constitution, whose nerves were unequal to the unexpected, and who regarded a guest at dinner in the light of a personal affliction, unless she had been allowed a fortnight to prepare for it. To inform her now, at eleven o’clock, that a gentleman—and a stranger—was expected at four, would be to invite an indisposition which might confine her to her chamber for a week.

“I will speak to mamma,” said Catherine, rising with an alacrity she did not feel. “And I will speak to Martha.”

But Martha, when applied to, was desponding. The larder, she said, contained nothing but a cold joint of mutton, sufficient for the family, and a few potatoes. The butcher had been that morning, but Mrs. Whitfield had given no order. The fishmonger did not come until Thursday. There was no time to send to Oxford, and the nearest neighbour who could be applied to for assistance was Lady Frances Delamere, at the Hall, with whom Mrs. Whitfield had been upon terms of cool civility ever since a disagreement respecting the arrangement of the flower-show.

“We must contrive,” said Catherine.

She contrived. She despatched a note to the Hall, not to Lady Frances, but to her housekeeper, with whom Martha was acquainted, requesting the loan of a fowl and such vegetables as could be spared; and she sent the boy to the village for a lobster, if any could be had, and for cream. Then she went to her mother, and, with a diplomacy which would have done credit to a minister of state, represented to her that Mr. Whitfield had chanced to meet a clergyman of his acquaintance, a most respectable and quiet gentleman, who had expressed a desire to pay his respects; that she had taken it upon herself to provide a small dinner; and that there was no occasion for the least exertion on Mrs. Whitfield’s part beyond the pleasure of receiving him.

Mrs. Whitfield received the intelligence with a look of injured sensibility. “Your father, Catherine, is very thoughtless. He knows that I cannot bear to be hurried. But since it is done, I must endeavour to support it. I shall lie down until dinner. You will do as you think best.”

Catherine did as she thought best. By three o’clock the fowl was roasting, the lobster had been converted into a dish of some elegance, the potatoes were boiled, and a tart had been constructed from the apples in the orchard. The table was laid with the best damask, and the silver candlesticks were polished. Catherine, having dressed herself with more than usual care in a gown of dove-coloured muslin, and having seen her mother installed upon the sofa in the drawing-room in a condition of resigned suffering, awaited the arrival of the guest with a composure which was not entirely unmingled with anxiety.

Mr. Ashcombe proved to be a young man of agreeable person and easy manners, with a countenance in which good sense and good humour were happily blended. He was received by Mr. Whitfield with the warmth of a fellow-scholar, and by Mrs. Whitfield with the languid dignity of an invalid who had made a great effort. Catherine, who had determined to say as little as possible, found herself, by degrees, drawn into conversation, and discovered that Mr. Ashcombe’s understanding was of that happy cast which renders learning agreeable rather than pedantic. He spoke of the Fathers without pedantry, and of the country without affectation.

The dinner was served. The fowl was pronounced excellent; the lobster, Mr. Ashcombe was pleased to say, was the best he had tasted since he had left the coast. Mrs. Whitfield, who had been prepared to find everything intolerable, was softened by the attentions of the guest, and condescended to partake of the tart with some appearance of enjoyment. Mr. Whitfield, who had forgotten the whole of his wife’s agitation, discoursed upon the Donatist controversy with great animation, and was only checked by a look from his daughter.

It was not until the removal of the cloth, and the appearance of the wine, that the conversation took a turn which Catherine had not foreseen.

“I understand, Miss Whitfield,” said Mr. Ashcombe, “that you are the mainspring of this household. I have heard as much from my friend Mr. Harwood, who has the living at Nethercott.”

Catherine coloured. “Mr. Harwood is very obliging. I do what I can.”

“You do more than that, I fancy. It is no small thing to keep a scholar’s house in order. I speak from experience—my own father was a scholar, and my mother used to say that she had married a library rather than a man.”

Mr. Whitfield laughed heartily. “Ha! Very good. A library rather than a man. I must remember that.”

“My mother,” said Catherine, with a smile, “would say that a library is at least a quiet companion.”

“And yet,” said Mr. Ashcombe, looking at her with an expression of some interest, “I have observed that the quietest companions are often the most exacting. They demand to be dusted.”

Catherine laughed, and the laugh became her. She was not accustomed to be so noticed, and she found the sensation not unpleasant.

When the guest had taken his leave, and Mr. Whitfield had retired to his study to consult a passage in St. Augustine which Mr. Ashcombe had quoted, Mrs. Whitfield turned to her daughter with an air of some complacency.

“Well, Catherine, I must say the young man is very presentable. I was prepared for something much worse. And the dinner was very creditable. I do not know how you managed it.”

“I had a little help from the Hall,” said Catherine.

“From Lady Frances?” Mrs. Whitfield’s countenance clouded.

“From her housekeeper,” said Catherine. “I did not think it necessary to trouble Lady Frances.”

Mrs. Whitfield was silent for a moment. Then she said, with an effort at magnanimity, “You did quite right, my dear. Quite right. I dare say Lady Frances would have been very glad to have been asked.”

Catherine smiled, and said nothing. She was thinking of Mr. Ashcombe’s remark about libraries, and of the manner in which he had looked at her when he said it. She was not a romantic young woman, but she was not without a heart; and she had discovered, in the course of a single evening, that a gentleman may be both learned and agreeable, and that a dinner of borrowed fowl and improvised tart may be the means of introducing one to a very pleasant acquaintance. She went to bed with a lighter heart than she had known for many a day, and left the morrow to take care of itself.
