The air in the witness box was not merely thin, it was conspiratorial, carrying the scent of stale tobacco, damp wool, and the particular, metallic anxiety of a man who knows his tongue is a trap. Thomas Pym, a coachman of no particular distinction save for the fact that his hands had once gripped the reins of the Marquis de Valois’s carriage on the muddy roads of the French countryside, sat hunched upon the wooden stool as if his spine were a rusted hinge seeking to fold him into the floorboards. He was a man of fifty winters, though the years had not aged him so much as they had eroded him, leaving behind a skeleton of dry bones wrapped in a coat that had seen better decades and better buttons. His eyes, small and watery, darted from the judge’s frown to the prosecutor’s sharp pen, and back again, seeking an exit that did not exist in the architecture of the law.

It was a dark day in London, the kind of grey that seemed to seep out of the cobblestones and settle in the lungs, a pervasive dampness that made the very walls of the courtroom feel as though they were weeping quietly. The memory, however, was not of London. The memory was of the Champs-Élysées, or rather, the less romantic byways of Paris in the year of the great upheaval, a time when the shadows were long and the carriage wheels turned with the rhythm of a ticking clock counting down to a revolution. Thomas had been young then, a boy of twenty with hair the colour of iron and a heart that beat with the foolish confidence of youth. He drove for the Marquis, a man of silk and soft hands, who spoke of art and philosophy while the world outside his windows was sharpening its knives.

The prosecutor, a gentleman of lean, predatory features, leaned forward. “You are certain of the date, Mr. Pym?”

Thomas swallowed, the sound loud in the hush. “I am not certain of the date, sir. I am certain of the smell. The smell of burnt sugar and fear.”

“Be precise,” said the prosecutor, his voice like a whip-crack. “You were driving the Marquis’s carriage. It was the night of the accident. Describe the events.”

Thomas closed his eyes. He did not wish to see the present; he wished to see the past, to ride back into the memory where the truth was muddied by the fog of affection and the terror of survival. He had loved the Marquis, or at least, he had loved the life the Marquis represented. It was a life of polished brass and lavender water, of a world where the wheels of the carriage spun smoothly over the cobblestones, unaware of the blood beneath. But the accident had changed the texture of the air. It had turned the smooth silk of memory into rough burlap.

“It was raining,” Thomas began, his voice trembling, not from fear of the court, but from the sudden, violent return of the cold. “A sleet, sir. Cold and hard. It struck the glass like gravel. We were moving fast. Too fast, perhaps. The Marquis was in a temper, for he had lost a wager, or a love, or a limb; it was all the same to him, for his anger was a constant companion, a ghost that rode in the back seat and did not speak.”

The judge cleared his throat, a dry, rattling sound. “Mr. Pym, please focus on the collision.”

“The horse,” Thomas said, his voice gaining a strange, detached quality, as if he were recounting a story to a child. “The horse was named Bismarck. A fine beast, but a temperamental one. He saw a shape in the alleyway. A small shape. A child. It was a boy, no more than seven or eight, standing in the shadow of the bakery, holding a loaf of bread that was half his size. He was looking at us. He was looking at the carriage. He was looking at the wheels.”

A murmur went through the gallery. The image was too vivid, too stark. The child, the bread, the wheels. The triad of innocence, sustenance, and death.

“You turned the wheel,” the prosecutor pressed. “You attempted to avoid the child.”

“I tried to,” Thomas whispered. “I pulled the reins with all the strength I had. But Bismarck was a creature of habit and fear. He did not want to stop. He wanted to run. The wheel spun, the leather creaked, and for a moment, it seemed as if the world would hold. It seemed as if the boy would step aside, as if the bread would fall, as if the accident would not happen. But the world is not kind, sir. The wheel caught the boy’s foot. Or perhaps his arm. I do not know. I cannot see. The sleet was in my eyes, and the fear was in my mouth. I heard a sound. Not a scream, but a thud. A soft, wet thud, like a book falling on the floor. And then, silence. The terrible, ringing silence that follows a gunshot.”

Thomas opened his eyes. He was back in the courtroom, in the grey London day. The faces around him were blurred, indistinct, like figures in a painting left out in the rain. He looked at the prosecutor, who was writing, writing, writing, capturing the words in ink that would dry and harden into judgment.

“Did you stop the carriage?” the prosecutor asked.

“I stopped the carriage,” Thomas said. “I jumped out. My hands were shaking so badly I could not open the door. The Marquis was already out. He was pale, his face white as the chalk of the road. He looked at the boy. He looked at me. And I saw something in his eyes. Not horror. Not guilt. Relief. Relief that it was not him. Relief that the wheel had taken the child, and not the man. He touched my shoulder. He said, ‘Go, Thomas. Go and wash your hands. We must go on.’”

The judge’s pen stopped. The room was so quiet that one could hear the rain dripping from the eaves outside, a steady, rhythmic tapping that seemed to count the seconds of Thomas’s confession.

“Go and wash your hands,” Thomas repeated, his voice rising, losing the tremor and gaining a bitter, rasping edge. “That is what he said. ‘Go and wash your hands.’ As if the blood were on my palms and not on the wheel. As if the death were mine and not his. I did not go. I stayed. I stood in the sleet and I looked at the boy. The bread was on the ground, broken, covered in mud. The boy did not move. The Marquis got into the carriage. He told me to drive. I drove. I drove until the wheels left the city and the road became a track, and the track became a field, and the field became the nothingness of the countryside. I drove until I could not drive no more, until my hands fell from the reins and I fell into the ditch.”

He looked at the judge, a figure of authority that suddenly seemed small and fragile. “I have driven many carriages, sir. I have driven through rain and snow, through joy and sorrow. But I have never driven away from a death before. I have never washed my hands of a ghost. I have carried that boy in my memory for twenty years. I have seen him in every shadow. I have heard the thud in every silence. And now, I am here, in this box, to tell you that I am a liar. I did not see the boy’s face. I did not see the Marquis’s face. I saw only the wheels. I saw only the sleet. I saw only the fear. And I am afraid, sir. I am afraid that the truth is not in my memory, but in the mud of that road, where the rain has washed it away, and where no one, not even the dead, can find it.”

The prosecutor looked up from his notes. The judge looked down at the paper. Thomas Pym sat back, his spine straightening, the rusted hinge finally releasing its hold. He was tired, so tired that his bones felt hollow, filled with the sound of the rain that had not stopped falling, not in London, not in Paris, not in the twenty years between. He closed his eyes again, and for a moment, he was not in the courtroom. He was in the carriage, the reins in his hands, the wheel turning, the road stretching out before him, endless and grey, leading nowhere, leading everywhere, leading to the place where the truth had gone to die.
