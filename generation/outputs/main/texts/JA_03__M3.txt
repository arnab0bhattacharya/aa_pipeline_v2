Eleanor Croft had long considered her understanding of human nature to be rather superior to that of her immediate circle. At one-and-twenty, she had surveyed the assembled society of her native village of Chaddleworth, in Kent, and found it entirely wanting in any puzzle she could not readily solve. Her godmother, Mrs. Penhaligon, a widow of comfortable fortune and boundless affection for her own consequence, had therefore applied to Eleanor in a matter of some delicacy. Her daughter, the amiable but remarkably insipid Miss Penhaligon, had reached the age of nineteen without forming any decided attachment, and Mrs. Penhaligon was anxious to see her settled before the close of the year.

“My dear Eleanor,” the lady had said, pressing her hand with a fervour that promised equal parts confidence and interference, “you know my poor Clara’s mind is all gentleness. She cannot choose for herself, and I have not the heart to command her. You, who see so clearly, must do it for her. There are three young men in the neighbourhood whom I could entirely approve—Mr. Fanshawe, Mr. Bradshaw, and young Mr. Vernon—and I leave it entirely to your discrimination to say which of them shall be my son-in-law.”

Eleanor accepted the commission with a pleasure that she endeavoured to moderate, but could not wholly conceal. She was sensible that such a trust was a high compliment to her judgement, and she determined to exercise it with all the care the occasion demanded.

Mr. Fanshawe was the eldest son of Sir Reginald Fanshawe, of Fanshawe Park, a gentleman of ancient family and very large estate. He was handsome in the conventional Kentish fashion—fair, tall, and possessing exactly that degree of address which resulted from being much noticed without ever having been obliged to exert himself. Eleanor found him agreeable at first, but upon closer observation she detected a flatness in his conversation that she could not overlook. He spoke of his horses, his tailor, and the weather with equal fluency and equal vacancy. She concluded that a man who had nothing to say could never make a lively companion, and she set him aside with the reflection that a fine estate was but a poor consolation for a dull dinner-table.

Mr. Bradshaw was of a different character altogether. His family had made their fortune in trade—a circumstance which Mrs. Penhaligon professed not to mind, provided the manners were unexceptionable. Mr. Bradshaw’s manners were, in fact, more than unexceptionable; they were studied. Eleanor observed that he never entered a room without appearing to have rehearsed his entrance, and that his compliments, though always elegantly turned, had the unmistakable air of having been composed the night before. He was exceedingly careful never to give offence, which Eleanor suspected arose less from genuine goodwill than from a fixed determination never to compromise his own interests. She could not approve a young man whose chief aim was to be universally agreeable, for she had observed that those who please everybody rarely please anybody very much.

Young Mr. Vernon was the youngest son of a neighbouring clergyman—a man of small fortune but evident good sense. He had read law in London and was now established in a modest practice in the market town. Eleanor found his conversation sprightly and well-informed, and she marked with approval the openness of his countenance and the ease of his address. Here, she thought, was a young man of real merit—neither empty nor affected, but governed by a plain and manly understanding. She had no hesitation in pronouncing him the best of the three, and she composed her recommendation to Mrs. Penhaligon with all the eloquence she could command.

She walked to Penhaligon Lodge on a Tuesday morning, prepared to deliver her judgement with that tempered assurance which she believed became a woman of sense. She was shown into the drawing-room, where she found Mrs. Penhaligon and Clara together, and the elder lady received her with an eagerness that would have flattered a less self-satisfied adviser.

“Well, my dear, and what is your conclusion? I have been on thorns. Clara has been perfectly indifferent, of course—she never has an opinion of her own—but I have been most impatient.”

Eleanor seated herself, arranged her thoughts, and began. She spoke of Mr. Fanshawe’s want of depth, of Mr. Bradshaw’s insincerity of manner, and of Mr. Vernon’s genuine merit, with a precision and a candour which she flattered herself was altogether unanswerable. She concluded by declaring that she could have no hesitation in recommending Mr. Vernon as a man who would make Clara both happy and respected.

Mrs. Penhaligon heard her out with an expression of growing surprise, which Eleanor chose to interpret as admiration of her analysis. When she had finished, there was a silence of some moments—during which Eleanor observed, with a slight uneasiness, that Clara was looking at her work-sampler with very particular attention.

“Mr. Vernon,” repeated Mrs. Penhaligon at last. “Yes. I see. You have reasoned it all out very prettily, my dear, and I dare say your arguments are very just. Only—” she paused, and turned to her daughter with a look that was not entirely comfortable—“only it happens, my love, that Clara is already engaged.”

Eleanor felt all the force of the word already, and sat perfectly still.

“Engaged!” she repeated, as soon as she could recover her voice. “To whom?”

“To Mr. Thorne,” said Mrs. Penhaligon, with the careful calmness of a woman who has been obliged to make the best of a surprise. “Mr. Henry Thorne—you know him, I am sure. The younger brother of Mr. Thorne of Malling Abbey. He has been these three months past in the neighbourhood, staying with his cousin at the Grange.”

Eleanor did know him, but only slightly. Mr. Thorne was a man of very moderate fortune, no profession, and no particular pretension to any of those qualities which Eleanor had been accustomed to value. He was quiet in company, rather reserved, and had escaped her notice precisely because he had never sought it. She had seen him at two or three parties and had thought him dull.

“And how long has this engagement subsisted?” she asked, in a voice which she strove to make perfectly composed.

“Since November,” said Clara, venturing at last to look up. Her colour was high, but her expression carried a mildness that was not, Eleanor thought, entirely free from consciousness. “He spoke to Mama first, and she gave her consent, and we did not like to say anything until his brother had settled the portion. Mr. Thorne of Malling Abbey has been very generous—but he wished the matter kept private till the settlements were drawn.”

Eleanor sat in a silence which she could not immediately break. She reviewed her own proceedings with a humility that was altogether new to her. She had examined three men with the care of a magistrate, and had pronounced her sentence with all the gravity of a judge, while the prisoner had been standing all the while in another dock, undiscovered. She had been so occupied with the exercise of her own discernment that she had never once considered whether Clara might have discernment of her own.

Mrs. Penhaligon, who was kindness itself when she was not managing, sought to soften the blow. “I am sure, my dear Eleanor, your account of Mr. Vernon was everything that was clever. I dare say he is a very superior young man. Only Clara had fixed her fancy before ever you began.”

Eleanor rallied her spirits sufficiently to wish Clara happy, and to say a great deal that was proper and becoming; but she walked home with a temper considerably sobered, and a conviction that she had been, for the first time in her life, a complete fool. The reflection was not agreeable, but it was salutary. She resolved, in future, to observe a little more and conclude a little less, and to remember that the human heart does not always wait for the judgement of a young lady of one-and-twenty.
