Captain Lennox had brought his sister to Bath with no very singular degree of pleasure, and with as little intention of remaining in it as could consist with the duties of a man who had promised his mother a fortnight’s attendance. He had seen enough of fashionable watering-places to know that they were much the same in their essential character—a collection of people assembled to drink water they disliked, and to talk of ailments they would be better employed in forgetting. He had no ailment himself that a return to his ship would not cure, and no wish to be thought a fashionable man by any one of his acquaintance. He was therefore prepared to dislike the place, and he did so with a promptitude that did infinite credit to the consistency of his temper.

His sister, Clara, was of a different disposition. She was twenty-one, of an age to feel that the world was opening before her, and of a temper to find most things agreeable until they were proved otherwise. She had been very little in society, for the Lennox family lived in a corner of Hampshire where society was scarce and not very brilliant when found; and Bath, with its rooms and its parades, presented to her an appearance of gaiety that was perhaps rather more in her own imagination than in the reality. Captain Lennox, who was fond of her in his way—which was to say, rather less fond than he ought to have been, and a great deal more critical—had already begun to feel that the journey had been a mistake. Clara was too easily pleased, too ready to smile, and too little aware that a man might address her with the utmost civility and yet be wholly undeserving of any return of regard.

They had been four days in Bath when the acquaintance with Mr Vernon was first brought about. It happened at the rooms, in the way that acquaintances are made where there is no proper introduction and yet no absolute impropriety—a mutual recognition of some distant name, a passing civility, a pause long enough to allow of conversation without committing either party to a formal claim of acquaintance. Mr Vernon was a man of perhaps thirty, well-looking in a style that was more elegant than striking, and possessed of that easy address which comes from having nothing particular to do and no particular object to pursue. He had spoken to Clara of the music, of the weather, and of the superior elegance of the Upper Rooms over the Lower—all subjects which required no depth of thought and betrayed no strength of character, and which Clara received as marks of a pleasing and cultivated understanding.

Captain Lennox, who had been standing at a little distance and observing the whole, felt no very agreeable sensations. He knew Mr Vernon by report, and the report was not such as to recommend him to the friendship of any man who valued his sister’s happiness. There had been a transaction, two years earlier, in which Vernon had been concerned—a matter of debts, of evasions, and of a friend left to settle what Vernon himself should have honourably discharged. The friend had been Lennox’s fellow officer, and the affair had left an impression on the Captain’s mind which was not likely to be effaced by a few civil words in a crowded room. He had no personal quarrel with Vernon, and he was far too just a man to pretend one; but he had a very decided opinion of his character, and the opinion was not favourable.

He said nothing, however, that evening. He walked home with Clara, listened to her praises of the rooms and the company, and made only one remark, which was that Mr Vernon seemed to talk a great deal without saying much. Clara allowed this with her usual good humour, but did not appear to think it a fault of any consequence. She was at an age, and in a situation, where talking without saying much was rather an accomplishment than an offence; and she had not yet learned, as her brother had, to value silence as the mark of a mind that had something better to do than exert itself for the admiration of a roomful of strangers.

The next morning brought a visit from Vernon himself, and Captain Lennox, who was sitting in the lodging-house parlour with a newspaper he was not reading, received him with a civility so cold that any man of real discernment would have understood it as a dismissal. Vernon, however, was not a man of real discernment. He was a man of that sort who mistakes the absence of rudeness for the presence of welcome, and who finds in every smile, however faint, an encouragement to proceed. He sat down, talked of the weather again, praised the situation of their lodgings, and addressed himself so entirely to Clara that the Captain might as well have been in another house.

Clara, it must be owned, was not wholly without discernment. She could perceive that her brother was not pleased, and she had sufficient feeling for him to wish that he should be. But she was young, she was flattered, and Mr Vernon had a manner which was exceedingly agreeable when he chose to exert it—which was, in fact, whenever there was a young woman present whose countenance gave promise of being pleased by it. Clara’s countenance gave that promise, and Vernon exerted himself accordingly. By the time he took his leave, he had secured an invitation to call again, and the Captain had secured a very uncomfortable conviction that his sister was already half-inclined to think well of a man whom he knew to be wholly unworthy.

Here was a difficulty. He was not her guardian; she was of an age to choose her own acquaintance; and he had no authority to forbid what he could not but disapprove. He had always been taught—and the doctrine was one he had never questioned—that a brother’s influence, to be of any value, must be founded on the sister’s voluntary confidence, and that any attempt to exercise a control which was not strictly his would only weaken the very influence he wished to preserve. He knew, moreover, that Clara had a spirit which, however gentle in its ordinary operations, was capable of being roused to resistance by anything that approached the appearance of tyranny. She would not bear to be dictated to, and if he were to say, “You must not know Mr Vernon,” she would very possibly answer, “And why, if you please?”—and the answer would be one which he could not give without entering into particulars that might be as painful to her feelings as they were disagreeable to his own.

He was therefore thrown upon his own discretion, and his discretion told him that he must be silent for the present, and watchful for the future. He must say nothing against Vernon till he had something more to say than general report; and he must trust to time and to Clara’s own good sense to discover what he already knew. This was a trial to his patience, for he was a man who liked to act promptly and to decide clearly, and there are few situations more irksome to such a temper than the being obliged to wait and observe, when every impulse urges to interference.

Vernon continued his visits. He appeared at the rooms, on the parade, and at the theatre, with a regularity which would have been flattering if it had not been suspicious. He was always civil, always agreeable, and always careful to address himself rather more to Clara than to any one else in the room. Captain Lennox observed it all with a growing uneasiness which he endeavoured, not very successfully, to conceal under an appearance of indifference. He was particularly provoked by Clara’s evident pleasure in the attention, and by the fact that she had begun, in the course of a few days, to speak of Vernon as a man of taste and information—as if taste and information were the qualities most to be desired in a person whose honour and integrity were open to serious question.

He was sitting alone one evening, revolving these matters in his mind with more of anxiety than he would have willingly acknowledged, when Clara came into the room with a letter in her hand and a countenance of some animation. She had heard, she said, from their mother, who mentioned that Mr Vernon was known to the family, and had been spoken of by Sir Henry Templeton—a connection of theirs—as a man of very good fortune and unexceptionable connexions.

“Sir Henry Templeton,” said the Captain, with a smile which was not wholly free from irony, “is a very good sort of man, and I dare say he believes what he says; but Sir Henry is not omniscient, and a man may have unexceptionable connexions and yet be a very exceptionable person himself.”

Clara looked at him with some surprise. “You speak as if you knew something to his disadvantage,” she said.

“I know nothing that I can prove to your satisfaction,” he replied; “and I would rather not speak from report. But I will say this, Clara: I have a very indifferent opinion of Mr Vernon, and I should be sorry to see you give him any degree of regard which might make you unhappy hereafter.”

He spoke with a seriousness which he seldom used, and Clara, though she was not convinced, was struck by it. She had too much good sense not to feel that her brother was not a man to express disapprobation without cause, and too much feeling not to be pained by the thought that she might be giving him uneasiness. She was silent for a few moments, and then said, with a hesitation which shewed that the subject was not wholly indifferent to her: “Perhaps I have been too ready to think well of him. He is so agreeable that one forgets to inquire whether he is anything more.”

This was enough for the present. Captain Lennox did not press the matter further, for he knew that a word in season is often of more value than a whole discourse, and that the mind which is left to its own reflections will frequently arrive at conclusions which it would resist if they were pressed upon it. He was content to have said what he had said, and to trust to Clara’s own judgment for the rest.

Whether his confidence was justified, the event must determine; but he had at least the satisfaction of knowing that he had done what was in his power, without exceeding the bounds which nature and propriety had prescribed to him. He had spoken as a brother, and not as a tyrant; and if Clara should still persist in thinking well of Vernon, it would at least be with a warning which she could not pretend to have never received.
