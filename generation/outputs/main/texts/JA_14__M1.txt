Mrs. Ashworth was a woman who had never in her life been compelled to form an opinion of her own; and now, at the age of fifty-three, she found the exercise so fatiguing that she preferred to adopt the opinions of others, particularly those of her son. This preference had served her admirably for thirty years. It had secured her a comfortable home, a respectable position in the neighbourhood, and the sincere esteem of all who knew her. It was therefore with no small degree of alarm that she perceived, upon the marriage of her son, that the source of her judgements had been multiplied. For now there were two people whose wishes she must divine, and their wishes were not always the same.

Her son, Mr. Henry Ashworth, was a man of excellent sense and quiet habits, the master of a fine estate in Shropshire called Alderwood. His new wife, the former Miss Catherine Bell, was a young woman of good family and a very handsome fortune, who had brought to Alderwood not only a settlement of considerable value, but also a small, well-bound library of her own. This last circumstance, which Mrs. Ashworth had at first viewed as a pleasing mark of a cultivated mind, she now began to suspect was the source of a certain coldness in her daughter-in-law’s manner. For Catherine, once the novelty of her situation had worn off, displayed a marked preference for the society of her books over the society of her new relations.

Mrs. Ashworth, whose life had been a series of small, well-timed attentions, could not comprehend a person who did not require them. She saw in her daughter-in-law’s quiet hours in the library not a gentle enjoyment, but a silent reproach. It was a withdrawal, a statement of some dissatisfaction that Mrs. Ashworth felt it her duty to discover and remedy. She therefore resolved to redouble her efforts. If Catherine was lonely, she should not be so; if she felt herself a stranger, Mrs. Ashworth would make her feel at home.

The campaign of kindness began at breakfast. “My dear Catherine,” she said, her voice full of solicitude, “I fear the bacon is not to your liking. You have taken but the smallest portion. Pray, allow me to send for some of the cold fowl. A young constitution requires a more substantial support than a slice of toast, however excellent the bread.”

Catherine, whose attention was on the morning’s post, looked up with a faint, polite smile. “You are too kind, Mrs. Ashworth. The bacon is perfectly good. I am simply not hungry this morning.”

“Not hungry!” Mrs. Ashworth repeated, as if the words were in a foreign tongue. “But that will never do. Henry, my dear, do speak to your wife. She will not listen to an old woman’s anxieties.” Henry, who was reading about the price of corn, merely murmured, “Let her be, Mother. She knows her own appetite.”

This first skirmish was followed by others throughout the day. “My dear Catherine, you are shivering. The air from that window is most unwholesome. I shall have a screen brought in.” (Catherine was not shivering, but was enjoying the mild September breeze). “My dear Catherine, you have been shut up in that library for three hours together. It cannot be good for the eyes. Come, let us walk to the dairy; the exercise will do you good.” (Catherine had intended to finish a chapter of Mr. Burke’s *Reflections*). Each offer was made with the utmost warmth, and each refusal was couched in terms of gentle, but firm, independence. To Mrs. Ashworth, this firmness was a terrifying symptom of a settled resolve to dislike her.

The crisis arrived on a wet Tuesday. Catherine had established herself in the morning-room, a volume of poetry in her lap and a cup of tea at her elbow. She had been left in peace for nearly an hour, a state of affairs she considered a great luxury. Her peace was shattered by the entrance of Mrs. Ashworth, who bore in her hands a shawl of an alarming pinkness.

“My dear child!” she began, her face a mask of tender concern. “I have been looking for you everywhere. I said to myself, ‘She will be catching her death in this damp air,’ and so I have brought you this. It was my dear mother’s; the wool is of the softest kind.” She advanced with the shawl held out as an offering.

Catherine looked from the shawl to her book and back again. The interruption was so complete, so utterly well-intentioned, that a sharp retort died on her lips. She saw the genuine, desperate anxiety in her mother-in-law’s eyes, and for the first time, she understood the situation not as a battle of wills, but as a simple clash of temperaments. One woman sought connection through action, the other through inaction.

She laid her book aside, the gesture deliberate and final. “Mrs. Ashworth,” she said, her voice calm and clear, “you are so very kind. But I am perfectly warm, I assure you.”

“But the fire is so low! And the shawl—”

“Please,” Catherine said, and her tone was so quiet and yet so absolute that Mrs. Ashworth fell silent. “I must beg you to believe me. Your attentions, which I know spring from the most amiable of hearts, are more than I can support. I am a person of very simple habits. I find my greatest comfort not in being cared for, but in being left to my own devices. A book and a quiet room are all the happiness I require. Indeed, when you seek to provide for me, I feel only the embarrassment of being a trouble to you.”

Mrs. Ashworth stood frozen, the pink shawl dangling from her hands. She had expected gratitude, or perhaps a polite dismissal. She had not expected this calm, gentle, and utterly devastating analysis of her character. To be told that her kindness was an imposition—that her very being was a source of fatigue—was a blow from which she felt she could not recover.

Catherine, seeing the distress she had caused, softened her manner. “I do not mean to wound you,” she continued. “I have the greatest respect and affection for you. But my affection is not shown in such bustling activity. It is shown in the wish for your happiness and your ease. And I beg you, for the sake of that ease, to allow me mine. Let us be comfortable together, without all this… exertion.”

For a long moment, Mrs. Ashworth was silent. The house felt very still. Then, slowly, she folded the shawl over her arm. The simple, ordered act seemed to calm her. “A quiet room,” she said, her voice barely a whisper. “Yes. I believe I understand.”

From that day forward, a new tranquillity settled upon Alderwood. Mrs. Ashworth no longer sought to anticipate her daughter-in-law’s every need. She would see Catherine in the library, and content herself with a nod from the doorway. She would pass her in the hall and merely ask if she was well, without pressing for further conversation. The attentions were not entirely abandoned, for Mrs. Ashworth was a creature of habit; but they were now offered with a deference that acknowledged the other’s right of refusal. The fear of being turned out had not vanished, but it had been replaced by a quieter, more rational hope: that in leaving her daughter-in-law to her books, she was, in fact, securing her own place in the house. It was a modest hope, but it was a comfortable one, and Mrs. Ashworth had always been a woman who valued comfort above all things.
