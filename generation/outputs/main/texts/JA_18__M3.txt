Mrs. Vane, the rector’s wife of Hartleigh, possessed a genius for arrangement that was entirely confined to the affairs of others. Her own domestic establishment was marked by a serene disorder that she never permitted to trouble her thoughts, but the lives of the Hartleigh parishioners were laid out in her mind with the precision of a military campaign. She had lately fixed her attention upon two inhabitants of the village whose mutual solitude appeared to her not as a condition of quiet satisfaction, but as an affront to social harmony, demanding immediate remedy.

The first was Mr. Whitfield, a physician whose practice demanded a steady temper and an unwavering command of the sickroom. The second was Miss Arundel, the niece of Squire Arundel of Hartleigh Park, whose twenty-eight years had been passed in the undisturbed enjoyment of a comfortable jointure and a large, south-facing garden. Both were entirely content with their respective states, a circumstance which Mrs. Vane considered to be a profound error of judgement on both their parts.

"He is so exceedingly rational," she observed to the rector one morning, as she arranged the visiting cards in her ivory case. "And she is so remarkably sensible. It is quite unnatural that two such persons should continue to vegetate in separate dwellings when they might so easily form a model of domestic propriety. I am determined to bring them together."

Mr. Vane, who had long discovered the futility of opposition in such matters, merely murmured his assent and returned to his newspaper. Mrs. Vane, thus encouraged, lost no time in setting her plan in motion. An evening party was arranged at the rectory under the guise of discussing the new charity school, though the actual business of the charity school was, in Mrs. Vane’s estimation, a secondary consideration to the union of souls she intended to orchestrate.

Miss Arundel arrived with the calm self-possession of a woman who has long learned to manage her own affairs, and was seated near the fire with a cup of tea. She was a tall, clear-eyed woman, whose features, if not strictly handsome, bore the stamp of intelligence and good humour. She had no notion of Mrs. Vane’s design, but she was well acquainted with that lady’s penchant for intervention, and had come armed with a quiet determination to be civil to everyone and committed to no one.

Mr. Whitfield arrived shortly after, bringing with him the sober air of a man who had spent the morning attending a fever at the local inn. He was five-and-thirty, of a plain countenance and grave address, whose observations on life were delivered with a dry precision that many mistook for severity. He bowed to Miss Arundel with formal politeness, and she returned the courtesy with equal composure, and for a few minutes they conversed on the weather and the state of the roads with the easy indifference of people who have no expectation of ever thinking of each other again.

Mrs. Vane, however, was not to be defeated by indifference. She swept across the room with a smile of great sweetness and seated herself beside them, declaring that she could not bear to see two such intelligent persons wasting their conversation on the weather when there were subjects of far greater importance to be discussed.

"I have been telling Mr. Vane," she said, "that the village is sadly in want of a guiding light. The school, the poor fund, the Sunday visits—everything falls upon my own shoulders. How I wish, Mr. Whitfield, that you might be persuaded to take a more active part in the parish! And you, Miss Arundel, with your knowledge of the estate and your interest in the poor—you would be an invaluable ally."

Mr. Whitfield, who had heard Mrs. Vane’s appeals before, replied that his professional engagements left him very little time for public exertions, and that he believed the parish to be in far more capable hands than his own. Miss Arundel, with equal promptitude, observed that her own efforts were always of the quietest kind and unlikely to be of any public use whatsoever.

Mrs. Vane was not in the least discouraged. She had anticipated resistance, and had prepared for it with the strategy of a general who expects the enemy to retreat but has already planned the advance. She proposed a walk in the rectory garden, where the late roses were still in bloom, and contrived, by a manoeuvre so artful that it appeared entirely accidental, to leave Mr. Whitfield and Miss Arundel standing together beside the arbour, while she herself lingered behind to examine a specimen of late-blooming jasmine with an attention that defied interruption.

Left thus abruptly to each other’s company, the doctor and the lady regarded one another for a moment with a mutual look of quiet comprehension. It was Miss Arundel who first spoke.

"Mrs. Vane is remarkably fond of gardens," she observed, with a slight smile.

"She is remarkably fond of arrangement," Mr. Whitfield replied. "Whether of roses or of people, her principle is the same. She cannot endure to see anything standing alone."

"It is a benevolent failing, no doubt," said Miss Arundel, "though I confess I have always found solitude to be a very agreeable companion."

"You are singularly fortunate, then, for I understand that solitude is a companion which the world is most anxious to deprive one of, particularly in a village."

Miss Arundel laughed—a low, clear laugh that was wholly without affectation. "I perceive, Mr. Whitfield, that we are of one mind upon at least one subject. I only wonder how we are to escape the kindness that has been planned for us."

Mr. Whitfield, who had not anticipated such frankness, regarded her with a degree of interest that was, perhaps, the first emotion he had felt in her presence that rose above mere civility. "I confess I am not accustomed to confederacy," said he, "but I should be very happy to enter into any alliance that had for its object the preservation of our mutual independence."

"Then let us agree," said Miss Arundel, "that whenever Mrs. Vane contrives to throw us together, we shall each of us be so remarkably agreeable that she can find nothing in our conversation to improve upon. The greatest defence against interference, I have always found, is a calm and uniform civility which gives no handle for speculation."

Mr. Whitfield bowed. "I am entirely at your service, Miss Arundel. I shall be as civil as you could possibly wish."

"You mistake me," she replied. "I did not say civil. I said agreeable. Civility, in Mrs. Vane’s hands, is but the raw material for a match. What we must aim at is a species of conversation so entirely rational and unexceptionable that it leaves no room for romance. We must talk of drainage, or the price of corn, or the management of fevers."

Mr. Whitfield’s mouth twitched. "I see you have given this matter considerable thought."

"I have had occasion to," said Miss Arundel, drily. "Mrs. Vane has lately taken to calling at Hartleigh Park every Tuesday, and I have observed that her visits invariably follow the same pattern: enquiries after my health, compliments on the garden, and then a long and pointed account of the virtues of some unmarried gentleman of her acquaintance. I have learned to be upon my guard."

"I honour your penetration," said he. "And I shall endeavour to be worthy of the alliance. Perhaps we might begin at once, for I observe Mrs. Vane is approaching, and I believe she expects to find us in the midst of an animated discussion of our future prospects."

"Then let us disappoint her," said Miss Arundel, turning to meet their hostess with a countenance of perfect composure. "Mrs. Vane, we have been admiring your late roses. Mr. Whitfield was just observing that the dampness of the season has been remarkably favourable to the growth of mildew, and I was agreeing with him that there is nothing so destructive to a garden as an excess of moisture."

Mrs. Vane, who had hoped to find them discussing something far more sentimental, was obliged to receive this intelligence with what grace she could. She looked from one to the other, searching for some trace of the tender inclination which she felt certain must be lurking beneath so indifferent a topic, but she could discover nothing but the calm, agreeable faces of two people who were entirely pleased with each other’s company and had not the slightest intention of being anything more.

In the weeks that followed, Mrs. Vane redoubled her efforts. She invited them to dinner, she seated them together at cards, she contrived to leave them alone in the drawing-room, and she even went so far as to suggest a joint visit to the poor cottages on the far side of the parish. But her ingenuity was met, at every turn, by a united front of cheerful rationality. Mr. Whitfield and Miss Arundel conversed with an ease and good sense that might have delighted any observer, but which drove Mrs. Vane to the verge of despair. They discussed the school, they discussed the poor, they discussed the drainage of the village green; they agreed upon every point and disagreed upon none; and yet, for all the warmth of their agreement, there was not a single observation that could be construed as tender.

At length, Mrs. Vane was forced to acknowledge defeat. She confided to the rector that she had done all that was possible, and that if two such persons could be thrown together so often and yet remain unmoved, there was nothing further to be done. "I am sure," said she, with a sigh, "that they are the most unaccountable creatures I have ever met. They are perfectly suited to one another, and yet they seem to have formed a pact to be nothing but friends."

Mr. Vane, who had observed the whole affair with quiet amusement, observed that perhaps friendship was, in this instance, a more rational outcome than matrimony, and that perhaps their mutual agreement to remain as they were was, in itself, a kind of union.

Mrs. Vane dismissed this sentiment with a wave of her hand. "Rationality," she declared, "is a very poor substitute for a wedding." And she turned her attention, with undiminished vigour, to the affairs of the next parish.
