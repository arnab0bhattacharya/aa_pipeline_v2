The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which, in the household of Mr. Thorne, was already considered an ill-omened day for domestic tranquility. It was a thick, cream-colored envelope, sealed with wax of a shade so deep it nearly approximated to purple, and the handwriting upon it was so large and flamboyant that it seemed to demand attention before the envelope had even been broken.

Mrs. Thorne, who possessed a constitution remarkably sensitive to any disturbance of the routine, had barely had time to read the first line before she fainted into the arms of her eldest daughter, Margaret. The second daughter, Eliza, whose eyes were sharp and whose tongue was sharper, seized the paper with a trembling hand, while the youngest, Harriet, stood in the corner, wringing her hands and staring at the ceiling as if the answer to the family’s predicament were written there in plaster dust.

"It is from Mr. Thorne," Eliza announced, her voice taking on the precise pitch of one who has discovered a great secret and wishes to be the sole keeper of it. "His brother. The one in India."

"India!" cried Harriet. "Is he dead? Or has he married? Or is he coming to ask for the estate?"

"Silence, Harriet," said Mrs. Thorne, regaining her composure with a dignity that was more performative than genuine. "We must not speculate. We must await the contents with the composure befitting our station."

But composure is a fragile thing in the presence of the unknown, and the unknown, in this case, was a letter that smelled faintly of jasmine and ink. Eliza, who had always prided herself on her superior intellect and her ability to decode the subtleties of human nature, began to read aloud. Her voice was clear, though it wavered slightly at the end of sentences, betraying the nervousness that her face did not.

"My dear Brother," the letter began, "I write to you from Calcutta, where the heat is oppressive, but my spirits are buoyed by the prospect of returning to England. I have made a fortune, as you are perhaps aware, through the trade in indigo and spices. I propose to visit you in Somerset, bringing with me my wife and two children. I trust you will receive me with the warmth of a brother, for I have long felt the sting of our separation, though I am not certain of the cause."

The room fell silent. It was a silence of such profound and uncomfortable density that one might have thought the walls themselves were holding their breath.

"The cause?" repeated Mrs. Thorne, her eyes wide with a mixture of horror and curiosity. "We do not know the cause."

"No, madam," said Eliza, lowering her eyes to the carpet, "we do not. Father has never spoken of it. He only says that they had a disagreement over a matter of principle. But since he has not spoken, and since the brother has not spoken, we are left to guess."

Harriet, who had been observing the scene with the intensity of a naturalist studying a new species of insect, now ventured to offer her theory. "I think he wants the money," she said softly. "He is rich now, so he must be greedy. Perhaps he wants to buy the house. Or perhaps he wants us to give him the garden. It is a very nice garden."

"Harriet!" hissed Eliza. "Do not be absurd. A man who has made a fortune does not come to beg for a garden. He comes to assert his right. I suspect he wants the title. Or perhaps he wants to marry one of us, to secure the inheritance. It is a common device in such cases. A distant relative, wealthy and charming, arrives to woo the daughter of a man of modest means."

Margaret, who had remained seated by the window, watching the rain streak the glass, turned her head slowly. Her expression was one of mild bewilderment. "I suspect he simply wants to see his brother," she said. "And perhaps to eat our chicken. Father always said that Mr. Thorne was a man of very good appetite."

"Your sister," said Eliza, with a slight lift of her eyebrow, "is always so literal. She sees the surface and mistakes it for the depth. But I see the current. I see the ambition."

Mrs. Thorne, who had been sitting with her hands folded in her lap, now opened them and began to wring them again. "I remember," she said, her voice dropping to a whisper, "that there was a quarrel about a horse. A brown mare. Father was very angry about the mare. He said it was a matter of honor. But I cannot recall who owned the mare, or what was wrong with it."

"A horse," said Eliza. "How typical of Father to make a matter of honor out of a horse. It shows his lack of imagination. But it also shows that he is capable of holding a grudge for years. And now, his brother is here, or rather, he is coming. What shall we do?"

"We must prepare the house," said Margaret, rising from her chair. "The guest room needs airing. And the carpets must be beaten. If he is to stay, we must ensure he is comfortable. It would be unkind to treat him with suspicion. He is our kin."

"But if he is a threat?" asked Eliza. "If he has come to take everything?"

"Then we must be gracious," said Margaret. "For it is easier to be gracious than to be afraid. And if he is kind, then we have nothing to fear. If he is unkind, then we have lost nothing but our comfort, which is a small price to pay for the safety of our dignity."

Eliza looked at her sister with a mixture of admiration and contempt. "You are too naive, Margaret. You believe in the goodness of men. I believe in their self-interest. But I suppose you are right. We must prepare the house. I will see to the linens. I will ensure that they are clean and that the buttons are all matched. A detail like that can reveal much about a person's character. If he notices the mismatched buttons, he is a man of perception. If he does not, he is a fool."

Harriet, who had been listening to the debate with growing anxiety, now broke in. "I am afraid," she said. "I do not like strange men. They are always so loud. And they ask so many questions. I do not want to tell them about my drawing. It is not very good."

"Your drawing is excellent, Harriet," said Margaret, patting her hand. "And your brother will be pleased to see it. He will say it is promising."

"Will he?" Harriet asked, with a note of hope in her voice. "Do you think he will?"

"I think," said Eliza, cutting in, "that he will say whatever is necessary to keep his hand in our pockets. But do not trouble yourself, Harriet. Focus on your manners. Smile when he enters. Do not stare. And for heaven's sake, do not mention the horse."

Mrs. Thorne, who had been silent for a while, now spoke up. "I believe we should write to him immediately. We must accept his visit. It would be rude to refuse. And besides, if we refuse, he may come anyway, and then we will be even more embarrassed. It is better to invite him in than to be invaded."

This logic, though circular, was accepted by all. The family agreed that a letter of welcome would be drafted, and that the house would be prepared. As they dispersed to their various tasks, the tension in the room did not dissipate, but rather settled into a low hum, like a bee trapped in a glass.

Eliza went to her room to compose the letter, her mind already spinning with theories about the brother's intentions. She imagined him as a tall, dark man with a ring in his nose, who would look at the Thorne estate and see only a canvas for his own ambition. She imagined Margaret as a victim, naive and soft, who would fall for his charm. She imagined herself as the savior, the one who would see through his lies and protect the family's honor.

Margaret went to the guest room, opening the windows to let in the rain and the scent of wet earth. She thought of the brother as a man who had been away too long, who missed the simple pleasures of home, who would be pleased to see his sister and brother-in-law, and who would perhaps ask for a cup of tea. She thought of the horse, and how strange it was that such a trivial thing could divide two men for so long. She wondered if the horse had been a metaphor for something else, but she could not find what.

Harriet sat at her drawing table, holding a pencil in her hand, but she did not draw. She looked out the window at the garden, where the roses were beginning to bud. She thought of the brother, and how he might like the roses. She thought of her own drawing, and how it might be a way to show him that she was not afraid. She thought of Eliza, and how she was always so sure of herself, and how that certainty was both comforting and frightening.

And Mrs. Thorne sat in the drawing room, staring at the letter, which she had placed on the table in front of her. She did not read it again. She did not need to. She knew what it said. It said that the past was coming back. It said that the walls of their little house were not as high as they had thought. It said that the world was larger, and more complicated, and more dangerous, than they had ever dared to imagine.

But it also said that they were still a family. And that, perhaps, was enough.

The rain continued to fall, tapping against the glass like a polite visitor who was not quite sure whether he should come in. The house was quiet, but it was a quiet that was full of expectation. The letters were being written. The rooms were being cleaned. The minds of the women were working, each in its own way, to prepare for the arrival of a man who was a stranger, and a brother, and a fortune, and a threat, all at once.

And in the garden, the brown mare, which had not been seen for ten years, was not there. But the memory of her remained, in the soil, and in the wind, and in the hearts of the women who would soon meet the man who had once loved her, and hated her, and forgotten her, and now remembered her.

The letter was sealed. The envelope was addressed. And the carriage was ordered. The world, which had been still, began to turn.
