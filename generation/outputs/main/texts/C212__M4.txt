The heat in the classroom was a solid, breathing thing by mid-morning, pressing the starch out of my blouse and making the ink run like thin blood on the boys’ examination papers. From the open window, the sounds of the port of Santa Clara rose and fell: the creak of hawsers, the shouts of stevedores loading coffee and hides, and beneath it all, the ceaseless, mournful cry of gulls. I had been Miss Eleanor Ashworth for fifteen years in this city, and for ten of them, the mistress of the Santa Clara Academy for Young Ladies. I had taught the daughters of merchants, of diplomats, and of the old Spanish families who still spoke of the mother country with a sigh. My school was a small, scrubbed island of English grammar and deportment in a sea of tropical uncertainty, and I had prided myself on keeping it so.

It was a point of pride that politics never crossed my threshold. The revolving door of presidents and generals in the capital, the intermittent skirmishes in the hills, the fiery editorials in the two competing newspapers—all of it was noise beyond the schoolroom walls. I taught the girls to parse a sentence, to play a little Chopin, and to recognise the difference between a salad fork and a dessert fork. These, I felt, were the true civilising forces.

Then, on a Tuesday, a boy came to the gate with a note that smelled of leather and horses. It was from General Rafael Ortega, the leader of the rebel forces in the eastern provinces. His name was a curse in the government gazettes and a prayer in the hovels of the poor. He was a man of the people, they said, who had risen from the coffee plantations to lead a revolution against the corrupt regime in the capital. He was also, according to the other side, a bloodthirsty bandit who would sell the country to the highest bidder.

The note was written in a firm, unadorned hand. It requested, with a courtesy that was almost jarring, that Miss Ashworth might receive him at her earliest convenience to discuss a matter of great personal importance. I was to reply to the Hotel del Puerto.

My first instinct was to refuse. To be seen with Ortega, to be linked to him in any way, was to invite the suspicion of the government and the fury of the loyalist families whose daughters I taught. Mrs. Alvarado, whose husband was a colonel in the government army, would have apoplexy. But curiosity, a trait I have never fully mastered, got the better of me. And there was a certain flattery in the request. A man who commanded armies was asking for my time.

He came the next afternoon, not with a military escort, but alone, on a dusty mule. He was smaller than I had imagined, with a lean, weathered face and eyes the colour of strong coffee. He wore a simple linen suit, and his hands were calloused. He introduced himself without flourish, and I offered him tea on the veranda, away from the curious eyes of the pupils.

“I will be direct, Miss Ashworth,” he said, his voice a low, steady rumble. “My daughter, Sofia, is twelve. Her mother is dead. She has lived her life in camps and on the move. She can saddle a horse and fire a pistol, but she cannot read a poem or name the capital of France. I want her to have what you have. I want her to be a lady.”

I was taken aback. “General, my school is for the daughters of Santa Clara’s establishment. Your… activities… place you in direct opposition to that establishment.”

He smiled, a thin, weary expression. “That is precisely why I want her here. I am fighting for a new world, Miss Ashworth. A world where a man’s birth does not dictate his worth. But a new world still needs its ladies. You represent a kind of order, a… a civilisation, that I respect. It is not the civilisation of my enemies, but it is a civilisation nonetheless. I want Sofia to have a choice. I want her to be able to move between worlds, as I cannot.”

What could I say? To refuse him would be to admit that my little academy was nothing more than a club for the elite, a finishing school for the daughters of the ruling class. He was appealing to the teacher in me, the woman who believed that education was a universal right, not a privilege of birth. I looked at his tired eyes, and I saw not a bandit or a saviour, but a father. A foolish, hopeful part of me thought that by teaching his daughter, I might be a small, neutral bridge between the warring factions.

“I will accept her,” I said, and his face relaxed into a genuine, fleeting smile. “But she will be treated like any other pupil. She will wear the uniform, learn her lessons, and obey the rules.”

“Of course,” he said. “She is a good girl. She will not disappoint you.”

Sofia Ortega arrived three days later. She was a silent, watchful child with her father’s dark eyes and a fierce intelligence that was immediately apparent. She was woefully behind the other girls, but she devoured books with a hunger that was almost frightening. She was clumsy with a teacup, but she learned the history of the Incas with a passion that made the Alvarado girl look like a simpleton. She was, as her father had promised, a good girl. She never spoke of the war, and she never mentioned her father’s name in class.

But her presence was a bomb in the heart of Santa Clara society. The whispers began almost immediately. The mothers who had once vied for a place for their daughters now eyed me with cold suspicion. Mrs. Alvarado withdrew her two girls, citing “a decline in the school’s standards.” The headmistress of the convent school, who had always been a rival, wrote an editorial in the government newspaper, thinly veiled, about a foreign woman who was “indoctrinating the youth with the poison of revolution.”

I was no longer a neutral party. I was the woman who was teaching the rebel’s daughter. I was a traitor to my class, to my country, to the established order. The fact that I was still teaching the daughters of government loyalists only made me more suspect. I was seen as a double agent, a spy in a petticoat. At the market, the fishmonger’s wife would no longer meet my eye. The ladies of the English Club, who had once been my friends, now fell silent when I entered the room.

The final blow came from the other side. One evening, a young man I recognised as one of Ortega’s lieutenants was waiting for me at the gate. He was polite, but his eyes were hard. He handed me a note, not from Ortega, but from his second-in-command.

“Miss Ashworth,” he said, his voice tight. “The General’s enemies are everywhere. He must know he can trust you. What do you hear from the government families? What are their husbands planning?”

I stared at him, a cold dread seeping into my bones. “I am a schoolmistress,” I said, my voice trembling despite my best efforts. “I teach grammar and music. I do not deal in secrets.”

He gave a short, dismissive laugh. “You are the Englishwoman who teaches the daughters of the enemy. You are either with us or against us. There is no middle ground in a war.”

He left, and I stood alone in the twilight, the scent of jasmine and frangipani mocking my former peace. I had been naive. I had thought I could be a bridge, but a bridge is a structure that is walked upon by both sides, and in a war, it is a prime target for destruction. I was not a bridge; I was a battlefield. I had tried to remain above the fray, to be a purveyor of a neutral, universal civilisation, and I had become a traitor to everyone. I looked up at the window of the dormitory where Sofia’s light was still burning. She was bent over a book, her dark hair falling across her face. She was the reason for it all, and she was the only one who did not look at me with suspicion or contempt. I was teaching her to parse a sentence, to play a little Chopin, to recognise a dessert fork. And in doing so, I had lost my school, my friends, and my name. I had lost my little island. I had not taught her enough to know if it was worth it.
