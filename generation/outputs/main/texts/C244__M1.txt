The air in Kelby market was a thick stew of smells: damp wool from the farmers’ coats, the sharp tang of woodsmoke from the braziers, and the earthy perfume of turnips and carrots piled in wooden carts. Eliza’s stall sat wedged between a flinty-eyed poultry seller and a man hawking mended crockery. Her small domain was one of simple, honest goods: eggs the colour of a winter sky, nestled in straw-filled baskets, and rounds of cheese that she had wrapped in vine leaves to keep them cool. She had a way with her cheeses, pressing the curds just so, salting them with a practiced hand until they were sharp and crumbly, a taste that spoke of the rich pasture her mother’s three cows grazed upon.

Her mother, Martha, stood beside her, her face a mask of patient endurance. To Martha, the market was not a place of commerce but of courtship, and her daughter was the prize. Her gaze swept over the crowd, dismissing the farmers with their muddy boots and the town women with their baskets of laundry, searching for one specific figure.

And there he was. John Croft, the grazier. He moved through the crowd with an easy confidence that parted the throng before him. He was a large man, not in fat, but in the breadth of his shoulders and the solid set of his jaw. His coat was of good, dark wool, and his hands, though strong, were clean. He approached the stall, and a slow smile touched his lips as he looked at Eliza.

“Good day, Miss Eliza,” he said, his voice a low rumble. “Your mother well?”

“Very well, Mr. Croft. And yourself?”

“Never better.” He turned his attention to her wares. “I’ll take two of your finest cheeses. The ones with the sharpest bite.” His eyes never left her face as she selected and wrapped them. He paid with a silver shilling, his fingers brushing hers as he handed it over. The touch was deliberate, warm. “You must save the first of the new season’s butter for me, too. I find nothing else tastes so good on a morning slice of bread.”

Martha was beaming. “Indeed she shall, Mr. Croft. A man of your standing appreciates quality.”

Eliza felt a familiar knot of anxiety tighten in her stomach. She could see her future laid out in John Croft’s confident gaze: a stone farmhouse, a good herd of his own cattle, a life of comfort and security, with her mother happily installed in a warm kitchen. It was a good life. A sensible life. But it felt like a coat cut for someone else, a little too broad in the shoulders, a little too formal for her to ever feel at ease.

After he had gone, Martha nudged her. “He is a fine man, Eliza. A man who can provide.”

“He is,” Eliza said quietly, her eyes on the last of the cheese.

“He could make you very comfortable.”

“He could,” she repeated, her voice hollow. For the thought that crowded her mind was not of John Croft and his silver shillings, but of a different man entirely. A man with dust in his hair and cleverness in his eyes.

Later that afternoon, as the market began to thin and the shadows of the church tower stretched long across the cobbles, Thomas Finch appeared. He was lean and wiry, his clothes patched at the elbows and his hands rough with the grit of stone dust. He was a mason, and a good one, but work was scarce and pay was thin. He didn’t approach the stall directly, but waited until Martha was distracted by a woman buying the last of the eggs.

He leaned against a post, a small, almost shy smile on his face. “Did you sell well today, Eliza?”

“I did, Thomas. The cheeses are gone.”

“Good. That’s good.” He looked at her, his gaze intense and direct. “I finished the carving on the Hargreaves’ mantle today. The oak leaves, just as I drew them for you.”

“I knew you would,” she said, and a genuine smile, the first of the day, lit up her face. She remembered him showing her the charcoal sketch on a scrap of paper, his fingers tracing the curl of a leaf with a tenderness that had made her heart ache. He saw beauty in the hard, grey stone, could coax life and softness from it.

“Eliza,” he said, his voice dropping lower. “Come with me to the river path after you’ve packed up. Just for a few minutes.”

She hesitated, glancing at her mother. Martha’s attention was fixed on a farmer arguing over the price of a ham. “I… I don’t know if I can.”

“Please,” he urged. “It’s important.”

The promise she had made to him a month ago felt like a stone in her own pocket, smooth and familiar from the constant turning of her thoughts. It had been a simple thing, said in the fading light of a summer evening. He had spoken of his dream of having his own workshop, of building a small, solid house with his own hands. “It won’t be grand,” he’d said, “but it will be true. And I want you to be in it, Eliza. I know I have nothing now, but I have my skill and my two hands. Wait for me. Give me a year.”

And she had. “I’ll wait, Thomas,” she had whispered, the words feeling both terrifying and wonderfully right.

Now, with John Croft’s silver shilling in her apron pocket and her mother’s hopeful eyes on her, the promise felt less like a dream and more like a chain.

She found him by the river, where the willows trailed their fingers in the slow-moving water. He didn’t waste time.

“I’ve heard talk,” he began, his hands clenching and unclenching at his sides. “About the grazier, Croft. They say he’s courting you.”

The words hung in the air between them. “He comes to the stall,” Eliza admitted, her gaze fixed on the water.

“My offer is a poor one compared to his, I know,” Thomas said, his voice tight with a frustration she’d never heard before. “He can offer you a life of ease. All I can offer is a promise and a future I have to build stone by stone. But I made you a promise, Eliza. I will build that life. I swear it. All I ask is that you remember yours.”

It was the unfairness of it that struck her. He was asking her to choose between a dream and a reality. Between the man who made beautiful things from stone and the man who owned the land the stone was quarried from.

“I remember,” she said, her voice barely a whisper.

“Then tell me it still stands,” he pressed, stepping closer. “Tell me that the words of a poor mason are not so easily forgotten when a rich man comes calling.”

His hurt was a palpable thing, and it wounded her. “It is not like that, Thomas.”

“Then what is it like? Because from where I stand, it looks exactly like that.”

He turned and walked away then, not waiting for an answer, his lean frame disappearing into the twilight. Eliza stood by the river until the chill of the evening seeped into her bones. She thought of Thomas’s clever hands, and John Croft’s steady gaze. She thought of the promise, and the comfort. When she returned to the stall, her mother was waiting, her face set with a new determination.

“He is a good man, John Croft,” Martha said, her voice gentle but firm. “A promise made in the heat of youth is a fine thing, child. But a warm fire and a full larder are finer. They are what will keep you through the long winters of your life.”

Eliza looked at her mother, at the lines of worry etched around her eyes, and she understood. It was not cruelty that guided her mother, but love. A hard-won love that knew the taste of empty promises. But as she looked down at her own hands, calloused from the churn and the cheese-press, she also knew that some things could not be measured in shillings or sold at market. And the choice, she realised with a heavy heart, was not between two men, but between two versions of herself. One she might become, and one she already was.
