The world had always been to Basil Merivale a sort of exquisite confection, and he the most fastidious of tasters. He had worshipped beauty with the ardour of a devotee and the discrimination of a connoisseur, collecting rare editions and rarer sensations, and arranging his life as a dilettante arranges a cabinet of enamels—each object in its place, each colour harmonious, each shadow calculated for its effect. His friends, who adored him, said that he was the most perfect thing in London, and he, who adored himself, was inclined to agree with them. He had a profile that sculptors sighed over, and a manner that suggested the last, most delicate flowering of a civilisation already over-ripe. To be bored was, in his philosophy, the one unpardonable sin, and he had cultivated his tastes with such assiduity that he had almost succeeded in making boredom impossible.

It was therefore with a sense of profound personal injury that he found himself, one grey October afternoon, seated beside Miss Honoria Quill at a country-house tea-table, and listening to her talk about the drainage system of the ancient Romans.

He had been placed next to her by the malignant caprice of his hostess, who had a theory that clever people should be thrown together, and who had never quite forgiven Basil for being beautiful. Miss Quill was, by common consent, the plainest woman in London. She was tall and angular, with a face that seemed to have been assembled from spare parts of other faces, and a complexion that defied every art of the toilette. Her hair was the colour of weak tea, and her eyes, which were her only tolerable feature, were hidden behind spectacles of a severe and unbecoming design. She dressed with a disregard for fashion that amounted to positive eccentricity, and she had a way of looking at one through those spectacles that suggested she was mentally dissecting one's soul and finding it wanting.

And yet, within five minutes, Basil Merivale, the most bored man in England, was listening to her with an attention that bordered on the indecent.

'You must understand,' she was saying, 'that the Romans were not merely engineers; they were poets of the practical. The Cloaca Maxima is not a drain; it is a symphony in stone. The arches, the gradients, the very curve of the conduit—all are calculated with a precision that is almost musical. I confess I find it more moving than the Colosseum.'

'You are joking,' said Basil, who was not accustomed to being moved by drains.

'I never joke,' she replied, 'about sanitation. It is the one subject that has never been known to fail me. People laugh at me for it, but I have observed that people laugh at anything they do not understand, and I have long since ceased to be troubled by laughter. It is the applause of the ignorant.'

Basil looked at her with a new interest. He had spent his life among people who said brilliant things for the sake of saying them, and who would have sacrificed their grandmothers for a well-turned epigram. Here was a woman who said what she thought, and thought what she said, and did not care a fig for the effect. It was, he reflected, the most extraordinary thing he had ever encountered.

He sought her out again at dinner, and again the next morning, and by the end of the week he had discovered that the only conversations in his life that did not bore him to extinction were those he held with Miss Honoria Quill. She talked of everything—of politics and poetry, of science and scandal, of the migration of birds and the morality of the Greeks—and she talked of it all with a clarity and a vigour that made the talk of his fashionable friends seem like the twittering of sparrows. She had no small talk; she had no vanity; she had no desire to please. She was, in short, the most refreshing person he had ever met, and he was, to his own immense astonishment, falling in love with her.

His friends were horrified.

'My dear Basil,' said Lord Arthur Feverel, who had the face of a cherub and the morals of a minor poet, 'you cannot be serious. The woman is a positive fright. I grant you she has a certain—how shall I put it?—a certain intellectual vigour. But vigour, my dear fellow, is not beauty. And you, of all people, to throw yourself away on a blue-stocking with a face like a wet Sunday!'

'She is not a blue-stocking,' said Basil, 'she is a whole wardrobe of stockings, and every one of them a different colour. And as for her face, I have come to the conclusion that it is the most interesting face in London.'

'Interesting!' cried Lord Arthur. 'My dear Basil, interesting is the last refuge of the ugly. It is what one says of a woman when one can think of nothing else to say. It is the consolation prize of the aesthetic lottery.'

'You are quite wrong,' said Basil, with a smile that was almost pitying. 'Beauty is a fact, and facts are the most tedious things in the world. They are what they are, and there is an end of it. But ugliness—ugliness is a mystery, and a mystery is the only thing that can keep a man of intelligence amused for a lifetime. Honoria Quill is the most mysterious person I have ever met. She is a riddle that I have no desire to solve, for the solving would be the end of the pleasure.'

Lord Arthur stared at him. 'You are mad,' he said. 'You have been reading too much Walter Pater, and it has gone to your head.'

But Basil was not mad. He was, for the first time in his life, in love, and he found the sensation so novel that he could not resist indulging it. He proposed to Miss Quill in the conservatory of a house in Belgrave Square, amid the scent of tuberoses and the sound of a distant waltz, and she accepted him with a composure that was almost insulting.

'I have no illusions about my appearance,' she said, 'and I have no illusions about yours. You are beautiful, and I am not, and the world will say that you have married me for my money, and that I have married you for your face. The world is a fool, and I have no intention of correcting it. But I will tell you this, Mr. Merivale: I have never been bored in your company, and I have been bored in the company of almost everyone else I have ever met. If you can promise me that the conversation will never flag, I will promise you that I will never bore you.'

'My dear Miss Quill,' said Basil, 'I would marry you if you had the face of a Gorgon and the tongue of a fishwife, provided only that you would continue to talk to me about Roman drains.'

And so they were married, and the world, which had been prepared to be scandalised, was forced to content itself with being merely bewildered. The beautiful Mr. Merivale and his plain wife became a fixture of London society, and the more malicious of their acquaintance predicted that the marriage would not last a year. But the years passed, and Basil Merivale, who had once been the most bored man in England, discovered that he had become the most contented.

They were sitting one evening in their drawing-room in Chelsea, the lamplight falling on the Japanese prints and the blue china, and Honoria was reading aloud from a treatise on the habits of the glow-worm. Basil, lounging in his favourite chair, watched her with a tenderness that had not diminished with familiarity.

'You know,' he said, when she had finished, 'Arthur Feverel told me the other day that he could not understand how I could bear to look at you across the breakfast-table.'

Honoria looked up from her book. 'And what did you tell him?'

'I told him that I did not look at you across the breakfast-table. I listened to you. And I told him that listening to you was the most beautiful thing in the world, and that beauty, being a form of truth, was not to be judged by the vulgar standards of the photographer.'

Honoria smiled, and for a moment her plain face was transfigured by something that was better than beauty.

'You are a great deal too clever for your own good, Basil,' she said. 'But I am glad you married me. You have saved me from becoming a blue-stocking, and I have saved you from becoming a bore. It is a very fair exchange.'

And she went on reading about the glow-worm, and Basil Merivale, listening, reflected that he had found the one thing that his fastidious soul had always sought—a conversation that was as endless as it was delightful, and a companion who was, in the truest sense of the word, a work of art.
