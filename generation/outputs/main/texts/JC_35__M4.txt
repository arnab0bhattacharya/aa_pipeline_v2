The bell above the shop door had a thin, apologetic note, as though aware that it announced no customer of consequence, and Mr. Hartwell, who was wrapping a volume of sermons in brown paper at the rear counter, did not immediately raise his head. The afternoon had been slow, the light in the shop the colour of weak tea, and the dust that hung in the long beams from the window seemed to him a kind of sediment of the day's quiet, settling by degrees over the morocco and the calf and the buckram of other men's thoughts. When at last he looked up, he saw not the expected curate or the retired schoolmaster, but three men in dark coats, standing quite still just within the door, and the largest of them held his hat against his chest in a manner that was somehow more alarming than any brandishing of a warrant.

"Mr. Hartwell," said this man, not a question. "I believe you have a back room."

The bookseller set down the sermons and folded his hands upon the counter with a deliberation that cost him something. He was a man of sixty, soft of voice and slow of gesture, who had come to London years ago from a northern town and had never quite lost the habit of measuring his words as though they were stock he could not afford to waste. "I have a back room," he said. "It is where I keep my ledgers and my tea."

"And your visitors," said the man.

"I have had visitors," Mr. Hartwell admitted. "A bookseller has visitors. It is the nature of the trade."

The man looked round the shop with the patient, incurious air of one who has seen many shops and many trades and has long since ceased to be surprised by any of them. He was not, Mrs. Hartwell thought afterwards, a brutal man; he was merely thorough, and thoroughness in his profession wears the face of an unanswerable calm. "You will not object," he said, "if we look at your ledgers and your tea."

It was at this moment that Mrs. Hartwell appeared from the passage behind the shop, wiping her hands upon her apron, and saw the three of them, and understood in a single flush of perception, as women of her sort do, more than her husband had understood in the whole course of his gentle and evasive life. She was a small, upright woman with grey eyes that had a trick of fixing upon a thing and holding it, and she fixed them now upon the largest man and said, "You will find nothing in the back room, sir, but the accounts and the kettle, and if it is my husband you want you may ask him here, for he is a poor hand at a lie and I would not have him disgraced in his own shop."

The man inclined his head slightly, as though he were acknowledging a point in a game of which he alone knew the rules, and moved past her into the passage. The two others followed. Mrs. Hartwell stood in the shop among her husband's books, and heard the door of the back room open, and the scrape of a chair, and the sound of a drawer. She heard, too, the small indrawn breath of her husband, who had followed them, and she knew, without seeing, what he was seeing: the round table with its cloth, the four chairs, the copy of the *Times* folded to a particular column, the little brass ashtray in which there were three ends of cigarettes of a foreign make, and the faint, persistent odour of tobacco that was not English tobacco, and the smell of strong tea, and the smell, she had always thought, of other men's homes, other men's countries, other men's grievances.

She had pretended not to know. That was the truth of it, and she knew it now with a clarity that was almost a relief, as a woman knows at last the name of the illness that has been wasting her. She had pretended not to know when the letters came addressed in hands she could not read, with the stamps of cities she had only seen in the illustrated papers; she had pretended not to know when her husband spoke of an evening's "business" and came to bed at two in the morning smelling of tobacco and of something else, something she could only call excitement, the excitement of men who talk of great things in low voices. She had kept his tea warm and his silence safe and had asked no questions, and in that long pretending she had come to know more than any question could have told her, for the things a woman learns in a house are learned through the walls and the floorboards and the small hours, and are not the less certain for being unspoken.

The largest man came back into the shop holding a sheet of paper, not a large sheet, a thing of poor quality, covered in a close, slanting hand. "This," he said, "was in the drawer of the table. It is a list of names, madam. Some of them are known to us. The others will be known to us presently. Your husband says he cannot read the language."

"He cannot," said Mrs. Hartwell.

"You can," said the man, not unkindly.

She looked at the paper and then at her husband, who stood in the passage with his hands hanging at his sides and his face the colour of the paper itself. She thought of the evenings she had carried in the tray and set it down and gone out again, closing the door softly behind her, and of the voices that had gone on and on in that room, voices of men who had lost their country and could not lose the habit of it, who spoke of it as other men speak of a woman who has died, with anger and with tenderness and with a kind of hopeless fidelity. She had understood enough to know that they were not wicked men. She had understood enough to know that they were dangerous. And she had understood, in the way that women understand these things, that her husband, who could not read a word of their language, had given them the one thing they could not get elsewhere in London, which was a room with a door that closed and a man who asked nothing and a kettle that was always hot.

"I can read it," she said. "It is a list of names. I do not know the names. I have never asked the names."

"You have heard them spoken."

"I have heard a great many things spoken through that door," said Mrs. Hartwell, "and I have forgotten them, sir, as a woman forgets the talk of men who are not talking to her. If you were to ask me what they said I could not tell you. I could tell you that they were cold and that they were poor and that they were far from home. That is what I know."

The man folded the paper and put it in his pocket. He looked at her for a moment with something that was not quite contempt and not quite respect, the look of a man who has found in an unexpected place a thing he recognises. "You will both come with me," he said. "There will be questions."

"I will come," said Mrs. Hartwell. "But you will not take my husband through the street in his slippers. He is a respectable man, sir, and he has kept a clean shop for thirty years, and whatever he has done he has done for the sake of a kindness, which is not a thing the law has any great opinion of, I know, but it is the only thing he has ever been guilty of."

The man said nothing. He waited while she fetched her husband's coat and helped him into it, her hands steady, her face composed, and then she took her own shawl from the hook and put it on and turned to the door. The bell above it gave its thin, apologetic note as they went out, and the dust in the long beams of the afternoon light stirred and settled again, and the shop stood quiet behind them, full of other men's thoughts, waiting for the custom that would not come.
