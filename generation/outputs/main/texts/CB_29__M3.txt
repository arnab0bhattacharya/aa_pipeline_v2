The fever had broken, but it left me a creature of thin glass and trembling sinews. I lay in the narrow bed of my attic chamber at Thornfield Grange, listening to the rain lash the Cumberland slates. The wind howled up from the fells, bringing with it the scent of wet peat and decaying pine. It was a savage country, this northern edge of England, and it bred a savage people; yet it was in this very wilderness of rain and rock that I had found my unexpected mercy. It was Mrs. Saxon who had brought me back from the dark brink. Mrs. Saxon, the housekeeper, a woman whose iron-grey hair and granite jaw had seemed to me, these six months past, carved from the very cliffs outside my window. She had never spoken a gentle word to me. Her regime was one of exacting, freezing correctness. I had believed she despised me for my youth, my low pay, my sex; perhaps, too, for the quiet defiance I could never wholly suppress when she found fault with my teaching of Miss Eleanor. 

When the fever struck me down with the suddenness of a lightning bolt, I had expected to be left to my fate in this isolated attic. The master was away in London; the other servants kept their distance, fearing the contagion. It was Mrs. Saxon who forced open my door, bringing with her the draughts of bitter medicine and the stern, unyielding commands to live. 

She sat beside me now, her hands—those large, capable, relentless hands—folding a coarse linen cloth. The fire in the grate was low, casting flickering, giant shadows on the wall. I watched her profile, sharp and severe against the firelight, and felt a sudden, desperate need to pierce her armour.

"You have been very good to me, Mrs. Saxon," I whispered, my voice a mere thread in the vast quiet of the room. "I did not think you cared whether I lived or died."

She did not pause in her folding. "It is my duty to see that the master's household does not fall to ruin through the death of a servant," she replied, her tone clipped and even. "A funeral in a house of mourning is an expensive disruption."

"Is that the only reason?" I asked, a sudden, feverish impulsiveness overcoming my usual prudence. "Because it was your duty?"

She looked at me then. Her eyes, usually so cold and unreadable, held a sudden, smouldering intensity. "What other reason should there be, Miss March? You are a governess. I am a housekeeper. We are both paid to do our respective tasks. Let us not pretend to a sentiment that has no root in this house."

"But you sat up with me for three nights," I persisted, raising myself on one thin elbow, ignoring the dizziness that swam in my brain. "You held my hand when the delirium was upon me. I remember your voice, Mrs. Saxon. You spoke to me as if I were a child of your own. You told me to fight."

She stood up abruptly, her skirts rustling against the bare boards. "You were dreaming, Miss March. The fever makes fools of the tongue and liars of the memory. I spoke to you only to keep you conscious, as one speaks to a drunken man to keep him from freezing in a ditch."

I looked at her, standing rigid and tall, her face half in shadow, half illuminated by the ruddy glow. She was a hard woman, yes; but the hardness was not the coldness of stone. It was the brittleness of iron that has been thrust, white-hot, into icy water. It was the hardness of a thing that has been broken, and then fiercely, stubbornly, set back together. 

"You cannot deceive me," I said, my voice gaining a sudden strength. "I know what it is to be alone in a house full of people. I know what it is to be looked at with cold suspicion, to be treated as neither servant nor family, but as a necessary evil. You know it too, Mrs. Saxon. I see it in your face."

She turned sharply, her breath catching in a hiss. "You see nothing but the shadows of your own weaknesses. You are weak, Miss March. You let the children wear you down; you let the master's indifference gnaw at your spirit. You are too soft. This house will eat you alive."

"Then why did you save me from it?" I cried, a sudden surge of passion breaking through my weakness. "Why did you not let the fever take me, if I am so weak? Why did you fight for my life with such fury?"

She stared at me, and for a moment I thought she would strike me, or turn and leave the room. Her breast heaved; her strong hands clenched at her sides. The wind outside shrieked against the windowpanes, and the fire crackled and spat. In the silence, I felt the whole weight of the house pressing down upon us—the dark, oak-panelled corridors, the echoing staircases, the rooms where the master's family lived in a cold, elegant isolation, untouched by the misery that festered in their service.

"Because I was once a governess," she said at last, her voice low and trembling, yet carrying a fierce, bitter truth. "Because I, too, was young, and poor, and proud. Because I, too, sat in that very schoolroom, and taught a previous generation of this family's children, and was treated as a piece of useful furniture, to be polished when needed, and kicked into a corner when not. Because I, too, loved a man who was above me, and was discarded when it suited him, and left to rot in this very attic, with nothing but my shame and my broken heart for company."

She stopped, her breath coming in short, harsh gasps. I lay perfectly still, the tears pricking my eyes. The revelation was like a blow, stripping away the layers of her severity, exposing the raw, bleeding wound beneath. I saw her, suddenly, not as the granite housekeeper, but as a young woman, sitting in this same room, facing the same bleak future, the same slow death of hope. 

"The master's father," she whispered, her eyes fixed on the fire. "He was the younger son then, with no expectations, and he professed to love me. I was a fool. I believed him. And when the elder brother died, and he became the heir, he cast me off without a thought. He married a woman of his own class, and I was given the choice of becoming his housekeeper, or leaving without a reference. I stayed. I stayed because I had nowhere else to go. I stayed because I wanted to see him suffer. And he never did. He lived a long, comfortable life, and died in his bed, surrounded by his legitimate children, and I was left to manage his household, and to watch his son grow up to be exactly like him."

She turned to me, and her face was no longer hard. It was ravaged, ancient, filled with a despair so profound it seemed to reach out and touch my very soul. "I saved you, Miss March, because I could not bear to see another young woman broken on this wheel. I saved you because I saw myself in your eyes, and I knew that if you did not fight, you would end up like me—a creature of duty and bitterness, with nothing left but the ashes of a life that might have been."

She moved towards the door, her steps slow and heavy. "Rest now," she said, her voice regaining some of its usual, measured tone. "You will need your strength. The master returns on the morrow, and the children will be restless."

"Mrs. Saxon," I called after her, my voice barely a whisper. "Thank you."

She paused, her hand on the latch. "Do not thank me, Miss March," she said, without turning. "Thank God that He has seen fit to spare you, and pray that He gives you the strength to flee this place before it claims you, as it claimed me."

The door closed softly behind her. I lay back on my thin pillow, the tears falling freely now, not for my own weakness, but for the tragic, hidden life of the woman who had nursed me back from the grave. The fire died down to a red glow, and the wind continued its mournful cry around the eaves of Thornfield Grange. I felt a sudden, fierce determination rising within me, a resolve not to be broken, not to be consumed by this house of shadows. I would live. I would endure. And I would remember.
