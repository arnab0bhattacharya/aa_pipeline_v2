The thirteenth engagement was announced with the precise, surgical delicacy of a surgeon cutting out a tumor that everyone suspected but no one wished to name. It was a Tuesday, a day of little consequence, and the news traveled through the drawing rooms of Mayfair not as a scandal, but as a curious meteorological event—something to be observed, recorded in the diaries, and then, perhaps, forgotten by the weekend. Lady Constance Pym, whose family had been firmly entrenched in the social stratosphere since the reign of George III, stood before her mirror, adjusting the knot of a emerald brooch that cost more than the annual income of a respectable parson. She looked, to her own eyes, perfectly composed. To the eyes of her mother, Lady Pym, who sat in the corner like a statue of disapproval carved from grey marble, she looked like a young woman who had just committed a social murder and was currently dusting the weapon.

"You are making a mistake, Constance," Lady Pym said. Her voice was low, a dry rustle of old silk. "Not a moral mistake, of course. You have no morals to speak of, and that is your one redeeming feature. But a practical one. You have broken twelve engagements. The thirteenth is an abomination. It is numerologically offensive. It invites the eye of the inevitable to rest upon us with a weight that no amount of lavender water can dispel."

"Mother," Constance replied, smoothing a strand of hair that had escaped its pins, "if superstition were a factor in human affairs, the world would have ended long ago. We would have died of the sheer accumulation of black cats and open umbrellas. Besides, I do not break engagements. Engagements break. It is a passive construction, but it is the only honest one. I am merely the site where the breaking occurs."

Lord Julian Vane, the thirteenth, entered the room at that moment. He was a tall man, possessing the kind of handsome, cold regularity that suggests a life lived in strict accordance with a schedule. He wore a morning coat of midnight blue, and his eyes, which were the color of polished steel, moved from Constance to her mother and back again with the impartiality of a judge assessing a plea for clemency.

"Madam," he said, bowing with a stiffness that was almost endearing in its lack of grace. "I trust I am not intruding upon a private moment of domestic harmony."

"Harmony is a word I have not used in this house since the last time you proposed, Julian," Lady Pym said, not looking up from the embroidery she was doing, though her needle pricked her finger, and a drop of blood, bright and startling, welled up on the linen. "You are a man of action, I am told. You wish to marry my daughter before the season ends. You wish to be the first to reach the altar."

"I wish to be the first to be accepted," Julian corrected, his voice smooth as poured glass. "There is a difference. One implies speed; the other implies validity. I have no intention of running, Madam. I intend to walk. But I intend to walk with purpose."

Constance smiled. It was a small, sharp smile, like a crack in a porcelain cup. "My mother believes that the number thirteen is unlucky. She believes that if I marry you, the house will collapse, the servants will strike, and the roses in the garden will turn to thorns. She is a very sensible woman, Julian. That is why she is so tired."

"Sensibility is often just fear with a better tailor," Constance added, turning to face the window. The view showed the garden, where the lilies were opening in a pale, ghostly white against the dark green of the ivy. "But tell me, Julian. Why me? You are a man of high standing and impeccable taste. You could have chosen a girl who had not broken a single engagement, a girl who was safe, boring, and utterly devoid of surprise. Why a girl who is a statistical anomaly?"

Julian adjusted his cufflinks. They were made of onyx and gold, engraved with a pattern that looked like a maze. "Because, Lady Constance, a girl who has broken twelve engagements is not a risk. She is a guarantee. If she has broken twelve, she has the strength of character to break a thirteenth. I do not want a wife who is held together by habit or fear. I want a wife who is held together by choice. And I suspect that your choice is the only thing in this family that is not broken."

Lady Pym stopped her embroidery. The needle hung in the air, trembling slightly. "You flatter her," she said. "You are flattering her to your own advantage. You think that because she is difficult, she is desirable. But difficulty is a disease, Julian. And I will not have my daughter infected by a man who thinks he can cure it with a ring."

"Madam," Julian said, "you are confusing the diagnosis with the cure. I am not here to cure her. I am here to marry her. There is a distinction. One requires a doctor; the other requires a priest. And I intend to provide the priest."

Conance walked to the center of the room. The light from the window caught the emerald brooch, throwing a small, green star onto the floor. "Mother," she said softly. "You fear the thirteenth because it completes a set. You think that if I do it, I am finished. But you are wrong. The thirteenth is not the end. It is the beginning. It is the point at which the pattern changes. It is the point at which I am no longer the girl who breaks engagements, but the woman who chooses them. And I choose this one, Julian, because he is the first man who has looked at my history and seen not a series of failures, but a series of rehearsals."

Julian looked at her, and for the first time, the steel in his eyes softened into something warmer, something almost like pity, or perhaps recognition. "Then we shall have a wedding," he said. "A very simple one. No speeches. No long processions. Just the vows. And then, perhaps, a short drive in the park. The air is good today. It smells of rain."

Lady Pym set down her embroidery. The blood from her finger had dried into a tiny, dark flake on the white linen. She looked at her daughter, and then at the young man who had just declared war on her superstitions with the calm assurance of a banker clearing a debt.

"You are a dangerous man, Julian," she said.

"I am an engaged man, Madam," he replied. "There is a difference. One is a threat to the status quo; the other is a promise of the future. I prefer the promise."

Constance took his hand. It was cold, but steady. She looked at her mother, who was watching them with the expression of a woman who has just watched a chess match and realized that the board has been turned upside down.

"Shall we go, Julian?" Constance asked.

"We shall," he said.

They walked out of the room, their footsteps silent on the thick carpet. The door closed behind them with a soft click, a sound that was final, and yet, in the quiet of the drawing room, it sounded like the first page of a book being turned. Lady Pym sat alone, the blood on her finger a small, red secret in the white silence of the room. She picked up her embroidery again, and began to stitch, the needle moving up and down in a rhythm that was as steady as a heartbeat, and as inevitable as fate. Outside, the rain began to fall, washing the dust from the windows, and for a moment, the world seemed perfectly, terrifyingly clear.
