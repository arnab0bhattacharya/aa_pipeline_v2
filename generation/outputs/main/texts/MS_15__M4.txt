The road stretched before me like a grey ribbon unspooled from some vast and indifferent hand, and I walked it alone, as I had walked it these many weeks, with no companion save the wind that sighed through the empty fields and the phantom footsteps of my own thoughts. England lay all about me, a country I had known in childhood as a place of bustling towns and crowded thoroughfares, now fallen into a silence so profound it seemed to press upon my very soul. The sickness had swept through like a scythe through wheat, and where once had dwelt the hum of commerce and the laughter of children, there remained only the hollow shells of houses and the melancholy cry of birds who had reclaimed their ancient dominion.

I had set forth from the village of Wren's Hollow in the county of Shropshire, where my brother Edmund and I had made our home after the great dying had taken our parents, and where I had waited through the long months of winter and the hesitant budding of spring for some word from him. He had gone south, he said, to seek news of our aunt in Canterbury, and he had promised to return within the month; but the month had passed, and another, and yet another, and no letter came, for the postal service, like all the machinery of civilisation, had crumbled into disuse. At last I could endure the torment of uncertainty no longer, and I resolved to go in search of him, though the journey should be long and the roads unsafe for a woman alone. What safety, indeed, remained in a world where the very air had been poisoned and the bonds of society dissolved? I feared no ruffian, for ruffians had perished with their fellows; I feared only the solitude, and the dreadful possibility that I might find my brother too late.

The first day of my journey I encountered no living soul, and the silence of the countryside was so complete that I found myself speaking aloud to break it, reciting verses my mother had taught me, though my voice sounded strange and thin in the vast emptiness. On the second day, as I approached the ruins of a market town whose name I have forgotten, I espied a figure seated by the roadside, wrapped in a cloak of faded blue, with a bundle at her feet and a look of such profound desolation upon her countenance that my heart went out to her ere ever we had exchanged a word.

She was an elderly woman, her hair white as the frost upon the hedgerows, and her eyes, though dimmed with years and weeping, held a gentleness that spoke of a spirit unbroken by sorrow. I approached her with such courtesy as I could muster, and asked if she had need of assistance; but she shook her head and bade me sit beside her, saying that she had walked as far as her strength would carry her and could go no further.

"I am called Margaret Thorne," she said, "and I have come from the north, from a village near the Scottish border, where I lived all my days with my husband and my son. They are both gone now, taken by the fever within a week of one another, and I am left alone in the world, with nothing to do but wander until I too am called home. I thought to find my sister in the south, but I have learned that she perished in the same manner as my menfolk, and now I know not whither to turn."

Her words pierced me with a grief I knew too well, and I told her of my own losses, of my parents taken in the first wave of the sickness, of my brother Edmund gone to the south and silent these twelve months past. She listened with that patient sympathy which only those who have suffered can offer, and when I had finished she took my hand in hers and pressed it warmly.

"You are young," she said, "and youth carries hope even into the darkest places. Seek your brother, child, and do not despair; for if he lives, he will be found, and if he does not, you will at least have the comfort of knowing that you did not abandon him to the silence."

I remained with her until the sun began to sink toward the western hills, and I shared with her the bread and cheese I carried in my satchel, and she in turn gave me a small flask of water from a spring she had passed. When at last I rose to continue my journey, she blessed me with words so tender that tears sprang to my eyes, and I left her seated by the roadside, a figure of patience and resignation, waiting for whatever end should come.

The days that followed blurred one into another, and I walked through towns whose streets were choked with weeds and whose windows stared at me like the empty sockets of skulls. In one such place I found a man—a young man, scarce older than myself—sitting on the steps of what had once been a church, with a book open upon his knees and a look of such abstraction that he seemed not to see me until I stood directly before him. His name was Thomas Ashby, he said, and he had been a student of medicine in London before the sickness came, and now he wandered from place to place, seeking he knew not what, unable to reconcile himself to the ruin of all his hopes.

"I studied to heal," he said, "and I could not heal them. I watched them die, one after another, and I could do nothing—nothing but hold their hands and murmur words of comfort that sounded hollow even to my own ears. What use is knowledge when it cannot save? What purpose has learning when the world it served has crumbled into dust?"

I had no answer for him, for I too had felt the impotence of which he spoke, the dreadful sense that all human endeavour was but a fragile thing, broken by a breath from the heavens. Yet as we spoke, I perceived that there was in his voice a note of hope that belied his words, a yearning for connection that drew him to me as it drew me to him, and I understood that even in the midst of desolation, the human heart clings to its fellows with a tenacity that no catastrophe can wholly extinguish.

We parted at a crossroads, he to continue his aimless wandering, I to press onward toward Canterbury, and I confess that I looked back more than once at his retreating figure, feeling that I had lost something precious in the parting. But my purpose was fixed, and I would not be turned aside from it; and so I walked on, through villages and hamlets, through fields that had once been cultivated and now ran wild with brambles and nettles, until at last, upon a day when the sky was heavy with clouds and the air smelled of rain, I came within sight of the cathedral spires of Canterbury, rising above the rooftops like the fingers of a hand stretched upward in supplication.

My heart beat violently as I entered the city, for here, if anywhere, I might find some trace of Edmund, some word of his passing, some sign that he had lived and breathed and hoped as I did. I inquired at the houses that yet remained inhabited, I questioned the few souls I met upon the streets, but no one could tell me aught of a young man from Shropshire, and my hope began to falter. At last, as evening drew on and I stood before the great western door of the cathedral, weary and heartsick, a figure emerged from the shadows of the porch—a figure I knew as well as my own soul, thin and worn and changed by suffering, but unmistakably my brother.

"Edmund!" I cried, and he turned, and his face, which had been grey with exhaustion, was transfigured by a joy that I shall remember to my dying day. He caught me in his arms, and we wept together, there in the shadow of that ancient fane, and I knew that all my weary miles had been as nothing, for I had found him, and in finding him I had found myself.
