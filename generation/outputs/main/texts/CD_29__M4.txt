The fog was of that particular Lambeth quality which seems not so much to fall from the sky as to be exhaled from the earth itself—a greasy, yellow-brown vapour that tasted of coal-smoke and river-mud, and which wrapped the gas-lamps in woollen mufflers of light, so that each appeared a solitary, rheumy eye peering through the general blindness. It was upon a raw November afternoon, when the damp had penetrated to the very marrow of every living creature, that there might be observed, planted upon the worn flagstones before the sign of the Jolly Waterman, a small and singular group: an old man, blind, who drew from a battered fiddle a strain of such melancholy sweetness that the very draymen paused in their labour, and a girl of perhaps twelve years, who stood beside him with a tambourine, her thin shawl drawn tight about her shoulders, her eyes—which were large and dark and singularly bright—fixed upon the passers-by with an expression of patient anxiety.

The old man was called Mr. Bartholomew Creel, and he had been blind these thirty years, having lost his sight, as he was wont to relate with a certain grim satisfaction, in the great frost of the year ’fifteen, when he had been a young fellow of spirit and had lain out three nights in a ditch for the love of a woman who had afterwards married a cheesemonger. His granddaughter, Miss Lucy Creel, was the only child of his only daughter, who had died when the girl was but an infant; and the two, thus thrown upon the world, had wandered from fair to fair and from street to street, subsisting upon the coppers which the old man’s music and the child’s pretty face could extract from the pockets of the charitable.

Now, it chanced that upon this particular afternoon, Mr. Creel was not himself. His fingers, which ordinarily moved upon the strings with the precision of long habit, stumbled and faltered; his head, which was usually held erect, drooped forward upon his breast; and the tune, which should have been the “Banks of Allan Water,” wandered away into a kind of mournful caterwauling, as though the fiddle itself had caught a cold in its head. Lucy, watching him with the solicitude of a mother, observed that his lips were blue and his breath came short.

“Grandfather,” said she, in a low voice, “you are ill.”

“Ill?” returned the old man, with a feeble attempt at his usual cheerfulness. “Not I, my dear. ’Tis the fog—the fog, and nothing more. It gets into the strings, Lucy, and makes them hoarse. A little brandy would set me right, but brandy is a luxury for the rich, and we are—we are—”

He stopped, swayed, and would have fallen had not the girl caught him by the arm. A chair was procured from within the public-house, and the old man was seated upon it, while a compassionate potboy brought him a mug of hot ale; but it was plain to all who looked upon his grey face and sunken eyes that he would fiddle no more that day, nor for many days to come.

Thus it was that Lucy Creel found herself, upon the morrow, alone in the world of the streets—alone with the fiddle, which she had learned to play after a fashion from her grandfather, and with the tambourine, and with that courage which is born of necessity. She took her station in a narrow court off the Westminster Road, where the houses leaned together like drunken men, and where the inhabitants, being poor themselves, were yet not ungenerous; and there, with the fog still thick about her, she played the airs which the old man had taught her, and sang in a voice which, though small, was sweet and true, and which had in it that pathos which belongs to childhood when it is sad.

It was while she was thus engaged, upon the third day of her solitary minstrelsy, that a gentleman of very genteel appearance, with a white hat, a green velvet waistcoat, and a pair of whiskers of such magnitude that they seemed to have been pasted upon his cheeks as an afterthought, stopped before her and listened with an air of profound attention. When she had finished, he clapped his hands softly together, and addressed her in a voice which was as smooth as oil and as sweet as honey.

“My little dear,” said he, “you have a genius—a positive genius. I am a judge of such matters, and I do not hesitate to say that I have never heard a more touching performance.”

Lucy, who was not accustomed to compliments, dropped a curtsey and said nothing.

“But this is not the place for you,” continued the gentleman, waving his hand with a lordly gesture. “This—this alley, these—these persons”—he indicated the ragged children who had gathered to listen—“are not worthy of you. You should be upon the boards, my dear—upon the boards, with the footlights before you and the audience behind you, and your name in letters of gold upon the bill.”

“I play for my grandfather,” said Lucy, simply. “He is ill.”

“Ah! your grandfather—the old gentleman with the fiddle. I have seen him—a fine old patriarch. But he is old, my dear, and the old must give way to the young. Now, I—I am Mr. Horatio Fipps, of Fipps’s Grand Travelling Exhibition and Museum of Curiosities, now exhibiting at the Surrey Gardens. I have a vacancy—a most desirable vacancy—for a young person of talent and appearance. You would be fed, clothed, and lodged; you would be taught to dance, to sing, to recite; you would be seen by the nobility and gentry; and your salary—your salary, my dear, would be a shilling a week to begin with, and a rise as you merited it.”

He paused, and smiled upon her with an air of such benevolence that a less discerning child might have been deceived. But Lucy Creel, who had lived all her life among the poor and the struggling, had learned to read faces as others read books, and she did not like the face of Mr. Horatio Fipps. There was something in the shifting of his eye, something in the too-ready smile, something in the very whiteness of his hat and the greenness of his waistcoat, which whispered to her of the wolf in sheep’s clothing.

“I thank you, sir,” said she, “but I cannot leave my grandfather.”

“Cannot leave him!” cried Mr. Fipps, with an air of the deepest concern. “My dear child, you mistake me. I would not separate you from him for the world. He should accompany you—he should be one of the company—he should fiddle in the orchestra. Only think of it! The old man, no longer exposed to the inclemency of the weather, but warm and comfortable, with his fiddle under his chin and a glass of something hot at his elbow, while you—you, my dear, would be the star of the evening.”

He drew from his pocket a card, upon which was engraved his name and the address of the Surrey Gardens, and pressed it into her hand.

“Think it over,” said he. “Think it over. I shall be here again tomorrow at this hour. Ask your grandfather—he is a man of sense, and will see the advantage of my proposal. Good day, my dear—good day.”

He raised his hat with a flourish, and was gone, leaving Lucy standing with the card in her hand and a troubled look upon her face. That night, when she had returned to the miserable room in which the old man lay, she told him all that had passed; and the old man, who had been dozing, started up with an energy which belied his weakness.

“Fipps!” cried he. “Horatio Fipps! Lucy, my child, I know that man. I knew him when he was a penny-showman at Bartholomew Fair, with a learned pig and a stuffed mermaid. He is a rogue—a very thorough-paced rogue. He has ruined more young people than I have fingers and toes. Promise me, Lucy—promise me that you will have nothing to do with him.”

“I promise, grandfather,” said the girl, and she meant it.

But the morrow brought the fog again, and with it came Mr. Fipps, more smiling, more benevolent, more persuasive than before. He brought with him a picture of the life she would lead—the lights, the music, the applause, the pretty dresses, the fine ladies and gentlemen who would throw their bouquets at her feet. And Lucy, who was but a child, and cold, and hungry, and weary of the struggle, felt her resolution waver. She thought of the long, dreary days, of the scant pennies, of the old man’s cough, which grew worse and worse; and she thought of the warm room, the good food, the shilling a week.

“I must ask my grandfather,” said she.

“Certainly, certainly,” said Mr. Fipps. “Ask him by all means. And if he should be so unreasonable as to refuse—why, my dear, you are young, and the young must think for themselves. I shall be here tomorrow.”

He was as good as his word. And upon the morrow, when he came, Lucy Creel was not there. Nor upon the next day. Nor upon any day thereafter. The fog rolled over Lambeth, thick and yellow as before; the draymen paused at the sign of the Jolly Waterman; the potboy brought out his mugs of hot ale; but the small figure with the tambourine was seen no more, and the old blind fiddler sat alone in his miserable room, calling upon the name of his child, and hearing, in the silence, only the echo of his own despair.
