It was on a raw, salt-laden morning in the late autumn of 1894 that I found myself upon the bare, wind-swept downs above the village of Tregenna, in the southwest of Cornwall. I am a mining engineer by profession, and my name is Vane. I had been summoned from Redruth by a Mr. Hendrick Trelawney, a man of substance and ambition, who had lately acquired the abandoned workings of the old Wheal Voracious. His design was a simple one: to pump out the flooded levels and to reopen the lode which, in the palmy days of the forties, had yielded a notable quantity of black tin. It was my task to survey the principal galleries and to report upon the nature and extent of the works necessary to render the mine once more fit for human labour.

The engine-house was a roofless ruin, and the great beam-engine, which had not drawn breath for twenty years, was a mass of rust. Trelawney, a stout, black-bearded man, in a grey tweed suit and rubber boots, met me at the shaft-mouth, and together we descended by means of the old wooden ladder. The water had been drawn off to a depth of some forty fathoms, and we found ourselves at last upon a staging at the bottom of the main shaft, from which a level had been driven northwards into the hill. The air was thick and damp, and our candles threw wavering, yellow gleams upon the grey, dripping walls of killas.

We proceeded along the main level for some two hundred yards. The props of seasoned Cornish elm were still sound, and the floor, though wet, was firm. Presently we came to a heavy oaken door, set into a brickwork frame. It was a door which had been placed there by the former adventurers to prevent the inrush of water from the eastern workings, which were of a lower level. It was secured by a massive iron lock, and Trelawney, with some display of pride, produced a key and flung it open.

"You will find nothing beyond this, Mr. Vane," said he, holding his candle above his head. "The eastern workings have been sealed since 1874. My surveyor, old Polwhele, who was a lad here in the old days, assures me that the roof fell in at the junction of the lodes, and that the whole eastern section is as dead as Pharaoh."

We passed through the doorway and into a gallery of somewhat narrower dimensions. The air here was surprisingly fresh, and I remarked upon the fact to my companion. "There must be a natural draught from some old adit on the hillside," said he, carelessly. We proceeded for another hundred yards, and I was noting in my book the condition of the roof, when my attention was arrested by a small, white object upon the floor. I picked it up. It was a lump of tallow candle, newly broken, and so soft that it yielded to the pressure of my thumb. I held it close to my candle. The wick was still fragrant with smoke.

"Here is something which your surveyor has overlooked, Mr. Trelawney," said I.

He stared at the lump of tallow with a face that changed from ruddy to pale. "It is impossible," he muttered. "No man has been in this level for twenty years."

"Then," said I, "the dead have been very busy with their candles. This piece is not a week old."

We stood for a moment in silence, looking at each other by the light of our flickering candles. Then, with one accord, we pressed forward into the gallery. The floor, which had been rough and uneven, became, as we advanced, noticeably smoother, as though it had been trodden by many feet. And now a second discovery rewarded our search. In the wall of the gallery, at a point where a small cross-cut had been made, there was a ladder, leading upwards to a higher level. It was not one of the old ladders of the mine, which were of rough elm, heavy and clumsy. It was a ladder of the lightest and best description, made of well-seasoned ash, with iron rungs, and it was unmistakably new. The wood was still pale, and the iron was bright.

I laid my hand upon it, and then I examined the ground beneath. The floor was stained with the marks of heavy boots—the fresh, sharp imprints of nails, which had bit into the soft clay. I turned to Trelawney. "Your mine is already being worked," said I. "There is a gang of men at work here, and they are not your men."

His face was a study in perplexity and anger. "But it is impossible!" he cried. "The door was locked. I have the only key. And the upper levels are fallen in."

"The door was locked, but the ladder was not," said I, pointing upwards. "They have come in from above, through some old shaft on the moor. This is not the work of a day, nor of a week. They have been here for months. Look at the floor, look at the ladder. They are working a lode, Mr. Trelawney, and they are working it at your expense."

As I spoke, there came to my ears a faint, rhythmic sound—the distant thud of a pick upon rock, borne to us upon the current of air which swept through the gallery. It was the sound of labour, sure and steady, and it came from the depths of the eastern workings. Trelawney ground his teeth and made as if to rush forward, but I laid my hand upon his arm.

"We must not be rash," said I. "We are two men with two candles, and we do not know how many there may be. Let us go back and secure assistance. In the meantime, we have learned enough to know that Wheal Voracious is not so dead as the world supposes."

We made our way back to the shaft, and as we climbed the ladder, I could not but marvel at the coolness and ingenuity of the men who had found and exploited so hidden a treasure. The discovery was destined to lead to consequences of a far more startling character than either of us could then have foreseen.

It was three days later that I found myself once more at the mouth of Wheal Voracious, but this time in very different company. With me were Trelawney, the village constable, a man named Prowse, and two sturdy fellows from the village, armed with stout cudgels. We had also provided ourselves with a good coil of rope and a couple of lanterns. The morning was grey and lowering, with a thin rain drifting up from the sea, and the moor was desolate and silent.

Our plan was a simple one. We had ascertained, by a careful examination of the moor, that there was, some half-mile from the main shaft, an old ventilation shaft, which had been supposed to be choked with rubbish. On going to it, we had found that the rubbish had been cleared away, and that a new wooden frame had been placed at the mouth. It was by this means that the intruders had entered the mine. We now proposed to descend this shaft, and, following the upper level, to come upon the workers from above, while they were still engaged in their operations below.

We descended, one by one, into the shaft, which was some thirty fathoms deep, and found ourselves in a narrow, low-roofed gallery, which ran southwards. The air was close and heavy, and we proceeded in silence, with our lanterns shaded. The gallery was evidently an old one, but the roof had been carefully propped, and the floor was smooth and trodden. We had not gone far before we came upon a heap of broken rock, which had been recently blasted, and beyond it, a small chamber, where a lantern stood upon a box, burning dimly. On the box were a number of tools—a pick, a shovel, and a hammer—and a canvas bag, heavy with something that clinked as I touched it. I opened it, and found within a quantity of rough, black ore, gleaming with the bright, metallic lustre of tin.

We pressed on, and the sound of the pick, which had been silent for a moment, was now resumed, and grew louder as we advanced. At last, turning a sharp angle in the gallery, we came upon the scene of operations. It was a small, irregular chamber, lighted by two candles stuck in crevices of the rock. In the centre, a man was kneeling, with his back towards us, plying a pick at the face of the lode. He was a short, thick-set fellow, in a flannel shirt and moleskin trousers, and his arms were bare and muscular. Beside him, holding a candle and a truck, stood a second man, taller and thinner, with a sharp, ferret-like face.

The constable stepped forward. "Now then, my men!" said he, in a loud, official voice. "What are you doing here?"

The kneeling man sprang to his feet with a cry, and the taller man dropped his candle. For an instant they stood, as if paralysed, gazing at us. Then the shorter man made a dash for a side gallery, but Prowse was too quick for him, and seized him by the collar. The taller man threw up his hands.

"It's no use, Jago," said he to his companion. "We're nabbed."

The affair was soon over. The men were handcuffed and brought to the surface, where they were taken in charge by the constable, and conveyed to the lock-up at Tregenna. Their names, it appeared, were Jago and Treloar, and they were two miners of indifferent character, who had for some months been systematically robbing the mine. They had discovered the old ventilation shaft, and, clearing it out, had entered the eastern workings, where they had found a rich patch of tin, which they had worked at their leisure, carrying the ore to the surface in bags, and disposing of it to a smelter at Hayle. They had been at the work for nearly a year, and had realized, it was said, a considerable sum.

It was a simple case enough, tell, but it serves to illustrate the truth that, in the darkness and solitude of a Cornish mine, a man may carry on his labours for months and years, unseen and unsuspected, and that the secrets of the earth are not always revealed to those who hold the keys.
