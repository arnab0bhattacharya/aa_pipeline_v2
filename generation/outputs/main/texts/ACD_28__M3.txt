It was upon a raw November morning, with a thick yellow fog rolling up from the Severn, that I found myself in a railway carriage bound for Shropshire. I had been summoned thither by a telegram from Mr. Silas Blackwood, the steward of the Marsham estate, a man whom I had known in former years as a methodical and unemotional person. His message had been brief and to the point: "Come at once. Matter of utmost delicacy. Tell no one." I had not hesitated, for I knew Blackwood to be no alarmist.

He met me at the little station of Marsham Halt, a tall, gaunt man, whose brown, weather-beaten face was lined with an anxiety which I had never seen there before. He greeted me with a grave nod and led the way to a dog-cart which stood waiting.

"You will forgive mycurtness, doctor," said he, as we drove along the muddy lanes, "but the matter is one which admits of no delay, and yet one which I am powerless to handle alone. It concerns Mr. Marsham himself."

"Mr. Marsham?" I asked, with some surprise. I knew the squire of Marsham by reputation as a retired Anglo-Indian, a man of proud and somewhat overbearing disposition, who held himself aloof from his neighbours and resented any intrusion upon his privacy.

"The same," said Blackwood, with a heavy sigh. "He is in a strange way, doctor. His health is failing, his mind is harassed, and yet he will not hear of police interference. It was but yesterday that he roundly rated me for suggesting it, and declared he would dismiss me upon the spot if I brought a constable within the gates. Yet something must be done, for the business is altogether beyond me."

He relapsed into a moody silence, and we drove on through the dripping countryside until we came to the lodge gates of Marsham Hall. The house was a large, red-brick building of the Queen Anne period, surrounded by formal gardens and a considerable park. But it was neither to the house nor the gardens that Blackwood conducted me. Leaving the dog-cart at the stables, he led me by a winding path through a plantation of larches until we emerged upon an open stretch of turf, at the bottom of which lay a long, narrow lake, perhaps a quarter of a mile in length. It was a gloomy sheet of water, black and sullen under the lowering sky, and bordered on the farther side by a dense wood of alders.

"That is the Mere," said Blackwood, stopping at the edge of the turf. "It has been there, to the best of my knowledge, for a hundred years and more. And yet, doctor, that lake is being drained of its water at night."

"Drained?" I exclaimed. "By whom, and for what purpose?"

"That is what I wish you to discover," he answered. "Three nights ago, when I chanced to be crossing the park at a late hour, I observed that the level of the water had sunk by several feet. The edges were all wet and muddy, and I could see the marks of the receding water upon the stones. I thought little of it at the time, supposing that some sluice or overflow had given way. But upon the following morning, when I came this way again, the lake was full to the brim as though nothing had happened. I examined the sluices and found them all in perfect order. Upon the next night I came again, and again the water was low—lower, indeed, than before. And again, by morning, it was full."

"It is very strange," I admitted.

"It is more than strange, doctor. It is sinister. Mr. Marsham has been aware of it, and it has preyed upon his mind in a manner which I cannot describe. He sits for hours in his study, starting at every sound, and when I ventured to ask him whether he had any knowledge of the affair, he turned upon me with a look which I shall not forget, and bade me hold my tongue. 'It is my affair, Blackwood,' said he, 'and I will have no meddling.' But I could see that the man was sick with fear."

We walked along the margin of the lake, and I examined the ground with care. The turf was soft and spongy, and here and there I observed deep footprints, which had been made, as I judged, by heavy boots. They led along the bank to a spot where a small boathouse jutted out over the water. Near this point the marks were more numerous and confused, and I noticed that the wooden piles of the boathouse were streaked with fresh mud, as though someone had clambered over them.

"You say the water is restored by morning," said I. "Is there any stream or spring which feeds the lake?"

"There is a small brook which comes in at the upper end," said Blackwood, pointing to where a trickle of water entered through the alder wood. "But it is a feeble feed, and would not account for so rapid a rise. I am at a loss to know how the water is replaced."

I stood for some time gazing at the dark surface of the lake, and then, turning to Blackwood, I asked him whether he knew anything of the history of the ground before the lake was formed.

He looked at me with some surprise. "I have heard it said that there was once an old chapel upon this spot," he answered. "It belonged to the Marsham family in the days before the Reformation, and was pulled down, I believe, by one of the old squires, who dammed the stream and made the lake in its place. But what of that?"

"Where are the records of the family?" I asked.

"In the library at the Hall. Mr. Marsham has a great chest of old papers there, which has been in the family for generations."

I bade Blackwood conduct me to the library. We found Mr. Marsham seated at his desk, a man of perhaps sixty years, with a hard, keen face and iron-grey hair. He looked up with a frown as we entered, and I saw his hand tighten upon the pen which he held.

"Who is this?" he demanded, turning to Blackwood. "I gave you no leave to bring strangers here."

"This is Dr. Vane, sir," said Blackwood steadily. "He is not a stranger, and I have brought him here because the matter is beyond my power. You are ill, sir, and this suspense is killing you."

For a moment the squire's eyes blazed with anger, and then, with a sudden gesture of weariness, he laid down his pen and passed his hand across his forehead.

"You mean well, Blackwood," said he. "I have no doubt you mean well. But you do not understand. This is no common business. It is a ghost from the past, and one which I had thought was laid long ago."

He was silent for a moment, and then he rose and, going to a great iron-bound chest which stood in a corner of the room, he drew from it a bundle of yellow and crumbling papers. These he spread upon the desk, and I saw that they were written in a crabbed hand, and bore dates which carried them back to the latter part of the sixteenth century.

"These are the records of my family," said he, "and they contain a matter which I have never breathed to any living soul. My ancestor, Sir Roland Marsham, who built this house, had a brother, a younger son, who was a priest. When the old chapel was pulled down and the lake made, this brother was away upon the Continent. He returned in secret, and, as the story goes, he brought with him certain vessels of gold and a sum of money, which he concealed within the chapel before it was destroyed. He was seized shortly afterwards by the pursuivant and thrown into prison, where he died. The secret of the hiding-place died with him."

"And you believe," said I, "that someone has discovered this tradition and is draining the lake in order to search for the treasure?"

"I know it," he answered. "I have received letters—anonymous letters—threatening me with exposure unless I paid a sum of money. I refused, and then this draining of the lake began. It is done by means of a pipe, which is inserted through the boathouse and carried by a hidden channel to the brook. I found it yesterday, but dared not remove it, for I feared what might follow."

"Let me see these letters," said I.

He handed me three epistles, written upon cheap paper and in a disguised hand. I examined them with attention, and then, turning to Blackwood, I asked him whether there was anyone upon the estate who had lately been in trouble for poaching or other offences.

"There is a man named Harker," said Blackwood, after a moment's thought. "He was a gamekeeper here, but he was dismissed two years ago for dishonesty. He is a cunning and dangerous fellow, and he has been seen lurking about the neighbourhood of late."

It was not difficult to put the pieces together. That night, with the help of Blackwood and two trusty men from the stables, I concealed myself within the boathouse and waited. It was a cold and dismal vigil, but it was not without result. Towards midnight I heard the splash of oars upon the lake, and presently a small boat drew up at the boathouse. A man stepped out, and by the light of a lantern which he carried, I saw a heavy, lowering face, with a scar upon the left cheek. It was Harker himself.

He worked swiftly and in silence, drawing from beneath the boathouse a long pipe of gutta-percha, which he connected with a pump concealed among the alders. The water began to pour out through this channel, and I could see the level of the lake sinking rapidly. When it had fallen by some four or five feet, he laid aside the pipe and took up a long pole, with which he began to sound the mud at the edge of the lake.

We allowed him to work for some minutes, and then, at a signal from me, Blackwood and his men rushed forward. Harker made a desperate attempt to escape, but he was seized and overpowered. When we searched the boat, we found within it a spade, a lantern, and a leather bag containing certain old and tarnished vessels of gold, which had doubtless been dislodged from their hiding-place by the receding water.

Mr. Marsham, when he learned the result of our enterprise, was like a man transfigured. The weight which had been upon him was lifted, and he grasped my hand with a fervour which I had not expected from so cold a man.

"You have saved me, doctor," said he. "I had feared that the matter would never be ended. But now I can rest."

He pressed upon me a handsome reward, which I accepted with such grace as I could, and he insisted upon presenting the recovered vessels to the parish church at Marsham, where they may still be seen. As for Harker, he was dealt with according to law, and received a sentence which, I trust, will keep him from further meddling for some years to come.
