The rain in Saffron Hill did not fall so much as it was extracted from the sky by a persistent, gloomy hand, squeezing the last grey drops from the clouds with a sort of malicious thoroughness. It was a drizzle that knew no season, a cold, penetrating mist that seemed to have been manufactured in the very basements of the tenements it lashed, and which crept into the bones of every soul who dared venture forth without a shawl of sufficient thickness. It was in this atmospheric state of distress, and with a heart equally unsettled by the prospect of his daily duty, that Mr. Silas Vane, clerk to the formidable Mr. Gristle, a moneylender whose name was a synonym for ruin in the district, ascended the steps of Number 4.

Mr. Vane was a man of middle age, middle height, and middle temperament, possessing a face that was neither handsome nor ugly, but which bore the peculiarly faded look of a photograph taken in bad light. His coat was tight across the shoulders, his cravat was knotted with a precision that bordered on the neurotic, and his eyes, behind a pair of spectacles with lenses as thick as the bottom of a bottle, held a perpetual look of suspicious squinting, as if he expected the world to be a forgery. He carried in his hand a leather portfolio, stiff with the weight of paper and the hardness of his own conscience, which he patted affectionately as he reached the door.

The door was open, or rather, it was ajar, held back by a broom handle that had lost its bristles long ago. Inside, the darkness was not merely an absence of light, but a presence, a thick, velvet curtain that hung over the room, smelling of damp wool, boiled cabbage, and the sharp, metallic tang of fear. Mr. Vane stepped inside, his boots making a wet, squelching sound on the floorboards that seemed to echo with an exaggerated boisterousness, as if the house itself were mocking the intrusion.

There was no one visible at first, only the shadows dancing in the corners, and the faint, rhythmic sound of a child coughing in a back room. Then, a figure emerged from the gloom. She was a girl, perhaps fifteen, with a face that was pale as the wash-water in the basin, and eyes that were large, dark, and wide with a terror that had no name. She wore a dress that had once been blue, but which the mud of the streets and the soot of the chimneys had reduced to a shade of grey so profound it might have been the color of her despair.

"Is that you, Miss Elspeth?" Mr. Vane asked, his voice taking on a tone of theatrical surprise, as if he had expected to find a dragon, or perhaps a pile of gold, but certainly not a starving girl standing in the center of her father’s ruin. "I trust I am not disturbing you? I am here, as you are aware, on business of a most unpleasant nature. Your father, I am informed, has failed to meet the terms of his lease. The landlord is, I am sorry to say, a man of few illusions and less patience."

Miss Elspeth did not speak. She stood there, trembling like a leaf in a gale, her hands clasped tightly in front of her. She looked at Mr. Vane, not with the hostility one might expect, but with a kind of bewildered, animal curiosity, as if she could not quite comprehend the reality of the man standing before her.

"Mr. Gristle’s instructions are clear," Mr. Vane continued, consulting his portfolio with an air of grave importance. "The eviction is to be carried out immediately. I have the writ in my hand. It is a very fine writ, Miss Elspeth. Very well drafted. I could show it to you, if you were interested in the legal phrasing, but I suspect you would prefer not to."

The girl’s eyes dropped to the floor, where a single, small object lay. It was a coin, a shilling, tarnished and worn, lying on the wet stone as if it had fallen from the sky. It was the only coin in the house, the last remnant of a wage that had not been paid, or perhaps a gift from a neighbor who had long since died. Miss Elspeth bent down, her movements slow and deliberate, and picked it up. She held it out to Mr. Vane, her hand shaking so violently that the coin seemed to dance in the air.

"Is that all?" Mr. Vane asked, a hint of genuine incredulity creeping into his voice. "You offer me a shilling? For a debt of thirty pounds? This is a joke, I am sure. A very bad joke, and not at all to my taste."

Miss Elspeth looked up, and in her eyes, Mr. Vane saw something that stopped him cold. It was not anger, nor pleading, but a stark, terrible honesty. It was the look of a person who has stripped away all pretense, all hope, and is left standing in the naked truth of their situation. She said nothing, but she held the coin out, her fingers white-knuckled, as if her very life depended on the transaction.

Mr. Vane stared at her. He looked at the coin, then at the girl, then at the dark, empty room behind her. He thought of Mr. Gristle, who sat in his warm office, counting his money and frowning at the ledger. He thought of his own cold hands and his tight cravat. He thought of the rain outside, and the mud, and the coughing child. And suddenly, the writ in his hand felt heavy, not with legal authority, but with the weight of a sin.

He took the coin. He placed it in his pocket. He closed his portfolio with a snap that sounded like a gunshot in the quiet room.

"I am afraid," Mr. Vane said, his voice suddenly sounding strange to his own ears, "that there has been a clerical error. I believe the account is actually in order. Or perhaps, I don't know. The ink has run. The figures are illegible. I shall have to check with Mr. Gristle. It is a very troublesome matter. I shall return tomorrow, perhaps the day after, to confirm the details. I am sure everything will be fine."

He turned and walked out of the house, his boots still squelching, but now the sound seemed less mocking, more like the soft steps of a man trying to walk on air. He did not look back. He knew he would not see her again. As he descended the steps into the drizzle, he felt a strange lightness in his chest, a hollow feeling that was not quite happiness, but was certainly not the heaviness he had carried that morning. He buttoned his coat, pulled his hat lower over his eyes, and disappeared into the grey, misty street, leaving the shilling in his pocket, a small, cold weight against his heart, a reminder that even the hardest men are made of flesh, and that sometimes, the most profound acts of mercy are the ones that are never spoken, never recorded, and never forgiven.
