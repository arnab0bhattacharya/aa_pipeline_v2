The train from Dover crawled through the southern counties, and Clara watched the landscape assemble itself outside the window like a half-remembered dream. The fields were greener than she had expected, softer than the sun-struck, brittle pastures of the Dordogne, and the hedgerows had a manicured, almost theatrical quality, as though someone had trimmed them for a stage set. She had been twenty-three when she left London, and now at forty-three she was returning for her mother's funeral, carrying a French passport and a French name—Clara Beaufort having become Claire Beaumont—and the accumulated weight of two decades spent among walnut trees and limestone houses and the slow, courteous rhythms of village life in the Périgord.

She had married Étienne in 1904, in the little church at Saint-Cyprien, and her mother had come once, the following summer, and had sat in the courtyard of the farmhouse and fanned herself with a magazine and said that the heat was unnatural and that the wine tasted of earth. Her sisters had never visited. They had sent letters, at first, and then Christmas cards, and then, gradually, just the Christmas cards, with their tight, dutiful signatures—Violet, Margaret—as though their names alone were all the news that could be spared.

Now her mother was dead, and Clara was crossing London in a taxi from Victoria Station, and the city was not the city she remembered. The streets were louder than she recalled, and faster, and the motorcars seemed to her unnecessarily numerous and aggressive, cutting in front of the taxi with a hornet's fury. She had left a London of hansom cabs and gaslight and women in trailing skirts, and she had returned to a London of electric lamps and short hemlines and men in soft hats who looked, to her provincial French eye, startlingly modern. The shop windows displayed goods she did not recognise—wireless sets, ready-made dresses in fabrics that looked like nothing a proper seamstress would touch—and the advertisements on the hoardings were for products with names that sounded American. She felt the displacement in her chest, a small, cold stone of alienation.

The funeral was at eleven the next morning, at St. Jude's, the same church where she had been baptised and confirmed and where, as a girl, she had sat beside her mother in the third pew and been told to stop fidgeting. Her sisters were already in the vestibule when she arrived, and the first thing she noticed was how much older they looked, and the second thing she noticed was how they looked at her.

Violet, the eldest, had the broad, practical build of their mother in her later years, and she wore a black dress that was plainly expensive and plainly unsentimental. Margaret, the middle sister, was thinner and more nervous, with a habit of touching her collarbone when she spoke, as though checking that she was still intact. They both kissed Clara on the cheek, and the kisses were correct and brief and dry.

"You look well," Violet said, and the words carried the faintest implication that looking well was, under the circumstances, a kind of impertinence.

"I brought flowers," Clara said, holding out the bouquet she had bought from a stall outside the station. She had wanted to bring something from the garden in Saint-Cyprien—late roses, perhaps, or a branch of the persimmon that Étienne had planted against the south wall—but November had stripped the garden bare, and cut flowers would not have survived the journey. These were hothouse lilies, stiff and funereal, and she was not sure they were right.

Margaret took the lilies without really looking at them. "She would have liked roses better," she said, not unkindly, and Clara felt the small correction land and settle, the way a stone settles into still water.

The service was brief and correct. The vicar, a young man with a reedy voice whom Clara did not know, spoke of Dorothy Hartwell with a fluency that suggested he had met her perhaps twice. Clara sat in the third pew, between her sisters, and tried to feel something sharper than this dull, floating grief, but the church was cold and the prayers were familiar in a way that made them feel like garments she had outgrown—the words too small, the syntax constraining, as though the language itself no longer fit her mouth. She had not spoken English with any regularity in years. In Saint-Cyprien, she spoke French with the villagers and a mixture of French and English with Étienne, who had picked up enough English to be dangerous—enough to mispronounce every word with enormous confidence. Her own English had grown stiff and slightly formal, like a dress kept in tissue paper, and she found herself reaching for words that hovered just out of reach, as though her vocabulary had been rearranged in her absence and she could not remember where anything had been put.

At the cemetery, the November wind came off the Heath and cut across the graves. Clara stood with her sisters at the edge of the open plot and watched the coffin lowered into the ground, and she thought of her mother's hands—the way they had kneaded bread, the way they had smoothed the tablecloth before setting out the tea things, the way they had waved from the platform at Victoria Station twenty years ago as the train pulled away. Had she waved? Clara could not be certain. Memory was unreliable; it edited and revised and sometimes invented, and she had spent so long remembering a version of her mother that might have been partly fiction.

After the burial, they went to Violet's house in Hampstead, a tall, narrow terrace with bay windows and a front step that had been scrubbed to a hostile whiteness. The house smelled of furniture polish and boiled ham. Women from the parish had laid out a cold supper on the dining table—sandwiches with the crusts removed, a trifle in a glass bowl, a seed cake that nobody would eat—and the mourners moved through the room with plates and cups of tea, speaking in low voices about the deceased.

Clara stood near the window and listened to the conversations around her, and the strange thing was not that the talk was unfamiliar, but that she was unfamiliar in it. People spoke to her with a careful, slightly exaggerated courtesy, the kind reserved for visitors from abroad. They asked about France the way one asks about a distant country—as though it were a curiosity, an exhibition—and they did not ask about her life there in any detail. Violet introduced her to the vicar as "my sister, Mrs. Beaumont, who lives in France," and the way she said it made France sound like an affliction, a chronic condition that Clara had contracted through some failure of precaution.

"How long do you stay?" Margaret asked, refilling Clara's teacup.

"Until Thursday. The train back to Dover on Thursday afternoon."

"Back to your Frenchman, then," Violet said, and though she smiled, there was something in the phrase—your Frenchman—that Clara recognised. It was the way the English had of making the foreign sound faintly absurd, faintly comic, a joke that only the English were in on. Étienne, who read Balzac in bed and could recite the entirety of La Fontaine's fables from memory and who had wept openly at the birth of their daughter, was reduced, in Violet's mouth, to a comic type: the Frenchman, the garlic-smelling, shrugging, philosophically lazy Frenchman of English prejudice.

Clara said nothing. She drank her tea and looked at the room—the heavy curtains, the framed photographs of Violet's sons on the mantelpiece, the clock on the shelf that ticked with a metronomic insistence—and she understood that she had become a stranger here, in the country of her birth, and that her sisters had long ago decided that she was someone who had chosen to leave and therefore could not fully return. The leaving had been, in their eyes, a defection, and every year she had stayed away had confirmed it.

That evening, alone in the guest bedroom at Violet's house, Clara sat on the edge of the narrow bed and wrote a letter to Étienne. She wrote it in French, because French was now the language in which she was most herself, the language in which she could be precise about what she felt. She told him about the funeral, about the cold church and the cold cemetery and the cold politeness of her sisters. She told him that London was not her city anymore, that it had moved on without her, and that she had become a tourist in her own past. She told him she missed the smell of woodsmoke in the valley, and the way the light came through the kitchen window in the afternoon and made everything golden, and the sound of the church bell at Saint-Cyprien, which was cracked and therefore always slightly flat, which Étienne said gave it character.

She sealed the letter and addressed it to the farmhouse, and then she turned off the lamp and lay in the dark and listened to the unfamiliar sounds of London—motorcars in the street below, the distant hoot of a train, the measured, indifferent striking of a clock she did not know—and she waited for Thursday, and the train, and the crossing, and the road home.
