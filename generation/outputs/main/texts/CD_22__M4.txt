Mrs. Prudence Pepperell, of the village of Nettlecombe-cum-Slush, in the county of Suffolk, was a widow of that comfortable, round, bustling description which seems to have been expressly designed by Nature for the keeping of a village shop and the spoiling of an only son. Her shop, which stood at the corner of the market-place, and was famous for bacon, cheese, pickles, and a certain blacking which the squire himself declared to be superior to any London article, had prospered through thirty years of honest dealing; and at the back of it, in a little parlour smelling of smoked ham and lavender, there hung over the chimney-piece a portrait of the late Mr. Pepperell, in a blue coat and a yellow waistcoat, with a face like a full moon in a fog, and an expression of such profound and settled satisfaction that it seemed to say, “I have done my duty by the blacking, and the blacking has done its duty by me.”

But the idol of that parlour—the treasure of the shop, the hope of the widow, the wonder of Nettlecombe—was her son, Master Adolphus Pepperell. Never was there a boy so curled, so collared, so waistcoated, so generally bedizened. From his cradle he had been fed upon the fat of the land and the cream of the flattery; at eight he wore gloves; at twelve he had a silver watch; at fifteen he smoked a cigar behind the pig-stye, and told the grocer’s boy that he was born for higher things. “Dolly,” his mother would say, as she wiped her hands upon her apron and gazed upon him with an admiration that fairly overflowed her spectacles—“Dolly, my precious, you was always the gentleman born, and don’t you let nobody tell you different.” And Dolly, who had no more intention of being told different than the sun has of going backwards, would stroke the first down upon his upper lip, and reply, “Never fear, Mother; I mean to be a credit to you, and no mistake.”

Now it came to pass, in the year eighteen hundred and forty-something, that a certain young fellow of Nettlecombe, who had made his fortune in the metropolis by the sale of a patent medicine for the curing of corns, returned to the village in a yellow chariot, with a servant in livery and a gold-headed cane, and was received by the whole population as though he had come down from heaven in a hackney-coach. Mrs. Pepperell beheld him from her shop-door, and her heart yearned within her. “Dolly,” said she that night, “Dolly, my darling, why should not you go to London and be a gentleman too?” Dolly, who had been thinking the very same thing for the last three years, required no second invitation. The savings of thirty years—the bacon, the cheese, the pickles, the blacking, the little hoards put by in the tea-caddy and the stocking-foot and the cracked blue teapot—were poured forth like water; and one bright morning in May, Master Adolphus Pepperell, in a new blue frock-coat, a white hat, and a neckerchief of the most killing pattern, kissed his mother at the coach-office, and was whirled away to London, leaving the widow to weep and to triumph, and to tell every customer who came for a penn’orth of cheese that her Dolly was gone to be a gentleman.

A year passed. The tea-caddy was empty, the stocking-foot was empty, the blue teapot held nothing but a few dried tea-leaves and a spider. And where was Dolly? Dolly was in London, it is true; but he was not exactly the gentleman he had intended to be. He had dined at the best tables, it is true, and had tipped the porters with a magnificence that made them stare; he had worn white gloves and a gold chain and a hat that was the envy of the Strand; and he had spent every guinea of his mother’s savings in the space of eleven months and three weeks, and had found, when the last sovereign was gone, that the gentlemen with whom he had dined were not so fond of him as he had supposed. So Adolphus Pepperell, being ashamed to go home, and having no other trade, no other accomplishment, no other resource, had taken the only situation that offered itself to a young man of his figure and his manners—he had become a waiter at the very hotel where he had once dined at the best table and tipped the porters so lavishly. It was called the Golden Turk, in the Strand; and there, in a black coat and a white cravat, with a napkin under his arm and a smile upon his face that was not quite so easy as it had been, Mr. Adolphus Pepperell handed the dishes and answered the bell, and was sometimes told by the head waiter that he would never do, and sometimes by the landlady that he was the genteelest young man that ever stood behind a chair.

Now it happened, upon a certain raw November afternoon, when the fog was so thick in the Strand that the lamps looked like blurred eyes in a swollen face, that a stout, round, bustling old lady, in a black bonnet and a grey cloak, with a basket on her arm and her spectacles pushed up upon her forehead, descended from the Suffolk coach at the Golden Turk, and stood in the passage, blinking and bewildered, and asking of every one she met whether they could tell her where she could find her son, Mr. Adolphus Pepperell, who was a gentleman in London, and who had not written to her for a twelvemonth. The porters stared, the chambermaid giggled, the landlady frowned; but at last the head waiter was called, and the head waiter, with a condescending smile, said that he believed there was a young man of that name in the house, and that he would send him to her directly. And Mrs. Pepperell sat down upon a bench in the passage, with her basket on her knee, and her heart beating so loud that she could hear it.

The door opened, and Adolphus came in. He was in his waiter’s coat, with his napkin under his arm; his face was pale, and his eyes were red, and his white cravat was not so spotless as it had been. He stopped short when he saw his mother, and the tray he was carrying shook in his hand. “Mother!” he said, in a voice that was half a whisper. “Dolly!” cried the widow, and she started up, and the basket rolled upon the floor, and the apples and the cheese and the little parcel of blacking that she had brought him as a present were scattered about the passage. “Dolly, my precious, my darling, my gentleman!” And she threw her arms about his neck, and kissed him, and cried, and laughed, and held him off at arm’s length to look at him, and then caught him to her heart again, and sobbed as though her heart would break.

Adolphus stood like a man turned to stone. The head waiter looked in at the door and grinned; the chambermaid tittered; a commercial gentleman with a cigar in his mouth put his head over the banisters and said, “Hallo, Pepperell! is this your mother?” And Adolphus, with the shame of the whole house upon him, and the memory of all his finery and his folly, and the sight of the grey cloak and the black bonnet and the scattered apples, could only say, “Yes, sir, it is; and I am not ashamed of her, sir, though I am ashamed of myself.” And then he took his mother by the hand, and led her into a little back room where the waiters ate their dinners, and sat down beside her, and hid his face in his hands, and cried as he had not cried since he was a boy.

“Dolly,” said the widow, when she could speak, “Dolly, my precious, don’t take on so. I don’t care for the money, not I. I’d have spent it twice over to see you a gentleman; and if you ain’t a gentleman, why, you’re my Dolly, and that’s better than any gentleman in London.” And she took his head between her hands, and smoothed his hair, and looked into his face with such a world of love and pardon in her spectacles that Adolphus Pepperell, waiter at the Golden Turk, felt himself for the first time in his life to be something better than a gentleman—he felt himself to be a son. And when the head waiter came to say that there was a gentleman in Number Four who wanted his dinner, Adolphus rose up, and wiped his eyes, and said, “Mother, you sit here till I come back; and then we’ll go home together, and I’ll be a credit to you yet, and no mistake.” And the widow sat in the little back room, with her basket on her knee and her spectacles upon her nose, and smiled, and cried, and was happy.
