Mr. Silas Pankhurst was a man of ideas, which is to say, a man of perpetual and ruinous expense. His villa in the suburban sprawl of Ealing was less a home than a workshop, its garden a graveyard of failed contraptions: a clockwork lawnmower that chewed the turf into a brown paste, a self-watering system that flooded the scullery, and a device for shelling peas that had, on its single trial, launched them like grapeshot through the kitchen window. His neighbour, Mr. Josiah Cribb, a gentleman of solid, immutable principles and a prosperous timber business, viewed Pankhurst’s activities with the profound suspicion a farmer reserves for a fox.

The source of their latest and most final disagreement was a machine Pankhurst called the Zoöphonic Harmoniser. It was an imposing affair of brass, mahogany, and vacuum tubes, crowned with a large, polished parabolic dish and trailing wires that snaked across the lawn like metallic ivy. Its purpose, Pankhurst had explained to a bored and sceptical Cribb over the fence, was to translate the complex emotional and linguistic content of animal vocalisations into the Queen’s English.

“Every bark, every miaow, every chirp,” Pankhurst had declared, his eyes alight with visionary fervour, “is a packet of information, Cribb! A packet of pure data! My machine simply unwraps it!”

Cribb had grunted, adjusted his waistcoat, and remarked that a dog’s bark was a dog’s bark, and a man’s peace was a man’s peace.

The great test, however, required a subject. Cribb’s bulldog, a formidable creature named Duke, was the obvious candidate. Duke was a champion of his breed, a monument of muscle and jowl, who regarded the world with a baleful, proprietary glare. Pankhurst, with the guileless audacity of the true inventor, waited until Cribb had departed for his club in the city, then slipped through the gap in the hedge.

“Good morning, Duke,” he said, laying out a plate of minced beef.

Duke regarded the offering, then Pankhurst, with an air of profound unimpressedness. He emitted a low, guttural rumble, a sound of seismic discontent.

Pankhurst clamped the machine’s listening device—a sort of metal stethoscope—to the dog’s collar and rushed back to his garden, where the Harmoniser’s speaker horn was aimed at his own ear. He threw the switch. The vacuum tubes glowed a sickly orange, and the machine hummed with the sound of a thousand furious hornets. Duke, meanwhile, began to bark, a series of short, sharp, percussive reports.

From the speaker horn, in a voice that was somehow both metallic and deeply weary, came the translation.

“*The meat is acceptable. The provider is adequate. The sun is in its proper place. The world, for the moment, is in order. Now, for the important business.*”

Pankhurst stared, slack-jawed. He adjusted a dial. Duke let out a longer, more complex series of growls and yelps.

“*The Male is departing,*” the machine rendered. “*Good. The air grows cleaner. One can breathe without smelling of cheap cigars and thwarted ambition. He walks with the gait of a man who has never once considered the intricate scent-map of a single lamppost. A tragedy.*”

Pankhurst sank onto a garden stool. This was beyond his wildest projections. He pointed the dish back at the dog, who was now sniffing intently at a patch of clover.

“*Ah, the clover. A symphony of damp earth and forgotten rain. The Female dropped a biscuit here on Tuesday. A superior oatmeal variety. Her taste is impeccable, unlike her choice in companions. The Male’s presence is a constant affront to the senses. He smells of leather and self-satisfaction. His voice is a blunt instrument used to beat the world into a shape he can comprehend.*”

The monologue, once begun, showed no sign of abating. Duke, it transpired, was a canine philosopher, a critic of the highest order, and a deeply resentful subject. He spoke of the indignity of the choke-chain (“*a barbaric tool for a barbaric mind*”), the inadequacy of his water bowl (“*a shallow puddle of tepid compromise*”), and the sheer, grinding boredom of life with the Cribbs.

“*The Female is better,*” the machine droned, as Duke yawned enormously. “*She has soft hands and the discerning palate for a good scratch behind the ears. But she is complicit. She enables the Male’s tyranny. She calls him ‘my dear Josiah’! My dear Josiah! He is no more ‘dear’ than a rusty gate! He is a monument to mediocrity, a walking, talking testament to the death of wonder!*”

Pankhurst, now thoroughly engrossed, forgot his scientific detachment. “What else?” he whispered into the machine’s relay. “What else does he think of him?”

The machine whirred, translating a particularly vitriolic growl. “*His boots! Those great, clomping, soul-crushing boots! They announce his approach like a declaration of war on all things small and interesting. He once kicked my water bowl. Kicked it! For no reason other than the sheer, brutish pleasure of displacing a liquid. He is a vandal of the domestic sphere!*”

It was at this precise moment, as the machine was rendering Duke’s opinion on Cribb’s choice of neckwear (“*a cravat the colour of a diseased sunset*”), that Mr. Josiah Cribb himself came striding down the garden path, having forgotten his umbrella.

The scene that greeted him was one of calculated madness. His neighbour was crouched by a monstrous contraption, wires running to his prize bulldog, who was barking furiously at a squirrel. From a large brass horn, a strange, disembodied voice was shouting about neckwear.

“Pankhurst!” Cribb bellowed. “What in God’s name are you doing to my dog?”

Pankhurst leaped to his feet, his face a mask of scientific triumph and social terror. “Cribb! A breakthrough! A monumental breakthrough! Duke is a critic! A philosopher! He has a great deal to say about your boots!”

The ensuing row was one for the annals of suburban history. Cribb, a man whose world was built on solid, tangible facts, was confronted with an absurdity so profound it felt like a personal insult. He saw not a scientific marvel, but a madman torturing his dog and broadcasting insults. Pankhurst, in his excitement, amplified the machine’s output, and Duke’s voice filled the afternoon air: “*He is raising his voice again. The auditory equivalent of being struck with a plank of wood. The sheer, unthinking aggression of the man!*”

That was the final straw. Cribb did not speak to Pankhurst again. He spoke to his solicitor.

The case of *Cribb v. Pankhurst* was a sensation. The charge was slander, a novel and perplexing concept when the slanderer was a dog. Pankhurst, representing himself, tried to enter the Zoöphonic Harmoniser as evidence, but the judge, a man of profound common sense, ruled it “a piece of theatrical claptrap” and disallowed it. Cribb’s barrister painted a picture of a malicious inventor, a harasser of animals and a defamer of character, who had used his infernal machine to invent insults and attribute them to an innocent bulldog.

Duke himself was brought into the courtroom. He sat beside his master, panting placidly, the picture of canine rectitude. When asked by the judge if he had, in fact, described his owner’s cravat as “a diseased sunset,” Duke simply blinked, yawned, and rested his great head on his paws.

Pankhurst lost the case. He was ordered to pay substantial damages and to dismantle his machine. As he carried the pieces of the Harmoniser to a bonfire at the bottom of his garden, he saw Duke and Cribb on the other side of the fence. Cribb was patting the dog’s head, murmuring praises for his “good, loyal, quiet” companion.

And as Pankhurst watched, Duke turned his great, jowly head, looked directly at his master, and then looked at Pankhurst. For a fleeting second, Pankhurst could have sworn he saw in the dog’s eyes a look of profound, intelligent, and utterly cynical commiseration. Duke then gave a soft, almost inaudible woof.

Pankhurst didn’t need a machine to understand it. It meant, quite clearly, “*I told you so.*”
