Julian Ashby had spent his twenty-eight years in a state of unbroken indulgence. The gaming tables at the Bellerive Club, the velvet-draped rooms of actresses in St. John’s Wood, the champagne suppers that dissolved into hazy dawns—all of it had been his domain. London in the 1890s was a city built for men like him: wealthy, unattached, and utterly without purpose. He had never given a moment’s thought to anyone but himself, and he had seen no reason why he should.

The reckoning came on a Tuesday morning in Dr. Hartfield’s consulting room off Harley Street. The diagnosis was delivered with a practiced, almost mechanical sympathy. A degenerative condition of the heart, irreversible, inoperable. Twelve months, perhaps fourteen. Julian had listened in silence, feeling as though the words were being spoken to someone else entirely. He had walked out into the pale November sunlight and stood on the pavement for a long time, watching the hansoms rattle past, and for the first time in his life he understood that he was going to die.

What surprised him was not the fear, which he had expected, but the shame. It came upon him that evening as he sat alone in his rooms in Mount Street, a glass of brandy untouched in his hand. He had done nothing. Nothing at all. He had left no mark on the world except the unpaid bills at his tailor and the broken hearts of a few women who should have known better than to love him. The thought was intolerable. He would spend his remaining months doing good. He would give away his money, help the poor, comfort the afflicted. He would become, in the time left to him, a decent man.

He began the very next day by walking to the East End, having read in the morning paper about a cholera outbreak in the tenements around Wentworth Street. He arrived in a hired carriage, wearing a tailored overcoat and carrying a purse full of sovereigns. The streets were narrow and reeked of sewage and sickness. Children with hollow eyes watched him from doorways. He had imagined that he would simply distribute money and be thanked, that the act of giving would be as effortless as everything else in his life had been.

Instead he found himself standing in a damp, windowless room where a woman sat on a straw mattress beside a child who was clearly dying. The woman looked up at him without expression. Julian fumbled in his purse and held out several coins. The woman did not take them. She said, “What do you want?” and her voice was flat, not hostile but exhausted beyond hostility. Julian could not answer. He wanted to help, he truly did, but he did not know how. Money seemed suddenly useless, an insult, a trivial thing dropped into the vastness of another person’s suffering. He left the coins on the table and walked out into the street, and the child’s eyes followed him through the open door, and he could not bear it.

In the weeks that followed he persisted, driven by the ticking clock inside his chest. He visited workhouses and hospitals. He stood in line at soup kitchens and tried to serve, fumbling with the ladle while the poor looked on with weary patience. He attempted to intervene on behalf of a young clerk who had been unjustly dismissed, writing letters to the man’s employer and calling at his office, only to be told that the matter was none of his concern and that his interference had made things worse. He gave five hundred pounds to a charitable society for orphans and later discovered, through a chance remark at a dinner party, that the society’s treasurer had embezzled a third of it.

Every good deed seemed to twist in his hands. He offered to pay for the education of a beggar’s son and found that the boy did not want education, wanted instead to remain on the streets where he knew how to survive. He tried to reconcile a pair of estranged siblings whose quarrel, once he understood it, was so tangled with old grievances that his attempts at mediation only deepened the wound. He sat with dying men at St. Thomas’s Hospital and discovered that comfort is not a matter of presence alone, that one must know what to say, and that he had never learned to say anything that mattered.

He had thought virtue would be easy. He had assumed that goodness was simply pleasure turned inside out, the same act performed in the opposite direction. But pleasure required nothing of him except money and appetite, while goodness demanded patience, humility, and a kind of intelligence he had never been forced to develop. He was clumsy at it. He was always saying the wrong thing, giving to the wrong people, arriving at the wrong moment. The poor did not trust him because he moved and spoke like a man who had never known want. They accepted his money and resented his pity, and he could not blame them, because he understood that pity, from a man in a tailored coat, was worth less than nothing.

By February his doctor suggested a change of climate, and Julian traveled to the Riviera. He took a villa above the harbor at Menton, a white house set among olive trees with a view of the sea. He had intended to continue his work there, but the south of France offered fewer opportunities. The poor existed, certainly, but they were harder to find, hidden behind the facades of hotels and casinos. He found himself instead walking alone along the cliffs, watching the light change on the water, and thinking about the people he had tried and failed to help.

He thought about the woman in Wentworth Street and her dying child. He thought about the clerk whose dismissal he had worsened. He thought about the beggar’s son who had refused his offer, and he realized now that the boy had been right to refuse it, that Julian had wanted to save him not for the boy’s sake but for his own, to feel that he had accomplished something before he died. The realization was bitter. He saw that his goodness had been, at least in part, another form of self-indulgence, a final pleasure he had attempted to purchase with his wealth and his remaining time. He had wanted the feeling of virtue without the cost of it, and the world had refused to sell it to him.

There was a widow in Menton, an Englishwoman named Mrs. Calloway, who lived in a small boardinghouse near the church. She was poor and in poor health, and Julian began to visit her. He did not bring money at first. He simply sat with her in her room and listened. She talked about her late husband, a naval officer who had died of fever in Bombay, and about her son, who had gone to Australia and did not write. She did not ask Julian for anything. She did not seem to care that he was wealthy or that he was dying. She treated him as she would have treated any visitor, with a mild, distracted courtesy that expected nothing.

It was the first relationship of his life in which he could not purchase anything. He could not buy her gratitude or her affection. He could only sit and listen and return the next day and listen again, and over the weeks he began to understand that this was perhaps what goodness actually was: not a grand gesture but a repeated, small, and thankless act of attention. It was harder than anything he had ever done. It was harder than gambling, harder than seduction, harder than any of the pleasures at which he had so effortlessly excelled.

In March he received a letter from Dr. Hartfield. It was brief and written in a hand that trembled slightly. The diagnosis, the letter said, had been reviewed. Further tests had been conducted on blood samples that Julian had given before his departure. There had been a mistake. The condition was not degenerative. It was a minor arrhythmia, entirely manageable, and Julian could expect to live a normal span.

He read the letter three times, sitting on the terrace of his villa with the sea glittering below. The relief that swept through him was so powerful that it made him dizzy. He was not going to die. He had decades ahead of him, decades of life and pleasure and all the things he had thought were lost.

He sat with the letter in his lap for a long while, and then he thought about Mrs. Calloway, and about the woman in Wentworth Street, and about all the clumsy, failed attempts at goodness that had filled the last four months of his life. He understood now that the clock had been false, that there had been no urgency, no deadline to force him into virtue. And he understood, with a clarity that startled him, that this changed nothing.

He would go back to Mrs. Calloway the next day. He would sit with her and listen. He would continue to be clumsy and to fail. He had learned, in the hardest way possible, that goodness was not a thing one accomplished but a thing one attempted, daily and without guarantee of success, and that the attempt was the whole of it. He folded the letter and placed it in his pocket and walked down the hill toward the town.
