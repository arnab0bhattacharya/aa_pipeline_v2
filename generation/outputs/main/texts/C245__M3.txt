The rain had not ceased for three days over Mayfair, turning the cobblestones of Mount Row into rivers of black water. Inside number fourteen, a mansion built on the profits of the Argentine railways, the gasoliers burned low in the long gallery, casting a sickly yellow glow upon the walls. Sir Bertram Molyneux stood before the empty space where his pride had hung, his hands clasped behind his back, his face the colour of old parchment. He did not turn when Inspector Aylesbury of Scotland Yard entered the room, accompanied by Sergeant Dacre.

"You will forgive my not greeting you properly, Inspector. I find I cannot look away from the thing. It is like visiting the bedside of a dying friend."

Aylesbury came to stand beside him. On the wall, in a heavy gilt frame of the Louis XIV style, hung a painting he had been summoned specifically to examine. It depicted a woman in a green velvet dress, seated at a harpsichord, her face turned three-quarters toward the viewer. The canvas was undeniably beautiful. Aylesbury, who had no claim to expertise in the fine arts, could see that much.

"That is the copy, sir?"

"That is the copy. The original was painted by Francesco Viani in 1623. I acquired it six years ago from the Colonna estate in Rome for a sum that would purchase this house twice over. And that—" Sir Bertram gestured at the painting with a trembling finger, "—is not it."

Aylesbury removed his spectacles and polished them on his cuff, a habit that Dacre had learned to recognise as the inspector's mind turning over like the flywheel of a great engine. He replaced them and stepped closer to the canvas, leaning forward until his nose nearly touched the surface.

"What leads you to the conviction that it is a forgery, Sir Bertram? You say it is near-perfect."

The collector's jaw tightened. "I have looked upon the Viani every morning for six years, Inspector. I have studied it by every light—morning light, evening light, lamplight, candlelight. I know the woman in that painting as I knew my late wife. The copy is masterful, I grant you. The forger has mimicked Viani's brushwork with extraordinary fidelity. But the eyes are wrong. In the original, the woman's left eye carries a particular warmth, a slight asymmetry that gives her face its haunting quality. Here, the eye is correct in placement and colour, but it is an eye without memory. It is the eye of a woman painted by someone who does not know her."

Aylesbury nodded slowly. He had learned, in fifteen years of pursuing thieves and confidence men, that the testimony of a passionate collector was often more reliable than that of a certified expert. Passion saw what scholarship overlooked.

"You state in your letter that the gallery has been under continuous guard."

"Night and day. My footman, Griggs, sleeps on a truckle bed in the antechamber at the far end of this corridor. The door to the gallery is locked at ten o'clock each evening and not opened until eight. Griggs holds the only key, and he is a man of twenty years' unblemished service."

"Yet the painting has been replaced," Aylesbury said, not as a question.

"Replaced, and the frame left undisturbed. You will observe that the seals on the frame's backing are intact. Sergeant, if you would be so good as to examine the lower edge."

Dacre knelt and produced a small magnifying glass. He studied the frame's joinery, the wax seals bearing Sir Bertram's crest, the pattern of the nails. After several minutes he straightened.

"The seals are unbroken, sir. The nails have not been drawn. Whoever made the exchange did not remove the canvas from the frame."

Sir Berram turned at last to face the inspector, and Aylesbury saw that the old man's eyes were wet. "How, then? The Viani was stretched and nailed in this frame in my presence. I watched the restorer secure it himself. If the frame was never opened, how can the painting have been changed?"

Aylesbury said nothing for a long moment. He walked the length of the gallery, studying the other paintings—none of them so valuable as the Viani had been, but a respectable collection of Dutch landscapes and English portraits. At the far end he found the door to the antechamber, and beyond it, the truckle bed where Griggs presumably slept. He noted the lock: a Chubb design, robust and recent. He noted also the fireplace, a vast Edwardian monstrosity of black marble, set into the gallery's inner wall.

"The chimney," he said.

Sir Bertram blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"You have had a chimney sweep in this gallery within the last month. I should like to speak with him. And with the picture restorer, and with your nephew. I take it they are the only persons besides yourself and your servants to have entered this room in that period?"

"They are. My nephew, Mr Cradock Molyneux, called upon me on the twelfth. The restorer, a Mr Anselm Veitch, came on the fifteenth to clean the Viani—it had developed a bloom of efflorescence on the lower left quadrant, a consequence of the damp. The sweep, a man named Polgreen, came on the eighteenth. The household accounts will confirm the dates."

Aylesbury requested that all three be brought before him, and within two hours they were assembled in Sir Bertram's study—a room of dark mahogany and leather that smelled of cigars and the century's decline.

Mr Veitch, the restorer, was a slight man in his sixties with ink-stained fingers and the cautious, precise speech of one accustomed to working in millimetres. He confirmed that he had cleaned the Viani, had removed it from the wall but not from its frame, and had applied a mild solvent to the affected area before re-hanging it. He had been alone in the gallery for approximately four hours.

Polgreen, the sweep, was a boy of no more than fourteen, undersized and soot-pale, who spoke in the clipped, defensive manner of the East End. He had swept the gallery chimney and the chimney of the study below. He had been attended at all times by Griggs. He had touched nothing.

Mr Cradock Molyneux was the last to speak. He was a young man of perhaps eight-and-twenty, well-tailored, handsome in the indolent way that inherited money often produces. He had called upon his uncle to discuss, he said, a matter of investments in a Canadian railway. He had spent twenty minutes in the gallery admiring the collection before taking tea with Sir Bertram in the study.

Aylesbury listened to each account without interruption. When Cradock had finished, the inspector rose and crossed to the window, where he stood looking out at the rain with his hands clasped behind his back in unconscious imitation of Sir Bertram.

"Mr Veitch," he said, "you are a restorer of considerable reputation. You were trained, if I am not mistaken, by Tito Palmaroli in Rome."

The old man inclined his head. "I was. Palmaroli was a master of the Venetian method. I was his apprentice for eleven years."

"Then you are more than a restorer. You are a painter in your own right, and one schooled in the techniques of the seventeenth century."

Veitch's composure did not falter. "I have painted, yes. Restoration and creation are not the same discipline, Inspector, but they are cousins."

Aylesbury turned from the window. "On the fifteenth of this month, you spent four hours alone with the Viani. During those four hours, you removed the painting from the wall and treated it for efflorescence. I believe that in that time you also painted a copy."

The silence in the study was absolute. Sir Bertram stared at the inspector. Cradock Molyneux shifted in his chair. Polgreen, who understood nothing of what was being discussed, stared at the carpet.

"The copy would have been prepared in advance, of course," Aylesbury continued. "You had seen the Viani before—Sir Bertram would have consulted you when the efflorescence first appeared. You would have visited the gallery, studied the painting, and returned to your studio to paint a facsimile on a canvas of identical age and provenance. Canvas of the correct period is not impossible to obtain in Rome, where you still have connections. On the fifteenth, you brought the copy concealed in your case of solvents and tools. You treated the original, then hung the copy in its place and carried the original out with you when you left."

Veitch raised a trembling hand to his collar. "This is—Inspector, this is absurd. I have spent forty years building a reputation—"

"Forty years building a reputation, and yet you are not a wealthy man, Mr Veitch. Restorers are craftsmen, not collectors. The Viani would fetch thirty thousand pounds on the continent. More in America." Aylesbury paused. "And there is the matter of the chimney."

Sir Bertram leaned forward. "The chimney? What has the chimney to do with Mr Veitch?"

"Nothing directly. But it confirms the method. The sweep, Polgreen, was attended by Griggs throughout his visit—so the boy is irrelevant, except that his presence establishes that the chimney was recently cleaned and therefore passable. The frame was never opened—so the original canvas was never removed from it by conventional means. The lock was never forced—so no one entered at night. The crime was committed in daylight, during Mr Veitch's four hours alone with the painting. But the question that troubled me was how the original was removed from the gallery unseen. Mr Veitch left by the front door, carrying his case. Griggs observed him go. The case was not large enough to conceal a rolled canvas of the Viani's dimensions."

Aylesbury crossed to the fireplace and looked up into the black throat of the chimney. "The chimney. The sweep cleaned it on the eighteenth—three days after the restorer's visit. But Mr Veitch visited on the fifteenth. The chimney was clean enough then to pass a canvas, wrapped in oilcloth, up into the flue and deposited on a ledge above the fireplace, where it would rest unseen in the dark. The cleaning on the eighteenth would have dislodged it, had Polgreen not been supervised. But the boy swept only the lower portion. Griggs, standing in the room, would not have thought to look upward into the flue."

Sir Bertram rose from his chair. "You are saying the original Viani is still in this house?"

"I am saying it was concealed in the chimney until it could be retrieved. But Mr Veitch did not retrieve it. He could not return to the house without arousing suspicion. He required an accomplice—someone with access to the gallery, someone who could remove the canvas from the flue and carry it away at leisure." Aylesbury turned to face the room. His gaze settled on Cradock Molyneux, who had gone very still. "Your nephew visited on the twelfth, before the crime. But he returned yesterday, did he not, Sir Bertram? A social call, unannounced."

Sir Bertram's face drained of what little colour remained. "He called yesterday afternoon. He spent some minutes alone in the gallery while I was speaking with my secretary in the study."
