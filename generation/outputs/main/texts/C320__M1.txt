The rain fell in a steady, silver sheet against the glass of the signal box, blurring the constellation of lamps that was Blackwood Junction into a watercolour of greens, reds, and ambers. Below, the rails glistened like wet licorice, disappearing into the London fog. Arthur Penhaligon wiped a grimy hand across his brow, though he was far from warm. The cold that had settled in his bones was not from the November air, but from the memory of the night before.

He had told them, of course. He had told the Station Master, a pompous man named Gable who smelled of sherry and self-importance. He had told the yard foreman. He had even, in a moment of desperate conviction, told the two constables who had come down from the city. They had all listened with the same look: a mixture of pity and professional skepticism. A man alone in a signal box for twelve hours, with only the wind and the rattle of the telegraph for company. A man who might be tempted to find solace in a flask. They hadn't said it, not in so many words, but the implication hung in the air, thick and choking as the coal smoke.

It had been just past midnight. The 23:40 goods train, ‘The Nightingale’, had been rumbling through on the up-line, a ponderous beast of iron and steam. Its wagons were a motley collection of open-topped coal carriers, sealed boxcars, and livestock pens. Arthur’s job was simple: watch the lights, listen for the whistle, and ensure the track was clear. But as the last few wagons rattled past, a flicker of light caught his eye. It was in the third wagon from the end, a high-sided, covered van that was conspicuously empty. The door was slightly ajar. And from within that shadowed interior, a hand-held lamp blinked.

It wasn't the random swing of a lantern. It was deliberate. Three short flashes. A pause. Three long flashes. A pause. Three short flashes again. S-O-S. A desperate, silent scream in the heart of the moving train. Arthur had frozen, his hand hovering over the brass lever of his signal. He saw a face, pale and indistinct, a mere smudge in the gloom behind the lamp’s glow. Then the train was past, its clatter swallowed by the night, leaving only the smell of hot oil and the echo of his own frantic heartbeat. He had pulled the emergency communication cord, and the line had been stopped, but by the time the next train had arrived to investigate, ‘The Nightingale’ was long gone, its trail lost in the sprawl of the London rail network.

The official story was that nothing was amiss. The train had reached its destination, the yard at Paddington. But then the report had come through the telegraph, a terse message that made Gable’s face turn the colour of old putty. A parcel, containing bearer bonds to the value of ten thousand pounds, had vanished from the very same train. It was supposed to be in a locked, iron-strapped strongbox in a different wagon entirely. Yet, it was gone. And now, Arthur’s story was no longer the ramblings of a lonely man. It was a detail in a serious crime, a detail that everyone, including Gable, was now determined to ignore or explain away. It was easier to blame a drunken signalman than to admit the possibility of a phantom on their railway.

Tonight, Arthur was not alone. A man sat on the small stool by the stove, nursing a cup of tea. His name was Mr. Thorne, and he was an investigator sent down by the railway company’s head office. He was a quiet, bird-like man with a sharp gaze that missed nothing. He hadn’t accused Arthur of anything. He hadn’t even mentioned the flask of brandy Arthur kept in his locker. He had simply asked him to describe the night, minute by minute, his questions precise and unemotional.

“An empty van, you say?” Thorne had asked, his voice soft. “Not a passenger carriage?”

“No, sir. A goods van. The sort they use for general freight. But it was empty. I could see straight through it from one side to the other, save for the shadow of that lamp.”

“And the signal. You’re certain of the pattern?”

“I’ve been on the railway for twenty years, sir. I know my Morse code. It was S-O-S. Clear as day.”

Thorne had nodded, making a small note in his pocketbook. Now, as the clock on the wall ticked towards midnight, he stood and stretched. “Well, Mr. Penhaligon. Let us see if your ghost has a schedule.”

The telegraph key began to chatter, and Arthur’s hands moved with practiced ease, acknowledging the message. “It’s coming,” he said, his voice tight. “The down-line goods. ‘The Flying Scotsman’s Shadow’, they call this one.”

He pulled the levers, the heavy iron rods shifting the points far below with a satisfying thud. A distant whistle shrieked, a mournful cry in the damp night. A pair of headlamps cut through the rain, growing rapidly until the engine roared past the signal box, a monstrous iron dragon breathing fire and steam. The heat and noise were a physical blow. And then the wagons began to pass, a long, rattling serpent of freight.

Thorne stood by the window, his face illuminated by the passing lights, his eyes scanning every wagon. Arthur felt a tremor of doubt. What if he had imagined it? What if the shadows and the rain and the loneliness had played a trick on him?

Then he saw it. The same type of wagon. High-sided, covered, with a heavy door. It was in the same position, third from the end. And the door was slightly ajar.

“Mr. Thorne!” Arthur hissed, pointing.

The investigator’s head snapped around. He pressed his face to the glass, his expression unreadable. The wagon drew level with the box. For a heart-stopping moment, there was nothing but darkness within. Arthur’s hope began to curdle into shame.

Then, a light. It was fainter this time, a weak, guttering flame. But the pattern was unmistakable. Three short flashes. A pause. Three long flashes. A pause. Three short flashes.

“Good God,” Thorne breathed, his composure finally cracking. He was already turning, his hand on the door handle. “Is there a telephone to the next station down the line? Chelmsford?”

“Yes, sir, but—”

“Get them. Tell them to prepare a boarding party. We’re stopping that train.”

But the train was already gathering speed, its tail lights shrinking into the gloom. The telephone call would take precious minutes. The train would be miles away.

“There’s no time, sir,” Arthur said, his mind racing. “But there’s the down-line express. The 00:15 to Ipswich. It’s due in five minutes. It’s faster. It can catch it.”

Thorne looked at him, a wild, desperate idea dawning in his eyes. “Can you get us on it?”

“I can flag it down at the platform. They won’t like it, but—”

“Do it.”

What followed was a frantic blur. They scrambled down the steep wooden stairs from the signal box, their boots slipping on the wet iron of the gantry. The rain lashed their faces as they ran along the ballast towards the small, empty platform of Blackwood Junction. The express was already a rumble in the distance, its powerful headlamp a blinding star.

Arthur stood on the edge of the platform, waving his signal lantern frantically. The driver, seeing the emergency signal, blew his whistle in angry protest but began to slow the train. The great machine hissed to a stop, and a furious guard leaned out of his van.

“What in God’s name is this?” the guard shouted.

“Railway business! Urgent!” Thorne yelled back, flashing a small, silver-edged warrant card. “We need to board!”

They didn’t wait for an invitation, scrambling up the steps into the guard’s van as the train lurched forward again, its wheels spinning for a moment before finding their grip. The chase was on. They stood in the cramped, swaying van, the smell of brine and oil in their nostrils, and watched the tracks fly by beneath them. The express was a powerful beast, and they could feel it eating up the distance.

Minutes later, they saw the tail lights of the goods train ahead. The express driver, now understanding the gravity of the situation, blew his whistle again, a long, commanding blast. The lights of the goods train began to slow.

They pulled alongside the stationary train in a lonely stretch of cutting, with nothing but dark woods on either side. Thorne and Arthur, followed by two burly railway porters who had been roused by the guard, clambered across the coupling and onto the wet, grimy wagons. They moved towards the back, their lanterns casting long, dancing shadows.

There it was. The empty van. The door was still ajar. Thorne pushed it open with a loud creak. The van was empty, just as Arthur had said. The floor was bare, save for a few loose straw bales.

“Nothing,” one of the porters grunted. “It’s a wild goose chase.”

But Thorne was on his knees, his lantern held low. “Look,” he said, his voice quiet. He pointed to the floorboards. One of them, near the centre of the wagon, was slightly raised. He took a penknife from his pocket and pried at the edge. The board came up to reveal a deep, hidden compartment cut into the frame of the wagon. And inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was a heavy, iron-strapped strongbox.

As they lifted it out, Arthur heard a faint sound. A scraping. It was coming from the other side of the wagon, from behind a stack of discarded packing crates. Thorne heard it too. He drew a small revolver from his coat. With a nod to the porters, they moved the crates aside.

Huddled in the corner, shivering and pale, was a young man in a railway porter’s uniform. His face was bruised, and his hands were bound with coarse rope. He looked up at them, his eyes wide with terror and relief. He was the face Arthur had seen, the ghost in the machine. He had been locked in, left to freeze or suffocate, a loose end to be disposed of when the train reached its final, remote destination. The lamp had been his only tool, his only voice.

As they untied him, the first light of dawn was beginning to soften the eastern sky. The rain had stopped. The mystery of the missing bonds was solved, and the phantom of Blackwood Junction was no longer a figment of a lonely signalman’s imagination. Arthur Penhaligon stood on the cold, hard ground beside the track, the chill of the night finally leaving his bones, replaced by the quiet, solid warmth of vindication.
