The sun had abandoned the trough of the Java Sea, leaving behind a copper bruise on the rim of the world, as though the sky had been violently struck. It was an immense, sultry quietude that weighed upon the seven men in the longboat, a silence made of heat, thirst, and the profound weariness of survival. Around them, the water, oily and sluggish, took on the hue of dark, unpolished metal, reflecting nothing of the heavens but a dull, sinister glow. The boat, wallowing heavily, seemed not so much to float as to be suspended in a vast, viscous fluid that whispered against the strakes with a sound like the dry rustling of old silk.

In the stern-sheets, propped against a rolled-up spanker that had served no purpose since the burning of the *Barangay*, lay Captain Vlaminck. He was a man once robust, heavy-shouldered, accustomed to the loud command of his own quarterdeck, but now he was merely a vessel of slow, escaping life. A tourniquet, bound tightly above his shattered knee, could not entirely conceal the dark, feverish stain that had soaked through the makeshift bandages and spread upon the boards beneath him. His eyes were open, gazing past the oppressive sky into some uncharted region of his own fading consciousness. He breathed with a faint, whistling labour, and he would not decide.

He had not spoken a word of command since the explosion and the subsequent, terrifying dispersal of the burning wreck. It was as if the violent severing of his flesh had also severed his bond to the living world, leaving his authority to evaporate in the acrid smoke that still lingered, perhaps, in his nostrils. He lay there, a monumental ruin of submission to fate, his men might have expected from a native chief, but which in a white man, a master of ships, wore the aspect of an obscure, terrible abdication.

Aft, by the useless tiller, stood Serapion, the mate. He was a thin, dark man from the Provençal coast, with a narrow, ascetic face and eyes that held a restless, hunting gleam. He chewed nervously at his lower lip, his gaze shifting constantly from the dying captain to the distant, hazy line of the horizon, where the threat of land lay hidden in the shimmering haze. Beside him, gripping the gunwale with knuckles white beneath the grime, sat Brouwer, the bosun—a broad, blond man from the Frisian islands, stubborn, practical, his mind anchored in the concrete realities of cordage, oakum, and the immutable necessity of water.

They could not agree. It was a quarrel conducted in low, tense tones, fraught with a suppressed ferocity that seemed out of place in the vast, empty indifference of the sea, yet was perhaps the inevitable product of it.

"East-northeast," Serapion was saying, his voice a harsh, breathless murmur. "I tell you, it is the only course. Three days, perhaps four, with the current favoring us, and we raise the island of Selat. Three days."

"Three days," Brouwer repeated, his heavy brows drawn together in a scowl of profound distrust. "And what will we find there, Serapion? You know what they say. A country of savages. Heathens. They will beach us on the reefs and take our throats before we taste the sand. You would lead us to the knife for a shadow on the water."

"And you would lead us to die of thirst in the open sea!" Serapion retorted, leaning forward, the cords standing out on his neck. "The casks are nearly empty. You have seen the men drink. The sun will be a furnace again tomorrow. We cannot last four days. We cannot last three. The land is water, trees, life. We must risk the shore."

"Risk is a word for a gambler," Brouwer said, his slow, guttural voice carrying the weight of an immovable objection. "I am a seaman. I say we make for the port of Bandar. It is known. There is law there. Dutch law. We steer north-northwest. It is farther, yes. A week. Perhaps more. But we have the sea, and the sea is our element. We do not run into the jaws of an unknown land."

"A week!" Serapion almost laughed, a dry, painful sound. "You speak of a week as if it were an hour. Look at them." He gestured vaguely toward the four ragged sailors huddled amidships, their faces drawn, their eyes sunk deep into hollow sockets, gazing at nothing. "They are already ghosts. They will not see Bandar."

"They will not see Selat, either," Brouwer said stubbornly. "If they are to die, let them die under a Christian sky, not butchered on a pagan beach."

The argument hung in the air, unresolved, a bitter, exhausting impasse. The heat of the evening pressed down upon them, thick and stifling. The copper bruise on the horizon deepened into a dark, angry purple, and the first stars began to prick through the velvet dusk, cold and remote, utterly indifferent to the agonizing calculus of human survival.

It was then that Captain Vlaminck stirred. A low groan escaped his lips, a sound that seemed to come from a great depth within him. Serapion and Brouwer both turned, their quarrel suspended, their faces suddenly attentive, held by the instinctive deference they still felt for the dying man.

Vlaminck’s eyes, which had been staring blankly, slowly focused. They turned towards Serapion, then towards Brouwer, and finally rested upon the dark, heaving waste of water that surrounded them. His lips moved, but no sound came. He struggled, his chest heaving with the effort of speech, and then, at last, a whisper reached them, hoarse, broken, almost inaudible.

"The sea . . . the sea holds no . . . no promise. Only the land . . . only the land holds . . . anything." He paused, his breath catching. "But the land . . . the land is a treacherous . . . mistress. She will . . . she will take you . . . if you go to her . . . in fear." He closed his eyes, and a shudder ran through his large frame. "I . . . I cannot . . . choose. Not . . . not for you. The darkness . . . it is the same . . . everywhere."

He said nothing more. His head rolled to one side, his chin resting upon his chest, and the faint, whistling breath resumed its rhythm, a metronome of mortality marking the time they had left. He had offered them no direction, only a riddle, a dying man's ambiguous testament to the universal treachery of both earth and water.

The silence that followed was heavier than the heat. Serapion stared at the captain's bowed head, his face working with a sudden, impotent fury, while Brouwer looked down at his own hands, calloused and raw, as if seeking in them the strength to grasp a certainty that eluded him. The men amidships did not move; they had not heard, or if they had, they had not understood. They sat in their own private despair, hunched against the coming of the night.

Serapion turned away first, his jaw set, his eyes burning with a desperate, almost fanatical resolve. He looked towards the east-northeast, where the land of Selat lay hidden behind the veil of the night. It was the unknown, the dangerous, the place of savages and sudden death, but it was near. It was a tangible hope, however fragile, set against the slow, agonizing certainty of the open sea. He could not endure the emptiness any longer. He needed to believe in the shore.

"We will run for Selat," he said, his voice low but carrying a hard, final inflection.

Brouwer raised his head, his pale eyes glinting in the starlight. He looked at Serapion for a long moment, his expression a mixture of defiance and resignation. He knew, perhaps, that to oppose Serapion now would lead to violence, and violence in a boat of dying men was the ultimate absurdity. He also knew that the sea would kill them just as surely as any spear.

"Then God have mercy on our souls," he said, very quietly, "for the captain will not."

He moved forward, his bulk shifting the boat slightly, and began, with a heavy, mechanical slowness, to unship the steering-oar. Serapion, with a nervous, jerky energy, went forward to rouse the men, to make them grasp the looms of the oars once more. They responded with the apathy of the half-dead, moving like automatons, their limbs stiff, their eyes vacant. They took their places, and at Serapion's whispered command, the blades dropped into the water, and the boat began to move, slowly, painfully, towards the dark line of the horizon.

The night closed in around them, profound and impenetrable. The stars burned with a fierce, unblinking intensity, casting no light upon the water, only a cold, cosmic mockery of their plight. The sound of the oars, dipping and pulling, dipping and pulling, was the only sound in the vast silence, a feeble, rhythmic protest against the indifference of the universe. Captain Vlaminck, in the stern-sheets, lay still, his face a pale blur in the darkness, his breath growing fainter, a dying ember in the ashes of his authority. He had given them nothing but his own end, and they were left to navigate the treacherous passage between the devil of the sea and the deep, unknown hostility of the land, guided only by the desperate, unreasoning instinct to survive.
