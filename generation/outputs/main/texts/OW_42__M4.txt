The cottage in which the little lame boy lived was the last house in the world, or so it seemed to him, for beyond it there was nothing but the great forest, which stood so thick and so black against the sky that the sun himself appeared to pause at its edge, as one who hesitates on the threshold of a house where he is not welcome. His name was Alaric, and because of his crooked foot he could not run with the other children of the village, nor dance at the fairs, nor do any of those gay and foolish things which are the chief business of the young. So he sat at his window and watched the forest, and the forest, in its turn, watched him.

It was on a night of winter, when the moon hung in the sky like a silver lamp in a cathedral of ice, that he first saw the white stag. It came out of the darkness of the trees without a sound, and stood in the little clearing before the cottage, and the moonlight lay upon its back like a mantle of woven pearl. Its antlers were like the bare branches of some enchanted tree, and its eyes were the colour of deep water. Alaric, who had been lying awake and listening to the wind, rose from his bed and pressed his face against the cold glass, and the stag looked at him, and it seemed to the boy that there was in its gaze a kind of pity that was worse than scorn.

On the next night it came again, and on the next, and on the next, and each time it stood a little nearer to the cottage, and each time its eyes said the same thing. And on the seventh night Alaric, who could resist no longer, wrapped his cloak about him and opened the door and went out into the snow. The stag turned and moved into the forest, and the boy followed, limping through the drifts, and the trees closed behind him like the pages of a book.

He walked for a long time, or it may have been a short time, for in the forest the hours are not measured as they are in the world of men. The stag went before him, always at the same distance, and the snow beneath its hooves made no sound, and the branches above his head were heavy with frost that glittered like the diamonds of some dead queen. And then, all at once, the trees thinned, and he came out into a wide plain, and there before him, rising from the mist, was a city.

It was a city of white marble and silver, and its towers were slender as the stems of lilies, and its windows were of coloured glass that burned with a soft and steady light. There were no walls about it, for it seemed to have no need of walls, and the gates stood open, and there was no guard at the gates. Alaric passed through, and the stag was gone.

The streets were full of people, and they were all beautiful. There was no one among them who was lame, or blind, or bent with years; there was no one whose face was seamed with sorrow or scarred with want. They were young, all of them, and fair, and their garments were of silk and velvet, and they wore jewels in their hair. And yet, as Alaric stood and looked at them, he was aware of a strange and terrible thing, for there was not one of them who smiled.

He approached a young man who sat upon a marble bench, and who was so beautiful that he might have been the model of a sculptor's dream. "Tell me," said Alaric, "what is the name of this city, and why is it that no one here is happy?"

The young man looked at him with eyes that were like the eyes of the stag, and there was no anger in them, nor any joy. "This is the city of the perfected," he said. "Here there is no pain, and no age, and no death. Here there is no striving, for all things are already attained. And for that reason there is no joy, for joy is the child of desire, and desire is the child of want."

"But surely," said Alaric, "you must be content."

"Contentment," said the young man, "is the virtue of those who have nothing to hope for. We are not content. We are merely without hope. There is a difference, though the world does not always perceive it."

And Alaric saw that it was true. He looked at the faces about him, and he saw that they were like the faces of those who have eaten of some strange fruit that takes away hunger but leaves no sweetness. They were beautiful, and they were kind, and they were utterly without life. The women wore gowns of pale green and silver, and their laughter, when it came, was like the sound of a bell that has no tongue. The men talked of art and of poetry, and their talk was very clever, but it was the talk of those who have read all books and written none.

Alaric wandered through the streets, and he came at last to a great square, and in the centre of the square there was a fountain, and the water that rose from it was not water but light, and it fell back into the basin with a sound like the sighing of the wind. And by the fountain there sat an old man—the only old man he had seen—and his face was full of wrinkles, and his hands were gnarled, and he was weeping.

"Why do you weep?" asked Alaric.

"Because I am old," said the old man, "and because I remember."

"Remember what?"

"I remember the world," said the old man. "I remember the rain, and the mud, and the hunger, and the pain. I remember the faces of those I loved, and I remember that they died. I remember the taste of bread that was earned, and the sweetness of sleep that was deserved. And I remember that once, long ago, I was happy."

"But here there is no pain," said Alaric.

"No," said the old man, "and there is no joy. For joy and pain are twins, and they cannot be separated. The city has taken away the one, and it has taken away the other, and it calls the emptiness peace. But I know better. I am old, and I am ugly, and I am full of sorrow, and I would not exchange my sorrow for all the marble of this city."

And Alaric, standing there in the light of the fountain, understood something that he had never understood before. He understood that his crooked foot was not a curse but a gift, for it had taught him to sit still and to watch and to dream, and dreams are the food of the soul. He understood that the children who ran and danced had something he did not have, but that he had something they did not have, and that the world was made of such differences, and that it was good.

He turned and went back through the streets, and the people looked at him as he passed, and there was in their eyes a kind of wonder, as though they saw in him something they had long forgotten. And he came to the gates, and beyond the gates the stag was waiting, and its eyes were no longer pitiful but glad.

He followed it home through the forest, and the snow was soft beneath his feet, and the stars were bright above the branches. And when he came to the cottage, the door was open, and the fire was burning, and his mother was sitting by the hearth, and she looked up and saw him, and she smiled.

And Alaric, the little lame boy, sat down by the fire and stretched out his hands to the flames, and he was happy. For he knew that he was alive, and that to be alive is to be both glad and sorry, and that the city of the perfected is the city of the dead.
