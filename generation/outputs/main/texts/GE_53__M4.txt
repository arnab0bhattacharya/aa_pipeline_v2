The news reached Mrs. Aglionby, as such news generally does, through the medium of a servant whose relish for the communication was but thinly veiled by an appearance of dismay; for in a provincial town, where the currents of life run in narrow channels, the mishap of a neighbour is apt to be felt as a species of dramatic entertainment, furnishing matter for that indefinite shaking of the head which is the tribute paid by the unoffending to the unfortunate. It was upon a Tuesday morning, at the hour when the young ladies of the Manor House Seminary were engaged with their globes and their copybooks, that the cook, a woman of a confidential habit, presented herself at the door of the study with a countenance of such portentous gravity as belongs to those who are the bearers of tidings they would not for the world be thought to enjoy.

“If you please, ma’am,” said she, with that air of reluctant duty which is the cook’s tribute to propriety, “there’s Betty has just come up from the town, and she says—but I’m sure I don’t know how to tell you, ma’am—she says that Miss Clemmow has been seen at the coach-office at Stony Stratford, and that Mr. Pargeter, the music-master, was with her, and they’ve taken the London coach, and it’s all over the town that they’ve gone off together.”

Mrs. Aglionby laid down her pen with a deliberation that cost her an effort, for her hand trembled, and she was conscious of a sudden coldness at her heart, as though the shadow of a coming calamity had fallen upon her. She was a woman of fifty, whose life had been a long discipline in the patient endurance of small adversities, and who had learned, in the school of widowhood and reduced circumstances, to meet the larger strokes of fortune with a composure that was not so much stoicism as the habit of a mind accustomed to look at consequences in their widest relations. She had kept this school for twelve years, and had built up its reputation by a scrupulous attention to those minutiae of decorum which, in the eyes of the world, constitute the difference between a respectable establishment and one that is merely fashionable; and she knew, with the fatal clearness of a woman who has long watched the currents of public opinion, that the elopement of a pupil would be as a stain upon the fair page of her prospectus, a thing that parents would remember when they read of it in the county newspaper, and that the fathers of her remaining pupils would look at their daughters with a new suspicion, as though the air of the Manor House were infected with a subtle contagion of romance.

But it was not the thought of her own prospects that chiefly moved her; it was the image of the girl herself—poor silly Harriet Clemmow, with her pretty face and her shallow head, who had been the pride of her father’s heart and the despair of her teachers, and who had sat at the piano on Saturday last, playing her scales with a docility that had seemed to promise a return to better things. Mrs. Aglionby had known the girl’s mother, a gentle, anxious woman, who had died when Harriet was a child, and she had felt for the daughter something of that pity which we give to those who have been deprived of the natural guardianship of a mother’s love. She had watched the growth of the girl’s vanity as a gardener watches the growth of a weed, and had tried, by admonition and by the mild discipline of her school, to check it; but she had not foreseen—no, she had not foreseen—that the music-master, a young man of prepossessing appearance and a certain melancholy grace of manner, who came twice a week to teach the young ladies the elements of harmony, would be the instrument of her undoing. It was a thing that had happened in other schools, she knew; she had heard of such cases, and had thought, with the complacency of one who has not yet been tried, that she would have been more vigilant. But Mr. Pargeter had come to her with the highest recommendations, and had been regular in his attendance, and had never, in her presence, exceeded the bounds of that respectful deference which a teacher of music owes to the mistress of a seminary. She had not known—how should she?—that he had a way of looking at a pretty girl over the top of his violin, and that Harriet Clemmow had a way of blushing when he did so, which was a language more eloquent than any words.

The news spread through the school with the rapidity of fire in a field of stubble. The young ladies whispered together in the intervals of their lessons, and cast glances of mingled horror and fascination at the empty seat which Harriet Clemmow had occupied at the piano. There were some among them who felt that the world had suddenly become a more interesting place, and who looked at their own music-books with a new and thrilling consciousness of the possibilities of life. But Mrs. Aglionby, going her rounds among them with a face of unaltered composure, felt that she was walking upon the thin crust of a volcano, and that the least sign of agitation on her part would be the signal for a general outbreak of hysterical excitement.

It was on the third day after the news had come that Mr. Clemmow arrived. He was a man of fifty-five, a manufacturer of hosiery and lace, whose mills at Drayton had made him one of the most considerable men in the county, and whose temper had been soured by the long struggle of a self-made man against the prejudices of a society that had not yet learned to regard the manufacturer as the equal of the landed proprietor. He was a man of strong passions and imperious will, who had loved his daughter with a fierce, possessive love that had in it more of pride than of tenderness, and who now felt the blow that had fallen upon him as a personal humiliation rather than as a sorrow. He came into Mrs. Aglionby’s study without waiting to be announced, and stood before her with his hat in his hand and his face flushed with a anger that he did not attempt to conceal.

“Well, ma’am,” said he, in a voice that was loud and harsh, “this is a pretty business you’ve brought upon me. I sent my daughter to your school to be taught what a lady ought to know, and you’ve let her run off with a fiddler. I don’t know what you call it, but I call it a pretty business.”

Mrs. Aglionby had risen to receive him, and she stood before him with a quiet dignity that was not without its effect upon a man who, with all his arrogance, was not insensible to the power of a calm and well-bred demeanour.

“Mr. Clemmow,” said she, “I will not attempt to palliate the misfortune that has befallen you. It is a grief to me as it is to you; and if I could have foreseen it, I would have given my right hand to prevent it. But I must tell you, in justice to myself, that I have spared no pains to guard the morals of the young ladies committed to my charge. I have watched over your daughter with an anxiety that you can hardly imagine; and if she has been led astray, it has been by the arts of a man whom I trusted, and against whom I had no warning.”

“And what good does that do me?” cried the manufacturer, with a gesture of impatience. “You might as well tell me that you’ve taken care of your own reputation, and let my daughter go to the devil. I tell you, ma’am, I’ll have the law of that fellow. I’ll have him up before the judges. I’ll make him pay for it, if it costs me half my fortune.”

“I hope you will,” said Mrs. Aglionby, with a faint smile; “but I would venture to suggest that, in the meantime, you should consider what is best to be done for your daughter. She is young, and she has been foolish; but she is not wicked, Mr. Clemmow—she is only weak and vain, and she has been deceived by a man who knew how to flatter her vanity. If you will let me advise you, you will not speak of the law until you have seen her, and have tried to win her back by kindness.”

The manufacturer looked at her with a sudden suspicion. “You seem to know a great deal about her,” said he. “How do you know she’s not wicked? How do you know she hasn’t been in a conspiracy with the fellow to deceive me?”

“Because I have studied her character,” said Mrs. Aglionby, “and I know that she is not capable of a deep-laid scheme. She is a girl of impulse, not of calculation; and if she has done wrong, she has done it thoughtlessly, and will repent of it bitterly when she comes to her senses. I have seen too much of human nature, Mr. Clemmow, to be surprised at the follies of the young; but I have also seen enough of it to know that the follies of the young are often the result of the neglect of their elders. Forgive me if I speak plainly; but have you not been too much absorbed in your business to give your daughter the sympathy and the companionship she needed? Have you not given her everything but your time?”

The manufacturer was silent for a moment, and his face worked with some emotion that he was trying to suppress. “I have worked for her,” he said at last, in a lower voice. “I have toiled early and late to make a fortune for her, and this is the return I get.”

“It is the way of the world,” said Mrs. Aglionby, gently. “We labour for those we love, and we forget that what they need is not our labour, but ourselves. But it is not too late to repair the past. Let me go with you to London. Let me see your daughter, and try to bring her back to a sense of her duty. I have no wish to shield myself; I am ready to bear my share of the blame; but I am sure that I can do more for you by persuasion than you can do by anger.”

The manufacturer looked at her with a new expression in his eyes—an expression of surprise, and something that was almost respect. He had come prepared to find a woman who would be eager to shift the blame from her own shoulders to those of her pupil; and he found a woman who was ready to take a share of the blame that was not hers, and to offer him the help of a sympathy that was both wise and generous. He was not a man who was accustomed to yield, but there was something in the quiet dignity of the schoolmistress that subdued him in spite of himself.

“Well, ma’am,” said he, after a pause, “I don’t know as I’m much of a hand at this sort of thing; but if you’re willing to go with me, I’ll not say no. I’m not a man that’s used to being beaten, and I don’t mean to be beaten now. But I’ll try your way first, and if it doesn’t answer, I’ll try mine.”

And so it was settled that they should go to London together on the morrow, and that Mrs. Aglionby should use her influence with the girl to bring her back to her father’s house.
