The laurel walk at Ashcombe was a place of perpetual twilight, even at noon in July. The bushes, planted a century before by a gloomy ancestor with a taste for the funereal, had grown into a dense, interlocking tunnel of waxy green leaves and pale, twisting stems. It was here, in this fragrant gloom, that Julian Ashcombe, heir to the estate, had taken to meeting Betsy Pring, the miller’s daughter.

He would arrive first, his boots crunching softly on the leaf-mould, his heart thumping a frantic, joyful rhythm against his ribs. He was a young man of three-and-twenty, with a poet’s face and a restless energy that his father, the Squire, despaired of. Betsy, when she came, was a vision of sun and earth. She smelled of the bakery and the river, her hands were rough from work, and her laugh was a sudden, unselfconscious burst of sound that made the silent laurels tremble. Their meetings were a stolen hour of perfection, a world away from the grand, cold rooms of the house and the dusty ledgers of the mill. They spoke of everything and nothing, their hands sometimes brushing, their eyes holding a conversation that words were too clumsy to articulate.

It was on a Thursday, as a shaft of sunlight pierced the canopy and illuminated a single, perfect spider’s web, that the world fell in. Julian had just taken Betsy’s hand, emboldened by the quiet, and pressed a kiss to her knuckles, when a sharp, indrawn breath cut through the silence.

‘Upon my word.’

They sprang apart as if scalded. Framed at the entrance of the walk, a basket of rose clippings on her arm, stood Miss Honoria Pring. She was Betsy’s aunt, a widow of formidable reputation and limited means, who had taken charge of her brother’s daughter after his death. Her face, usually a mask of genteel resignation, was now a study in astonishment, swiftly followed by a gleam of something far more calculating.

‘Betsy,’ she said, her voice dangerously calm. ‘And Mr. Ashcombe. I see I have interrupted a… botanical discussion.’

Julian’s face flushed a deep, mortified crimson. ‘Miss Pring, I—’

‘You will not speak, sir,’ Honoria commanded, her eyes sweeping over him with an appraising light that made him feel like a prize heifer at a fair. ‘Betsy, you will go to the house. We shall speak of this later. Mr. Ashcombe, I believe my niece and I are to take tea with your mother this afternoon. We shall continue this conversation then.’

That afternoon, in the vast, silk-hung drawing room, Honoria Pring performed a masterstroke of social engineering. The Squire and his wife, already predisposed to see the Pring girl as a fortune-hunter, were prepared for tears and recriminations. Instead, Honoria presented the situation as a delicate matter of honour. She spoke of a youthful indiscretion, of a young man’s passionate nature, and of a poor girl’s vulnerable position. She did not demand marriage; she lamented the impossibility of it. She painted a picture of a scandal that would inevitably follow, of whispers that would forever taint the Ashcombe name and leave poor Betsy utterly ruined.

‘The only honourable course,’ she concluded, her voice trembling with feigned emotion, ‘is for the connection to be managed. Quietly. With propriety. Under my own supervision, of course. I shall act as chaperone. We must give the appearance of a formal courtship, sanctioned by both families, to quell any hint of impropriety. The young people may meet, but only in the drawing room, and only for a prescribed hour. It is the only way to save my niece’s reputation and your son’s good name.’

The Squire, seeing a way to avoid a scandal and, he believed, a marriage, readily agreed. Julian, trapped by the very honour he had been taught to revere, could only nod in miserable assent.

Thus began the most excruciating courtship in the history of Wiltshire. The laurel walk was abandoned for the stifling formality of the drawing room. Their meetings, once spontaneous and joyful, were now scheduled events, observed by the gimlet eye of Honoria Pring, who sat in a high-backed chair, her knitting needles clicking a relentless, disapproving rhythm.

She managed everything. She dictated the topics of conversation, which were to be confined to the weather, the health of mutual acquaintances, and the state of the roads. She censored Julian’s attempts at poetry, declaring it ‘too flighty’ for a young woman of Betsy’s station. She criticised Betsy’s dress, her posture, her very laugh, which she now deemed ‘too loud, my dear, entirely too loud for a drawing room.’ She turned their love into a performance, a tedious, joyless pantomime of propriety.

Julian, who had loved Betsy’s wildness, her unvarnished honesty, now saw her only through a filter of anxious restraint. Betsy, who had loved Julian’s impetuous spirit, now saw him as a puppet, dancing to her aunt’s tune. Their eyes would meet across the tea table, and in them was a shared, desperate misery. The easy intimacy was gone, replaced by a chasm of awkwardness and frustration. They had nothing to say, for every real thought was forbidden.

The climax came in August. Honoria had arranged a small, formal dinner, a ‘coming-out’ of sorts for the courtship. The guests were the local vicar and a neighbouring squire. Julian, desperate to break the suffocating spell, sought a moment alone with Betsy in the conservatory. He found her by the orange trees, her back to him, her shoulders shaking.

‘Betsy,’ he whispered, his heart aching. ‘Forgive me. This is all my fault.’

She turned, her eyes red. ‘It is not your fault, Julian. It is this… this cage she has built for us. I feel I cannot breathe. I am not a lady of the drawing room. I am a miller’s daughter, and I am beginning to think I was a fool to ever dream of anything else.’

‘No,’ he said, taking her hands. ‘You are everything. It is this… this management I cannot bear. I would rather be banished than endure another hour of this.’

He pulled her to him, and for a moment, they clung to each other, a small island of genuine feeling in a sea of artifice. It was, of course, at this precise moment that Honoria Pring, who had noted their absence with alarm, swept into the conservatory.

‘Mr. Ashcombe! Betsy! This is precisely the kind of uncontrolled behaviour I have sought to prevent! You are proving yourselves unworthy of my efforts!’

But Julian had reached his limit. He turned to face her, his own temper finally rising. ‘Your efforts, madam? Your efforts have been to turn a sincere affection into a transaction, a spectacle for your own vanity! You have not managed a courtship; you have managed to ruin it. I would rather have been forbidden to see Betsy than to be forced to court her under your tyrannical eye!’

Honoria was taken aback, her face mottling with anger. ‘You forget yourself, sir. I am doing this for my niece’s honour!’

‘You are doing it for your own pride!’ Betsy cried, finding her voice at last. ‘You have made me feel like a shameful secret, and him like a criminal. I love him, Aunt, and you have made that love feel like a sin.’

The words hung in the air, raw and true. Honoria stared at them, her mouth opening and closing. For the first time, she saw not a social problem to be solved, but two young people she had wounded with her meddling. The vicar and the neighbouring squire, drawn by the raised voices, stood in the doorway, their faces a picture of astonishment.

In the ensuing, terrible silence, Julian took Betsy’s hand. ‘I love you, Betsy Pring,’ he said, his voice clear and steady. ‘And I will not let anyone, not even your aunt, manage that.’

He led her past the stunned guests, out of the conservatory, and into the fading evening light. They did not go to the laurel walk. They walked straight to the Squire’s study, and Julian, for the first time in his life, spoke to his father not as a boy, but as a man. He did not ask permission. He stated his intention. And in the face of such unmanaged, undeniable honesty, the Squire, for all his bluster, had no choice but to relent.
