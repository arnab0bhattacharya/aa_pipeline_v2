It has been remarked by profounder philosophers than myself—and if I might be so bold as to add, by more impoverished ones—that Fortune is a lady of exceedingly capricious temperament, dealing her favours with the indiscriminate zeal of a blindfolded chimney-sweep hurling soot at a whitewashed wall. Nowhere in the environs of the great metropolis was this melancholy truth more amply demonstrated than at the annual fair of Tottenden Common, a sprawling, muddy, cacophonous encampment situated just beyond the third milestone on the Great North Road, where the smoke of London began to thin and dissolve into the sweeter, though no less aggressive, aromas of the countryside.

The Tottenden fair was a city of canvas and damp timber, possessing all the noise of London with none of its dignity. Here were booths of astonishing variety, crowded together as though jostling for warmth: the exhibition of the Wild Man of Borneo, who suffered from a chesty cough; the peep-show of the Coronation, in which the King appeared to be made of painted cardboard; and the marvellous mechanical automaton that played the violin with all the grace of a decapitated headmaster. It was into this riotous spectacle that Mr. Absalom Trenchard had brought his singular prize: the Learned Pig, Porphyrius.

Porphyrius was a grunter of extraordinary intellectual attainments. He could solve arithmetic, distinguish between a king and a knave, and spell the name of the Lord Chancellor with the deliberate precision of a clerk in Chancery. He was, in Mr. Trenchard’s own impassioned rhetoric, the foremost porcine scholar of the age. Mr. Trenchard himself was a man of a lank and weatherbeaten aspect, possessing a threadbare velvet coat that had once known better days and a voice that boomed with the hollow resonance of an empty barrel.

But alas for the vanity of human ambitions! Mr. Trenchard, in a moment of deplorable weakness, had sat down to a game of cribbage with Mr. Simeon Snatchit, a rival showman whose soul was as crooked as the hat upon his brow. Mr. Snatchit was a man who shuffled cards as though he were born with a full deck concealed in his sleeves, and who possessed a silver tongue coated thickly with the most brazen falsehoods. The game commenced with a cheerful clatter of pegs, proceeded through a fog of pipe-smoke, and concluded with Mr. Trenchard staring in pale and utter ruin at his empty hands. Porphyrius, the pride of his life, the pillar of his livelihood, had been won, and was now being led away in triumph by the unscrupulous Snatchit, wearing a look of quiet resignation.

‘Gone!’ muttered Mr. Trenchard, staring at the vacant spot where his prodigy had stood. ‘The light of my existence is snuffed out. I am a ruined man, and my heart is a hollow gourd!’

It was at this nadir of his fortunes that Mr. Trenchard found himself in the vicinity of three persons of his acquaintance. The first was Mr. Barnaby Crump, a hot-pie man of considerable girth and equally considerable good nature, whose pies emitted a heat and fragrance that seemed to mock the biting October wind. The second was Mrs. Nelly Nettles, a ballad-seller, whose voice was perpetually hoarse from shouting the titles of her tragic ballads, and who wore a cloak of so many patches it resembled a geographical map of a famine-stricken country. The third was little Jenny Cob, a girl of perhaps ten years, who sold watercress from a battered wooden tray, and whose eyes were as sharp and bright as the brass buttons on a Beadle’s coat.

‘Cheer up, Absalom!’ cried Mr. Crump, thrusting a steaming pie into the unfortunate showman’s hands. ‘A pie will settle the stomach, if not the spirits! My pastry, though I say it myself, is a marvel of architectural engineering. It will stand up by itself, which is more than most men can do after a pint at the Boar and Whistle!’

‘Alas, Barnaby, no pie can mend a broken heart!’ returned Mr. Trenchard, wringing his hands. ‘Porphyrius is gone! Lost to that fiend, that serpent, that bag of moral putrefaction, Simeon Snatchit! He won him at cards, the scoundrel, the shark, the—augh!’

Mrs. Nettles, who had been listening with her head cocked to one side, struck her wooden tray with a resounding thwack. ‘A black deed!’ she cried, in a voice that rasped like a rusty hinge. ‘A deed worthy of the gallows! I have a ballad here that fits the occasion exactly: “The Cruel Showman, or, The Pig’s Lament!” Four verses, and a chorus that would melt a heart of stone! Only a penny, printed on the finest paper!’

‘We don’t need ballads, Nelly, we need a plan!’ said Jenny Cob, looking up from her watercress with an expression of shrewd determination. ‘I’ve watched that Snatchit. He’s set up his booth by the beech-tree, and he’s charging tuppence to see the pig do its letters. But he’s a fool, Absalom. He’s a great, blustering fool, and he drinks. I’ve seen him take three noggins of gin already, and the sun’s barely set.’

Mr. Trenchard looked at the watercress girl with a dawning, desperate hope. ‘Have you, Jenny? Have you indeed?’

‘I have,’ said Jenny, nodding sagely. ‘And if he drinks, he plays cards. And if he plays cards, he cheats. And if he cheats, he thinks everyone else is as honest as he is—which is to say, not at all. But he’s greedy, Mr. Trenchard. He’s greedier than a pig at a trough, if you’ll forgive the expression.’

‘Forgive it! I embrace it!’ cried Mr. Trenchard, a wild light springing into his eyes. ‘But what can we do? I have nothing left to stake. I am stripped to the bone, financially speaking!’

‘You have us,’ said Mr. Crump, laying a hearty, flour-dusted hand on Trenchard’s shoulder. ‘And I have my pie-stand. It’s worth a pretty penny, though I say it shouldn’t be. And Nelly has her ballads.’

‘And I have my cress,’ said Jenny, ‘though it’s not worth much. But I have my wits, which is more than Snatchit has. We’ll get your pig back, Mr. Trenchard, or my name isn’t Cob.’

The plan, such as it was, was hatched over a mutual consumption of one of Mr. Crump’s steak-and-kidney pies, which Mr. Trenchard declared was the finest thing he had ever tasted, though whether this was a compliment to Mr. Crump’s culinary skill or a reflection of Mr. Trenchard’s desperate hunger, I leave the reader to judge. It was decided that Mr. Crump, with his booming voice and jovial manner, should approach Mr. Snatchit and engage him in a game of cards, staking his pie-stand against the pig. Mrs. Nettles would provide a distraction by singing one of her more lugubrious ballads at the top of her voice, thereby drawing away the crowd and leaving Mr. Snatchit in a state of irritated privacy. And little Jenny Cob, with her quick eyes and nimble fingers, would watch Snatchit’s hands like a hawk, ready to signal if he attempted any of his customary sleights.

The party set off across the muddy common, their feet squelching in a rhythm that might have accompanied Mrs. Nettles’s singing, had she not been clearing her throat in preparation. They found Mr. Snatchit outside his booth, a squalid tent of mildewed canvas, counting his tuppences with the covetous glee of a dragon hoarding gold. He was a thin, wiry man, with a nose like a pair of closed gates and eyes that darted about like water-skeeters on a pond.

‘Good evening to you, Mr. Snatchit!’ cried Mr. Crump, with a heartiness that could have been heard in London. ‘A fine evening for business, and a finer one for pleasure! What do you say to a game of cards? A friendly wager, man to man, pie-stand to pig?’

Snatchit looked up, his narrow eyes glinting with avarice. He saw Mr. Crump’s substantial person, his flourishing trade, and his apparent simplicity, and he smiled a smile that was all teeth and no warmth. ‘A pie-stand, you say? Against the Learned Pig? Why not? I’m always willing to accommodate a friend. Come inside, come inside!’

But before they could enter, Mrs. Nettles planted herself firmly before the booth, threw back her head, and launched into a rendition of “The Murdered Sweetheart, or, The Bloody Knife.” Her voice, torn and strained by years of street-singing, soared over the fair like a wounded bird, and within moments a crowd of fifty or more had gathered around her, drawn by the irresistible lure of tragedy. They pressed forward, leaving Mr. Snatchit’s booth deserted.

‘Confound the woman!’ muttered Snatchit, glaring at the crowd. ‘She’s driving away my custom!’

‘Never mind her, Mr. Snatchit,’ said Mr. Crump, clapping him on the back. ‘We’ll have a quiet game while she’s at it. More privacy for us, eh?’

They entered the booth, where Porphyrius sat in his pen, looking up at his old master with an expression that, if pigs can be said to have expressions, was one of dignified reproach. A tallow candle guttered on a barrel, and a pack of cards was produced from Snatchit’s pocket with a flourish that was, in itself, a confession of guilt.

The game was écarté. Mr. Crump, whose knowledge of cards was roughly on a par with his knowledge of Sanskrit, blundered through the first hand with a cheerful ineptitude that delighted Snatchit. The pie-man lost, and lost again, his face a mask of jovial bewilderment. But Jenny Cob, perched on a stool in the corner with her tray of watercress, watched Snatchit’s hands. She saw the way his little finger curled, the way his thumb slipped a card from the bottom of the deck, the way his eyes flickered to his sleeve. And at the crucial moment, when the stake was the pie-stand and the pig, she coughed.

It was a small cough, a delicate cough, the cough of a girl with a tickle in her throat. But it was accompanied by a sharp look at Mr. Crump, who, for all his simplicity, was not entirely a fool. He had been losing on purpose, drawing Snatchit into a false sense of security, and now, with Jenny’s signal, he struck. He played his cards with a sudden, startling precision, laying down his hand with a triumphant roar that shook the canvas.

‘The pig is mine!’ bellowed Mr. Crump, sweeping the cards aside. ‘Mine, by all that’s holy and just! Absalom, my boy, your Porphyrius is restored to you!’
