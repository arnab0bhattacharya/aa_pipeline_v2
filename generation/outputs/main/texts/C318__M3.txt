The telegram arrived on a Tuesday, brought up from the harbour office by a boy with bare feet and a reluctance to climb stairs. Arthur Pendelton found it on his desk when he returned from his mid-morning inspection of the British cemetery, where a laurel hedge had recently died of an unfamiliar blight. He set his pith helmet on the side table, poured himself a measure of gin from the decanter he kept in the bottom drawer of his filing cabinet, and sat down to read the yellow paper.

RECEIVED FROM FOREIGN OFFICE LONDON STOP ROYAL NAVY VESSEL HMS PERSERVERANT DISPATCHED ARRIVING SAN CRISTOBAL PORT WITHIN FOURTEEN DAYS STOP DIRECTED TO PROTECT BRITISH INTERESTS STOP FULL INSTRUCTIONS TO FOLLOW STOP ENDS.

Arthur read the telegram three times. He held it up to the window, as though the light might reveal a meaning hidden between the printed words. Then he folded it, placed it beneath a paperweight shaped like a miniature of Nelson's Column, and sat for some time looking at the wall behind his desk, where hung a map of the Republic of Costaverde, its borders faded to the colour of weak tea.

Twenty years. He had been consul in San Cristobal for twenty years, arriving in 1874 as a young man of nine-and-twenty with a good reference from the legation in Lisbon and a conviction that the posting, though modest, would lead to better things. Better things had not materialised. What had materialised was a routine so finely worn that it had acquired the texture of a second skin. He wrote reports. He filed them. He sent them to London in the monthly diplomatic bag, aboard whatever steamer happened to be calling. He kept a copy of each in the filing cabinet, and the filing cabinet was now nearly full.

The reports themselves were models of their kind. He had perfected a style of bureaucratic observation that revealed nothing while appearing to reveal a great deal. "The political situation remains fluid," he would write, or "the Republic continues to experience a period of adjustment." These phrases had served him through seven changes of government, three attempted coups, one brief civil war, and an epidemic of yellow fever that had killed a quarter of the town. He wrote about the export of cinchona bark and coffee, about the activities of the German trading houses, about the condition of the roads leading to the interior mines. He recorded the names of ministers and the shifting allegiances of the provincial garrison commanders. He described the weather, the harbour improvements, the occasional eruption of the volcano that brooded over the town like a dissatisfied uncle. He did all of this with care, with precision, with the quiet satisfaction of a man performing a task he has come to believe in simply because it is his.

He had never once received a reply that engaged with the substance of a report. London acknowledged receipt. Occasionally a clerk would forward a request for clarification on some statistical matter, and Arthur would supply the clarification, and the matter would end there. He had stopped expecting anything else years ago.

Now a warship was coming to protect British interests, and Arthur Pendelton, standing at his window and looking down at the harbour where the fishing boats rocked in the afternoon swell, realised that he could not say with any certainty what those interests were.

There had been British interests once, clearly enough. The Costaverde Mining Company had operated silver workings in the highlands, staffed by Cornish engineers and protected by a private security force of dubious legality. The company's offices had occupied the best building on the Plaza de Armas, and its manager, a red-faced man from Truro named Holloway, had been a fixture at the consul's table. But the company had folded in 1882, when the veins ran thin and the political situation made further investment unwise. Holloway had gone home. The offices were now a pharmacy.

There had been British railway interests too. A consortium of investors in Manchester had funded a line from San Cristobal to the mining town of Alta Gracia, and for a time Arthur had served as an informal intermediary between the consortium and the Republic's ministry of public works. The railway had been built, then nationalised, then poorly maintained, then partially washed away by a flood. The consortium had dissolved. Arthur had written a long and detailed report on the whole affair, which London had acknowledged with its customary brevity.

There were British subjects, of course. A handful. Archibald Poole, who ran a shipping agency and had married a Costaverdan woman and raised six children who spoke no English. The Misses Cavendish, two elderly sisters who kept a school for girls and had not set foot outside San Cristobal since 1879. William Trant, an engineer who drank and repaired machinery for the municipal water board. These were the British interests in the sense that they were British, and they had interests, and Arthur had a duty of care toward them. But he could not imagine that the Admiralty had dispatched a warship for the sake of Archibald Poole's shipping agency.

He moved away from the window and sat down again at his desk. He opened the filing cabinet and began to read through his old reports, looking for something he might have missed, some interest he had recorded and then forgotten. The reports were there, neatly bound, year by year. He read the first page of the report for March 1881. He read the first page of the report for November 1885. He read the first page of three more, and in each case found the same phrases, the same careful observations, the same fluid political situation, the same period of adjustment.

He closed the cabinet and sat in the stillness of the office. The ceiling fan turned slowly, its rhythm irregular. From the street below came the sound of a cart and a vendor calling out the price of mangoes. The gin in his glass had gone warm.

The truth, Arthur thought, was that he had become Costaverdan without ever intending to. He knew the town the way one knows a body one has lived in for a long time. He knew which streets flooded in the rains, which officials could be trusted, which priests drank. He knew the smell of the market at dawn and the particular quality of the light on the cathedral wall in the late afternoon. He had watched children grow up and marry and have children of their own. He had buried the British dead and attended the weddings of the Costaverdan living, and the distinction between the two categories had come to seem, if not meaningless, then at least less significant than it had once been.

He thought about the warship. It would arrive in the harbour with its guns and its white ensign and its crew of disciplined young men who would come ashore and look at the town with the frank curiosity of people who had no stake in it. The captain would call on him, and they would sit in this office, and the captain would ask him about the British interests, and Arthur would have to explain them. He would have to give an account of something he no longer understood.

He picked up the telegram and read it once more. PROTECT BRITISH INTERESTS. The words had the confidence of a dispatch written in London, in a room with mahogany furniture and a view of a park, by someone who had never stood on the Plaza de Armas at dusk and watched the lamplighter go about his work while the old women sat on their stoops and the children chased each other through the gathering dark.

Arthur finished his gin. He took a sheet of consulate letterhead from the drawer and dipped his pen. He would write a report. He would describe the situation as he saw it, which was to say he would describe it as he had always described it, in terms that were careful and precise and that revealed nothing while appearing to reveal a great deal. He would note the dispatch of the warship and express his readiness to assist in whatever measures the Admiralty deemed appropriate. He would file the report, and he would send a copy to London, and he would place another copy in the cabinet, and the cabinet would grow fuller, and the years would continue their accumulation, and the warship would come and then it would go, and the British interests would remain, as they had always remained, undefined.

He began to write. The ceiling fan turned. The afternoon light shifted on the wall. Somewhere in the harbour a bell rang, and the sound carried up through the open window and hung in the air like a question that no one had thought to ask.
