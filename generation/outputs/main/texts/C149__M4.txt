Theodora Mapplethorpe arrived at the survey camp above the village of San Jerónimo de las Cumbres on a Tuesday, and by Friday she had already learned that theodolites and moral philosophy made poor bedfellows. The camp consisted of four canvas tents, a corral of mules, and a cook named Baltasar who spoke to the pots as though they were difficult children. Below them the village clung to the mountainside like a lichen, its red-tiled roofs the colour of dried blood. Above them the passes went up into cloud, and somewhere in that cloud sat General Aurelio Vásquez, who held the only road worth holding.

She had come from Sheffield with a crate of instruments, a commission from the Ministry of Public Works of the Republic of Valdoria, and a conviction—inherited from her father, who had built bridges in India—that iron rails were the surest civilising force known to man. The Ministry paid her in drafts on a London bank and in promises. The promises were more plentiful.

Don Ignacio Ferrer, the Minister’s man in the district, came up to the camp on the Friday with a bottle of aguardiente and a smile that never reached his eyes. He wore a white linen suit and kid gloves in the dust, and he spoke of the railway as a god might speak of a temple.

“You will see, señorita,” he said, spreading his hands over the valley, “when the locomotive comes, the bandits will go. The priests will have no more hold on the people. Schools, telegraphs, commerce—all of it follows the iron. The President himself has said that the railway is the spine of the nation.”

“And General Vásquez?” she asked.

Ferrer’s smile thinned. “The General is a patriot who has not yet understood that the nation has outgrown him. He controls the passes. We control the coast. Between us, the railway will be built. You have only to survey the line the Ministry has approved—the eastern approach, through the Cuchilla Negra.”

“That line crosses the General’s country.”

“It crosses Valdorian country,” Ferrer said, and poured her a glass.

She drank it because the nights were cold and because she was thirty-one years old and had crossed an ocean to build a railway, and she was not going to be frightened by a man in kid gloves. But she noted the way he had said the General’s name, as one notes a cracked sleeper under a rail.

The General came himself on the following Wednesday, at the head of forty horsemen, and he did not send word ahead. She was at the theodolite on the ridge above the camp, taking a back-sight, when the sound of hooves came up the trail and she turned to find a broad-shouldered man in a faded blue military coat watching her with frank curiosity. His beard was grey and trimmed close, and his eyes were the colour of the slate she had surveyed in Wales.

“You are the English engineer,” he said. It was not a question.

“I am the engineer,” she said. “The English part is incidental.”

He laughed—a short, surprised bark—and dismounted. His men spread out along the ridge and sat their horses with the ease of men who had been born in the saddle. He introduced himself as though she might not have heard of him, which she thought was either modesty or a kind of theatre.

“You are surveying the Cuchilla Negra line,” he said.

“I am surveying the line the Ministry has instructed me to survey.”

“And if I instruct you to survey a different line?”

“You are not my employer, General.”

“No,” he agreed. “I am merely the man who controls whether your mules eat, whether your men are shot at, and whether the Ministry’s drafts are worth the paper they are printed on. The Ministry has not paid its soldiers in eleven months. Did you know that? The President builds railways in his imagination while his army goes barefoot. I pay my men in cattle and in promises, and they follow me because the promises are kept.”

He walked with her along the ridge, his boots crunching on the scree, and he spoke of the railway as Ferrer had spoken of it—as a civilising force, a spine, a future. But his version had a different grammar. The railway, he said, would carry grain from the high valleys to the coast, and the high valleys were his, and the grain was grown by men who had never seen a school or a telegraph and who would not thank a government in the capital for either. “Civilisation,” he said, “is not a thing you send down a wire from the coast. It is a thing you build where you stand. The railway will build it. But it must be our railway, señorita, or it will be only another rope around our necks.”

She said, because she was an engineer and not a diplomat, “A railway is a railway. The gradient does not care who owns the pass.”

“Everything cares who owns the pass,” he said.

That night she wrote in her notebook: *V. is intelligent, which is worse than honest. He believes what he says. F. believes nothing. I am to build a line that both of them will claim, and neither will forgive me for.*

The trouble came in the third week. Ferrer rode up with a company of government infantry—fifty men in patched uniforms, their rifles oiled but their boots wrapped in rags—and informed her that the Ministry required the survey to be completed within the month, and that a detachment would accompany her party for protection. The detachment’s lieutenant, a boy of nineteen named Cifuentes, had a face like a worried egg and a copy of the Ministry’s map with the Cuchilla Negra line drawn on it in red ink.

Two days later, Vásquez’s men stopped the survey party at the ford of the Río Turbio and informed her, with great courtesy, that the General regretted that no survey party could pass the ford until the question of the passes was settled, and that the survey party’s mules would be cared for in the General’s corral until such time as the question was settled. Lieutenant Cifuentes put his hand on his pistol. The General’s men did not put their hands on anything. They simply sat their horses in the river and waited, and the water went past them brown and loud, and Mapplethorpe understood that she was watching two versions of the future negotiate over her instruments.

She went to see the General that evening, alone, against the advice of everyone. His headquarters was a farmhouse at the head of the valley, and he received her in a room that smelled of leather and maize and gun oil. On the wall behind him was a map of Valdoria with the railway drawn on it in charcoal—not the Ministry’s line, but another, swinging west through the high valleys, through villages she had not yet surveyed.

“You have drawn a railway,” she said.

“I have drawn a hope,” he said. “You have drawn a line on a map that will never be built, because the Ministry will run out of money before it reaches the mountains, and then the President will blame the General, and the General will be shot, and the railway will be a story that old men tell. Unless.”

“Unless.”

“Unless the engineer who draws the line draws it where it can be built. Where the people who will use it live. Where the grades are kind and the passes are held by men who will keep them open.” He tapped the charcoal line. “This is the line, señorita. You know it is. You have walked the ground. The Ministry’s line is a politician’s line—it goes through the Cuchilla Negra because the President’s brother-in-law owns land there. Mine goes through the valleys because the valleys are where the grain is.”

She looked at the map for a long time. She thought of Ferrer’s kid gloves and the President’s brother-in-law and the lieutenant’s worried egg of a face. She thought of her father in India, who had built bridges for a government that had never once asked the people who crossed them what they wanted. She thought of the theodolite on the ridge, and how it did not care who owned the pass, and how that was both its virtue and its uselessness.

“I am an engineer,” she said at last. “I survey the ground. I do not choose the ground.”

“Then you will be a very fine engineer,” said Vásquez, “and a very poor railway.”

She went back to the camp in the dark, and in the morning she sent a letter to Ferrer stating that the Cuchilla Negra line could not be surveyed within the month owing to the condition of the fords, and a letter to the General stating that the survey party would require its mules returned if the Ministry’s line was to be abandoned, and then she sat down with her notebooks and began, very slowly, to survey a third line—one that ran through the high valleys but kept the Ministry’s eastern approach for the last thirty miles, so that each man could see his own future in it and neither would see the other’s.

Baltasar brought her coffee and said, “The pots are difficult today, señorita.”

“So are the passes,” she said.

She worked until the candle guttered. Outside, the mountains stood in the dark like patient creditors, and somewhere above the cloud the General’s horsemen rode the ridgelines, and somewhere below the village the Ministry’s soldiers slept in their rags, and between them, in a tent that smelled of kerosene and wet canvas, an English engineer drew a line that neither of them had asked for, and hoped it would hold.
