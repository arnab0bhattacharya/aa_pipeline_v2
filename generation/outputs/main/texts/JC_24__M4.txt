The affair began, as such affairs will, in a manner so slight as to seem scarcely worthy of record—a mere feverish flush at evening, a hand pressed for a moment against the mahogany side of the cabin table, and the captain’s voice, ordinarily so brisk and final in its judgments, trailing off into a vague murmur about the heat and the need for a little rest. I confess I thought nothing of it at the time. We were nine days out of the Plate, standing to the nor’east with a fair wind, and the old *Themistocles* was running before it with that easy, long-drawn swish of waters which is the very music of a fortunate voyage. Captain Wainwright had been complaining of the heat, as indeed we all had, for the South Atlantic in those latitudes will press upon a man like the hand of an invisible oppressor, and the pitch would bubble in the deck seams and the tar soften and run down the bulwarks in slow black tears. But there was more in it than heat, though none of us guessed it then.

By the third day he was raving. The doctor—a mild, grey-haired man named Ffoulkes, who had come aboard at Liverpool with his chest of medicines and his air of mild bewilderment—pronounced it a fever of the country, some malarial taint contracted perhaps in the low quarters of Buenos Aires, and prescribed quinine and quiet. The quiet was easy enough to secure in that vast and empty sea; the quinine was another matter, for the captain would not have it, and swore at the steward in a voice that carried clear through the skylight, so that the watch on deck paused in their hauling and exchanged glances. It was I, as first mate, who took the responsibility—I, Elias Gannet, who had never in my life disobeyed an order or questioned a superior, and who now found myself standing in the dim cabin with the medicine glass in my hand and the old man’s eyes, bright and hating, fixed upon me from the bunk.

“You’ll take it, sir,” I said, “or I’ll have the doctor hold your nose.”

He laughed at that—a harsh, dry cackle that turned into a cough—and took it, and after that he sank into a stupor that lasted four days. And in those four days the *Themistocles* ran on, and I ran her, and I discovered—I, who had never thought myself more than a tolerable seaman—that the ship answered my hand as she had never answered his. It is a strange thing to say, and I set it down with all the hesitation which the admission merits, but it is true: the old man had been driving her too hard, carrying on too long, pressing her when she would have been better eased. I found the weather helm easing under my touch, the yards trimming sweeter, the whole fabric of her groaning less in the night watches. The men, too—I noticed it without wishing to—the men obeyed me with a different alacrity, a quicker comprehension, as though some knot had been loosened in the rigging of their spirits.

On the fifth day the captain sat up. He was weak, yellow as a guinea, his hands trembling, but his eyes had come back to themselves—those hard, pale eyes that had looked upon ice and fire and the faces of mutinous crews and never flinched. He asked for the log. I brought it. He read it through in silence, his lips moving, and when he had finished he looked at me over the top of it with an expression I could not fathom.

“You’ve been carrying on,” he said. “Royal sails, stu’nsails—by God, Mr. Gannet, you’ve been flying kites.”

“The wind was fair, sir, and the ship light.”

“The ship light,” he repeated. “Ay, she’s light. And you’ve been making her walk, haven’t you? Making her walk.” He put the log down and leaned back, and for a moment his face was old and tired and something else besides—something I had never seen there before, and which I can only call a sort of wondering bitterness. “I’ll be on deck tomorrow,” he said.

But he was not on deck tomorrow, nor the next day, nor the next. The fever had left him weak as a child, and the doctor would not hear of his rising. And all that time I held the ship, and the fair wind held, and we ran on toward the Line with every stitch drawing and the wake humming behind us like a plucked string. I was happy—I say it plainly, I was happy—in a way I had never been happy before. It was not the pride of command, though that was sweet enough; it was the ship herself, the sense of her as a living thing that had come to trust me, that answered my least touch with a willing grace. I would stand at the binnacle in the dog watches, when the stars were thick as white blossoms in the black sky, and feel her lift and settle under my feet, and think that this was what the sea had meant all along, and that I had only now begun to understand it.

On the eighth day Captain Wainwright sent for me. He was dressed—that is to say, he had his coat on over his nightshirt—and he was sitting in the chair by the table with a chart spread before him and a pencil in his hand. He looked up as I entered, and his face was composed, almost genial, and that frightened me more than his anger would have done.

“Mr. Gannet,” he said, “I’ve been thinking. You’ve done well. The owners will hear of it. But I’ll take her now.”

I stood there, and the words would not come. The cabin was very still. I could hear the water hissing past the counter, and the faint creak of the rudder-head, and the distant voice of the lookout in the bows, and it seemed to me that all these sounds were the voice of the ship herself, calling to me, telling me not to let her go.

“Sir,” I said at last, “you’re not fit. Another week—”

“Another week be damned,” he said, without heat. “I’m master of this ship, Mr. Gannet, fit or unfit. I’ll take her now.”

And then I said the thing that I had not known was in me. I said it quietly, and I looked him in the face, and I felt as though I were watching another man speak.

“You’ll take her to the bottom, sir. You’ll drive her till she splits her seams. You did it off the Horn, and you’ll do it again. I’ve had her eight days, and she’s never been so sweet. You know it. You’ve seen the log.”

He did not move. His hand lay on the chart, and the pencil snapped in his fingers, and the two pieces fell on the paper with a little click. He looked at me for a long time, and his face was neither angry nor surprised, but only very old and very still, as though he had been waiting for this moment for years and had always known it would come.

“You damned mutineer,” he said softly. “You damned, damned mutineer.”

I said nothing. There was nothing to say. The ship ran on, and the sun sank, and the shadows lengthened across the cabin floor, and still we stood there, the two of us, in the presence of a thing that neither of us could name.
