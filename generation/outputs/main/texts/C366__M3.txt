The mist off the canals of Brulle never quite lifted in November; it merely thinned to a yellowish haze that crept through the iron windowbars and settled on the floorstones of the Sint-Catharinainstituut. In the late autumn of 1842, I had been engaged to instruct the upper forms in English composition, and it was in that capacity that I first received a summons from the office of Mevrouw Verhaegen, the director. She sat behind an oak desk that seemed to grow out of the very foundations of the old convent building, her hands folded with a dry, mechanical precision. Beside her, in a chair too large for her, sat her daughter Aleida—a girl of fifteen, pale, neat, and observant, whose eyes moved over me with the unhurried attention of someone cataloguing a specimen. From that day forward, I was to exercise a special supervision over Aleida’s English studies and, as Mevrouw Verhaegen phrased it, over her moral formation. The girl’s own form-mistress had requested transfer, and the two before her had resigned their posts entirely. Mevrouw Verhaegen did not say this outright, but I learned it from the other teachers within the hour.

I understood the arrangement for what it was. Aleida was clever, fluent in three languages, and possessed of a memory that retained every word spoken in her presence and every word omitted. She had long since discovered that the ordinary machinery of school discipline could be turned against those who operated it, provided one had access to the director’s ear. Reports reached me of small cruelties—a mistress accused of drunkenness because she had smelled of the sacramental wine used in the chapel; a kitchen woman dismissed for theft after Aleida planted a silver spoon among her effects—each accusation calibrated to be just plausible enough to demand investigation. What frightened me was not the girl’s malice, which was real enough, but its calmness. She did not act from rage or frustration. She acted as a naturalist conducts experiments, to see what would happen, and because the results confirmed what she already believed about the authority of adults.

My first lesson with her alone confirmed the nature of the contest. I had set a passage from Addison to translate into French and back again, a tedious but useful exercise. Aleida completed it in a quarter of the allotted hour, flawlessly, and then sat watching me with an expression of courteous expectation. I reviewed the work, set it aside, and produced a second passage, this time from Bacon. She read it, smiled, and said in English—her accent was nearly flawless—that the essayist’s prose was coarse and unchristian, and that her mother would be interested to know I considered such material suitable for a young woman’s instruction. I replied, in the same language, that Bacon was unsuitable for precisely no one, that the passage concerned the fallibility of human reason, and that if she found the sentiment unchristian she was welcome to say so in a written refutation, which I would read and grade on its merits. She wrote three pages. The argument was specious but the English was impeccable. I marked a grammatical error in the second paragraph—a genuine one—and returned the paper without comment on the theology. She looked at the red mark for a long moment, and I saw something shift behind her eyes. It was not anger. It was the recalculation of a chess player who has found an unexpected defence.

Over the following weeks, she tested every boundary I set, not with open defiance but with a patient ingenuity that would have impressed me under other circumstances. She arrived precisely one minute late to each lesson, never enough to warrant a formal report but enough to establish that she could. She asked questions designed to draw me into theological or political territory where any answer I gave might be construed as improper. She contradicted my translations of English idioms with such confidence and fluency that a less careful teacher might have yielded out of sheer uncertainty. I did not yield. I also did not punish. When she was late, I simply began the lesson without her and refused to repeat what had been covered, so that the cost of her lateness fell on her alone. When she attempted to draw me into controversy, I referred her to the written assignment, which by then she had come to dread, not because the work was hard but because my corrections exposed the gap between her cleverness and her actual knowledge—a gap she could not abide having named.

The critical test came in January. I had assigned the class a theme on the subject of duty to one’s parents, a topic chosen by Mevrouw Verhaegen herself for the monthly composition. Aleida produced a paper of extraordinary elegance, arguing with devastating logic that filial duty was founded not on affection, which was variable and unreliable, but on the practical recognition that parents held power and that obedience was therefore a matter of prudence rather than virtue. The composition was technically perfect and morally poisonous. I understood at once what she had done. If I praised it, I endorsed the sentiment; if I condemned it, I could be reported to her mother for punishing a girl whose only offence was intellectual honesty. I read the paper twice, then sat for a long time considering my course. In the end, I marked it as I would have marked any other: I corrected two minor errors of phrasing, awarded full marks for grammar and structure, and beneath the grade wrote a single marginal note—“The argument is soundly constructed. Is it true? Defend or refute in next week’s theme, using only examples from your own experience.”

She stared at the note for a long time after I returned the paper. Her expression was unreadable. That evening, I received a visit from Mevrouw Verhaegen, who told me, with the careful neutrality of someone selecting her words, that her daughter had reported I found her composition lacking in moral seriousness. I said that I had found it entirely serious, that I had given it full marks, and that I had asked Aleida to test her own argument against her own life, which was the highest compliment one could pay a young scholar. I offered to show the paper. Mevrouw Verhaegen declined to look at it. She sat for a moment, her hands folded in that mechanical way, and then said that Aleida had also reported that I corrected her English in front of the other girls. I said that I corrected every girl’s English in front of the other girls, as was the custom of the school, and that Aleida’s English, while superior to most, was not yet beyond correction. Mevrouw Verhaegen said nothing further. She rose, inclined her head, and left.

The following week, Aleida submitted her second theme. It was a careful, almost reluctant acknowledgment that the example of her own mother’s conduct suggested that affection could, in some cases, sustain duty beyond what prudence alone would require. The argument was hedged with qualifications and quietly furious in its restraint. I corrected the grammar, which this time contained four errors—she had been careless, or agitated—and wrote beneath the grade, “Better. You have discovered that an argument which cannot survive contact with one’s own experience is not an argument but a performance.” She read the note, folded the paper, and placed it in her satchel without expression. But the next morning, when she arrived for our lesson, she was on time. And the morning after that, she brought me a passage she had translated on her own initiative—a piece of Burke, which she said she had found in her father’s library—and asked me, with an air of studied indifference, whether I thought the translation adequate. It was not adequate. It was, however, the first honest piece of work she had done for me, and I told her so, and we spent the hour on it, and neither of us mentioned duty, or her mother, or the notes in the margins, and the canal mist outside the window turned the colour of pale ivory, and the lesson ended, and the next one began.
