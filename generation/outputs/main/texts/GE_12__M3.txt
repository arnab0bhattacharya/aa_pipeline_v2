It is a common error in the arithmetic of human motives to suppose that a young woman’s obstinacy is but the inverse of her vanity. Mrs Clara Linwood, left a widow at three-and-twenty with a settlement that yielded a clear eight hundred a year, was universally held by her acquaintance in the provincial town of Duxborough to be a person requiring guidance; and because she met this benevolent assumption with a quiet but inflexible resistance, she was presently set down as headstrong. She had retreated, after her husband’s sudden demise, to Leamington, drawn thither by the mild air and the hope of escaping the watchful eyes that had catalogued her grief and measured her crape with such unimpeachable exactitude. But a watering-place, however restorative its chalybeate springs, is no sanctuary from the intricate machinery of social scrutiny; it merely exchanges one set of observers for another, and in Mrs Linwood’s case, the observers were armed with a more refined dialectic.

Captain Hubert Faversham possessed precisely that complexion of manner which is least resistible to a woman conscious of being judged: an appearance of complete surrender to her judgment. He was a man of six-and-thirty, with the easy carriage of one who has spent his best years in the cavalry without acquiring any very distinct habits of industry. His complexion was bronzed, his address impeccable, and his finances, had they been subjected to the same rigorous inspection as his buttons and his boot-tops, would have been found to present a painful contrast to his exterior. He had drifted to Leamington with a small encumbrance of debt, a larger one of expectations, and that charming versatility of attention which, in the minds of sensible matrons, constitutes the most alarming symptom of a bachelor’s moral constitution.

Mrs Perrott, a widow of substantial fortune and unimpeachable acumen, who had established herself at Leamington for the season, lost no time in communicating her suspicions to Clara. She was a woman who had survived a singularly undemonstrative husband and three awkward sons, and she regarded the male sex with a benevolent but profoundly analytic skepticism.

"My dear Clara," she had said, fixing her sharp grey eyes on the younger woman’s face over the rim of her teacup, "I speak to you as one who has no personal interest in the matter whatsoever, beyond the general desire to prevent a foolish catastrophe. Captain Faversham is a very agreeable man, and I dare say his horsemanship is everything it should be; but I have observed that he is exceedingly particular in his attentions to women who happen to possess settlements, and remarkably remiss in his attentions to those who do not. You may think me cynical, but cynicism is merely experience arranged in epigrams."

Clara had received this warning with a composure that Mrs Perrott found exasperating. She was not blind to Captain Faversham’s charm—she was, indeed, more susceptible to it than she chose to betray—but she resented, with a vehemence she did not fully acknowledge even to herself, the implication that her perception was inferior to Mrs Perrott’s. The suspicion that she was being managed awoke in her that dormant perversity which is the shadow of a generous nature. She would not be dictated to; she would not be treated as a cipher in a calculation she had not author’d. And so, when Captain Faversham proposed to her on a damp Tuesday afternoon in the crescent walk, with an eloquence that had the merit of appearing entirely unstudied, she accepted him with a deliberateness that was, in its own way, as calculated as anything in his own address.

The marriage took place before the Leamington season was quite spent, and the couple removed to London, where Captain Faversham’s tastes found a more congenial field of exercise. The house in Pont Street, selected with a regard to Mrs Linwood’s fortune rather than her preferences, was furnished with a lavishness that startled Clara’s provincial habits, though she said nothing. She had made her election, and she was not a woman to retreat from a position she had deliberately occupied. Her pride, which was of that quiet sort which suffers most invisibly, sustained her through the first weeks of astonished adjustment.

It was not any single act of extravagance or unkindness that awakened her to the precise nature of her situation, but the slow accumulation of evidences, each trivial in itself, but forming in the aggregate a pattern she could no longer refuse to read. Captain Faversham was never brutal; he was merely, by degrees, impersonal. He had attained his object with the same easy competence with which he managed a difficult horse, and having attained it, he regarded it with the same tranquil indifference. His attentions, which had been so delicately adjusted to her susceptibilities during courtship, were now distributed among a wider constituency: the club, the racecourse, certain assemblies where Clara’s presence was not desired. The bills came in with a regularity that was the only regular thing about him; and Clara, examining them with the careful intelligence she had learned in her first widowhood, perceived that the sum total represented not so much a multiplication of comforts as a dispersion of resources.

She wrote to Mrs Perrott, but not to acknowledge her error. She wrote concerning the weather in London, the opera, the new bonnets; and Mrs Perrott, reading between the lines with the practiced eye of one who has herself composed such letters, perceived that Clara was suffering, and said nothing.

It was on a bleak evening in the following March, when the London fog pressed against the windows of the Pont Street drawing-room with a thick, yellow persistence, that Clara sat alone and confronted the question she had so long evaded. Captain Faversham had gone to Brighton for the races; he had mentioned the necessity of the journey with an air of careless apology that left no room for remonstrance. She held in her hand a letter from Mrs Perrott, the last of a series that had grown, by imperceptible degrees, more tender and less admonitory. "You know, my dear," it said, "that my house is always open to you."

She read the sentence twice, and her lips tightened. It was not the prospect of her friends’ triumph that distressed her—for she knew Mrs Perrott well enough to be sure that no triumph would be visible—but the admission to herself that she had been wrong, and that her wrongness had been of that particular kind which is the fruit of pride rather than of passion. She had not loved Captain Faversham blindly; she had married him in a flush of self-assertion, to prove that her judgment was her own. And now she must live with the consequences of that proof, or lay down her pride and acknowledge that the very independence she had championed had been its own species of bondage.

She rose and went to the window, pressing her fingers against the cold glass. The gas-lamps in the street below were dimmed to faint orange smears. Somewhere in the vast, indifferent city, her husband was pursuing pleasures that did not include her, funded by the fortune she had brought him. And in Duxborough, and in Leamington, and in Mrs Perrott’s drawing-room, the women who had warned her were going about their business with the quiet satisfaction of prophecy fulfilled.

Clara turned from the window. She would not write tonight; she would not decide tonight. But she knew, with the slow, sure knowledge that comes to those who have long refused to know, that the letter she would eventually write to Mrs Perrott would contain no mention of the weather, the opera, or the bonnets—and that in the writing of it, she would for the first time be entirely honest with herself.
