If any hopeful youth of tender years, standing upon the perilous threshold of life, were desirous of a perfect and comprehensive pattern of human misery, let him repair to the Borough of Muddleford-in-the-Marsh, in the county of Kent. Let him seek out a certain crooked lane, where the very houses, with their crushed and melancholy gables, appeared to be suffering from a chronic rheumatism, and where a damp and mouldering atmosphere hung about the eaves as though it had taken a long lease of the premises and meant to keep it. In this lane, overshadowed by a precipitous and crazy stack of chimneys which seemed to threaten annual destruction to the passers-by, stood the shop of Mr. Simeon Slumber.

Mr. Slumber was a maker of coffins; and such a maker of coffins! So thoroughly and entirely did he enter into the spirit of his calling, that he seemed to have been born in a mahogany shell, and to have been weaned, so to speak, upon shavings. He was a gaunt, pale-cheeked man, with a countenance of such profound and settled melancholy, that he appeared to be perpetually attending the funeral of his own spirits. He measured out life in inches of elm and oak, and calculated the duration of human existence by the varying thickness of his black cloth. He was a man who took a grim professional pride in the exact fit of his work, and would regard a plump, healthy citizen with the secret and sorrowful satisfaction of knowing that, sooner or later, that citizen’s measurements would be wanted.

In this establishment, and beneath the shadow of this cheerful master, dwelt his apprentice, young Oliver Pipkin. Oliver was a youth of an imaginative and inflammable disposition, possessing a bosom that beat high with the desire to be—what? A tragedian! A roarer! A player! He longed to tread the boards, to wear the sock, to utter sentiments of burning passion, and to die nightly amid the thunders of an applauding gallery. He was, however, condemned to tread the workshop, to wear the apron, to utter no sentiments beyond the price of nails, and to die to no applause, save the creaking of Mr. Slumber’s saw.

“Ah, Cato!” Oliver would exclaim to himself, as he polished the elbow of a finished elm, “how long shall this degradation last? How long shall the divine fire within me be quenched by the cold water of this trade?”

But the divine fire was quenched all day, and Oliver went on planing and sandpapering, until his soul was as rough as the boards he handled.

Now it came to pass, in the autumn of the year one thousand eight hundred and thirty-something, that a travelling theatrical company, led by the renowned Mr. Bellamy Blood, arrived in Muddleford-in-the-Marsh. This event was as a thunderclap to the stagnant society of the town. A booth was erected, a drum was beaten, and Mr. Blood, with a crimson scarf and a voice like a rusty trumpet, proclaimed from the steps of the Market Cross that he and his company would present that very night the soul-stirring tragedy of “The Bloody Baron; or, The Spectre of the Silver Mine.”

Oliver Pipkin, stealing an hour from his elm, stood open-mouthed before the manager. He gazed upon Mr. Blood as a devotee gazes upon a shrine. He drank in every word. He was entranced; he was bewitched. And, lo! as he stood there, Mr. Blood, who was short of a man to carry a halberd and say “My lord, the carriage waits,” cast his managerial eye upon him.

“Youth,” said Mr. Blood, “you have a frame. You have a presence. You have a leg. Would you tread the path of glory?”

“I would, sir,” replied Oliver, trembling. “I would tread it till I dropped.”

“Then,” said Mr. Blood, with the magnificent liberality of his profession, “you shall be the Baron’s halberdier. Be at the booth at seven. Your name shall be writ in the bill, if there is room.”

The boy’s heart leaped. He rushed back to the shop, regardless of Mr. Slumber’s scowl, and burst into the parlour, where sat his mother, the widow Pipkin.

His mother was a woman of a weeping and tremulous disposition. She wept at morning, at noon, and at eventide; she wept when the sun shone, and she wept when it rained; she had a tear ready for every occasion, and a sigh for every interval. She was now engaged in stitching a piece of black silk, and her eyes were red.

“Mother!” cried Oliver. “I am engaged! I am to be a player! Mr. Blood himself has engaged me. I am the Baron’s halberdier!”

The widow dropped her work, and raised her eyes and her voice together.

“Oh, Oliver, Oliver!” she sobbed. “And is it for this I have nursed you? Is it for this I have brought you up? To be a play-actor? To be a creature of paint and tinsel? Oh, that I should live to see my only son a harlequin! Oh, my poor head! Oh, my poor heart!” And she wept so copiously that the black silk was in danger of being entirely dissolved.

“Nonsense, mother!” cried Oliver, with the heroic fire of a youthful Roscius. “It is not harlequin. It is high tragedy. I am to say, ‘My lord, the carriage waits.’ And I am to carry a halberd!”

“Oh, the halberd!” sobbed the widow. “Oh, that I should hear of a halberd in connection with my child! Oh, Oliver!”

At this moment, Mr. Simeon Slumber stalked into the parlour. He had heard the concluding words, and his gaunt frame seemed to elongate itself with indignation.

“A halberd?” said Mr. Slumber, in his deep, dismal tones. “A halberd! And what, pray, is a halberd to a screw? What is a stage to a workshop? What is a Baron to a respectable undertaker? I tell you, boy, that you are an apprentice, bound by indentures, and that you shall not go. The only stage you shall tread is the stage of a hearse. The only carriage you shall wait for is the carriage of the dead. I forbid it. I put my foot upon it.” And he brought his foot down upon the floor with a hollow sound, as of a muffled drum.

Oliver was in despair. He looked at his master, and he saw determination in the very seams of his black coat. He looked at his mother, and he saw a fountain of tears that showed no sign of ceasing. He was in the very depth of gloom, when he bethought him of one who might, perchance, be his advocate.

This was Miss Priscilla Prim, the daughter of the town milliner. Miss Priscilla was a young person of considerable personal attractions, and of a still more considerable opinion of them. She wore her bonnets with an air of superiority, and her ribbons were always in the newest fashion of Muddleford-in-the-Marsh. She was the cynosure of Oliver’s eyes, the goddess of his idolatry.

To her he hastened, and poured out his tale of woe and of ambition.

“And so,” he concluded, “I am to be the Baron’s halberdier, if I can but escape my master; and I shall be a great actor yet, Priscilla. You shall see me strut and fret my hour upon the stage.”

But Miss Priscilla did not melt. She did not even smile. She adjusted a ribbon with a provoking air of composure, and said:

“A halberdier, indeed! And pray, Mr. Pipkin, what is a halberdier? A man in a tin cap, I suppose, with a long stick, looking like a fool. You an actor, Oliver! You, who have been measuring coffins all your life! You would be ridiculous. You would be a laughing-stock. You would be hissed. You had better stick to your shavings, and leave the stage to them that are fit for it. You ridiculous boy!”

And with that, she turned on her heel, and left him to his reflections.

Oliver stood alone. The world was against him. His master forbade him; his mother wept over him; the girl he admired derided him. The theatrical fire was damped; the halberd was broken; the Baron was left without his carriage. He went slowly back to the shop, and took up his plane, and planed away in silence, while Mr. Slumber watched him with a grim smile, and the shavings fell about his feet like the vain aspirations of his youth.

And so the travelling company departed, and Mr. Blood’s drum was heard no more in Muddleford-in-the-Marsh, and Oliver Pipkin remained an apprentice to the coffin-maker. But, to the end of his days, whenever he heard a trumpet, or saw a piece of crimson cloth, or read of a Baron, he would heave a sigh, and think of the lost halberd, and of the carriage that never waited for him. And Miss Priscilla Prim, having married a respectable grocer, lived to declare, in her old age, that she had always known Oliver Pipkin would come to no good, and that he was, and always had been, a most ridiculous boy.
