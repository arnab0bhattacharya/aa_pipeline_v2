The letter lay on Mr. Edward Ashby’s desk like a white tombstone. It was not a letter, precisely, but a memorandum, a single sheet of foolscap covered in the tight, forward-slanting hand of the director, Monsieur Delacroix. The words themselves were a model of administrative clarity, but their cumulative effect was that of a bludgeon. The position of Head of English Studies, a post that would double his salary and grant him a small apartment overlooking the courtyard, was his. It was his, that is, upon the satisfactory conclusion of a certain personal matter. The matter was Mademoiselle Claire Delacroix, the director’s niece.

Ashby let his gaze drift from the page to the window of his small, rented room. The rain, a fine, persistent drizzle that was the city of Verviers’s default weather, streaked the glass, blurring the grey slate roofs and the spire of St. Catherine’s into a watercolour smudge. He had come to this Belgian city three years ago, a young man of twenty-four with a Cambridge degree, a romantic notion of European culture, and a rapidly diminishing purse. The position at the Institut Delacroix had been a godsend. It was a respectable school for the sons of the prospering bourgeoisie, and he had found a certain satisfaction in instilling the rhythms of Pope and the declamations of Shakespeare into the sons of wool merchants and ironmongers.

He was a good teacher. He knew this with the quiet certainty of one who has found his calling. He could make the boys feel the thrum of iambic pentameter, could trace the lineage of a tragic hero from Sophocles to Racine. His classes were orderly, his students respectful, his colleagues… well, his colleagues were a mixed lot. There was the sour-faced Monsieur le Brun, who taught mathematics and resented Ashby’s popularity; the pious Madame Petit, who instructed the younger boys in catechism and regarded all Englishmen as potential heretics; and then there was Elodie.

Elodie Vautier. The thought of her name was a soft, painful pressure behind his ribs. She was not a master, but a teacher of the youngest class, the *petits*, and she moved through the school’s dusty corridors with a quiet grace that seemed to belong to another, gentler world. She was the daughter of a failed clockmaker, with no dowry, no connections, and a wardrobe of dresses that had been turned and re-turned so many times the fabric was worn thin at the elbows. She had no prospects but her own small salary, which she supplemented by giving private lessons in pianoforte to the daughters of the town’s more ambitious families.

Ashby had first noticed her in the refectory, helping a small, sobbing boy who had spilled his soup. She had not scolded, but had simply knelt, her voice a low, soothing murmur, and wiped his hands and his face with her own handkerchief. There was a profound kindness in her, a practical compassion that made the grand, abstract virtues he taught in his literature classes seem pale and theoretical. He loved the way her dark hair escaped its severe bun at the end of a long day, the way her laugh was a sudden, surprising burst of music in the staff room, the way her grey eyes could hold a depth of understanding that made him feel both known and forgiven.

They had never spoken of anything beyond the school, beyond the weather and the progress of their respective pupils. But there were moments. A shared glance across a crowded assembly hall. The brush of her fingers when they both reached for the same book in the library. An hour spent together in the empty classroom, ostensibly reviewing a new primer for the infants, but in reality simply talking, their voices low, the space between them charged with all the things that could not be said. He knew she felt it too. It was in the hesitant smile she gave him, a smile that was both a promise and a farewell.

And now, this. Monsieur Delacroix’s memorandum was not a crude ultimatum; it was a masterpiece of polite coercion. It spoke of his esteem for Ashby’s work, of his desire to see him “anchored” to the school and the city. It painted a picture of a secure future, of influence and respect. And at its heart was Claire.

Claire Delacroix was not a monster. She was, in fact, a thoroughly decent woman of twenty-two, with a plain, intelligent face and a shy, reserved manner. She was well-read and spoke three languages. She helped her uncle with the school’s accounts and was known for her quiet acts of charity. Ashby respected her. He enjoyed their occasional conversations, which were always thoughtful and well-informed. He could imagine a life with her. It would be a life of comfort, of order, of mutual respect. It would be a life lived in a well-appointed apartment, with a fire in the grate and a respectable position in society. It would be a life, he thought with a sudden, cold clarity, without a single moment of unscripted joy.

He thought of Elodie’s laugh. He thought of the worn elbows of her dress. He thought of the fierce, protective love he felt for her, a love that was tangled up with a desire to shield her from the world’s casual cruelties. To marry Claire would be to betray that love, and in doing so, to betray the very principles he tried to instil in his students. He taught them about honour, about integrity, about the courage to choose the right path, not the easy one. What would his lessons mean if he, their master, sold his heart for a promotion and a view of the courtyard?

He stood up and paced the small room. The rain was easing. A pale, watery sun was attempting to pierce the clouds, casting a weak, lemony light on the wet cobblestones below. He saw a figure hurrying along the street, a woman with a basket, her head bowed against the wind. For a moment, his heart lurched, thinking it was Elodie, but it was just a stranger.

The choice was a simple one, really. It was a choice between two kinds of poverty. The poverty of a life with Elodie, a life of small means, of scrimping and saving, of perhaps never achieving the recognition his talents deserved. A life on the margins, always. And the poverty of a life with Claire, a life of plenty, but a life of spiritual penury, a gilded cage where his heart would slowly starve.

He walked back to his desk and looked at the memorandum. The director’s words seemed to mock him. “Anchored.” A ship was anchored to keep it from drifting, to keep it safe in harbour. But what was the point of a ship that never sailed?

He picked up his pen. It was a fine pen, a gift from his father, and it felt heavy in his hand. He dipped the nib in the inkwell, the black liquid a stark contrast to the white paper. He would write a letter to Monsieur Delacroix. He would be respectful, he would be grateful, but he would be firm. He would decline the position.

He would not, he decided, mention Elodie. That was not a matter for the director’s ledger. This was a matter of his own conscience. He would simply state that he did not feel himself able to accept the conditions attached to the promotion, that he valued his independence as a teacher and as a man too highly to accept a post on such terms. It was the truth, even if it was not the whole truth.

As he began to write, the sun finally broke through the clouds, and a square of pale light fell across his hand. He thought of Elodie, of the quiet courage in her grey eyes. He did not know what the future held. He did not know if she loved him enough to wait, to share a life of struggle and uncertainty. He only knew that he could not begin a new life on a foundation of falsehood. The words flowed from his pen, clear and steady. He was not writing a letter of refusal. He was writing a declaration of intent.
