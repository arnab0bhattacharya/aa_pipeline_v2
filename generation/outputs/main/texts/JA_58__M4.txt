The indisposition of Mr. Charles Ellington, which had begun as a trifling cold caught upon the water-meadows at Weybourne, had in the space of a fortnight assumed a character sufficiently alarming to summon his sister from Bath and to detain his betrothed—a young lady of whom Miss Ellington had formed no very favourable opinion—under the same roof. It was a circumstance, she could not but feel, of the most trying description; and she had submitted to it with a resignation which she trusted was not unobserved, though she had taken care to observe it very particularly herself.

Miss Ellington was a woman of nine-and-twenty, of a decided understanding and an undecided temper, who had long since arranged the world into two classes: those whose conduct was agreeable to her own notions of propriety, and those whose conduct was not. Into the latter class she had, upon evidence which she considered ample, consigned Miss Harriet Fenwick; for she had heard from a lady of undoubted veracity that Miss Fenwick laughed too much, danced too often, and had been seen at Weybourne in a bonnet of so pronounced a colour as to border upon impropriety. That a woman so circumstanced could nurse a sick man with patience, or bear with a sister-in-law's ill humour, was not to be expected; and Miss Ellington had accordingly gone down into Surrey prepared to endure, to be civil, and to be proved right.

The first week did not disappoint her. Miss Fenwick was cheerful in the sick-room, which Miss Ellington thought frivolous; she was silent when Mr. Ellington slept, which Miss Ellington thought affected; and she was frequently to be found at the window, looking out upon the garden with an air of great contentment, which Miss Ellington thought indefensible in a woman whose lover was feverish. She said as much to herself with great frequency, and was gratified to find her judgment confirmed by the very composure of her companion.

It was upon the ninth night, when the fever had reached its height and the physician had spoken with a gravity which silenced all lesser considerations, that Miss Ellington discovered the error of her conclusions. She had come down at three o'clock, unable to sleep, and had found Miss Fenwick already in the passage, with her hair confined beneath a plain cap and her hands not quite steady, though her voice was.

"You are come to relieve me," said Miss Fenwick. "I am glad of it. He has asked for you twice."

"He has asked for me?" said Miss Ellington, surprised into a sincerity which she afterwards regretted.

"Twice, and by name. I told him you were sleeping, and that he must not be selfish." She smiled, and there was a trembling about the mouth which spoke of four nights' watching. "He said that was very like you—to sleep when you were told. I hope you are not offended."

"I am not offended," said Miss Ellington, and went in.

The room was dim, and her brother lay with his face turned towards the door, and his eyes, which were bright with fever, fixed upon her with an expression of such entire confidence that she was obliged to sit down very suddenly upon the chair beside the bed. He took her hand and said something which she could not distinguish, and then, as though the effort had exhausted him, closed his eyes and slept. Miss Ellington sat holding his hand, and found herself, for the first time in many years, entirely without an opinion.

It was in the small hours, when the fever had begun to abate and the fire had burned low, that she began to observe the room. It was neat, and it was quiet, and it was arranged with a thousand small attentions which she had not expected and could not account for. There was a glass of barley-water within reach, and a book laid face-down upon the table with a marker at the page, and the curtains had been drawn against the morning with a care which suggested a familiarity with sick-rooms. Upon the mantelpiece stood a little nosegay of late roses, withered now, but placed there with a precision which argued a daily renewal.

Miss Ellington looked at these things for some time in silence. Then she looked at her brother's face, which was thinner than she remembered it, and at the hand which lay in hers, and she said, half aloud, "I have been very foolish."

"You have been very quiet," said Miss Fenwick from the doorway, "which is what he wanted. I have brought you some tea. You need not drink it, but I shall be obliged if you would hold it, for it is very hot, and I have carried it up two flights."

Miss Ellington took the cup. "Miss Fenwick," said she, "I have done you an injustice."

"I daresay you have," said Miss Fenwick, seating herself composedly upon the edge of the bed. "It is very natural. Charles has told me a great deal about you, and I know you do not approve of me. You think me a frivolous woman who wears improper bonnets. I am not offended. I have a great many bonnets, and some of them are very improper."

"You are not what I expected," said Miss Ellington, with some difficulty.

"No," said Miss Fenwick. "I am a great deal better than I look, and a great deal worse than I seem. It is the same with most people. Charles is the only person I ever met who is precisely what he appears—which is why I am going to marry him, and why you and I shall be very good friends, if you will allow it, for I have no intention of quarrelling with my husband's sister over a matter of bonnets."

Miss Ellington was silent for a moment. The tea was very hot, and her eyes, she found, were not entirely dry. "I have been prejudiced," she said at last, "and I have been proud of my prejudice, which is worse. I cannot think how I came to be so mistaken."

"From report," said Miss Fenwick, "which is the worst possible ground for any opinion, and the commonest. I was told that you were a blue-stocking who despised amusement, and I was prepared to dislike you very heartily. But here you are, sitting up at four in the morning with your brother's hand in yours, and I find I cannot dislike you at all. So we are both of us disappointed, and we must make the best of it."

Mr. Ellington woke at eight, much improved, and found his sister and his betrothed asleep in the two chairs beside his bed, with their heads inclined towards one another in a manner which he had not expected and which gave him great satisfaction. He lay still for some time, considering them, and then he said, in a voice still weak but of a tolerable cheerfulness, "I am very glad to see that you have not killed one another."

Miss Ellington opened her eyes. "We have not," said she. "We have been quarrelling over bonnets, and I have been obliged to give way."

"You see how it is," said Miss Fenwick, without opening hers. "I have conquered her with tea and good sense, and she has conquered me with silence and a superior air. It is a very fair exchange, and I hope you are satisfied."

"I am very well satisfied," said Mr. Ellington, "and very hungry, and I should like some breakfast, if either of you can be prevailed upon to leave the room."

They left it together, and upon the stairs Miss Ellington said, "I shall write to my aunt in Bath, and tell her that I was mistaken in you, and that she was mistaken too, and that we are both of us very glad to be so."

"That will be a very proper letter," said Miss Fenwick, "and I hope you will not spare yourself in it. There is nothing so improving as a confession of error, provided it is made to someone who will remember it."

Miss Ellington laughed—a thing she had not done in Surrey, and had not often done in Bath—and they went down to order breakfast. The bonnet of the pronounced colour was produced that afternoon, and worn, and admired, and Miss Ellington, who had never in her life admired a pronounced colour, was heard to observe that it was very becoming, and that she had always thought so.
