I had read her letter upon the morning of its arrival, and its singular import had brought me with all speed to the corner of Wych Street, where I alighted from a four-wheeler in a drizzle of rain. The thoroughfare was narrow and gloomy, lined on either side with shops whose wares lay obscured behind unwashed glass. It was a quarter little frequented by the well-to-do, and the appearance of a young woman of evident breeding, standing at the threshold of a faded brass plate, was a sight that drew more than one lingering glance from the passers-by. She was tall, and possessed of that clear, sun-touched complexion which speaks of distant climes. Her grey dress was plain, and her bearing, though anxious, was marked by the self-possession of one reared in wide and solitary places.

She observed my approach with a swift, searching glance, and held out her hand as I ascended the steps. "You are Mr. Vane?" she asked. "I wrote to you, as you see, because there is no one else in this great city to whom I may turn."

I bowed, and followed her within. The room into which she led me was scantily furnished, and bore the sour smell of damp plaster. She closed the door, and turned to face me, her hands clasped tightly before her.

"My father, Mr. Elias Carstairs, held a sheep-run in the back country of Canterbury, in New Zealand," she began, her voice low and rapid. "He was a recluse, and a man of peculiar habits, but he was also a man of considerable means. He died there in January last, and his papers disclosed a property in London, left to me as his sole heir. I was advised by the colonial agent to sail at once, and I did so upon the barque _Wairarapa_, arriving at the docks at Tilbury but three days since."

I nodded. The facts, as she narrated them, were simple enough. "And the solicitor?" I asked.

"He met me upon the quay," she continued, and her lip curled with something akin to disgust. "A Mr. Silas Greet, of the firm of Greet and Poulter, of Old Jewry. He was a short, thickset man, very precise in his speech, and dressed in a black coat which was shiny at the elbows. He bore a letter, which he stated was from my father’s London agent, and he presented a card. I had no reason to doubt him. I was alone, and weary from the voyage."

"But you doubt him now," said I.

"I do," she answered, and drew from her pocket a folded paper. "He took me to lodgings in Kennington, to a house kept by a Mrs. Slaney, a stout, deaf woman who asked no questions. He stated that the transfer of the property would require some days, and that he would call upon me daily with documents to sign. These documents I signed, Mr. Vane, without reading them, for I was exhausted, and he was pressing. It was not until the second evening that I observed a thing which set my heart beating. He had left his satchel open upon the table, and within it I saw a bundle of letters, bound with a cord. The uppermost letter bore a New Zealand postmark, and the signature at its foot was that of my father."

She handed me the paper. It was a tracing, executed in pencil upon thin tissue, of a signature. The name was "Elias Carstairs." Below it, she had traced another signature, taken, as she informed me, from one of the documents she had signed at Greet’s behest. The two hands were identical in every flourishing particular.

"He has forged my father’s name," she said, with a tremor. "I know it, for my father’s hand was cramped and broken, owing to an old injury to his right arm, sustained in a fall from horseback. This signature is smooth and fluent, a copy made by one who had studied the form, but knew nothing of the impediment. The man is not a solicitor. I am convinced he is an impostor, and that he designs to possess himself of my inheritance by means of these forged conveyances."

She ceased, and stood regarding me with a steady, resolute gaze. There was that in her manner which bespoke the colonial breed—a hardihood, and a directness, that was wholly wanting in the drawing-room miss. I was struck by her intelligence, and by the singular promptitude with which she had acted upon her suspicions.

"You did not confront him?" I asked.

"I am alone in London, sir," she replied. "I do not know the law here, nor its officers. He is a man of forceful address, and he has already taken possession of my father’s papers. I feared, if I accused him, he would simply vanish, and I should never recover them. So I wrote to you."

It was a sound judgement, and I told her so. I inquired as to the nature of the London property, and she gave me the address—a narrow house in a square near the British Museum, which her father had owned for twenty years and never visited. I inquired, further, as to the man’s habits, his speech, and any small particulars which might serve to mark him. She reflected for a moment.

"He wore a ring," said she, "upon the little finger of his left hand. It was a heavy gold ring, and upon its face was a stone, dark red, which I took to be a garnet. I noted it because he turned it continually with his thumb when he spoke, as one who is ill at ease. His boots were new, and of a brown leather, quite unsuited to the London mud. And his nails, Mr. Vane, though clean, were bitten to the quick."

I committed these particulars to memory. I then requested that she should return to Mrs. Slaney’s, and conduct herself precisely as though nothing had occurred. I would call upon her the following morning. In the meantime, I would visit the house near the Museum, and make such inquiries as the situation demanded.

I found the square without difficulty. It was a sombre place, enclosed by iron railings, with a few stunted trees at its centre. The house in question was of yellow brick, and bore a brass plate upon its door, which announced it as the offices of "The Canterbury Estates Company." I rang, and was admitted by a clerk, a pale young man with ink-stained cuffs. I inquired for Mr. Greet. The clerk stated that Mr. Greet was not in attendance, but was expected within the hour. I left my card, with a pencilled note, and withdrew.

I had not long to wait. I had taken up a position in a doorway across the square, whence I could observe the entrance, when a four-wheeler drew up, and a short, thickset man descended. He was dressed in a black coat, shiny at the elbows, and he carried a leather satchel in his left hand. As he turned to pay the driver, I observed the ring upon his little finger, and the brown boots, splashed now with mud. It was the man beyond all question.

I allowed him to enter, and then crossed the square. The door was opened once more by the pale clerk, who recognized me and permitted me to wait in a small, dim room at the rear. I heard the murmur of voices beyond the partition, and then a heavier step, and the closing of a door. Presently the clerk appeared, and stated that Mr. Greet would see me.

I was conducted to an inner office. Mr. Silas Greet sat behind a desk, his hands folded upon a pile of papers. He regarded me with a sharp, inquiring eye. He was, as Miss Carstairs had described him, a man of forceful address, with a broad, smooth face and a small, pursed mouth.

"Mr. Vane, I believe," said he, in a smooth, even voice. "You left a card. How may I be of service?"

"I am here," said I, "upon a matter concerning the Carstairs estate."

His expression did not alter by so much as a flicker. "Indeed?" said he. "And what is your interest in that estate, sir?"

"I act," said I, "for Miss Eleanor Carstairs, the sole heir."

There was a silence. He turned the ring upon his finger with a slow, deliberate motion, and his small eyes contracted. "I was not aware," said he, "that the young lady had placed her affairs in other hands."

"She has placed them in my hands," I answered, "because she has reason to believe that certain documents, which she was induced to sign under false pretences, bear a signature which is not that of her late father."

He drew a sharp breath, and his fingers ceased their movement. "That is a serious allegation, Mr. Vane."

"It is," said I. "It is an allegation of forgery, and of fraud. I would advise you, sir, to place upon that desk, at once, the papers which you have obtained from Miss Carstairs, together with any correspondence bearing upon the Canterbury property."

He rose from his chair, and his face had gone a shade greyer. "You are no more a solicitor than I am," he cried, with a sudden, ugly violence. "You are a spy, and a meddler, and I will have the law upon you!"

"You will find," said I, "that the law is already upon you. I have taken the precaution of communicating with Scotland Yard, and an officer is, at this moment, stationed outside the door."

It was a falsehood, but it served its purpose. He cast one glance toward the door, and then, with a swift, convulsive movement, he snatched the satchel from beside his chair and hurled himself toward the window. I had anticipated the move, and was across the room before he could raise the sash. I seized him by the collar, and we staggered together against the desk. He was a powerful man, and his resistance was fierce, but I locked my arm about his throat and held him fast. The pale clerk, white as a sheet, appeared in the doorway. I bade him fetch a constable, and he vanished without a word.

It was a matter of ten minutes before the officer arrived. In that time, Mr. Silas Greet made no further attempt at escape. He stood, sullen and dishevelled, in the corner of the room, while I examined the contents of his satchel. Within it, bound with a cord, were the letters of Elias Carstairs, together with the forged conveyances, and a quantity of scrip, which represented the income from the Canterbury property over a period of five years. It was a complete and damning record.

The man was taken in charge, and removed to the station. I returned at once to Wych Street, and found Miss Carstairs awaiting me, pacing the narrow room with an air of controlled agitation. I placed the satchel upon the table.

"You have done a very judicious thing, Miss Carstairs," said I. "The man is in custody, and your father’s papers are restored."

She stood for a moment, looking down at the satchel, and then she extended her hand. "I am greatly obliged to you, Mr. Vane," said she. "I was alone, and I did not know where to turn."

"You turned to the right quarter," I answered. "And I shall be happy to see the matter through to its conclusion."
