I have often remarked—and I think with some justification—that the most impenetrable of all fogs is not the one that comes rolling up the Thames, thick as a blanket and yellow as a bruise, but the one which settles, imperceptibly and without warning, between two men who were once boys together. It does not announce itself. There is no horn, no bell, no cry of warning. You simply discover, after a time, that you can no longer see the other man clearly, that his voice reaches you muffled and strange, and that the familiar landmarks by which you had once navigated the friendship have been swallowed up entirely.

Such was my situation upon the evening when I received, at my rooms in a quiet street off the Vauxhall Bridge Road, the card of Mr. Edgar Vane.

I had not seen Vane for the better part of twelve years. At school we had been inseparable—or, to speak more accurately, I had been the inseparable one, for Vane was a boy of a solitary and brooding temper, given to long silences and sudden, disconcerting observations, and it was my privilege, or perhaps my weakness, to attach myself to him with the devotion of a spaniel. He was clever, undeniably; there was in him a certain fire, a certain impatient brilliance, which had won him the grudging admiration of masters and boys alike. But there was also a darkness in him, a capacity for resentment that seemed to feed upon itself in secret, and I had sometimes felt, in his company, the vague uneasiness which one experiences in the presence of a machine whose governor has been removed.

He had gone up to Oxford; I had entered the service of the Crown. Our paths had diverged, as paths will, and for years I had heard of him only through the obscure and fragmentary reports which reach one in the corridors of Whitehall—rumours of wild speeches at obscure meetings, of pamphlets printed in cellars, of an association with certain foreign persons whose names were better known to the Special Branch than to the general public. I had thought of him often, I confess, with a kind of sorrowful affection, as one thinks of a ship one has once sailed in and which has since been reported lost with all hands.

And now he stood before me, in my own sitting-room, with the gaslight falling upon his thin, sharp-featured face and his threadbare coat, and I saw at once that the fire was still there, but that it had burned inward, as fires will, consuming the man himself rather than the fuel.

"You have done well for yourself, Henry," he said, glancing about the room with a smile that was not quite pleasant. "Very well indeed. The carpets, the books, the little clock on the mantel—all the appurtenances of respectability. I congratulate you."

I offered him a chair and a glass of sherry, which he refused with a gesture that was almost contemptuous. He remained standing, his back to the fire, his hands clasped behind him, and for a long moment he said nothing, but looked at me with an expression I could not read.

"I have come to ask a favour," he said at last. "A small thing, for a man in your position. I wish to see Mr. Alderton."

Mr. Alderton was, at that time, the Minister of the Interior—a man of great ability and greater caution, whose name was never absent from the newspapers for more than a day together. That Vane, of all men, should wish to see him was sufficiently remarkable; that he should come to me, his old friend, to arrange it, was more remarkable still.

I said as much, perhaps with some bluntness. Vane smiled again.

"You misunderstand me, Henry. I have no intention of assassinating the good gentleman, if that is what alarms you. I am not the wild beast your newspapers make me out to be. I have—how shall I put it?—I have a proposition to make. A matter of policy. There are things which I know, and which certain persons in my—shall we say, my circle—know, which it might be of advantage to the Government to learn. I am weary of the wilderness, Henry. I am weary of shouting in cellars and being hunted from pillar to post. I wish to come in from the cold."

I confess that my heart leaped at these words. I had dreamed, in my sentimental way, of just such a reconciliation—of the lost sheep returned to the fold, of the old friendship renewed upon a basis of mutual understanding and public service. I was, you must understand, a man of orderly habits and optimistic temperament, and I believed—as men in my position are apt to believe—that all human differences are susceptible of reasonable adjustment if only the parties can be brought to the same table.

I questioned him further. He was guarded, oblique, but he let fall enough to convince me that his intentions were serious. He spoke of a certain group—he would not name it—whose designs were more dangerous than the authorities suspected. He spoke of documents, of names, of a plan that might, if unchecked, bring about a catastrophe. He wished to trade this knowledge, he said, for a measure of immunity, for a hearing, for the chance to state his case in his own words to a man who had the power to act.

I agreed to arrange the meeting. I did so, I may say, with a certain warmth, with a certain sense of my own importance as a peacemaker, and I did not observe—or did not permit myself to observe—the faint, sardonic curl at the corner of his mouth as he thanked me and took his leave.

The meeting was arranged for a Thursday evening, in Mr. Alderton's private room at the Ministry. I was present, as was my duty; and I remember the scene with a vividness that time has not dimmed. The great man sat behind his desk, portly, imperturbable, his heavy face illuminated by the green-shaded lamp, his fingers drumming softly upon the blotting-pad. Vane sat opposite him, erect and pale, his eyes glittering in the shadow. I sat to one side, in a position of obscurity, and said nothing.

Vane spoke for perhaps twenty minutes. He spoke well—I will do him that justice—with a lucidity and a command of detail that surprised me. He named names. He described meetings, printing-presses, hiding-places. He traced the connections between certain foreign agitators and certain discontented elements at home. Mr. Alderton listened without interruption, his face expressionless, his fingers drumming, drumming.

And then, quite suddenly, Vane stopped. He leaned back in his chair. He smiled—that same smile I had seen in my sitting-room, sardonic and remote.

"And now, Mr. Alderton," he said, "I have a question to ask you. A small question. It concerns a certain transaction—a loan, shall we say—which was negotiated some three years ago between your Ministry and a certain firm in the City. The firm of Garroway and Sons. You know the name, I think."

I saw Mr. Alderton's fingers stop their drumming. I saw his heavy face grow, for an instant, strangely still.

"I know the name," he said, slowly. "What of it?"

"I have in my possession," Vane said, "certain papers relating to that transaction. Papers which, if they were made public, would—how shall I put it?—would cause a great deal of embarrassment. To you personally, Mr. Alderton. And to certain of your colleagues."

There was a silence in that room which I shall never forget. It was not the silence of peace, but the silence of a held breath, of a trap about to be sprung. I looked from one man to the other, and I understood—with a clarity that came upon me like a blow—that I had been used. That I had been the instrument, the convenient and credulous instrument, by which this man had gained his entrance. That the reconciliation I had dreamed of was a fiction, a bait, a thing of air.

"You see, Henry," Vane said, turning to me with that same smile, "I told you I had a proposition to make. I did not tell you what it was."

Mr. Alderton rose from his chair. He was a large man, and in that moment he seemed larger still, looming over the desk like a thundercloud.

"Get out," he said. "Get out of this building, and be thankful I do not have you arrested."

Vane rose also. He was still smiling.

"Arrest me, and the papers go to the press tomorrow morning. You know that as well as I do. I am not asking for much, Mr. Alderton. A small pension, a passage to the Continent, a guarantee of safety. That is all. You will find it cheaper than the alternative."

I do not know what passed between them after that. I left the room—I could not bear to stay—and I stood in the corridor, trembling, while the gas-jets hissed and the great clock in the hall ticked on towards midnight. I heard voices raised, then lowered, then the opening and closing of a door. And presently Vane came out, alone, and walked past me without a word, without a glance, his coat-tails flapping behind him, and was swallowed up in the darkness of the stairwell.

I never saw him again. He went abroad, I believe, and died some years later in obscure circumstances. But I have thought of him often—of that evening in my rooms, of the firelight on his thin face, of the smile that I had not understood. And I have come to see that there are men in this world—and he was one of them—for whom the past is not a garden to be revisited but a weapon to be used; for whom friendship itself is but a door, to be opened when needed and closed again upon the darkness. I was a fool, I suppose. But I have learned since that the most dangerous of all fogs is the one that rises from the heart, and that the man who believes he is bringing about a reconciliation may be, all unknowing, the bearer of the knife.
