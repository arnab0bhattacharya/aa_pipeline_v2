The jointure which had been settled upon Mrs. Ellingham at the death of her husband was a sum sufficient to preserve her and her three daughters from the necessity of employment, but not from the necessity of economy; and it was this distinction, so finely understood by the neighbourhood of Wrenfield, which made the arrival of Mr. Aldworth at the White Hart an event of such peculiar interest. He was a merchant, retired from the China trade, with a fortune reported to be considerable and a person reported to be plain; and he had come to Hertfordshire, it was said, for no other purpose than to find a wife.

The ladies of Wrenfield were unanimous in their disapprobation. A man in trade, however wealthy, could not be received; and a man who had actually sold tea over a counter—for so the rumour ran, though the rumour was inaccurate—was beyond the pale of polite consideration. Mrs. Ellingham's neighbours pitied her, in that comfortable manner which requires no exertion, and wondered how she would contrive to keep her distance.

They were not prepared for the intelligence that she had received him.

The first visit was paid upon a Tuesday, and lasted precisely three quarters of an hour. Mr. Aldworth was a stout gentleman of fifty, with a complexion somewhat weathered by foreign suns, and a manner that betrayed no consciousness of inferiority. He spoke of the China seas, of the price of silks, of a storm off the Cape which had nearly proved fatal to his vessel and his fortunes; and Mrs. Ellingham, who had expected to be wearied, found herself attending with an interest she could not account for. He was not elegant. He was not even particularly genteel. But when he observed, in answer to a question from Miss Ellingham the elder concerning the customs of the Chinese, that he had once been obliged to drink tea with a mandarin who had never in his life been persuaded to laugh, and that he had succeeded at last only by the relation of a story so very indifferent that he blushed to repeat it, Mrs. Ellingham laughed herself—a circumstance so unusual that her daughters exchanged glances of astonishment.

"You laugh, madam," said Mr. Aldworth, with a bow. "I am honoured. It is the first time I have been so fortunate."

"It is the first time, sir," she replied, "that I have had occasion."

The acquaintance, thus begun, proceeded with a rapidity that alarmed the neighbourhood. Mr. Aldworth called again upon the Thursday, and upon the following Monday sent a basket of pineapples, a fruit so exotic in Wrenfield that Mrs. Ellingham's cook was at a loss whether to roast or to worship it. He walked with the ladies upon the common, and was observed to offer his arm to Mrs. Ellingham with a gallantry that sat oddly upon his figure but was not without its effect. He talked of his warehouses at Wapping, of the ships he still owned, of the nephew who would inherit his business and the competence he intended to settle upon any woman who would do him the honour to accept his hand. He made no secret of his intentions, and he made no secret of his admiration.

"You are very direct, Mr. Aldworth," said Mrs. Ellingham, when he had spoken thus at large before her daughters.

"I am too old, madam, to be otherwise. I have spent thirty years in bargaining, and I have learned that the shortest way is the best. I admire you. I wish to marry you. I have a house in Bedfordshire and a house in town, and I would give you the choice of them."

"That is a great deal, sir."

"It is all I have, madam, and I would lay it at your feet."

The elder Miss Ellingham, who was prudent, observed to her sister that Mr. Aldworth's offers were such as no reasonable woman could despise. The second Miss Ellingham, who was romantic, observed that he was fat and old, and that their mother could not possibly be in love with him. The youngest, who was but fifteen, said only that he made their mother laugh, and that she had not heard her laugh so much in three years.

Mrs. Ellingham herself said nothing. She was a woman who had been married at eighteen to a man of good family and small understanding, and who had spent twelve years in the exercise of a patience so unremitting that it had become a habit of mind. She had been a dutiful wife, and she had been, in her quiet way, a widow of great propriety. She had not expected to laugh again. She had not expected to find herself listening with pleasure to the conversation of a stout merchant who had made his fortune in trade and spoke of it without apology. But she found that she did listen, and that she did laugh; and she found, moreover, that the laughter did her good.

The neighbourhood, meanwhile, was not idle. Lady Chetwode, of Wrenfield Hall, who had condescended to notice Mrs. Ellingham upon the score of their distant connection, was pleased to observe that the widow must be in great distress indeed if she could think of such a match. Mrs. Pulteney, the rector's wife, remarked that money was money, and that a woman with three daughters could not afford to be nice. Mr. Pulteney, who was a man of some reading and more kindness, observed that Mr. Aldworth appeared to be a very sensible fellow, and that he wished him joy. This remark was received in silence, and Mr. Pulteney was afterwards heard to say that he had never known a man to be forgiven for speaking truth at a tea-table.

It was Lady Chetwode who at last carried the matter to Mrs. Ellingham herself. She called upon a Tuesday, at an hour when she knew the daughters to be from home, and she began with a general lamentation upon the state of the times, which she contrived to bring round, by degrees so gentle that they were scarcely perceptible, to the subject of trade.

"I am no enemy to commerce," said her ladyship. "Far from it. My brother has a stake in the East India Company, and I am sure I have never objected. But there is commerce and commerce, Mrs. Ellingham. There is the merchant who is received, and the merchant who is—how shall I say it—not received."

"You are very kind, Lady Chetwode, to take an interest in my acquaintance."

"I take an interest in your welfare, my dear madam. You are connected with my family, and I cannot see you throw yourself away without a word. Mr. Aldworth is a respectable man, no doubt. But his connections! His manners! His—I speak plainly—his person!"

"His person is not elegant," said Mrs. Ellingham. "I have observed it."

"And his conversation! All of ships and cargoes and the price of tea! One would think one were in a counting-house."

"His conversation is of the world, Lady Chetwode, and I find it instructive. He has been in places I shall never see, and he describes them with a plainness that is not without its charm. He is not a clever man, but he is a man of sense; and he has the art, which I had thought lost, of making me laugh."

Lady Chetwode was silent for a moment. "Laughter," she said at last, "is not everything."

"No," said Mrs. Ellingham. "But it is a great deal more than nothing. I have had twelve years of gravity, Lady Chetwode, and I find that I have no taste for a repetition of it."

Her ladyship withdrew, and the neighbourhood concluded that the match was settled. The conclusion was correct. Mr. Aldworth, who had never doubted of success, was pleased to find his confidence justified; and Mrs. Ellingham, who had doubted of everything, was pleased to find that she had been wrong. The daughters were satisfied, the neighbours were scandalised, and the wedding was fixed for the following month.

On the morning of her marriage, Mrs. Ellingham stood at the window of her little parlour and looked out upon the common, where Mr. Aldworth was already walking with her youngest daughter upon his arm. He was pointing at something in the distance, and the girl was laughing. Mrs. Ellingham watched them for a moment, and then she smiled—not the smile of a woman who has made a prudent match, nor of one who has made a romantic one, but of one who has discovered, rather late in life, that she may yet be permitted to be happy.

"It is not what I expected," she said to her eldest daughter, who stood beside her. "But I believe it will do very well."

"I believe it will, mama," said Miss Ellingham. "He is not a gentleman."

"No," said Mrs. Ellingham. "He is something better. He is a good man, and he makes me laugh. And I find, upon consideration, that I can ask no more."
