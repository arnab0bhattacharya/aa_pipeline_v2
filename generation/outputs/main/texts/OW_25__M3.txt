Lady Gwendolen Marlowe had broken twelve engagements, which is the only true test of a woman’s popularity in London. To have broken one is a misfortune; to have broken twelve is an art. She was universally admired, for society forgives a woman everything, provided she does it with sufficient style. Her twelfth engagement, to a young man whose only crime was an excessive fondness for the violin, had ended when she discovered he practised scales before breakfast. It was, she felt, a habit that could only lead to moral decay. Now, in the heat of the London season, she found herself engaged for the thirteenth time, to Lord Cedric Vane, a gentleman whose fortune was matched only by his entirely unjustified confidence in his own uniqueness.

Lord Cedric was determined to be the first of the thirteen to reach the altar. He had studied the previous twelve disasters with the cold detachment of a scientist examining a particularly fascinating species of moth. He had compiled a list of their errors. One had written poetry, which is always a mistake in a fiancé. Another had taken to wearing mauve cravats. A third had attempted to discuss politics at dinner, which Gwendolen found as vulgar as discussing money. Cedric resolved to avoid all such pitfalls. He would be charming, expensive, and entirely silent on matters of substance. He was, in short, the ideal man, and he knew it.

The engagement had been announced at a dinner in Belgrave Square, at the tall, narrow house of Gwendolen’s mother, the Dowager Lady St John. The drawing-room had been filled with orchids, which are the only flowers that truly understand the importance of being expensive, and with people, who do not. Lady St John had worn her famous black lace, which she always put on for significant occasions, as though mourning in advance for whatever was about to go wrong.

"My dear Gwendolen," Lord Cedric had said, as he stood by the fireplace in the drawing-room of Lady St John's house, "I am not superstitious."

"Then you are lacking in imagination, which is a great relief," Gwendolen had replied, adjusting a yellow rose in her hair. "Imagination in a man is merely the ability to lie to oneself with conviction. I prefer a man who lies to others. It shows a proper consideration for social harmony."

"I intend to marry you," Cedric continued, with the calm of a man who has never been contradicted.

"Thirteen is a dangerous number," said Gwendolen. "It is the number at which society begins to lose count and starts to whisper."

"Society always whispers. It is the only thing it knows how to do quietly. But I shall not be the thirteenth disappointment, Gwendolen. I shall be the first husband."

"There is no such thing as a first husband," said Gwendolen. "There are only second husbands, which is what a first husband becomes the moment he is married. It is the tragedy of matrimony that it turns a romance into a precedent."

Lady St John, who had been listening from behind a vast fan of ostrich feathers, interposed. She was a woman of formidable principles, none of which she had ever practised. She had determined that the thirteenth engagement must not be broken. Superstition, she felt, was the only thing standing between her daughter and permanent social ruin. A woman who breaks twelve engagements is a topic of conversation. A woman who breaks thirteen is a warning.

"You will marry him, Gwendolen," she said, with the tone of a Greek chorus that has taken up drawing-room etiquette. "I have consulted Madame Vrillon, the clairvoyant in Bond Street, and she assures me that to break a thirteenth engagement is to invite the very worst sort of karma. She did not use the word karma, of course. She said 'a disturbance in the astral body,' which I take to mean the same thing."

"I did not know you believed in astral bodies, Mama."

"I believe in nothing, my dear. But I believe in the consequences of not believing. It is the only sensible form of faith."

The days that followed were a delicate performance. Lord Cedric arrived each afternoon with a fresh bouquet of flowers so costly they might have been picked from the garden of a minor deity, and a conversation so light it might have floated on the summer air. He never mentioned the future, which Gwendolen found refreshing. He never mentioned the past, which she found wise. He spoke only of the present, and even then only in the most general terms.

"You are very quiet today, Lord Cedric," she said one afternoon, as they sat in the conservatory, where the air was thick with the scent of gardenias and the faint hum of a society that was always slightly bored.

"I am being careful," he replied. "I have made a study of your previous engagements. Sir Philip talked too much. Mr Hartley talked too little. The Honourable Frederick talked at the wrong times. I have decided that the safest course is to talk as little as possible, but to do so with great elegance."

"Elegance in silence is a virtue I have rarely encountered," said Gwendolen. "Most men fill every silence with the sound of their own opinions. It is the one thing more vulgar than a stained glove."

"I shall never give you an opinion," said Cedric. "I shall only give you jewellery."

"That," said Gwendolen, "is the most romantic thing you have ever said, which is to say, the most practical."

Lady St John, meanwhile, had not been idle. She had hired a private detective, which is what every sensible mother does when her daughter's future is at stake, and had discovered that Lord Cedric, in addition to his many virtues, kept a collection of exotic birds in his country house. This she took to be a sign of instability. A man who collects birds, she reasoned, is a man who prefers things that can fly away. It was, she felt, the very opposite of the marital instinct.

"He keeps parrots, Gwendolen," she said, over breakfast, as though announcing a criminal conviction.

"How charming," said Gwendolen. "A man who appreciates repetition. He will do well in marriage."

But Lady St John was not to be deterred. She invited Lord Cedric to dinner and seated him next to Mrs Arbuthnot, a woman of such crushing seriousness that she had once reduced a cabinet minister to tears by discussing the Poor Laws. Lord Cedric, however, survived the evening with consummate grace. He agreed with everything Mrs Arbuthnot said, which is the only way to deal with a serious woman, and then contradicted her entirely on the subject of Italian painting, which is the only way to interest one. By the end of the evening, Mrs Arbuthnot had declared him the most intelligent man in London, which Lady St John took as a further sign of his unfitness.

The wedding was set for a Saturday in June. The church had been decorated with white lilies, which are the only flowers that look as though they disapprove of everything, and the guest list had been drawn up with the precision of a military campaign. Lady St John had engaged a special guard, a retired detective from Scotland Yard, whose sole duty was to ensure that Gwendolen did not leave by a side entrance, as she had done on three previous occasions.

On the morning of the wedding, Gwendolen sat in her room, wearing a dressing-gown of pale blue silk, and looked at herself in the mirror. She had done this twelve times before, and each time she had seen something in her own eyes that had made her send a note, return the ring, and begin again. It was not cold feet. It was a kind of aesthetic horror. She could not bear the thought of being understood by one person for the rest of her life. To be known was to be finished, like a portrait that has been varnished.

Her maid brought in a small posy of stephanotis and orange blossom, tied with a silver ribbon. With it came a note, on thick cream paper, in Cedric's handwriting. It read: "I have cancelled the church. I have cancelled the flowers. I have cancelled the detective. Meet me at the railway station at noon. We shall go to Paris and be married by someone who does not know your name. I am told it is the only way to ensure a proper beginning."

Gwendolen read it twice. Then she laughed, which was a thing she did rarely, and only when she was genuinely surprised. She put on a grey walking dress, took up a parasol of pale green silk, and went down the stairs. Her mother was in the hall, directing the arrangement of the lilies with the authority of a field marshal.

"Where are you going?" Lady St John asked.

"To keep an engagement," said Gwendolen.

"But you are to be married in two hours."

"Then I shall be married in Paris, which is the only city that understands the importance of doing things improperly."

Lady St John turned pale. "You cannot break this engagement," she said. "It is the thirteenth. Think of the astral body."

"I am thinking of nothing at all, Mama, which is the proper state of mind for a bride."

She stepped into the carriage, and the horse moved off through the bright London morning, past the green squares and the white houses, towards the station, where Lord Cedric was waiting with two tickets and no opinions. It was, Gwendolen reflected, the most beautiful beginning she had ever contrived, and the only one she had any intention of finishing.
