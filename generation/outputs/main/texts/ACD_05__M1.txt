It was upon a morning of thick yellow fog, the kind which clings to the throat and muffles the sound of the street, that our visitor made his appearance. He was a man of some sixty years, with a face as brown and seamed as an old chart, and a bearing which suggested the quarter-deck even in the cramped confines of our sitting-room. This was Captain Elias Thorne, lately of the merchant service, and his trouble was of the heart.

“My boy, sir,” said he, setting his gold-headed cane upon the carpet. “My son, Ned. He has been missing from his post at the docks for three weeks. The police have been polite, but they are useless. They see only a common labourer who has run off to sea or the bottle. But I know Ned. He is a steady lad, and he would not leave me in this silence without cause.”

My companion, who had been observing the Captain’s sea-boots with a listless eye, suddenly became animated. “You say he was employed at the docks?”

“At the Ratcliffe Reach, sir. He worked for a Mr. Silas Croft, a warehouse owner.”

“And Croft’s establishment?” asked Holmes. “It is a prosperous one?”

“It is a strange one,” replied the Captain, leaning forward. “Croft pays his men in gold, at the week’s end, in cash. He keeps no ledger, no clerk. He writes nothing down. He says he trusts his memory, which is a thing I do not like in a shipping man.”

Holmes rubbed his hands together. “Excellent. A man who keeps no record usually has something to record which he would rather forget. And the cargo?”

“Crates, sir. Iron-bound crates, heavy on the quay. But I have heard it said by the stevedores that when those crates are opened at the other end of the world, they are light. Too light. But no one can prove it, for the seals are never broken here.”

“We shall go to the docks,” said Holmes, rising with an energy that dispelled the morning’s gloom. “The fog will serve as a cover. Come, Watson, bring your revolver. The atmosphere of Ratcliffe Reach is likely to be bracing.”

The journey to the eastern reaches of the river was a descent into a labyrinth of wet cobbles and looming brickwork. The fog pressed close, smelling of coal smoke and the stagnant tide. We found Croft’s warehouse easily enough; it stood apart from its neighbours, a tall, windowless block of soot-stained masonry near the entrance to the Shadwell Basin.

Holmes examined the heavy oak doors. “Padlocked, and the lock is new. But observe the ground, Watson.”

The mud around the entrance was trampled by many boots, but one set of tracks arrested my attention. They were the prints of a heavy, square-toed boot, but the heels were worn down on the outer edge—the gait of a man who spent his life on the rolling deck of a ship.

“Ned Thorne was a sailor before he turned docker,” Holmes muttered. “He came here. But where did he go?”

We circled the building. At the rear, overlooking the black water of the basin, was a small, iron-grated window, set high in the wall. Beneath it, the cobbles were stained with a dark, rust-coloured smear.

“Blood,” I said, with a professional’s certainty.

“Old blood,” Holmes corrected, touching it with his finger. “And look here.” He pointed to a length of frayed rope lying in the gutter. It was a fine, waxed cord, not the rough hemp used for cargo. “This is a garrote, or I am a Dutchman. But the struggle did not end here. The trail leads to the water.”

We walked to the very edge of the quay. The tide was low, exposing the slime-covered pilings. There, wedged between two timbers, was a small, leather-bound notebook. It was sodden, but the ink, being of a good quality, had not entirely run.

Holmes opened it with delicate fingers. “E. Thorne,” he read. “He kept a diary. ‘C. suspects I know about the weights. He is meeting the buyers tonight. I must see where the goods go.’ That is the last entry. Dated three weeks ago.”

“Then he saw too much,” I said. “But the crates, Holmes? The lightness?”

Holmes stood up, his eyes gleaming in the gloom. “Think, Watson. A warehouse owner who pays in cash, employs no clerk, and ships crates that lose weight in transit. It is not a case of theft, for the goods arrive. It is a case of substitution. Or rather, concealment.”

He strode back towards the warehouse doors, which were now being opened by a surly-looking fellow with a ring of keys.

“Halt!” cried Holmes. “We are here on the business of Captain Thorne.”

The man, a brute with the shoulders of a bullock, sneered. “Croft ain’t seeing no one.”

“Then we shall see Croft,” said Holmes, and before the man could raise a hand, my friend had slipped past him into the gloom of the interior.

The warehouse was cavernous, smelling of damp sacking and iron. In the centre of the floor stood a single crate, freshly packed and ready for the night’s tide. Holmes tapped it.

“Listen to that, Watson. It is hollow. But not with air.”

He produced a pocket-knife and pried at a loose board. The wood came away, and there, packed in straw, were not bolts of cloth or machinery, but dozens of small, lead-lined casks.

“Water,” said Holmes, tapping one. “Or rather, the lack of it. These casks are buoyant. They are packed in the crate to give it weight when it is empty of real cargo. But the real cargo—”

He turned to the surly man, who had drawn a heavy iron bar. “Where is the body?”

The man lunged, but I was ready. A blow from my stick sent the iron bar clattering to the floor, and I pinned the fellow against a stack of barrels.

“Speak!” I commanded.

“Croft did it!” the man gasped. “He caught the boy snooping. He knocked him on the head and put him in the crate. He’s in the river by now. The crate’s a coffin, see? Weighted to sink, but the water gets in and the body floats free eventually. That’s why they’re light when they land. The sea takes the meat.”

Holmes looked at me grimly. “A gruesome trade. Croft has been smuggling live cargo—stowaways, perhaps, or worse—and disposing of the evidence in the deep water channel.”

Just then, the door swung open and a tall man in a grey coat entered. He stopped dead when he saw us.

“Silas Croft,” said Holmes softly. “You are under arrest.”

Croft was a cool one. He looked at the crate, then at his man, and then at us. “You have found a crate of ballast,” he said smoothly. “I am a shipping agent. I am guilty of nothing but poor bookkeeping.”

“Your bookkeeping is your undoing,” said Holmes. “You have no ledger, but the river keeps accounts. We have found young Thorne’s diary.”

Croft’s hand went to his pocket, but Holmes was faster. He stepped forward and seized the man’s wrist. A pistol clattered to the stones.

“A clumsy weapon for a man of business,” Holmes remarked.

We led the two men out into the fog, where a constable, summoned by a whistle from Holmes, took them into custody. As we walked back towards the cab, the fog began to lift, revealing the grey waters of the Thames.

“It is a sad end for the boy,” I said.

“Do not despair yet, Watson,” said Holmes. “The crate was meant to sink. If the weights were tampered with, as the diary suggests, the crate may have drifted. There is a chance, a slim one, that Ned Thorne is clinging to a spar somewhere downstream.”

He hailed a cab. “To the river police station at Wapping. If I am not mistaken, there was a report of a drifting crate picked up near the Isle of Dogs this morning. The game is not over yet.”

It was, as Holmes had surmised, a matter of timing. At the station, we found the crate in question, battered and waterlogged. It had been hauled from the river by a patrol boat an hour before. Holmes ordered it opened immediately.

With a crash of timber, the side was torn away. Inside, amidst the straw and the water, lay a young man, pale and unconscious, but breathing.

“Ned!” cried the Captain, who had followed us in a frenzy of anxiety. He fell to his knees beside his son.

“He is merely chilled and stunned,” said Holmes, feeling the lad’s pulse. “The lead casks kept the crate afloat long enough for the river to do its work. Mr. Croft’s ingenious method of disposal saved the life of the witness against him.”

As the young man was revived and the Captain wept over him, Holmes drew me aside.

“A simple case, Watson, in its essence. The motive was greed, the method was crude, and the evidence lay in the mud. But it required a certain delicacy of touch to see that the weight of a crate is not always what it seems.”

“And the lighter crates on arrival?” I asked.

“Ballast, my dear fellow. Ballast and buoyancy. A sailor’s trick, turned to villainy. Come, let us leave them to their reunion. I believe there is a violin waiting for me in Baker Street, and I find myself in need of something less discordant than the cry of the river police.”
