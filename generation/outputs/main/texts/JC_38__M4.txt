The morning had come up grey and heavy over the bay of Puerto Almeja, a morning without sun, as though the sky itself had turned to lead and lay upon the water, pressing the breath out of it. Doña Eufrasia had risen before the light, as was her habit since the death of her husband—a death which had left her with a boat she could not row, a net she could not mend, and a silence in the house that seemed to her at times more substantial than the walls that contained it. She had eaten her piece of bread standing, and had drunk her coffee black and scalding, and had gone down to the shore where the sea, under that oppressive sky, moved with a slow and sullen violence, like a creature dreaming of some old grievance.

It was there, at the turn of the tide, in the grey light that seemed to have no source but only a general diffusion, that she found him. He lay half in the water and half out, his face turned to the sand, his arms spread wide as though he had been trying to embrace the earth itself in his last extremity. A soldier, by his dress—a tunic of coarse blue cloth, torn now and dark with wet, and trousers of the same stuff, and one boot, the other having been carried away by the sea or by some violence before the sea. The water had dealt with him roughly, or perhaps it was the men who had killed him; it was not possible to say, and Doña Eufrasia, standing above him with her skirts gathered in one hand and her lips pressed tight, felt the old, familiar numbness come over her—that numbness which had been her portion since the day they brought her husband home from the rocks, and which she had come to regard as a kind of mercy, since it permitted her to do what must be done without the inconvenience of feeling.

She saw the satchel then. It was slung across his body by a strap of leather that had cut into his shoulder, and it was of oiled canvas, and it had kept its contents dry, as she discovered when she knelt beside him and drew it from under the dead weight of him. There were papers inside, a great many papers, and they were wrapped in a piece of oilcloth and tied with a string. She did not open them there upon the beach, for the wind was rising and the spray was beginning to carry, and besides, there were men on the cliff path above, men of the village, who would be coming down soon to see what the tide had brought, and Doña Eufrasia had learned long ago that in a village there are no secrets, only delays.

She carried the satchel to her house and hid it beneath the floorboard in the corner where she kept her few valuables—a silver crucifix, her marriage ring, and the knife her husband had carried. And then she sat in her chair by the window and looked out at the grey sea and thought about what she had done, and whether it had been wise.

She could not read. This was not a thing that troubled her greatly in the ordinary course of her life, for what had a fisherman's widow to read? The priest read to her from the holy books, and the alcalde read the notices when there were notices to be read, and the rest of the world came to her through the mouths of others, which she had long suspected was not always the same as the world itself. But now she had in her possession a thing that was nothing but words, words that might mean anything, words that might mean everything, and she could no more read them than she could read the flight of birds or the pattern of the stars.

She knew what they were, of course. The whole village knew what was happening in the capital, and in the hills beyond, and in the other villages along the coast. The government of Don Ignacio Valverde had been at war with the rebels of General Ruiz for three years now, and the war had settled into that condition which wars assume when neither side can win and neither side can admit it—a condition of raids and reprisals, of villages burned and men taken from their homes at night, of letters that came and went and were never spoken of, of soldiers who appeared and disappeared and left behind them, sometimes, a body on the beach.

The dead man had been carrying letters to both sides. This much Doña Eufrasia understood from the mere appearance of the papers when she had glimpsed them in the satchel—some folded small and sealed with wax, others larger and covered with the close, cramped writing of a clerk, and all of them bearing the marks and the seals of authority. She did not know which were for the government and which for the rebels, and she did not know what they contained, and she understood that in the wrong hands they might mean death for someone, perhaps for many someones, perhaps for herself.

There were three men in the village who could read. The first was Padre Anselmo, who was old and fat and kind, and who had baptized her children and buried her husband, and who was, she knew, a man of God in the truest sense, which is to say a man who believed what he said he believed and tried to live accordingly. The second was Don Melchor, the alcalde, who was a man of the government and who wore his authority as a man wears a coat that is too tight for him, constantly, uncomfortably, and with a certain anxious aggression. The third was a young man named Tomás who kept the cantina and who had been to the city and had learned to read there, and who was said to be a friend of the rebels, though this was said quietly and never in his hearing.

Doña Eufrasia considered these three men as she sat by her window, and she considered the letters in their oilcloth wrapper beneath her floorboard, and she considered the dead soldier on the beach, who was by now being examined by the men of the village and would shortly be buried in the paupers' ground behind the church, and she felt the weight of a decision that she had not asked for and did not want.

It was not, she thought, a question of loyalty. She had no loyalty to the government, which had done nothing for her, nor to the rebels, who had done nothing for her either, nor to the war, which had taken her husband's brother and her husband's cousin and would, she did not doubt, take more before it was finished. It was a question of what was right, and this was a question she could not answer, for she had never been able to read the words that men wrote about right and wrong, and she had observed that those who could read them did not always agree.

She thought of her husband, who had been a simple man and a good one, and who had said to her once, in the last year of his life, when the war was beginning and the men of the village were taking sides, that the sea did not care who was right and who was wrong, and that a man who went out in a boat and did not come back was the same whether he had been a government man or a rebel, and that the only thing that mattered was that he had been a man. She had not understood him then, or she had thought she understood him, which is not the same thing. But she understood him now, or she thought she did, and she thought that perhaps the letters were like the sea, and that what mattered was not which side they served but what would become of the men and women and children whose lives depended on what was in them.

At noon she went to the church and found Padre Anselmo in the sacristy, and she told him what she had found on the beach, and she asked him if he would read the letters for her. The old priest looked at her for a long moment, and she saw in his eyes something that she had not seen there before—a kind of fear, or perhaps a kind of sorrow, which in a man of his age and his calling was the same thing. He said that he would read them, but that she must understand that what she asked of him was not a small thing, and that there were those in the village and beyond who would not be pleased to know that the letters had been read by him, and that she must be prepared for what might follow. And Doña Eufrasia said that she was prepared, though she was not, and that she would bring the letters to him that evening, when the village was asleep.

But she did not bring them that evening. She sat in her house as the light failed and the sea grew loud beyond the window, and she thought of the dead soldier with his arms spread wide, and she thought of the three men who could read, and she thought of the words that were in the letters, which she would never read herself, and which would be read to her by someone, and which would mean something, and which would change something, and she understood that she was no longer a fisherman's widow sitting by a window, but a woman who held in her hands the fate of others, and that this was a thing that could not be undone.

She rose at last and went to the corner and lifted the floorboard and took out the satchel, and she held it in her hands for a moment, feeling its weight, which was not great, and yet seemed to her heavier than the boat, heavier than the net, heavier than all the things of her former life. And then she put on her shawl and went out into the darkness, toward the church, where a light was burning in the sacristy, and where an old man waited for her, and where the words that she could not read would be read at last.
