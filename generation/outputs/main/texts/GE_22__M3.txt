There are few provinces of human folly more fertile than that wherein a young woman’s affections are made to grow alongside the calculations of her elders, so that the tender shoots of genuine inclination are ever being pruned and trained against a trellis of maternal ambition. Such was the horticultural enterprise undertaken by Mrs Bodkin upon the character of her daughter Hetty, who, at the time this history opens, was carrying her basket of new-laid eggs and pale yellow cheese through the Wednesday market at Carby Magna, in Nottinghamshire, with a degree of self-consciousness that perhaps no young woman entirely lacks when she knows her comeliness has been recently remarked upon by a man of substance. For Mr Joel Felling, a grazier whose meadows lay in the rich bottoms near the Trent, and whose herds were as finely fattened as any in the shire, had begun to manifest an interest in Hetty’s wares that had very little to do with dairy produce.

Mrs Bodkin, a widow of practical energies and a constitutionally sanguine view of her own discernment, had observed these manifestations with an satisfaction she took no pains to conceal. She had been heard to observe in the hearing of her neighbour, Mrs Toft, that it was a poor-spirited girl who did not know when fortune was making eyes at her across a market stall. And indeed, to a woman who had known the narrow economies of selling eggs and making shift with a cottage garden, the prospect of a daughter established in a stone house, with a mahogany table and a joint of meat on Sundays, was a prospect that cast the hues of moral obligation over the path of prudence. Mr Felling was a man of forty, broad in the chest and somewhat taciturn in the market-place, but with a slow, considering smile that was not unpleasing, and a reputation for fair dealing among the farmers of the Trent-side.

But the slow growth of feeling, as we know, follows laws as yet obscure to the moral botanist, and is often found putting forth its blossoms in soils that practical wisdom would pronounce barren. Hetty Bodkin, for all her mother’s watering and pruning, had already suffered a seed to take root in the unobserved corners of her heart—a seed cast there by a hand far less prosperous than Joel Felling’s. Martin Thorpe, a stone-mason from the village of Eckington, some six miles distant, was a young man whose clever hands could coax a block of gritstone into mouldings as delicate as any carved by the old abbey builders, but whose worldly prospects were as yet confined to day-wages and the occasional commission for a lintel or a threshold. He was dark, and lean, and quick of speech, with a certain restless wit that marked him among his fellows as one who might, given capital and opportunity, rise to be a master-builder; but at present he was merely a journeyman mason with a chisel and a strong right arm.

It had happened in the preceding September, when the hedgerows were heavy with hips and haws, that Hetty, carrying her basket along the lane toward Eckington, had been overtaken by Martin, who was returning from a job at a gentleman’s estate beyond the village. The afternoon was warm, and they had walked slowly, and Martin had told her of the carved doorway he was working on, explaining the pattern of the foliage with an eagerness that seemed to draw breath from some deeper spring than mere professional enthusiasm. And Hetty had listened, and had looked at his hands, which were rough and grey with dust, and had thought them the most eloquent things about him. At the stile by Eckington Wood, where the lane dips under a canopy of elder, he had asked her, with a directness that was characteristic, whether she would look kindly on him if he should come to her mother’s door one Sunday evening. And Hetty, with that rash generosity of youthful hearts which have not yet learned to calculate the consequences of their own impulses, had said yes. She had even, in the fulness of her feeling, given him a promise—a half-whispered, eager promise—that she would not give her word to any other man before he had spoken to her mother.

It was a promise such as young people make in lanes at sunset, seeming to themselves as light as the seeds of thistledown and yet carrying within them the stubborn weight of an obligation that conscience, that most inconvenient of counsellors, refuses to dismiss as trivial. And now, with the winter past and the spring market bringing Mr Felling’s attentions into a plainer light, Hetty found herself in that peculiar torment of the ingenuous mind, where the very clarity of one’s duty produces only a more tangled perplexity. For she could not but acknowledge that Mr Felling’s slow regard had stirred in her a certain pleased complacency—a sense of being valued that was not without its warmth—and yet the thought of Martin, standing at the workshop door with stone-dust on his sleeves and that eager light in his eyes, checked her at every turn, as a bridle checks a horse inclined to bolt.

Mrs Bodkin, for her part, was not blind to her daughter’s preoccupation, but she interpreted it with that confident misreading which affectionate parents often practice upon the young, supposing that silence and thoughtfulness in a girl of marriageable age must necessarily refer to the suitor they themselves prefer. "Thee’rt mopy, wench," she said one Thursday morning, as Hetty sat by the fire turning a cheese in its cloth with absent fingers. "Thee shouldst put a bit of colour i’ thy cheeks and look braw when Mr Felling comes to t’ stall. A man likes to see a lass look as if she knowed her worth. I’n seen many a good match slipped through a wench’s fingers for want of a bit o’ sense and a clean apron."

Hetty said nothing, but turned the cheese with a more determined pressure. She knew that her mother’s counsel, however coarsely phrased, proceeded from a genuine concern for her welfare, and this knowledge only deepened the groove of her distress. For it is one of the cruel ironies of our moral constitution that we are often most tortured by those very claims of gratitude and filial duty which, in cooler moments, we should acknowledge as the bonds of a reasonable affection. Had Mrs Bodkin been a harsh or a selfish woman, Hetty’s path might have been clearer; but her mother was neither—only practical, and somewhat impatient of sentiments she had not herself felt since the days when Thomas Bodkin, then a young carter with nothing but his arms and his good name, had walked her home from the harvest supper at Elksley.

It was on the following Sunday that Martin Thorpe came to the cottage, as he had promised, wearing his blue coat and carrying himself with that air of resolute composure which a man puts on when he is about to ask for what he knows he may be refused. Mrs Bodkin received him civilly enough, for she was not a woman to turn any visitor from her door, but there was a certain dryness in her manner, a punctilious economy of hospitality, that was not lost upon Hetty, who sat with her hands clasped in her lap, feeling the promise she had made pressing upon her like a physical weight. Martin spoke with her mother of the weather, and of the new bridge being built at Worksop, and of the price of stone; and then, with the directness that was his nature, said that he had come to ask if he might keep company with Hetty, for he hoped one day, God willing, to set up as a master-mason, and he had twenty pound saved, and would have more.

Mrs Bodkin heard him out with that expression of measured consideration which is more formidable than any open hostility, because it implies that the speaker’s mind is a ledger in which every item is being coldly weighed. "Twenty pound is a good sum for a young man," she said, "and I’m not one to undervalue industry. But Hetty is but seventeen, and I’m not for hurrying my girl into anything. She’s a good girl, and a good worker, and I look to see her comfortable." This was spoken with the air of one who has placed a barrier not by refusing passage but by making the conditions of passage so exquisitely conditional that no reasonable applicant would presume to press through.

When Martin had gone, having obtained nothing more definite than Mrs Bodkin’s promise to "think on it," Hetty went upstairs to the low chamber under the eaves, and sat on the edge of her bed, and looked out across the darkening fields toward Eckington Wood, where the elder branches were just putting forth their first pale leaves. The promise she had made at the stile now seemed to her both more binding and more terrible than she had foreseen; for she saw, with the terrible clairvoyance of an uneasy conscience, that Martin’s prospects, however honestly grounded, were such as would keep her in the very station of meagre striving from which her mother’s hopes were designed to rescue her. And yet the thought of telling Martin that she wished to be released from her word caused her a pain that surprised her, for it was not merely the pain of disappointing a suitor, but the deeper pain of violating something in herself—that inward fidelity to her own past utterance which is, perhaps, the most sacred form of honour the human heart knows, precisely because it binds us not to another person but to the self we have already been.

She sat thus while the light faded, and the rooks came home to the elms behind the cottage, and the smell of the cheese in the dairy below rose faintly through the floorboards—a smell that was the very odour of her daily life, of the basket and the market stall and the counting of pennies. And she understood, with the slow, heavy understanding of one who perceives that the great decisions of life are not made in moments of dramatic clarity but in the quiet accumulation of small recognitions, that she would have to choose, and that no promise, however tenderly given, could absolve her from the anguish of the choice itself.
