The substance, designated in the laboratory notebooks as Solution X, was not, in its initial conception, intended to be a weapon of cosmic disruption, but merely a reagent of considerable curiosity. Professor Alistair Craven, a man whose reputation in the Faculty of Natural Philosophy rested largely on a certain tedious thoroughness and a propensity for explaining the obvious in terms that baffled the unintelligent, had been working in his cellar for three weeks. He was a small man, precise in his movements, and possessed of the particular sort of confidence that comes from having never once been wrong in a matter of academic procedure. He believed that gravity, that ancient and unchallenged tyrant of the physical world, was not a force at all, but a defect in the fabric of the ether, a kind of static charge that could be neutralized.

It was a Tuesday in late October, the kind of damp, grey afternoon that settles over the Midlands like a wet wool blanket, that the experiment reached its critical stage. Craven had prepared a small quantity of the solution, a viscous, opalescent liquid that smelled faintly of ozone and burnt sugar. He had decided, with the casual arrogance of a man who views the laws of nature as mere suggestions, to test its efficacy on a scale that would be beyond dispute. A garden chair. An ordinary, wicker garden chair, slightly rickety on one leg, which he had dragged from the porch.

He was not alone. Mrs. Gable, the housekeeper, a woman of robust constitution and limited imagination, had been waiting in the kitchen for her tea. When Craven called for assistance, she appeared, wiping her hands on her apron, her face set in the expression of one who expects to be asked to fetch something from the cellar or mend a torn stocking.

"It is merely to hold the chair steady, Mrs. Gable," Craven said, his voice brisk and impersonal. "The application must be uniform. You will sit upon it. It is essential that there be a mass within the field of influence to demonstrate the cancellation of the gravitational pull. You are, I trust, not afraid of a little science?"

Mrs. Gable looked at the opalescent vial, then at the rickety chair. She did not understand the word 'field,' nor the concept of 'cancellation,' but she understood the tone of command in her employer’s voice. She was a woman who had served the Craven family for twenty years; she knew that Professor Craven was eccentric, but she also knew that he was not malicious. She sat down.

Craven dipped a cotton swab into the solution and applied it to the seat of the chair, then to the back, then, with a swift, decisive motion, to the fabric itself. He stepped back, a look of intense anticipation on his face. He expected a tremor. He expected the chair to lift an inch, or perhaps a foot. He did not expect the immediate, violent detachment from the earth.

For a second, there was silence. Then, a sound like a plucked string, a sharp *thrum*, and the chair began to rise.

It did not rise gently. It rose with a sudden, jerky acceleration, as if a rope had been cut. Mrs. Gable, who had been sitting with her hands in her lap, looked up. Her eyes widened, not with terror, but with a sort of dazed confusion, as if she had woken from a dream and could not yet recall where she was. She looked at Craven, who was standing on the cellar stairs, his mouth open, his spectacles askew.

"Professor?" she said. The word was barely audible in the growing rush of air.

Then the chair was gone.

Craven rushed to the cellar window. Below, in the garden, the earth was empty. Above, against the grey sky, a small, dark speck was moving upwards with terrible speed. It was not drifting; it was being projected. The neutralization of gravity had not merely removed the downward pull; it seemed to have released some residual energy, or perhaps the reaction of the ether itself had created a vacuum that sucked the object upward.

The speck was Mrs. Gable.

She passed over the roof of the house. Craven could see her clearly now, a small figure in a dark dress, her face turned upward, her expression still one of bewildered inquiry. She was rising over the chimneys, over the ivy-covered walls of the university buildings, over the spire of the old chapel. The town of Oakhaven spread out below her, a patchwork of slate roofs and narrow streets, quiet and unsuspecting.

The chair carried her higher. It did not stop. It did not slow. It shot up into the thinning air, a dark dot against the white clouds. Craven stood at the window, his mind racing, trying to calculate the trajectory, the velocity, the point at which the atmosphere would become too thin for breath. He realized, with a cold, sickening clarity, that he had not merely cancelled gravity; he had unleashed a force he did not understand. He had broken the tether that held humanity to the earth, and he had used his own housekeeper as the first casualty.

He ran to the street, shouting for help, but the people of Oakhaven were engaged in the quiet rituals of their afternoon. A baker was closing his shop; a student was walking home with a book under his arm. They looked at Craven, disheveled and frantic, but they could not see the chair. They could not see Mrs. Gable, who was now far above the rooftops, a speck no larger than a star, climbing into the infinite.

Craven stood in the middle of the street, the damp air soaking through his coat. He looked up. The sky was empty. There was no sign of the chair, no sign of the woman. It was as if she had never existed. The silence of the town was profound, heavy with the unspoken knowledge that something had been broken, something fundamental and irrevocable. The laws of the world were no longer what they had been. Craven knew that he would never explain this. He knew that the scientific establishment would dismiss it as a hallucination, a trick of the light, or a madman’s delusion. He knew that he would be laughed out of the university.

But he also knew that he was right. He had seen it. He had felt the *thrum* of the ether shifting. He had seen the chair rise. And he had seen Mrs. Gable’s face, that look of dazed confusion, as she left the world behind.

He went back inside. He closed the cellar door. He did not turn on the lights. He sat in the dark, listening to the rain begin to fall, a soft, steady tapping against the glass. He thought about the chair, about the way it had cut through the air, about the sheer, indifferent power of the thing he had created. He thought about Mrs. Gable, floating up, up, into the cold, dark void, a small, fragile human being caught in the gears of a universe that did not care.

He did not sleep that night. He sat in the dark, waiting for the sun to rise, waiting for the world to change, waiting for the moment when the first person would look up and see something that should not be there. But the sun rose, and the sky was clear, and the town of Oakhaven went about its business, oblivious to the fact that the ground beneath their feet was no longer as solid as they had always believed. The chair was gone. Mrs. Gable was gone. And the world continued, indifferent and vast, spinning on its axis, unaware that one small man had, for a brief, terrible moment, held the power to let go.
