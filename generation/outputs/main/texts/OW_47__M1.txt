The drawing-room at Wychwood Hall, in the county of Wiltshire, was an apartment of such profound and cultivated silence that the ticking of the long-case clock in the hall was felt, rather than heard, like a dull pulse in the floorboards. It was a room that had long since ceased to be a place for living and had become a museum for the living, where the Chippendale chairs stood in attitudes of elegant despair and the Aubusson carpet bloomed with roses that had never known a thorn. Here, Sir Alistair Finchley, Baronet, sat in the fading light of a Tuesday afternoon, a man of eighty years whose face was a handsome ruin, all sharp angles and fine grey hair, as if carved from a block of weathered marble. His wife, Lady Beatrice, sat opposite him, a creature of silks and subdued anxieties, her world as neatly ordered as the porcelain shepherdesses on the mantelpiece.

Between them, on a delicate rosewood table, lay the agent of their imminent undoing: a document, thick with the dust of ages, bound in cracked vellum and sealed with the faded crest of a long-defunct herald. It had been sent by a Mr. Pemberton, a genealogist of some repute, whom Sir Alistair had engaged to provide official, unimpeachable confirmation of a truth the family had held as gospel for four centuries: that they were the direct, if somewhat obscure, lineal descendants of King Edward IV through a clandestine but noble union.

For an hour, Sir Alistair had been studying the sprawling, ink-stained chart with the detached air of a connoisseur examining a newly acquired Titian. Lady Beatrice had been watching him, her own anxiety mounting with every second of his silence. She saw the future in that vellum—a future of coronets and royal blood, of a legacy that placed them not merely among the gentry, but in the very antechamber of history. It was the ultimate aesthetic object: a pedigree as perfect and flawless as a Fabergé egg.

Finally, he looked up, and a strange, brilliant light was in his pale blue eyes. It was not the light of triumph she had expected, but something far more complex and unsettling.

‘Beatrice,’ he said, his voice as smooth and cool as polished agate, ‘it appears we have been labouring under a delightful misconception.’

A small, cold tremor passed through her. ‘Misconception, Alistair? What do you mean? Mr. Pemberton is the most reliable—’

‘Oh, he is reliable to a fault,’ Sir Alistair interrupted, a faint smile playing on his lips. ‘That is precisely the trouble. He has been most meticulous. He has followed the thread of our lineage not to the royal boudoir, but to the royal kitchens.’

Lady Beatrice stared at him, her mind refusing to grasp the shape of his words. ‘The… kitchens?’

‘Indeed. It seems our illustrious ancestor, the one we have always called Geoffrey the Valiant, was in fact a Geoffrey of the same name and station who served as Master Cook to the said King Edward. A man, one presumes, of considerable talent with a saucepan and a spit. The royal blood we so cherish is, in point of fact, a jus of some sort.’

There was a silence, the kind of silence that precedes a scream or a fainting spell. Lady Beatrice did neither. She simply grew very pale, the colour draining from her cheeks as if drawn out by some invisible syringe. The porcelain shepherdesses on the mantelpiece seemed to mock her with their vacant, painted smiles. Their entire life, their social standing, the very air they breathed at Wychwood, was predicated on this single, glittering fiction. To be descended from a king was to be an aristocrat of the blood; to be descended from a cook was merely to be the descendant of a servant, however skilled. It was a fall from Olympus to the scullery in a single, silent moment.

‘Oh, Alistair,’ she whispered, her voice a thread of sound. ‘We are ruined.’

Sir Alistair Finchley, however, did not look like a ruined man. He looked, in fact, as if he had just been presented with the most exquisite and surprising jewel of his life. He leaned forward, his eyes alight with an intellectual fervour she had not seen in decades.

‘Ruined? My dear Beatrice, quite the opposite! We are, for the first time, interesting! For fifty years, I have been a baronet with a rather threadbare claim to a rather common king. A tedious business. But a cook! A royal cook! Now that is a narrative!’

He rose and began to pace, his movements imbued with a new, boyish energy. ‘Think of it! The artistry, the passion, the sheer, magnificent vulgarity of it! To have descended not from a man who merely wore a crown, but from a man who fed the head that wore it. There is far more character in a good ragout than in a dozen acts of Parliament. A king’s reign is a matter of dates and battles; a cook’s genius is a matter of taste and fire. I have spent my life surrounded by things that are merely old and expensive. Now, my own history is deliciously common. It is the most interesting thing that has ever happened to me.’

He stopped before the great fireplace, his face illuminated by the low fire. ‘We shall not hide this, Beatrice. We shall celebrate it. I shall have the family crest redesigned. Instead of a lion rampant, it shall be a silver turnspit, and the motto… ah, the motto must be changed. *De Gustibus Non Disputandum Est*. “In matters of taste, there can be no disputes.” It is perfect. It is witty. It is true.’

Lady Beatrice watched him, her horror slowly curdling into a kind of weary despair. She saw her husband, a man she had thought as predictable as the seasons, suddenly transformed into a stranger, a bohemian delighted by his own degradation. He was treating their ancestry not as a sacred trust, but as a new and fashionable plaything, an epigram to be worn at dinner parties.

‘The Duchess of Alnwick will cut us,’ she said flatly. ‘Lord Bramble will not call.’

‘Let them!’ he cried, waving a dismissive hand. ‘Their lineage is a dull page of Debrett’s. Ours is a chapter from a picaresque novel! We shall be the talk of the county. The Finchleys, who cook. It is a paradox worthy of a Greek philosopher. I am utterly delighted.’

He picked up the vellum chart again, not with reverence, but with a gourmand’s relish, tracing the line back to the kitchen. ‘I wonder what his signature dish was,’ he mused, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. ‘I imagine a formidable roast swan, or perhaps a subtlety of spun sugar in the shape of a castle. He was an artist, Beatrice. Our ancestor was an artist, and we have been misrepresenting his medium for four hundred years.’

He looked at her, his eyes shining with an almost feverish pleasure. For him, the world had been a drawing-room of exquisite but suffocating boredom. Now, a door had been thrown open, not into a grander hall, but into a hot, chaotic, and infinitely more vibrant kitchen. The dust of ages had been swept away, and in its place was the glorious, steaming reality of a life lived in service to sensation.

Lady Beatrice sank back into her chair, the scent of lavender and beeswax suddenly cloying. She looked at her husband, who was now humming a little tune, and at the ancestral portraits that stared down from the walls with their painted, noble disdain. They were all fakes, she realised, every last one of them. And her husband, the old fraud, had never been happier.
