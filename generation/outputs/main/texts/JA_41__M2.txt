It was a truth universally acknowledged, though often contradicted in practice, that a fortune of twenty thousand a year could buy almost anything in England, save perhaps the genuine affection of a woman who possessed more sense than vanity. Mr. Arthur Hale, heir to the considerable estates of Oakhaven Park in Wiltshire, was a man of such immense property and such unpretending manners that he had succeeded, in the space of three weeks, in making himself indispensable to the drawing rooms of the neighbourhood. Miss Clara Vane, however, was not of that neighbourhood. She was the daughter of a clergyman of moderate means and an aunt of limited imagination, residing in a modest cottage within sight of the park walls. Their meetings, conducted with the strictest secrecy behind the holly trees at the southern end of the park, were not, in the strictest moral sense, clandestine, as they were conducted in the broad daylight and under the gaze of the indifferent sky; but they were, in the social sense, most dangerously so, for they lacked the sanction of propriety and the approval of those who held the keys to happiness.

It was on a Tuesday of extraordinary mildness that the secret was betrayed, not by a whispering maid or a suspicious footman, but by the blunt instrument of Aunt Lydia’s well-meaning curiosity. Mrs. Vane, a woman who considered herself a pillar of society in their small circle and who possessed a mind that ran upon the same smooth, unvaried track as a well-oiled carriage, had resolved to inspect the grounds of Oakhaven Park. Her purpose was not to admire the views, but to ensure that Clara was not lingering too long in the shade, where the air was damp and the company, she suspected, might be of a questionable character.

She found them, indeed, seated upon a stone bench beneath the holly trees, Mr. Hale reading a volume of poetry with an air of studied indifference, and Clara listening with that peculiar alertness which only arises when the heart is fully engaged. Mrs. Vane’s discovery was not met with the fainting or the screaming that might have been expected of a less composed woman. She merely adjusted her bonnet, cleared her throat with a sound that seemed designed to shake the leaves from the branches, and said, in a tone of mild reproach, "I see you have found a place to sit, Clara. I trust the air is not too damp for your constitution."

Mr. Hale, who had the good sense to rise immediately, bowed with a grace that masked his surprise. "Madam," he said, "I was hoping to have the pleasure of introducing myself properly, but I find my manners have been preempted by your aunt’s arrival. I am Mr. Hale, of Oakhaven Park."

"Indeed," said Mrs. Vane, her eyes narrowing slightly as she assessed the quality of his coat and the shine of his boots. "I had heard of your name. A gentleman of some standing, I am told, in the county. Though I confess, I have always found that those with the most to lose are the most likely to seek out the company of those with the least to offer."

Clara, whose face had flushed to a hue that would have been becoming in a rose but was distressing in a human cheek, looked between them with an expression of such bewilderment that it was almost comical. She could not comprehend why her aunt, who had spent the morning complaining of the price of eggs, was now engaging in what appeared to be a diplomatic negotiation with the richest man in Wiltshire.

Mr. Hale, however, was no man to be daunted by such oblique criticism. He smiled, a smile that was equal parts charm and calculation. "Madam, I confess I am not entirely skilled in the language of your circle. If I have given you the impression that I seek to exploit the simplicity of your niece, I assure you, it is quite the reverse. It is her simplicity, her untouched goodness, that I admire most. I have no wish to rush her into a decision, but I fear that if I do not make my intentions clear, I may be forced to withdraw, and I am not prepared to bear the loss of such a companion."

Mrs. Vane paused, her fan stopping its gentle oscillation. Here was a proposition that required not rejection, but management. To forbid the match would be to invite scandal, and scandal was a currency she could not afford to spend. To accept it without condition would be to lose face, as she had arrived at the scene of the crime with the air of a guardian, not a participant. She decided, with the rapidity of a general commanding troops, to take the reins.

"Mr. Hale," she said, her voice dropping to a confidential whisper that was, in fact, loud enough for the nearby parson’s dog to hear. "I am glad to hear you speak with such sincerity. I have always believed that a union between people of different stations is most dangerous unless it is governed by the strictest rules of propriety. I am not one to stand in the way of happiness, but I am one who insists on it being conducted with order. I shall not forbid your attentions, provided, of course, that they are conducted under my supervision. I must know when you are coming, when you are leaving, and precisely what you have said. I will be the intermediary. You will call at my house, not at the cottage. You will speak to Clara only when I am present. And you will understand that I will be judging your conduct with the severest eye."

Mr. Hale looked at Clara, who was staring at her aunt as if she had suddenly grown a second head. He saw in Clara’s eyes a mix of terror and a dawning, terrified hope. He turned back to Mrs. Vane and extended his hand. "You are most considerate, Madam. I trust your judgment. I shall call tomorrow at four o’clock. I trust the tea will be strong, for I find I am in need of something to steady my nerves after such a revelation."

Mrs. Vane took his hand, gripping it with a force that suggested she intended to shake the very bones of his arm. "Four o’clock, Mr. Hale. Not a minute before. And do bring a book. I find it helps to pass the time while I observe your manners."

As they parted, Mrs. Vane walked back to her cottage with a step that was lighter than it had been in years. She had managed the crisis. She had protected her niece from the wildness of a sudden romance, and she had gained, in the process, a patron of immense wealth who would, she was certain, be grateful for her guidance. She could already envision the future: a marriage conducted with such precision that not a single stitch in the social fabric would be torn. The only slight inconvenience was that she would now have to host Mr. Hale, a man whose income alone exceeded that of the whole parish, and she was not entirely sure if her tea cups were clean enough for such a guest.

Clara, left alone with the holly trees and the fading light, sat down again on the stone bench. The mystery of her aunt’s conduct was as impenetrable as the park walls, but there was a comfort in the certainty that something had been decided. She was no longer a girl playing at love in the shadows; she was a young woman engaged in a contract of marriage, supervised by a woman who believed she knew best how to save the world. She looked up at the sky, where the stars were beginning to prick through the twilight, and thought that if this was the price of happiness, it was a high one, but perhaps, with a little patience and a great deal of tea, it might be paid. The shrubbery, which had seemed so private and safe, now felt like a stage set for a play that was only just beginning, and she wondered, with a mixture of dread and excitement, what part she would be expected to play next.
