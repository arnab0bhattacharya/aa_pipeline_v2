The letter which reached me on the morning of the fourteenth of October was written upon a paper of good quality, though the hand betrayed a certain agitation in its hurried strokes and in the frequent blots where the pen had pressed too hard upon the nib. It was dated from Ravenscar Hall, near Warkworth, in Northumberland, and ran as follows:

“Dear Sir,—I am directed by Miss Eleanor Vance to entreat your immediate assistance in a matter of the gravest urgency. My mistress arrived at Ravenscar Hall upon Tuesday last, having inherited the property upon the death of her uncle, Mr. Archibald Vance, in the spring. She finds the household—the housekeeper, the butler, and the two maids who have served the family these twenty years—in a state of sullen disobedience, and, more than this, in open assertion that she is not the person she claims to be. They point to a portrait in the great hall which they declare is the likeness of the late Mr. Vance’s daughter, who died abroad many years ago, and they say that my mistress is an impostor who has come to steal the estate. The family solicitor, Mr. Percival Grieve, of Alnwick, who alone could have silenced these calumnies, died suddenly upon the very day of our arrival, of a seizure, as the doctor says, though he was in his usual health when last seen. My mistress is nearly distracted, and dare not leave the house lest these people should bar the doors against her. I beg you to come at once, or to advise us by return of post what we should do. I am, sir, your obedient servant,

“Martha Croy, Maid to Miss Vance.”

I had no case of moment upon hand at that season, and the affair, apart from its human pathos, presented certain features which appealed to me. That a young woman should be denied her inheritance by her own servants was strange enough; but that the one man who could establish her identity should die upon the very day of her coming savoured of something more than coincidence. I despatched a telegram to say that I would come by the afternoon train, and, having packed a small valise, I found myself upon the platform at Warkworth shortly after four o’clock, with the grey North Sea fretting against the harbour wall and a keen wind blowing the sand across the links.

Ravenscar Hall stands some two miles from the village, upon a promontory of dark rock which juts out into the sea. It is a gaunt, square-built house of the last century, with tall chimneys and narrow windows, and a general air of having been planted there to watch the horizon for enemies. The sky was overcast when I reached it, and the sea, beating against the base of the cliff, sent up a dull, persistent thunder which seemed to shake the very stones of the drive.

I was admitted by a heavy-jowled man of sixty, in decent black, who announced himself as Hobbs, the butler, and who received my card with a civility that was somehow more offensive than rudeness. His eyes travelled over me with a slow, insolent scrutiny, as though he would gauge the depth of my pockets and the quality of my tailor.

“Miss Vance is expecting you, sir,” said he. “She is in the morning-room. I will show you the way.”

As we passed through the great hall—a lofty, panelled apartment, dark with the smoke of a hundred winters—I paused before the portrait which had been the cause of so much trouble. It was a full-length of a young woman in the dress of thirty years ago, painted by some hand of competence, and I confess that I started as I looked upon it. The features were refined and delicate, the eyes grey and thoughtful, the mouth firm yet sensitive; and there was that in the poise of the head and the carriage of the figure which I was to see again within the minute. Beneath the picture, upon a small brass plate, was engraved: “Eleanor Vance, 1858–1876.”

I said nothing, but followed the butler to the morning-room, where I found Miss Vance awaiting me. She was a woman of perhaps five-and-twenty, plainly dressed in mourning, with a face which was pale and drawn from want of sleep, but which was, beyond all question, the face of the portrait in the hall. The resemblance was not merely general; it was exact in every line, save that the living woman had the fuller cheek and the more confident glance of health and youth.

“You have seen the picture,” said she, as I entered. “They have made you look at it, I suppose. Well, Mr. Holmes, I am not afraid of the picture. I am Eleanor Vance, the daughter of Archibald Vance’s brother, and I have come from Australia to claim what is mine. My uncle acknowledged me in his letters; he sent me money for my passage; and now that he is dead, these people tell me that I am an adventuress, and that the real Eleanor died in Florence in 1876.”

“Have you any proofs of your identity?” I asked.

“I have my father’s letters, my own passport, and the certificate of my birth at Melbourne. But they will not look at them. They say that such things can be bought, and that the only evidence they will accept is the word of Mr. Grieve, who is dead.”

“And the doctor who attended Mr. Grieve?”

“He is an old man, Dr. Thurlow, who has known the family for years. He says that Mr. Grieve died of heart disease, and that there is no reason to suspect anything else. But he is a friend of Hobbs, and I do not trust him.”

I asked leave to examine the servants, and Miss Vance, with a gesture of weariness, rang the bell. They came in one by one—Hobbs, the butler; Mrs. Tredgold, the housekeeper, a thin, acid woman with folded lips; and the two maids, who stood together near the door and said nothing. Their story was always the same: that the real Miss Eleanor had been a delicate child, who had been taken abroad for her health and had died at Florence; that the master had never been quite himself since; that he had spoken of his niece coming home, but that they had always understood it was a fancy; and that when this woman arrived, with her colonial manners and her strange ways, they had recognised at once that she was an impostor.

“And yet,” said I, “the likeness is undeniable.”

“The likeness is a trick,” said Mrs. Tredgold, with a toss of her head. “There are people in the world who can make themselves like anybody, for a consideration.”

I asked whether Mr. Grieve had seen Miss Vance before his death. They said that he had not; that he had been taken ill upon the very morning of her arrival, before he could be brought to the Hall; and that he had died in the afternoon without recovering consciousness.

“Who was with him when he died?”

“Dr. Thurlow and his own clerk, a young man named Sadler.”

“And where is Sadler now?”

“He has gone to Newcastle, sir, upon the business of the estate.”

I made a note of the name, and then, having dismissed the servants, I turned to Miss Vance.

“You have, I think, a photograph of your father?”

She produced it from her writing-case—a faded carte-de-visite, taken in Melbourne twenty years before. I looked at it long and earnestly, and then I asked for the letters which her uncle had written to her. They were in a firm, clear hand, and spoke of the estate, of the servants, and of his hope that she would come home. In one of them, dated only three months before his death, he said: “I have told Grieve to expect you, and he will have all the papers ready. I have also spoken to Thurlow, who will attend you if you are ill. The servants are old and set in their ways, but they will obey you when they know that you are indeed my niece.”

“You observe,” said I, “that your uncle makes no mention of the portrait, or of the dead child. That is significant. If the story of the servants were true, he would surely have warned you.”

“I have thought of that,” she answered. “But what can I do? The man who could prove that I am not the daughter of the dead Eleanor is dead, and the man who could prove that I am is also dead.”

“Not so,” said I. “The man who could prove it is Dr. Thurlow, if he chose; and the man who could prove the contrary is the clerk, Sadler, who was present at Mr. Grieve’s death. I will see them both before I sleep.”

I found Dr. Thurlow at his house in the village—a stout, red-faced man, with a manner that was hearty to the point of offensiveness. He repeated what he had said before: that Mr. Grieve had died of heart disease, that there was no reason to suspect foul play, and that he had never seen Miss Vance until after the solicitor’s death.

“And yet,” said I, “you were the family doctor. You must have known the daughter Eleanor.”

“I knew her as a child,” said he, “before she went abroad. She was a delicate little thing, with fair hair and blue eyes.”

“The portrait in the hall has grey eyes,” said I.

He hesitated for a moment. “The portrait was painted when she was older,” he said. “Children’s eyes change.”

“They do not change from blue to grey,” said I. “And the portrait is dated 1858, which would make her a child of ten. It is the portrait of a woman of twenty.”

He had no answer for that, and I left him with the impression of a man who was either very stupid or very much afraid.

Sadler, the clerk, I found at the office in Alnwick, a young man of pale complexion and nervous manner, who started when I mentioned the name of Grieve.

“I have come to ask you,” said I, “whether Mr. Grieve said anything before he died.”

He looked at me with a frightened air. “He said nothing, sir. He was insensible from the first.”

“And yet,” said I, “you were with him for six hours. You must have seen something.”

He was silent for a moment, and then he said, in a low voice: “I saw Dr. Thurlow give him an injection. He said it was to stimulate the heart. But Mr. Grieve was already dead.”

“You are sure of that?”

“I am sure, sir. I felt his pulse, and there was none. And yet the doctor said he was alive, and gave him the injection. And then he said he was dead.”

“And you said nothing?”

“What could I say, sir? I am a poor clerk, and Dr. Thurlow is a great man in these parts.”

I returned to Ravenscar Hall that evening with the threads of the case in my hand. The servants had lied about the portrait; the doctor had lied about the daughter; and the solicitor had been murdered to prevent him from speaking. The motive was plain enough: the estate was a valuable one, and the servants, with the doctor’s connivance, had hoped to keep it in their own hands by denying the heiress. But they had reckoned without the portrait, which they could not destroy without suspicion, and without the letters, which they did not know existed.
