The news arrived with the post, a single line in a spidery hand that turned the morning’s quiet order to dust. It was a note from Miss Vance, the music mistress, who wrote only that she could no longer continue her duties at Blackwood Hall and begged Mrs. Blackwood’s forgiveness. The letter slipped from Eleanor Blackwood’s fingers, landing on the polished mahogany of her desk beside the ledger of household accounts. The ticking of the longcase clock in the hall suddenly seemed unnaturally loud, each second a hammer blow against the fragile edifice of her life’s work.

It could only mean one thing. For a week, there had been a subtle discord in the school, a whispering and a rustling of silk whenever Miss Vance’s name was mentioned. Little Clara Gable, the daughter of Mr. Josiah Gable, had been the centre of it all. She had taken to sighing over her embroidery and staring out of the window with the tragic air of a heroine in a penny dreadful. Eleanor had dismissed it as the usual vapours of a sixteen-year-old, a passing fancy to be corrected with a brisk walk and a dose of salts. She had been a fool.

The realisation settled not as a shock, but as a cold, heavy weight in her stomach. The music master. Not old Mr. Hemlock who taught the pianoforte with such tedious precision, but the new one, Mr. Dubois, with his dark, brooding eyes and his French accent that made the girls blush and fidget. He had been hired for the summer term to provide a touch of modern sophistication. He had provided a catastrophe.

Eleanor rose and moved to the window, her hand resting on the cool glass. Below, the manicured lawns of Blackwood Hall sloped down to the town of Ashworth. She had built this school from the ashes of her widowhood, from the respectable pension left by her late husband, Arthur. It was a place of order, of moral instruction, of gentle accomplishments. She had poured every ounce of her strength, her savings, her reputation into it. And now, a girl with more sentiment than sense and a man with a fiddle and a pocketful of empty promises had threatened to tear it all down.

The clock struck ten. Mr. Gable was due at eleven. There was no time for despair, only for preparation. She summoned her housekeeper, Mrs. Plimpton, a woman whose face was a permanent mask of disapproval.

“Mrs. Plimpton,” Eleanor said, her voice steady, betraying none of the turmoil within. “Miss Gable and Miss Vance have left the school. They are not to be spoken of. See that the girls’ afternoon schedule is adjusted accordingly. The French conversation circle can take the music room.”

Mrs. Plimpton’s eyes widened a fraction, the only sign of her astonishment. “Left, madam?”

“Eloped, I believe. The details are not for discussion.” Eleanor’s tone was final. “And please, lay out my black silk. The one with the jet buttons.”

The hour that followed was a blur of quiet, frantic activity. Eleanor walked the halls, her posture ramrod straight, her gaze sweeping over the familiar scenes: the neat rows of desks in the classroom, the girls bent over their needlework in the parlour, the sunlight catching the dust motes dancing in the air. She was a general inspecting her troops before a battle she was certain to lose, but she would not surrender without a fight. Every polished floorboard, every well-tended plant, every girl in her crisp white pinafore was a testament to the order she had created. She would not let one act of reckless passion define it.

At precisely eleven o’clock, the heavy brass knocker of the front door sounded, a sound as final as a judge’s gavel. Eleanor took a deep breath, smoothed the front of her silk gown, and went to meet him.

Mr. Josiah Gable filled the doorway. He was a man built like one of his own steam engines: broad, powerful, and radiating a sense of inexorable force. His face was florid, his eyes small and sharp, missing nothing. He smelled of coal smoke and money.

“Mrs. Blackwood,” he boomed, not waiting for an invitation before striding into the hall. “I trust you have some explanation for this… this disgrace.”

Eleanor led him into her private sitting room, a place of quiet femininity with its chintz curtains and delicate watercolours. He seemed to shrink the room with his presence, his heavy boots sinking into the Aubusson carpet.

“Please, Mr. Gable, be seated,” she said, gesturing to a chair. She remained standing, a deliberate choice. She would not be put on the defensive by sitting.

He ignored the offer, pacing before the fireplace. “My daughter! My Clara! Gone! Vanished with some fiddling nobody! And I have to hear of it from a neighbour who saw their carriage on the Ashworth road at dawn! I hold you responsible, madam. Utterly and completely responsible.”

“Your distress is entirely understandable, Mr. Gable,” Eleanor began, her voice calm and measured. “I share it. Miss Gable was a valued pupil.”

“Valued?” he spat. “She was my daughter! And she was entrusted to your care! For a king’s ransom, I might add. I paid for her to learn deportment, French, the pianoforte. Not… not this!”

“Indeed,” Eleanor said, her gaze unwavering. “You paid for her to be educated in the finest traditions of a young lady’s seminary. And I have provided that. I cannot, however, police the heart of a young woman determined to be reckless.”

“Cannot? Or would not? It was under your roof! With your staff! This music master… he was your employee!”

“He was engaged to provide instruction, Mr. Gable, not to act as a chaperone. His character was presented as impeccable. His references were from the conservatoire in London. I can assure you, his deception was as much a shock to me as it is to you.” She allowed a note of steel to enter her voice. “The fault, sir, lies not with my school, but with a man who chose to abuse his position and a young lady who chose to encourage him.”

Gable stopped his pacing and glared at her, his face purpling. “You dare to blame my child? She is a victim! And you are a fraud. A respectable school! Hah! I’ll ruin you. I’ll see to it that every family in this county knows that Blackwood Hall is a den of iniquity, a place where girls are spirited away by vagabonds. My influence reaches far beyond Ashworth, madam.”

Eleanor felt a tremor of fear, but she held her ground. This was the moment. She had to meet his bluster with a force of her own.

“You may do as you see fit, Mr. Gable,” she said, her voice dropping, becoming more intense. “But consider what you will achieve. You will not find your daughter by destroying the reputation of the woman who last saw her. If you make this a public scandal, you will succeed only in ensuring that your daughter is not merely an eloped girl, but a ruined one. Her name will be a byword for disgrace. You will have closed the door to her return.”

She took a step closer to him, her eyes locked on his. “I have already dispatched a discreet inquiry to her aunt in Derbyshire, the one she so often spoke of. It is my belief that is where they have gone. I am prepared to help you find her. Quietly. But I will not have my school, my life’s work, sacrificed to your anger.”

Gable stared at her, his chest heaving. The fight seemed to drain out of him, replaced by a dawning, desperate calculation. He saw the logic in her words. A public scandal would be a stain on his own name, a stain on the Gable manufactories. His business partners were men of rigid morality. A disgraced daughter would be a liability.

“You… you would help me?” he asked, the bluster gone from his voice, leaving only a father’s raw anxiety.

“I would,” Eleanor said. “My primary concern is for Miss Gable’s safety and, if possible, her future. But my school must be protected. I will not have it said that I am negligent. I will have it said that I was a victim of the same deception as you.”

They stood in silence for a long moment. The ticking of the mantel clock was the only sound. Gable looked at the woman before him, seeing not a flustered widow but a formidable adversary, a woman of iron will disguised in silk and lace. He had come to bully and threaten, but he found himself in the presence of a force equal to his own.

“Very well, Mrs. Blackwood,” he said, his voice low and grudging. “We will do this your way. For now. But if my daughter is not found…”

“She will be,” Eleanor said with a confidence she did not entirely feel. “Now, let us sit, and I will tell you what I know. We have a great deal of work to do.”

As he finally took the offered chair, Eleanor felt a profound exhaustion wash over her. The battle was not over, but the first, most crucial skirmish had been won. The respectability of Blackwood Hall, fragile as a porcelain teacup, had, for the moment, been preserved. She poured the tea, her hand perfectly steady, and began to plan.
