The sea, which had given up its dead with a certain grim reluctance, now offered the living nothing but its own immense and unmeaning continuity. We had been three days in the boat, and the sun, a disc of molten brass, had beaten down upon us with a persistence that was less a heat than a physical pressure, a weight upon the very substance of our thoughts. It flattened the water into a sheet of hammered pewter, upon which our little craft, a mere chip of wood and resin, seemed an impertinence, a temporary and fragile assertion of human will against the vast, indifferent calm of the Java Sea.

In the stern, propped upon a heap of sodden sailcloth, lay the captain. He was not dead, not yet; but the vitality of life had retreated from the extremities of his being, leaving his body a mere husk, a vessel for the slow, inexorable process of its own dissolution. His face, which had once held the ruddy confidence of command, was the colour of old tallow, and his eyes, when they opened, were filmed and fixed upon some point in the terrible emptiness that surrounded us. He had not spoken since the first day, when he had mumbled something of his wife in Portsmouth, a name that now hung in the air like the ghost of a forgotten sound. His silence was the central fact of our predicament. The authority that should have guided us had become a mere weight of dying flesh, a silent, breathing testament to the failure of all authority.

For the rest of us, the world had contracted to the length of this boat and the circumference of the horizon. There was Mr. Calloway, the mate, a man of London and its hard certainties, whose face, beneath a stubble of beard, was a mask of pragmatic calculation. His hands, chapped and raw, were never still; they tested the tension of a rope, adjusted a thwart, or counted, with a grim ritual, the remaining drops of water in the cask. And there was the bosun, a Swede named Larsen, whose soul seemed as vast and featureless as the ocean itself. He sat in the bow, his great, fair head bowed, his blue eyes lost in the water, a man of the sea, for whom the land was a mere interruption of the true life of the waves.

The decision, which had been a matter of urgent debate on the first day, had now curdled into a silent, festering antagonism between the two men. It was a question of geography, of maps and distances, but it had become a question of faith. To the west, Calloway insisted, lay the coast of Java, a known quantity. To be sure, the last trading vessels had brought whispers of trouble in the settlements there—a restlessness among the natives, a simmering hatred of the white man’s presence. But it was land. It was water and shade and the possibility of rescue. To the east, a week’s pull or more if the winds turned against us, lay the port of Batavia, a city of law and commerce, of solid quays and European faces.

“Fifty miles, Larsen,” Calloway had said on the second day, his voice a dry rasp. “A day’s pull, perhaps two. You know the currents here. You know them better than any man. We could be ashore by tomorrow’s sunset.”

The Swede had not looked at him. He had only continued to stare into the water, his silence a form of profound disagreement. Finally, he had spoken, his English slow and guttural. “The Dutch ships… they say the men in that village have knives. Many knives. They say the headman has sworn an oath on a white man’s skull.”

“And the port is a hundred and fifty miles!” Calloway had retorted, his composure cracking. “We have water for four days, perhaps five. We have no food. We would die of thirst long before we saw the masts of Batavia harbour. It is a gamble on our lives.”

“It is a certainty of our deaths,” Larsen had rumbled, his gaze finally lifting to fix upon the mate. “To go to those who wait with knives. The sea is cruel, but it is not wicked. It does not hate.”

Since then, they had not spoken of it. The argument had become an invisible wall between them. Calloway would look west, his eyes narrowed against the glare, his mind wrestling with the arithmetic of survival. Larsen would look east, his expression one of fatalistic acceptance, as if he were waiting for the sea to make the decision for him. I, the third mate, a man of no great consequence in the ship’s hierarchy or in this one, was caught between them, a creature of observation, seeing the logic in Calloway’s desperate arithmetic and feeling the truth in the Swede’s elemental dread.

This afternoon, the silence had acquired a new quality. It was heavier, charged with a terrible expectation. The captain had begun to breathe in shallow, rattling gasps, and a fleck of foam, pink with blood, appeared at the corner of his mouth. His time was coming. The end of the man who would not choose was upon us, and the choice would then fall to us with a final, crushing weight.

It was Calloway who broke the stillness. He crawled aft, his movements stiff and careful, and knelt by the captain’s side. He did not touch him, but leaned close, as if to impart some confidence to the failing spirit.

“Captain,” he said, his voice low but insistent. “The wind is shifting. It will be from the west by morning. A good wind for the coast. It is the only chance.”

The captain’s eyelids fluttered. A tremor passed through his frame, and for a moment, his eyes seemed to clear, to fix upon Calloway’s face with a spark of the old authority. His lips moved, forming a word, but no sound came forth. It was a desperate, silent plea, a final command to be absolved of the command that was now impossible to give. Then the light in his eyes went out, and he was once more a mere body, breathing its last.

Calloway drew back, a look of profound frustration on his face. He had sought a decision, a word, a sign, and had received only the empty gaze of a dying man. He turned to Larsen, who had watched the scene from the bow, his expression unchanged.

“You see?” Calloway said, his voice hard with conviction. “He is finished. The decision is ours. We make for the coast. We must.”

Larsen shook his head slowly, a gesture of immense and weary finality. “No,” he said. “We wait for the wind to choose for us. If it is from the west, we go west. If it is from the east, we go east. We do not choose. We obey.”

“That is madness!” Calloway hissed. “That is suicide! We are men, not driftwood!”

“Are we?” the Swede asked, his gaze sweeping the empty horizon. “Out here, I think we are very much the same.”

I looked from one to the other, at the fanatic certainty of the mate and the bleak fatalism of the bosun. The captain lay between them, a silent, useless arbiter. The sun was beginning to dip towards the west, its light turning the pewter sea to a sheet of copper and gold. A faint breath of air, a mere sigh of the atmosphere, touched my cheek. It was from the east. It was a whisper, a nothing, but it carried with it the whole weight of our fate. The decision had not been made by us. It had been made for us by the vast, impassive indifference of the world. And as the boat began to drift, imperceptibly, towards the west, I understood that the captain, in his refusal, had been the most honest man among us. He had known that the choice was an illusion.
