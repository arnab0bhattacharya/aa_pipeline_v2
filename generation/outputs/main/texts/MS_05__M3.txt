It was in the autumn of my eighteenth year that the heavy mists of Hampshire began to gather with a peculiar and mournful significance around the ancient turrets of Lynwood Park. I had known no other home since that bleak and tempestuous day when my benefactor, Mr. Marchmont, had plucked me from the desolate shores of my native Cornwall—an orphan, destitute and forsaken—and brought me to reside beneath his roof as the adopted child of his house. For nine years I had been treated with a kindness so unwavering, a tenderness so profound, that I had almost forgotten the bitter circumstances of my birth and had come to look upon the lady of the manor, Mrs. Marchmont, as a mother. I was educated with their daughter Clara, shared her studies and her recreations, and was taught to believe that my presence was a blessing to them rather than a burden. But the illusion was destined to be shattered, and the gentle sleep of my security was to be rudely broken by the very passions which, in their excess, prove how frail a thing is human happiness.

The son of the family, Edward Marchmont, had been absent during my earlier years at Lynwood, pursuing his education upon the Continent, and it was not until the summer of my eighteenth year that he returned to his ancestral home. He was a young man of ardent temperament and elevated mind, possessed of a countenance that bespoke both the nobility of his sentiments and the melancholy which seemed the inheritance of his race. From the first hour of his arrival, a deep and sympathetic understanding sprang up between us—a communion of thought and feeling that needed not the tedious aids of commonplace conversation, but flowed onward, swift and clear, like some mountain stream that has found its natural channel. We read together, we wandered together through the dim and venerable avenues of the park, and in the silence of those long autumnal evenings, when the wind moaned among the ancient elms and the rain beat its melancholy summons against the casement, I felt my soul drawn to his by ties that I dared not name even to myself.

But I was blind—wilfully, foolishly blind—to the consequences that must needs flow from so dangerous an attachment. Mrs. Marchmont had long since formed designs for her son far grander than any which a penniless ward could fulfil. She had looked, with the calculating ambition of a mother whose affections are interwoven with the most worldly considerations, towards an alliance with the house of Sir Godfrey Walsingham, whose daughter, the fair and accomplished Emmeline, would bring to her son not merely a princely dowry but connections of the highest consequence in the county. That Edward should bestow his heart upon one who could bring him nothing but her love—that he should prefer the obscure and dependent Clara to the radiant heiress of Walsingham Park—was a prospect at once insupportable and alarming to her maternal pride.

The change in her demeanour towards me was at first so gradual, so exquisitely wrought, that I could scarcely determine whether it were the creature of my own suspicious fancy or a genuine alteration in her sentiments. She would speak to me, as she had ever done, with that soft and silver tone which had once been the music of my childhood; but there was now an undercurrent of meaning in her words, a veiled and melancholy emphasis, which struck a chill to my bosom. "My dear Clara," she would say, as she seated herself beside me with an air of plaintive resignation, "you must not think me unkind if I sometimes speak to you of the obligations under which you labour. You are sensible, I know, of all that my husband has done for you—how he rescued you from poverty and obscurity, and has brought you up as his own child. It is a heavy debt, Clara, and one that can only be repaid by the most scrupulous attention to what is due to us, and to the expectations we have formed." And then she would sigh, and press my hand with a pressure that was meant to convey affection, but which I felt rather as the grasp of some invisible chain that was slowly, inexorably tightening around me.

Day after day, and hour after hour, did these gentle but unceasing reminders pursue me. If Edward and I were observed in conversation—if he lingered by my side at the pianoforte, or walked with me apart from the rest of the family in the garden—Mrs. Marchmont would seek me out ere long, and with a countenance expressive of the most touching sorrow, would remind me of my station, of my dependence, of the sacred duty I owed to those who had been as parents to me. "You know, my dear girl," she murmured one evening, as the last red rays of a stormy sunset fell upon her pale and beautiful face, "how much Edward is to all of us—how bright are the prospects we have cherished for his future. It would be cruel—cruel in the extreme—were you, by any encouragement of his partiality, to mar the fair edifice of his fortunes. You who have received so much at our hands should be the last to occasion us sorrow. Indeed, Clara, I have sometimes feared that your influence over him is more than it ought to be—and I must beg you, as you value our continued regard, to be watchful of yourself."

Her words were spoken with such an appearance of gentle reasonableness, of maternal solicitude, that I could frame no reply save the tears that sprang unbidden to my eyes; and yet each syllable was as a dagger plunged into my heart, turning and twisting in the wound. For I knew now, with a terrible and assured knowledge, that I loved Edward Marchmont—that I loved him with all the depth and fervour of a nature which had known little enough of earthly happiness, and which clung to the image of his goodness as the drowning mariner clings to a spar in the tempestuous sea. And I knew, too, that he loved me—that the glances he cast at me across the dinner table, the tones of his voice when he read aloud to us those passages of poetry which seemed to speak our mutual thoughts, were the genuine utterances of a passion as deep and as hopeless as my own. But between us stood the cold and impenetrable barrier of my dependence—the cruel consciousness that every morsel I ate, every garment I wore, was the gift of his parents, and that to encourage his addresses would be to violate the most sacred obligations of gratitude.

The struggle within my bosom was violent beyond description. On the one hand, my heart cried out for Edward with an anguish that partook of the very madness of despair; on the other, conscience and duty, armed with the stern and admonitory voice of Mrs. Marchmont, bade me renounce him, and, by renouncing, prove the sincerity of my affection. I retired to my chamber, and, flinging myself upon my knees beside the narrow casement where the autumn wind made mournful music, I wept such tears as I had not wept since the day when I was first torn from my father's grave. The moon shone coldly through the rifted clouds upon the wide and desolate heath that stretched away towards the dark hills, and the solitary cry of some night bird came borne upon the gale, as if it, too, lamented its loneliness. "O God," I exclaimed, "why hast Thou placed me in a station where the best affections of my heart must become my curse? Why hast Thou shown me happiness only to withdraw it, and bound me by ties which make my very virtues the instruments of my torture?" Such were the wild and impious lamentations that escaped my lips in the agony of that long and sleepless night—I, who had been the pride and joy of the Marchmont household, now felt myself an alien and an intruder, trembling on the precipice of a fate I could neither avert nor contemplate with calmness.

The next morning, pale and hollow-eyed, I descended to the breakfast parlour with a heart that beat with sickening apprehension. Edward was there, and as our eyes met, I saw in his the same sleepless trouble, the same unspoken appeal, that had rendered my own night so terrible. Mrs. Marchmont sat at the head of the table, her countenance wearing that serene and composed expression which concealed, beneath its placid surface, a purpose as inflexible as iron. She addressed me with her usual suavity, inquired after my health with an appearance of tender interest, and then, with a glance towards her son, observed, in a tone of melancholy reflection, that she trusted I would not soon forget the conversation of the preceding evening—that she had spoken, she knew, with unwonted severity, but that it was the severity of affection, of one who had watched over me from childhood and who desired above all things to see me act with that propriety which the peculiar circumstances of my situation demanded. I bowed in silence, for speech was denied me; and Edward, who had listened with visible emotion, turned away his head, and I saw that his hand trembled as he raised the cup to his lips.
