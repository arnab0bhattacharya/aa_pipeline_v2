The fire had burned low, and the last of the daylight, a wan and sickly grey, was failing over the shoulders of the hills, when they came to me—all of them, the whole remnant of our little world, some twenty souls whose faces I had learned to read as I read the sky for signs of storm. They stood in a half-circle before the door of the old mill-house where we had made our shelter, and their spokesman, a man named Harrow who had been a schoolmaster in the days when there were still schools, put their errand plainly enough. They would have me for their leader. I, who had never in my life commanded so much as a household, who had come to this valley a fugitive with no more than the clothes upon my back and a heart sickened by the desolation of the cities, was to be set above them all, to hold their lives in my keeping, to say whether we should stay and perish slowly in the place we knew, or go north into the unknown, toward a coast I had never seen, on the strength of a rumour borne by a dying man.

I beseech you to believe that I refused them. I pleaded my youth, my inexperience, the weakness of a frame that had never been equal to the labours of the field; I wept—I am not ashamed to confess it—I wept before them all, and besought them to choose some other, some elder, some stronger spirit, one whose word would carry the weight of years. But they were resolute in their folly, or in their hope, which is the same thing seen from another side; and Harrow, with a gentleness that cut me more deeply than any reproach, said that it was because I had never sought the office that they pressed it upon me, and that the one among them who had no hunger for power was the only one fit to be trusted with it. So I bowed my head, and the weight descended upon me, and I knew that henceforth I could not walk nor eat nor sleep but that the shadow of their expectation lay across my path.

That night I could not close my eyes. I rose and went out beneath the stars, and the valley lay before me in the moonlight, so still, so fair, so terrible in its indifference. There was the river, silvered and silent, winding among the meadows; there the dark woods upon the farther slope; there the broken spire of the church where we had laid the dead of the first winter, and where the yew trees, unpruned for a generation, had grown into strange and mournful shapes. All this I loved with an agony of love, for it was the last home of my people, the last habitation of anything human that I had known. And yet—I cannot hide it from myself—there was a canker at the heart of it. The sickness had passed us by, but it had left behind a famine that crept upon us year by year; the soil was weary, the game was scant, and I had seen the children grow thin and listless, and the old men sit with vacant eyes, waiting for a spring that brought no plenty. To remain was to die by inches, in the place we loved, among the graves of our fathers; to go was to abandon all certainty, to cast ourselves upon a rumour, to trust the lives of these innocents to the mercy of a wilderness that had swallowed up the whole world.

Oh, the bitterness of that hour! I thought of my mother, who had perished in the city, alone, among strangers; I thought of the faces of the little ones who looked to me for bread, and of the fierce and terrible responsibility of saying, This way, and no other, when the way was dark and the end unseen. Had I been a man, I think I should have felt it less; but I was a woman, and my nature yearned over them with a tenderness that unmanned me, that made me long to gather them all in my arms and hold them back from every peril, even at the cost of my own life. And yet I knew that tenderness without wisdom is a cruel thing, and that to keep them in safety for a little while and then to watch them perish was a betrayal worse than any hazard of the road.

The wind rose in the night, and the clouds drove across the moon, and the valley was blotted out in darkness. In that darkness I seemed to see the northern coast, of which the dying man had spoken with his last breath—a place of grey cliffs and sounding seas, where, he said, there were still houses standing empty, and boats, and the fish in the waters, and the salt wind that carried no contagion. It might be a vision of hope, or it might be the delusion of a fevered brain; I had no means of knowing. But as I stood there, the storm gathering above me and the river murmuring below, there came upon me a strange and solemn calm, such as I had not felt since the day I first entered the valley. I felt that I was not alone; that the same Power which had upheld the earth when the cities fell, which had bade the sun to shine upon the graves of the dead and the cradles of the living, was with me still, and would not suffer me to be wholly lost, even though I should err. It was not pride that filled me then, nor confidence in my own judgment, but a humble and awful sense that I was a creature among creatures, bound by the same laws, subject to the same doom, and that the courage which I lacked would be given me, not because I deserved it, but because it was demanded of me by the love I bore these people.

I went back to the mill-house at dawn, and I found them already stirring, and I saw in their faces the question they dared not speak. And I said to them: We will go north. We will go while there is yet strength in us to go, and we will carry with us the memory of this valley, and the graves of our dead, and the hope that is stronger than fear. I did not tell them of the trembling of my heart, nor of the long nights of doubt that I foresaw; for a leader must bear her own burden in silence, and give to others only the light by which they may walk. But when they raised their voices in that poor and broken cheer which is the only thanksgiving of the wretched, I turned away my face, and I wept; and I prayed, if prayer be permitted to one so erring, that I might not lead them into a worse desolation than that from which we fled, and that, if I must fail, I might fail last, and bear the blame alone.
