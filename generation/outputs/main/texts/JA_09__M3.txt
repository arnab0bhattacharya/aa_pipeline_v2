Mr. Henry Courtenay, of Courtenay Park in Derbyshire, was in possession of seven thousand pounds a year, a robust constitution, and an unshakable conviction that he required a wife. As he was nearly five-and-thirty, and his housekeeper had lately given notice, the matter had begun to press upon him with some urgency. A man of his fortune and expectations might look anywhere, but Mr. Courtenay, having a strong partiality for his own judgment, preferred to look within his own family. His cousin, the Reverend Charles Eversleigh, of Hayworth Parsonage in Northamptonshire, possessed three daughters, and it was to Hayworth that Mr. Courtenay directed his travelling carriage one clear morning in late September.

The Eversleighs were prepared. Mrs. Eversleigh had spent the better part of a fortnight in adjusting caps, altering muslins, and counselling her two elder daughters on the proper arrangement of their features. Catherine, who was one-and-twenty, received these instructions with becoming gravity; Anne, who was nineteen, received them with a trembling eagerness that rather betrayed the absence of genuine composure. Lydia, who was fifteen, and whose figure was not yet quite settled into the graces of womanhood, received no instructions at all, her mother having long since discovered that instructions to Lydia were an incitement rather than a restraint.

Mr. Courtenay arrived with the ease of a man who expects to be well received, and was, in fact, well received. He was not handsome, his countenance bearing the ruddy impress of much port wine and outdoor exercise, but he had an air of decided consequence, and his clothes were of the finest cloth. Mrs. Eversleigh thought him everything that was proper. Catherine thought him rather stout. Anne thought him magnificent. Lydia, who had been watching from the staircase window, thought he looked exceedingly like the pompous baronet in the novel she had concealed beneath her prayer-book that very morning.

At dinner, Mr. Courtenay surveyed his field of action with quiet approbation. Mrs. Eversleigh had placed Catherine at his right hand, and Anne directly opposite, where the candlelight fell becomingly upon her complexion. Lydia sat at the far end of the table, a situation her mother had chosen with considerable strategic care, and which Lydia entirely subverted by leaning forward with such attention that she might have been seated among them.

"You will find the country about Hayworth very pleasant, I dare say, Mr. Courtenay," said Mrs. Eversleigh, with a smile of elegant insipidity. "We have some pretty drives, and the neighbourhood is, upon the whole, quite sociable."

"I am no great judge of pretty drives," replied Mr. Courtenay. "I am more accustomed to inspecting the condition of fences than admiring prospects. A well-maintained estate is, in my view, the finest landscape in England."

"How very improving," said Lydia, in a tone of such mild gravity that her mother started, and Catherine applied her napkin to her lips with unnecessary earnestness. "I suppose, Mr. Courtenay, you find the inspection of fences so absorbing that you have no attention left for the people who live behind them."

Mr. Courtenay turned his head with some surprise in the direction of this small, clear voice. Lydia met his gaze with an expression of perfect composure, her dark eyes bright with an intelligence that was not, strictly speaking, necessary at the dinner table.

"I am not sure I quite understand you, Miss Lydia," said he.

"It is perfectly simple," returned she. "A man who loves fences must, of necessity, be rather indifferent to human beings, for human beings are perpetually leaning on fences and breaking them. I conclude you find your tenants a great nuisance."

Mr. Courtenay laughed, a sound that was unusual enough from him to draw a glance of astonishment even from the Reverend Mr. Eversleigh, who had been peacefully engaged with his partridge.

"You are very severe upon me," said Mr. Courtenay. "I hope I am not quite so hard-hearted as you suggest. I have a very tolerable regard for my tenants, provided they pay their rent and do not keep more dogs than they can afford."

"And what, in your estimation, is the proper number of dogs for a man to afford?" enquired Lydia, with an air of genuine inquiry.

"Lydia!" murmured Mrs. Eversleigh, in a voice of gentle anguish.

Mr. Courtenay, however, appeared to find no cause for anguish. "I should say one," he replied, "and that one a spaniel. A man who keeps a hound is inviting his own ruin."

"Then you would condemn the whole of Northamptonshire," cried Lydia. "Every farmer here keeps at least two, and the squire has five. You must find us a very extravagant county."

"I find it a very interesting county," said Mr. Courtenay, and looked at Lydia with an attention that was not lost upon Catherine, who began to feel a slight uneasiness which she could not immediately account for.

In the drawing-room after dinner, Mrs. Eversleigh endeavoured to repair the evening's direction by seating herself near Mr. Courtenay and engaging him in a careful survey of her elder daughters' accomplishments. Catherine played the pianoforte with more correctness than feeling, and Anne sang a little, in a voice that was agreeable though faint. Mr. Courtenay listened with civility, pronounced the music charming, and then, upon Mrs. Eversleigh's leaving the room for a moment to speak with the housekeeper, turned abruptly to Catherine and said, "Your younger sister is very lively."

Catherine, who had been turning the pages of a music book, paused. "Lydia is very young," she said. "She is not always — I mean, she is sometimes rather — "

"Rather what?" said Mr. Courtenay, with an appearance of interest that Catherine found wholly disproportionate to the subject.

"Rather inconsiderate in what she says," Catherine managed, with a blush.

"I should call it refreshing," said Mr. Courtenay. "I have spent a great deal of my life among people who never say what they mean. I find Miss Lydia's directness quite — quite remarkable."

Catherine said nothing, but closed the music book with a firmness that was perhaps greater than the occasion required.

The following days established a pattern that Mrs. Eversleigh observed with increasing bewilderment. Mr. Courtenay, who had been expected to devote his mornings to Catherine and his evenings to Anne, appeared instead to devote a considerable portion of both to Lydia. He walked with her in the garden, where she informed him that the rose bushes were diseased and the gravel path uneven. He rode with her to the village, where she pointed out the defects of every cottage they passed. He sat beside her at breakfast, where she told him that his tea was too strong and his toast too cold, and that a man of his consequence ought to employ a better cook.

Anne was in tears at least twice a day. Catherine preserved a steady silence that might have been mistaken for philosophy, had it not been for the frequency with which she gazed from the window and sighed. Mrs. Eversleigh, in a private conference with her husband, confessed herself wholly at a loss.

"I cannot comprehend it," said she. "She is a child. She has no accomplishments, no elegance, and her manners are — well, you have seen them, Mr. Eversleigh. She told him yesterday that his carriage was out of fashion. What sort of encouragement can that be to a man?"

The Reverend Mr. Eversleigh, who was a man of more penetration than his wife generally credited him with, looked up from his sermon and said, with a quiet smile, "I rather think, my dear, that it is precisely the sort of encouragement Mr. Courtenay has been wanting. A man who has spent thirty-five years being agreed with is perhaps not ill-pleased to meet with contradiction."

Mrs. Eversleigh was silenced, but not convinced.

On the fifth evening of Mr. Courtenay's visit, Lydia was standing by the drawing-room window, observing the moonlight upon the lawn, when Mr. Courtenay approached her with a manner that was, for him, almost diffident.

"Miss Lydia," said he, "I am to leave Hayworth on Thursday."

"Are you indeed?" said Lydia, without turning from the window. "I hope your fences have not suffered in your absence."

"My fences are very well," said he, "but I find I am not quite so well. I have been considering a matter of some importance, and I find I cannot settle it without your assistance."

"Mine?" said Lydia, now turning to look at him with genuine surprise. "I cannot think what assistance I could possibly offer. I know nothing of estate management."

"It is not a question of estate management. It is a question of — of judgment. I have a house in Derbyshire which is in want of a mistress, and I find myself unable to fix upon any candidate, unless — " He paused, and the pause was so uncharacteristic that Lydia regarded him with something approaching alarm. "Unless the young lady who has so freely censured my carriage, my cook, and my character, would consent to undertake the office herself."

Lydia stared at him. The colour rose in her cheeks with a swiftness that entirely undid the composure she had cultivated for fifteen years. For the first time in her life, she appeared to have nothing whatsoever to say, and Mr. Courtenay, who had never before found himself in the company of a silent woman, found the experience so singular and so agreeable that he took her hand and pressed it with a warmth that left no room for misunderstanding.
