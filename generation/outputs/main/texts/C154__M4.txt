My dear Father,

I write to you from a city that roars. That is the only word for it, and even that is a poor, thin word. The roar does not stop. It is not the clean, sudden thunder of the high passes, which rolls and is gone. This is a continuous, grinding bellow, a sound made of a thousand thousand smaller sounds all crushed together: the iron-shod clatter of the great horse-carts on the stone, the shriek of the steam-engines that drag their long black tails through the streets, the ceaseless, shuffling tide of men’s feet. I confess to you, Father, that on my first night in this house on Fetter Lane, I lay upon my bed with my hands pressed to my ears, convinced that some great beast was devouring the city from the inside out.

My host, Mr. Abernathy, is a kindly man, a merchant in dry goods, but he is as loud as his city. He greets me each morning with a slap upon the back that nearly unseats me, and he speaks in a constant, cheerful shout. His wife, a thin, nervous woman, seems to communicate chiefly by ringing a small silver bell for the servant, a girl named Nell, who moves through the rooms like a ghost, her eyes downcast. They are a strange tribe, these Londoners. Their customs are baffling. They seem to have a terror of silence, and so they fill every moment with talk of the weather, of the price of coal, of the doings of a distant Queen, as if to pause for breath would invite some calamity. They eat their largest meal in the evening, a great, groaning board of meats and puddings, and they call it ‘dinner,’ though it is nothing like the simple, hearty meals we share at midday. They dress for it, too, the men in stiff black cloth that seems designed to strangle them, the women in gowns of a thousand colours that rustle and hiss like angry snakes.

But it is the machines that hold me in a kind of terrified thrall. You spoke of the great pumps at the mission in Kaelen Vale, and how they were a marvel, and so they were. But they are children’s toys compared to the monsters that live here. There are machines that fly through the streets on rails, breathing fire and shrieking like demons, and they carry hundreds of people at a time, packed together like salted fish in a barrel. I rode one yesterday, at Mr. Abernathy’s insistence. He called it an ‘omnibus.’ It is a great, swaying box on wheels, and it is pulled not by horses but by a churning, clanking engine that smells of hot oil and sulphur. I held the rail with a grip of iron, certain that at any moment the beast would leap its track and dash us all to pieces. The people inside, however, sat as calmly as if they were in their own parlours, reading their papers or staring out with vacant eyes. They are a fearless people, or perhaps a foolish one.

The greatest wonder and horror is the Underground. Mr. Abernathy took me to see it, descending a great winding stair into the very bowels of the earth. The air is thick and hot and smells of coal-smoke and damp stone. And then, from out of the black tunnel, a train comes. It is not like the engines above; it is a blind, dark thing, and it moves with a shrieking of metal on metal that sets my teeth on edge. It is drawn by a ‘locomotive,’ a hissing, fire-breathing engine that fills the tunnel with a foul, choking mist. To be inside it is to be swallowed by a dragon. The noise is a physical pressure on the ears, a pounding in the chest. I looked at the faces of my fellow travellers, lit by the flickering gas-lamps, and they were as placid as cattle. They are a tribe that has made a pact with the underworld, Father, and they walk through it without a tremor.

I find myself observing them as I would the hill people of the Sereth. They have their own superstitions. They believe most fervently in the power of a small, round, brown coin, which they call a ‘penny,’ and they will perform great feats of endurance and even rudeness to acquire one. They queue, which is a ritual of standing in a long, orderly line, one behind the other, and they will do this for hours, for bread, for a seat in a theatre, for a chance to see a man they call a ‘cricketer’ strike a ball with a piece of wood. They seem to find this waiting a comfort, a form of social communion.

Their gods, too, are strange. They do not worship the sun or the mountain, but a thing called ‘Respectability.’ It is a powerful and demanding deity. One must wear the correct clothes, speak in the correct manner, and live in a house of the correct colour. Any deviation is met with a pursing of the lips and a shaking of the head. I wore my heavy coat from the Vale to the market yesterday, for the wind was sharp, and I saw a woman look at me as if I had committed a great sin. Her eyes were full of a pity that was worse than any scorn. I felt as though I were a savage who had painted his face for a feast, only to find himself at a funeral.

And yet, Father, for all my fear and confusion, I am fascinated. There is a vitality here, a relentless, churning energy that is not in the Vale. The Vale is eternal; it is the same today as it was when the first shepherd drove his flock to the high pastures. London is a thing of the moment, a great, sprawling, breathing creature that is building itself and tearing itself down with every tick of the clock. I saw a great building of stone and glass being raised up, and next to it, another being pulled down into a heap of rubble by men with pickaxes. It is a place of constant, violent change. It is terrifying, and it is magnificent.

I miss the silence of the mountains. I miss the clean air and the sight of the stars, for here the sky is a brown, bruised colour, and the only stars are the gas-lamps that line the streets. But I would not trade this experience. I feel as though I am being tempered, like iron in a forge. I am learning to see the world through new eyes, even if those eyes are wide with fright.

Give my love to Mother. Tell her I am well, if not yet at peace. Tell her the city is a savage place, but that I am learning its language.

Your son,
Elias
