The pestilence had passed over us as a scythe passes over wheat, and when the reaping was done there remained but a scattered remnant, a few score souls who crept forth from their hiding-places into the silence of a city that was itself a sepulchre. The great houses stood empty, their windows like the sockets of dead eyes; the churches were open to the rain, and the wind made a melancholy music through the broken roofs, as if the very stones lamented. It was in this desolation that we gathered, the survivors of Viremont, and sought to make of the ruins a habitation.

Our leader was a man named Aldric, who had been a soldier in the wars that preceded the pestilence, and who bore upon his person the scars of many wounds, and in his countenance the habit of command. He was not a man of many words, but his speech was earnest and his purpose fixed; he looked upon the fallen city and saw not a tomb but a foundation, and his heart was filled with the desire to raise up again what had been cast down. He would gather the stones, he said, and build new walls; he would bring water from the river and plant gardens in the ashes; he would teach the young to labour and the old to hope, and in the course of years, perhaps, the name of Viremont might be spoken again without the accent of mourning.

But there was another among us, a former priest named Father Anselm, who had seen the pestilence in all its horror, who had shriven the dying and buried the dead until his hands were weary and his soul sick with grief, and who had come forth from that trial with a conviction that burned within him like a fire that cannot be quenched. The pestilence, he declared, was no accident of nature, no mere contagion borne upon the wind; it was the judgement of Heaven upon a wicked generation, a visitation of divine wrath that had swept away the proud and the sinful and left only a remnant to inherit the earth in humility and fear. To rebuild, he said, was to defy that judgement; to raise again the towers that God had cast down was to say that the hand of the Almighty had been stayed by the will of man. The ruins were a monument to human pride; let them remain, he cried, as a warning to the children of the new age, and let us dwell among them in penitence and prayer, seeking not to restore what Heaven had destroyed, but to prepare our souls for the world to come.

Thus was our little community divided, and the division ran deep, for it touched not merely the question of what should be done, but the very nature of the calamity that had befallen us, and the meaning of our survival. The old people, who had seen their children perish and their homes laid waste, inclined to the priest, for they found in his words a consolation that the soldier could not offer; they wished to believe that their suffering had a purpose, and that the pride of the city had been justly punished. But the young people, who had known the pestilence only as a shadow over their childhood, and who had come into the world with the hunger for life strong within them, looked to Aldric, and their hearts burned within them at his words of labour and rebuilding.

Among these young people was a maiden named Elara, the daughter of a physician who had died in the pestilence, and she was beloved by a youth called Corin, whose father had been a mason before him, and who had inherited his father's tools and his father's skill, and with them a fierce desire to build. These two had grown up together in the shadow of the ruins, and their love had flowered in that desolation as a flower flowers in a crevice of stone, the more precious for the barrenness around it. Corin was eager to join himself to Aldric, to put his hands to the work of restoration, to see the walls rise and the roofs close over, and to make for Elara a home that should not be a tomb. But Elara was torn, for she had sat at the feet of Father Anselm, and his words had sunk deep into her soul; she feared lest the pride of building should draw down upon them another judgement, and she prayed in secret that the will of Heaven might be made known to her.

It chanced upon a certain evening, when the autumn winds blew cold across the ruins and the sky was overcast with clouds of sombre hue, that the whole community assembled in the great square before the fallen cathedral, and there arose a disputation between Aldric and Father Anselm that was long remembered among us. The soldier stood forth, his grey hair stirred by the wind, and he spoke of the duty that lay upon the living to care for the living; he spoke of the children who would perish of cold and hunger if we did not build; he spoke of the gifts that God had given to man, of reason and of skill, and he asked whether it were not a sin to bury those gifts in the earth as the unprofitable servant buried his talent. And the priest answered him with words of fire, declaring that the wisdom of this world was foolishness with God, that the pestilence was a sign that could not be mistaken, and that to build upon the ruins was to build upon the graves of the dead, and to forget the lesson that Heaven had written in blood.

The people listened, and their hearts were divided; but when the disputation was ended, and the wind had died away, and the moon had risen over the broken towers, Elara rose from her place and spoke in a voice that trembled, yet was clear. She said that she had prayed for light, and that light had been given her; she said that the pestilence had come, she knew not why, but that the dead were dead, and the living must live; and she said that she would cast her lot with those who would build, for she believed that God was not pleased with the desolation of His creatures, but with their hope. And when she had spoken, Corin came and stood beside her, and took her hand in his, and the young people gathered around them, and there was a murmur of assent that ran through the assembly like the sound of many waters.

Father Anselm stood silent, and his countenance was dark with grief and anger; but Aldric came forward and laid his hand upon the shoulder of the young mason, and his eyes were bright with a light that was not of the sun. "Let us build," he said, "not in pride, but in hope; not in defiance of Heaven, but in trust that Heaven is merciful; and if we err, let the error be upon our heads, and let the children who come after us judge between us and the priest." And so the work began, and the sound of the hammer and the chisel was heard again in Viremont, and the walls rose slowly from the dust, and the gardens were planted in the ashes; and though the shadow of the pestilence lay heavy upon the land, and the priest withdrew into solitude and lamentation, yet there was a new spirit abroad among the people, a spirit of life and of labour, and it seemed to those who had survived the desolation that the world was not yet ended, but that a new world was struggling into birth.
