In the small Lincolnshire market-town of Barrowby, where the level fields stretch away to the marsh, bisected by a sluggish drain that serves as a boundary to the neighboring parish, the firm of Phipps & Son had maintained a sober and respectable frontage for the better part of forty years. The original Phipps having been gathered to his fathers in the spring of eighteen hundred and thirty-four, the signboard remained unaltered by a tacit consent that was creditable to the filial piety of his children; for the son, Jeremiah, was not sufficiently vain of his proprietorship to demand the erasure of the paternal title, and the daughter, Hetty, was too severely alive to the practical wisdom of retaining a designation that promised stability to the minds of elderly customers. Thus for ten years they had carried on the business in unison—Jeremiah measuring the heavier cloths and conducting the bargaining with a certain slow insistence that gave purchasers a comfortable sense of their own shrewdness, while Hetty presided over the haberdashery, the ribbons, and the finer linens, with a quiet vigilance that suffered no thread to be misplaced and no account to be mislaid. Their partnership was one of those mixed growths which flourish best in the shade of a common difficulty: it had the firmness of a root system that has had to push its way through stubborn clay, and was perhaps the more robust for the absence of those flowering sentiments that make a show in the sun but are easily shaken by the wind.

But the moral economy of a household, like the material economy of a shop, is subject to perturbations from causes so slight in their first appearance that the keenest observer might pass them by without remark. The fact that Jeremiah Phipps, now in his thirty-sixth year, had conceived a strong attachment to Miss Mary Toft, the daughter of the retired corn-dealer, was of itself no more surprising than the rotation of the seasons; yet it carried within it the seeds of a disruption that threatened to overset the careful balance of the establishment. Miss Toft was a young woman whose personal attractions were of that blooming and obvious kind which make an immediate appeal to the senses, without requiring the slower and more discriminating action of the understanding. She had a complexion of notable brightness, a profusion of light hair, and a manner of lively complaisance that seemed to promise a constant stream of cheerful discourse; but her conversation, when subjected to the test of repeated listening, was found to flow in somewhat shallow channels, circling round the small incidents of her own acquaintance and the visible furnishings of Barrowby society, without ever deepening into reflections that could engage a mind of any gravity.

Hetty, who had observed her brother’s growing attentions with the silent and anxious arithmetic of one who knows that every change in the constitution of a household must be paid for in some coin or other, had at first tried to believe that the attachment would exhaust itself in the natural course of things, as a stream dries up when the rains cease. She had even, with a subtlety that was not without its element of self-deception, contrived to throw Jeremiah into the company of other young women at the dissenting chapel’s tea-meetings, hoping that comparison might do the work of argument. But the heart, when it has once fixed its preference, is not to be diverted by the gentle methods that serve to turn a shallow rivulet; and Jeremiah, who in all other matters was amenable to his sister’s judgment, showed on this one point a steadiness of resolution that was the more formidable because it was clothed in a mild and deprecatory manner.

The quarrel—which it would perhaps be more just to call a collision of unspoken renunciations—came to a head on a Tuesday evening in October, when the shop was closed and the shutters put up, and the brother and sister sat together in the back parlour over their supper of cold mutton. The room was one of those apartments that seem to preserve the very atmosphere of a past generation: it was wainscoted in dark oak that had been grained in imitation of mahogany, and furnished with a horsehair sofa and a gateleg table that bore the stains of many a hot teapot. A single oil-lamp cast a yellow glow that flattened the features of both faces into a uniform pallor, so that each might have been looking at a mask of the other.

“I ha’ gotten a thing to say to thee, Hetty,” Jeremiah began, folding his hands on the table with the air of a man who has rehearsed his opening, “and I would as lief say it now as any time. I am minded to ask Mary Toft to be my wife.”

Hetty, who had been in the act of lifting her cup, set it down again with a precision that was more eloquent than a tremor. She was a woman of forty, whose face, once handsome in the firm, regular style that belongs to the women of that countryside, had taken on a certain settled look of self-command that was not far removed from austerity. Her eyes, of a clear grey, were fixed upon her brother with an expression in which affection and anxiety were so interwoven that neither could be said to predominate.

“I have thought as much, Jeremiah,” she said, after a pause. “And what then?”

“Why, then, it must follow that we should make some new arrangement. The shop, as thou know’st, will not support two establishments. I have been turning it over in my mind, and I see no way but that thou should’st take the dwelling in Mill Lane, which is to be let at twelve pound a year, and I should remain here with Mary, and we should divide the stock and the goodwill as may seem equitable.”

“What thou meanest,” said Hetty, in a voice that was steady but had the quality of a strained cord, “is that I should go out from the business that I have helped to build up, and leave thee to manage it with a wife who knows nothing of the trade and less of the customers.”

Jeremiah coloured. “I mean no such thing. I mean that the business is thine as much as mine, and that I would not have thee think I sought to take advantage. But Mary is a good girl, Hetty, and she has a right to a home of her own; and I am not so young as I was, and I would fain have a fireside that is my own before I am much older.”

“And dost thou think,” said Hetty, with a sudden flash that showed the fires beneath her composure, “that I am so young that I can afford to be turned out of my own fireside? I have given ten years to this shop, Jeremiah—ten years of my life, and the best of them—and I have asked nothing for it but the right to stay where I am. And now thou would’st bring in a woman who can tell a yard of calico from a yard of cambric no better than a child in the charity school, and set her above me in my own place.”

“She would not be above thee,” said Jeremiah, with an earnestness that was not without a certain clumsy tenderness. “Thou should’st be as thou hast always been. But a man must have his own life, Hetty, and a wife must come first.”

“And so I must go,” said Hetty, in a lower voice, as if she were speaking to herself rather than to him. She looked down at her plate, and her lips pressed together in a way that gave her face a sharp outline against the lamplight.

There was a silence of some minutes, during which the clock on the mantelpiece ticked with an emphasis that seemed to measure out the distance between them. Then Jeremiah, leaning forward, said, with a hesitancy that was more moving than any eloquence:

“I did not mean it so, Hetty. I thought—I thought maybe thou would’st be glad to be quit of the shop, and to have a quiet life of thy own. Thou hast worked hard, and I have seen thee tire thyself over the books and the inventory when I would have had thee rest. I thought it would be a kindness to thee to set thee free.”

Hetty looked up at him with an expression in which the hardness of a moment before had melted into something that was very near to pity. She saw, with the sudden clearness that sometimes comes in the midst of contention, that he was speaking what he believed to be the truth: he had built his plan upon the assumption that he was providing for her ease, and the discovery that she saw it as a banishment had genuinely bewildered him. And yet she could not but feel, with a resentment that pity did not wholly extinguish, that his kindness was of that sort which is most easy to bestow because it coincides with the giver’s own desire. He wished to marry Mary Toft, and he had persuaded himself that in marrying her he was conferring a benefit upon his sister—a form of self-deception that is perhaps the most insidious of all, because it clothes selfishness in the garb of benevolence and silences conscience with the language of generosity.

“Thou art a good brother, Jeremiah,” she said at last, with a weariness that was the more touching because it was so quietly spoken, “but thou dost not understand. This shop is my life, and to leave it would be to leave the only thing that has made my life worth having. I have had no other interests, no other pleasures. I am not like Mary, with her youth and her brightness and her friends. I am forty years old, and this is my world.”

Jeremiah was silent, looking at his hands. He was a man of slow perceptions, but he was not without a certain stubborn honesty that made him unwilling to press a claim which he felt to be unjust, even while he could not relinquish the desire that prompted it. And so they sat, each believing that the other’s happiness depended upon their own surrender, and each unwilling to make the surrender that would secure it—united in their affection and divided by their need, like two trees whose roots are so interwoven that neither can grow without encroaching on the other, yet which cannot be separated without damage to both.
