In the manufacturing town of Thoresby, where the red brick of the bustling workshops stood in stark contrast to the pale stone of its aging Dissenting meeting-house, the year 1834 had brought with it a fervent zeal for architectural improvement. A new chapel was to be built—a structure that should not only accommodate the swelling ranks of the dissenting congregation but stand as a monument to the civic piety of its founders. Yet, as is frequently the case when spiritual endeavors become entangled with the substantial realities of mortar and social standing, the proposed erection became the theatre of a deeply human conflict. For the laying of the foundation stone, that primary ceremonial act which solemnly dedicates the earth to heaven, was a prize that two of Thoresby’s most prominent families considered their unequivocal due: the Arkwrights, whose wealth flowed from the relentless looms of the great Mill, and the Heywoods, whose fortunes were rooted in the older, steadier trade of tanning and land agency.

The Reverend Theodore Vane, minister of the existing meeting-house and the spiritual guide designated to oversee the birth of the new, found himself in a predicament that tested the very fibres of his moral constitution. He was a man of delicate conscience and somewhat fragile physique, possessed of a mind that delighted in theological subtleties and a heart that shrank instinctively from the robust collisions of provincial vanity. To Mr. Arthur Arkwright, the senior deacon of his chapel, Mr. Vane owed his position; it was Arkwright’s commanding influence that had secured his call to the pastorate three years prior, and Arkwright’s subscriptions that quietly supplemented a meagre salary. But to old Mr. Samuel Heywood, Mr. Vane owed the quickened pulse of his conscience. It was Heywood who had first spoken to him of the inward light, who had sat with him through a night of spiritual desolation during the influenza epidemic of ’32, anchoring his wavering faith with a rugged, unyielding piety that commanded both awe and deep affection.

The rivalry between these two families was not of recent origin, but had accumulated the slow, stratified bitterness of generations, rooted in a boundary dispute over a mill-stream that had soured their grandfathers and hardened their fathers. Mr. Arkwright, a man of large, commanding presence and a voice accustomed to overcoming the clatter of spinning-jennies, regarded the new chapel as a natural extension of his industrial empire. He had subscribed three hundred pounds to the building fund—a sum that made the other deacons look like mere gleaners in the field of benevolence—and he expected, with the tranquil certainty of a man who measures the world by the yardstick of his own ledger, that his hand should place the stone. Mr. Heywood, on the other hand, was of a leaner and more acid temperament. His wealth was considerable, but he spent it with a grudging, calculating exactness, reserving his larger generosity for occasions where it might serve to check the arrogance of the Arkwrights. He had given fifty pounds to the chapel, but he brought to the enterprise the weight of a family name that had been affixed to Dissenting causes in Thoresby since the ejection of 1662—a antiquity of piety which he valued far above any recently acquired mechanical fortune.

Mr. Vane had hoped that the matter might settle itself through the quiet workings of Providence, or at least through the discreet management of the building committee. But Providence, in the affairs of Thoresby, seemed to operate with a frustrating slowness, and the building committee was hopelessly divided. And so, on a damp Tuesday evening in October, Mr. Vane found himself compelled to visit Mr. Heywood at his substantial, if somewhat gloomy, residence on Tanner’s Lane, to seek an accommodation before the Sunday congregation should be rent asunder by open faction.

He was shown into the parlour, where Mr. Heywood sat by a meagre fire, reading the *Evangelical Magazine* by the light of a single tallow candle. The old man’s face was deeply lined, bearing the sharp imprint of a life spent in self-denial and the scrutinizing of motives—both his own and others’.

“Good evening, Mr. Vane,” said Heywood, without rising, but offering a chair with a brief, angular motion of his hand. “You are come about this foolishness of the stone, I make no doubt.”

“I am come, sir, in the hope that we may avoid any public manifestation of that disunity which the Apostle so earnestly warned us against,” Mr. Vane replied, seating himself with a nervous precision that betrayed his inward tension. He was acutely conscious that Mr. Heywood’s dark eyes were fixed upon him with that searching directness which made the younger man feel as though his very thoughts were being laid bare upon a dissecting table.

“Aye, ‘avoid disunity,’ that is ever your cry, Mr. Vane,” said Heywood, laying down his magazine and folding his hands. “But there is a unity that is no better than a hollow barrel, sounding loud but holding no grain. You know well that Arkwright thinks to buy the place of honour as he buys his raw cotton—by the weight of his purse. And because you sit in a pulpit that his money helped to build, you tremble lest you should displease him.”

The charge struck Mr. Vane with a painful accuracy. He felt the blood rise to his thin cheeks, and for a moment, the cautious, conciliatory speech he had prepared entirely forsook him. “I cannot deny, Mr. Heywood,” he said, in a low voice, “that I am bound to Mr. Arkwright by ties of no common gratitude. But I am bound to you, sir, by a gratitude of a still higher and more sacred character. It was you who led my soul out of the wilderness of doubt. If I am to choose, I would rather offend the whole world than do violence to the convictions you have instilled in me.”

Mr. Heywood’s severe expression softened, though but slightly. He was a man who valued sincerity above all things, and the minister’s evident distress touched that deeply buried vein of tenderness which existed beneath his austere exterior. Yet he was not to be turned from his purpose by a fine phrase.

“Well spoken, Mr. Vane,” he said, “and I believe you mean it. But words will not lay a stone. The question is whether the honour belongs to the man who gave the most money, or to the man whose family has upheld the cause of truth in this town when to do so meant something more than writing a cheque. My father worshipped in a barn at the edge of the town, Mr. Vane, and was pelted with mud for his pains. Arthur Arkwright’s father was a tenant farmer who cared no more for religion than the beasts in his yard, until the spinning-frame made him rich enough to find it convenient. If that stone is laid by Arkwright’s hand, it is a testimony not to the Gospel, but to the supremacy of mammon.”

Mr. Vane sighed. He perceived, with the analytic clearness that often brought him more sorrow than comfort, that the old man’s argument was a compound of genuine religious feeling and deeply rooted family pride—a mixture which, however common in human affairs, is exceedingly difficult to separate or to combat. He knew, too, that Mr. Arkwright’s claim, though resting on a less venerable basis, was not without its own justification; the chapel could not have been built without his subscription, and to slight the principal benefactor was to risk the alienation of a man whose worldly power could easily withdraw its support from a struggling cause.

“It is a heavy burden you lay upon me, Mr. Heywood,” said Mr. Vane at length. “For I am placed between two duties, neither of which I can discharge without wounding the other. If I yield to Mr. Arkwright’s claim, I seem to sanction the idea that spiritual privileges are to be sold to the highest bidder; but if I insist upon your right, I provoke a quarrel which may rend the congregation and imperil the very building we seek to erect.”

“Then let the congregation be rent, if it must be,” said Heywood, with a sudden flash of his old fire. “A house built on the foundation of compromise with worldly pride will not stand. Better that the walls should remain unbuilt than that they should enclose a false peace.”

The words had the ring of a prophetic denunciation, and Mr. Vane felt himself borne down by their weight. He sat for some moments in silence, his gaze fixed on the flickering candle, while the slow, heavy ticking of the clock in the corner marked the passage of a time that seemed to him charged with the destiny of Thoresby Dissent. He was aware, in that interval, of a curious double movement within his own mind: a shrinking from the conflict that lay before him, and a simultaneous, almost involuntary, recognition that Mr. Heywood was, in the main, right—that the spiritual integrity of the chapel was a trust which he, as its minister, was solemnly bound to preserve, even at the cost of his own interest and comfort.

At last he raised his head, and there was a quiet resolve in his voice that surprised even himself. “I cannot promise you, Mr. Heywood, that I shall be able to accomplish what you desire. But I will speak to Mr. Arkwright to-morrow, and I will tell him, with such plainness as God shall give me, that the honour he seeks is not one that can be claimed as a right of property. I will entreat him, for the sake of peace and of the cause we all profess to serve, to waive his claim. If he refuses—” Mr. Vane paused, and the thin hand that rested on his knee trembled slightly—“if he refuses, then I shall lay the stone myself, in the name of the congregation, and dedicate it to no family, but to the worship of God alone.”

Mr. Heywood looked at him long and searchingly. Then, with a grunt that might have been approval or mere acquiescence, he took up his magazine again. “Do as you think right, Mr. Vane,” he said. “But remember that a minister who tries to please everybody usually ends by pleasing nobody, and least of all Him whom he is called to serve.”

Mr. Vane rose and took his leave, stepping out into the damp October night, where the smell of tan-pits mingled with the distant smoke of the mill chimneys. He walked slowly homeward, his mind burdened with the task that lay before him. He knew that Mr. Arkwright would not easily brook a refusal, and that the consequences might be disastrous to his own slender fortunes. Yet he felt, beneath the anxiety, a certain strange peace—the peace of a man who has at last perceived, amid the tangle of competing claims and worldly calculations, the narrow line of duty, and has resolved, however feebly, to walk in it.
