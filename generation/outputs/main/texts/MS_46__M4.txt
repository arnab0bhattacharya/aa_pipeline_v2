The candle had guttered to a thread of light, and the fire, which I had suffered to burn low, cast but a fitful crimson glow upon the walls of my chamber, when I at last arose from the posture in which I had remained since the closing of the door upon my brother’s footsteps. The hours had passed unmarked by me; the ticking of the clock upon the stair had ceased to be a sound, and had become a part of the silence which oppressed me, and I knew not whether it were noon or midnight that reigned beyond the curtained windows, nor cared to inquire. Since the morning of my desolation—that morning when the letter had been placed in my hands which scattered to the winds the fair fabric of my future, and left me standing amid the ruins of a happiness I had believed eternal—I had abandoned myself to a grief which I cherished as the only possession remaining to me. The world without had become hateful; the sun, which but a week before I had welcomed as the harbinger of my wedding-day, now rose only to mock me with the memory of hopes extinguished; and the very air of the house, laden with the scent of the flowers I had gathered for my bridal, seemed to stifle me with its sweetness.

I had loved him, my cousin Edmund, with a love which I had believed as immutable as the hills which encompassed our valley, and as pure as the springs which gushed from their bosoms. From childhood we had been companions; our hands had gathered the wild strawberries upon the slopes of Winstermoor together; together we had watched the storms come sweeping down the dale, and had laughed at the lightning as it played upon the crags. I had thought his heart the mirror of my own, and when he had spoken of our union, I had listened as to the confirmation of a vow already registered in heaven. But the letter—that fatal letter, written in a hand I had once adored, and now abhorred—had told me, in cold and measured phrases, that he had mistaken the nature of his affections; that a passion of longer standing, and of a more vehement character, had resumed its empire over his soul; and that he trusted I would forgive him, and would remember him only as a friend. A friend! The word was a dagger. I had read the lines again and again, until the characters swam before my eyes, and the paper dropped from my nerveless grasp; and then I had sunk upon the floor, and prayed that I might die.

From that hour I had shut myself within my chamber, and refused all consolation. My father, whose affection was of that silent and undemonstrative kind which knows not how to speak in the hour of trouble, had stood at my door, and had gone away without a word; my mother had wept and entreated, and had at last left me to my sullen solitude; but my brother Lionel, my young brother, the child of my father’s declining years, had been my constant tormentor. He was but fourteen, a boy of restless and untamable spirits, whose laughter had once been the music of my life, but whose mirth now grated upon my ear like the jangling of discordant instruments. He would not be repulsed. He would sit upon the edge of my bed, and talk to me of his dogs and his birds, and of the trout he had caught in the brook, as though I were still the sister who had once shared in all his sports; and when I turned from him in silence, he would take my hand, and press it to his cheek, and say, “You are not angry with me, Constance? You will come out tomorrow?” And I would answer, “No, Lionel, not tomorrow,” and he would go away with a sigh, only to return upon the morrow with the same entreaties.

It was upon the fifth morning of my seclusion that he came to me with an air of determination which I had never before remarked in him. He had risen early, and the dew was still upon his shoes, and his cheeks were flushed with the freshness of the upland air. “Constance,” said he, standing before me with folded arms, “you shall not remain in this dungeon any longer. I have been to the top of Winstermoor, and the sun is shining, and the larks are singing, and the whole world is alive with joy. You shall come with me. I will not take a refusal.”

I looked at him with a listless eye, and bade him leave me. “I am not well, Lionel; I cannot walk today.” But he was not to be daunted. He came to me, and took my hand in his, and his grasp was warm and strong, and his young face, in which the bloom of boyhood yet lingered, was lighted with a resolution that became him well. “You are not well,” he repeated, “and therefore you must come. The air of this close room is poison to you. You shall breathe the breath of the hills, and you shall feel the sun upon your face, and you shall forget—” He stopped, as if he feared to wound me, and then, with the boldness of a nature that knew not how to dissemble, he added, “You shall forget him who is unworthy of your remembrance.”

I started at the words, and a pang shot through my heart; but there was a truth in the boy’s blunt speech which I could not deny, and a warmth in his affection which I had not the strength to resist. I suffered him to lead me from the room, and down the stairs, and out into the open air; and when the sunlight fell upon my face, and the keen breeze from the hills smote upon my brow, I felt a sensation which was almost pain, so long had I been a stranger to the elements.

We walked in silence for a while, he supporting my steps, for I was weak from long fasting and from the violence of my grief; and as we ascended the path which wound among the heather, the landscape opened before us in all its wild and solemn beauty. The valley lay beneath us like a map of emerald and silver, the river winding through it like a thread of light, and the cottages of the hamlet sending up their thin blue smoke into the clear morning air. Beyond, the hills rose in successive ridges, purple and brown and golden, till they melted into the dim distance, where the sky stooped down to kiss them. The larks were singing, as Lionel had said, and the sound of their song came down to us like a shower of silver rain; and the air was fragrant with the breath of the heather and the wild thyme, and the whole scene was so fair, and so calm, and so utterly indifferent to my sorrow, that I felt a strange and sudden shame at the thought of my self-imposed imprisonment.

Lionel had been watching me, and now he spoke. “Is it not beautiful, Constance? Is it not worth living for? I knew you would feel it. I knew the hills would speak to you as they speak to me. They have no patience with our little griefs, and yet they comfort us. They seem to say, ‘We have been here for ages, and we shall be here when you are gone; and yet we are not sad.’ And then the sun comes up, and the birds sing, and we forget.”

I turned to him, and the tears, which had been frozen in my heart, gushed forth, and I wept upon his shoulder. “Oh, Lionel,” I cried, “I have been very wicked and very foolish. I have shut out the light of heaven, and the love of those who are dear to me, and I have brooded over a grief which was unworthy of the name. He has broken his faith, but I have broken mine to all that is good and true. Forgive me, my brother, and let us go home.”

He pressed my hand, and his young face beamed with a joy which I had not seen upon it for many days. “There is nothing to forgive,” said he; “only promise me that you will come out with me every morning, and that you will not shut yourself up again. The hills are the best physicians, and I am their apothecary.”

I promised, and we descended the path together; and as we went, I looked back upon the heights which we had climbed, and I thought of the words of the Psalmist, “I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help.” And I felt that the help had come, though it was but the help of a boy’s love and a boy’s simple faith; and I resolved that I would no longer be the slave of a sorrow which had no dignity, but that I would strive to be worthy of the affection which had sought me out in my darkness, and had led me back to the light of day.
