The little cottage at Briarwood, bequeathed with such unexpected partiality by Mrs. Pennington to her two goddaughters, was scarcely calculated to inflame the breasts of young women accustomed to contemplate a much higher style of grandeur. It was a commodious, neatly-roofed dwelling, situated on a gentle eminence half a mile from the village of Kington, possessing every requisite for comfort, and utterly destitute of any pretension to splendour. The annuity of four hundred pounds which accompanied it, and which was to be equally divided between Miss Helena Vernon and Miss Clara Vernon, was an income just sufficient to render the possessors independent of the world’s bounty, without inspiring them with any very formidable ideas of their own consequence.

To Helena, the elder sister, it was all that she could wish. Her habits were domestic, her tastes moderate, and her mind was strongly imbued with a conviction that happiness was far more likely to be found in the tranquil exercise of good sense, than in the restless pursuit of fashionable amusements. She rejoiced in the possession of a small but select library, a neat flower-garden, and the prospect of a society confined to a few select families of the neighbourhood, whose claims to notice were founded rather on merit than on rank. To Clara, however, the prospect was gloomy in the extreme. She was a young woman of very lively parts, a sanguine temper, and an unbounded admiration for the metropolis. To be shut up in a Dorsetshire cottage, with only four hundred a year between them, and that divided, was a sentence of the most cruel description. She had always understood Mrs. Pennington to be a woman of considerable fortune; she had always been led to suppose that the godmother’s affection for her goddaughters was of a nature to ensure them a handsome independence; and to find that the whole inheritance consisted of a cottage and an annuity, which must preclude all hopes of a London season, was a disappointment she could hardly contemplate with any degree of patience.

“I am sure, Helena,” said she, on the very first morning of their taking possession, “you may be very well satisfied with this dull place, for you never cared for any thing but your books and your garden; but for my part, I cannot endure it. To be buried alive in the country, at my time of life, is perfectly shocking. I had calculated upon a different sort of thing entirely. Mrs. Pennington used always to say that she wished to see us established in the world; and I cannot think what she meant by it, if she did not mean to give us enough to live in London.”

“I dare say she meant to give us enough to live comfortably wherever we were,” replied Helena, who was arranging a small collection of china on a chimney-piece, with as much composure as if her sister had been talking of the weather. “And I am sure we are very much obliged to her. Four hundred a year is a very handsome income for two single women, in a place where living is so cheap.”

“Cheap! Yes, it is cheap enough, I dare say; but what is the advantage of its being cheap, when one is dying of weariness? I do not call it living at all. It is merely existing. And as for the four hundred a year, I am sure it is very well for you, who never had any idea of spending a hundred; but for me, who have always looked forward to a certain style of living, it is absolutely nothing. I could spend the whole of it in London, without being a bit extravagant.”

“I dare say you might,” said Helena, smiling; “but as we cannot both have the whole, and as I am by no means inclined to give up my share, I think we had better make up our minds to be contented with what we have.”

Clara was silenced, but not convinced. She fretted and fumed in secret; she declared that it was very hard that she should be tied to a person of such low views; she asserted that Helena had no right to dictate to her; and she was on the point of writing to a friend in town, to inquire whether a lodging could not be obtained in some fashionable street, on the credit of her expectations, when the arrival of their brother, Mr. Charles Vernon, gave a new turn to her thoughts.

Mr. Charles Vernon was a young man of good address, fashionable appearance, and very slender fortune. He had been bred to the bar, but had not yet acquired either business or reputation; and as his expectations from Mrs. Pennington had been fully as sanguine as Clara’s, he was proportionably disappointed at finding himself disinherited. He had flattered himself that the old lady’s house in town, and her funded property, would have descended to him; and to be told that every thing was left to his sisters, and that his own share was absolutely nothing, was a mortification he could not easily digest. He came down to Briarwood, professedly to congratulate the ladies on their accession of fortune, and to offer his services in the arrangement of their affairs; but his real motive was a hope that he might, by some means or other, secure a part of the property to himself.

He found Helena employed in the kitchen garden, instructing the gardener in the art of pruning apricots, and Clara seated at the drawing-room window, in a very becoming deshabille, looking as melancholy as she could conveniently look.

“Well, Charles,” said Clara, as he entered, “this is a pretty business, is it not? I suppose you are come to laugh at us.”

“Not at all, my dear Clara,” replied Charles, seating himself with an air of great good humour. “I am come to offer my services. I understand that there is some little difference of opinion between you and Helena, respecting the disposal of your income; and as I am an impartial person, I think I may be of use in settling the matter.”

“I do not know that there is any difference of opinion,” said Clara. “Helena is determined to bury herself here, and I am determined not to be buried. That is all.”

“And what do you propose to do, my dear Clara?”

“To go to London, of course. I have a great mind to take my two hundred a year, and spend it all in a season. I am sure I should not be a bit extravagant. There are many girls who spend twice as much.”

“And what is to become of Helena?”

“Oh, Helena may do as she pleases. She may stay here, and prune her apricots, and read her books, to the end of the chapter. I shall not interfere with her.”

“But, my dear Clara, you must consider that this house belongs to both of you. You cannot very well live in it, and not live in it, at the same time. And if you go to London, you must have a house, or lodgings, and that will cost money. Two hundred a year will go a very little way in town.”

“I know that,” said Clara, colouring; “but I do not see what is to be done. I cannot stay here. I shall die of a decline, I am sure, if I do.”

Charles saw that there was an opening for his own project, and he proceeded with great caution. “I think I see a way of arranging it,” said he. “If Helena is willing to let you have the whole of the annuity, on condition that you give up your share of the house to her, and that I, as your brother, undertake to manage the property for you, and to allow you a certain sum every year for your expenses—”

“But what right have you to manage it?” said Helena, who had entered the room in time to hear the latter part of the proposal. “I am sure, Charles, you are very kind to offer your services, but I think we can manage our own affairs. The annuity is left to us, and we must divide it. If Clara chooses to spend her share in London, she is very welcome; but I shall not give up my share of the house, nor shall I allow any body to manage it for me.”

Charles was a good deal disconcerted. He had hoped that Helena’s quiet habits would have made her an easy prey; and he had calculated on her yielding, without much resistance, to a proposal which would rid her of her sister’s company, and leave her in sole possession of the cottage. But he had underrated her good sense. She saw through his design at once; and though she was too well bred to say so, she was determined that he should not profit by the arrangement.

“I am sure I do not know what you mean,” said Clara, with some warmth. “Charles only wants to be of use to us, and I am sure we ought to be very much obliged to him. I think it is a very good plan, and I cannot conceive why you should object to it.”

“I object to it,” said Helena, “because it is not a good plan for either of us. If Charles were to manage your money, Clara, he would very soon have it all in his own hands; and then where would you be? You had better stay here, and be contented with what you have. Two hundred a year is a very pretty income, if you will but make up your mind to spend it quietly.”

Clara was very much displeased. She thought Helena unkind, and Charles ill used. She was certain that her brother’s intentions were of the purest kind, and that he would be a most excellent manager of her affairs. She declared that she would not stay in the cottage to be dictated to; and she flounced out of the room, leaving Helena and Charles to settle the matter as they could.

Charles, finding that his scheme was likely to fail, changed his ground. He began to talk of the expenses of a London season, the temptations to which a young woman of Clara’s beauty and disposition would be exposed, the difficulty of finding suitable lodgings, and the danger of falling into bad company. He enlarged on the comforts of Briarwood, the beauty of the country, the excellence of the society, and the happiness which two sisters might enjoy in a snug cottage, with a good income, and a neat garden. He was so very eloquent on the subject, that Helena began to suspect he had some motive for wishing Clara to remain at home, which he did not choose to avow; and she was confirmed in this suspicion, when he added, that if Clara would but give up her wild scheme, he should be very happy to come down and spend a month with them now and then, and that he would even consent to take charge of the garden, if Helena would allow him.

Helena smiled, and said she should be very happy to see him whenever he pleased; but she was too wise to entrust him with the management of her affairs, or to believe that his advice was disinterested. She knew that his own fortune was very small, and that he had hoped to derive some advantage from Mrs. Pennington’s will; and she was not sorry to find that his disappointment had not made him desperate. She resolved to keep her own counsel, to manage her own income, and to leave Clara to settle her own plans. If she chose to go to London, she might; but she should not have the whole annuity, nor should she have any part of Helena’s share.

In the end, Clara’s resolution gave way before the difficulties of her situation. She found that lodgings in a fashionable street were not to be had for nothing; that a season in London required a much larger income than two hundred a year; and that the friend on whom she had calculated for introductions was just setting off for Bath, and could not possibly receive her.
