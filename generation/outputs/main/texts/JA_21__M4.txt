The question of Miss Harriet Ellison’s marriage was one upon which the whole of the village of Steventon Green considered itself entitled to an opinion, and the prevailing opinion was that she was a very fortunate young woman. Mr. Thomas Ashby, the gentleman in question, was not, it is true, possessed of those brilliant parts which dazzle in a ball-room or sparkle in a drawing-room; but he was a man of unblemished character, of a comfortable, if not a large, independence, and of a temper so uniformly placid and so steadily affectionate, that to be the object of his regard was to be considered, by every matron in the parish, as a most enviable distinction. His manners were grave, his conversation was not various, and his person was of that description which is best praised by the epithet respectable; but he was the eldest son of a baronet, and his preferment to a valuable living was hourly expected, and these circumstances, in the eyes of Mrs. Ellison, more than compensated for any deficiency in wit or vivacity.

The engagement had now subsisted for two years, and Harriet, as she sat at her needle-work in the small but elegant parlour of the White House, was beginning to feel that two years was a very long time. The expected living had not yet been bestowed; the baronet, Sir John Ashby, was a gentleman of vigorous constitution and of a tenacity of life which his son’s prospects could not but lament, though his filial piety forbade his saying so; and Harriet had been left, for four-and-twenty months, to the undisturbed contemplation of a sentiment which she was daily less able to define. She had accepted Mr. Ashby, she was well aware, with a very proper sense of his merits and of her own good fortune. He had been attentive, he had been respectful, he had been everything that a man ought to be; and if, when he had made his declaration, her heart had not beaten with that tumultuous emotion which she had been taught by novels to expect, she had attributed the calmness of her pulse to the superior rationality of her attachment, and had congratulated herself upon being guided by judgement rather than by passion.

But the two years had done their work. She had begun to observe that Mr. Ashby’s conversation, when it did not relate to the weather or to the duties of his profession, related invariably to herself; and that his compliments, though sincere, were of a nature which admitted of little variation. He admired her drawings, but he admired them equally on every occasion; he praised her singing, but he requested the same song. She had lately detected in herself a disposition to be silent when he was present, and to be conscious of a sensation of relief when the door closed behind him; and this discovery had given her no small uneasiness. A young woman of delicate feelings and of a serious turn of mind, she was shocked at her own ingratitude. She represented to herself, with great force, the excellences of Mr. Ashby’s character, the security of the establishment which he offered, the displeasure of her mother, the astonishment of the neighbourhood, and the probable conclusion of every acquaintance, that she had been capricious and undutiful. Yet, in spite of these reflections, she could not bring herself to contemplate the marriage with any degree of satisfaction; and she was beginning to suspect that she had mistaken a rational esteem for a warmer sentiment, when a circumstance occurred which threw her thoughts into a new and painful confusion.

Mr. Ashby had a friend, a Mr. Charles Wentworth, a young man of lively manners and of a fortune not more than sufficient for his wants, who had lately come into the country to pass a few weeks at the house of a relation in the neighbourhood. He was introduced to the White House; he was found to be agreeable; he sang, he talked, he drew caricatures, he had read the books which Harriet had read, and he admired the very passages which she had admired. He was not, perhaps, so handsome as Mr. Ashby, nor so dignified; but there was a spirit in his eye, and a vivacity in his manner, which she had never witnessed in her betrothed. He spoke to her of poetry and of foreign travel; he seemed to understand her at a word; and Harriet, who had never before been understood, was sensible of a pleasure which she was very unwilling to acknowledge. She was not so weak as to imagine herself in love with Mr. Wentworth; she was too well principled to permit her fancy to dwell upon a man who was not her affianced husband; but she could not avoid making a comparison, and the comparison was fatal to Mr. Ashby.

It was upon a Tuesday morning, when the rain had confined her to the house, and when Mr. Ashby was expected to dinner, that Harriet received a letter from her friend, Miss Jane Langford, a young lady of great penetration, who had been married the year before to a clergyman in a neighbouring county. Miss Langford had always been Harriet’s confidante, and to her she had lately imparted some portion of her uneasiness. The letter was as follows:

“My dear Harriet,

I have thought much of what you told me when we last met, and I cannot conceal from you my opinion, that you are in a situation which requires the most serious consideration. You say that you esteem Mr. Ashby, that you are grateful for his attachment, and that you are sensible of his worth; but you confess that you do not love him. My dear girl, esteem and gratitude are excellent things, but they are not love; and a marriage without love is a bargain, not a union. You will say that love may come after marriage; but I would ask you, whether you have ever known it to come under such circumstances? For my own part, I never knew a woman who did not love her husband before marriage, who loved him after; and I have known several who have been most miserable, and who have been told, by way of consolation, that they had done their duty. I do not say that Mr. Ashby is not a worthy man; I believe he is; but worth is not everything. You are young, you are amiable, you are not without accomplishments; and you have a right to expect something more than a negative happiness. If you do not love him, break the engagement. It will be a painful thing to do, and your mother will be very angry; but it is better to bear a little displeasure now, than to repent at leisure for the rest of your life. I know that I am giving you advice which the world would call romantic; but I am persuaded that it is the advice of true friendship. Write to me soon, and believe me,

Your affectionate,

Jane Langford.”

Harriet read this letter with a beating heart. It spoke the very language which she had been afraid to hold with herself; it confirmed her in the opinion which she had been struggling to suppress; and yet it terrified her by the boldness of its conclusion. To break the engagement! It was a step which she had never ventured to contemplate as a possibility. She thought of Mr. Ashby’s unvarying kindness, of his confidence in her, of the pain which such a determination must inflict upon him; she thought of her mother’s anger, of the remarks of the neighbourhood, of the difficulty of ever being again engaged; and she was almost resolved to submit to her fate, and to become Mrs. Ashby, with all the resignation of a martyr.

But the arrival of Mr. Ashby himself, an hour before his time, interrupted her reflections. He came in with his usual air of complacent gravity, and after having hoped that she had not been incommoded by the rain, and having observed that the weather was very seasonable for the farmers, he took his seat opposite to her, and fell into a silence which she had not the courage to break. She looked at him as he sat, and she thought that she had never seen him to less advantage. His countenance was placid, but it was vacant; his dress was neat, but it was formal; and his manner, though kind, was without animation. She contrasted him with Mr. Wentworth, and the contrast was so little to his credit, that she was ashamed of herself for making it. She endeavoured to recall his good qualities, his integrity, his steadiness, his affection; but these virtues, though she acknowledged them, did not touch her heart. She felt that she was about to do a very wrong thing, and she felt, at the same time, that she could not do otherwise.

“Mr. Ashby,” said she, at last, in a voice which trembled, “I have been thinking a great deal lately of our engagement.”

Mr. Ashby looked up with an air of mild surprise. “Indeed, my dear Miss Ellison! I hope you have not been uneasy. I am sure you have no reason to be. My father is very well, and the living is as good as certain.”

“It is not that,” said Harriet. “It is not the living. It is—I am afraid I have been too hasty. I am afraid I do not feel towards you as I ought.”

Mr. Ashby was not a man of quick perception, but he was not without a certain slow understanding, which arrived at conclusions by a process of its own. He looked at her for a moment in silence, and then he said, with great composure, “I am sorry to hear it. I had hoped that your feelings were as mine. But if it is otherwise, I would not wish to constrain you. I am not a man of many words, but I can assure you that I have a very sincere regard for you, and that I should be very sorry to be the means of making you unhappy.”

There was something in this speech which touched Harriet more than any eloquence could have done. She saw that he was not angry, that he was not even surprised; she saw that he was willing to release her, and she was struck with the generosity of his conduct. She felt that she had been about to sacrifice a worthy man to a fancy, and she hesitated.

“I do not know what to say,” she cried. “I am very unhappy. I am afraid I have been ungrateful. You are very good, and I am very foolish.”

“Do not distress yourself,” said Mr. Ashby, with the same calm kindness. “If you have any doubt, it is better to speak it now. I am not a young man of spirit, perhaps, but I am not without feeling. I would rather lose you now than be a burden to you hereafter.”

Harriet was overcome. She saw before her a man who, if he was dull, was at least honest and generous; and she felt that to break with him would be an act of ingratitude which she could never justify to herself. She thought of Miss Langford’s letter, and she thought that Miss Langford was romantic. She thought of Mr. Wentworth, and she thought that she was a fool. She rose from her seat, and, with a manner which was meant to be decisive, but which was only hurried, she said, “No, Mr. Ashby, I will not break my engagement. I have been foolish, but I am sensible of your goodness. Let us say no more about it.”

Mr. Ashby bowed, and said that he was very glad to hear it; and then he observed that the rain had ceased, and that he thought he would walk down to the village. He took his hat, and was gone; and Harriet, left alone, sat down by the window, and looked out upon the wet garden, and felt that she had done her duty, and that she was very miserable.
