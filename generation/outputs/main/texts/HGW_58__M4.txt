It was on the second day of his annual holiday, and Albert Pottle, of the firm of Messrs. Hickey and Blunt, estate agents, of Clapham, was free of all the world. He had a new bicycle—a Swift, with a nickel-plated handle-bar and a most agreeable free-wheel—and he was proceeding at a cheerful ten miles an hour along the leafy lanes that lie between Michelmersh and the valley of the Test. The morning was bright and the hedges were full of the small, shrill business of birds, and Mr. Pottle, in a tweed suit of a rather pronounced check and a cap set at a jaunty angle, was at peace with all mankind. He whistled as he went, and now and then he remarked aloud to the scenery that it was “a fair treat.”

It was just as he came down a gentle incline, with the tall elms closing overhead, that he beheld the figure of a young woman sitting upon a stile by the roadside, and exhibiting every symptom of distress. She was dressed in a grey cycling-skirt and a neat jacket, and there was a small valise at her feet. Her face was flushed, and she was dabbing at her eyes with a handkerchief.

Mr. Pottle was a young man of a sanguine and obliging disposition. He dismounted at once, with a great show of ease, and touched his cap.

“Beg pardon,” said he, “but I hope there’s nothing wrong. Not an accident, I hope?”

The young lady looked at him with a quick, appraising glance, and then, as though she had come to a sudden resolution, she spoke.

“I am in a very awkward position,” she said. “I have run away from my guardian.”

Mr. Pottle’s whistle died upon his lips. He had read of such things in the papers, but he had never supposed that they happened in Hampshire, and certainly not to a neatly-dressed young woman with a very pleasant voice.

“Run away!” he echoed. “Lor’!”

“My name is Miss Alderney,” she went on, with a kind of desperate frankness. “My guardian is a Mr. Chaffey, of Fordingbridge. He is a very—he is a very trying man. He has ideas of what a girl should do that I cannot endure. I have an aunt at Lyndhurst, a Miss Wimple, and I was going to her. But I have walked from Fordingbridge, and I have lost my purse, and I do not know the way, and I am very tired.”

Mr. Pottle’s heart was touched. He was a young man of a chivalrous, if somewhat unreflective, turn.

“I’m going through Lyndhurst myself,” he said, with a fine assumption of carelessness. “It’s only about twelve miles, and the roads are good. If you’d allow me to put your bag on the carrier, and you could take my machine, and I could walk alongside—or, better still, there’s a bit of a hill, and I could give you a lead——”

Miss Alderney hesitated, but only for a moment. She was clearly at the end of her resources, and Mr. Pottle had a good, honest, if somewhat round and freckled, face.

“You are very kind,” she said. “I don’t know how to thank you.”

“No thanks at all,” said Mr. Pottle, who was already busy with the valise. “It’s a pleasure. My name’s Pottle—Albert Pottle. I’m in the estate line in Clapham, on my holiday. And I’m sure I’m very glad to be of use.”

They set off, then, in a curious procession—the young lady upon the Swift, and Mr. Pottle, with his cap pushed back, walking briskly at her side and occasionally breaking into a trot to keep pace with the machine. He talked as he went, in the cheerful, inconsequent fashion of a man on holiday. He told her about Clapham, and the difficulties of the house-agency business, and the extraordinary behaviour of a client who had insisted on a house with a garden that faced south and a landlord who would not allow cats. Miss Alderney listened and laughed, and the colour came back to her cheeks. It was, she said, the first really pleasant hour she had known for a month.

They were within three miles of Lyndhurst, and had just emerged from a deep lane into a broader stretch of road, when there came a sound behind them that made Mr. Pottle turn his head. It was the rapid whirr of a bicycle, and a large, portly man in a black Norfolk jacket was coming down the hill at a pace that was altogether reckless. His face was purple with exertion and his hat was jammed down upon his brows.

“That’s him!” cried Miss Alderney, with a little gasp. “That’s Mr. Chaffey!”

Mr. Pottle had barely time to consider the situation before the pursuer was upon them. Mr. Chaffey came to a skidding and undignified halt, flung his machine upon the grass, and advanced upon them with a trembling forefinger.

“Ha!” he shouted, in a voice that was very like a bark. “I’ve caught you! I’ve caught you both! This is a pretty business! A pretty business, upon my soul! You—you scoundrel! You infernal young rascal! What do you mean by it?”

Mr. Pottle, though his heart beat fast, stood his ground. He was a clerk, but he was an Englishman, and he had a certain fundamental decency.

“I don’t know what you mean,” he said, with as much dignity as he could muster. “I met this young lady on the road. She was in distress. I am escorting her to her aunt’s at Lyndhurst. That is all.”

“All!” foamed Mr. Chaffey. “All! Do you take me for a fool? You—you meet a girl in a lane—you, a stranger—and you think I don’t know what’s at the bottom of it! You’ve been plotting together! You’ve been—you’ve been——”

“Mr. Chaffey!” cried Miss Alderney, in a clear, indignant voice. “You are insulting a gentleman who has been nothing but kind to me. I left your house because I could not bear your tyranny. I am going to my aunt, and I am of age, and you have no right to stop me.”

“Of age!” Mr. Chaffey laughed a bitter, contemptuous laugh. “Of age! And what has that to do with it? You’re a foolish, headstrong girl, and you’ve been decoyed away by this—this——”

“I have not been decoyed!” she cried. “I never saw Mr. Pottle until an hour ago. He is a perfect stranger to me.”

“A likely story!” said Mr. Chaffey, with immense sarcasm. “A very likely story! A stranger! And you on his bicycle! And he carrying your bag! And the two of you as thick as thieves! I’m not a fool, I tell you. I’m not a fool.”

Mr. Pottle, who had been growing hotter and hotter, now broke in.

“Look here, sir,” he said, “I don’t know who you are, but I’m a respectable man, and I won’t be called a scoundrel by any man. I met this young lady——”

“I’ve heard your story,” said Mr. Chaffey. “You can tell it to the police.”

“The police!” said Mr. Pottle, aghast.

“The police,” repeated Mr. Chaffey, with a kind of gloomy satisfaction. “I’ve a good mind to give you in charge. I’ve a good mind to. It would serve you right. A young hussy and a—a——”

“Sir!” said Mr. Pottle, and took a step forward.

Mr. Chaffey took a step back. He was a man of words rather than of action, and Mr. Pottle, though not tall, was broad and solid.

“I—I shall not be bullied,” said Mr. Chaffey, with a nervous glance at the young man’s fists. “I shall take my ward home. She is under age—she is not of age—she is twenty, and her father’s will——”

“I am twenty-one,” said Miss Alderney, with a calm that was more alarming than passion. “I am twenty-one, and I have a copy of the will, and my aunt knows all about it. And if you attempt to touch me, Mr. Chaffey, I shall scream for the police myself.”

There was a moment of strained silence. Mr. Chaffey looked from one to the other, and his purple face grew a shade more purple. He was not an evil man, but he was a stupid and obstinate one, and he had convinced himself that he was the injured party.

“Very well,” he said at last, with a kind of snort. “Very well. Go to your aunt. I wash my hands of you. But I shall write to her. I shall write to her to-day. And as for you, sir,” he added, turning to Mr. Pottle, “if I ever see you again——”

He did not finish the sentence. He picked up his bicycle, mounted it with a great deal of difficulty, and rode away in the direction of Fordingbridge, wobbling as he went.

Mr. Pottle drew a long breath.

“Well!” he said. “Well, I’m blowed!”

Miss Alderney turned to him with a smile that was a little unsteady.

“I am so sorry,” she said. “It is all my fault. I have got you into trouble.”

“Trouble!” said Mr. Pottle. “Bless you, that’s nothing. I’m only glad I was here. But he’s a nasty one, he is. He’d have had the police on me as soon as look at me.”

“He is a very stupid man,” said Miss Alderney, with a sudden flash of spirit. “And a very wicked one, too, to think such things. But it doesn’t matter now. We are near Lyndhurst, and my aunt is a very sensible woman.”

They went on again, but the cheerfulness of the morning had gone. Mr. Pottle was thoughtful.

“I say,” he said at last, “I hope you won’t think me impertinent, but—if you’re of age—why did you run away at all? Why didn’t you just walk out?”

Miss Alderney sighed.

“I tried that,” she said. “He locked me in my room. I had to climb out of the window.”

Mr. Pottle whistled.

“Well,” he said, “I’m glad I was here. That’s all I can say. I’m glad I was here.”

They came into Lyndhurst in the early afternoon, and found Miss Wimple’s house without difficulty.
