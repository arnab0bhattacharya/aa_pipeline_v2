It was a fact, well known in the village of Stebbingford, that Mr. Josiah Marrable, churchwarden and principal upholder of the vestry, was a man of strict and even severe propriety. His word had weight in the parish; his very coat, of a drab so sober that it seemed to have taken its hue from his own moral complexion, spoke of a man who walked by rule. And it was a fact, equally well known, that Mrs. Priscilla Goward, who kept the alehouse at the bottom of the lane, was the only person in the world who knew that this same churchwarden, on certain evenings of the week, when the shutters were closed and the lamps lit, would come tapping at her back door with a stealthy insistence, and would sit for an hour in the little parlour behind the bar, drinking gin-and-water in a silence that was almost funereal, and staring at the fire with the eyes of a man who is trying to read his own thoughts in the coals.

Now, it is not to be supposed that Mrs. Goward had come by this knowledge through any arts of espial. She was a woman of a large and patient habit of mind, who had learned, in the course of a widowhood of fifteen years, to let the world go by her door without asking too many questions of it. But a publican's back door is a sieve through which many secrets must pass, and Mrs. Goward had simply been present, on the first occasion, when Mr. Marrable, having taken a little more brandy than his wont at the funeral of a fellow churchwarden, had found his way to her parlour in a confusion of grief and shame, and had there sat down and wept with the tears of a man who has lost his oldest friend and his last defence against the world. She had given him a chair, and a glass of something hot, and had said nothing; and from that evening, by a species of tacit covenant which neither had ever put into words, he had come again and again, always at the same hour, always with the same air of a man who is committing a crime and knows it, and always to sit in silence, drinking his gin-and-water, and going away at last with a nod and a muttered "Good-night, Mrs. Goward," which had in it something of the gratitude of a condemned man for a gaoler who does not rattle the keys.

The alehouse was a small place, with a sanded floor and a settle of black oak, and a fire that was never allowed to go out; and Mrs. Goward, who was a woman of a comfortable presence and a slow, deliberate speech, had a way with her customers that made them feel that they were not so much drinking as paying a visit. She had a daughter, Nancy, a girl of nineteen, with a pair of quick dark eyes and a tongue that was apt to be too ready for her mother's liking; and it was Nancy who first brought home the news, one Saturday evening in March, that Mr. Marrable was going to move at the next vestry for the closing of the alehouse, on the ground that it was a nest of idleness and a peril to the souls of the young men of the parish.

"An' he's gotten a paper drawn up," said Nancy, with the exultation of a herald who brings tidings of a battle, "an' he's been to Mr. Toller the lawyer at the county-town to get it made out proper, an' he says as how the parish 'll be a sight the better for it, an' he'll not rest till it's done."

Mrs. Goward was standing at the bar, polishing a glass with a deliberation that was not so much the result of care as of a habit of mind that refused to be hurried by any event, however startling. She set the glass down, and looked at her daughter with an eye that was not without a certain humorous patience.

"An' what does thy father's old friend, Mr. Marrable, say to that?" she asked, "for he's been a customer of mine these many years, an' I reckon he'll not be for turning me out of my living without a word."

Nancy stared. "Why, mother, 'tis Mr. Marrable hisself as is doing it. He's the churchwarden, an' he says as how the alehouse is a scandal to the parish."

Mrs. Goward said nothing for a moment. She took up another glass, and held it to the light, and breathed upon it, and rubbed it with her cloth, and set it down again. Then she said, in her slow way, "I reckon he'll not do it, Nancy. I reckon he'll think better of it."

But the weeks went on, and it became plain that Mr. Marrable was not thinking better of it. He had the vestry with him; the parson, a mild and well-meaning man who was apt to mistake the voice of the churchwarden for the voice of the parish, had given his consent; and the paper was to be presented at the Easter vestry, and the alehouse was to be closed from the following Michaelmas. Mrs. Goward heard the news with the same deliberation with which she had heard everything else. She went about her business, drawing the ale, and serving the bread and cheese, and listening to the talk of the village, which was all on one side, for the young men were afraid to speak up for the alehouse, and the old men said that it was a shame, but that Mr. Marrable was a good man and knew best. And all the while Mrs. Goward kept her own counsel, and said nothing.

But there came an evening in the week before Easter when Mr. Marrable came tapping at the back door as usual, and sat down in the little parlour, and drank his gin-and-water in silence, and stared at the fire. And Mrs. Goward, who had been expecting him, came in and stood before him with her hands folded on her apron, and looked at him for a long time without speaking.

"Mr. Marrable," she said at last, "I've been thinking as how you're going to close my house at Michaelmas."

The churchwarden started, and his face, which had been pale, grew paler. "It is a matter of public duty, Mrs. Goward," he said, with a voice that was meant to be firm and was only harsh. "The vestry has decided. I am but the instrument of the parish."

"Ah," said Mrs. Goward, "I reckon the parish doesn't know as how its churchwarden comes here of a Tuesday evening to drink his gin-and-water in my back parlour."

There was a silence. Mr. Marrable set down his glass, and his hand trembled, and he looked at her with a kind of horror in his eyes, as a man might look who sees the ground open under his feet.

"Mrs. Goward," he said, "you would not—you could not—"

"I'm not a-going to do it," said Mrs. Goward, quietly. "I'm not a-going to do it, Mr. Marrable. I've known you too long, an' I've seen you in trouble too often, to do you a mischief. But I'm thinking as how you might do me a mischief, an' that's a different thing. You're a good man, Mr. Marrable, an' a just man, an' I'll not say a word against you; but you're a man as has a secret, an' a secret is a heavy thing to carry, an' it makes a man hard sometimes, without his knowing it. I reckon you've been a-thinking as how, if you could shut up my house, you'd shut up the place where your own sin was committed, an' that's a kind of religion as I don't hold with. It's like the man in the Bible as was for burning the house where he'd lost his money, for fear he should go back to it."

The churchwarden sat with his head bowed, and his hands clasped between his knees, and said nothing. The firelight played upon his drab coat, and upon the grey hairs at his temples, and upon the little table with the glass upon it, and upon the face of Mrs. Goward, who stood looking down at him with an expression that was not unkindly.

"I'm not a-going to threaten you," she said, "an' I'm not a-going to ask you for a promise. But I'm thinking as how you'll go home to-night, an' you'll think over what I've said, an' you'll ask yourself whether it's the parish as is speaking, or whether it's your own conscience as is trying to make a virtue out of a weakness. An' if you find as how it's the last, why, then, Mr. Marrable, I reckon you'll know what to do."

She went out of the room, and left him alone with his fire and his glass; and after a while he rose, and went out by the back door, and walked home through the dark lane with the stars above him, and the hedges on either side, and the thought of what he had done and what he might yet do, going with him like a companion. And at the Easter vestry, when the paper was presented, Mr. Marrable rose, and said, in a voice that was not quite steady, that he had been thinking over the matter, and that he was not sure it was the part of Christian charity to close a house that had been a comfort to many a poor man in his trouble, and that he would beg the vestry to let the matter rest for a while. And the vestry, who were accustomed to follow his lead, let it rest; and the alehouse at the bottom of the lane was not closed at Michaelmas, nor for many a Michaelmas after.

And Mrs. Goward, when she heard of it, said nothing, but went about her business with the same deliberation as before; and if Mr. Marrable came no more to her back door, she never spoke of it to a living soul, but kept his secret as she had kept it from the first, in the quiet of her own heart, where it lay like a stone at the bottom of a well, unseen and unfathomed, but making a silence round about it that was not without its own solemnity.
