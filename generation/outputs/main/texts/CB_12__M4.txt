The rain had beat against the mullioned windows since noon, and now, at four o’clock, it ran in steady rivulets down the panes, blurring the grey moor beyond into a soft and shifting veil. Within the parlour of Haythorn Parsonage, the fire burned with a sullen, hissing pertinacity, as if it too were out of humour with the day. I sat at the table with my needle, hemming a sheet for the spare chamber, though my eyes, I confess, wandered oftener to the window than to my work; for the weather without seemed but a fit image of the dull confinement of my life within.

My sister, Ellen, was in her accustomed corner by the hearth, wrapped in the old plaid shawl that had served us both in turns these ten years. She had a book upon her knee, but she did not read it. Her thin hands lay folded upon the cover, and her gaze was fixed upon the embers with that dreamy intensity which I had long since learned to interpret as the prelude to one of her silent, inward seasons. She was five years my junior, and though she had never known a day of robust health, her spirit possessed a quality of stillness that I, with all my restless vigour, could neither emulate nor comprehend.

“You are idle, Nell,” said I, with a lightness I did not feel. “Shall I read to you? There is the new number of the Review, and a paper on the Corn Laws that will serve to sharpen your wits.”

She turned her head slowly, and the firelight caught the pallor of her cheek and the faint, patient smile that always hovered at her lips. “Presently, Margaret. I am thinking.”

“Thinking! You are ever thinking. And what, pray, is the subject of such deep meditation? The state of the roads? The price of candles?”

“A verse,” said she, simply. “It came to me with the rain.”

I set a stitch with unnecessary force. “A verse! You and your verses. They will never buy you a new shawl, Nell, nor put a guinea in your purse.”

“No,” she answered, without offence. “But they put something in my heart, and that is worth the having.”

I said no more. It was an old wound between us—not a quarrel, for Ellen never quarrelled, but a silent divergence of path. She wrote her little poems in a copybook she kept hidden in the drawer of her work-table, and she showed them to no one, not even to me. I had found them once, by chance, years ago, and had read a page or two before shame and a strange, hot anger had made me close the book. The lines were delicate, melancholy, touched with a music I could not name; and I had felt, in that moment, a pang that was not all admiration. I had crushed it down then, as I crushed down most feelings that did not serve the day’s necessity. But it had left its mark.

The sound of hoofs in the lane broke upon my reverie. I rose and went to the window. A gig was labouring up the wet hill, and in it sat a man in a dark cloak, his hat pulled low against the weather. “A visitor,” said I, in some surprise. “Who can it be, in such rain?”

Ellen had risen too, and stood beside me, her hand upon the sill. “It is Mr. Lacy,” she said, quietly. “He wrote to Papa last week. He is making a tour of the northern parishes, collecting antiquities.”

“Mr. Lacy the critic? The London man?”

“The same.”

I looked at her sharply. Her face was calm, but a faint colour had risen in her cheeks, and her eyes, fixed upon the approaching gig, held a light I had not seen there for many a day. It was the light of one who sees a messenger from a world she has long since resigned.

My father received Mr. Lacy with the grave courtesy of a country clergyman, and we were all presently assembled in the parlour, the fire built up, the best china brought out, and Ellen, at my father’s bidding, seated in the chair of honour, for our guest had expressed a particular desire to make her acquaintance. He was a spare, grey man, with keen eyes and a manner that was at once polished and abrupt. He spoke of cathedrals and crumbling abbeys, of old ballads and the decay of rural customs; and Ellen, to my astonishment, answered him with a quiet readiness that showed she had not dreamed away all her solitary hours. She quoted a line from an old Northumbrian song; she described a tradition of the moor-folk concerning a fairy well. Mr. Lacy listened with growing attention.

“You have a fine memory, Miss Margaret,” said he, turning to me, “but I perceive your sister is the antiquary.”

“I am nothing of the sort,” I answered, with a laugh that sounded hollow in my own ears. “I am the practical one. Ellen has the poetry; I have the mending.”

“Poetry?” said he, quickly. “You write, then, Miss Ellen?”

She flushed deeply. “A little—for my own amusement only. It is nothing.”

“Nothing that a critic should see, I am sure,” I put in, with a smile I meant to be kind. “Ellen is very modest. Her verses are for the drawer, not the press.”

But Mr. Lacy was not to be turned aside. He pressed her with a gentle persistence, and at last, at my father’s suggestion, she rose and fetched the copybook. She would not read aloud herself; her voice failed her. So Mr. Lacy took the book and read, in his clear, measured tones, a poem upon the moor in winter—a poem of bleak winds and frozen streams, of a heart that waits in silence for a spring that never comes. The words fell into the quiet room like stones into still water. I sat with my needle in my hand, and I felt the colour drain from my face.

When he had finished, he closed the book and was silent for a moment. Then he said, “Miss Ellen, I have read much verse in my time, and much of it is forgotten before the ink is dry. This will not be forgotten. You have the true gift.”

Ellen said nothing. She looked at her hands, and her eyes were bright with tears. My father beamed with a pleasure he did not often show. And I—I rose from my chair, and I said, “You are very kind, Mr. Lacy. Ellen has always been clever. But you must not encourage her too much; she is an invalid, and her strength is not for such exertions.”

The words were out before I could stop them, and they sounded in my own ears like the crack of a whip. Ellen’s eyes lifted to mine, and in them I saw a wound that went deeper than any words of mine could reach. Mr. Lacy looked at me with a cool, appraising glance that made me ashamed.

“I think, madam,” said he, “that the strength of the spirit is not always measured by the strength of the body. Your sister has written what will live. That is a great thing.”

I could not answer. I gathered up my work and left the room, and I stood in the dark passage with my hands pressed to my burning cheeks. I had given up my youth to this house, to the care of my father and my sister; I had buried my own small talents in the daily round of duty; and now, in a single hour, the world had come to the door and passed me by to crown the one who had done nothing but dream. I hated myself for the envy that rose in me, black and bitter as the moorland bog. I hated my pettiness, my hardness, my ungenerous heart. And yet I could not cast it out.

That night, when the house was still, I went to Ellen’s room. She was awake, her candle burning low, and the copybook lay open on the counterpane. I sat down beside her and took her hand.

“Nell,” said I, “I was a fool and a brute this afternoon. Forgive me.”

She turned her head on the pillow and looked at me with those patient eyes. “There is nothing to forgive, Margaret. I know what you have sacrificed. I know it better than anyone.”

“And yet I would not have you sacrifice your gift,” I said, and the words came hard. “Mr. Lacy is right. Your verses are—they are not nothing. They are a part of you, and I have been jealous of that part, because it is the part I cannot reach.”

She pressed my hand. “You have the greater part,” she said. “You have the doing. I have only the dreaming.”

I kissed her forehead and left her, and I went down to the parlour and stood at the window. The rain had ceased, and the moon was breaking through the clouds, silvering the wet moor and the distant fells. I thought of the poem she had written, of the heart that waits in silence for a spring that never comes, and I knew that I had been waiting too—waiting for a life I had never been given, and blaming her because she had found, in her quiet way, a life within the life. The jealousy was still there, a dull ache beneath my ribs. But I would not let it rule me. I would learn to be glad of her gift, even if I could not share it. And perhaps, in time, the gladness would become real.
