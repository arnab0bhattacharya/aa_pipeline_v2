The letter had come at noon, and Clara had read it in the gardens of Blackwood Hall with the September sun warm against her back, and now, at dusk, she sat in the window seat of the library waiting for him to come, her fingers still ink-stained from the reply she had not yet written. The letter itself lay folded on the sill beside her. She had read it so many times the creases were softening into velvet.

The letter was brief. It spoke of his nine years as her guardian—since she was ten, since her mother had died and her father before that, since she had arrived at Blackwood a small bewildered thing with black ribbons on her sleeves. It spoke of his duty and his affection, and then it set duty aside and said plainly that he loved her, that he had loved her for longer than he perhaps ought, and that he would be honoured if she would be his wife. It was signed, *Yours, as ever, R.*

Richard Alderman. She had always called him Mr. Alderman, though he had told her a hundred times she might use his Christian name. He was forty-three. She was nineteen. He was wealthy in the way of men who had always been wealthy and wore it lightly—Blackwood was the smallest of his properties, a minor seat given over to his steward, but to Clara it had been the whole world. Its pale stone walls, its gardens that ran down to the ancient oaks at the river, its library with its twelve thousand books—he had let her read every one.

She had been happy here. That was the thing. She had not been a prisoner or a poor relation or a burden. He had given her a tutor when she asked for one, a horse when she outgrew the first, a piano when she showed an interest. He had asked nothing in return except that she eat well and ride in the mornings and tell him at dinner what she had read that day. She had grown up in his care like a tree planted in good soil, and she loved him for it. She was fond of him. She was grateful. She had told herself for a year, perhaps longer, that fondness and gratitude were enough, that they were more than most women were given, and she believed it.

She had nearly written her yes. She had the pen in her hand and the paper before her. Then Mrs. Hallet, the housekeeper, had come to light the candles, and in the way of Mrs. Hallet, had begun to talk, and Clara, who was fond of Mrs. Hallet too, had listened.

Mrs. Hallet had been at Blackwood for thirty years. She had known Mr. Alderman when he was young, when he had first inherited from his uncle. She had known the house before Clara, before the tutors and the pianos. She had known it when there had been another young woman here, one who had stayed in this very room, in the chamber that was now Clara’s.

“Your mother,” Mrs. Hallet said, setting the candle on the desk. She said it the way she said most things—calmly, without emphasis, as though it were a fact about the weather. “She was engaged to Mr. Alderman, you know. Before she met your father.”

Clara had stared at her.

“I thought everyone knew,” Mrs. Hallet said, though her expression suggested she had known perfectly well that Clara did not. “It was a long time ago. 1812, perhaps. She came to stay with her aunt in the village. Mr. Alderman was just down from Oxford. They were engaged within the season.”

“What happened?”

Mrs. Hallet had lit the second candle. “She met your father at the summer assizes in Maidstone. He was a solicitor’s clerk. He had nothing at all. She broke the engagement within the month.”

Clara had asked more, but Mrs. Hallet had little else to give. The engagement had been brief, the break clean. Her mother had married her father within the year and gone to live in a small house in Canterbury. Mr. Alderman had gone travelling. When Clara’s parents had died—her father of a fever in 1824, her mother of grief or consumption or the same fever the year after—he had come back from Italy and taken the girl in. That was all Mrs. Hallet knew or would say.

Now Clara sat with this knowledge, turning it over the way she would turn a strange object found in a drawer. Her mother had lived in this house. Her mother had sat in this window, perhaps, looking out at the same darkening lawn. Her mother had worn Mr. Alderman’s ring and had given it back and chosen instead a man with nothing, a solicitor’s clerk from Canterbury, and had lived in a narrow house with thin walls and a small garden where she had planted roses and died at thirty-two.

She tried to find the shape of her mother’s choice and could not. Mr. Alderman was kind. He was handsome in his way—tall, still dark-haired, with the quiet authority of a man who had never needed to raise his voice. He would be a good husband. She knew this as certainly as she knew anything. There would be no cruelty in him, no indifference. She would live at Blackwood or at his larger seat in Surrey, and she would read her books and play her piano and bear his children, and she would be comfortable, and she would be grateful, and she would be fond.

But Mrs. Hallet’s words kept turning in her mind: *She broke the engagement within the month.* Something in her mother had chosen otherwise. Something in her mother had looked at this house, at these gardens, at this quiet and generous man, and had said no. Clara tried to imagine what her mother had felt—a sudden and overwhelming love, perhaps, the kind that undid propriety and prudence. Or perhaps something subtler. Perhaps a fear, a sense that comfort was not the same as life, that to be grateful forever was its own kind of slow dying.

She heard his step in the hall before she saw him. He knocked and entered, as he always did, with a kind of deference that she had once found charming and now found unsettling. He was dressed for dinner, his coat dark, his cravat neatly tied. He looked at her and smiled, and she saw in his smile the thing she had not seen before, or had not let herself see—a patience, a waiting, as though he had been coming to this room for nine years and had only now arrived.

“Clara,” he said. “I hope you received my letter.”

“I did.”

He waited. He did not press her. He never pressed her. That was part of his kindness, and she felt the weight of it now, the way it made refusal feel like cruelty, the way it made every kindness a thread in a net she had not noticed being woven.

“I have been speaking with Mrs. Hallet,” she said.

A change crossed his face—brief, quickly managed, but she saw it. A tightening around the eyes. He moved to the mantelpiece and stood with one hand resting on it, looking not at her but at the fire.

“Ah,” he said.

“She told me about my mother.”

“Mrs. Hallet tells many things.” His voice was even. “I should have told you myself. I had intended to, when the time was right.”

“Was I the time?” she asked. “Or was I something else?”

He looked at her then, and she saw that he understood. There was no surprise in his expression, only a kind of weariness, as though he had known this conversation would come and had been dreading it.

“You are not your mother, Clara.”

“No,” she said. “But you loved her first.”

He was silent. The fire shifted. A log settled with a soft thud, sending up sparks.

“I was twenty-three,” he said. “She was eighteen. We were children, both of us. What I felt then is not what I feel now.”

“But you brought me here. You raised me. You gave me everything. And now you ask me to be your wife. You must see what it looks like.”

“I see what it is,” he said, and there was a sharpness in it, the first she had ever heard from him. “I see a man who has cared for a girl and watched her become a woman and fallen in love with that woman. I see nothing wrong in it.”

“Neither do I,” she said, and meant it, and hated the way his expression softened at her words, the hope that bloomed there. “But I need to understand it. I need to know whether you see me, Mr. Alderman—truly see me—or whether you see something left behind.”

He turned back to the fire. He did not answer for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was quiet.

“I have asked you to marry me, Clara. I have not asked you to be a ghost.”

She rose from the window seat. The letter lay where she had left it, folded, softening at the creases. She picked it up and held it.

“I will give you my answer tomorrow,” she said. “I need the night.”

He bowed his head. She left him there, standing before the fire, and climbed the stairs to her chamber—her mother’s chamber, she understood now—and sat on the edge of the bed and looked at the walls that had once been her mother’s walls and tried to find herself somewhere within them.
