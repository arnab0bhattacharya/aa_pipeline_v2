The tempestuous winds of the March equinox beat furiously against the mullioned windows of Pennarth Cliff, my solitary retreat upon the wild Kentish coast, as if the very elements conspired to awaken the slumbering furies of my soul. I had lived sixty years beneath the weight of a silence so profound that it had become a second skeleton, housing the marrow of a secret anguish; yet, on that eve, the fragile seal was to be broken, not by my own tremulous hand, but by the unrelenting zeal of a youth who knew not the abysses he approached. Mr. Edward Ashford, a historian of tender years and ardent disposition, had sought my parlor, bearing the collected annals of the late war, and he sat opposite me by the waning fire, his eyes alight with that perilous flame which seeks to illuminate the darkest corners of human suffering. He spoke of a mutiny—a tragedy of the deep, suppressed in the year of my youth, upon the frigate *Ardent*—and he asked me, with an eloquence that pierced my very vitals, to narrate the particulars for his proposed volume.

"Sir," I replied, and my voice faltered even as I sought to command it, "you ask me to drag forth from its ocean grave a vessel freighted with unutterable woe. I have drawn a veil over those days, not from cowardice, nor from any misplaced shame, but because the human heart, frail as it is, cannot endure the reawakening of such agonies and survive. I suppressed a rebellion, it is true; I hung the perpetrators from the yardarm; but to recount the scene, to feel again the spray of the Atlantic mingling with the lifeblood of those I had once called comrades, would be to tear asunder the fragile cicatrice that has formed over my wounded spirit. I cannot, I will not, speak of it."

He regarded me with a gaze that mingling pity with a steadfast, almost tragic resolve. "Captain Hargreaves," he ejaculated, "I perceive the depth of your suffering, and I would not willingly inflict a needless wound. Yet, truth is a deity that demands sacrifice, and history, that stern and impartial judge, calls upon you to lay your offering upon her altar. If you will not speak, perhaps this voice, long silenced by the noose, may supply the deficiency." He drew from his bosom a letter, yellowed and frayed by the damp of years, and unfolding it with a reverent hand, he read aloud. It was written by Elias Thorne, the chief mutineer, in the final hours before his execution; it was addressed to his wife, and it told a tale so wildly, so agonizingly different from the narrative I had sealed within my own breast, that I felt the solid earth reel beneath my seat.

"‘I go to my Maker,’ Ashford read, his voice trembling with the emotion of the departed spirit, ‘not as a rebel against lawful authority, but as a martyr to the cries of a starving crew. We were but phantoms haunting the decks of the *Ardent*, our ribs visible through our parchment skin, our throats parched by the scurvy and the brine, while the captain held his feasts in his cabin and denied us even the rations of a dog. I did not seek command; I sought but a crust of bread for the dying. When I raised my hand against him, it was the hand of necessity, driven by the whip of despair. I am hung, and he remains to glory in his triumph; but let the world know that the blood of the *Ardent* cries out from the sea, and that I, Elias Thorne, am not a murderer, but a victim of a tyrant whose heart was forged of the same cold iron as his cannons.’"

As Ashford’s voice died away, and the paper fluttered to the table like a fallen leaf, a sickness of the soul came over me, contagious even to my physical mechanism. My knees knocked together, my teeth chattered, and the current of my blood, clotted by sudden cold, painfully forced its way from my heavy heart. I did not fear for myself, but it was misery to think that I could not defend my own truth against the dead. That those I had condemned might in a few days be as clay-cold in the memory of men as they were in the deep; nor could strength of body or energy of mind ward off the blow of this posthumous accusation. A sense of degradation came over me. Did God create man, merely in the end to become dead earth in the midst of healthful vegetating nature? Was he of no more account to his Maker, than a field of corn blighted in the ear? Were our proud dreams thus to fade? My name was written "a little lower than the angels;" and, behold, I was no better than the dust the tempest scattered. I had called myself the "paragon of officers," and, lo! I was a "quint-essence of dust." I repined that the pyramids had outlasted the embalmed body of their builder. Alas! the mere shepherd’s hut of straw we passed on the road, contained in its structure the principle of greater longevity than the whole race of man. How reconcile this sad change to our past aspirations, to our apparent powers!

"Captain," Ashford said, his tone now soft and almost entreating, "I do not read this to accuse, but to understand. Can you not tell me what truly happened?"

The words seemed to tear from my throat. "You know nothing, youth!" I cried. "You know nothing of the howling gale, the shattered masts, the fevered brains! You were not there when the crew, maddened by the scurvy, sought to cast their officers into the boiling sea, not for bread, but in a paroxysm of delirium! You did not see the bosun’s brains dashed upon the capstan! You did not see the knife at my own throat, the wild, bloodshot eyes of men who had forgotten they were men! I hung Thorne because he was the ringleader, because if I had not, the ship would have been lost and all hands drowned. I did it with a heart of lead, and I have carried his face in my mind’s eye every day since, as if branded there by a hot iron!"

Tears streamed from my eyes. "Again and again," I continued, "will the tragedy be acted; again I must hear the groans of the dying, the wailing of the survivors; again witness the pangs, which, consummating all, envelope an eternity in their evanescent existence. Why am I reserved for this? Why, the tainted wether of the flock, am I not struck to earth among the first? It is hard, very hard, for one of woman born to endure all that I endure!"

Hitherto, with an undaunted spirit, and an high feeling of duty and worth, I had fulfilled my self-imposed task of silence. I had contemplated myself with a fruitless desire of imitation of the stoics. I now offered a few words of explanation and sympathy, not to Ashford, but to the shade of Thorne. I hid my face in my hands, and while I strove to calm myself, I ejaculated, "For a few months, yet for a few months more, let not, O God, my heart fail, or my courage be bowed down; let not sights of intolerable misery madden this half-crazed brain, or cause this frail heart to beat against its prison-bound, so that it burst. I have believed it to be my destiny to guide and rule the remnant of that crew, till death extinguish my government; and to this destiny I submit."

I raised my head, and the firelight danced upon the walls like the spirits of the drowned. "Pardon me, Ashford," I cried, "I pain you, but I will no longer complain. Now I am myself again, or rather I am better than myself. You have known how from my childhood aspiring thoughts and high desires have warred with inherent disease and overstrained sensitiveness, till the latter became victors. You know how I placed this wasted feeble hand on the abandoned helm of human government. I have been visited at times by intervals of fluctuation; yet, until now, I have felt as if a superior and indefatigable spirit had taken up its abode within me or rather incorporated itself with my weaker being. The holy visitant has for a time slept, perhaps to show me how powerless I am without its inspiration. Yet, stay for a while, O Power of goodness and strength; disdain not yet this rent shrine of fleshly mortality, O immortal Capability! While one fellow creature remains to whom aid can be afforded, stay by and prop your shattered, falling engine!"

My vehemence, and voice broken by irrepressible sighs, sunk to my heart; my eyes gleamed in the gloom of night like two earthly stars; and, my form dilating, my countenance beaming, truly it almost seemed as if at my own appeal a more than mortal spirit entered my frame, exalting me above humanity. I turned quickly towards the young historian, and held out my hand. "Farewell, Ashford," I cried, "brother of my love, farewell; no other weak expression must cross these lips, I am alive again: to our tasks, to our combats with our unvanquishable foe, for to the last I will struggle against her."

I grasped his hand, and bent a look on him, more fervent and animated than any smile; then turning away, I strode to the window, and gazed out upon the raging sea. The tide was coming in, the waves leaping upon the shingle like ravening beasts, and in their roar I heard the voices of the dead, calling me to join them in the deep. But I would not. I would live, and endure, and suffer, until the end appointed.

A man last night had died of the plague in the village below. The quiver was not emptied, nor the bow unstrung. We stood as marks, while Parthian Pestilence aimed and shot, insatiated by conquest, unobstructed by the heaps of slain. A sickness of the soul, contagious even to my physical mechanism, came over me. My knees knocked together, my teeth chattered, the current of my blood, clotted by sudden cold, painfully forced its way from my heavy heart. I did not fear for myself, but it was misery to think that we could not even save this remnant. That those I loved might in a few days be as clay-cold as Idris in her antique tomb; nor could strength of body or energy of mind ward off the blow. A sense of degradation came over me.
