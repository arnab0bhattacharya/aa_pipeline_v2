The night was far advanced, and the wind, sweeping over the bare brow of Ashdown Hill, rattled the shutters of the observatory with a melancholy importunity, when Mr. Erasmus Vale, who had for thirty years been the most indefatigable of astronomers, rose from the instrument he had been adjusting, and pressed both hands over his eyes with a gesture of such despair as I have never seen equalled. He had complained, during the past weeks, of a mist that seemed to gather between his sight and the objects it sought; of stars that wavered and grew dim, as if the heavens themselves were withdrawing their light from him; and now, upon this night, when the comet he had predicted and awaited for the better part of a decade should have risen in the north-east, he found that he could distinguish nothing—neither the bright particular star he sought, nor the very wall of the chamber where he stood.

His daughter, Margaret, who had been his constant companion in that lonely place since her mother's death, and who had learned to read the heavens almost as her father read them, though he would never allow that her knowledge was other than a pleasing amateur's trick, heard the stifled sound he made and came to him from the adjoining room, where she had been copying his observations into the great folio that was to be his monument. She found him standing rigid, his face turned upward, his hands clenched at his sides; and when she would have spoken, he silenced her with a gesture so imperious that she drew back.

"It is nothing," said he, "a passing weakness—the glare of the lamp, perhaps—it will pass." But even as he spoke, he knew that it would not pass; and Margaret, watching him grope for the edge of the table, felt her heart sink within her with a presage of that which was to come.

The following days confirmed the worst. Mr. Vale's sight, which had been failing by imperceptible degrees, now deserted him altogether; the oculist who was summoned from London spoke of a disorder of the optic nerve, of the slow but certain extinction of the faculty, and could offer no hope. The old man heard him in silence, and when he was gone, sat for hours in his great chair, his sightless eyes fixed upon the window through which he had so often watched the stars wheel in their courses. Margaret moved about him with a tenderness that was almost anguish; she read to him, she described the heavens to him as she saw them through the telescope—for she had learned, in her years of attendance, to use the instrument with no inconsiderable skill—and she sought, by every gentle art, to soften the bitterness of his affliction. But he would not be comforted.

"What is a man," he cried, "when his eyes are taken from him? I have spent my life in the study of the skies; I have mapped the paths of comets; I have numbered the stars; and now I am as one blind from his birth, and the great work of my existence must be abandoned. The folio is but half completed. Who shall finish it? Who shall carry on the observations? I had thought to leave behind me a name that should endure; but it is gone—it is all gone."

At this, Margaret, who had long meditated the proposal she now made, summoned her courage and said: "Father, I can continue the work. You have taught me to observe; you have taught me the method; I know the positions of the stars as I know the paths of the garden. Let me be your eyes. Let me carry on the observations until the folio is complete."

Mr. Vale turned toward her voice, and his face, which had been drawn with grief, now contracted with a passion that was almost anger. "You!" he exclaimed. "You are a woman, Margaret. You are a woman, and you speak of continuing the work of an astronomer. It is not possible. The delicate adjustment of the instrument, the long watches, the calculation of the tables—these are not for a woman's strength, nor for a woman's mind. You have been a good daughter, and you have been a pleasant companion in my solitude; but do not speak to me of such things. It is a cruelty to mock me with a hope that cannot be realized."

Margaret's cheeks burned, but she did not yield. "You have often said," she replied, "that I have a quick eye and a patient hand. You have praised the neatness of my figures, and the accuracy of my transcriptions. I have watched you for ten years; I have learned your methods; I have even, in your absence, made observations of my own, which I have compared with yours, and found them to agree. I do not ask to be believed upon my word alone. I ask only that you will permit me to attempt the work, and that you will judge of the result."

But the old man was obdurate. He would not listen to her; he bade her leave him; and for many days thereafter he sat in his chair, refusing to be comforted, and speaking of his life as a thing that was ended. Margaret, however, was not idle. She rose each night at the hour of observation, and, mounting the ladder to the great telescope, she made her notes with a care that was almost painful, and reduced them to order in the folio which her father had begun. She did not tell him what she did; she knew that he would only have forbidden it; and she hoped that, when the work was done, he might be persuaded to examine it, and to acknowledge that she had not been presumptuous.

Weeks passed, and then months. The winter came, with its long nights and its clear frosty air, and Margaret, who had never been robust, felt the cold pierce her to the bone; but she did not falter. She learned to manage the instrument with a precision that surprised even herself; she checked her observations against the tables; she discovered a small error in her father's calculations of the preceding year, which she corrected with a trembling hand. The folio grew; and at last, upon a night in March, when the comet her father had predicted should have reached its perihelion, she found it—a faint, misty speck, but unmistakably the wanderer he had foretold—and she recorded its position with a joy that was almost too great for utterance.

It was then that she determined to speak to him. She entered his chamber with the folio in her arms, and, kneeling beside his chair, she said: "Father, the work is done. The comet has appeared, as you predicted; I have observed it; I have recorded it; and the folio is complete. Will you not look at it—will you not tell me whether I have done well?"

The old man started; his sightless face turned toward her; and for a moment he was silent. Then he said, in a voice that trembled: "You have done this—you, a woman? You have watched the heavens, and recorded what you saw, and corrected my errors, and finished the work that I had begun? Margaret, is this true?"

"It is true, father. I have done what I could. I have not your genius; but I have your method, and I have your love of the science. I have not slept the nights that you slept; but I have watched the stars that you watched, and I have tried to be worthy of the name of your daughter."

Mr. Vale was silent for a long time. Then he reached out his hand and laid it upon the folio; he turned the pages, though he could not see them; he traced the lines of figures with his fingers; and at last he said, in a voice that was broken with emotion: "I have been blind indeed—blind in more senses than one. I have been proud and foolish, and I have done you an injustice. You have shown me that the mind is not bounded by the body, and that the love of knowledge is not the exclusive possession of my sex. I ask your pardon, my child. I am proud of you—prouder than I can say."

And Margaret, kneeling beside him, wept; but her tears were not bitter. She knew that she had won the victory, not over her father, but over the prejudice that had held him captive; and she felt that the work to which she had devoted herself was now indeed complete, since it had been acknowledged by the one whose approval she valued above all things. The wind still rattled the shutters of the observatory; the stars still shone in their courses; but within that lonely chamber there was a peace that had not been there for many a day, and a light that was not of the heavens.
