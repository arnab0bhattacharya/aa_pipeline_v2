I do not think that the ordinary man understands the peculiar satisfaction of a machine that works. I mean the typewriter. To him, it is merely a contrivance of keys and levers, a source of noise, and, I am afraid, a symbol of the modern invasion of the feminine sphere. But to me, who had spent ten years—ten years, mark you—in the service of Messrs. Crump & Bellingham, the Linotype Number Four was a creature of precision, a companion of iron and steel that never tired and never, so long as the ribbon was fresh, made a spelling error.

It was the eve of my wedding. To-morrow, at half-past eleven, in St. Jude’s, Kensington, I was to become Mrs. Arthur Penhaligon. Arthur was a good man. There was no denying it. He was a partner in a firm of wine merchants, possessed of a comfortable income, a bald head, and a disposition so kind that it was almost impossible to quarrel with him. He wished to give me every comfort. He wished to take me away from the City, from the noise of the omnibuses and the smell of the fried-fish shops, and install me in a villa at Surbiton, where I should have nothing to do but arrange flowers and wait for him to come home.

It was a generous offer. It was the offer every girl in the typing pool was supposed to dream of. Yet, as I let myself into the office that Tuesday evening, I felt a strange reluctance to let go of the life I knew.

The City of London at seven o’clock in the evening is a place of transition. The great tide of clerks and shopmen has ebbed away, leaving behind a silence that is not quite peace, but rather a suspension of activity. The gas lamps were being lit along Cheapside, casting a jaundiced glow upon the wet pavement. I unlocked the heavy door of Crump & Bellingham, went up the narrow stairs—counting the creaks, as I always did—and entered the outer office.

The air was thick with the smell of dust, stale tobacco, and the peculiar oil of the machinery. It smelled of work. It smelled of independence.

I took off my hat and coat and hung them up. I did not switch on the electric light immediately; the gloom was comforting. I ran my hand over the desk. Here was the inkwell, the bottle of gum, the pile of carbon paper. Here was the small, chipped saucer where I kept my paperclips. These were the artifacts of my sovereignty. For ten years, I had earned my own wage. It was not a large wage—thirty-five shillings a week—but it was mine. I paid my landlady, Mrs. Gable, four shillings a week for a back bedroom in Islington. I bought my own clothes. I bought my own tickets to the music hall when I felt inclined. I was beholden to no man.

Arthur did not understand this. He called it "drudgery."

"It is unseemly, Clara," he had said to me only that Sunday, as we walked in Kew Gardens. "A woman of your refinement should not be tapping away at a machine all day. You shall have a maid. You shall have a motor car, eventually. Why cling to this... this clerking?"

I had tried to explain. "It is not the clerking, Arthur. It is the... the autonomy."

He had looked at me with that puzzled, benevolent frown of his. "The what, my dear?"

"The freedom," I said, simplifying.

"The freedom to be tired? The freedom to eat sandwiches at your desk?"

He meant well. That was the trouble. If he had been a brute, I could have defied him easily. But Arthur was offering me safety, comfort, and a gentle, undemanding affection. He was offering me a future where I would never again have to worry about the price of coal or the durability of my boots. In exchange, I was to surrender the control of my days.

I sat down at my machine. The metal was cold. I touched the keys. *A-S-D-F-G.* The action was ingrained in my muscles. I did not need to look. My fingers knew the geography of the keyboard better than they knew the geography of my own face.

I decided to type a letter. There was no work to be done; the ledgers were closed, the correspondence finished. But I needed to hear the rhythm. I fed a sheet of paper into the roller and turned the knob. *Click-whirr.*

"Dear Sir," I began.

What should I write? I looked around the empty office. The desks of the junior clerks were bare. The window looked out onto the back of a warehouse. It was not a view to inspire poetry. But the machine was inspiring. It was a tool of the new century. It was the thing that had allowed me to leave my father’s house when he died and not have to live on the charity of my cousins. It had given me a room of my own.

"The undersigned wishes to state," I typed, the keys clattering in the quiet room like a sudden hailstorm, "that the contract of marriage, while socially advantageous, entails a cessation of economic activity which the undersigned finds... "

I stopped. This was ridiculous. I was typing a manifesto to myself.

Suddenly, there was a noise from the street below. A heavy thud, followed by the shouting of men. I went to the window and looked down. A dray horse had slipped on the cobbles near the entrance to the alley. Two men were struggling to get the beast up again. The drayman was shouting abuse at the horse; the horse was rolling its eyes, terrified.

It was a brutal, ordinary scene. The struggle of muscle against gravity, of man against beast.

Arthur would have looked away. He would have said, "How distressing," and hurried me along. But I watched. I watched the men heave and strain. I watched the horse scramble and finally regain its footing, shaking its harness. The drayman cracked his whip—not cruelly, but in irritation—and the cart rumbled on.

Life went on. It was hard, and noisy, and required effort.

I turned back to the typewriter. I took the sheet of paper out, crumpled it, and threw it into the wastepaper basket. I sat for a moment longer, listening to the silence of the office.

I thought of Arthur’s villa. I imagined the polished floors, the silence of the suburbs, the heavy curtains drawn against the evening. I imagined sitting in a drawing-room, waiting. Waiting for the clock to strike six, waiting for the sound of his latchkey.

I thought of my room in Islington. The narrow bed, the view of the chimney pots, the gas meter that had to be fed with shillings. It was precarious. It was precarious, but it was alive. Every shilling I put in that meter was a shilling I had earned.

"Goodbye," I said aloud.

The word hung in the air. I stood up and put on my coat. I looked at the Linotype. It sat there, a dark, hulking shape in the gloom. It was not a friend, exactly. It was a machine. But it had been the engine of my life.

I walked to the door and put my hand on the switch. Then I paused. I went back to the machine. I took a clean sheet of paper and rolled it in. I typed one sentence, very slowly and carefully.

*Clara Vance. Typist. 35 shillings a week.*

I left the paper in the machine. I did not turn out the light. Let the night watchman find it. Let him wonder. It was my signature on the world.

I went out into the corridor, locked the door, and walked down the stairs. The street was dark now, the gas lamps flaring. I buttoned my gloves. To-morrow I would be Mrs. Penhaligon. I would be comfortable. I would be safe.

But as I walked toward the omnibus stop, my fingers twitched at my side. *A-S-D-F-G.* I could still feel the keys. I could still hear the clatter. It was a small noise, but it was the sound of my own life, and I knew, with a sudden, sharp clarity, that I would miss it terribly.
