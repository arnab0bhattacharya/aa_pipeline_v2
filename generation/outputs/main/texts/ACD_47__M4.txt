The rain had fallen steadily since morning, and the pavements of Croydon were a dull, shining grey as I turned into the narrow thoroughfare which led to my shop. I had been a soldier once, in the days when the Queen’s commission took a man to the heat and dust of India, and I had learned there that a quiet life is the most precious thing a man can win. Yet it was precisely that quiet life which was now threatened, and by a face I had not seen for fifteen years.

My name is Edward Hartley, and I keep a modest establishment for the sale of tobacco and fancy goods in the town of Croydon. It was on a Tuesday afternoon, when the fog had thickened to the colour of weak tea, that the bell above my door rang and a gentleman entered. He was tall, stooped a little at the shoulders, with a grey moustache and a manner that spoke of the East. His skin had the yellow parchment look which the Indian sun bestows, and he carried a silver-mounted walking-stick which he placed upon the counter with a care that was almost fastidious.

“A packet of your best Turkish,” said he, “and a box of those vestas.”

I served him, and as I did so I chanced to look into his eyes. They were pale, cold, and set very close together, and in that instant the years rolled back and I was standing again in the cantonment at Meerut, in the year ’78, with the smell of dust and cordite in my nostrils. I had seen those eyes before, and I had seen them above a smoking revolver. The man before me was Captain James Corliss, late of the Bengal Infantry, and I had watched him shoot a native servant in the compound of the officers’ mess and swear upon his honour that the man had attacked him.

I am not a nervous man, but I confess that my hand trembled as I gave him his change. He thanked me politely, remarked upon the dampness of the weather, and went out. I stood for a long moment staring at the door, and then I sat down upon my stool and wiped my forehead with my handkerchief. It was fifteen years since that night in Meerut, and I had told myself a hundred times that the matter was buried as surely as the poor fellow in the native burying-ground. Yet here was Corliss, in Croydon of all places, buying tobacco as coolly as any honest citizen.

I said nothing to my wife, for she is of a anxious disposition, but I resolved to keep a watch. The next evening, when I went to lock up, I found the back door of my premises forced and the till empty. That was bad enough, but worse was to come. My old regimental photograph, which had hung above the fireplace in the little parlour behind the shop—the picture of the officers of the Bengal Infantry, taken in ’77—had been taken from its frame. The glass was broken, and the photograph itself was gone.

I am not a fool, sir. I knew at once that Corliss had recognised me as surely as I had recognised him, and that he had come back in the night to remove the one piece of evidence which could place him in Meerut. I went at once to the police, but the inspector was a decent, dull fellow who spoke of tramps and bad characters and could see nothing in my story but the fancies of an old soldier. It was then that I remembered the name of Mr. Sheridan Blake, the private inquiry agent of whom I had heard in the town, and I resolved to lay the whole matter before him.

Mr. Blake received me in a small room above a chemist’s shop in the High Street. He was a lean, dark man of about forty, with a pair of keen grey eyes and a habit of sitting with his finger-tips together and his head sunk forward upon his chest, so that he seemed to be listening with his whole body. I told him my story from beginning to end, and he heard me out without a single interruption.

“You are certain of the man’s identity?” said he at last.

“As certain as I am that I sit in this chair. I saw him fire, and I saw the man fall. There was an inquiry, but Corliss swore it was self-defence, and there were those who were ready to back him. I was a sergeant then, and my word counted for little against an officer’s.”

“And the photograph?”

“It was taken in the mess compound. Corliss is in it, and I am in it, and so are a dozen others. If he has it, he has the proof that I was there.”

Mr. Blake was silent for a moment. “You say he did not recognise you in the shop?”

“He gave no sign.”

“That is the curious part of the business,” said he. “A man who commits a murder does not forget the face of the only witness. He knew you, depend upon it, and he came back for the photograph. But why should he risk a burglary for a picture which he might have bought from you for a shilling?”

“Because he did not wish me to know that he had recognised me.”

“Precisely. He wished to remove the evidence without alarming you. That argues a cool head and a careful plan. Has he left the town?”

“I have made inquiries. He lodged at the Queen’s Arms, but he paid his bill yesterday and took the morning train to London.”

Mr. Blake rose and went to the window. “Then we must act quickly. The photograph is the key to the whole matter. If he destroys it, your story is one man’s word against another’s, and a man who has been fifteen years in the shade will not be easily brought to book. But if we can recover it—” He turned to me with a smile which had something of the schoolboy in it. “You have done a soldier’s duty, Mr. Hartley. Now you must do a detective’s. Can you describe the photograph exactly?”

I described it as well as I could—the group of officers, the native servants in the background, the date upon the border.

“And Corliss—where does he stand?”

“On the left of the group, with his hand upon the shoulder of the man he shot.”

Mr. Blake’s eyes gleamed. “Then the photograph is not merely evidence of your presence. It is evidence of the murder itself. The dead man is in the picture, and Corliss is standing beside him. He will destroy it, and he will do so at once. We must find him before he does.”

He took down his hat and coat with an energy which I had not expected from so languid a man. “Come, Mr. Hartley. We have a train to catch, and I fancy that the game is only beginning.”

We went out together into the wet Croydon street, and as we walked towards the station I felt for the first time since that Tuesday afternoon that the matter was in hands more capable than my own. Yet even as we reached the platform, a thought struck me which I could not shake off.

“Mr. Blake,” said I, “if Corliss knew me, why did he not simply kill me in the shop and have done with it?”

Mr. Blake stopped and looked at me with a curious expression. “Because, my dear sir,” said he, “a second murder in Croydon would be a very different matter from a shooting in Meerut fifteen years ago. He is a careful man, and he has waited a long time. But I fancy that he has not finished with you yet.”

The train came in with a clatter, and we stepped aboard.
