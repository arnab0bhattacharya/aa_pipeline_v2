The Court of the Old Bailey, on that memorable morning in the November term of the year eighteen hundred and forty-five, was a roaring, sweating, seething human furnace, into which the grimy coals of London’s justice were daily shovelled. It was a place of high wigs and low morals, where the law, in all its ponderous, be-wigged majesty, sat enthroned upon a mountain of precedent, and looked down upon the miserable, shivering wretches in the dock as if they were so many curious insects, pinned and labelled for the edification of the gentlemen of the jury. The air was thick with the smell of sawdust, stale humanity, and the dreadful, heavy scent of impending doom.

Into this maelstrom of misery was brought one Silas Cobb, a coal-heaver of Wapping, a man whose very trade seemed to have been indelibly written upon his person. He was broad as a dray-horse and black as a thundercloud, his face a mask of soot and bewilderment, his great hands, which could toss a hundredweight of coal as another man might a tennis ball, now clasped before him in the dock with a pathetic, helpless air. He was accused of stealing three sacks of the finest Wallsend coal from the premises of his employer, a Mr. Ahab Grimshaw, and the case against him was as black and solid as the commodity in question.

Mr. Grimshaw, a man whose heart was evidently a lump of anthracite and whose countenance was a study in sour, self-righteous indignation, had given his evidence with the air of a martyred saint. His clerk, a foppish young man by the name of Figgins, with a watch-chain that jangled like a prisoner’s fetters and a sneer that seemed permanently affixed to his lip, had corroborated every damning word. The missing sacks, the locked yard, the solitary figure of Cobb lingering after hours—it was a narrative of guilt so neat, so perfectly constructed, that it required no embellishment. The only witness for the defence was the wretched Silas Cobb himself, who could only stammer, in a thick, coal-dust voice, “I never done it, my lord. I swear it on my mother’s grave. I never done it.”

And this was the hopeless task that had fallen to the lot of Mr. Arthur Penhaligon, a young barrister of more heart than experience, whose wig sat upon his head with a kind of earnest anxiety, and whose brief consisted of a few scrawled notes and a profound sense of impending failure. He had questioned the witnesses, he had protested, he had implored, but the mountain of evidence remained immovable. As the prosecutor, a portly gentleman with a voice like a bassoon, rose to make his final, crushing address, Mr. Penhaligon felt the cold hand of defeat upon his shoulder.

It was at this precise moment, as the last glimmer of hope was about to be extinguished, that the door at the back of the court creaked open, and a small, sharp-featured boy, the office boy of Mr. Penhaligon’s chambers, one Timothy Timpkins by name, squeezed his way through the throng. Timothy was a creature of infinite resource and impudence, a human ferret who could navigate the labyrinthine alleys of the law with an instinct that baffled his betters. He had been sent an hour before to deliver a summons in a neighbouring court, and his late, breathless arrival now caused a minor stir. He scurried up to the counsel’s table, his eyes alight with a peculiar fire, and, tugging at Mr. Penhaligon’s gown, he whispered with an urgency that was quite electrifying.

“Sir! Mr. Penhaligon! I’ve seen him! The clerk, sir! The sneering one!”

“Not now, Timothy, not now,” whispered the barrister, his eyes fixed on the prosecutor’s devastatingly logical conclusion. “We are lost.”

“But the boots, sir!” hissed Timothy, with the conviction of a prophet. “The boots!”

Mr. Penhaligon, in a moment of desperation, turned to look at the witness, Mr. Figgins, who was lounging against a pillar, polishing his fingernails on his lapel with an air of triumphant boredom. And there, indeed, were the boots. They were not the worn, respectable footwear of a counting-house clerk; they were a magnificent, arrogant, and quite astonishingly new pair of Hessian boots, polished to a mirror-like sheen, with tassels that danced with every supercilious twitch of their owner’s leg. They were the boots of a dandy, a man of sudden and unexplained prosperity.

A spark, long dormant in the tinderbox of Mr. Penhaligon’s mind, was suddenly struck into a flame. He rose to his feet, his manner entirely transformed. The hesitant, defeated young lawyer was gone; in his place stood an avenging angel of the Old Bailey.

“My lord, I beg your pardon,” he began, his voice ringing with a new-found confidence. “Before my learned friend concludes, I must recall the witness, Figgins, to the stand. There is a small matter of his personal attire which I find myself curiously, nay, overwhelmingly, interested in.”

A murmur ran through the court. The judge, a wizened old gentleman who had seen everything twice, peered over his spectacles. “This is highly irregular, Mr. Penhaligon.”

“My lord, justice itself is irregular when it stumbles upon the truth in such an unexpected pair of boots,” returned the young lawyer, with a flourish that would have done credit to a stage actor. “I beg the court’s indulgence for but two minutes.”

The judge, intrigued despite himself, nodded. The sneering Mr. Figgins was summoned back to the witness-box, his swagger somewhat diminished by this unexpected recall.

“Mr. Figgins,” began Mr. Penhaligon, circling him like a terrier who has cornered a rat. “You are a clerk, are you not? Employed by Mr. Grimshaw for, I believe, some years?”

“I am,” replied Figgins, with a sniff.

“And your wages, sir, are, I presume, commensurate with that station? A respectable, but not a magnificent, salary?”

“I do not see what my salary has to do with this case,” the witness retorted, his colour rising.

“Oh, but it has everything to do with it, sir! Everything!” cried Mr. Penhaligon, his voice rising in theatrical crescendo. “For I wish to draw the court’s attention to your boots! Your magnificent, soul-destroying, and quite exorbitantly expensive Hessian boots! When, sir, did you acquire that splendid pair of footwear? Was it before or after the lamentable disappearance of three sacks of Wallsend coal from your master’s yard?”

The effect was instantaneous. The sneer vanished from Figgins’s face, replaced by a sudden, guilty pallor. He stammered, he shuffled his feet, the very tassels on his boots seemed to droop in shame.

“I… I do not recall the exact date,” he mumbled.

“You do not recall!” thundered Mr. Penhaligon, pointing a dramatic finger. “You do not recall the day you transformed yourself from a humble clerk into a veritable Beau Brummell of the counting-house! My lord, I submit that this witness, consumed by avarice and opportunity, is the true culprit! He, with his keys and his knowledge, purloined the coal, sold it to some unscrupulous merchant in the dead of night, and with the proceeds, strutted into this very court, shod in the very evidence of his crime! The prisoner, Silas Cobb, a man of simple mind and sooty hands, was but the convenient scapegoat, the dumb ox sacrificed to cover the tracks of a calculating fox!”

He then called for the court’s officer and demanded that the boots be produced as evidence. The court, now in a state of high excitement, buzzed and hummed. The judge, a slow smile spreading across his ancient face, rapped for silence. The jury, twelve honest men of London, leaned forward as one, their eyes fixed on the now-trembling clerk.

The case, which had been a dead weight of despair, was now a soaring kite of vindication. The prosecutor’s speech was forgotten. Mr. Grimshaw’s sanctimonious face was a mask of dawning horror. The truth, like a shaft of pure sunlight breaking through the perpetual London fog, had illuminated the courtroom. And there, in the dock, the great, bewildered coal-heaver, Silas Cobb, looked on with a slowly dawning comprehension, as if he were watching a miracle performed for his benefit alone. He did not understand the words, the law, the wigs, or the wigs’ wearers; he only understood the tone, the pointing finger, and the sudden, glorious shift in the atmosphere. He was saved. And all because of a pair of boots.
