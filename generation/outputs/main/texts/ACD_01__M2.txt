The morning fog lay thick upon the City, a grey blanket that muffled the footsteps of the clerks and absorbed the clatter of the hansom cabs on the damp pavements. It was a day of such heavy, oppressive atmosphere that even the iron lions at the entrance of St. Jude’s Bank, in the shadow of the Temple, seemed to slumber under the weight of the damp. I sat at my desk, a junior clerk in the paying-in office, watching the clock hands inch toward eleven, when the door opened and in stepped Mr. Silas Vane.

Mr. Vane was a man of seventy, perhaps more, though his spine remained uncurved and his eyes, beneath the brim of a worn felt hat, held a startling clarity. He was a figure of such rigid consistency that he had become, to us junior staff, a sort of living monument. Every morning, without fail, at ten o’clock, he would enter, remove his coat, and place a single gold sovereign on the counter. Every evening, at half-past four, he would return, produce the receipt, and take the coin back. It was a ritual of such precise mechanical nature that it ceased to be interesting and became, in time, invisible. I had watched him for three years. I had seen him in the heat of August and the sleet of January. The sovereign was always the same, worn smooth at the edges, and the receipt was always signed with the same careful, cramped hand.

It was on a Tuesday in November that the routine was broken.

The clock struck ten. I looked up, expecting the familiar shuffle of footsteps, the slight creak of the floorboards. I looked at the door. It remained shut. I glanced at the manager, Mr. Halloway, who was busy balancing a ledger, his spectacles pushed up onto his forehead. He did not look up. To him, Vane’s absence was no more significant than a missing semicolon in a report. But to me, it was a discordant note in a symphony I had come to rely upon. The silence where Vane’s presence should have been felt was not merely an absence; it was a void.

By noon, my unease had grown into a sharp, gnawing anxiety. I excused myself from my duties, citing a sudden headache, and walked out into the mist. I did not go home. Instead, I made my way to a small, unmarked office in Fleet Street, above a chandler’s shop. The sign above the door read simply: *Blackwood & Sons, Private Inquiry Agents*.

The interior was dim, lit by a single gas jet that hissed in the corner. The air smelled of stale tobacco and old paper. A man sat behind a desk that was a chaotic landscape of files and ledgers. He was a small, wiry individual with a face like a dried apple, his eyes twinkling with a predatory intelligence. This was Mr. Blackwood.

“Mr. Blackwood?” I asked, feeling the absurdity of my own request as the words left my lips.

He looked up, not with surprise, but with a weary recognition. “We have been expecting you, Mr. Pym. Sit down. You have the look of a man who has seen a ghost, or perhaps one who has been told a lie he cannot bear to swallow.”

I sat, my hands trembling slightly. “I am a clerk at St. Jude’s. I am here regarding a customer, Mr. Silas Vane.”

Blackwood’s eyes narrowed. “Mr. Vane. The Eccentric. You are not the first to come to me about him, nor the first to be troubled by his habits. But tell me, Mr. Pym, what is it that has disturbed your peace? Has he failed to pay his fees? No, I see. It is his absence, is it not?”

“He has not come in this morning,” I said. “He has not come in for three days. And he has not withdrawn the sovereign. He has never failed to do so in three years. I am convinced, Mr. Blackwood, that this is not mere caprice. There is something hidden here. A secret. Perhaps a crime.”

Blackwood leaned back, steepling his fingers. “You speak of crime based on the absence of a coin. That is a thin foundation, Mr. Pym. But I respect your instinct. You have observed details that others have ignored. Very well. I will look into it. But understand this: if there is a mystery, it is not in the bank. It is in the life of the man who places the coin. And such men are rarely to be found by force. They must be followed with patience and precision.”

He gave me an address, a small terraced house in Kennington, far from the City. “Go there. Watch. Do not speak. Do not interfere. Report to me at six o’clock.”

I went to Kennington that evening. The house was modest, its windows dark. I stood on the pavement, my collar turned up against the chill, and watched. For two hours, nothing moved. The street was quiet, save for the distant rumble of a cart. Just as I was about to give up, a light flickered in the upper window. A shadow passed across the glass. It was thin, angular, and moved with a jerky, unnatural stiffness.

I waited until midnight. The shadow did not return. I returned to Blackwood’s office the next day, and he was waiting for me.

“I sent a man to Kennington yesterday,” he said, his voice low. “The house is empty. It has been empty for a month. The rent is paid in advance, but no one has lived there since October. And Mr. Vane? He died in a workhouse in Whitechapel four months ago. The inquest was brief. A failure of the heart. No relatives claimed the body. It was buried in a pauper’s grave.”

I felt the blood drain from my face. “But I saw him. I speak to him every day. I know him.”

“Then who did you speak to?” Blackwood asked, his tone sharp. “Who has been depositing that sovereign for the last four months? And why?”

I had no answer. I went back to St. Jude’s. The office was empty. The counter was bare. I looked at the ledger, at the entries for the last few months. The receipts were there. The signatures were there. *Silas Vane*. But the ink was different. The handwriting, which I had thought so distinctive, was now a forgery. A clever, meticulous forgery, but a forgery nonetheless.

I went to the manager, Mr. Halloway. I told him what Blackwood had said. I told him about the empty house, the dead man, the forged signatures. Halloway listened, his face pale. He did not deny it. He simply said, “There are debts, Mr. Pym. And there are ways to pay them that are not written in the books. The sovereign was not a deposit. It was a token. A signal. And now that the signal has been broken, the matter is closed.”

He dismissed me. I left the bank, walking out into the fog that was still clinging to the City. I did not know who the man had been, or what the sovereign signified, but I knew that I had touched the edge of a darkness that I was not meant to see. I returned to my desk, but I did not sit down. I walked out into the street, and I did not look back. The city continued its indifferent rhythm, the cabs clattering, the fog rolling in, and I was left alone with the knowledge that the world is full of rituals that conceal rather than reveal, and that the most dangerous secrets are those that are kept by the dead.
