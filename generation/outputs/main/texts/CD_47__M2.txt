The air in the muddy, stinking expanse of the Tooting Common fair was thick enough to chew, a dense conglomeration of roasting chestnuts, damp wool, horse dung, and the sharp, metallic tang of cheap gin. It was a place where respectability went to die, and where the poor, in their ragged multitudes, came to pretend, for a few hours, that they possessed a little power over the world. In the very heart of this chaotic circus, under a canopy of faded blue calico that had seen better centuries, stood Barnaby Fizzle, a man of such profound and dignified thinness that the wind seemed to try to fold him up and post him to the nearest charity. Barnaby was the proprietor of a pig. Not merely any pig, mind you, but a pig of the highest intellectual pedigree, a creature of such startling intellect and moral rectitude that one might have sworn it had been educated at Eton, provided Eton had admitted swine and charged them by the pound.

This pig, whose name was Archimedes, was the pride of Barnaby’s soul and the terror of his creditors. Archimedes could read, could count to seven, and could, with a grimace of immense effort, recite the alphabet in perfect unison with a small, trembling hand. It was a sight to stop the heart of the most hardened cynic, and to make the softest lady weep into her handkerchief, which she would then, with a pious air, drop into the collection box. But tonight, the light in Barnaby’s eye was extinguished, and a shadow, blacker than the soot on his coat, had settled over his face. He sat on an upturned crate, his knees drawn to his chest, staring at the empty straw where the genius of the hog world had once lain.

Gone. Vanished. Stolen into the very bowels of the earth by the devil himself, or so it seemed. For the rival, a smooth-tongued varlet named Silas Vane, who exhibited a dog that could bark in three languages and a cat that could pretend to be a lion, had won the pig at a card game of such treacherous complexity that Barnaby had been forced to wager not only his animal but his dignity, his reputation, and, in a moment of desperate panic, his best frock coat. Silas Vane, a man with a smile like a crack in a windowpane, had taken the pig and vanished into the night, leaving Barnaby to the mercy of the jeering crowd.

It was then that the rescue party assembled, a trio of misfits who had gathered around Barnaby’s plight with the curiosity of magpies. First was Jemmy Pudd, the hot-pie man, a man of such immense girth that he seemed to be made entirely of dough and gravy. He held his trolley, a wheeled altar of grease and heat, and looked upon Barnaby with an expression of sympathetic horror. "A fine piece of work, sir," Jemmy rumbled, his voice like a barge pole striking the mud. "To lose such a clever beast. It’s a tragedy, I say. A proper tragedy. I’d give my last pie to have him back, for the sake of the trade."

Next was Mrs. Gable, the ballad-seller, a woman of such shrill and piercing voice that she could shatter a teacup from across the street. She clutched her satchel of broadsheets, which fluttered in the breeze like the wings of injured doves. "I know a way," she declared, her eyes narrowing with sudden, malicious intelligence. "Vane is vain, sir. Vain as the devil. He loves to show off. If we can lure him into a public display, a moment of vanity, we might just catch him off his guard. We must make a spectacle. A grand, ridiculous, unmissable spectacle."

The final member of this unlikely confederacy was little Lizzie, a girl of perhaps ten years, who sold watercress from a basket so large it threatened to crush her under its weight. She was silent, watchful, and possessed of a face so innocent it was almost deceptive, like a trapdoor in a parlour floor. She said nothing, but her eyes, bright and quick as a sparrow’s, moved constantly, scanning the crowd, noting the exits, watching the shadows.

The plan was hatched in the corner of a tavern, smelling of stale beer and regret. They would go to the main stage, where Silas Vane was about to perform his nightly exhibition. Jemmy would create a disturbance, a glorious, greasy, chaotic disturbance, by tipping his trolley onto the stage, creating a mountain of hot pies that would smell of heaven and burn like hell. Mrs. Gable would sing a ballad of such agonizing, heart-wrenching sorrow about a lost pig that it would bring the audience to tears and draw their eyes away from the stage. And Lizzie? Lizzie would do what Lizzie did best: she would be invisible. She would weave through the crowd, a ghost in a cress basket, and find the pig.

The night was cold, and the mist rolled in off the river, wrapping the fair in a grey shroud. Barnaby, now dressed in a borrowed coat that smelled of tobacco and old sweat, marched to the stage with the air of a man going to his execution. Jemmy pushed his trolley, rolling with a heavy, ominous creak, while Mrs. Gable adjusted her shawl and took a deep, shuddering breath.

The crowd gathered, a sea of faces, curious and hungry. Silas Vane, standing proudly on the stage, saw the approaching procession and sneered. "Oh, is it the pig-man?" he called out, his voice echoing in the damp air. "Has he come to beg for his property? Come on, then, old man. Come and have a look at the new tricks I’ve taught the beast. He can now recite the entire *Iliad* in Greek. Or so I am told."

The crowd laughed, a harsh, barking sound. Barnaby stopped at the edge of the stage, his face pale, his hands trembling. He looked at Jemmy, who gave a nod. Then, with a sudden, violent shove, Jemmy tipped the trolley. It crashed onto the stage, and a cascade of hot pies, still steaming, spilled across the boards, splattering the audience, burning the feet of the front row, and filling the air with a thick, cloying aroma of meat and pastry.

Chaos erupted. People screamed, ran, and slipped in the grease. Silas Vane, his face a mask of fury, lunged forward to protect his animal, which was standing in the corner, looking utterly indifferent to the carnage. "You fool!" Vane shouted, grabbing Barnaby by the collar. "You madman! You’ve ruined my show!"

But Barnaby did not pull away. He simply stood there, staring past Vane, staring at the pig. And in that moment, Mrs. Gable began to sing. It was a ballad of such devastating pathos, a tale of a mother who lost her child in the fog, that the anger in the crowd softened, and the tears began to flow. The audience, confused by the pies and moved by the song, turned their attention to the stage, to the weeping Barnaby and the furious Vane.

It was in this moment of distracted attention that Lizzie moved. She slipped from the crowd, a small, quick shadow, and darted across the stage. She did not go for the pig. She went for the cage. With a speed that belied her size, she unlatched the door and slid the pig out, not away from the stage, but into the arms of Barnaby, who had finally broken free of Vane’s grasp.

Silas Vane, still shouting, turned around, and found the stage empty, save for the pies, the weeping audience, and Barnaby, who held the pig close to his chest, the animal’s snout twitching in contentment. Vane’s face went from red to white to a pale, sickly blue. "Where is it?" he screamed. "Where is my pig?"

But the pig was gone. Or rather, the pig was safe. Barnaby turned to the crowd, the pie-grease on his face, the ballad still echoing in the air, and the mist rolling in around them, and for the first time in a long while, he smiled. It was a thin, fragile smile, but it was real. And as he walked away, the pig in his arms, the crowd parted before him, not out of respect, but out of a sudden, inexplicable awe, as if they had witnessed a miracle, or perhaps just a very good joke. The fair went on, the pies were cleaned up, and the night settled back into its muddy, silent sleep, but Barnaby Fizzle, the man with the learned pig, was no longer alone. He had his companions, his allies, and most importantly, he had his pig. And in the cold, dark world of the fair, that was enough.
