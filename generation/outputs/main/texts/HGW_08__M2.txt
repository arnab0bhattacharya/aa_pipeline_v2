The first thing one must understand about Dr. Armitage’s new method was that it did not create energy; it merely deleted the exhaustion. This distinction is vital, for it explains the peculiar, glassy stillness that settled over the village of Dumbleton in the autumn of 1893. Armitage was not a man of grand theories, but a practical assistant to the late, lamented Dr. Halloway, a man who had died of apoplexy while prescribing for a gouty farmer. Armitage, who had been Halloway’s shadow, his scribe, and his silent partner in the grim mechanics of country medicine, had spent three years in the back room of the surgery, distilling extracts from a rare moss found in the clay pits near the river. He called it *Somnium Suppressa*. It was a clear, colorless liquid, smelling faintly of ozone and old pennies.

The initial results were, to put it mildly, spectacular. Armitage administered a drop to a laborer with the ague, who rose from his bed at dawn to go to work, fresh as a daisy. He gave it to a widow with a failing heart, who walked to the church with a spring in her step that surprised even her own vicar. The town began to whisper. Not with fear, yet, but with the kind of eager, speculative curiosity that ripples through a market town when the price of bread drops or a new electric light is installed in the High Street. Armitage became the talk of the tea tables. "He works night and day," they said. "He doesn’t sleep." It was said with admiration, mixed perhaps with a grudging respect for the sheer brute force of his constitution.

Armitage did not sleep. This was the secret, the terrible, beautiful secret. He had taken the serum himself on a Tuesday night, when the last patient had gone and the rain was hammering against the windows of the surgery. He had expected a mild stimulation, a lifting of the fog. What he had found was a void. A clean, hard, infinite void where the need for rest had been. He had worked through that night, and the next, and the next. He wrote papers for the *Lancet*. He translated medical texts from German. He organized his prescriptions into a system of such elegant efficiency that Dr. Halloway’s widow, a practical woman, wept with pride. He attended to every patient, every case, with a patience that was not human, because human patience is born of weariness and the hope of a break. Armitage’s patience was born of the absence of time.

But men are observant, even in small places like Dumbleton. The change in Armitage was subtle at first, like the changing of the light in a room when the sun goes behind a cloud. He no longer yawned. He no longer rubbed his eyes. His speech became shorter, more precise, stripped of the colloquial padding that binds ordinary men together. He stopped joking. He stopped eating much, for the sensation of hunger seemed to him a minor inconvenience, a biological noise that could be tuned out.

It was Mrs. Puddicombe who first noticed the eyes. She was a stout, sensible woman who sold fish on Fridays and had a chronic cough. One evening, waiting in the surgery for her prescription, she watched Armitage across the counter. He was writing a note, his pen moving with a rapid, mechanical consistency. He did not look up. But his eyes, when he finally raised his head, did not meet hers. They looked *through* her, or perhaps *at* her, with a focus so intense it was frightening. They were pale, the color of the serum itself, and they did not blink. Not once. Not while she coughed, not while she shifted her weight, not while the clock on the wall ticked its slow, wooden beat.

"Doctor," she said, her voice trembling slightly. "Are you well?"

Armitage stopped writing. The silence in the room was sudden and absolute. "I am functional, Mrs. Puddicombe," he said. His voice was smooth, devoid of the warm, gravelly texture it had held a month prior. It was the voice of a machine that had been oiled and calibrated. "Is the cough improved?"

"I don't know," she said, feeling a cold prickle run down her spine. "I don't know, Doctor. You look... thin."

"I am efficient," he corrected.

She left the surgery with a bad feeling in her stomach, a sensation she had not felt since the war, though the war was over. She told her husband, a quiet man who worked in the tannery, and he only shrugged. "He’s a doctor," he said. "Doctors are always odd. They have to think too hard."

But the oddness grew. The town began to notice that Armitage no longer went to the pub on Saturdays. He did not attend the choir. He did not walk in the park. He stayed in the surgery, day and night, the lights burning through the window like a beacon. Patients reported that he did not ask about their families, their crops, their horses. He asked about their symptoms, their temperatures, their heart rates. He treated the body as a mechanism, a complex clockwork that required maintenance. He was brilliant, undeniably so. He cured things that had been deemed incurable. But there was no warmth in it. No hand on the shoulder. No word of comfort.

One night, a young boy, the son of the butcher, came in with a broken arm. Armitage set it with a speed and precision that left the boy screaming, not in pain, but in the sheer shock of the violence of it. There was no anesthetic. No apology. Just the snap of the bone and the cold application of the splint. The butcher, a large man with red hands, stood in the doorway, staring.

"Doctor," the butcher said, his voice low and dangerous. "What are you doing to my boy?"

"He is healed," Armitage said. He was already turning back to his desk, where a stack of papers awaited. "The bone will knit in four days. He will be able to work by next week."

"He is crying," the butcher said.

"The pain is a signal," Armitage replied, not turning around. "It indicates damage. It is not meant to be comforted. It is meant to be resolved."

The butcher stepped into the room. The air in the surgery was cold, despite the fire in the grate. It smelled of that ozone and penny scent, stronger now, pervasive. Armitage stood there, back to the room, his hands resting on the desk. He did not move. He did not breathe, it seemed.

"Turn around," the butcher said.

Armitage turned. The light from the gas lamp caught his face. It was pale, almost translucent, and the eyes were wide, unblinking, and utterly empty. There was no humanity in them. There was only the serum. Only the suppression. Only the work.

"You are not a man," the butcher whispered. "What have you done to yourself?"

Armitage smiled. It was a thin, tight expression that did not reach his eyes. "I am better," he said. "I am free. Do you not see? I am no longer bound by the flesh. I am no longer tired. I am no longer weak. I am perfect."

The butcher raised his hand, but he did not strike. He could not. The sheer, terrifying stillness of Armitage held him in check. It was the stillness of a predator, or a statue. Or a thing that had stepped out of the world of men and into the world of mechanics.

"Go," Armitage said. "Go home. Sleep. You are so inefficient in your sleep."

The butcher left. The doors closed behind him. The silence returned. Armitage sat down at his desk. He picked up his pen. He began to write. The pen moved rapidly, consistently, across the paper. Outside, the rain continued to fall on the sleeping town of Dumbleton, but inside the surgery, the light burned on, and the doctor worked, and worked, and worked, into the endless, empty night.
