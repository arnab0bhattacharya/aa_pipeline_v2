The workshop of Mr. Silas Crabb, Funeral Undertaker and General Mourner, was a place where the air hung heavy with the scent of linden shavings and the damp, earthy smell of new wood. It was a grim, cathedral-like silence that reigned within the four whitewashed walls, a silence so profound that one might hear a ghost clearing its throat. Here, in the heart of the Kentish market town of Fawkham, young Arthur Penrose, with hands stained black by the charred edges of his apprenticeship, stood before a half-finished pine shell, holding a chisel with the reverence of a priest holding a host. Arthur was not a large man, nor a particularly handsome one, but he possessed in his eyes a strange, feverish light that did not belong to the trade of death. It was a light that looked not at the hollows of the caskets, but through them, towards a stage, a spotlight, a roar of applause, and the sweet, intoxicating delusion of being someone other than the son of a washerwoman and the apprentice of a coffin-maker.

It was on a Tuesday, when the fog lay thick upon the market square like a burial shroud, that the travelling company of the illustrious Mr. Ignatius Vane arrived in Fawkham. They came in a squalid, rattle-trap wagon that looked as though it had been pieced together from the wreckage of a shipwreck and the bones of a dead mule. The manager, Mr. Vane himself, was a man of immense girth and negligible dignity, dressed in a velvet coat that had seen better decades, if not better centuries. He stepped into Crabb’s workshop with the air of a conqueror surveying a conquered province, his eyes rolling until they nearly met in the back of his head.

“You, my lad,” Vane bellowed, pointing a sausage-finger at Arthur, who had frozen mid-chisel, his breath held in his lungs like a trapped spirit. “You. The one who looks as if he has swallowed a key and is waiting for the lock to open. I see fire in you. I see a soul that is not content to carve boxes for the dead. I want you in my play. The part of the Page. A small part, mind you, but a part. You shall speak three lines, look sad, and run off. It is a start. It is a beginning. It is the first nail driven into the coffin of your mediocrity.”

Arthur’s heart beat against his ribs like a bird in a snare. He did not speak; he could not. He merely nodded, a single, violent jerk of the chin that nearly dislocated his neck.

When the news reached the ears of Mr. Silas Crabb, the master of the house, the silence of the workshop broke into a roar of indignation that shook the dust from the beams. Crabb was a man of few words, but those few words were struck with the force of a hammer upon an anvil. He was a man who believed that a coffin-maker’s business was to make the dead look comfortable, not to make the living look ridiculous.

“Absurd!” Crabb thundered, his face turning the color of a ripe plum. “Ridiculous! Insane! You, a boy who cannot even sand a lid flat, think to stand before a crowd and pretend to be someone else? You will be the laughingstock of Fawkham! You will bring shame upon this shop, upon my name, and upon the memory of your father, who was a man of solid timber and solid character! I forbid it. You will stay here. You will finish this pine shell. You will learn to make a good lock, or you will learn to starve. There is no third option, and I will not hear of it.”

Arthur, pale as the unpainted wood before him, opened his mouth to plead, but the words died on his tongue, strangled by the sheer weight of his master’s authority. He looked to the corner of the room, where his mother, Mrs. Penrose, sat on a low stool, mending a sock with the rhythmic, monotonous clicking of her needles. Her face was a map of worry lines, etched deep by years of scrubbing floors and washing clothes for the wealthy. At the mention of the stage, her hands stopped. She did not look up; she could not bear to look at her son. She merely began to weep, a soft, stifled sound, like water dripping from a leaky roof. The tears fell into the basin of water beside her, disturbing the reflection of the chandelier that hung above them, a chandelier that was, in truth, a very plain iron hook from which a lamp was suspended.

“It is a waste of time, Arthur,” his mother whispered, her voice trembling like a leaf in a gale. “It is a foolishness. You have a trade. A trade is a shield. A shield keeps the cold out. The stage... the stage is a door that is always open to the wind. You will catch your death in it. You will catch your ruin. Go back to your wood, my love. Go back to your wood.”

And then there was Eliza. Eliza, who sold ribbons and laces at the market stall near the church, and who had, for the past six months, been the quiet, steady beat of Arthur’s heart. She had come into the workshop that very afternoon, ostensibly to buy a small pine box for her cat, a creature of considerable size and dignity. She stood by the counter, her arms folded across her chest, her chin lifted with an air of critical assessment that Arthur had always found both charming and terrifying.

When he tried to explain, with the halting, clumsy eloquence of a boy who had spent his life in silence, that this was his destiny, that the words had been living in his mouth for years, waiting to be spoken, Eliza did not weep. She did not look away. She simply smiled, a thin, tight smile that did not reach her eyes.

“Ridiculous,” she said. The word was dropped like a stone into a well, sinking without a sound. “You, Arthur? Standing on a stage? Looking at the audience? You would be ridiculous. You would be a joke. A man who makes coffins trying to be a man of mirth? It is a contradiction in terms. It is a visual pun. I cannot watch you do it. I would be forced to look at your face, and I do not wish to see you looking foolish. Go back to your chisel, Arthur. It suits you better. It is the only thing that fits you, really. Everything else is too big for you.”

Arthur stood in the center of the workshop, the chisel still in his hand, the wood splintering beneath it. The sound of the chisel against the pine was a sharp, staccato rhythm, *tick, tick, tick*, like the ticking of a clock counting down the seconds of a life that was slowly, painfully narrowing. He looked at his master, who was already turning his back, muttering prayers to the god of commerce. He looked at his mother, who was weeping into her sleeve, the tears soaking the wool. He looked at Eliza, who was examining a ribbon with the dispassionate interest of a jeweler inspecting a flawed diamond.

He realized, with a cold, clear shock that was more terrifying than anger, that he was alone. Not alone in the room, for there were three people there, but alone in his soul. The stage, which had seemed so vast and inviting from the inside of his head, now seemed distant, impossibly far away, like a star that had burned out while he was still looking at it. The part of the Page, with its three lines and its sad look, shrank in his mind until it was no bigger than a splinter.

He set the chisel down. It made a dull, final thud against the workbench. He did not pick it up again. He walked to the door, his footsteps soft on the straw-covered floor, and opened it. The fog rolled in, grey and indifferent, swallowing the light of the streetlamp, swallowing the market square, swallowing the town of Fawkham. He stepped out into the mist, leaving the workshop, the weeping, the judgment, and the wood behind him. He did not go to the wagon. He did not go to the stage. He walked, and walked, and walked, into the grey, wondering if the fog would ever lift, or if he would simply dissolve into it, becoming nothing more than a memory of a boy who once, briefly, had dared to dream of being seen.
