The rain lashed the high windows of the salon in merciless, driven sheets, and the wind made the ancient leaded panes rattle in their casements, as if some vast, invisible hand sought a violent entry into the still, close room. It was a drear, dark afternoon in late autumn at the Pensionnat Hébert, a establishment of some repute situated in the heart of the old Flemish city of Luycke. The trees in the courtyard below bent and groaned beneath the gale; their stripped branches scraped against the stone walls like the fingers of mendicants seeking alms at a cathedral door. Within, however, the salon was warm with a large fire of Flemish coal, which glowed with a sullen, steady heat, throwing long, flickering shadows across the polished oak floor and the severe, straight-backed chairs arranged in formal, unyielding rows.

I stood before the chimney-piece. I had been standing there for a quarter of an hour, and the numbness of suspense was beginning to creep from my chilled extremities into the very core of my heart. Opposite me, on the other side of the wide hearth, sat Madame Hébert, the directress of the school. She was a woman of large frame, resolute features, and an air of unbending, worldly piety. Her cap was of stiff, unyielding lace, and her dark eyes, small and acute, surveyed me from beneath brows drawn together in a stern, suspicious arch. Beside her chair stood Mademoiselle Vane, my chief and most malicious accuser—a girl of fifteen, tall for her years, possessed of a fair, blooming face, and a mind as shallow and treacherous as a quicksand.

I had no friend in that room. I was a stranger in a strange land; an Englishwoman, alone, unfriended, and, to my bitter sorrow, only half-armed with the harsh, guttural tongue of the country. I could read and write the Flemish of Luycke with tolerable correctness, but when my blood was roused, when my spirit was goaded to its depths, the necessary words scattered like frightened birds before the wind, and I was left dumb, or stammering in an alien phrase that sounded barbarous to my own ear.

"You see, Mademoiselle Grey," said Madame Hébert, breaking the heavy silence with a voice that was measured, cold, and fatally deliberate, "the accusation is grave. Mademoiselle Vane declares that she entered the schoolroom this morning, before the hour of assembly, and found you standing by her desk, in your hand a letter—a private letter, addressed to her, which she had concealed in her atlas. She affirms that you were reading it."

"I was not reading it, Madame," I answered. My voice sounded strange and hollow to myself. I strove to keep it steady, to bind down the tumult that raged within. "I found it upon the floor. It had slipped from between the leaves of her book. I was merely replacing it."

"Merely!" echoed Madame Hébert, with a faint, incredulous smile, a smile that cut like a blade. "A convenient word. And you expect Mademoiselle Vane to believe this convenient tale? You expect me to believe it?"

"I expect nothing, Madame, but justice. I am incapable of the baseness you impute to me. My conscience is clear."

"Conscience!" repeated the directress, as if the word were a species of foreign absurdity. "We must deal with facts, Mademoiselle Grey, not with sentiments. Mademoiselle Vane is a pupil of high standing, the niece of a Baroness. She has no motive for falsehood. You are a salaried teacher, a stranger, with no ties of blood to bind you to our interests, and no one to vouch for your character in this city."

I turned my eyes upon the girl. She stood with her hands folded demurely over her grey apron, her fair head bent, but I could see the curve of her lip, the faint, triumphant tremor at the corner of her mouth. She knew her power. She knew that I was defenceless. I had rebuked her, a fortnight since, for a piece of cruel, deliberate insolence toward a younger child, a poor, timid orphan from the colonies; and this was her revenge—subtle, cowardly, and complete.

"It is a lie," I said, and the English word broke from me before I could arrest it. I recovered myself instantly, and spoke in the harsher tongue. "It is a falsehood. Mademoiselle Vane hates me because I did my duty. She seeks to ruin me because I corrected her."

"Silence!" cried Madame Hébert, her dark face flushing. "You forget yourself. You speak of a pupil in my presence in terms of violence. This very heat, this wildness of speech, confirms my fears. An innocent person does not rave."

"An innocent person does," I retorted, the gathered passion of years, the long-smouldering sense of wrong, bursting its bounds at last. "An innocent person, tortured and defamed, will rave. I am no hypocrite, Madame. I do not hide my wrongs beneath a placid brow. I am poor, I am friendless, I am in a foreign land, but I have a soul, and that soul is as proud and as sensitive as the soul of any Baroness who ever breathed. You have no right to condemn me without proof. Where is your proof? The word of a malicious girl against the word of a woman who has never lied!"

I had spoken with a rapidity and a force that surprised even myself. The Flemish words came tumbling forth, rough, perhaps ungrammatical, but vital, burning with the fire of a deep, righteous indignation. Madame Hébert drew back a pace, her brows contracting, as if my words had been a physical blow. Mademoiselle Vane shifted uneasily, and the faint colour in her cheek deepened to a burning red.

"It is enough," said the directress, coldly. "I see that you are not mistress of yourself. I do not argue with an angry woman. You will confine yourself to your room this evening. Tomorrow, I will decide upon your fate. Until then, you are suspended from your duties."

She waved her hand, a gesture of dismissal, imperial and final. I looked at her; I looked at the trembling, guilty girl beside her; I looked at the steady, sullen glow of the fire, and the rain beating against the dark panes. A great wave of loneliness swept over me, a loneliness so vast, so unutterable, that it seemed to swallow up my anger and leave only a cold, gray despair. I was alone. No human being in all the world cared whether I lived or died, whether I was vindicated or cast out.

I turned and walked toward the door. My limbs were heavy, as if leaden weights were bound to them. I crossed the threshold, and the heavy oak panel closed behind me with a sullen, echoing thud. I stood in the long, stone-paved corridor. It was dim, cold, and silent as a tomb. A gust of wind, sweeping down from some unfastened casement at the far end, extinguished the single lamp that hung from the vaulted roof, and left me in total darkness.

I did not grope my way forward immediately. I stood still, and drew a long, deep breath. The darkness was a relief. It hid the burning tears that at last, despite my fierce pride, forced themselves to my eyes. I pressed my hands together until the nails dug into the flesh. I would not weep. I would not yield. I had said that my conscience was clear, and it was clear. The eternal God, who sees the secrets of all hearts, knew that I was innocent. He was my Judge, not Madame Hébert, not the false, smiling girl who had plotted my ruin.

I thought of the morrow, of the certain dismissal, of the journey back to England, of the poverty, the disgrace, the hopeless seeking for another post, with this dark stain upon my name. I thought of the cold, hard faces of English employers, their cautious, suspicious questions, their ultimate refusals. The future stretched before me like a vast, unlit plain, swept by the same bitter wind that raged without, a plain without shelter, without guide, without hope.

And yet, in the very depths of my desolation, a small, fierce spark leaped up—a spark of stubborn, unquenchable defiance. I would not grovel. I would not beg. If they cast me out, I would go with my head erect, my eyes unclouded. I would bear my cross as others had borne theirs before me, with a steadfast, silent endurance. The world might be against me, but I would not be against myself. I would hold fast to my truth, my honour, my integrity, as a shipwrecked sailor clings to the last spar of his vessel in the midst of the howling tempest.

I moved forward at last, my footsteps ringing hollow and solitary on the stones. The wind wailed dismally through the corridor, and somewhere, in a distant part of the house, a door slammed with a sharp, violent report. The storm was rising.
