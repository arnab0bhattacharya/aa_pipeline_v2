Lord Arthur Pomeroy was a young man whose singular beauty was matched only by the perfection of his debts. He possessed that exquisite art of spending money which is the sole consolation of those who have not the wit to make it, and his life in London was a masterwork of elegant dissipation. He was universally admired, partly because he said very little, and partly because what he did say was entirely devoid of meaning. 

It was, therefore, with a profound sense of the dramatic that he sat in the consulting room of Sir William Pym. Sir William was the fashionable physician of his day, a man who treated the livers of the aristocracy with a gravity that was almost ecclesiastical. He looked at Arthur over his spectacles and uttered the single sentence that ruins both lives and reputations.

Arthur smiled a pale, aesthetic smile. He had always felt that dying young was a privilege reserved for poets; it was charming to discover that men of fashion might share in it. "A year," he repeated, adjusting his pearl-grey gloves. "I suppose I must make some arrangements. I have a great many bills to pay, and a great many more I had intended not to. But one must not be selfish, Sir William. I have given my whole life to pleasure. I shall give my last year to virtue."

Sir William, who knew that virtue was merely a lack of imagination, sighed and handed him a prescription. Arthur declined it. He had no intention of taking medicine. He intended to take up philanthropy.

He began the very next evening by refusing an invitation to Lady Windemere’s balloon soirée, an event so entirely dull that his absence was universally considered a triumph of good breeding. He then proceeded to the East End, a region of London he had previously believed to be a geographical fiction invented by novelists. He arrived at a dismal alley in a hansom cab, carrying a purse of gold and a heart full of moral purpose.

His first attempt at goodness was directed at an old flower-seller. She was a wizened creature with an extraordinary talent for complaining. Arthur gave her ten pounds, which she immediately declared was not enough to buy a new shawl, and then accused him of having stolen the money from a nobleman. When he tried to speak kindly to her, she threw a bruised cabbage at him. He retreated to his cab, feeling that philanthropy was a very messy business.

He next endeavoured to reform a young man about town who was rapidly drinking himself to death. Arthur cornered him at their club, a temple of purple velvet and gilt bronze, and spoke at length on the virtues of sobriety. The young man listened with rapt attention, agreed that his life was a hollow sham, wept into his fourth glass of champagne, and was found the next morning in a gutter, holding a copy of a moral tract which Arthur had pressed upon him, and which he was using as a pillow.

Arthur was deeply discouraged. Vice, he reflected, was so wonderfully easy. It required no thought, no effort, no self-denial. It was merely the absence of restraint, which is the natural state of the idle. Virtue, on the other hand, demanded a tedious amount of planning. To be wicked, one merely had to follow one’s inclinations; to be good, one had to go against them, which was both exhausting and deeply unfashionable. 

He decided that London was not the place for moral reform. Londoners were far too cynical to be saved, and far too amusing to be endured. He would go to the Riviera, where the air was soft and the sins were prettier. Perhaps under the blue skies of Cap Ferrat, he might find a soul light enough to lift up.

At a magnificent villa near Menton, he found the Duchess of Staines. She was a woman of exquisite manners and a complexion that cost her chemist a fortune. She was also entirely without a heart, which she considered a superfluous organ, useful only to sentimental novelists and the lower classes. Her greatest pleasure was in ruining the reputations of her friends, a pursuit at which she displayed a genius that was almost artistic.

Arthur, feeling the heavy hand of mortality upon his shoulder, resolved to make her his final, masterful good deed. He would teach her charity. He would show her the beauty of self-sacrifice. He spent a fortnight by her side, reading to her from the works of the Russian moralists, which she found exceedingly amusing, and speaking of the poor, which she found exceedingly boring. 

One afternoon, as they sat on a terrace of white marble, overlooking a sea that was coloured like a crushed turquoise, Arthur made his most impassioned plea. He spoke of the suffering of others, of the duty of the fortunate, of the exquisite joy of giving. The Duchess listened, fanning herself with a fan of dyed ostrich feathers. When he had finished, she looked at him with her beautiful, hard eyes.

"My dear Arthur," she said, "you talk exactly like a parish priest, only you are much better looking. But you are quite wrong. Suffering is the only thing that gives people any character. If one were to relieve it entirely, society would be insufferably dull. The poor are so much more interesting when they are wretched. It gives them such delightful expressions."

Arthur realised, with a sinking heart, that she was entirely right. Wickedness was not merely easy; it was profoundly logical. Goodness was an impertinence. It interfered with the natural order, which was designed by Providence to be aesthetically pleasing and morally inconvenient. He had spent six months trying to make people better, and had only succeeded in making them angry. He had given money to the ungrateful, advice to the foolish, and sympathy to the heartless. He was tired of it. 

He decided to return to London and die as he had lived: with perfect taste and no purpose whatsoever. He visited Sir William Pym to inform him of his decision, and to request a sedative strong enough to make the final months bearable. 

Sir William was in his consulting room, surrounded by the morbid majolica he collected. He looked at Arthur with a strange expression. "You are not dead," he said.

"I am painfully aware of that, Sir William," Arthur replied. "But I have a year."

"A year?" Sir William frowned. "My dear Lord Arthur, you have a lifetime. There was a mistake with the charts. I was looking at the vital signs of a retired colonel from Bath. You are in perfectly splendid health."

Arthur stared at him. The room seemed to tilt. The majolica gleamed with a sudden, sinister brilliance. "You mean I am not going to die?" he whispered.

"Good heavens, no," said Sir William. "You will probably live to be a hundred."

Arthur walked out into the bright, noisy street. The world was before him, vast, shallow, and infinitely entertaining. He had been given his life back. He thought of the Duchess, of the East End, of the Russian moralists. He thought of the heavy burden of goodness, the tedium of self-sacrifice, the sheer boredom of being virtuous. 

He hailed a passing hansom. "To the club," he said, leaning back against the cushions with a sigh of absolute relief. "And driver, do not go via the East End. I have no wish to be improved." 

He had discovered the great secret of life: that it is far better to be selfish and happy than to be good and miserable. The world, he felt, was a comedy for those who think, and a tragedy for those who feel. He had no intention of feeling anything ever again.
