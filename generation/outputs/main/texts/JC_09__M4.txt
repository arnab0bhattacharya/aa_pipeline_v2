The afternoon had declined into that dubious hour when the gas-lamps of the narrow street, not yet lit, seem to promise rather than to give light, and the furniture of Madame Korzeniowska’s little salon—the horsehair sofa, the oval mirror, the table with its photographed dead—stood invested with a mournful and expectant solidity, as if they too awaited a revelation. She had been sitting thus for a long while, her hands folded in her lap over a piece of needlework she had not touched, listening to the rain which fell with a persistent, dreary equability upon the stones of the courtyard. It was a sound that had the quality of a voice, monotonous and accusing, and it filled the room with the chill of an unseasonable dampness. She was a woman of fifty, perhaps, with the fine, worn features of her class, and in her black dress, with the white linen at her throat, she had the air of one who has made a peace with sorrow, a peace that was not resignation but a kind of stern and patient truce.

The bell rang. It was a hesitant, apologetic sort of ring, and she started, for visitors were rare. When the servant-girl announced a name she did not catch, she rose, and the man who entered was young, very thin, and clad in a dark, foreign-looking overcoat, the collar of which he had turned up as if against a wind that was not blowing. He stood just within the doorway, holding his hat in his two hands, and his eyes, which were of a pale, restless grey, went past her, over her shoulder, to the window, to the portrait on the table, to the shadows in the corners of the room, and returned to her face only to flit away again with a sort of guilty haste. He had the aspect of a man who has walked a long way in the rain, and whose thoughts have been walking further still.

“Madame Korzeniowska?” he said, and his voice was low and not unpleasant, but it had the flatness of a man speaking in a room where he fears to be overheard. “I ask your pardon. I am Stanislas Wierzbicki. I have come from Warsaw. I—I have a message for you.”

She motioned him to a chair, and he sat down on the edge of it, still holding his hat, and for a moment there was no sound but the rain and the ticking of the clock on the mantelpiece, a clock which had been her husband’s and which now seemed to tick with a deliberate and ironic emphasis, as if it counted the seconds of some approaching crisis.

“You knew my son,” she said. It was not a question. It was a statement, offered to him like a key, and her voice was steady, though her hands, clasped in her lap, had tightened until the knuckles showed white.

“I was with him,” the young man said, and then, as if the phrase had been too bold, he qualified it. “That is to say, we were in the same prison. In the Citadel. For a time. He spoke of you often. He was—he was very brave, madame. Always.”

She looked at him. She looked at him with the terrible, searching gaze of a mother who has nothing left to lose and therefore nothing to fear, and under that gaze the young man’s eyes, which had been wandering, became fixed upon a point on the carpet at his feet, and his fingers, which had been turning his hat round and round, became still. It was a small thing, that inability to meet her look, but it was a thing she noticed, and it lodged in her mind like a grain of sand in the eye, a small, persistent irritation that would not be blinked away.

“He gave me a message,” Wierzbicki continued, and his voice had grown more formal now, as if he were reciting a lesson. “He said to tell you that he was not afraid. That he thought of you. That you were not to grieve. He said—” and here the young man paused, and a strange, uncertain smile, which was not a smile of pleasure, flickered for an instant at the corners of his mouth and was gone. “He said that the dawn would come, and that you were to remember him as one who had waited for it.”

She leaned forward. “His last words? These were his last words?”

“His last words to me,” the young man said, and his voice was very low. “I was—I was taken away the next day. I did not see him again.”

There was a silence. The rain had ceased, and the room seemed suddenly larger and colder, and the shadows in the corners had deepened. Madame Korzeniowska rose and went to the window, and stood with her back to him, looking down into the grey, wet courtyard where a single, withered leaf clung to a branch and shivered in the wind. When she spoke, her voice was muffled.

“You are very kind to have come. It is a long journey. You will take some tea.”

He refused, with a polite, hasty gesture, but she insisted, and while the servant was gone to fetch it, he sat in the chair and did not move, and his eyes, no longer restless, were fixed upon the portrait of the young man who had died, a portrait in which the face was proud and eager and the eyes looked straight ahead into a future that had never come. He looked at it as a man looks at a mirror in a strange house, with a kind of recognition that is also a kind of dread.

“He was very like you,” he said at last, and she, turning from the window, replied:

“Yes. He had my father’s face. It was a face for the world, not for a prison.”

The tea came, and she poured it with a steady hand, and they spoke of small things, of the weather, of the journey, of the price of bread in Warsaw, and all the while she watched him, and all the while he would not look at her, and the grain of sand in her mind grew and grew until it was a stone, and the stone was a question she could not ask. He was, she thought, like a man carrying a burden he dare not set down, and his politeness was the politeness of a man who is afraid of his own voice. When at last he rose to go, he took her hand and held it for a moment, and his hand was cold and dry, and he said, with a sudden, almost violent earnestness:

“You must believe that he was not afraid. You must believe that.”

“I do believe it,” she said. “I believe it because I must.”

He looked at her then, for the first time fully, and in his pale eyes there was something that was not grief and not pity, but a kind of desperate appeal, as if he were asking her for something he had no right to ask. Then he turned and went out quickly, and she heard his steps on the stairs, and the door below opened and closed, and the rain, which had begun again, beat against the panes with a renewed and dreary insistence.

She stood for a long time in the middle of the room, and the clock ticked, and the portrait looked at her with its proud, eager eyes, and she thought of the young man’s hand, cold and dry, and of his eyes that would not meet hers, and of the message that had been so formal and so careful, and she knew, with a knowledge that was beyond reason, that something had been left unsaid, something that lay between the words like the shadow between the stones of a pavement. She went to the table and took up the photograph and held it to her breast, and the tears came at last, not for the son who was dead, but for the living man who had come to her with a lie in his mouth and a truth in his heart, and who had gone away into the rain carrying his burden alone.
