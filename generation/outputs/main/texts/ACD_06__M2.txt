The rain lashed against the windows of the Baker Street sitting-room, drumming a rhythmic, insistent tattoo that did little to soothe the tension in the air. I sat hunched in my chair, my pipe long since gone out, while Sherlock Holmes paced the length of the rug with the agitated energy of a caged panther. On the center table lay a single, unassuming envelope, its edges slightly frayed, bearing a postmark from Shropshire. Beside it, arranged with meticulous precision, were six other envelopes, each containing a single pressed flower, a small slip of paper, and a date.

Mrs. Agatha Vane sat in the corner, her posture rigid, her hands clasped so tightly around her lapel that her knuckles had turned the color of old ivory. She was a woman of perhaps fifty, her face a map of deep, sorrowful lines, her eyes red-rimmed but bright with a feverish intensity. She had come to us that morning, driven by a confusion that had slowly curdled into terror, yet she had kept her composure until this very moment.

"It is the seventh, Mr. Holmes," she said, her voice thin and trembling. "It arrived this morning. A foxglove. From Shropshire."

Holmes stopped pacing. He turned to her, his pale eyes narrowing as he took in her face. "You say, Mrs. Vane, that your late husband, Mr. Arthur Vane, was a man of considerable scientific interest. A botanist, if I may recall your introduction."

"A devoted one," Mrs. Vane replied. "He spent the last ten years of his life cataloguing the flora of the British Isles. He was meticulous, Mr. Holmes. Obsessively so. Every specimen he collected was pressed, labeled, and filed with a precision that bordered on the religious."

"And these flowers?" Holmes asked, gesturing to the collection on the table.

"They are not merely flowers," she whispered. "They are the final entries in his logbook. But he died six months ago, Mr. Holmes. He is in the ground. So who is sending them?"

Holmes picked up the first envelope, the one from Cornwall. He held it up to the light, examining the watermark of the paper, the specific shade of the ink used for the address. It was a common practice, he noted, but the choice of paper was distinct—high-quality, heavy stock, the kind used by the better stationers. He moved to the next, from Devon. Then Sussex. Then Kent.

"Observe, Watson," Holmes said, his voice taking on that sharp, clinical edge that I knew signaled a breakthrough. "Look at the dates on the slips. Do they correspond to the days of the week?"

I leaned forward, squinting at the small cards. "No. They are spaced irregularly. Every three or four days, perhaps. But the locations..."

"The locations," Holmes interrupted, his eyes gleaming, "form a pattern. Mrs. Vane, when you first began receiving these, did you notice a connection between the counties?"

Mrs. Vane shook her head slowly. "I thought it was a prank. A cruel joke by some former colleague of Arthur’s. But the flowers... they are rare. The foxglove is a specific variety, *Digitalis purpurea*, found only in the wild chalk downs. The one from Kent was a rare orchid, *Orchis militaris*. These are not random. They are collected."

"And the route," Holmes said, turning to the large map of Great Britain that hung on the wall. He took a red pin and a blue pin from his pocket. "Allow me. Cornwall. Devon. Sussex. Kent. Shropshire." He placed the pins in the appropriate counties. "And the one we have just received? Shropshire is the last. But look, Mrs. Vane. Look at the line they trace."

I looked up. The pins did not form a circle, nor a straight line. They traced a jagged, winding path across the southern and central parts of England.

"I do not see it," I admitted.

"You do not see it because you are looking for a geographic shape, Watson," Holmes said, a faint smile playing on his lips. "You are looking for a map. But Mr. Vane did not think in geography. He thought in time. Mrs. Vane, do you recall the last summer your husband was alive? Did he go on a tour?"

Mrs. Vane’s face drained of color. She stood up, her chair scraping harshly against the floorboards. "The Great Botanical Tour," she breathed. "He had planned it for years. To visit the specific sites where he had previously made his most significant discoveries. He was to leave in May."

"And he left," Holmes said softly. "Did he not?"

"He went to Cornwall first," she said, her voice rising in a panic. "Then Devon. He wrote to me from a small inn in Torquay. Then he was in Sussex, staying with a friend. Then Kent. And then..." She stopped, her breath hitching. "And then Shropshire. He was to stay with his brother in Shropshire for the final month. But he never returned from Shropshire. He died of a sudden fever. A stroke, the doctors said."

Holmes moved to the table and picked up the foxglove. He held it between his thumb and forefinger, examining the brittle petals. "A stroke," he mused. "And yet, here is the foxglove, pressed with care, sent from Shropshire. And here, on the slip of paper, is the date. June 12th. The very day he died."

"The others?" Mrs. Vane asked, her eyes wide.

"The orchid from Kent was sent on May 20th. The day he left Kent. The fern from Sussex, May 10th. The day he arrived there." Holmes turned to face her, his expression grave. "Mrs. Vane, these are not posthumous tributes. They are a record. Someone is following your husband’s route. Not to pay homage, but to complete the journey. And they are doing so with a precision that suggests they know exactly where he was, exactly when he was there, and exactly what he collected."

"But why?" I asked. "Why send the flowers to the widow? It seems... macabre."

"Or perhaps," Holmes said, "it is a message. A clue. Or a threat. We must determine who has access to his logbooks. Who knew of the tour? Who had a motive to disturb his peace, or to claim his legacy?"

Mrs. Vane sank back into her chair, looking as though she might faint. "My husband’s brother, Edward. He was in Shropshire. He was with Arthur when he died. But Edward is a man of few words and less conscience. He stood to inherit Arthur’s collection, which was worth a small fortune to the right collector. But why send the flowers? Why wait until now?"

Holmes picked up the Shropshire envelope again. "The ink is fresh, Mrs. Vane. But look at the corner. There is a smudge. A slight discoloration. It is not ink. It is pollen."

He held the envelope up to the light again. "Foxglove pollen is yellow. This is a faint, dusty yellow. But it is not from the flower itself. It is from the hands of the sender. Whoever sent this was handling fresh foxglove, or had been in a field where it was growing, just before sealing the envelope."

"Shropshire," I said. "It is June. Foxglove is in bloom."

"Precisely," Holmes said. "And the route is complete. Cornwall, Devon, Sussex, Kent, Shropshire. The tour is over. But the question remains: who is the final collector? And what is the prize?"

Mrs. Vane looked at the map, her eyes tracing the red and blue pins. "The Shropshire pin is near a small village called Harrowby," she said. "Arthur’s brother lives there. And Arthur died in the cottage there. In the garden, in fact. He fell while collecting."

Holmes nodded slowly. "Then we have our destination. But we must be careful. If this is a trap, it is a well-laid one. The sender is intelligent, methodical, and obsessed. They have been watching, waiting, and recording. And now that the route is complete, they may move to the next phase."

"What is the next phase?" Mrs. Vane asked, her voice barely a whisper.

Holmes looked at the six envelopes, then at the seventh, the one that had just arrived. "The collection is finished. The specimens are secured. Now, the collector must possess the archive. The logbook. The true value of Mr. Vane’s work lies not in the flowers, but in the data. The measurements, the locations, the soil conditions. It is all in the logbook."

"Where is the logbook?" I asked.

"It was with Arthur when he died," Mrs. Vane said. "I kept it. In my safe. But I have not opened it in months. I have been too... afraid."

Holmes smiled, a cold, thin expression. "Then we had best see if it is still safe. Mrs. Vane, will you show me? And Watson, I think we had better have our pistols ready. It seems we are no longer dealing with a mere prankster. We are dealing with a man who has spent the last six months walking in the footsteps of a dead man, and who now intends to take what is left."

As we prepared to leave, the rain continued to lash against the glass, blurring the lights of London into a smear of gold and grey. I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the weather. The flowers on the table lay still, their colors muted, their life long since drained, yet they seemed to watch us with a silent, predatory patience. The game was afoot, and the stakes were higher than any we had yet faced.
