The world, for Miss Evelyn St. Clair, had always been a mirror, polished and held at a flattering angle. At Blackwood Manor, where the oaks were as ancient as her lineage and the silver service was a continent of its own, she was the sun around which all things orbited. Suitors arrived with the regularity of the seasons, their compliments as predictable as the blooming of the dahlias in the west garden. She had accepted their flattery as her due, a natural consequence of her fortune, her name, and the pale, elegant face that looked back at her from the drawing-room mirrors. Mr. Davies, with his earnest eyes and his substantial shipping concerns, had been a particular favourite. He spoke of a future as grand and solid as the manor itself.

Then came the letter. It arrived on a Tuesday, a morning of thin, watery sunlight. The paper was cheap, the ink a stark, business-like black. The Imperial Bank of England, a name that had once been a byword for security, had collapsed. It was a name whispered in clubs and shouted on street corners, a synonym for ruin. The St. Clair fortune, built on generations of careful investment and shrewd land management, had been entrusted to its vaults. It was gone. All of it. The letter was a clinical post-mortem of their financial life, ending with a line of such brutal simplicity it felt like a physical blow: *The estate is hereby seized in settlement of outstanding debts.*

The mirrors in Blackwood Manor seemed to crack and splinter overnight. Her reflection became a stranger, thin and hollow-eyed. The first to fall away was Mr. Davies. His letter was short, regretful, and utterly without substance. A man of business, he wrote, could not afford such… complications. Soon, the other suitors followed, their excuses becoming more flimsy, their visits ceasing altogether. The invitations to picnics and balls dried up. The world, which had once bowed to her, now looked through her as if she were made of glass. She was no longer Miss Evelyn St. Clair, the heiress; she was merely a problem, a piece of social wreckage to be politely ignored.

Her parents, whose spirits seemed to have aged a decade in a week, spoke of distant cousins in the Americas, of cheap lodgings in a provincial town. But for Evelyn, there was only one option that did not involve complete surrender. She, who had never lifted a finger to dress herself, would seek employment. The notion was so monstrous it was almost amusing. She imagined herself as a governess, a creature she had only ever seen as a piece of the furniture, quiet and functional. It was a role for women of good birth but no means, a purgatory of stained pinafores and childish lispings. Pride, a cold and heavy stone in her chest, told her it was unthinkable. Necessity, a sharper, more insistent voice, told her it was the only thing left.

The advertisement was found for her by a former housekeeper, a kind woman who still felt some loyalty. *A gentleman of substance in London requires a governess for two young ladies of quality. A lady of impeccable breeding and education is essential. Discretion is paramount.* The address was in Belgravia, a place Evelyn had only visited for shopping and afternoon tea. Now, she would go there to be judged, to be weighed and measured not for her fortune, but for the paltry remnants of her accomplishments.

The house on Eaton Square was a monument to cold, new money. It lacked the faded grace of Blackwood Manor, its marble hall echoing with a hollow grandeur. Mr. Alistair Croft, the gentleman in question, was a man who seemed to be carved from the same granite as his mantelpiece. His eyes were the colour of a winter sky, and he assessed her with a chillingly direct gaze. He did not offer tea. He did not ask after her family’s misfortune. He spoke of Latin, of deportment, of the precise schedule his daughters, Amelia and Clara, were to follow.

“You have no experience,” he stated, his voice flat. It was not a question.

“No, sir,” Evelyn replied, her own voice a stranger to her, thin and reedy. She felt the heat of shame prickle her neck. “But I have been educated in all the necessary subjects. I was raised to… to be a lady.”

A faint, humourless smile touched his lips. “That is precisely what I am purchasing, Miss St. Clair. The *illusion* of a lady, for my daughters. They are to be presented in society in five years. They will not have the advantage of a title, but they will not be found wanting in manners or intellect. You will ensure that.”

The word ‘purchasing’ struck her like a slap. She was not a governess; she was an acquisition. An ornament. The final humiliation was the salary he named. It was a pittance, enough for a shabby room and little else, but it was more than she had. He laid the contract on the desk between them. The ink was black, the paper stark. It felt like the bank letter all over again, another document sealing her fate.

For a long moment, Evelyn looked at the paper. She thought of the endless, empty days stretching before her in a cheap boarding house. She thought of the pitying glances of her former friends. Then, she thought of the two small girls upstairs, who were to be polished and perfected like jewels in a case. She was to be the instrument of their transformation. The pride that had been the architecture of her entire life screamed in protest. It was a humiliation too vast to contemplate. But beneath it, the cold, hard ground of necessity remained. A lady did not beg. A lady did not starve in a gutter. A lady, when faced with the abyss, found the only bridge available, no matter how rickety.

Her hand, which had once signed notes for a new carriage with a careless flourish, trembled slightly as she picked up the pen. The nib scratched against the paper, a small, ugly sound in the quiet room. She wrote her name: *Evelyn St. Clair*. The letters were neat, elegant, and utterly devoid of the person she had once been. Mr. Croft took the document, his expression unchanged. He rang a small, silver bell.

A maid appeared. “Show Miss St. Clair to the schoolroom,” he commanded, not looking at Evelyn again. “And tell Cook to send up bread and milk for the young ladies. Their new governess is here.”

As Evelyn followed the maid out of the grand, cold room, she caught her reflection in a dark, polished wall. The woman looking back was a stranger in a travelling dress, her face pale but resolute. The mirrors of Blackwood Manor were gone. In their place was only this, a hard and unforgiving truth. She was no longer the sun. She was the moon now, borrowing what little light she could, and she would have to learn to govern the small, dark world that was now hers.
