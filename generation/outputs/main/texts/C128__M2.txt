The heat in the village of Trewyn hung heavy and stagnant, a thick blanket of humidity that turned the air into something solid and suffocating. It was the kind of summer that slowed time, stretching the hours until noon felt like dusk and dusk stretched into an endless, starless night. Elara sat at the small, scarred desk in the corner of her bedroom, the window thrown wide to admit the breeze that did little more than fan the dust motes dancing in the shafts of sunlight. In her hand, she held a pen, its nib poised over a sheet of cream-colored paper. The ink was still wet from the previous line, a dark, deliberate stroke in the corner.

*My dearest Thomas,* she wrote, the letters forming with a care that bordered on reverence. *The roses in the garden are late this year. I suspect the frost in March has stilled them, just as it seemed to still the world for you.*

Thomas had been dead for four months. He had died of the consumption, the wasting sickness that had taken him slowly, leaving his body as a hollow shell and his mind as a sharp, bright flame until the very end. They had been inseparable, two children of the same village, bound by a silence that was comfortable and deep. Now, Elara wrote to him as if he were merely away on a long journey to the cities, or perhaps to the colonies. She did not write of death. She wrote of the weather, of the price of bread at the market, of the new song the vicar’s wife had learned. She kept his spirit tethered to the living world, refusing to let the grave have the final word.

Her mother, Mrs. Ashworth, moved through the house with the quiet, anxious steps of a woman watching for a storm. She had noticed the change in Elara since the burial. The girl had not wept at the funeral; she had stood rigid, her face a mask of calm that terrified her mother more than any sobbing could have. Since then, Elara had retreated into her room for hours at a time, emerging only for meals, where she ate mechanically and spoke in short, polite sentences.

Mrs. Ashworth found the envelope on a Tuesday afternoon. It had slipped from the stack of papers on the desk, the seal broken, the address written in Elara’s neat, slanted hand: *Mr. Thomas Halloway, The Rectory, Trewyn.* But Thomas did not live at the rectory; he had lived in a cottage on the edge of the moor, which was now empty and rented to a farmer’s son.

The mother stood in the doorway, her heart hammering against her ribs. She knew the address was wrong, but the name was right. She stepped inside, the floorboard creaking under her weight, and picked up the letter. Her hands trembled as she unfolded it.

*My dearest Thomas,*

*The heat is unbearable today. I spent the morning by the river, watching the water rush over the stones. I thought of your laughter, how it sounded like that same rushing water. Do you think the water remembers us? I poured a cup of tea for you, just in case you were to walk in, though the door is locked. I left the window open. I hope the breeze found you.*

Mrs. Ashworth felt a chill rise up her spine, despite the oppressive heat. This was not the writing of a grieving daughter; it was the writing of a mind unraveling. To speak to the dead as if they were present, to leave tea for ghosts, was a symptom she had read about in the penny dreadfuls her husband used to buy, stories of madness and despair. She looked at her daughter, who was now writing again, her back turned, her shoulders hunched in concentration.

"Elara," Mrs. Ashworth said, her voice softer than she intended.

Elara did not turn. "Yes, Mother?"

"I saw the letter," her mother said, the words tasting like ash in her mouth. "The one to Thomas."

Elara’s hand paused. The nib scraped against the paper, leaving a jagged tear in the fiber. Slowly, she turned in her chair. Her eyes were dry, clear, and utterly sane. There was no feverish glaze, no wildness in her gaze. She looked at her mother with a confusion that was almost painful.

"I know you saw it," Elara said. "I was not hiding it. I wanted you to read them."

"Elara, Thomas is gone," Mrs. Ashworth said, her voice cracking. "He is in the ground. You cannot write to him. You are losing your grip."

Elara stood up. She was slight, pale from the lack of sun, but she moved with a steady grace. She crossed the room and placed a hand on her mother’s arm. Her touch was cool, grounding.

"I am not losing my grip, Mother. I am holding on," Elara said. "If I stop writing to him, I stop remembering him. And if I stop remembering him, then he truly dies. I cannot let him die twice."

Mrs. Ashworth looked into her daughter’s eyes and searched for the lie, for the delusion. She found none. She saw only a profound, aching love, a refusal to accept the cruelty of the world. The consumption had taken Thomas’s body, but it had not taken his spirit, not in Elara’s heart. To force Elara to stop would be to murder the last living piece of him.

But the fear remained. The world was not safe for those who spoke to ghosts. People would whisper. The vicar would frown. The neighbors would cross the street to avoid them. Elara was becoming a figure of pity, or worse, suspicion. Mrs. Ashworth thought of the isolation that would follow, the slow erosion of her daughter’s sanity under the weight of a secret grief that had no outlet.

"Who are you sending them to?" Mrs. Ashworth asked, the question slipping out before she could stop it.

Elara smiled, a sad, thin expression. "No one. I burn them. I have been burning them for months. I write them, I read them, and then I watch them turn to ash. It is my way of speaking to him. It is my way of letting him go, a little bit at a time, so that I do not have to let him go all at once."

Mrs. Ashworth felt her knees weaken. She sank into the chair opposite the desk. The room seemed to grow smaller, the shadows in the corners lengthening. She had feared madness, but she had not feared this quiet, methodical ritual. It was more terrifying because it was so rational, so deliberate. It was a dam holding back a flood.

"Is it working?" Mrs. Ashworth whispered. "Is it letting him go?"

Elara looked at the pile of ashes in the small brass tray on the desk. The grey dust was thin, barely visible.

"Not yet," Elara said. "But I will keep writing. Until the words feel heavy enough to drop. Until the silence is not so loud."

Mrs. Ashworth reached out and took her daughter’s hand. She held it tightly, grounding herself in the physical reality of the flesh and blood before her. Outside, the wind picked up, rattling the windowpane. The heat began to break, a distant thunder rolling across the moor. The storm was coming, and with it, perhaps, a change. But for now, in the quiet of the room, they sat together, the mother and the daughter, bound by a secret grief that neither could speak aloud, waiting for the rain to wash the dust away.
