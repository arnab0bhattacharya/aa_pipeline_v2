It was a fact known to every inhabitant of Brackenford—from the prosperous shopkeepers in the Market Place to the poorest framework-knitters in the alleys behind the mill—that the two great families of the town, the Harlowes and the Chetwoods, had stood in a relation of polite and unremitting hostility for the better part of three generations. The origin of the quarrel was buried in a dispute over a right of way, or a disputed boundary, or perhaps only a fancied slight at a subscription ball; for in provincial towns, where the currents of feeling run deep and narrow, a cause of enmity is often as difficult to trace as the source of a brook that has worn a channel through the meadows for a hundred years. What mattered was not the cause but the fact: the Harlowes drove their carriages past the Chetwood warehouses with an air of conscious rectitude, and the Chetwoods, on the rare occasions when they attended the same assembly, conversed with a pointed and laborious indifference which was more eloquent than any reproach.

It was into this delicate balance of forces that the project of the new chapel was introduced—a project which, like many another scheme of pious improvement, owed its existence less to a pure zeal for religion than to the desire of certain persons to assert their consequence in a manner that should be at once public and unimpeachable. Mr. Ebenezer Chetwood, a man of large and somewhat aggressive benevolence, had subscribed the sum of eight hundred pounds, and had caused it to be understood, in a manner that left nothing to the imagination while committing him to nothing in writing, that the foundation stone of the new Bethel ought properly to be laid by himself, as the largest contributor and the most conspicuous pillar of Dissenting respectability in the town. But Mr. Josiah Harlowe, whose wealth was of older date and whose family had worshipped in the old meeting-house when the Chetwoods were still, as he phrased it, “in the shop,” had also signified his intention of contributing a like sum, and had added, with a suavity that was more cutting than any direct assertion, that he should be happy to perform any office which the committee might think proper to assign to him.

The minister of the new congregation—for the chapel was not yet built, and the worshippers assembled in a large upper room over a corn-chandler’s store—was a young man named Theophilus Merriman, whose lot it was to be placed in that most uncomfortable of positions, a dependence upon two patrons who were at once his supporters and his rivals. He owed his call to the ministry to the influence of Mr. Chetwood, who had heard him preach at a neighbouring town and had been struck by the vigour of his exposition and the neatness of his black coat; but he owed his conscience, or so he believed, to a higher authority, and it was this conscience which now began to make itself heard in a manner that disturbed his peace. For Mr. Merriman was not one of those happy natures to whom a decision between two courses is a simple matter of interest. He had a mind that delighted in distinctions, and a heart that was apt to be moved by considerations which the world calls romantic; and the more he reflected upon the question of the foundation stone, the more he was convinced that the laying of it ought to be attended by some spiritual fitness in the person who performed the act, and not merely by the weight of his subscription.

It was in this state of mind that he betook himself, one November afternoon, to the house of Mrs. Chetwood, who had requested his presence on a matter of parish business. The house stood in a pleasant situation at the end of the town, with a lawn before it and a shrubbery that was the admiration of all who had not the misfortune to be on terms of rivalry with its owners. Mrs. Chetwood received him in a parlour that was furnished with a solid comfort which spoke of the prosperity of the firm, and after some preliminary remarks upon the weather and the health of the congregation, she came to the point with a directness that was characteristic of her.

“I am told, Mr. Merriman,” she said, “that Mr. Harlowe has been making himself very busy about the chapel. I hear he has spoken to the builder, and has even gone so far as to suggest a design for the front elevation. It is not for me to say that a man may not take an interest in a good work, but I think you will agree with me that there is a time for everything, and that the foundation stone is a matter which concerns those who have been foremost in the cause from the beginning.”

Mr. Merriman replied, with a cautiousness that he felt to be a little cowardly, that he was sure no one could be more sensible than himself of the liberality of Mr. Chetwood, and that he hoped the matter might be settled in a manner agreeable to all parties. But Mrs. Chetwood was not to be put off with generalities. She fixed her eyes upon him with an expression that was at once kind and penetrating, and said—

“I hope, Mr. Merriman, that you will not allow yourself to be guided by those who have only lately come among us. My husband has been your friend from the first. It was he who brought you here; and though I say it that should not, you have no cause to complain of the treatment you have received at our hands. I am not a woman to boast of what we have done, but I think you will acknowledge that we have not been backward in the good work.”

There was that in her tone which conveyed a whole history of benefits conferred and of a claim upon his gratitude which it would be at once ungracious and impolitic to dispute. Mr. Merriman bowed, and murmured something about his sense of obligation; but as he walked homeward through the grey November twilight, he was conscious of a certain sinking at the heart. The image of Mr. Harlowe rose before him—a tall, spare man with a cold and courteous manner, who had never once alluded to his subscription in the minister’s hearing, but who had once, in the vestry, after a prayer-meeting, said quietly that he hoped the new chapel would be built to the glory of God and not to the gratification of any man’s vanity. The words had been spoken without apparent reference to any particular person, but they had lodged in Mr. Merriman’s mind, and they returned to him now with a new force.

The next day brought a visit from Mr. Harlowe himself. He came on foot, and was shown into the little study where the minister sat surrounded by his books. He declined refreshment with a courteous gesture, and took the chair that was offered him.

“I will not detain you long, Mr. Merriman,” he said. “I am aware that there are those who think I have been forward in this matter of the chapel, and I wish to set your mind at rest. I have no desire to lay the foundation stone. I have no desire to be conspicuous in any way. I have given my money because I believe the cause to be a good one, and because I hope that the new chapel may be a means of grace to many who are now living without the knowledge of the truth. If the committee think proper to ask Mr. Chetwood to perform the ceremony, I shall be the last to object. I only trust that the service may be conducted in a spirit of humility and not of ostentation.”

He rose as he spoke, and took his hat. At the door he paused, and said, with a slight smile that had in it something of sadness—

“I have lived in this town a great many years, Mr. Merriman, and I have learned that a man may be very zealous for the ark of God and yet have a great deal of the Philistine about him. I do not say it of any one in particular. I only mention it as a general observation.”

When he was gone, Mr. Merriman sat for a long time in deep thought. The words of Mrs. Chetwood and the words of Mr. Harlowe lay side by side in his mind, and he weighed them as a man weighs the evidence of two witnesses who contradict each other. He was not a man of vigorous decision, and the more he considered the matter, the more he was persuaded that no course was open to him which should be at once prudent and honest. If he gave the stone to Mr. Chetwood, he would be accused of ingratitude by the Harlowes and of servility by his own conscience; if he gave it to Mr. Harlowe, he would be accused of treachery by the Chetwoods and of ingratitude by all the world. There was, indeed, a third course—to lay the stone himself, as the minister of the congregation—but this he knew would be resented by both families as an assumption of dignity to which his position did not entitle him.

It was while he was in this state of perplexity that there came to him, in the stillness of the night, a thought which he afterwards regarded as a special providence. He would invite both gentlemen to be present at the ceremony, and he would request them to stand one on his right hand and the other on his left, while he himself, as the servant of the congregation, performed the act which neither could perform without giving offence to the other. He would speak of the chapel as the house of God, not the monument of any man’s liberality; and he would trust that the good sense of both parties would prevent any open rupture. It was a compromise, and Mr. Merriman was aware that compromises are apt to be regarded by the zealous as a species of cowardice; but he was a man who had learned to distrust the heroics of party feeling, and he was content to be thought a little weak if he might thereby preserve the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace.

The day of the ceremony arrived, and the weather was propitious. A great crowd assembled on the waste ground where the foundations were to be dug; the workmen stood in their aprons, the school-children were marshalled in a long line, and the principal inhabitants of the town were gathered in a semicircle about the spot where the stone was to be laid. Mr. Chetwood and Mr. Harlowe stood on either side of the minister, each with a countenance composed to an expression of benevolent neutrality; and when Mr. Merriman, after a short address in which he dwelt upon the vanity of human distinctions and the importance of building upon the true foundation, took the trowel in his hand and spread the mortar, there was a silence that was almost solemn. The stone was lowered into its place, the minister struck it thrice with the mallet, and pronounced the words which declared it well and truly laid. Then, turning to the two gentlemen who stood beside him, he said, with a voice that trembled a little—

“I trust that this day’s work may be a pledge of unity among us, and that the house which is here begun may be a house of prayer for all people.”

Mr. Chetwood bowed, and said it was a very proper sentiment. Mr. Harlowe bowed, and said he heartily concurred in it. The crowd dispersed, and the two families went home to their respective dinners, not greatly loving one another, but with a sense that a crisis had been passed without a breach of the peace.
