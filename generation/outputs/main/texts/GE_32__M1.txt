It was an inevitable consequence of the catastrophe at Redmayne House that the social atmosphere should undergo a change more rapid and more perceptible than the mere alteration of bank balances would seem to justify; for the circulation of credit is, in many respects, like the circulation of the blood, depending less upon the absolute quantity of the fluid than upon the heart’s power to propel it, and Miss Adeline Vane found, to her bitter instruction, that the heart of the county was easily chilled. That the firm of Huxtable and Son should suspend payment was a matter of arithmetic and bad management; but that Adeline’s fortune—which had seemed as fixed and inevitable as the geological strata beneath the park—should vanish into the thin air of insolvency, was a fact that required a new category of interpretation. She had been taught, by the silent but potent logic of her upbringing, that her value in the marriage market was a composite of her features, her accomplishments, and the round sum of sixty thousand pounds; and when the latter element was subtracted, she discovered that the remainder was not, as she had fondly believed, a sufficient weight to tip the scales of worldly esteem.

It is a common error of the young and wealthy to attribute the deference paid to them to the intrinsic quality of their own minds, whereas the wise observer knows that society, like water, seeks its own level and respects the dam more than the stream. Adeline, having been the dam, now found herself the stream, and the suitors who had circled the Redmayne estate with the persistence of hounds scenting a fox now dispersed with a rapidity that suggested they had been hunting not the lady, but the acres. There was Mr. Penrose, the barrister, whose conversation had formerly sparkled with wit and whose proposals had been of the most flattering tenor; he was now occupied with a sudden and urgent brief in the North, and his letters, when they came at all, possessed that chilly brevity which denotes the withdrawal of the affections in proportion to the withdrawal of the funds.

In the solitude of the shuttered drawing-room at Redmayne, Adeline sat with the dawning, painful consciousness that her life had been a fraud perpetrated upon herself. The mirrors still reflected a handsome woman of eight-and-twenty, with eyes that held a proud light and a carriage that spoke of command; but the mirrors were deceitful, for they did not show the invisible subtraction that had rendered her, in the eyes of the world, a pauper. She was forced to confront the humiliating truth that she had no trade, no profession, and no kin near enough to offer more than pity, which is a commodity as cheap as it is unsatisfying. The only offer that came to her—arriving not from a lover but from a distant connection, Lady Milbanke, who had a brood of unruly children and a dislike of paying full price for instruction—was the post of governess.

To accept it seemed a descent into a lower circle of Dante’s hell, a voluntary submission to a servitude that her blood revolted against. To refuse it was to face the workhouse or a dependence on the grudging charity of cousins. The pride of the Vane family, which had been a stiffening starch in their character for generations, now stood in her way like a suit of armor that had rusted shut, imprisoning the wearer. She weighed the necessity against the dignity, a scale that tipped heavily and painfully. She knew, with the analytic precision of a mind forced to grow up suddenly, that pride cannot feed the body, but she also knew that to surrender it was to lose the last remnant of the self she had known.

It was Mrs. Prickett, the housekeeper, who brought the reality of the situation into the room with the tea tray. Mrs. Prickett was a woman of the earth, stout and unpolished, whose philosophy was comprised in the maxim that "a hungry belly has no ears." She had served the Vanes for thirty years and felt a proprietary right to their misfortunes as well as their triumphs.

"Well, miss," Mrs. Prickett said, setting down the cup with a rattle that bespoke a lack of ceremony born of the times, "it's no use cryin' over spilt milk, nor yet over spilt money. Them bankers in Lunnon has had their fingers in the pie, and we're left with the dish to wash. But wash it we must."

Adeline looked up, her face pale. "You think I should accept Lady Milbanke’s terms, Mrs. Prickett? To go into a stranger’s house and teach the alphabet to infants?"

Mrs. Prickett snorted, a sound of robust dismissal. "And what else, miss? You can't eat your pride, though it's fat enough to fry. I've seen gentlefolks go down in the world before, and the ones that holds their heads too high when there's nothin' underneath 'em usually comes a cropper. It's better to be a governess with a full belly than a lady of quality in a garret. You've got your wits about you, miss, and that's more than some has. Use 'em."

"But the humiliation," Adeline whispered, the word tasting of ash.

"Humiliation is only what's in your own head, miss," Mrs. Prickett retorted, wiping her hands on her apron. "The world don't think half as much about you as you do. They'll forget you was an heiress in a twelvemonth, and they'll see you as a governess. And if you do the work well, they'll respect you for it—after a fashion. It ain't the respect of the ballroom, I grant ye, but it's bread and butter."

The old woman’s dialect was rough, a burr of the local soil that grated against Adeline’s refined ear, yet the wisdom of it cut through the fog of her despair. She saw, with a sudden clarity, that the "humiliation" was a phantom created by her own vanity. The necessity was real; the pride was an illusion. To cling to the past was to perish; to embrace the future, however grim, was to survive. She thought of the governesses she had known in her childhood—shadowy women who sat in corners and were treated as superior servants—and she shuddered. But she also thought of the empty larder and the unpaid bills.

The decision, when it came, was not a sudden inspiration but a slow, grinding capitulation of the spirit to the facts. She would write to Lady Milbanke. She would accept the position. She would submit to the loss of her fortune and the loss of her place in the sun. It was a bitter medicine, but she swallowed it.

As she took up the pen, she felt a strange shift in her own nature. The heiress, who had expected the world to revolve around her, was dying; and in her place, a new creature was being born—a woman who knew that life was not a matter of entitlement, but of endurance. She wrote the letter with a steady hand, the ink flowing black and final upon the page. It was a document of surrender, but it was also, she realized with a flicker of surprise, a document of liberation. She was no longer a prize to be won, but a worker to be hired. And in that humble identity, there was, perhaps, a strange and unexpected dignity that her sixty thousand pounds had never been able to buy.
