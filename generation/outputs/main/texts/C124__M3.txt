The cottage at Nettlecombe sat in a hollow of the Dorset hills, its walls so thick with ivy that from a distance it appeared to be sinking gently back into the earth. Inside, the air smelled of dried lavender and the particular mustiness of papers long undisturbed. Eleanor Tresham stood at the parlour window, watching her sister cross the garden with a basket of cuttings, and tried to reconcile herself to the fact that the house was now theirs. Theirs, and not Henry's. She turned the words over in her mind the way she turned the iron key in the lock each evening, with a kind of careful disbelief.

Godmother Prue had been dead a fortnight. The will had been read in this very room, with its faded green draperies and the portrait of a man no one could identify above the mantelpiece. Prue had left the cottage to Eleanor and Charlotte jointly, and with it an annuity of three hundred pounds, to be paid to them both for the duration of their natural lives. To Henry, her godson, the man who had driven down from London every spring to carry her baskets and listen to her stories, she had left her writing desk and a set of silver candlesticks. Henry had received this news with a smile that did not reach his eyes, and had said something about Prue's well-known eccentricity, and had then gone quiet in a way that made Eleanor uneasy.

Charlotte came in through the kitchen door, her cheeks flushed, her bonnet dangling from one hand. She was twenty-two, four years younger than Eleanor, and possessed of a restlessness that the cottage could not contain. She set the basket on the table and began to sort the cuttings with an energy that seemed almost aggressive.

'We should write to Mrs Aldercraft,' she said, without preamble. 'She wrote to me in February, you remember, before Prue was taken ill. She said the house in Cavendish Square had room for another young woman this season. She said the invitations were already beginning to come in.'

Eleanor sat down in the chair nearest the fire, which was not lit, and folded her hands in her lap. 'Charlotte.'

' Three hundred pounds a year, Eleanor. Do you know what that means? It means we need not scrape and count. It means I need not sit here watching the ivy grow over the windows while the whole world passes me by.'

'The whole world does not pass through London.'

'A great deal of it does. And what passes through here? The butcher's boy on Tuesdays. Mrs Fossett with her complaints about the drain. I am twenty-two. In three years I shall be twenty-five, and then I shall be—'

'You shall be what?'

Charlotte stopped sorting the cuttings. A sprig of rosemary trembled between her fingers. 'Unmarriageable. At least, that is what Mrs Aldercraft will tell everyone, and she ought to know, having married off four nieces. I do not want to be a niece who was not married off. I do not want to be a woman who stayed in Dorset and grew into her aunt.'

Eleanor understood the force of this. She understood it the way she understood the pull of the tide, something vast and impersonal that would carry her sister away whether she consented or not. But she had her own reasons for wanting to stay, and they were not inconsiderable. She had spent the last six years attending Godmother Prue, not out of duty alone but out of a genuine preference for the old woman's company, for the quiet rhythms of the cottage, for the garden and the hills beyond it. She had imagined, perhaps foolishly, that this life would continue. That she would keep the house as it was, tend the herbs, read by the fire, and be troubled by nothing larger than the question of whether the roof needed re-thatching.

'I do not wish to go to London,' Eleanor said.

'I am not asking you to.'

'But you cannot go alone.'

'Why not? Mrs Aldercraft would chaperone me. She has done it before. And the annuity is mine as much as yours. Half of it would be a hundred and fifty pounds. That is enough for one season, if I am careful.'

Eleanor looked at her sister standing there with rosemary in her hand, and felt the familiar weight of being the elder, the steady one, the one who had never wanted anything she could not have, because she had never wanted very much. It was a quality that others called patience and that she sometimes suspected was merely a form of cowardice.

Before she could answer, there was the sound of hooves on the lane, and then a knock at the kitchen door. It was Henry. He had come down from London, he said, to see how they were settling in, and to offer his help in sorting Prue's affairs. He stood in the kitchen with his hat in his hands and his coat dusty from the road, and he looked at the two of them with an expression of such open concern that Eleanor felt a pang of guilt for having doubted him.

They sat in the parlour and took tea. Henry spoke first about practical matters: the transfer of the annuity, the small repairs the cottage needed, the question of whether Prue's servant, Mrs Huck, should be kept on. He was reasonable and thorough, and Eleanor found herself agreeing with everything he said. Charlotte said very little, which was unusual for her.

Then Henry leaned forward and said, 'I have been thinking about the annuity. It seems to me that the arrangement, as Prue left it, is bound to cause difficulty. You cannot both live here and both draw on the same funds without some disagreement arising. I wonder whether it might not be simpler if I were to administer the annuity on your behalf. I could allocate each of you a quarterly sum, and manage the remainder—'

'No,' Charlotte said.

Henry blinked. 'I only mean to help.'

'You mean to control it. If you administer the annuity, you decide how much we receive and when. You decide whether I may go to London. You decide whether Eleanor may stay here. And in the meantime, the money sits in your hands.'

'That is not what I intended.'

'What did you intend?'

There was a silence. Henry looked at Eleanor, plainly expecting her to intervene. Eleanor looked at the tablecloth, which was old and stained with tea, and thought about how Godmother Prue had never allowed anyone to speak for her, not even when she was ill and could barely sit up in bed. Prue had left the annuity to the two of them, and not to Henry, and Prue had been a woman who did nothing without purpose.

'I think,' Eleanor said slowly, 'that we must divide the annuity. Not in your hands, Henry. At the bank in Dorchester. Charlotte is right that her share is hers to spend as she wishes. And my share is mine.'

Charlotte stared at her. Henry opened his mouth and then closed it again.

'You will let me go?' Charlotte said.

'I cannot stop you. And I do not think I should try.'

'And you will stay here alone?'

'I will have Mrs Huck. And the garden. And I daresay you will write to me from London and tell me all about it, and I shall read your letters by the fire and be glad that I am not there.'

Charlotte crossed the room and put her arms around her sister. She smelled of rosemary and the road dust that had blown in from the garden. Henry watched them with an expression that Eleanor could not quite read, something between resignation and a kind of hurt pride, and she thought that she would have to manage him carefully in the coming months, because he was her brother and she loved him, and because he had expected something that he had not received, and that expectation would not simply dissolve because the will was settled.

When Henry left, he kissed them both and said he would write to the bank in Dorchester on the morrow. Charlotte walked him to his horse and Eleanor heard them talking in low voices on the lane, but she did not strain to hear what they said. She went to the window instead and looked out at the hills, which were turning the colour of brass in the late light, and she thought that the cottage would be quieter without Charlotte, and that quiet was what she had wanted, and that she would have it now, for better or worse, and that it was strange how a thing one desired could feel, at the moment of its attainment, so much like a loss.
