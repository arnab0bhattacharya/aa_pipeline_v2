The machine sat in the corner of Arthur Pym’s front garden like a squat, brass-topped beetle, humming with a low, persistent vibration that made the catshark eyes of the passers-by twitch. It was not, in the strictest sense, a machine for translating animals, for that would imply a facility with languages that no dog, however intelligent, possesses. It was, as Pym explained to anyone who would listen—and he found many, provided they were far enough away to not smell the ozone and burnt copper—a resonance amplifier. It caught the sub-vocalizations, the ultrasonic clicks, and the emotional resonances of the canine mind and converted them into a rough, monotonous English, delivered through a diaphragm that sounded distinctly like a wet trombone.

Pym was a man of considerable intellect and negligible social grace. He had spent the previous decade in a damp cellar in Whitechapel, perfecting the device, and had only recently moved to the respectable, semi-detached suburb of Highgate Hill to test it in a domestic environment. His neighbor, Mr. Josiah Thorne, was a solicitor of middling success and middling temperament, a man who prided himself on the orderliness of his garden and the obedience of his bulldog, Barnaby.

It was on a Tuesday, the air crisp with the first bite of autumn, that Pym decided to conduct his first public experiment. He had secured the cooperation of Mrs. Thorne, who was away in Bath with a friend, and the presence of Mr. Thorne, who had returned early from the club, expecting a quiet evening of sherry and silence.

"Now then," Pym said, adjusting a dial on the brass casing. "Barnaby, if you please. Approach the aperture."

Mr. Thorne stood by the garden gate, his face a mask of polite bewilderment. "Arthur, I must say, this is most irregular. What exactly are you doing with that contraption?"

"Listening, Mr. Thorne. That is all. The animal mind is a noisy place. I am merely quieting the noise so we can hear the signal."

Barnaby, a heavy, slothful beast, trotted over, his tail wagging with the dull enthusiasm of a creature who had just been offered a scrap of ham. He placed his broad, leathered head near the opening of the machine. The hum rose, a pitch above the mosquito, and the brass diaphragm began to flutter.

At first, there was only a static crackle, like a radio tuned between stations. Then, a voice emerged. It was not Pym’s voice, nor Thorne’s. It was a voice like gravel grinding in a cement mixer, devoid of inflection, yet terrifyingly articulate.

"SMELL," the voice droned. "FISH. OLD. STALE. MOUTH DRY. PAIN. HIPS. HURT."

Mr. Thorne’s hand, which had been reaching for his top hat, froze in mid-air. "What in the name of—"

"DO NOT MOVE," the voice continued, faster now. "OWNER. LIES. SAYS 'GOOD DOG.' HANDS. HARD. KICKS. WHEN HIDE BONES. UNDER SOFA. HIDE. SAFE. BUT PAIN. HIPS. HURT. FISH STALE. WATER BOWL. COLD. ALONE. LONG. HOURS. DARK. CAGE. COLD FLOOR. SLEEPLESS. NOISE. TRAFFIC. FEARS. NOISE. HURT."

Thorne’s face had gone the color of old parchment. He stepped back, his foot catching the edge of the lawn. "This is absurd. This is a fraud. Mr. Pym, I demand you stop this at once."

"Observe the correlation," Pym said, his eyes shining with the fervor of a man who has found the answer to a question he hadn't known he was asking. "The dog is speaking. Or, rather, the machine is interpreting the neural impulses. Look at his eyes, Thorne. The fear is genuine. The pain in the hips is genuine. You kick him, don't you, when he steals the bones?"

"I do not!" Thorne sputtered. "I am a gentleman. I treat my animals with..."

"WITH DISREGARD," the machine interrupted. "DISREGARD. COLD. HARD. KICKS. HIDE. HIDE. HIDE."

The bulldog, Barnaby, looked at his owner, then at the machine, and then back at his owner. He let out a low, whining sound, which the machine translated as: "GUILT. SORRY. BUT HUNGARY. PAIN. HIDE."

Thorne stared at the machine, then at Pym. "This is slander. This is a device of diabolical intent. You are twisting the mind of my property to defame my character. I shall have you arrested for malicious injury to my reputation."

Pym sighed, a long, weary exhalation. "You see, Mr. Thorne, this is the problem with the modern age. We pretend that the world is a place of reason and order. We build houses with straight lines and gardens with clipped hedges. We believe that because we do not hear the cry, the cry does not exist. But the cry is there. It is always there. The dog does not lie. The machine does not lie. Only you do. You lie to yourself. You lie to him. You kick him because you are tired, and you are afraid, and you are cruel, and you call it 'training.'"

Thorne’s face was now a shade of purple that suggested a severe headache or a stroke. "I say I shall have you arrested!" he shouted, his voice cracking. "And I shall sue you for every penny of my fortune! I shall have you locked up in Bedlam for this insanity! I shall—"

"SMELL," the machine droned, ignoring the outburst. "FEAR. OWNER. ANGRY. HURT. HIDE. HIDE. HIDE."

Barnaby turned and trotted back toward the house, his tail tucked between his legs, a posture of such profound, dignified sorrow that it seemed to puncture the air.

Thorne watched him go. He looked at Pym, who was already disconnecting the wires, his face calm, almost benevolent. "You have made your point, Mr. Pym," Thorne said, his voice suddenly very small. "You have made your point, and it is a very ugly one."

"Is it?" Pym asked. "Or is it simply true? I find truth to be the most economical form of explanation. You will sue, I expect. You will call me a madman. The newspapers will love it. 'The Dog Who Spoke,' they will call it. And you will stand in the court and deny everything, while the jury looks at you and wonders why your eyes are so red, and why you are holding your hat so tightly, as if it were the only thing keeping you from falling apart."

Thorne did not answer. He turned and walked up the path to his house. He did not go in. He stood by the door, staring at the dark window of the study where Barnaby was now hiding beneath the sofa.

Pym packed his tools into a leather case. He felt a strange, cold satisfaction, not of triumph, but of clarity. It was like the feeling of a surgeon who has finally found the tumor, knowing that the operation will be painful, but necessary. He walked to the gate, and as he stepped over the threshold into the street, he heard the machine humming one last time, a faint, distant sound.

"GOODNIGHT," it said. "COLD. HURT. SLEEP."

Pym paused. He looked back at the house. The lights were on in the kitchen, but not in the study. He smiled, a thin, ironic smile, and walked on into the London dusk, the city around him a vast, silent machine, full of voices that no one thought to listen for.
