If any honest man, conversant with the stony thoroughfares of Clerkenwell in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and thirty-something, had found himself standing upon the damp and slippery curbstone of Saffron Hill on a raw November morning, he might have observed a strange and melancholy procession making its irregular way towards the Police Office. The fog, which seemed to have given up all pretence of being a respectable country mist and had settled down into a coarse, sooty, snuffling sort of vapour, appeared to have a grudge against the gas-lamps, for it hugged them so closely that their light was reduced to a mere sickly yellow blush in the surrounding gloom. Through this unwholesome atmosphere, the constables went, bearing with them a boy. He was a wretched boy, to be sure, with a jacket that had manifestly been made for a smaller and shorter boy, and boots that had belonged, perhaps, to a man of mature years, being gaped and gouted at the toes, and slapping against the wet pavement with a sound like the flippers of a melancholy seal.

Mr. Justice Stelling was seated upon the magisterial bench, a gentleman of a most exact and methodical turn of mind, who had a profound respect for the law, and a corresponding dread of being made to look foolish by it. He was a man who carried his conscience in his waistcoat pocket, as it were, where he could always feel its weight; and if there was one thing he liked less than a disorderly prisoner, it was a disorderly prisoner who insisted upon being pitiful. He adjusted his spectacles, which had a habit of slipping down his judicial nose, and surveyed the court with the air of a man who feels that society is held together entirely by his own personal exertions.

The clerk read the charge. It was a charge of larceny, and a very simple larceny at that, being no more nor less than the felonious abstraction of one loaf of bread, of the value of fourpence, from the shop of a baker named Grumes. 

‘Bring the prisoner forward,’ said Mr. Stelling, in a voice that was meant to be terrible, but which was, in truth, only a little husky.

The boy was thrust forward into the dock, and there he stood, looking not so much defiant as deeply uncomfortable, shifting his weight from one vast boot to the other. He had a pale face, sharp and angular, like a piece of bread that had been cut with a very dull knife, and a pair of eyes that were far too large for it. His hair was of that singularly unhelpful colour which is neither brown nor yellow, but seems to have been designed by nature as a sort of apology for both.

Mr. Stelling looked at the boy. He looked at him with a sudden, sharp, and searching scrutiny, which was not his usual manner of looking at prisoners, for he generally looked at them as if they were unpleasant insects that had somehow found their way into his parlour. But there was something in the boy's face, a trick of the jaw, a peculiar droop of the right eyelid, which struck a chord in the magistrate's memory, and struck it with a force that made him start so violently in his chair that his spectacles fell off.

‘God bless my soul!’ said Mr. Stelling, under his breath, and not with any intention of bringing a theological observation into the court, but merely as a natural expression of human astonishment. 

He knew that face. He knew it as well as he knew the face of his own grandmother, which was saying a great deal, for he had been remarkably fond of his grandmother, and had a miniature of her in his dining-room. The boy was the son of his old coachman, Humphrey Todge.

Humphrey Todge! The very name brought back a rush of recollections. Todge had been a man of a most cheerful and rotund disposition, who had driven Mr. Stelling's gig for fifteen years without an accident, and who had been dismissed, with a small pension, when rheumatism had made it impossible for him to hold the reins. The magistrate had heard, vaguely, that Todge had died in a workhouse, and that his family had gone to the dogs, or to some equally undesirable destination. But he had not expected to see the family's misfortunes standing in his dock, in a pair of leaking boots, charged with the theft of a loaf of bread.

The constable, a stout man named Grimp, who had a voice like the grating of a rusty hinge, and a profound belief that every prisoner was guilty until they were proved to be more guilty, stepped forward to give his evidence.

‘If it please your Worship,’ said Grimp, ‘I apprehended the prisoner at the corner of Chick Lane. He was running, your Worship, as if the very Old Gentleman himself was behind him. I called upon him to stop, in the name of the law, and he did stop, your Worship, but only because he fell over a broken bottle. I found the loaf concealed about his person, your Worship, in a manner that was highly suspicious.’

‘Highly suspicious!’ repeated Mr. Stelling, mechanically. He was looking at the boy, who was looking at his boots, as if he hoped that they might open and swallow him up.

‘What have you to say for yourself, boy?’ asked the magistrate, in a voice which he endeavoured to make stern, but which was, in reality, trembling with a most unjudicial emotion. ‘Why did you steal the bread?’

The boy looked up, and his eyes, which were very bright, and very frightened, met the magistrate's.

‘I was hungry, sir,’ he said.

It was a simple answer, and an answer that had been given in that very court, by very many boys, on very many occasions. But Mr. Stelling, who had a heart somewhere under his judicial gown, though he generally kept it buttoned up very tightly, felt the answer go through him like a sword. He thought of old Humphrey Todge, sitting on the box of the gig, humming a tune, and cracking his whip; and he thought of old Humphrey Todge's son, standing in the dock, charged with a felony, for the want of a piece of bread.

The baker, a fat man, with a very red face, and a very clean apron, was called. He deposed that the loaf was his, that he had baked it himself, that it was a particularly fine loaf, and that he had missed it from his window. He had no wish, he said, to press the charge against a starving boy, but he had a business to attend to, and he could not have his bread stolen with impunity.

‘I am sure, your Worship,’ said the baker, ‘I have no wish to be hard upon the lad. But if I let one boy steal my bread, I shall have the whole parish stealing my bread, and then where shall I be?’

‘Where, indeed?’ murmured Mr. Stelling. He felt that he was in a very awkward position. If he sent the boy to prison, he would be disgracing the son of an old and faithful servant, and he would be sending a boy to a place where he would learn to be a worse boy than he was already. But if he let the boy go, he would be setting a bad example, and he would be violating the law, which he was sworn to uphold.

He sat for a few minutes, in a state of the most painful indecision. He looked at the boy, and he looked at the baker, and he looked at the constable, and he looked at the clerk, and he looked at the gas-lamp, which was flickering in a very uncertain manner. And then, suddenly, an idea struck him. It was not a very brilliant idea, but it was an idea.

‘Boy,’ said Mr. Stelling, ‘what is your name?’

‘Tom Todge, sir,’ said the boy.

‘Tom Todge,’ repeated the magistrate. ‘And your father was Humphrey Todge?’

‘Yes, sir,’ said Tom, with a look of surprise.

‘I remember your father,’ said Mr. Stelling. ‘He was a very honest man, and a very good coachman. And I am sorry to see his son in this position.’

The boy coloured up, and his eyes dropped again.

‘I am sure, your Worship,’ said the constable, Grimp, who was anxious to show that he knew his duty, ‘that the boy's father was a very respectable man, but the boy is a thief, and he ought to be punished.’

‘The boy is hungry,’ said Mr. Stelling, with a sudden energy that made the clerk jump. ‘And hunger, Grimp, is a very powerful motive. It is a motive which, I am sure, has driven many a better man than you or I to do things that they would not have done if they had been less hungry.’

The constable looked abashed, and the baker looked as if he were not quite sure whether he had been complimented or insulted.

‘But,’ continued the magistrate, ‘the law must take its course. The boy has stolen a loaf of bread, and he must be punished for it. But I am not going to send him to prison. I am going to fine him. I am going to fine him five shillings.’

The constable gasped, and the baker opened his mouth as if he were going to speak, but thought better of it.

‘And,’ added Mr. Stelling, ‘as the boy has no money, I shall pay the fine myself.’

He put his hand in his pocket, and produced five shillings, which he laid upon the table. 

‘And now,’ he said, turning to the boy, ‘I have one thing more to say to you. I knew your father, and I know that he was an honest man. I am going to give you a chance. I am going to take you out of this court, and I am going to find you a place where you will be fed, and clothed, and taught a trade. But if you ever come before me again, I shall not be so lenient. Do you understand me?’

‘Yes, sir,’ said Tom, who was crying now, and making no attempt to hide his tears.

‘Then go,’ said Mr. Stelling, ‘and see that you make the most of your chance.’

The boy went, and Mr. Stelling sat back in his chair, and wiped his spectacles, which were rather dim. He felt that he had not done a very judicial thing, but he felt, also, that he had done a very human one. And he was not sorry.
