The swing doors of the club had clapped to behind him, shutting off the fumes of toast and old leather, and now the air of Pall Mall, sharp with the first frost of November, bit at his cheeks and cleansed the smoky recesses of his brain. Arthur Pendle stepped out, buttoning the heavy grey overcoat across his chest, and the gravel of the London morning crunched beneath his boots—a sound that seemed, for a moment, the only sound in the world, until the clatter of a hansom on the far side of the road shook the frost from the bare branches of the plane trees and dissolved that solitary crunching into the general, indistinguishable roar of the city. He stood still. He had a speech in his breast pocket, folded into a tight, angular square, and he could feel its weight there, against his ribs, as if it were not paper at all but a stone, or a metal plate, something that had been riveted into his flesh while he slept and which he must now carry, cold and cumbersome, down Whitehall. The sky above the roofs was a pale, watered grey, streaked with the colour of bruised plums where the sun, hidden, strove to break through; and the houses opposite, with their stuccoed facades and white porticoes, looked infinitely respectable, infinitely remote, like old men in a club who have long since ceased to care whether the government falls or stands.

He crossed the road, stepping neatly between the wheel-tracks, and as he did so the face of his wife, Eleanor, rose before him with that startling immediacy which characterised all her appearances in his mind—not as she had been at breakfast that morning, when she had poured his coffee and spoken of the gardener and the boiler with a flat, weary competence, but as she had been twenty years ago, standing in the orchard at Beechcroft with apple blossom falling around her, her head thrown back, laughing at something he had said. He could not remember what he had said. It had been trivial, no doubt, some boyish joke about politics or the law, but she had laughed, and the blossom had fallen, and the air had been thick with a sweet, fermenting scent; and now, walking past the high railings of St. James's Park, he felt a sudden pang of something so like grief that he stopped, and stared at the bare trees, and the water, and the ducks moving on the water, as if he expected them to explain it to him. But the ducks only moved in their slow, circular patterns, and the water lay flat and grey, and the trees stood stripped and patient, offering nothing. He thought: *She is at home now, in the drawing-room, moving the chrysanthemums from one vase to another.* And the image was so sharp, so complete, that he saw her hands, her thin, veined hands, lifting the heavy bronze vase, and the water spilling on the polished table, and her face, bent and secret, as if she were engaged in some solemn, private ritual which had nothing to do with him and which he could never understand.

He walked on. The speech pressed against his ribs. In an hour he would be on his feet, in that long, green-lit chamber, with the sound of shuffling papers and muffled coughs all around him, and the words would come—would they come?—flowing out in that measured, pompous cadence he had cultivated over twenty years, the cadence of a man who has risen by caution and seniority and the careful avoidance of strong opinion. He would speak of the amendment, of the clause, of the financial provisions; he would gesture with his spectacles, as was his habit, and pause, and let the silence gather, and then drive home his point with a phrase he had prepared, a neat, epigrammatic phrase that would be quoted in the papers tomorrow. And yet, as he turned into Whitehall and saw the Cenotaph rising before him, that blank, white, unadorned shape against the grey sky, the phrase suddenly seemed to him the most ridiculous thing in the world—a scrap of rhetoric, a tissue of words, no more substantial than the mist that hung over the Horse Guards Parade, and as quickly dissolved. What did it matter? What did any of it matter—the clause, the amendment, the financial provisions—when men had died, were dying still, in muddy fields, and Eleanor was at home moving chrysanthemums from one vase to another, and the ducks moved in their slow circles on the grey water?

And then, quite without warning, another face came to him—not Eleanor's, not the face of any colleague or constituent, but the face of Clara Vere, whom he had loved when he was three-and-twenty and she was engaged to a man named Hargreaves, a man with a small estate in Hampshire and a large, confident, red face, who rode to hounds and spoke of nothing but horses. Clara had been dark, with a pale skin and a way of looking at one sideways, from under her lashes, that had made him feel, for the first and only time in his life, that the world was a place of wild and terrible possibility. They had met in a bookshop in Charing Cross Road—he had reached for a volume of Donne, she for a volume of Marvell, and their hands had touched—and now, walking past the Treasury with his overcoat buttoned to his chin and his speech in his breast pocket, he seemed to feel again the brush of her fingers, light and electric, and to smell the scent she wore, which was something dark and heavy, like gardenias left too long in a hot room. He had loved her for a summer. He had written her letters—long, passionate, ill-judged letters—which she had read, he suspected, with a mixture of pleasure and amusement, and which she had finally returned, tied with a blue ribbon, when she married Hargreaves in the autumn. And he had put them in a drawer, and gone on with his life, and married Eleanor, and risen, step by careful step, through the ranks of the party, until he had become what he was now: a man of substance, a man of influence, a man who carried a speech in his breast pocket as other men carried a pocket-watch, with the same regular, mechanical, utterly unreflecting certainty that it would tell the time.

But Clara's face lingered. It floated before him, pale and sidelong, as he passed the gates of Downing Street, and he thought: *She would be fifty now. Her hair would be grey. She would have grown stout, perhaps, or thin, or lined.* And the thought was unbearable, not because she had aged—for he had aged too, his own hair was grey, his own face was lined—but because the image of her, the perfect, untouchable image he had carried in his mind for a quarter of a century, was beginning to fray and dissolve, like a photograph left in the sun, and he could not hold it fast. He tried to recover it: the dark hair, the pale skin, the sideways glance, the scent of gardenias. But it was no use. The face blurred, shifted, became the face of Eleanor, pouring coffee, speaking of the gardener and the boiler; and then it was not a face at all, but only the blank, white shape of the Cenotaph, rising against the grey sky, saying nothing, meaning nothing, standing there in its terrible, unadorned simplicity as if to remind him that all faces dissolve, all voices fade, all speeches are forgotten, and there is nothing left at last but the stone, and the sky, and the slow, circular movement of the ducks on the grey water.

He had reached Parliament Square. The great tower rose above him, perforated with birdsong and the steady, mechanical pulse of the clock, and the building stretched out its long, grey flank, studded with windows that caught the thin November light and gave back nothing. He paused at the entrance, his hand on the cold iron of the railing. Inside, the lobby would be full of men—men in dark coats, men with papers, men with voices and opinions and the urgent, breathless certainty that this clause, this amendment, this financial provision, was of the utmost, the paramount, the earth-shattering importance. And he would go in, and take his seat, and rise to his feet, and speak. The words would come. They always came. They would flow out in that measured, pompous cadence, and the men would nod, and the papers would shuffle, and the coughing would subside, and the phrase—the neat, epigrammatic phrase—would hang in the air for a moment, like a bird, before it fell, forgotten, into the general, indistinguishable roar of the world.

He took the speech from his pocket and looked at it. The pages were curled and damp at the edges, and the ink had smudged where his thumb had pressed. He could not read his own handwriting. The words ran together, became illegible, became nothing—a scrawl, a scratch, a meaningless mark on paper. He folded the pages again, and put them back in his pocket, and went in.
