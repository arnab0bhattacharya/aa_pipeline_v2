In the city of Isola, where the towers were as slender as needles and the snow fell with the tedious persistence of a moralist, there lived a little match-seller whose name was Yseult. She was a child of extreme poverty, which is the only form of misery that society truly despises, for wealth is merely the ability to command the labour of others, and poverty is the inability to conceal one's failure to do so. Yseult had never owned anything that was beautiful. She had owned torn shawls that served only to remind her of the wind, and broken matches that served only to remind her of the dark. She knew the price of everything, for she was forced to sell it, but she knew the value of nothing, for she had never been permitted to keep it.

It was the eve of the Epiphany, and the snow lay in the streets like a covering of white ermine thrown over the city's filth. The palace of the Duke, a structure of frozen marble and gilded iron, threw out into the night the warm, saffron glow of a thousand tapers. Yseult stood beneath the great gates, her bare feet leaving dark, wet stains upon the pristine snow, and offered her matches to the lords and ladies as they passed in their sleighs. They did not buy, for to buy a match is a confession that one is cold, and in Isola, it was considered a breach of etiquette to admit that the climate was anything but invigorating.

As she stood there, shivering—a motion which, had she been a duchess, would have been called a thrilling sensibility—she saw something gleaming at her feet. It was half-buried in the snow, a thing of pale gold and moonstones, fashioned into the shape of a peacock with eyes of sapphire. It was a comb, of such exquisite craftsmanship that it seemed to have dropped not from a lady's hair, but from the loom of a god. Yseult picked it up. The cold metal burned her fingers, yet it was a burn that brought with it a strange delight. It was the first beautiful thing she had ever held.

Now, a match-seller is expected by the sentimental to be above the venality of the rich; but Yseult, though cold and hungry, was not without the instincts of an artist. She knew that to sell the comb would bring her bread for a month, but bread, however nourishing, is a terribly prosaic thing. It is the fuel of the merely existent, not the food of the living. To possess the comb was to possess a fragment of the ideal, a tangible proof that the world contained something other than frost and splintered wood. She thrust it into the bosom of her ragged dress, and felt, for the first time in her life, the sensation of wealth—the sensation of having something which one has no intention of losing.

The comb, however, was the property of the Princess Ottilia, the Duke's daughter, who was as beautiful as a winter dawn and as capricious as a spring torrent. She had worn the comb that evening in her hair, and had lost it as she leaned from the balcony to throw a rose to a young guardsman—a gesture which, in Isola, was considered the highest form of flirtation, as it combined the peril of the weather with the extravagance of the gesture. When she discovered her loss, Ottilia had wept, not from grief, but from the sheer inconvenience of being deprived of an ornament. She declared that the city should be searched, and the Duke, who doted upon his daughter to the point of moral blindness, ordered the guard to find the comb, offering a reward of a hundred gold pieces.

The news spread through the city like a stain through silk. The match-sellers, the chimney-sweeps, the beggars, and the thieves all turned out to scour the snow, for a hundred gold pieces is a sum that makes even the honest momentarily visionary. Yseult, standing in her accustomed place by the gate, heard the proclamation from the lips of a herald whose voice was as pompous as his fur-lined cloak. She pressed her hand to her breast, where the comb lay hidden, and felt the sharp teeth of the peacock against her skin.

She was confronted, at once, by the great problem of life: whether to be good or to be happy. For to return the comb would be virtuous, and virtue is its own reward, which is precisely why it is so terribly unpopular with those who have experienced it. To keep the comb would be wicked, and wickedness, as everyone knows, is the only thing that pays dividends. But Yseult, standing in the snow, did not think of virtue or of wickedness. She thought of the moonstones, and of the way they had gleamed in the pale light of the street lamps, and she knew that she could not part with them.

"They belong to the Princess," she said to herself, "but the Princess has many beautiful things, and I have none. It is merely a question of distribution. The rich are always telling us that we should be content with our lot; I am merely extending the same advice to them."

So she reasoned, and the reasoning was, of course, entirely specious, for it is the nature of reason to justify whatever the heart desires. But as she stood there, a sleigh drew up before the gate, and the Princess Ottilia herself alighted, wrapped in a cloak of white fox fur, with a circlet of diamonds upon her head. She was accompanied by her lady-in-waiting, the Countess Heloise, who had a face like a frost-bitten peach and a soul to match.

"The child may have seen it," said the Countess, glancing at Yseult with the casual disdain that the wealthy reserve for those who serve them. "These street Arabs pick up everything."

Ottilia looked at Yseult. She had never looked at a match-seller before, except to wave her away, and she was struck by the girl's face, which, beneath its mask of dirt and cold, had the pale, translucent beauty of a cameo. The Princess was not cruel; she was merely indifferent, which is the worst form of cruelty, as it admits of no remedy.

"Have you seen a comb, child?" she said. "A comb of gold, with a peacock of moonstones?"

Yseult looked up at the Princess, and at the diamonds that blazed upon her brow. She saw the ermine, the velvet, the sables, and the heavy, cloying scent of attar of roses that hung about the Princess like a visible aura. And she felt, suddenly, the weight of the comb against her breast, and she knew that she could not lie to this woman. Not because lying was wrong, but because the Princess, in her beauty and her wealth, was so utterly defenceless against the truth.

"I have it," said Yseult.

She drew it from her dress, and the moonstones flashed in the torchlight like captured stars. The Countess Heloise gave a little cry of avarice, and reached out her hand, but Ottilia stopped her with a gesture.

"Where did you find it?" said the Princess.

"In the snow, by the gate," said Yseult.

"And why did you not return it? There is a reward."

"I know," said Yseult. "But I had never owned anything beautiful."

There was a silence. The snow fell softly, and the torches hissed. The Countess Heloise looked as though she had been offered a half-eaten sandwich by a stranger, but Ottilia did not move. She stood quite still, and looked at the girl, and at the comb, and at the girl again.

"It is a terrible thing," said Ottilia at last, "to have never owned anything beautiful. I have owned so many beautiful things that I have forgotten what beauty is. It is like a language one speaks from childhood; one ceases to hear it."

She took the comb from Yseult's hand, and for a moment, their fingers touched—the warm, gloved fingers of the Princess and the frozen, bare fingers of the match-seller. Then Ottilia lifted the comb and looked at it, and then she looked at Yseult's hair, which was black and tangled, and fell about her shoulders like a shower of ink.

"Give me your other comb," said Ottilia.

"My other comb?" said Yseult, bewildered. "I have no other comb."

"Then I shall have to use my fingers," said the Princess.

She knelt down in the snow, in her gown of white satin, and she took Yseult's head in her hands, and she began, very gently, to untangle the girl's hair. The Countess Heloise was so shocked that she dropped her muff, and the guardsmen stared as though they had seen a saint walking on water. But Ottilia paid no heed. She combed the girl's hair with her fingers until it lay smooth and shining, and then she took the jewelled comb and placed it in Yseult's hair, above her ear, where the moonstones gleamed against the black strands like stars against a midnight sky.

"There," said Ottilia, rising. "Now you own something beautiful, and I own the memory of having given it. A gift is of no value unless it is given to someone who cannot return it. I have given it to you, and you cannot return it, for I have already given it away."

She turned and walked back to her sleigh, her white satin gown trailing in the snow, leaving a long, dark furrow behind her. The Countess Heloise followed, speechless with indignation, which is the natural condition of those who serve the great. The sleigh drove away, and Yseult was left alone in the snow, with the comb in her hair and the cold biting at her feet.

She stood there for a long time, and then she took the comb from her hair and looked at it. The moonstones gleamed softly, like the eyes of a cat in the dark. She put it back into her hair, and then she took it out again, and then she put it back. At last, she left it where it was, and went home through the silent streets, her bare feet leaving dark stains upon the snow.

She did not sell the comb. She did not eat. She sat in her garret, with the comb in her hair, and looked out at the towers of Isola, which were white against the black sky. She was cold, and she was hungry, but she was happy, for she had learned the secret of the rich, which is that one may possess beauty without possessing anything else, and that the possession of beauty is, in itself, a form of salvation.

She died, of course, before the spring. The cold was too much for her, and the hunger, and the garret. They found her in the morning, with the comb still in her hair, and the moonstones gleaming in the pale light. The Princess Ottilia, when she heard of it, wept for a quarter of an hour, and then ordered a new gown of black velvet, for mourning, she said, was the only art that the living had been permitted to borrow from the dead.
