The apparatus was, to begin with, a monstrously ugly thing. It occupied the better part of my laboratory in Cheyne Walk, and consisted principally of a pair of copper induction coils, each some four feet in diameter, mounted upon a heavy oaken frame in such a manner that they could be inclined at any angle to one another. Between them hung a curious arrangement of selenium cells and a small screen of my own devising, coated with a sulphide of zinc, which I had found to be peculiarly sensitive to electrical vibrations. The whole was connected by a labyrinth of gutta-percha covered wires to a battery of Leyden jars and a small dynamo, which I drove from the main shafting of the building. My neighbours in the adjacent studios had long since ceased to inquire what I was about, and were content to regard me as a harmless lunatic, an opinion which, I am bound to confess, my appearance and habits did little to discourage.

My object, stated plainly, was to see at a distance. I had reasoned—and I believe my reasoning was sound—that since light is but a form of electrical disturbance, and since electrical disturbances are propagated through the ether at a finite velocity, it should be possible, by a sufficiently delicate arrangement of receivers, to detect and render visible the electrical commotion set up by objects at a considerable remove from the observer. The notion was not entirely original; indeed, the whole tribe of telegraphists had been nibbling at the edges of it for years. But I had carried the idea further than any of them, and I had the satisfaction, on a certain evening in November, of seeing my screen flicker into life.

The first image was a blurred and wavering thing, a mere suggestion of form, like the shadow of a shadow. I adjusted the selenium cells, increased the potential, and presently there emerged upon the screen a view, astonishingly clear, of the interior of a room I had never seen before. It was a bedroom, plainly furnished, with a washstand and a chest of drawers and a small window looking out upon a street. A woman was moving about it, engaged in the business of dressing. I confess I turned away, somewhat shocked, and busied myself with the apparatus until the image had faded. I had no desire to play the Peeping Tom, and I resolved to direct my instrument upon the open street, where at least the spectacle would be public.

The next evening I trained the coils upon the King’s Road, and was rewarded with a view of that thoroughfare so distinct that I could read the name upon a grocer’s cart and count the buttons upon a policeman’s tunic. I saw a hansom cab go by, and a costermonger with his barrow, and a young couple walking arm in arm, and the whole business was so ordinary and so exactly what I might have seen with my own eyes had I stood at the window, that I was for a moment disappointed. I had expected something more—I hardly knew what—some revelation, some glimpse behind the veil.

Then I noticed something curious. The policeman had stopped and was looking at his watch. I knew that policeman; his name was Withers, and he was accustomed to pace that beat from eight in the evening until four in the morning. He had no business to be consulting his watch at half-past nine, and still less to be turning upon his heel and walking rapidly in the direction of the bridge. I watched him go, and then I saw the reason. A crowd was gathering at the corner of Oakley Street, and there was a sound of shouting and the blowing of whistles. I had been so intent upon the screen that I had not heard the actual disturbance, though it was taking place not two hundred yards from my door.

I turned to my window and looked out. The street was quiet. There was no crowd, no shouting, no policeman. I looked back at the screen. The crowd was still there, larger now, and I could see the helmets of two constables pushing through it. Then, quite suddenly, the whole scene vanished, and the screen went dark.

I was puzzled, but not greatly excited. I supposed that some irregularity in the current had caused the image to fail. I spent the next day in overhauling the apparatus, and by evening had it in better order than before. I directed it once more upon the King’s Road, and was gratified to see the familiar scene reappear. But now I noticed that the light was different. The evening was overcast, with a threat of rain, and yet upon the screen the sky was clear and the shadows were those of a bright afternoon. The costermonger was gone from his corner, and in his place stood a newspaper boy, bawling something which I could not hear. The policeman Withers was nowhere to be seen. Instead, there was a constable I did not recognise, and he was standing quite still, his arms hanging at his sides, staring at something beyond the range of my vision.

I shifted the coils slightly, and the view moved down the street. I saw a barricade of overturned carts and furniture, and behind it a group of men in rough clothes, some of them armed with rifles. I saw a column of smoke rising from the direction of the river, and I heard—or rather, I felt—a deep, sullen boom that seemed to shake the very walls of my laboratory. And then, quite distinctly, I saw a man in a grey uniform, with a rifle at his shoulder, step out from a doorway and fire point-blank at the constable, who fell without a sound.

I sprang to my feet with a cry, and knocked over a jar of sulphuric acid, which fortunately did no great damage. When I looked again at the screen, the scene was gone. The King’s Road was as it had been, quiet and orderly, with the costermonger at his corner and Withers pacing his beat.

I sat down and wiped my forehead. I am not, I hope, a superstitious man. I had set out to see at a distance, and I had succeeded. But what I had seen was not a distance in space, but a distance in time. I had been looking, not at the present, but at the future. How far into the future I could not tell, but the evidence of the newspaper boy and the barricade and the man in the grey uniform was not to be mistaken. Somewhere, not far ahead, there lay a war—a war that would come to the King’s Road, and to Chelsea, and to London.

I spent the next weeks in a fever of verification. I found that by a nice adjustment of the coils I could advance or retard the view, as it were, and I saw many things. I saw the Houses of Parliament in ruins, and the Thames choked with wreckage, and a great crowd of starving people fighting for bread outside a government depot. I saw Zeppelins in the sky over London, and the flash of guns in the streets, and men in grey uniforms marching past the Albert Hall. I saw all this, and I knew it for the truth, and I knew also that I could not prove it.

I went to the Royal Society, and was received with polite incredulity. I went to the War Office, and was shown out by a civil but firm young man who had evidently taken me for a lunatic. I wrote letters to *The Times*, which were not printed. I buttonholed my friends at the Savage Club, and saw their eyes glaze over as I spoke. “My dear fellow,” said one of them, a man of some scientific reputation, “you have been overworking. Take a holiday. Go to Margate.” And he patted me on the shoulder as though I were a child.

I am not a man of action. I am a scientist, and my business is with facts. But I have the facts, and they are of no use to me. I can see the future, but I cannot make anyone believe it. And so I sit here in my laboratory, night after night, watching the thing come nearer, and wondering whether, when the crash comes, there will be anyone left to remember that I told them so.
