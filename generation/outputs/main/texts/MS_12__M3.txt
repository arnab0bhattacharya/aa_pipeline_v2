It was in the autumn of the year 1794, when the mists hung heavy and desolate over the ancient spires of Wittenberg, that I, Ludwig Vohr, first yielded to the desperate promptings of my own heart, and resolved to put to the supreme test that singular tincture which my long vigils in the laboratory of chemistry had at length enabled me to compound. I had devoted the flower of my youth to the investigation of those subtle essences which sustain the failing powers of the human frame; and it was the bitterest anguish of my existence to behold the mind of my grandfather, the venerable Lord Aldric, sinking by slow degrees into the twilight of oblivion. He had forgotten the faces of his children, the very name of the town where his noble life had been spent, and, most mournful of all, he looked upon me, his sole companion and descendant, with the vacant stare of a stranger. The keenest sorrow, such as only those can know who have watched the gradual eclipse of a revered intellect, had driven me to the borders of madness; and in the wildness of my despair, I believed that I could call back the fleeting shadows from the past, and compel them to reanimate the ruined temple of his brain. The tincture, of a pale and luminous amber, distilled from herbs gathered beneath the winter moon and combined with minerals whose properties I had studied to the peril of my own reason, seemed to glow in the vial with a secret and vital flame. I gazed upon it, and my heart beat with a tumult of hope and terror; for I felt that I was about to tamper with the most awful decrees of Nature, to lift the veil which Time and decay had drawn over the sanctuary of memory.

The chamber where my grandfather sat was vast and dimly lighted, for he loved not the glare of day, and the heavy curtains of faded crimson were drawn close against the stormy sky. Without, the winds of October howled through the streets of the old Saxon town, shaking the casements with their mournful voices; and the rain beat upon the roof as if the spirits of the air were lamenting over some hidden crime. The old man sat in a great chair of carved oak, his hands folded upon his knee, his white hair falling in thin locks over a brow that once had been the seat of commanding thought. His eyes, once bright and imperious, now wandered restlessly over the tapestried walls, resting on vacancy. I approached him with a trembling step; I knelt beside his chair, and taking his passive hand, I poured into the slender crystal goblet a few drops of the amber tincture mingled with water. "Drink, my father," I murmured,—"drink, and remember." He raised the goblet to his lips with a mechanical obedience that rent my heart; he swallowed the draught; and then, for a space, the silence of the tomb fell upon the chamber, broken only by the shrieking of the blast and the heavy beating of my own heart.

I watched his countenance with an intensity that froze my very breath. The pale hue of his cheek gave place to a deep and burning flush; a tremor passed through his wasted frame; his hands clenched the arms of his chair, and his eyes, suddenly dilated, seemed to look into some awful distance. Then, with a cry that thrilled through every fibre of my being, he started up, and stood erect, a figure of awful majesty, his white hair streaming about his head, his whole aspect irradiated by the returning splendour of a mind restored. "Ludwig!" he exclaimed, in a voice that rang with the clear tones of a bell,—"Ludwig, my boy! I know you now; I know you all!" The tears gushed from my eyes; I threw myself at his feet; I sobbed aloud in the violence of my joy. The past had returned; the dead was alive again. But my transports were soon to be exchanged for emotions far other than those of joy.

His features, which had been lit up with a sudden tenderness, underwent a dreadful change; the light in his eyes became a lurid glare, and a shudder, as of one who beholds a spectre, convulsed his whole frame. He turned upon me a glance so wild and fearful that I shrank back appalled. "What have you done?" he cried, in a voice that seemed to issue from a tomb. "What have you done, rash boy? You have called me back to life, and I find hell!" He pressed his hands upon his brow, and the words poured from his lips in a torrent that I was powerless to stay. "Forty years," he continued, pacing the chamber with a swift and unsteady step, his voice rising and falling like the wail of the wind without,—"forty years I have been spared that memory; and now it rushes upon me with the force of a cataract, and I see it all—I see it as if it were but yesterday. The night of the great storm, when the Elbe overflowed its banks and the town was given up to ruin; the night when my brother Konrad came to me in his wrath, and demanded of me the account of those moneys which the trust of our dead father had committed to my hands. He stood where you stand now, Ludwig; his face was inflamed with anger, but I saw in it the righteousness of his cause. I had squandered the inheritance in riotous living, in the pursuit of pleasures that left me only ashes. I denied the deed; I loaded him with falsehoods; and when he turned to leave me, vowing he would expose me to the courts and the world, a frenzy seized me—a madness such as I had never known. I seized the heavy iron candlestick from the table; I struck him once, and he fell without a groan."

I listened, frozen into stone, while the old man, with broken gestures and eyes that glared as if they saw the bleeding form of his brother stretched before him, proceeded to tell how he had borne the body to the river's edge, and cast it into the swollen flood; how the townsfolk, finding Konrad missing, had concluded that he had perished in the waters; and how he himself had sat at the funeral feast, and shed tears of hypocritical sorrow, and from that hour had been haunted by a phantom which no penitence could lay. "And now you have restored me to myself!" he cried, with a terrible laugh. "You have given me back my memory, and with it the damnation of my soul! For forty years I have walked the earth a peaceful and honoured man, and the grave has kept my secret; but now the seal is broken, the dead is alive, and his blood cries from the ground against me!" He fell back into his chair, his face ashy pale, and a groan, such as I hope never again to hear, burst from his lips.

The storm had ceased; the clouds had parted, and the moon, cold and serene, looked in through the casement upon a scene of woe such as words are weak to paint. I stood motionless, my eyes fixed upon the ghastly face of my grandfather, and my soul was tossed by a tempest more dreadful than that which had raged without. I had sought to restore the past; I had succeeded, and the past had risen like an avenger. The tincture had done its work; the memory had returned; but with it had come a revelation that poisoned the very fountain of life. I had lifted the veil, and beheld only horror; I had called the dead to life, and the dead bore in his hands the dagger of destruction. I cast my eyes upward to the tranquil moon, and a cry of anguish burst from my lips; for I felt that I was alone, alone with a dreadful secret, and that the remainder of my days must be consumed in the endurance of a misery which my own rash hand had called into being.
