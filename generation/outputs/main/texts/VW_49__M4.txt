The house received her as it had always received her, with the smell of beeswax and the faint, sweetish must of old roses, and the sound—she had forgotten the sound—of the clock in the hall clearing its throat before it struck, as though it had something difficult to say. Margaret stood in the doorway with her bag still in her hand and let the hall arrange itself about her: the barometer, the brass umbrella-stand, the print of the bridge at Richmond, all of them exactly where they had been, as if the house had been holding its breath for three years and had now let it out, softly, without reproach. Nothing had changed. The light came through the fanlight over the door and lay upon the tiles in the same yellow parallelogram; it had lain there, she supposed, every afternoon at this hour, faithful, unremarked, while she had been reading in libraries and walking in other people’s gardens and learning, in ways she could not have explained to anyone in this house, that she was not the person who had gone away.

“There you are,” said her mother, coming out of the morning-room with her spectacles in her hand and her finger still marking the place in her book. “There you are, my dear. You look tired. You look—well, you look as though you had been thinking, which is worse than any fatigue. Come and have tea.”

Tea was laid in the morning-room, where it was always laid, on the round table with the pie-crust edge, and the silver teapot that had been Aunt Beatrice’s, and the three kinds of cake, of which no one ever ate more than one. Her father was in his chair by the window with the *Times* folded on his knee, and he looked up and said, “Ah, Margaret,” and returned to his paper, and then, after a moment, looked up again as though something had occurred to him. “The German business,” he said. “You will have been following it, at Cambridge. They say there will be war. I dare say they always say it.” And he folded the paper differently and became absorbed in it, and Margaret, who had followed the German business with an ardour that would have astonished him—had sat up in a cold room in Newnham with three other women and a map, had argued, had felt the world tilt and slide under her like a floor—said only, “Yes, Papa,” and took her tea.

It was the “Yes, Papa” that did it. She heard it come out of her mouth, small and smooth and perfectly rounded, like a pebble worn by years of water, and she thought: I have not said anything at all. I have made a sound, and the sound has been accepted, and the tea has been poured, and my mother is asking whether I shall want the blue room or the little room at the back, and I have said the blue room, and it is settled, and nothing has been said. The blue room had the view of the garden and the chestnut tree, and the chestnut tree would be in flower, and she would stand at the window in the early morning and watch the blossoms—candles, they were called, and they were like candles, upright and creamy and extravagant—and she would think of the life she had left, and the life she had come back to, and the space between them would widen until it was not a space but a country, with customs and a language of its own, and she would be a foreigner in her mother’s house.

“You will want to rest,” said her mother. “You will want to see people. The Wexfords have asked after you. And there is a subscription dance at the Assembly Rooms—I said you might care for it, though I don’t know. You will find it all very quiet after Cambridge, I am afraid.”

“I shall like the quiet,” said Margaret, and meant it, and knew, even as she meant it, that it was not true, or not true in the way her mother took it. She wanted quiet the way a field wants rain: to be filled, to be made to grow something. She wanted a room of her own and work in it, and the work would not be tea and calls and the Assembly Rooms. She had a degree—oh, they would not call it a degree, they would call it a certificate, a piece of paper that said she had passed examinations, as though she had passed them like a train through a station, without stopping; but she had the knowledge in her, and the knowledge was a kind of engine, and it would not idle forever in a Richmond drawing-room.

Her father turned a page. The clock in the hall struck the half-hour with its little preparatory cough. Her mother talked of the Wexfords, and of a cousin who had married a man in the Indian Civil Service, and of the difficulty of getting good servants, and Margaret watched her mother’s hands—the rings, the swollen knuckles, the way the left hand held the saucer while the right poured—and thought, with a tenderness that was almost anguish, how completely her mother inhabited this room, how the room was an extension of her, and how she, Margaret, sat in it as a visitor sits in a museum, admiring, and reading the labels, and knowing that she could not stay.

“I have been thinking,” she said. The words came out before she had arranged them, and the room seemed to draw itself together, her father’s paper lowering by an inch, her mother’s hand pausing over the sugar. “I have been thinking that I ought not to stay here very long. I ought to find something to do.”

“To do?” said her mother. “My dear, there is a great deal to do. There is the parish. There is the Girls’ Friendly Society. Mrs. Anstruther was saying only last week—”

“Work,” said Margaret. “I mean work. Teaching, perhaps. Or the Civil Service—they have examinations now, for women, in the Post Office and the Board of Trade. I could sit for them. I have looked into it. There is a woman at Newnham who went to the Board of Trade, and she says—”

“Margaret,” said her father. He had put down the paper entirely now, and he looked at her over his spectacles with an expression she remembered from childhood, a mild, puzzled, wounded expression, as though she had contradicted him at dinner before guests. “There is no need for you to go into an office. There is no need for you to earn your bread. You are not—forgive me—you are not one of those women.”

“Which women, Papa?”

“The ones who—well. The ones who have to. It is not a question of what you are capable of. It is a question of what is fitting. Your mother and I have given you an education, a very expensive education, and we are glad to have done it, and we had rather hoped—” He stopped, and took up his paper again, and said from behind it, “We had rather hoped you would be a comfort to us.”

A comfort. The word lay in the room like a stone in a stream, and the afternoon flowed round it, the tea and the cake and the sound of a cart going by in the road, and Margaret felt the whole weight of the house settle upon her, the beeswax and the old roses and the clock and the three kinds of cake, and it was not a weight she could argue with, because it was made of love, and love, she had discovered, was the most difficult thing in the world to refuse. She looked at her mother, who was looking at her with an anxiety that was almost anger, and at her father, who was pretending to read, and she thought: they have given me everything, and they do not know what they have given me, and if I stay I shall become a comfort, and I shall be comforted, and the engine will rust, and one day I shall be an old woman in a chair by this window, and I shall not remember that I ever wanted anything else.

“I know,” she said. “I know you hoped that. But I have been three years at Cambridge, and I have learned—I cannot explain it, Papa—I have learned that I am not the same person who went away. I should be a bad comfort to you if I stayed and was unhappy. And I should be unhappy. Not because of you. Because of me.”

Her mother set down the teapot. The lid rang faintly against the pot, and the sound was very clear in the quiet room. “And what,” she said, “are we to say to people?”

Margaret almost laughed. It was the question, the real question, the one that lay under all the others, and it was so small and so human and so entirely beside the point that she felt a great wave of love for her mother, and pity, and a kind of despair. “You may say,” she said, “that I have gone to London to work. People will say what they say. They always do.”

Her father folded his paper, deliberately, and laid it on the arm of the chair, and took off his spectacles, and polished them with his handkerchief, and put them on again, and looked at her. “And if we say no?” he said. “If we say that we would rather you stayed?”

The chestnut tree stood at the end of the garden, and the light was beginning to go, and the candles on the tree were turning from cream to a faint, dusty gold, and Margaret saw them through the window and thought of the early mornings, and the cold room at Newnham, and the map, and the world tilting. “Then I should stay,” she said. “And I should be here, and I should be your daughter, and I should be very grateful, and something in me would go out, quietly, like a candle, and you would not even notice it had gone.”

There was a long silence. The clock in the hall struck four, with its preparatory cough, and the sound went through the house and out into the road, and a dog barked somewhere, and a door closed, and the afternoon went on, indifferent, immense, carrying them all with it. Her mother’s hands were trembling on the saucer. Her father looked at her for a long time, and then he did a thing she had not expected: he nodded, slowly, once, as though he had heard something in her voice that he recognized, something that had been in his own voice once, a long time ago, before the paper and the chair and the three kinds of cake. “Well,” he said. “Well. We shall have to think about it.” And he took up his paper again, and the room relaxed, and her mother poured more tea, and the light went on fading, and Margaret sat with her cup in her hands and felt the engine turn over, once, deep in her chest, and catch.
