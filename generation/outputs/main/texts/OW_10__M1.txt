It was the hour when London, that great and terrible jewel, begins to glow with the false fires of its evening, and in his rooms in Cheyne Walk, Mr. Vivian Lyle had just declared a small, personal apocalypse. He stood before the fireplace, where a coal-fire cast a tremulous, theatrical light upon the silk of his dressing-gown, and addressed his publisher with the serene detachment of a man who has just committed a most elegant suicide.

‘My dear Mr. Penrose,’ he said, gesturing with a languid hand towards the piano, where a sheaf of manuscript lay like a fallen wing, ‘the day of the drawing-room ballad is over. I shall write no more of them.’

Mr. Penrose, a man whose soul was composed entirely of ledgers and royalty statements, let out a sound that was halfway between a gasp and a groan. He was a stout, florid gentleman who believed that art, in its highest form, was a well-constructed waltz that sold ten thousand copies before the season was out. ‘But Vivian!’ he spluttered, his face the colour of a winter sunset. ‘The *Sigh of the Rose*? *My Lady’s Locket*? They are the very breath of the fashionable world! They are played in every drawing-room from Mayfair to Manchester!’

‘Precisely,’ murmured Mr. Lyle, inspecting his fingernails with an air of profound interest. ‘They are the breath of the fashionable world, and like all such things, they are exhaled and forgotten. I have grown tired of composing pretty lies. I intend to tell the truth.’

‘The truth?’ cried Mr. Penrose, as if the word were a vulgarity. ‘What truth can there be in a symphony that no one can hum? You will ruin us! You will ruin yourself!’

‘One is only ever ruined by success, Mr. Penrose,’ Lyle replied with a sad, sweet smile. ‘Failure is merely a change of address.’

As if on cue, the door was opened by the manservant to announce Lady Annabel, Mr. Lyle’s most devoted patroness. She swept into the room in a rustle of sea-green satin and a cloud of violet perfume, her face alight with the rapture of a new conquest. ‘Vivian, my poet!’ she exclaimed, ignoring the distressed Mr. Penrose entirely. ‘I have just come from the Duchess’s, where they were playing your *Nocturne for a Fading Heart*. We were all in tears. Lady Carrington fainted quite beautifully onto a Louis XV sofa.’

‘I am desolate to have been the cause of such a costly inconvenience to the furniture,’ said Lyle, bowing.

‘Nonsense! It was divine. But what is this dreadful rumour I hear?’ she continued, her eyes narrowing. ‘That you are to write some dreadful, serious thing? A cantata, or some such nonsense?’

‘A symphony, Lady Annabel. In C minor.’

Her face fell, the light in her eyes extinguished as if by a sudden draught. ‘Oh, Vivian, you must not! Serious music is so… so *unpleasant*. It is full of discordant chords and has no tune one can possibly remember. It is like a conversation with a clever man who wishes only to prove his own intelligence. Your ballads, on the other hand, are like beautiful flowers. They do not ask to be understood, only to be admired.’

‘Ah, but a flower is a very brief and simple thing,’ Lyle countered gently. ‘I find I have a sudden longing to cultivate a forest.’

Lady Annabel looked at him with a mixture of pity and horror. ‘You will lose us all, Vivian. We do not wish to walk through a forest. We wish to be handed a single, perfect rose.’ And with a little shiver of disappointment, she turned and swept out, leaving behind her the fading scent of violets and the chilling prospect of a world without waltzes. Mr. Penrose, seizing his opportunity, wrung his hands one last time and scurried after her, a man fleeing a shipwreck.

Mr. Lyle was left alone in the quiet room. The fire had begun to settle into a deep, glowing heart of embers. He felt a curious peace, the kind one feels after a long and tedious illness has finally broken. He sat at the piano and placed his hands upon the keys, not to play, but to feel their cool, ivory silence. It was then that the servant re-entered, his face a mask of professional neutrality.

‘Mr. Alistair Finch.’

The name fell into the quiet room like a stone into a still pool. Mr. Finch was the most feared critic in London, a man whose pen was said to be dipped in ice-water and whose aesthetic judgments were as final and as cold as a death sentence. He had, for years, championed the cause of Wagner and Brahms, and had consistently dismissed Mr. Lyle’s compositions as ‘sentimental confections’, ‘pretty poison’, and ‘the aural equivalent of a cheap chocolate’.

Now, he stood in the doorway, a tall, gaunt figure in a severe black overcoat, his face pale and ascetic. He did not smile. ‘Mr. Lyle,’ he said, his voice as dry as autumn leaves.

‘Mr. Finch,’ Lyle replied, rising. ‘To what do I owe the honour? I had thought you only called upon composers whose work you intended to bury.’

‘On the contrary,’ said the critic, stepping into the room and letting his gaze sweep over the luxurious chaos of books and silks. ‘I am here to offer my congratulations. I have heard the rumour that you intend to abandon your… little gewgaws… and turn your hand to something of substance. It is the first sensible thing you have ever done.’

Lyle inclined his head, a faint, ironic smile playing on his lips. ‘I am flattered that my move towards respectability should meet with your approval, Mr. Finch. I was under the impression that my work gave you nothing but pain.’

‘It gave me nothing at all,’ Finch corrected him coolly. ‘Which is worse. Pain at least suggests a nerve has been touched. Your ballads were merely an insult to the silence they interrupted. They were pretty, which is the most insidious form of ugliness. But this… this is different. This is a renunciation.’

‘A renunciation of what, precisely?’

‘Of the tyranny of the obvious,’ Finch declared, his eyes burning with a cold fire. ‘Of the easy tear and the cheap thrill. You have been a purveyor of auditory sweets, and you have made the public into spoiled children. Now, you will give them something to chew on. You will give them something that is difficult, and perhaps even bitter, but that is, at its core, real. It is a far braver thing to write a symphony that is hissed at than a ballad that is encored.’

For a long moment, Vivian Lyle looked at his critic, the one man in London who had seen through the gilded cage of his talent. He saw the strange truth of the matter: the admirers who had flocked to him for his melodies now deserted him, while the man who had despised him was the only one to understand the nature of his flight. It was a paradox of the most exquisite kind.

‘You are the first to wish me well, Mr. Finch,’ Lyle said softly. ‘And you are the only one who has ever despised me.’

The critic gave a thin, skeletal smile. ‘My dear Mr. Lyle, it is only because I despised the man I thought you were that I can admire the artist you are determined to become. Admiration is the privilege of the enemy. Your friends are merely the audience.’

And with a curt nod, as if he had settled a point of critical theory, Mr. Alistair Finch turned and left the room as silently as he had entered. Vivian Lyle stood alone once more, the silence now deeper and more profound than before. He looked at the piano, at the blank manuscript paper waiting in the candlelight. He had lost his fortune, his fame, and his friends. He had been left with nothing but the truth. And he began to compose.
