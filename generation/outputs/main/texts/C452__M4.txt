The valley lay under a sky the colour of wet slate, and the river that had once turned the wheel of Hollowbeck Mill ran brown and swollen with the thaw. Ellen Marchbank stood on the mill’s stone threshold, her skirts heavy with the damp, and watched the last of the day drain out of the hills. She had bought the ruin in the spring with her own money—her father’s money, honestly come by from the wool trade in Halifax—and she had promised the parish of Stannergarth that by winter there would be work again. Now winter had come, and the work was there, but so was the doubt.

The mill had been dead for twenty years when she first saw it. Its roof sagged like an old horse’s back, and nettles grew between the flagstones of the yard. The gentry of the valley—the Heskeths of Low Fold, the Rev. Mr. Pemberton at the parsonage—had been kind enough to tell her exactly what they thought of the scheme. “A woman alone, with machinery,” Mrs. Hesketh had said, her voice sweet as arsenic. “And the poor will blame you when it fails, my dear. They always blame the one who tries.” Ellen had smiled and said she would risk it. She had not expected to risk quite so much.

The first months had gone well enough. She had hired a millwright from Bradford, a sober man named Caleb Rigg, who patched the wheel and coaxed the old carding engine into something like obedience. She had taken on nine hands—the Widow Tattersall’s two sons, the crippled girl from the almshouse who could sort wool with her one good hand, a half-dozen others whose names she had learned by heart. They had made blankets that first autumn, coarse but warm, and sold them at Halifax market for a fair price. Ellen had begun to believe the worst was behind her.

Then the frost came early, and the river dropped, and the wheel turned slower and slower until it stopped altogether. Rigg spent three days in the wheel-pit with a lantern, his breath clouding the air, and came up with a face like a funeral. “The shaft’s cracked, miss,” he said. “The iron’s rotten. It’ll want a new one, and that’ll want money, and the roads are that bad I can’t say when the carrier’ll get through.”

She had the money. Barely. But the money was also the wages, and the wages were also the coal, and the coal was also the bread. She stood in the counting-house—a room so small that her desk touched the wall on either side—and did sums until her eyes ached. Then she went out to the yard and told the hands the truth.

“The wheel will be mended,” she said. “It may take a fortnight. Until then there’s the hand-carding, and the sorting, and the mending of the blankets we’ve already made. I’ll pay you half-wages until the wheel turns again.”

They nodded. They were polite. But she saw the looks that passed between them—the Widow Tattersall’s eldest, Thomas, with his mouth set hard, and the girl with the one good hand staring at the ground. They had heard promises before. They had heard them from the Heskeths, who had closed the old mill and said it was for the best, and from the Pembertons, who had said God would provide, and God had provided a workhouse.

The fortnight became three weeks. The carrier’s cart lost a wheel in the snow at Luddenden Foot, and the new shaft sat in a barn for eight days while the roads froze and thawed and froze again. Ellen walked up to the Heskeths’ house on the second Sunday to ask if their man might fetch it in their cart, and Mrs. Hesketh received her in the drawing-room with a fire so large it seemed indecent.

“You see how it is, Miss Marchbank,” she said. “We did warn you. The poor are not grateful, and machinery is not a woman’s business. And now you have half the parish depending on you, and no wheel turning. It is very sad.”

“It is not sad,” Ellen said. “It is inconvenient. There is a difference.”

Mrs. Hesketh’s smile did not waver. “Mr. Hesketh says the mill will never pay. He says you will have spent your fortune in two years and be glad to sell the ruin to him for the stone.”

“Then Mr. Hesketh may keep his money,” Ellen said, “and I will keep my mill.”

She walked home in the dark, and the cold got into her bones. She had not told anyone—not even Rigg—that her suitor, a solicitor from Halifax named Henry Fawcett, had written to her the week before the frost. He had been very kind. He had said he admired her spirit. But he had also said that a wife with a mill and nine hands and a lawsuit with the Heskeths over water rights was not a wife he could in conscience ask his mother to receive. He had hoped she would see reason. He had signed himself, “Your obedient servant.”

She had burned the letter in the counting-house grate and watched the flame eat the word “obedient.”

The third week of the frost, Thomas Tattersall came to her at the mill door. He was a big man with a slow voice, and he had never been anything but civil. Now he stood with his cap in his hands and his eyes not quite meeting hers.

“Miss,” he said, “the men are asking. They say the wheel’s not mended, and the half-wages are not enough for the coal, and the bairns are cold. They say—” He stopped.

“They say what, Thomas?”

“They say you’ve spent the money on the mill and there’s none left for us. They say the Heskeths were right.”

Ellen felt the cold go through her like a blade. She looked past him at the yard, where the other hands had gathered—not a mob, just a knot of tired people in thin coats, waiting to see what she would say. The girl with the one good hand was there, and the Widow Tattersall, and the boy who had carried the new shaft’s bolts in his apron because they had no cart.

“Tell them to come into the mill,” she said. “All of them. Now.”

They came, shuffling, and stood among the idle machines. Ellen climbed onto the low platform by the carding engine so they could see her.

“You think I have spent your wages,” she said. “I have not. I have spent my own money, and I have kept back enough for the coal, and I will show you the ledger if you want to see it. The wheel is not mended because the roads are frozen, not because I have been idle. And I will tell you something else. The Heskeths want this mill to fail. They want you to go back to the workhouse and say that a woman cannot do what a man can. If you doubt me, you are doing their work for them.”

There was a silence. Then the girl with the one good hand said, “I don’t doubt you, miss.”

The Widow Tattersall said, “Nor me.”

Thomas looked at the ground, then at Ellen. “I’ll go up to Luddenden Foot myself,” he said. “I’ll fetch the shaft on my back if I have to.”

The wheel turned again on the second day of the new year. It turned slowly, and the blankets were coarse, and the money was nearly gone. But when Ellen stood in the yard that evening and heard the old machinery groan into life, she did not think of Henry Fawcett or Mrs. Hesketh or the cold that had settled in her bones. She thought of the girl with the one good hand, who had believed her when no one else would. The river ran brown and swollen under the darkening sky, and the wheel went round, and round, and round.
