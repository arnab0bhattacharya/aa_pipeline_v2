The scent of the workshop was the scent of Elias’s life: the sharp, sweet perfume of pine shavings, the mineral tang of varnish, and the heavy, funereal dust of seasoned oak. For six years, since he was a boy of twelve, he had known this air. He knew the grain of the wood as a scholar knew the pages of a book, could tell elm from ash by the feel of it under his plane. His hands, calloused and stained with glue and stain, were skilled at their trade. He could dovetail a joint so finely it seemed grown rather than made, could sand a lid to a silkiness that was almost a cruelty, given what it was meant to contain. But his hands, he felt, were meant for other gestures.

The longing was a constant, low hum beneath the rhythm of the mallet and the saw. It lived in the hollow of his chest, a secret theatre he performed in the silence of his mind. He would practice faces in the reflection of a half-varnished coffin lid—the king’s fury, the lover’s despair, the jester’s glee. He would rehearse speeches under his breath as he drove nails, his voice a low murmur against the percussive clang. The stage was a world away from Aylesford, a market town in Kent where the greatest drama was the weekly haggle over livestock prices. Yet for Elias, it was the only world that felt truly real.

The company arrived in a great, lumbering coach, its sides painted with faded, theatrical scenes of gods and heroes. They were ‘Mr. Abernathy’s Magnificent Theatrical Troupe,’ and their arrival was the most exciting thing to happen since the harvest festival. Elias saw them first from the workshop window, a splash of impossible colour against the town’s familiar browns and greys. His heart hammered against his ribs. He saw a woman in a feathered hat, a man with a magnificent moustache, and finally, the manager himself, Mr. Abernathy, a man whose worn velvet coat and air of weary grandeur seemed to have been imported from another age.

That evening, leaving the workshop, Elias found his courage. He approached the troupe’s lodgings at The King’s Head, his hands twisting the brim of his cap. He found Mr. Abernathy holding court in the taproom, regaling a small crowd with a story. Elias waited, his throat tight, until the man’s eyes fell upon him.

“Something for you, my boy? A coffin, perhaps? Hoping to be a long-term customer, are we?” The company laughed. Elias felt his face burn.

“No, sir. I… I wish to act.”

Abernathy’s shrewd, tired eyes swept over him, from his sawdust-flecked hair to his sturdy workman’s boots. “Do you, now? And what, pray tell, have you ever acted in?”

“In my head, sir,” Elias said, his voice barely a whisper. “I know all the great parts. I can recite Shakespeare.”

The manager raised a skeptical eyebrow. “Can you indeed? Give me a taste, then. A bit of Richard. The winter of our discontent.”

Elias closed his eyes. The noise of the taproom faded. He was no longer a coffin-maker’s apprentice in a Kentish inn. He was a crook-backed king, his soul twisted by ambition and envy. He spoke the lines, not with the booming voice he imagined a real actor would use, but with a quiet, simmering intensity that seemed to draw the very air from the room. When he finished, there was a moment of profound silence.

Abernathy was silent for a long time. He stroked his chin. “Well,” he said at last. “There’s a fire in there. A small one, mind you, but a fire nonetheless. We have a small part. A messenger. Three lines. You would be a fool to think it’s a start on the road to Drury Lane, but it’s a part.”

A joy so fierce it was painful bloomed in Elias’s chest. He could have wept. He could have danced. He stammered his thanks and practically ran home, the three lines already singing in his head.

The news was not met with joy. His master, a man named Silas Croft, was a creature of wood and silence. His face was a roadmap of fine lines, his hands gnarled like oak roots. He was finishing the lettering on a lid—*Beatrice Ann Halloway, aged 74*—when Elias blurted out the news.

Silas did not look up. The small, fine brush continued its steady path across the painted wood. “You are an apprentice, Elias,” he said, his voice as dry as dust. “You have a duty. A promise made to me and to your mother. This is not a game.”

“But it’s only for one night, Master Croft! It’s a chance I might never have again!”

“Chance?” Silas finally set down his brush and turned. His eyes were pale and unyielding. “The only chance you have is to learn a trade that will feed you and a family for the rest of your days. This… this is a fool’s errand. Men who build dreams on air end up with nothing. Now, get back to work. The sanding on that lid is not finished.”

His mother’s reaction was a deluge of tears. She sat at the small kitchen table, her apron twisted in her hands. “Oh, Elias, my boy,” she wept. “The stage? With those… those wandering people? What will the town say? They’ll laugh at us. They’ll say you’re flighty, unreliable. After all we’ve done for you, after all Master Croft has taught you.”

“Mother, please, it’s not a disgrace,” he pleaded, his heart sinking. “It’s an art.”

“It’s a disgrace to throw away a good, solid future for a night of prancing about in costume,” she sobbed into her apron. “You’ll bring shame on us.”

Defeated, he walked through the town as dusk began to settle, the last of his joy turning to ash in his mouth. He saw her then, standing outside her father’s bakery. Elspeth. Her hair, the colour of wheat in the sun, was tucked under a simple cap. She was the quiet poetry in his noisy, wooden world. He had admired her from afar for years, imagining conversations, shared glances, a future.

He approached her, a desperate hope flickering within him. Perhaps she would understand. Perhaps she would see the fire Mr. Abernathy had seen.

“Elspeth,” he began, his voice trembling slightly. “I have some news. The travelling players… they’ve given me a part. A small one.”

She looked at him, her blue eyes wide and serious. “A part? You mean, on the stage?”

“Yes! It’s only a messenger, but it’s a beginning.”

A small frown creased her brow. She looked him up and down, and for the first time, he felt her gaze was not one of gentle curiosity, but of appraisal. “But Elias,” she said, her voice soft but firm. “You are a coffin-maker. Or you will be. You have sawdust in your hair. What would you look like, up there with all those painted people, pretending to be someone else?”

The words struck him with the force of a physical blow. “It’s not pretending, not really. It’s…”

“It would be ridiculous,” she said, and the word was a small, sharp stone thrown into the quiet pool of his heart. She gave a small, pitying smile. “You would look ridiculous.”

She turned and went inside the bakery, the bell above the door giving a cheerful, final jingle. Elias stood frozen in the gathering dark. His master forbade it. His mother wept for it. The girl he admired declared him a fool. The world he knew, the world of solid things and clear duties, had closed its doors on him. He looked at his hands, at the fine calluses from the chisel and the plane. They were the hands of Elias, the coffin-maker. But tonight, for one night, he wanted them to be the hands of a king, a hero, a messenger. He walked home, his mind made up. The world could call him ridiculous. He would rather be a fool for a night than a ghost for a lifetime.
