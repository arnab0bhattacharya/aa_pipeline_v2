It is a curious thing how the most extraordinary revolutions in human affairs often hinge upon the most trivial and domestic objects. I had been engaged for the better part of two years in the investigation of gravitation as a polarised rather than a perfectly radiated force, a theory which my colleagues at the college in Stonebridge, Staffordshire, dismissed with that air of superior weariness peculiar to the English don. They desired only to read their lectures, collect their dividends, and enjoy their port. But I had found a substance—a dull grey powder, precipitated from an obscure salt of bismuth by means of a very high-tension electrical discharge—which, when interposed between any mass and the earth, appeared to neutralise the gravitational pull of the latter entirely. It was, in short, a screen against gravity. 

Upon the afternoon of the fourteenth of May, 1894, I determined to test the powder upon a larger scale than I had yet attempted. I had previously managed to levitate a china teacup and a heavy brass microscope, but the forces involved had been so minute as to leave my scientific mind dissatisfied. I wished to see whether a human being, provided with a sufficient area of the substance, might be raised into the air. 

To this end, I painted a thick layer of the substance, mixed with a common shellac varnish, upon the seat and back of a common garden chair. It was a wooden chair, of the sort one finds in any suburban greenhouse, and I had chosen it precisely because it was light and offered a considerable flat surface. I left it to dry in the back garden of my lodgings, while I retired to my study to fetch a measuring tape. 

My housekeeper, Mrs. Vass, was a woman of a singularly literal and unexcitable disposition. She had been with me for three years, and in that time I dare say she had seen enough of my chemical apparatus to be immunised against surprise. She was a large, comfortable woman, much addicted to the reading of penny weeklies, and she possessed a magnificent disregard for the dictates of theoretical physics. 

It appears that she had come out into the garden to air the rugs, and finding the garden chair conveniently placed in the sun, she sat down upon it. 

I was at my window, adjusting my spectacles, when I witnessed the thing that happened. The chair, bearing Mrs. Vass, rose. 

It did not rise with any violence or sudden jerk. It rose with a slow, steady, and altogether majestic acceleration, exactly as a soap bubble detaches itself from a pipe. It went up perhaps a foot, then two feet, clearing the top of the rose trellis. Mrs. Vass, I perceived, was sitting bolt upright, her hands gripping the arms of the chair, her face turned towards the earth with an expression of profound indignation rather than terror. She had, I imagine, supposed the thing to be some trick of her senses, or perhaps an earth tremor, for she did not immediately cry out. 

Then, as the chair cleared the ridge of the house and the full force of the upper air currents caught it, she gave a shout that was perfectly audible to me through the open window. "Professor!" she bellowed, in a voice of immense accusation. "Professor Bickford!"

I rushed downstairs and out into the garden, but by that time the chair was already thirty feet above the chimney pots, and ascending with a most disquieting rapidity. The powder, it seemed, was entirely effective. 

The town of Stonebridge, it must be understood, is a quiet place, devoted chiefly to the manufacture of boots and the education of the young. It is not accustomed to phenomena. The afternoon was a bright, still day, with a gentle breeze blowing from the south-west. It was this breeze that now took charge of Mrs. Vass. 

She drifted over the rooftops in a long, sweeping curve, passing over the High Street at a height of perhaps two hundred feet. I ran out into the lane, bareheaded, and watched her progress. She was sitting in the chair with the rigid composure of a woman in a railway carriage, her skirts tucked neatly about her ankles. She was, I noticed, still holding the rug she had been airing, and it streamed out behind her like a tattered banner. 

A chimney sweep, a man of considerable dirt and limited imagination, was coming out of the Greyhound Inn. He looked up, dropped his brushes, and remained staring with his mouth open. A baker's boy on a tricycle rode into a lamppost. Several ladies of my acquaintance, who were taking tea in the vicar's garden, were moved to a state of extreme hysteria. 

I should have felt some compunction at the peril of Mrs. Vass, but the scientific interest of the spectacle was so enormous that I am afraid it quite overrode my humanity. Here was a woman, an ordinary, unscientific woman, suspended in mid-air by means of a grey powder and a garden chair. It was a triumph. It was, however, a triumph that required immediate management.

I procured a hired carriage and drove at a gallop in the direction she was drifting. The wind was carrying her northward, towards the open country beyond the canal. From the top of the hill at the edge of the town, I had a clear view of her. She was now perhaps five hundred feet up, and her voice, which was a strong one, came down to me in intermittent gusts. She was not screaming; she was shouting directions. "I want to come down!" she roared, with an air of personal injury. "I am coming down, Professor! I will not be held responsible for the chair!"

The chair, however, showed no signs of descending. The powder, I reflected, would continue to neutralise gravity until the shellac dried completely, or until the substance was washed away by the moisture of the upper air. I had not calculated for this. I had not, in short, provided any means of steering or arresting the apparatus. 

She drifted over the canal, and over the first fields of the Stonebridge estate. A ploughman in a field below looked up, fell to his knees, and began to pray. A flock of rooks rose in a dense, clamouring cloud about her, and she beat them away with the rug. 

It was at this point that the scientific nature of the experiment gave way to a problem of a more practical kind. Mrs. Vass was, as I have said, a large woman. She was also a woman of decided views. She had no intention of continuing her aerial voyage indefinitely. 

"Take off your shawl!" I shouted through my hands, but the wind carried my voice away. 

She, however, had arrived at a solution of her own. I perceived her fumbling with the rug, and then, with a dexterity that surprised me, she managed to tear a strip from its edge. She leaned over the side of the chair and dropped the strip. It fluttered down, and I observed that it was weighted with a brass button from her boot. She was, it seemed, attempting to establish communication with the earth. 

Then she did a thing that was at once desperate and highly intelligent. She stood up in the chair. 

The chair, relieved of her weight, shot upward with a suddenness that was almost explosive. It vanished into the blue of the afternoon sky, a dark speck against the clouds, and I saw it no more. Mrs. Vass, on the other hand, was now in free fall. 

She fell, I should say, for about a hundred feet, in a posture of considerable rigidity, her skirts ballooning about her in a most indecent but impressive manner. Then, as she descended into the lower and denser air, her fall slowed. She was, I reflected, a woman of considerable mass, and the resistance of the air was not inconsiderable. 

She came down in a haystack. 

It was a large haystack, recently built, in the second field beyond the canal. She struck it with a dull, heavy thud, and sank into it up to her waist. The impact threw up a cloud of dust and straw. 

When I arrived, breathless and hatless, she was already extricating herself from the hay. She was dusty, dishevelled, and her hat was lost. But she was entirely uninjured. She looked at me with an expression of unutterable reproach. 

"Professor Bickford," she said, in a voice that trembled with passion, "if you wish to go up in a balloon, I wish you would do so yourself. I am not a bird, and I will not be made a public spectacle of." 

She refused to speak to me for the rest of the week. The chair, I may add, was never recovered. It was seen by a farmer in Derbyshire, some sixty miles to the north, descending into a field of turnips, but by the time he reached the spot it had vanished again. It is, I suppose, still drifting somewhere in the upper reaches of the atmosphere, a monument to the efficacy of my powder and the endurance of Mrs. Vass. 

I have since prepared a more manageable apparatus, and I have no doubt that the conquest of the air is now merely a matter of engineering. But I confess that the memory of Mrs. Vass, drifting over the rooftops of Stonebridge in a garden chair, remains the most vivid and instructive experiment in the whole history of gravitational science.
