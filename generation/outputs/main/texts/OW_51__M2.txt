The candle in the tower of Aldous Vane burned with a steadiness that was almost offensive in its clarity. It was a single tallow dip, unadorned, yet it cast a light so pure and unvarying that it seemed less a physical flame and more a moral certainty. Aldous, a man whose face had long since acquired the texture and hue of old parchment, sat before his hoard. He did not count the coins with the frantic, greedy haste of a common thief, but with the slow, deliberate precision of an apothecary weighing grains of poison. To him, gold was not merely currency; it was a form of eternal silence. It did not speak, it did not beg, it did not die. It was the only thing in the northern kingdom that remained exactly as it had been the moment it was struck, unaffected by the rot of seasons or the decay of virtue.

The wind outside howled against the stone, a sound like the wailing of a widow who had forgotten the name of her husband. Aldous ignored it. The wind was a debtor who had never paid his rent. He turned a coin over in his fingers, admiring the profile of the King, a face that was now just a stamp of authority, stripped of all humanity. "You are safe," he murmured to the metal, his voice dry as dust. "You are the only true things in this false world."

It was then, as the minute hand of the clock crept toward the hour of ten, that the knock came. It was not a polite tap, nor a desperate pounding, but a small, hesitant sound, like a mouse trying to open a door that was too heavy for its paws. Aldous did not move. He knew, with the certainty of a man who has traded his soul for security, that no one knocked on his door unless they wanted something. And what they wanted, he was too poor to give.

"Go away," he called out, his voice carrying the flat, metallic ring of the coins on the table. "I am dead to the world."

But the knock came again, softer this time, tinged with a kind of shy defiance. It was the knock of a child, he realized. Children were the only beings who persisted in the face of obvious refusal, believing, in their tragic ignorance, that the world was obligated to be kind.

Aldous rose, his joints cracking like dry twigs. He crossed the room, his footsteps silent on the thick rug, and opened the door. The cold air rushed in, carrying the scent of snow and the distant, bitter smell of the town’s chimneys. Standing in the shadow of the doorframe was a girl, no older than seven, her cheeks flushed a vivid crimson, her eyes large and dark as two holes in the earth. She wore a coat that had been mended so many times it looked like a patchwork quilt of rags, and she clutched a small, snuffed-out lamp in her hands.

"Please," she said, her voice trembling not with cold, but with the effort of maintaining dignity. "My mother is ill. The lamp has gone out, and we have no match. Will you give me a light?"

Aldous looked at her, and for a second, a strange vertigo seized him. He had expected a beggar, a scoundrel, a man with a knife hidden in his sleeve. He had not expected a child. Children were too messy, too prone to breaking, too easily corrupted by the world they entered. They were the great error of nature, the flaw in the design.

And yet, as he looked at the snuffed lamp in her hands, he felt a sudden, irrational irritation. It was not anger at the girl, but anger at the inefficiency of her situation. Why should a lamp go out? Why should a mother be ill? Why should a child be standing in the snow? It was all so terribly, unnecessarily tragic.

"I have no matches," Aldous said, his tone perfectly level. "And I do not keep a fire for the convenience of passersby."

"The candle," the girl said. She pointed to the light glowing in the room behind him. "I only need a light to light the wick. I will be gone in a moment."

Aldous stared at her. To give a light was to give a part of his night. It was to admit that his solitude was not absolute, that his tower was not a fortress but a house, and that in a house, there are guests, even unwanted ones. It was a concession to the world he had locked out.

"Wait," he said, and the word surprised him.

He went back to the table. He picked up the candle. It was a beautiful thing, smooth and white, like a piece of bone. He held it up to the single flame of his hearth, which he had kept burning for the gold, not for warmth. He lit the end of the candle he was holding. Then, with a movement that was almost surgical, he broke the candle in two. He handed the smaller piece to the girl.

"Here," he said. "Take this. And do not think that this is charity. It is merely a transaction. You have taken a piece of my time, and I am paying for the inconvenience of your presence."

The girl took the candle. She did not thank him. She simply looked at him, and in her eyes, he saw a reflection that was not his own. It was not a reflection of greed, or of coldness, or of power. It was a reflection of sorrow. A deep, quiet sorrow that seemed to have no end.

She turned and ran down the stone steps, the small flame in her hand bobbing like a star falling through the dark. Aldous watched her go. He watched the light diminish, shrink, and finally vanish into the gloom of the town.

He closed the door. He locked it. He bolted it. He went back to his table and sat down. He picked up the other half of the candle. It was still burning, but it seemed to him that it burned with a different quality now. It was no longer a light of clarity, but a light of loss.

He looked at the gold. The coins lay there, gleaming, perfect, immutable. But for the first time in his life, they seemed to him to be dead. They were beautiful, certainly, but they were the beauty of a marble statue, cold and lifeless. They did not burn. They did not give. They did not care.

Aldous Vane, the miser, the hermit, the man who had locked the world out of his tower, found that he could not sleep. He sat in the chair, watching the candle burn down, and he thought of the girl’s face. He thought of the redness of her cheeks, the darkness of her eyes, the way she had held the lamp so carefully, as if it were the most precious thing in the world.

He realized, with a shock that was almost physical, that he had been counting his wealth for thirty years, and in all that time, he had never once possessed anything that was truly valuable. He had the gold, but he had not the light. He had the silence, but he had not the voice. He had the tower, but he had not the warmth.

The candle sputtered and died. The room plunged into darkness. Aldous did not light another. He sat in the blackness, listening to the wind, and for the first time, he did not hate it. He heard it, and in its howling, he heard a kind of music, a wild, untamed song that belonged to no one and everyone. He felt a strange, terrifying freedom in the dark. He was no longer the master of his gold; he was a servant of the night, and the night was a generous, if capricious, mistress.

He waited for morning. He waited for the sun to rise, to see if the world was still there, if the girl was still there, if he was still himself. He knew, with a certainty that was deeper than fear, that he would go down into the town. He would not give them his gold. That was dead. He would give them something else, something he had never known he had. He would give them a light.

And as the hours passed, the darkness in the tower did not feel like a prison. It felt like a chrysalis.
