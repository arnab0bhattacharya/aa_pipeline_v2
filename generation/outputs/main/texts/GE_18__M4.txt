It was a truth universally acknowledged in the parish of Ashcombe St. Mary, though never perhaps distinctly formulated in the minds of its inhabitants, that a shepherd’s life is a long patience, a slow accumulation of small certainties against the large uncertainties of weather and market. For forty years, through frosts that cracked the very earth and summers that baked the downland to a pale ochre, Silas Marner—no, let us call him Silas Tregarthen, for the other name belongs to a different county and a different story—had tended the flocks of Marsh End Farm. He had known three generations of the Durbey family as masters, and had outlived two of them; he had seen the old stone barn rebuilt, the great elm at the gate struck by lightning, and the sheep-wash deepened by the stream that ran, in wet seasons, with a brown and hurrying water. His life was measured not in years but in lambings, in shearings, in the slow circuit of the stars above the fold. He was a man of few words and those few drawn from the old Wessex speech, so that his meaning, when he gave it, had the weight of something quarried rather than spoken.

It was in the Michaelmas of the year 1815 that the new tenant, a Mr. Percival Gannet, a man of improved methods and a brisk, decided manner, came to Marsh End. He had money, it was said, from the north, and a desire to see the farm “brought on.” For a while he said nothing to the shepherd, but walked the fields with a surveyor’s eye, and consulted a book of agricultural reports, and spoke of “economy of labour” in a way that made the older men exchange glances. Then one morning, as Silas was penning the ewes for the winter dipping, Mr. Gannet came to him with his hands behind his back and a certain air of having rehearsed a disagreeable duty.

“Tregarthen,” said he, “you have been a good servant to the farm, I don’t deny it. But you are getting on, you know. You are getting on. I have a nephew, a young fellow from Dorset, who wants a place, and he can do the work at half the wage. I shall want you to go at the Lady Day quarter.”

The shepherd stood with his crook in his hand, and the wind, which came fresh from the high down, lifted the grey hair at his temples. He did not speak for a moment. He looked at the sheep, at the sky, at the distant spire of the church where he had been christened, and where his wife lay buried. Then he said, in a voice that was not angry but strangely flat, as if the words had been pressed out of him:

“I have been here forty year, sir. I know every hoof on this down. I know the springs that dry in summer and the hollows that hold the snow. The sheep know me.”

“That may be,” said Mr. Gannet, with a slight impatience, “but the sheep will know your nephew too, in time. It is a matter of business, Tregarthen. I cannot afford sentiment.”

And he turned away, and the shepherd was left standing in the yard with the smell of the dip and the low bleating of the ewes about him. It is a curious fact in human nature that a great sorrow often finds no immediate expression, but sinks down into the mind and lies there, like a stone dropped into a deep well, sending up its circles long after the splash has been forgotten. Silas Tregarthen went home that night to his cottage at the end of the lane, and sat by the fire without speaking, and his daughter-in-law, a widow with three children, looked at him and said nothing, for she knew the look on his face. It was the look of a man who has been told that he is of no more use in the world.

But there was in that cottage a boy of twelve years, named Jem, the shepherd’s grandson, who was quick and observant and had been taught his letters by the parish clerk. Jem had seen his grandfather come in with that silent face, and had heard, through the thin partition, the words “Lady Day” and “half the wage,” and his young heart had swelled with a fierce and helpless indignation. He loved his grandfather with the whole force of his nature; he had followed him over the downs since he could walk, had learned the names of the stars from him, and the signs of the weather, and the ways of the ewes with their lambs. And now to hear that this old man, who was the wisest person he knew, was to be turned off like a worn-out horse—it was more than Jem could bear.

He lay awake that night thinking. He had heard the men in the village talk of the landlord, a certain Sir Aubrey Ffolliott, who lived in a great house in another county and had never been seen at Marsh End within the memory of the oldest inhabitant. The farm belonged to him, and Mr. Gannet only rented it. It was to Sir Aubrey, therefore, that the appeal must be made. And Jem, who had a bold spirit and a turn for composition, resolved that he would make it. He rose before daylight, and by the light of a tallow candle, with a pen that he had to mend himself, and a sheet of paper that he had hoarded for a month, he wrote the following letter—not without many pauses and much labour, for the art of writing was still new to him, and he was not certain of the spelling of “humbly” and “prayer”:

“Honoured Sir,—I am Jem Tregarthen, grandson of Silas Tregarthen, shepherd at Marsh End, which is your farm, though you have not seen it. My grandfather has been shepherd there forty years. He is not too old. He is strong yet, and he knows the sheep better than any man in Wiltshire. The new tenant, Mr. Gannet, says he must go at Lady Day, and he has a nephew who will do it for half the wage. But the nephew does not know the down. He does not know the springs or the hollows. If my grandfather goes, the sheep will suffer. I am learning to write, and I write this for him because he cannot write himself, but he does not know I am writing it. I am not asking for charity, only for justice. If you please, sir, will you not let him stay?—Your obedient servant, Jem Tregarthen.”

He read it over twice, and his heart beat hard. Then he sealed it with a wafer, and walked three miles to the post town to put it in the mail. It was a bold thing for a boy to do, and he told no one.

The letter reached Sir Aubrey Ffolliott at his house in Gloucestershire, and it happened—for such things do happen, and the wise narrator must not omit them because they seem like the contrivances of fiction—that Sir Aubrey was a man of a conscientious, if somewhat indolent, disposition, who had long been troubled by a vague sense that his estates were something more than a source of income. He read the boy’s letter twice, and the second time he smiled, and then he looked grave. “Forty years,” he said to himself. “And I have never seen the place.” He called for his steward, and asked certain questions about Marsh End, and the answers were not satisfactory. Mr. Gannet, it appeared, was a pushing man, but not beloved. And Sir Aubrey, who had a vein of sentiment in him that his business acquaintances would not have suspected, resolved to go and see for himself.

He came in the spring, when the downland was green with the young corn and the larks were singing. He came on a day when Silas Tregarthen was out on the hill with the ewes and the lambs, and Jem was with him, and the old man was pointing out a hawk that hovered in the distance, and telling the boy how to know the signs of a hard winter. Sir Aubrey came up the path on foot, a plain man in a plain coat, and stood watching them for a moment before he spoke. Then he said:

“Tregarthen, I have had a letter about you.”

The shepherd turned, and his face was troubled, for he thought of Jem’s secret, which he had discovered by finding the ink-bottle under the boy’s pillow.

“I hope, sir,” he said, “the boy meant no harm. He is young and hasty.”

“He meant no harm,” said Sir Aubrey. “He meant a great deal of good. He has done what I ought to have done myself long ago. I have come to see the farm, and to hear from your own lips how matters stand.”

And there, on the green slope of the down, with the sheep grazing about them and the lark singing overhead, the shepherd told his tale—not with any eloquence, but with the plain force of truth. He told of the forty years, of the lambings in the snow, of the ewes he had saved, of the winters he had weathered. And Sir Aubrey listened, and looked at the old man’s hands, which were brown and knotted with work, and at the boy’s eager face, and he made a resolution in his heart.

The end of it was that Silas Tregarthen did not go at Lady Day. Mr. Gannet received a letter from the landlord which caused him to speak of “interference” and “old-fashioned notions,” but he kept his nephew at home. And the shepherd remained at Marsh End, though he was not so strong as he had been, and Jem was formally apprenticed to him, and learned all he knew, and more, for he could read the books on sheep that his grandfather could not. And Sir Aubrey Ffolliott came once a year after that, and was known in the parish as a good landlord, though it was remarked that he always asked for the Tregarthens first of all. As for Jem, he grew up to be a man of some substance, and in after years, when he was old himself, he would tell the story of how he wrote a letter to a landlord who had never seen the farm, and how that letter saved his grandfather from the cold charity of the world. And he would add, with a smile, that it is a fine thing to learn to write, for the pen is a poor man’s advocate, and a boy’s heart is sometimes wiser than a man’s prudence.
