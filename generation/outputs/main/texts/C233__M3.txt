The assembly rooms at Weymouth glowed with such a profusion of wax candles that the light seemed to press against the windows, a beacon drawn out onto the dark September sea. Lucy Ashford stood near the French doors, the salt breeze lifting the curls at her neck, and counted the figures in the set. Her cousin Clara moved down the line with bright precision, her slippers barely touching the floor, her gloated hand placed just so in her partner's palm. Clara had not lacked for partners all evening. She had danced every dance, beginning with a lively major general and now, near midnight, a cotillion with a lieutenant whose regimentals caught the candlelight.

Lucy held her dance card in her hand. The small card hung from its ribbon, blank, the tiny pencil tied to the tassel never used.

She had refused the first offer — a stout man who smelled of port — and then there had been no second offer. The intervals between sets had lengthened into a kind of exile. She had moved from the refreshment room to the gallery and back again, nodding at acquaintances from the parish with a composure she did not feel, until the evening settled into its present shape: she would stand by the doors and watch the sea and wait for it to be over.

Clara had told her it would be different here. In the country, at the harvest suppers and the winter parties at the assembly room above the Greyhound, Lucy had always been the one who was asked. She had a way of listening that made men feel clever, and a laugh that carried, and she had thought these qualities would translate to the wider world. But the wider world had its own logic. Here, where no one knew her family or her father's living, she was simply a girl in a white dress standing near a wall, and the men who passed her did not pause.

She was watching Clara when the woman spoke to her.

"You are Miss Ashford, are you not?"

Lucy turned. The woman was somewhere past forty, her grey hair dressed plainly, her gown a deep blue that caught no candlelight. She stood with the stillness of someone who had long ago ceased to care whether she was noticed.

"I am Mrs. Mallet," the woman said. "I sit at your aunt's table at the boarding house."

Lucy knew her by sight — she had seen her at breakfast, a figure at the end of the table who ate her toast in silence and read a folded newspaper. They had not spoken.

"You must think it very strange," Mrs. Mallet said, "that I should speak to you now. I have been watching you this evening. Not to be impertinent. I was watching because you remind me of someone."

Lucy waited. On the floor, Clara was turning under the lieutenant's arm, her face flushed, her mouth open in a kind of breathless delight.

"I had a sister," Mrs. Mallet said. "She is dead now. She was like you. She stood at the edges of rooms and watched, and she thought it meant she had failed. But she was the one who saw everything. She saw which marriages would last and which would not. She saw which men were kind and which were merely charming. She saw, and she did not dance, and she believed herself the poorer for it."

The music ended. There was a rustling silence, the particular sound of a room full of people preparing to move.

"I did not dance because no one asked me," Lucy said. She had not meant to say it so plainly.

Mrs. Mallet regarded her. "I know," she said. "That is not a failure. That is an evening. You will have many more evenings, Miss Ashford, and some of them will be worse than this one, and some of them will be better, and some of them you will not understand until years afterward."

She reached out and took Lucy's dance card. With the small pencil she wrote a line on the blank surface — not a name, not a number, but an address: a street in Weymouth, a house number.

"Come tomorrow morning," she said. "I should like to introduce you to my nephew. He does not dance either."

Lucy wanted to protest, to say she did not need to be introduced, that she was not desperate, that she would sooner go home to the parish than be pitied by a stranger. But Mrs. Mallet was not looking at her with pity. She was looking at her with the steady attention of someone who recognized what she saw.

Clara arrived in a sweep of warmth and lavender water. "Lucy, you must come — they are serving supper — oh, Mrs. Mallet, I did not see you there." She spoke the name with the bright carelessness of someone who had never been overlooked.

"I was just leaving," Mrs. Mallet said. She inclined her head to Lucy. "Eleven o'clock. Do not be late. My nephew is always late, and I should like someone to be punctual for once."

She moved away through the crowd, her blue gown disappearing between the uniforms and the white muslin, and Lucy stood with the card in her hand, the address written across its empty face.

The next morning was grey, the sea hidden under a low haze. Clara slept late, exhausted from dancing, and Lucy dressed quietly and slipped out of the boarding house before her aunt had finished her tea.

She walked along the Esplanade. The resort town looked different in the morning light — smaller, shabbier, the grand façades revealed as plaster and paint. A fisherman on the beach was mending nets, his hands moving in a rhythm as old as the sea. Lucy watched him for a moment, then turned up the narrow street Mrs. Mallet had named.

The house was modest: three stories of dark brick, a green door, a knocker shaped like a lion's head. Lucy lifted it and let it fall. The sound echoed in the quiet street.

Mrs. Mallet opened the door herself. "You are punctual," she said. "Come in. He is in the parlour."

The parlour was small and dark, the windows facing a walled garden. The furniture was old but good. Books were stacked on every surface — on the tables, on the chairs, on the floor beside the sofa. A young man stood by the fireplace, holding a cup of tea. He was tall and thin, with dark hair that fell across his forehead, and he looked at Lucy with the startled expression of someone who had been interrupted in a private thought.

"This is my nephew," Mrs. Mallet said. "Mr. Edmund Hale. Edmund, this is Miss Ashford. She did not dance last night either."

Edmund Hale set down his cup. "I do not dance," he said. "I have never danced. My aunt believes this is a virtue. I believe it is a limitation. You must judge for yourself which of us is correct."

Lucy looked at him. He did not smile when he said this, but there was something in his voice — a dry warmth, like a fire that had burned down to its embers.

"I did not dance because no one asked me," she said. She had said it to Mrs. Mallet, and she would say it again. She would not pretend otherwise.

Edmund Hale looked at her for a long moment. "Then we are not the same," he said. "I have been asked, and I have refused. You have not been asked, and you have not refused. The distinction matters, I think."

"It does," Lucy said. "You are a man. You may refuse and it is a choice. I am a woman. If I am not asked, it is a verdict."

He nodded slowly. "Yes," he said. "I had not thought of it that way."

Mrs. Mallet left the room. Lucy heard her footsteps in the hall, and then the sound of a door closing. She and Edmund Hale stood on opposite sides of the parlour, the stacks of books between them.

"You have read Cowper," Lucy said. She had seen the volume on the table, the spine cracked, the pages marked with slips of paper.

"I have read everything," he said. "It is what I do instead of dancing. My aunt thinks it is a waste. She thinks I should go into society. She brings me to Weymouth every September and hopes the sea air will make me sociable."

"Has it?"

"No. But this morning she brought me a cup of tea and told me a young woman was coming to call, and I found I was curious. I am not often curious."

Lucy sat down on the chair nearest her. The book on its arm was a volume of natural history, open to a page about seabirds. She picked it up.

"Tell me about the seabirds," she said.

Edmund Hale sat down across from her. He began to talk. He talked the way Lucy listened — with full attention, with the quiet intensity of someone who had been waiting a long time to be heard. The grey light came through the window and settled on the books and the teacups and the space between them, and Lucy found that she was no longer thinking about the assembly rooms, or the empty dance card, or the fact that she had stood by a wall all night while Clara turned under a lieutenant's arm. She was thinking about seabirds, and about the way Edmund Hale's voice changed when he spoke of something he loved, and about how it was possible to spend an evening believing oneself a failure and then to wake in the morning and find that the world had not ended, that the sea was still there, and that a stranger's parlour could become, for an hour, the centre of the world.
