If any honest traveller, pursuing that crooked and uncomfortable thoroughfare which writhes itself from the region of Holborn into the shadows of Saffron Hill, had stumbled upon the narrow blotch of pavement known to its inhabitants—and indeed to the constabulary, when they were forced to acknowledge it—as Grubber’s Court, he would have found himself in a place where the very houses seemed to have turned their backs upon the world in a fit of chronic ill-temper. The street was so narrow that the leaning frontages, like two parties engaged in a violent quarrel across a gutter, almost touched at their upper stories, thereby excluding what feeble sunlight might have ventured into such a locality, and enveloping the area below in a permanent twilight of soot, damp, and mystery. At the darkest corner of this court, occupying a building whose shutters were so securely fastened, so rusted, and so entirely given over to decay, that they presented to the public gaze the appearance of a face winking in a permanent and sardonic slumber, stood the shop of Mr. Gideon Slum.

Mr. Slum was a retired pawnbroker; a trade which, of all the trades that ever flourished under the sun, is perhaps the most fatal to the preservation of a cheerful and confiding disposition. He was a gentleman who had spent upwards of forty years in the daily contemplation of human distress, human fraud, and human improvidence; and having seen so many wedding rings pass over his counter in exchange for a few shillings, and so many family Bibles sacrificed for a pint of gin, he had arrived at the fixed and unalterable conclusion that humanity was a vast conspiracy against his own property. He was a shrivelled, brittle, yellow-skinned man, with a face like a cracked parchment, and eyes that peered out from beneath two tufts of bristling grey hair with the sharp, restless, and suspicious glare of a rat guarding a particularly desirable piece of cheese.

It was into this cheerful retreat, and beneath the critical eye of this amiable patriarch, that Master Timothy Pipkin, a boy of ten years old, small for his age, pale for his age, and altogether much put upon by his age, was introduced on a damp Tuesday evening in November, by an aunt who was distinctly in a hurry to be rid of him.

‘Here he is!’ said the aunt, propelling Timothy into the gloom of the shop with a shove that was only one degree removed from a kick. ‘A boy of ten. His mother being dead, and his father being shipped to Botany Bay for certain misunderstandings respecting a silk handkerchief, he is yours by right of blood. Make a man of him, Mr. Slum, if you can; though I don’t expect it.’

‘Hem!’ said Mr. Slum, surveying the boy as if he were a doubtful article of plate, and a very base metal at that. ‘So. This is the child.’

‘This is the child,’ returned the aunt. ‘He eats, but he is not expensive. He sleeps, but he does not snore. And he can read print, which is more than I ever could. Good evening to you, Mr. Slum. I have a coach to catch.’ And she was gone, leaving Timothy standing in the middle of the dark shop, shivering, and feeling very much as if he had been swallowed by a great, dusty whale.

‘Well, boy,’ said Mr. Slum, after a long and uncomfortable silence, during which he walked slowly round Timothy, inspecting him from various angles, as a connoisseur might inspect a piece of doubtful porcelain. ‘What do you know of the world?’

‘Very little, sir,’ Timothy stammered.

‘A blessing,’ said Mr. Slum, with a nod of his head that set all the grey bristles quivering. ‘The less you know of it, the better. The world is a cheat, boy. Every man’s hand is against his neighbour, and his neighbour’s pocket is against him. I have seen it. I have lived it. I have taken the watches and the rings and the snuff-boxes of a thousand fools, and I tell you that there is no honesty in man, woman, or child. You will sleep in the back-room. You will not touch the pledges. You will not ask questions. You will earn your bread by making yourself useful, and if you break a single article upon those shelves, I will cast you into the street as I would cast a cracked basin. Do you understand?’

‘Yes, sir,’ said Timothy, who was beginning to think that Botany Bay might have been the more cheerful destination.

Thus began the domestic arrangement between Mr. Gideon Slum and his grandson; an arrangement which, if it was not founded upon mutual affection, was at least founded upon mutual wariness, and a profound respect on Timothy’s part for the heavy ebony walking-stick which the old man carried, and which he applied to the furniture, the floorboards, and occasionally the air around Timothy’s head with a vigour that argued a strong belief in the virtues of corporal persuasion.

Timothy’s days were passed in a perpetual twilight. The shutters of the shop were never opened. The door was never unbolted, save when Mr. Slum himself, with a caution that would have done credit to a conspirator in a tragedy, admitted some mysterious visitor who came by night, and who was invariably shown into the back-parlour, and invariably departed with a heavier pocket and a lighter heart. The shop itself was a museum of forgotten misery. It was crowded, from floor to ceiling, with the wrecks of human vanity and human hope. There were watches that had ceased to tick, and seemed, in their silent cases, to be holding their breath for eternity; there were fiddles that had danced to every riot and waltzed at every wedding, and now hung dismally from the ceiling, like so many dead and dried-up insects; there were suits of clothes that had outlived their wearers, and stared at the boy with a ghostly and reproachful air; there were rings, and brooches, and trinkets of every description, all huddled together in dusty trays, like the bones in a charnel-house.

And there was one peculiar feature of this ghastly collection which, before Timothy had been a month in the place, had fixed itself upon his mind, and filled him with a curiosity so intense that it almost amounted to a physical pain. Every article in the shop bore a little ticket, written in the old man’s cramped and crabbed hand, with a date and a number. And every date was old. There was nothing new in the shop. There was no recent pledge. The dust of thirty years lay upon the shelves, and the spiders had spun their webs over the watches and the rings, as if to provide a shroud for the dead vanities of a past generation.

‘Grandfather,’ said Timothy, one evening, when the old man was sitting over a meagre fire of coals, nursing his ebony cane, and looking, in the flickering light, like a gigantic and melancholy goblin—‘grandfather, why do you never sell the pledges?’

Mr. Slum raised his head, and fixed his sharp eyes upon the boy with a glare that had in it something of surprise, something of anger, and something of a dull, dead pain.

‘Sell them?’ he croaked. ‘Sell them? Who spoke of selling them? They are my security, boy. My security! Every one of them represents a sovereign lent, and a sovereign not repaid. Do you think I am going to let the rogues who borrowed my money redeem their trumpery trash? Not I. I keep the pledges. I keep the money. I keep the security. Let them come for their goods, and I will give them the law. I will give them the sheriff. I will give them the jail!’

‘But they are all so old,’ said Timothy, who was a thoughtful boy, and had a habit of looking at things with his own eyes, which is a very dangerous habit for a boy, and one that generally leads him into trouble. ‘There is nothing here that has been pledged this year, or last year, or the year before. The tickets are all yellow and faded. Why is that?’

The old man’s face underwent a strange convulsion. He gripped his cane with a hand that trembled, and for a moment Timothy thought he was going to strike him. But he did not. He only said, in a voice that was hoarse and hollow, and that seemed to come from a great distance: ‘Because—because I am a just man, boy. I keep what is my own. I keep it safe. I keep it here. And I will not be robbed. I will not be robbed by the world, nor by the law, nor by any man that breathes.’

He rose, and hobbled to the window, and peered out through a crack in the shutter, as if he expected to see the whole world assembled in Grubber’s Court, with intent to deprive him of his watches and his rings. And Timothy, sitting by the fire, looked after him, and thought that he had never seen a man who looked so much like a prisoner in his own dungeon, and a jailer of his own soul.
