Lord Adrian Vane had always been a devoted High Priest of Beauty. By the age of five-and-twenty, he had amassed a collection of enamelled snuff-boxes, Venetian glass, and faded tapestries that was the envy of every connoisseur in London. His drawing-room in Mayfair was a symphony of pale silks and rare orchids; his life, a carefully composed still-life of exquisite and utterly meaningless poses. He held the firm conviction that nothing in the world was really worth a man’s attention unless it was perfectly useless, and he governed his existence by this infallible aesthetic principle. He was universally admired by those who did not know him, and deeply pitied by those who did, for it is a melancholy truth that a man who has found everything he wants has very little left to live for.

It was at Lord and Lady Blackwood’s autumn gathering at Thoresby Park that the catastrophe occurred. Lady Blackwood, a hostess of impeccable taste and entirely artificial hair, had gathered together the most ornamental people in England. The drawing-rooms were filled with a glittering mob of politicians who had never thought, and duchesses who had never felt. In the great conservatory, where the heavy scent of stephanotis hung upon the air like a polite sigh, Adrian first beheld Miss Hester Wynn.

She was seated alone, reading a French treatise on moral philosophy. Her gown was of a severe and unrelieved black, totally devoid of the lace and jet that fashion demanded. Her face was a profound disappointment to the symmetry of nature. Her features were not merely irregular; they were arranged with the stubborn defiance of a mind that refused to conform to any classical standard. She had the forehead of a scholar, the chin of a reformer, and the nose of a woman who asks too many questions. She was, in the strict and limited language of society, distinctly plain.

Lord Adrian was utterly fascinated. He approached her, made his profoundest bow, and ventured a trivial remark about the weather. She looked up at him with a pair of remarkably luminous grey eyes, and replied with a sentence so perfectly turned, so entirely devoid of a cliché, that he felt as though he had been listening to a barrel-organ all his life, and had suddenly heard a string quartet. He sat down beside her. They spoke of Italian poetry, of the decay of faith, and the absolute necessity of lying in art. She possessed the rare and dangerous gift of making one think. She contradicted him, not with the bluntness of a bluestocking, but with the delicate irony of a true artist. When she spoke, her plainness vanished entirely. She became a brilliant piece of intellectual sculpture, alive with the fire of a hidden gem.

Within a fortnight, Lord Adrian announced their engagement. London was profoundly shocked, and London, being deeply moral, was secretly delighted. To be shocked is the highest form of entertainment a metropolis can offer. 

At his club in St. James’s Street, his friends gathered around him in a state of elegant dismay. 

"My dear Adrian," said the Duke of Heatherstone, stroking his magnificent and quite genuine moustache, "you have always been the arbiter of elegance. It is monstrous. You, who would not look at a china plate unless it had a flaw in the glaze, to marry a woman who is, frankly, a flaw in the glaze of society itself!"

"Beauty," replied Adrian, balancing a macaroon on the edge of his coffee cup, "is merely a form of genius. But it is, unfortunately, the only form of genius that fades. Miss Wynn possesses a beauty which time can only improve. To converse with her is like walking through a garden of the most extraordinary and poisonous flowers. One is continually being pricked into thought."

"But her complexion!" cried young Lord Edgar Grafton, a man whose own intellect was entirely confined to the cut of his coats. "It is decidedly muddy. And her hair! It is the exact colour of a wet November pavement."

"Quite so," said Adrian, with a smile of supreme composure. "A perfectly pretty face is a thing of a season. It blooms, it withers, and one is left with nothing but the tedious necessity of remembering one’s vows. Hester’s face is a landscape. At first glance, one might think it a mere barren moor, but upon closer inspection, one discovers the most fascinating minerals. I assure you, Edgar, I have found the one face in England that will never bore me."

"You will be a laughing-stock," said the Duke.

"I would infinitely rather be a laughing-stock than a wit," Adrian answered. "Wits are so terribly common nowadays. A scandal is the only thing that keeps society awake."

They were married in the early spring, and Adrian carried his bride to his house in Charles Street. 

Those who expected to see the young Lord transformed into a miserable recluse were entirely disappointed. He and his wife were inseparable, not with the cloying sentimentality of a pair of ordinary lovers, but with the fierce, intellectual comradeship of two duelists. Their drawing-room became the most brilliant salon in London. It was no longer hung merely with pale silks; it was hung with epigrams. 

Adrian discovered, to his vast amusement, that his exquisite china and rare enamels had lost their charm. He would sit for hours watching Hester as she dismantled the pretensions of a visiting politician, or peeled the veneer of virtue from a duchess. She did it with such charming gravity, such an air of innocent inquiry, that the victims never knew they had been dissected until they found themselves in pieces upon the floor. She was a master of the art of conversation, which, as Adrian often remarked, is the art of saying the most outrageous things in the most respectable manner.

One evening, as they sat together in the library, the gaslight casting soft, amber shadows on the spines of the calf-bound books, Adrian took her hand. He noticed, with a certain abstract pleasure, that her hand was quite entirely devoid of rings, save for the heavy gold band he had placed upon it. 

"I am told," he said, "that Lady Blackwood is weeping into her lace pillows every night, declaring that I have ruined my life by marrying a woman who does not possess a single feature that could be reproduced in marble."

"She is quite right," said Hester, turning a page of her volume. "Marble is a very cold and unforgiving medium. It is entirely unsuited to the complexities of my character."

"Do you never regret it?" he asked, leaning forward. "Do you never wish that Nature had made you beautiful, so that you might have walked through life with the easy tyranny of a pretty woman?"

Hester looked up, and the grey eyes sparkled with a sudden, deep merriment. 

"On the contrary, Adrian," she said. "I am profoundly grateful to Nature. Had she given me a Grecian nose and a flawless complexion, I should have spent my entire life being admired by men who had nothing to say, and I should inevitably have ended by marrying one of them. As it is, I was forced to develop a mind, and I have been rewarded by marrying the only man in London who is clever enough to know that a beautiful face is merely a mask, and that a plain one is a window."

Adrian smiled, and pressing her hand, sank back into his chair. He had realized, at last, the supreme paradox of his existence. He had spent his youth worshipping the beautiful and the useless, and he had found his profoundest joy in the useful and the plain. For he knew now that a man may possess all the treasures of art, and all the orchids of the tropics, but if he has not found a mind that can contradict him, he is the most impoverished creature in the universe. And to be bored, after all, is the only unpardonable sin.
