The discovery, like most of its kind, was a matter of accident and a certain stubbornness of temperament. My name is Arthur Penhale, and at the time of which I write I was a demonstrator in chemistry at a minor London college, a post which afforded me a cramped laboratory, a modest stipend, and ample leisure for those inquiries which the more fortunate call hobbies and the less fortunate call follies. My particular folly was a study of the alkaloids of the common hedgerow, a subject which had already consumed three years of my life and was likely to consume several more. It was in the root of the wild bryony, that inconspicuous creeper which the rustic calls “devil’s turnip,” that I found the substance I came to call—in the privacy of my notebooks—veritine.

The first intimation of its peculiar property was entirely incidental. I had been feeding small quantities of the extract to a guinea-pig of a placid and uncommunicative disposition, and had observed nothing more remarkable than a certain tendency to somnolence. It was my landlady’s boy, a lad of eleven with a passion for the laboratory and a disregard for my instructions, who provided the illuminating accident. He had been assisting me—or, more accurately, hindering me—upon a Thursday afternoon, and had contrived to transfer a trace of the alkaloid from a watch-glass to his tongue. I expected convulsions, or at least a severe retching. Instead he stood quite still for a moment, regarding me with an expression of singular candour, and then announced, unprompted, that he had broken the glass tube I had been at such pains to calibrate, that he had eaten two of my biscuits the previous Tuesday, and that he considered my necktie to be in execrable taste. The candour was momentary, followed by a look of profound confusion and a hasty retreat. But I had my clue, and I followed it with the ardour of a man who sees the end of a long and thankless inquiry.

I need not detail the weeks of patient experiment, the careful measurement of dosage, the administration of the drug to the unsuspecting and the suspicious, the gradual establishment of the fact that a quantity so small as to be invisible in a glass of wine would produce, for a period of some four to seven minutes, a complete and involuntary suspension of the capacity for falsehood. The subject so treated did not become, as one might imagine, a mere blunt instrument of confession. The effect was subtler and, to my mind, more terrible. The ordinary conventions of discourse, the polite fictions, the small lubrications of social intercourse, all these were not so much abandoned as rendered impossible. The subject spoke the truth as he apprehended it, with a directness that was frequently embarrassing and occasionally devastating. And then the effect passed, leaving behind it a blank and a sense of having been momentarily dispossessed.

It was my aunt, Lady Marrable, who provided the occasion for the first public demonstration. She was a woman of considerable social ambition and a somewhat inflexible determination to see her family advanced in the world. My scientific pursuits she regarded as an unfortunate phase, to be indulged in private and never mentioned in company. She had, however, a certain grudging respect for my intellect, and it was this respect which led her to invite me to her dinner-party in Kensington on the twenty-third of November.

The party was, as my aunt intended, a distinguished one. There was Sir Hector Vane, the retired diplomatist, a man of polished and impenetrable courtesy; his wife, Lady Vane, whose tongue was a byword in three counties; a certain Mrs. Pemberton, the widow of a manufacturer, whose wealth was new and whose pretensions were older than her husband’s grave; and young Mr. Cyril Pemberton, her son, a vapid and agreeable young man who had been marked by my aunt as a suitable match for my cousin Edith. Edith herself was present, a pale, intelligent girl of twenty, whose quiet observations I had always suspected of a certain ironic penetration.

I had brought with me a small phial of veritine in solution, and a plan which I now regard as the height of scientific folly. I had conceived the notion of administering the drug to the entire company, in order to observe its effects upon the complex organism of a formal dinner. I had told myself that the opportunity was too good to be missed, that the cause of science demanded it, that no harm could possibly result. I had not, I think, seriously considered the possibility that the truth, when spoken by a roomful of people of fashion, might prove to be something more than a scientific curiosity.

The soup was served, the fish was discussed, and I found my opportunity in the matter of the wine. My aunt’s butler was a man of inflexible routine and a weakness for the cellar; a brief conversation and the substitution of a decanter which I had prepared in the privacy of my own room accomplished the rest. The wine circulated. The conversation, which had been flowing in its usual channels of gossip and innuendo, began to take on a curious and disconcerting quality.

Sir Hector, who had been speaking of his experiences in Constantinople, paused in the middle of a sentence, looked about him with a bewildered air, and said: “I must confess that I have never in my life been so bored as I was by the Sultan’s accounts of his own wisdom. I have heard it said that the man is a monster, but I can assure you that he is merely a tedious one.”

Lady Vane, who had been complimenting my aunt upon her table, remarked with a sudden and startling clarity: “I have always found your cook to be inconsistent, my dear Lady Marrable. The soup was excellent, but this fish is distinctly overdone, and I am certain the man has been drinking.”

Mrs. Pemberton, who had been speaking of her charitable works, announced that she had never in her life felt the slightest interest in the poor, that she considered the whole business a form of self-display, and that she only continued it because it was expected of a woman in her position.

My aunt, whose face had been growing steadily more rigid, now turned to me with an expression of terrible suspicion. “Arthur,” she said, “what have you done?”

I had not intended to speak. But the drug was in my wine as well, and I found my tongue loosened against my will. “I have administered a drug which renders the subject incapable of falsehood for a period of several minutes,” I said. “I did it in the interests of science. I now perceive that it was a mistake.”

The effect of this announcement upon the company was instantaneous. Sir Hector rose to his feet, his face purple. “Good God, sir,” he cried, “do you mean to tell me that I have been drugged? That I have been made to say—to say—” He could not bring himself to repeat his remarks about the Sultan.

“You have,” I said. “And so has everyone else at this table. I can only offer my profound apologies and assure you that the effect will pass in a few minutes.”

The few minutes that followed were the longest of my life. The truth, once released, proved to have a momentum of its own. Mr. Cyril Pemberton confessed to my cousin Edith that he had never read a book in his life, that he found her conversation intimidating, and that he had only been courting her at his mother’s insistence. Edith, thus addressed, replied that she had known this for some weeks, that she found him agreeable but entirely without substance, and that she had been considering a proposal from a young doctor in Bloomsbury which she now intended to accept. Mrs. Pemberton informed my aunt that her invitations had been sought only because she was known to be wealthy, and that her social position was a matter of general derision among the older families. And Lady Vane, turning to Sir Hector, remarked that she had known of his affair with the wife of the Portuguese ambassador for three years, and that she considered it both vulgar and unhygienic.

The drug wore off, as I had predicted, in a matter of minutes. But the silence that followed was not the silence of forgetfulness. It was the silence of a room in which every person present was frantically reviewing what had been said, and calculating the damage. My aunt rose from her chair with a dignity that I could not but admire. “The evening is concluded,” she said. “I trust that no one will feel obliged to remain.”

The consequences were, as my aunt had predicted, catastrophic. Within a week, the story had circulated through every drawing-room in Kensington. Sir Hector and Lady Vane were understood to be negotiating a separation. Mrs. Pemberton had withdrawn her son from London and was said to be taking the waters at Baden-Baden. My aunt, who had invested considerable capital in her social position, found that capital suddenly worthless. She did not speak to me for a year, and when she did, it was to inform me that she had burned my copy of the proceedings of the Chemical Society and that I was no longer welcome at her table.

I have not repeated the experiment. The veritine remains in my laboratory, in a locked cabinet, and I take it out occasionally to look at it. It is a small thing, a few grains of white powder in a glass tube, and it has taught me a lesson that I am not likely to forget. The truth, I have concluded, is a dangerous commodity. It is not that people cannot bear it; it is that they cannot bear each other after they have heard it. The social fabric, whatever its faults, is held together by a thousand small fictions, and the man who tears them away does not liberate his fellows—he merely leaves them standing in the cold, shivering and exposed, and wondering why they ever invited him to dinner.
