The gas-lamps in Camden Road flared and dimmed, flared and dimmed, as if they too were breathing the thick November fog that seeped under the door and made the chalk damp. It was half-past six, and Eleanor stood at the long deal table, smoothing the edges of the printed sheets with her thumb. The ink had come off on her finger; a small blue-black crescent under the nail, and she thought, rubbing it, how strange it was that ink, which was dry, should still be wet, should still stain, hours after the press had rolled. The room smelled of damp wool, of gas, of the slightly sour smell of men who had been working since six in the morning in rooms where the fires were never allowed to go out. She knew that smell well enough now, after three years of evening classes. It was the smell of the working men's college, which had once seemed so noble an undertaking and now seemed merely a habit, like brushing one's hair or winding the clock—something one did without thinking, and yet could not stop doing without feeling that some essential thread in the fabric of the day had been cut.

They came in one by one, trailing the fog behind them. First Mr. Petty, the older man with the walrus moustache who worked at the goods yard and could never get the hang of the semicolon; then the two young ones, Clark and Naseby, who smelled of oil and giggled together at the back like schoolboys, which indeed they still were. And then, last, always last, so that the door seemed to open specifically for him and for no one else, Elias Mowbray.

He was a printer. He had told her that the first evening, standing in the doorway with his cap in his hands, turning it round and round, so that she had noticed the ink under his thumbnail, under the nail, a blue-black crescent, exactly the colour of—no, she would not think what it was the colour of. He was a printer, and he had a wife, and three children, the youngest still a baby. She knew this because he had mentioned it once, in connection with an absence—he had missed two Tuesdays in October, and when he came back he said, simply, that the baby had been ill but was better now, and Eleanor had felt something loosen in her chest, a knot she had not known was there, and she had been frightened by the loosening, more frightened than by the knot.

Tonight he carried something under his arm, wrapped in brown paper. She saw it as he came in, and looked away quickly, because she had learned—had she not?—that to look at him too long, or at the wrong moment, was to feel the room tilt slightly, as if the floor had shifted, and everything on it, the desks, the inkwells, the printed sheets, the very fog itself, slid imperceptibly towards the place where he stood. He sat down in his usual seat, third row, by the window, and laid the package on the desk beside his inkwell and his pen and the sheet of paper on which he had been practising his copperplate, which was already quite good, quite remarkably good, though she had told him so only once and then had bitten her lip, because praise, she felt, should be rationed, like sugar, like coal, like all the necessaries of life this winter.

"Now then," she said. "Page fourteen. The passage from Sir Thomas Browne."

They read. Or rather, she read, and they followed, some of them mouthing the words, Mr. Petty's moustache working up and down over the syllables like a small animal grazing. The fog pressed against the window. Somewhere a tram bell rang, and the sound came through the glass muffled and distant, as if it were ringing at the bottom of a pond. And all the while she was aware of the brown paper package on the desk in the third row, aware of it as one is aware of a clock ticking in an empty room—not hearing it exactly, but feeling the pulse of it in the blood, the regular insistence of it, so that when at last the hour ended and the men began to gather their things, she felt a kind of relief, as if a weight had been lifted, and then immediately a kind of dread, because he was not gathering his things, he was staying behind, and the others were filing out, Clark and Naseby jostling each other in the doorway, Mr. Petty nodding to her with his familiar, slightly mournful courtesy, and then the door closing and the two of them alone with the gas hissing in its bracket and the fog thickening against the glass.

He picked up the package and came towards her. He had a way of walking, a slight rolling gait, the left foot turning in a little, that she had come to recognise from a distance, so that even before she could see his face she knew him by the walk, the way one knows a bird by its flight. He set the package on the table between them, where the printed sheets had been, and pushed it towards her with his ink-stained hand.

"I printed this," he said. "For the class."

But it was not for the class. She knew that as soon as she touched the paper, which was heavy, good paper, not the cheap stuff they used for exercises. She unwrapped it. It was a book. A small book, octavo, bound in blue-grey boards with a paper label on the spine, and the label printed, in the finest type, with a title she did not recognise—some verses, it seemed, some poems—and beneath the title, in smaller type, his name. E. Mowbray. She opened it. The pages were clean and sharp, the margins generous, the type beautifully chosen, a Caslon, she thought, or something near it, and the ink laid down with the even richness that only a good press, well handled, could achieve. She turned the pages slowly, feeling them beneath her fingers, the slight bite of the type on the paper, the way the light caught the impression, and she thought of him standing at the press, inking the type, pulling the lever, the sheets coming off one by one, damp and shining, and all the while thinking—for he must have been thinking—of whom? Of what?

"It is very fine," she said. "The setting is excellent. You have a good eye for leading."

She heard her own voice as if it belonged to someone else, someone calm and knowledgeable, someone who assessed books the way a doctor assesses a pulse, with professional detachment, and she thought, what a lie, what a monstrous lie, for she did not feel detached at all, she felt as if the room had filled with water and she were drowning in it, drowning in the smell of ink and paper and damp wool, and his face, which was above her, was the face of someone she could not have, and the book, which lay between them on the deal table, was not a book at all but a question, a question she could not answer because she did not know what was being asked.

He stood there, his hands at his sides, turning his cap again, that slow rotation of the brim, and she saw that the ink on his thumb was the same colour as the ink on hers, and that their hands, if they were to touch, would leave identical marks, blue-black crescents, on each other's skin.

"Thank you, Elias," she said. "I shall read it tonight."

And he nodded, once, and put on his cap, and went out into the fog, which swallowed him at once, so that she saw him only as a shape, a darkness, and then not at all. The door closed. The gas hissed. She stood for a long moment with her hand on the book, feeling the ridges of the type through the paper, and the fog pressed against the window, and somewhere a tram bell rang, and rang again, and was still.
