The singular business at Ardenmere Hall came to my attention upon a raw morning in late October, when a brisk east wind was stripping the last of the leaves from the limes along the drive. My companion, Sherlock Holmes, had been three days in a state of ennui, pacing our Baker Street rooms like a caged panther, and I confess I was glad of the interruption.

The card announced a Mr. Silas Croft, Steward to Sir Humphrey Vane. The man himself was a model of Shropshire sobriety—square-shouldered, weather-beaten, and dressed in a serviceable tweed that had seen better days. He sat upon the edge of the chair opposite Holmes, turning his bowler hat in hands that were thick and calloused, yet curiously restless.

“Sir Humphrey is my employer, Mr. Holmes,” Croft began, his voice a low, steady burr. “He is a good master, but he is a proud man, and a secretive one. He will not have the police in the matter, and yet I cannot sleep easy in my bed until it is settled.”

“Proceed, Mr. Croft,” said Holmes, steepling his fingers. “We are all attention.”

“The estate lies some twelve miles from Shrewsbury. It is a fine place, with a lake—Lilywater, we call it—situated in the valley below the Hall. It is fed by the Severn brook and is a good fifty yards across. Now, sir, for the past fortnight, I have found the lake drained of a night and refilled by morning.”

I looked up from my notes. “Drained? You mean the water level has dropped?”

“I mean, sir, that the bed of the lake has been laid bare,” Croft said, with a shudder that spoke of some profound disturbance. “I have seen it with my own eyes. I rose at three in the morning, having had a suspicion. The moon was behind a cloud, but the white clay of the bed shone like a ghost. The water was gone. Yet when the sun rose, the lake was full again, and not a ripple to tell the tale.”

Holmes’s lethargy vanished instantly. He sat up, his grey eyes alight with that singular intensity which I have so often described. “A most remarkable phenomenon. And the mechanism of this drainage?”

“There is none, sir. That is the devil of it. There is no sluice, no pump, no channel cut in the bank. The water simply… ceases to be there. And then it returns. I have measured the depth; it is normal. I have walked the perimeter; the banks are untouched.”

“And Sir Humphrey?”

“He laughed at me. Said I had been at the brandy. But he forbade me to call the constable. He said it was a natural trick of the soil, a sink-hole, perhaps. But I have farmed this land for thirty years, Mr. Holmes. There is no sink-hole in Lilywater.”

Holmes sprang to his feet and reached for his ulster. “We must see this for ourselves. Watson, your revolver, if you please. One never knows what the Shropshire clay may conceal. Mr. Croft, we shall take the twelve-forty train.”

The journey was accomplished in silence, save for Holmes’s occasional, sharp questions regarding the topography of the Vane estate. We arrived at Ardenmere Hall—a heavy, Jacobean pile of red sandstone—late in the afternoon. Sir Humphrey Vane received us in the library. He was a massive man, stooped by age and a permanent scowl, with a complexion the colour of old parchment.

“I am obliged to you for coming, though I think it a fool’s errand,” he grumbled, waving a hand that trembled slightly. “Croft is a good man, but fanciful. The lake is a lake. Do as you please, but let there be no scandal.”

We left the Hall as the shadows began to lengthen. The wind had dropped, and a heavy mist was rolling up from the brook, muffling the sound of our footsteps. Croft led us down a winding path through a belt of alders to the shore of Lilywater.

It was a desolate spot. The reeds were brown and brittle, and the water was black and still. Holmes walked the bank with the quick, nervous energy of a terrier. He tapped the earth with his cane, examined the clay for fissures, and once lay flat upon his stomach to peer into the murky depths.

“There is no outlet here,” he muttered. “The soil is impervious clay. It is a basin. And yet, the water leaves.”

He turned to the steward. “You say the water returns before dawn. Does it return with force? Is there a sound?”

“No sound, sir. It is as if it seeps back through the very ground.”

“And the history of this spot, Mr. Croft? Is there any legend attached to Lilywater?”

Croft hesitated, glancing nervously toward the dark outline of the Hall. “There is a story, sir. An old tale. They say that before the lake was dug, three hundred years ago, there stood a chapel upon this site. A place of some ill repute, belonging to the old religion. It was pulled down and the valley flooded to drown the memory of it.”

“A chapel,” Holmes repeated, his eyes gleaming in the gloom. “And what was the name of this chapel?”

“I believe it was the Chapel of St. Cuthbert the Silent. Though why a saint should be silent, I cannot say.”

“Silent,” Holmes murmured. “Indeed. Watson, look at the bank here. Do you observe the reeds? They are bent away from the water, not by the wind, but by a mechanical force. Someone has been drawing a heavy weight over them.”

We waited. The mist thickened, and the cold bit through our coats. At two in the morning, the world was a grey, impenetrable void. Suddenly, Holmes gripped my arm.

“Listen,” he hissed.

There was a low, rhythmic sound—a groaning of timber and rope. It came from the centre of the lake. We crept to the edge, our boots sinking into the mud. The mist seemed to part for a moment, revealing a sight that defied reason.

The water of Lilywater was not draining away; it was rising. Or rather, the bed of the lake was rising.

Slowly, ponderously, a flat expanse of stone and timber heaved itself above the surface of the black water. It was a vast, square platform, slick with green weed and dripping mud. Upon it stood the ruined walls of a low building, roofless and skeletal. It was the chapel, brought up from the depths like a diver gasping for air.

“Good God!” I cried. “A caisson! A submersible platform!”

“Precisely,” Holmes whispered, his voice vibrating with excitement. “A feat of engineering, though rusted and ancient. Look, Watson, the mechanism is not entirely modern. Those are hydraulic rams, hidden beneath the structure. But who works them?”

As if in answer to his question, a lantern flared upon the rising platform. A figure moved within the ruined walls—a man in oilskins, carrying a spade. He began to dig frantically in the centre of the chapel floor.

“It is Sir Humphrey!” Croft gasped. “It is the master himself!”

Holmes stepped out from the shadows of the reeds. “A cold night for excavations, Sir Humphrey,” he called out.

The figure froze. The lantern swung round, casting wild shadows across the wet stone. Sir Humphrey Vane stood there, his face a mask of fury and terror.

“You meddling fools!” he roared. “I told Croft to leave it be!”

“You were searching for the chalice,” Holmes said, stepping onto the submerged platform as the water lapped at his boots. “The treasure of the Chapel of St. Cuthbert. I recall the records of the Civil War; the plate of this parish was buried here before the Parliamentarians came. You built this mechanism years ago, did you not? A secret lift to raise the ruins and allow you to search the foundations without the world knowing. But you could not work it alone, and you could not trust your men. So you did it at night, raising the ruin, digging, and lowering it again before dawn.”

Sir Humphrey slumped against the broken altar. “It is mine,” he said sullenly. “It is the heritage of my house. I have spent half my fortune on this hydraulic device. But the iron has rusted. The platform sticks. I cannot get deep enough.”

“And you feared the police would seize the treasure if they knew,” Holmes concluded. “A foolish fear, Sir Humphrey. The law is interested in the living, not the dead. However, this device is a danger to the public. If it were to fail while a boat were passing, the result would be catastrophic.”

Holmes turned to the steward. “Mr. Croft, you have done your duty. The mystery is solved. It is not a supernatural event, but a mechanical one, driven by avarice.”

Sir Humphrey looked at us with a pathetic sort of pride. “I have found nothing yet,” he muttered. “But the stones speak of it. It is there.”

“It may be,” Holmes said dryly, stepping back onto the solid earth. “But I fancy you will need more than a hydraulic ram to lift the weight of your own stubbornness. Come, Watson. The mist is clearing, and I believe we may just catch the early express to town.”

As we walked back toward the Hall, leaving the old man to his dripping, subterranean ruin, Holmes lit a cigarette.

“A curious case, Watson,” he observed. “A man will go to the greatest lengths to conceal his history, even if he must drown it and raise it again every night. It is a metaphor for the human mind, is it not? We all have our submerged chapels.”

“Perhaps,” I said, shivering. “But I prefer my mysteries dry, Holmes.”

He laughed, and we quickened our pace toward the light of the Hall.
