The house at Ravensdale stood, as I had often thought, like a proud, cold secret kept from the world, its grey stone face turned from the village and the valley, looking only to the bleak and barren hills. Within its walls, the very air seemed to hold its breath. I had been but three months in the service of the Ashbys, and in that time I had learned to walk softly, to speak low, and to keep my eyes fixed upon my duties, for the silence of the place was a living thing, and it watched.

My charge was the younger daughter, a child of ten, with a spirit like a lark in a cage, who would have been merry enough had she not been so often reminded of the necessity for a grave and sober deportment. But my heart, of its own accord, had gone out to Miss Ashby—the elder, Miss Eleanor—who at eighteen was the undisputed jewel of the house, and, I sometimes feared, its appointed sacrifice. She was beautiful, but with a beauty that seemed to me less a gift than a burden, for it was a passive, marble sort of loveliness, pale and still, as though the fire within had been banked down and near extinguished. Her father, Sir Richard, was a man of iron rule and iron will, whose word was law from the cellar to the attic. Her mother, Lady Ashby, was a faded, anxious woman, who lived in perpetual dread of her husband’s displeasure, and whose only rebellion was a silent, helpless sort of love for her children.

The shadow that had fallen across the house, and which I had felt from the first day of my arrival, had a name: Mr. Mortimer. He was a man of fifty, perhaps more, with a face the colour of tallow and eyes that were small and restless, like those of a rat in a granary. He was rich—fabulously so, it was said—and he had lately become a widower. He was a guest at Ravensdale with increasing frequency, and I soon perceived that his visits were not for the pleasure of Sir Richard’s conversation, nor for the charms of Lady Ashby’s table. His gaze followed Miss Eleanor with a persistence that made my blood run cold; it was not the look of admiration, but of appraisal, as a man might eye a fine piece of land he intended to purchase.

One afternoon, in the grey light of a November day, I was seated in the schoolroom with my little pupil, who was struggling over a map of the Holy Land, when the door opened and Miss Eleanor entered. She was wrapped in a shawl, and her face was paler than usual, and there was a trembling about her lips that spoke of a great agitation. She dismissed her sister with a gentle word, and then, turning to me, she said, in a voice that was scarce above a whisper:

“Miss Winter, I must speak with you, or I think my heart will break.”

I laid down the book, and bade her sit. She sank into a chair by the window, and for a moment looked out upon the sodden lawn and the dark, dripping trees. Then she turned her eyes upon me, and in them I saw a wildness, a terror, that went through me like a knife.

“My father has spoken to me,” she said, and her voice was dry and hard. “He has told me that Mr. Mortimer has done me the honour to desire my hand in marriage. He has told me that the match is a most excellent one, that it will secure the fortunes of the family, and that a refusal would be an act of disobedience and ingratitude that he would never forgive.”

She paused, and a strange, mirthless smile crossed her lips. “He did not ask me if I loved him. He did not even ask me if I liked him. He spoke of duty, of position, of the settlement of the estate. I felt as though I were a parcel of land, being made over to a neighbour.”

“And what did you answer?” I asked, though my heart already knew.

“What could I answer? I was silent. My mother was in the room, and she looked at me with such a piteous entreaty in her eyes—not to refuse, but to submit, to keep the peace. And my father—he stood over me like a judge, and said that the wedding should take place in the spring. He said it as though it were already done. He said it as though I had no more power to alter it than the stones of this house.”

Her composure, which had been but a thin veneer, now cracked, and she buried her face in her hands. “Oh, Miss Winter,” she sobbed, “I am so afraid. I am so afraid of him. Not of his anger—though that is terrible enough—but of himself. When he is near me, I feel as though the life were being drawn out of me. He looks at me, and I feel unclean. I cannot bear to be in the same room with him. And to think of being his wife—to be bound to him for ever—it is a living death!”

I rose and went to her, and took her hand. It was as cold as ice. I had no comfort to offer, no counsel that could avail. I was but a governess, a dependent, a creature of no consequence in the eyes of Sir Richard Ashby. To speak a word against his will would be to sign my own dismissal and to bring down his wrath upon the head of the very girl I wished to save. I was powerless, and the knowledge of my own impotence was a bitter gall in my throat.

“Have you no friend,” I asked at last, “to whom you might appeal? A relative, a godmother, anyone whose word might have weight with your father?”

She shook her head. “My godmother is dead. My only living relative besides my parents is my father’s brother, my uncle, who is a clergyman in a distant parish. He is a good man, but he is poor, and he has quarrelled with my father over some matter of the estate. He would have no influence.”

“And Mr. Mortimer himself?” I said. “Does he know of your aversion? Have you never shown him?”

“I have tried,” she answered, “to be cold, to be distant. But he seems to take my coldness as a kind of coyness, a modesty that he finds becoming. He smiles at it, and says that he likes a girl who is not forward. He speaks of the day when I shall learn to love him. He speaks of it with a confidence that is horrible.”

She looked at me with an intensity that was almost fierce. “Miss Winter, you are the only person in this house who has ever spoken to me as if I had a mind of my own. You have taught my sister, and you have been kind to me. I know you cannot help me—I know you have no power. But I had to tell someone, or I think I should have gone mad. I had to say it aloud, that I am to be sold, and that no one will save me.”

The words hung in the air between us, and the rain beat against the window-panes with a mournful, insistent rhythm. I thought of the words of the Apostle, that the wages of sin is death; and I thought, too, that the wages of a fortune might be a living death, and that the world called it a prudent match. My heart burned within me with a sense of injustice so keen that it was like a physical pain. I, who had no rights, no prospects, no voice in the councils of the great, felt a fierce, rebellious anger against the whole order of things that could make a father a tyrant and a daughter a chattel.

“You have done right to tell me,” I said, and my voice, though low, was firm. “You have done right to speak, and to refuse to let your soul be silenced. I cannot promise to save you, but I can promise to be your friend, and to do whatever lies within my small power to aid you. And I can tell you this: that a marriage entered into against the conscience is no marriage at all, but a sacrilege. You are not a parcel of land, Miss Eleanor. You are a woman, with a soul that is your own, and no man—not your father, not Mr. Mortimer, not all the world—has the right to take it from you.”

She looked at me, and the tears stood in her eyes, but there was a light in them now that had not been there before. “You speak,” she said, “as if I had a choice. But what choice have I? If I refuse, I am penniless, homeless, and my father will never forgive me. I shall be an outcast.”

“Perhaps,” I said. “But an outcast with a conscience is better than a queen without one. And who knows what doors may open, if you have the courage to knock. Your uncle, the clergyman—is he a man of courage?”

“He is,” she said, “and he is kind. He would take me in, I think, if I could but reach him. But my father would never permit it.”

“Then,” I said, “we must wait, and watch, and pray. And when the moment comes, we must be ready to act. Do not despair, Miss Eleanor. Despair is the devil’s own weapon. Keep your courage, and keep your silence, and trust that the right way will be shown to you.”

She pressed my hand, and rose. “I will try,” she said. “You have given me a little strength. I will try.”

She went out, and I was left alone in the grey, silent room, with the rain still falling and the wind moaning in the chimneys. I looked out upon the darkening landscape, and I felt a strange exaltation, as though I had been permitted to see, for a moment, into the very heart of things, and had found there, amidst all the cruelty and the greed, a spark of something divine—the courage of a human soul refusing to be enslaved. It was but a spark, and the darkness was great, and I knew not what the morrow might bring. But I held to it, as a drowning man holds to a spar, and I prayed, in my own fashion, that it might be fanned into a flame.
