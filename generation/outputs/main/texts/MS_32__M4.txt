The autumn of eighteen hundred and thirty-one had been singularly dreary in the west of England. Rain had fallen almost without intermission from the first days of September, and the roads about the assize town of Marston were become so many channels of liquid clay, in which the wheels of the farmers' waggons sank deep, and the foot-passenger might count himself fortunate if he reached his journey's end with whole limbs and a dry coat. It was a season to depress the most sanguine spirit; and in the ancient house of Mr. Justice Ellwood, that stood somewhat apart from the town, upon the brow of a hill whose wooded sides sloped downward to the river, the gloom of the elements seemed but the reflection of a deeper and more settled darkness within.

Judge Ellwood was a man whom the county had long regarded with that mingled respect and awe which attaches to the unimpeachable administrator of the law. His career had been one of those which appear, to the outward observer, the very flower of honourable success. Sprung from no distinguished family, he had raised himself by his own talents, and still more by his own integrity, to the highest seat upon the western circuit; and it was said of him, by those who knew him best, that no man ever ascended the bench with a purer desire to do justice, or descended from it with a conscience more entirely at ease. He was now past the middle term of life; his hair was whitened, his countenance bore the deep lines which thought and responsibility engrave; but his eye was still clear and his step firm, and there were those who declared that the venerable judge had never appeared more dignified than in this last year of his public service, when the approach of his retirement lent a kind of solemn grace to his demeanour.

Yet, within that breast which the world believed so tranquil, there had lately sprung up a canker whose corroding touch no dignity could repel. It was upon a night in the latter part of October, when the wind blew gustily about the old walls, and the rain beat against the casements with a sound like the wailing of a spirit in pain, that the judge sat alone in his library, with a letter open before him, and his hands clasped upon the table, and his eyes fixed upon the embers of a dying fire. The letter was from the chaplain of the county gaol, and it conveyed intelligence which had shaken the strong man to his soul.

One Richard Hallett, a poacher, had been convicted at the assizes of Marston, some seven years before, of the murder of a gamekeeper in the neighbouring parish of Ashby, and had been executed upon the gallows in the presence of a great concourse of the people. The evidence against him had been circumstantial, but it had appeared conclusive. He had been seen near the spot; he had been heard to utter threats against the deceased; and a struggle had been traced in the trampled grass, and a knife, which was proved to be his, had been found in the ditch hard by. The man had protested his innocence to the last; but such protests are the commonplaces of guilt, and the judge had summed up with that grave severity which he ever deemed it his duty to display toward the crime of murder, and the jury had brought in their verdict without leaving the box. The sentence had been pronounced; the law had been satisfied; and the name of Richard Hallett had been suffered to pass into the oblivion which awaits the poor and the friendless.

But now, upon his deathbed, one Abel Grimes, a labourer of Ashby, had made a confession, sworn before the chaplain and two witnesses, that it was he, and not Hallett, who had struck the fatal blow. The quarrel had been his; the knife, which he had snatched from the hand of the doomed man in the struggle, had been the instrument of death; and Hallett, who had fled from the spot in terror, had been innocent of the crime for which he had suffered. Grimes declared that he had been goaded to the confession by a dream, in which the dead man had appeared to him night after night, pointing at him with a bloodless finger, and crying out for vengeance; and he had died, the chaplain wrote, with the name of Hallett upon his lips, praying that the truth might be made known, and that the stain might be removed from the memory of the guiltless.

The judge read the letter again and again, and as he read, the perspiration started from his brow. Seven years ago! Seven years of honour, of usefulness, of conscious rectitude—and now, in a moment, the whole fabric of his life was shaken to its foundations. He had sent an innocent man to the gallows. The thought was madness. He strove to reason with himself; he strove to persuade himself that the confession of a dying felon was not to be trusted; that Grimes might have been delirious; that the evidence, though circumstantial, had been such as to satisfy a conscientious jury; that no human tribunal could be infallible; that the blame, if blame there were, lay not with the judge, who had but administered the law according to the light afforded him. But the sophistry was vain. He knew—he felt in the inmost recesses of his soul—that he had been the instrument of a judicial murder; and the cry of the innocent blood cried unto him from the ground.

And then there arose before him the terrible alternative which now demanded his decision. Should he make the truth public? Should he proclaim from the bench the awful error into which he had fallen, and thus, while doing tardy justice to the memory of the dead, bring down upon his own head the ruin which such a disclosure must inevitably entail? For he knew the world too well to doubt what would be the consequence. The fame which he had built up by the labours of a lifetime would crumble in an hour. The respect of his fellow-citizens would be turned to scorn; the confidence of the suitors would be withdrawn; the very name of Ellwood would become a byword and a reproach. He would go down to his grave a disgraced and broken man, and the good which he had done would be forgotten in the evil which, in a moment of human fallibility, he had unwittingly wrought.

On the other hand, should he conceal the confession? Should he suffer the truth to remain buried in the breast of the chaplain, who was pledged to secrecy, and who, indeed, had written to him as to the proper guardian of the secret? Should he suffer the memory of Richard Hallett to remain under the stigma of a crime which he had never committed, and the real criminal to sleep in his grave unpunished and undetected? His soul revolted from the thought. He was a man of stern and unbending principle, who had ever held the truth in honour, and who had taught himself to look upon the slightest deviation from it as a baseness unworthy of a gentleman and a Christian. Yet the struggle was terrible. The love of reputation, the fear of shame, the dread of poverty and contempt—these are powerful adversaries to the best resolves of our nature; and the judge, as he sat alone in the silence of that stormy night, felt that he was fighting the battle of his life.

The storm without increased in violence. The wind howled among the chimneys, and the rain dashed against the glass, and the old house rocked to its foundations. The judge rose from his chair and paced the room with hurried steps. The fire had burned low, and the shadows lay thick upon the walls, and the portrait of his father, which hung above the chimney-piece, seemed to look down upon him with a stern and sorrowful gaze. He thought of his youth, of the high hopes with which he had entered upon his profession, of the vows which he had then registered in his secret heart, that he would be ever the faithful minister of justice, and never the servile instrument of power or of prejudice. He thought of his wife, who had been the companion of his earlier struggles, and who had died in the midst of his prosperity, and whose last words had been a prayer that he might be kept faithful unto the end. He thought of his children, and of the name which he should leave behind him; and the tears started to his eyes, and he bowed his head upon his hands and wept.

But even in the midst of his agony, a light broke in upon his soul. It was not the light of reason, but of that higher and diviner principle which had guided him through life, and which now, in the hour of his extremest need, refused to desert him. He remembered that he had ever taught himself to regard the approbation of his own conscience as of more value than the applause of the world; that he had ever held that the man who sacrifices truth to expediency is a traitor to his own soul, and unworthy of the trust which his fellow-men repose in him. And he remembered, too, that the law which he had administered was not his law, but the law of God and of his country; and that if he had erred in its administration, it became him to make the amplest reparation in his power, and to bear the consequences with a fortitude worthy of the cause in which he suffered.

He raised his head, and his countenance was calm. The struggle was over. He went to the window, and looked out upon the darkness. The rain had ceased, and the clouds were breaking, and a few stars were shining through the rift. He thought of the poor man who had died upon the gallows, protesting his innocence to the last, and he prayed that the mercy which he had denied to him on earth might be extended to him in heaven. And then he turned to his desk, and with a firm hand he wrote to the chaplain, desiring that the confession might be laid before the proper authorities, and that he himself might be permitted to make the first public avowal of the error into which he had fallen.

The morning dawned, and the sun rose bright and clear upon the rain-washed fields and the glittering roofs of Marston. The judge descended from his chamber, and his face was pale, but his step was firm. He said nothing to his family of the resolution which he had formed; but when the hour of the assizes came, he took his seat upon the bench, and with a voice that faltered not, he made known to the assembled court the dreadful truth. He told them of the confession of Abel Grimes; he told them of the innocence of Richard Hallett; and he prayed that the memory of the dead might be cleared, and that he himself might be permitted to retire from a station which he had found himself unworthy to fill. A deep silence fell upon the court as he concluded; and then a murmur of sympathy and admiration ran through the crowd, and the tears of the auditors bore witness to the respect which his noble confession had inspired.

The judge retired from the bench, and the world did not forget him. His name was handed down to posterity as that of a man who had loved truth better than fame, and who had not feared to sacrifice his reputation upon the altar of justice. And in the annals of the western circuit, the story of the Honourable Edward Ellwood is still told, as an example of that rare and sublime integrity which prefers the approbation of conscience to the applause of men.
