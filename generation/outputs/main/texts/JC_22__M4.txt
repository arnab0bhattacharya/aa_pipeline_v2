The light in that high valley had the quality of a thing remembered rather than seen, a pale, inexorable diffusion that seemed to issue from the snowfields themselves and to find its way into the meanest room, there to lie upon the rough-hewn wood and the worn flagstones with a sort of reproachful patience. It was in such a light that Vassily Petrovitch Orlov first came to himself, and for a long time he lay still, content to be mystified, watching the slow passage of a shadow—a bird’s, perhaps, or a drifting cloud’s—across the plastered ceiling, and listening to a sound which he took at first to be the sea, so vast and rhythmic was its murmur, but which he came to understand, by degrees, was the voice of a torrent, the very blood and pulse of the mountain, hurrying down its stony channel with an indifference older than any human hope.

He had been told, in the close and conspiratorial rooms of a certain city, that the journey would be simple, the arrangements faultless. And so they had been, until his own body had betrayed him. A fever, born of the miasmas of a crowded third-class carriage and nourished by a diet of black bread and dubious sausage, had taken hold of him with a grip more tenacious than any police agent’s. He remembered a confusion of faces, the jolting of a diligence, the smell of wet wool and pine-wood smoke, and then a great darkness, from which he had emerged into this room of whitewashed walls and a crucifix, to find himself as weak as a child and in the hands of strangers.

These strangers were the family Ganz. The father, a man named Ulrich, was a peasant of the old school, slow of speech and deliberate of movement, with a face weathered to the colour and texture of a walnut shell, and eyes that held the placid, unthinking depths of a mountain tarn. It was he who had found the traveller, insensible by the roadside, and had carried him, with the help of his son, a lad of perhaps sixteen, to their chalet. The mother, Anna, was a creature of a different, quicker temper, all bustle and anxiety, her hands never still, her tongue clacking a ceaseless, comforting patter of endearments and scoldings over her patient, her pots, her chickens, and the eternal, infernal weather. There was a daughter, too, a girl of nineteen or twenty, named Leni, who moved through the sick-room with a quiet, downcast efficiency, her presence a silent, luminous fact that Orlov found himself awaiting with an impatience that had nothing to do with the state of his health.

It was she who brought him his first bowl of broth, a thin, golden liquid that tasted of the earth and of kindness. He had tried, in his weak, formal French, to thank her, to explain that he was a traveller, a student, a man of no consequence, and that he must trouble them no further. She had listened, her head slightly inclined, the long, pale-gold braid of her hair falling over one shoulder, and had answered only, “You must not speak. You must drink,” in a voice so low and matter-of-fact that it seemed to dismiss the entire fabric of his invented identity as a thing of no moment whatever.

And this, he discovered, was the true nature of his ordeal. He had been prepared for interrogation, for suspicion, for the sullen hostility of the peasant towards the stranger. He had been prepared, even, for the crude, noisy patriotism of the innkeeper who boasts of his country’s army. He had not been prepared for the Ganz family. Their decency was not a thing of words or gestures; it was an atmosphere, an emanation from their very being, as natural and as unconsidered as the scent of the pine-woods that came in at the open window. Ulrich would sit by his bed in the long evenings, carving a wooden spoon or mending a harness, and would speak of the cattle, of the price of cheese, of the great avalanche of ‘ninety-eight, in a slow, rumbling monotone, as if he were reciting a litany. He asked no questions. He seemed to have no curiosity. The world beyond the valley was, for him, a vague and somewhat disreputable rumour, a place of wars and wickedness and men who did not know the true value of a good cow.

It was this very absence of curiosity that Orlov found most disturbing. In his own world, every man was an agent, every word a move in a vast, invisible game, every silence a confession. He had lived for years in an atmosphere of such intense and universal suspicion that it had become the very air he breathed. He had learned to read the smallest inflection of a voice, the briefest flicker of an eyelid, as a soldier reads a map. And now, in this high, clear, terrible silence, his instruments were useless. The Ganz family looked upon him and saw only a sick man. They did not see the emissary of a committee, the bearer of a mandate, the instrument of a justice that was as remorseless as the torrent outside. They saw a fellow-creature in need, and they ministered to him with a simple, unquestioning directness that was more disconcerting than any cunning.

One afternoon, when he was strong enough to sit by the window, Leni came to him with a cup of milk and a piece of black bread. The sun was setting, and the great peaks were a pageant of rose and gold and amethyst, a spectacle of such cold, inhuman splendour that it seemed to mock the petty concerns of men. She stood beside him, looking out, and for a long time neither spoke.

“You are looking at the Weisshorn,” she said at last, in her quiet way. “It is very beautiful, but it is a killer. My uncle died there, in the spring, when the snow is treacherous.”

He turned to her, and the words he had been preparing for days, the careful, ironic phrases about the beauties of nature and the indifference of the universe, died on his lips. He saw, in the clear, grave eyes that met his, a profound and untroubled acceptance of the fact. Her uncle had died. The mountain was beautiful. These two truths existed side by side, without contradiction, without the need for any philosophical reconciliation. It was a simplicity of vision that he, who had spent his life in the analysis of motives and the dissection of ideals, found suddenly and utterly incomprehensible.

“Why do you do this?” he asked, abruptly, the question torn from him against his will. “Why do you take a stranger into your house, a man you know nothing of? He might be a criminal. He might be… anything.”

She looked at him for a moment with a faint, puzzled frown, as if he had asked her why the sun rose in the east.

“You were ill,” she said, simply. “What else could we do?”

The words fell into the silence of the room and lay there, heavy and irrevocable. What else could we do? He turned his face to the window again, but he no longer saw the mountains. He saw, instead, the face of the minister he had come to kill, a fleshy, complacent face, the face of a man who had signed a certain decree and had then gone to his dinner with a good appetite. He saw the faces of the men in the committee, with their cold, doctrinal eyes and their talk of the masses and the inevitable course of history. He saw all the great, grinding machinery of cause and effect, of oppression and retribution, in which he had believed himself a necessary and conscious cog. And he saw this girl, standing beside him, with her cup of milk, asking, What else could we do?

He felt a profound and nameless shame. It was not the shame of the assassin, caught in a moment of weakness. It was the shame of a man who has spent his life in a dark room, arguing passionately about the nature of light, who is suddenly led out into the sun. The arguments, the theories, the grand and terrible abstractions, all shrivelled and died in that clear, high air. The simple decency of these people was not a counter-argument; it was a different order of reality altogether. It was not a weapon to be used in the struggle; it was the thing itself, the ground on which all struggles were a mere trampling of the grass.

He recovered slowly, and the day came when he announced his departure. Ulrich hitched the horses to the cart to take him down to the valley. Anna packed him a parcel of bread and cheese and pressed a small, carved wooden cross into his hand. Leni stood apart, saying nothing. As the cart began to move, he turned and looked back at the chalet, at the three figures standing in the yard, the man, the woman, and the girl, small and solid against the vast, indifferent backdrop of the mountains. They were not waving. They were simply watching him go, as they would watch a cloud pass, or a season change.

He carried the memory of them down into the world of men, into the cities of smoke and intrigue, into the long, dark corridors of his purpose. He did not abandon his mission. But something had been irrevocably altered. In the sleepless nights that followed, he would see not the face of his intended victim, but the clear, puzzled eyes of the girl, and hear her voice, quiet and final, asking him the only question that mattered: What else could we do? And he knew, with a certainty that was like a cold stone in his breast, that he had no answer. The torrent of history rushed on, and he was a part of it, but he could no longer hear its voice as the voice of truth. It was only a noise, a great and terrible noise, and in the silence of the high valley, he had learned to listen to something else.
