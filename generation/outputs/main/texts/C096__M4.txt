The fog had come down thick as a wool blanket over Camden Town, swallowing the gas lamps whole and leaving only their fuzzy halos to guide the way. Inside the narrow house on Wren Street, Mrs. Ada Pemberton was wrestling with a mountain of damp linen. Her lodger, a Mr. Silas Thorne, stood in the doorway of the scullery, his thin frame silhouetted against the light from the hall.

“Mrs. Pemberton,” he said, his voice a dry rustle, like pages turning. “I could not help but notice your distress.”

Ada straightened her back with a wince. The washboard had been her adversary for the better part of an hour, and the pile of wet sheets seemed to mock her. “It’s this weather, Mr. Thorne. Nothing dries. And with the morrow being Monday, I’ve the week’s linens for the whole house to see to. I shall be up till cockcrow.”

Thorne stepped into the room. He was a peculiar man, always dressed in the same grey suit, his hands perpetually stained with graphite, his eyes holding a distant, calculating light. He was a physicist, he’d told her upon taking the rooms, engaged in ‘private researches of a temporal nature.’ Ada had assumed it was a polite fiction for a man of independent means with a fondness for solitude.

“I believe I can be of assistance,” he said. He gestured towards the small, windowless box-room off the scullery, a place she used for storing coal and old preserves. “If you would be so good as to bring your washing in here.”

Ada frowned. “In the coal hole? Mr. Thorne, I hardly think—”

“Please,” he insisted, a rare earnestness in his tone. “It is an experiment. One that will cost you but a minute of your time.”

Curiosity, and the deep ache in her lower back, won out. She gathered the heavy, sodden bundle in her arms and followed him into the cramped little room. It was empty, save for a strange, faintly humming apparatus of copper coils and glass valves in one corner, connected to a series of large, wet-cell batteries. The air felt strange, thick and silent, as if the very dust motes had paused in their dance.

“Set them down,” Thorne instructed, pointing to a clear patch of floor. “Now, I must ask you to step out and close the door. I will signal when it is done.”

“Done?” Ada asked, bewildered. “What is to be done?”

“The washing,” he said, with a thin smile. “You will see.”

She retreated to the scullery, feeling foolish. She heard the door click shut, followed by a low, resonant hum from within that seemed to vibrate in her very teeth. It lasted for perhaps ten seconds, then ceased. A moment later, the door opened.

“There,” Thorne said, stepping aside. “Your washing, Mrs. Pemberton.”

She peered past him, expecting to see the pile of wet linen exactly where she’d left it. Instead, the floor was empty. In its place, neatly folded into a perfect stack, was her washing. Every sheet, every pillowcase, every towel was immaculate, crisp, and bone-dry. They smelled faintly of ozone and the clean, sharp scent of a winter morning. She reached out and touched a pillowcase. It was warm, as if it had been dried in a strong sun, not a foggy London cellar.

“How…?” she breathed, her eyes wide.

“A localized temporal field,” Thorne explained, adjusting a valve on his machine. “I have learned to slow the passage of time within this small space. What took you an hour of drudgery, I subjected to the equivalent of a full day of dry, breezy weather, all in the span of ten seconds. A minute for you, perhaps, but a day for the linen.”

Ada was a practical woman. She did not understand the science, nor did she wish to. But she understood a miracle when she saw one. From that day, a new arrangement was struck. Once a week, Ada would bring her washing to the box-room, and in a minute, it would be returned to her, perfectly dry and folded. Mr. Thorne asked for no payment, only her silence on the matter. She was happy to oblige.

At first, she was simply grateful. But soon, gratitude curdled into a gnawing curiosity. What else, she wondered, did Mr. Thorne do with his minute-long day? He never left the house. His meals were taken in his room, left on a tray outside his door. She heard the low hum of his machine at all hours, and sometimes, a sound that was not the machine at all. It was a faint, rhythmic tapping, like a fingernail against a glass, coming from the box-room. And once, she heard a voice. Not Thorne’s dry rustle, but a woman’s voice, raised in what sounded like a desperate, muffled sob.

Her suspicions grew. She began to linger by the door, to invent excuses to pass through the scullery. One afternoon, while Thorne was out—a rare occurrence, to collect a delivery of chemicals—she found the door to the box-room unlocked. Her heart hammering against her ribs, she pushed it open.

The machine hummed with a low, menacing power. The air was cold, unnaturally so, and the silence was absolute. On the floor, where her washing usually lay, was a small, framed photograph. It showed a young woman with Thorne, her smile bright, her hand resting on his arm. Ada picked it up. The glass was cold as ice.

A shadow fell over her. Thorne stood in the doorway, his face a mask of cold fury.

“That is not for you,” he said, his voice dangerously low. He snatched the photograph from her hand.

“Who is she?” Ada demanded, her own fear giving way to indignation. “What are you doing in here, Mr. Thorne? What is that tapping? That crying?”

He looked at her for a long moment, his eyes seeming to see not her, but something far beyond. “Her name was Eliza,” he said, his voice softening. “My wife. She died of a fever three winters ago. I was… too slow. I could not save her.”

He gestured to the machine. “This is not merely a dryer of linen, Mrs. Pemberton. It is a crucible. I am not slowing time to preserve it. I am slowing it to reverse it. Within that field, a minute for me is a day for the world outside. But for what is inside… I am trying to turn back the clock. To find the precise moment the fever took her, and to halt it.”

Ada stared, horrified. “But the voice… the crying…”

“Echoes,” he whispered, his eyes haunted. “Echoes of moments I have captured. The last time she spoke to me. The last time she wept. I am trying to piece them back together. To find the right sequence. To bring her back.”

He looked at the neat stack of her washing, which she had come to collect. “Your linens are an… elegant side effect. A test of the field’s stability. They dry because the water evaporates. But Eliza… she is not water. She is memory and matter. And I fear I am losing her, one minute at a time.”

Ada backed out of the room, her mind reeling. She took her washing, her hands trembling, and never again did she bring her linens to the box-room. The low hum from the little room continued, a constant, mournful presence in the house. She no longer saw it as a marvel, but as a tomb. And she began to suspect that the tapping she heard at night was not an echo of the past, but a plea from the present, from a woman trapped in a minute that had lasted for years, begging for someone, anyone, to let time begin again.
