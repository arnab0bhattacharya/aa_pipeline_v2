The water in the Serpentine did not so much reflect the sky as it held it, suspended in a grey, trembling film that shimmered with the faintest hint of green. It was that particular hour, just before ten, when the light in St James’s Park was neither fully day nor fully twilight, but a liminal, watery state where things seemed to lose their edges. Mrs. Elspeth Tupper sat on the wooden bench, her back rigid against the slats, her hands resting on her lap, folded over one another with a precision that spoke of a discipline long since eroded by grief. She held the crumbs of a stale bun, broken not by force but by a gentle, persistent pressure, releasing them into the air one by one. They drifted down like snow that had forgotten how to melt, landing on the surface of the lake with a soundless sigh.

Below, the ducks gathered. They were not the ducks of the postcard, the pristine and orderly flocks of illustration; they were a chaotic, greying mass of feathers, splashing and quacking in a cacophony that sounded, to Elspeth, less like birds and more like the distant, muffled roar of a city waking up from a nightmare. She watched them, not with joy, but with the dull, mechanical attention of a woman who had performed this ritual for three years. It was the hour Julian had walked here. In 1914, Julian had walked here, his young face bright with the future, his eyes fixed on the horizon as if he could see the end of the street, the end of the war, the end of everything, all at once. Now, the future was a closed door, and the past was a room with the lights turned off, into which one had to feel one’s way, step by step, bumping into the furniture of memory.

She dropped another crumb. A mallard, dark-headed and iridescent, darted forward, beating the water into a spray that caught the weak sun. Elspeth’s heart gave a small, involuntary lurch. It was not the duck, but the motion, the sudden violence of life asserting itself against the stillness of the water. She thought of Julian’s hands, how they had moved when he played the piano, how they had reached for her, how they had been lost. The water rippled and settled. The ripples were the only thing that changed. The world remained, indifferent and vast, turning on its axis while she sat still, anchored to this bench by the weight of a name she could no longer say aloud.

Then, the shadow fell across her feet.

It was not the sun, for the sun was low and to the left. It was a boy. He sat on the bench next to hers, not close enough to touch, not far enough to be ignored. He was perhaps sixteen, dressed in a jacket that had seen better days, with sleeves too long for his arms, the cuffs frayed. He held a book, but he did not read it. He sat with his knees drawn up, his elbows resting on them, his head bowed. He looked like a statue of a boy, carved from the same grey stone as the London fog.

Elspeth did not turn her head. She knew, with the certainty of a woman who has spent years in silence, that to turn would be to invite intrusion. To turn was to acknowledge that he was there, that he was a part of this moment, and she did not want him to be. She wanted the morning to be hers, to be Julian’s, to be the empty, sacred space between the living and the dead. If she looked at him, he would become real, and if he became real, he would have to speak, and if he spoke, the words would break the spell, and the spell was all that held the pieces of her life together.

But the silence was not empty. It was full of the boy’s breathing, a soft, rhythmic sound that cut through the noise of the ducks. *In, out. In, out.* It was a human sound, a sound of life, of metabolism, of the body continuing its work regardless of the soul’s distress. Elspeth felt a strange, cold curiosity prick at the base of her spine. Who was he? Did he know of the war? Did he know that the air he breathed had once tasted of cordite and blood? Or was he simply a boy, sitting in a park, waiting for something, waiting for anyone, waiting for the world to give him a sign?

She wanted to speak. The impulse was sudden, violent, a bird trapped in a cage of ribs. *Speak*, it whispered. *Ask him what he is waiting for. Ask him if he is afraid. Ask him if he knows what it is to lose everything.* But the words died on her tongue, choked by the fear that if she opened her mouth, the grief would pour out, uncontrolled, a black flood that would drown them both. She was a widow; she was a ghost. Ghosts do not talk to boys. Ghosts watch. Ghosts endure.

The boy shifted. The book slipped from his lap and fell with a soft thud onto the pavement. He did not pick it up. He watched it lie there, open to a page that Elspeth could not read. The wind, which had been still, stirred, lifting the corner of the page, flapping it like a wing. The boy’s hand reached out, not to retrieve the book, but to touch the air above it, as if he were trying to catch the wind itself.

Elspeth’s eyes, without her permission, drifted toward him. She saw the line of his jaw, the way his hair was tousled, the absolute stillness of his face. He looked like Julian. Not in his features, for Julian had been sharp-featured, intense, but in the posture of his waiting. There was a quality of suspended animation in the boy, a holding of breath, as if he were waiting for a cue, for a permission to exist. And in that recognition, a thread of connection, thin as spider-silk, stretched between them.

She wanted to reach out. She wanted to touch his shoulder, to say, *I know. I know what it is to wait for a sound that never comes.* But she remained still. Her hands tightened on the bun, the crumbs crumbling further, falling not into the water, but onto her lap, a small, grey avalanche. The boy did not look up. He did not see her. He was in his own world, a world of shadow and silence, a world that was, in its own way, just as vast and terrifying as hers.

The sun moved, a fraction of a degree, and the light shifted, illuminating the boy’s face with a pale, ghostly glow. For a moment, he was not a boy, but a spirit, a memory, a fragment of the past that had wandered into the present. Elspeth felt a pang, sharp and sudden, like a needle in the heart. It was not pity, exactly, but a recognition of kinship. They were both waiting. They were both lost. They were both sitting on the edge of the world, watching the water, watching the ducks, watching the time pass, knowing that it would never stop, knowing that it would never return.

She dropped the last of the crumbs. They fell into the water, and the ripples spread out, touching the boy’s reflection, distorting it, making it waver and break. The boy picked up the book. He did not look at her. He opened the cover, and began to read. The sound of the page turning was the only sound in the world. Elspeth closed her eyes. She did not move. She did not speak. She simply sat, in the grey light, in the silence, in the presence of the boy, and in the absence of her son, and felt the weight of the morning settle upon her, heavy and still, like the water in the Serpentine.
