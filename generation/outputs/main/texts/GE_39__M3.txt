In the low-lying Lincolnshire town of Biresby, where the river Trent coils like a sluggish brown serpent through the flat, rich pastures, the air in the year eighteen hundred and thirty-five held a heavy mixture of agricultural damp and municipal ambition. It was a town where the grazing of kine and the gossip of the market-cross contended with the newer instincts of commerce, and where the old timbered houses, with their overhanging upper stories, were gradually yielding to the prim brickwork of provincial respectability. Among the inhabitants of this shifting district, none stood upon a more confident, if somewhat unexamined, social elevation than the Fentons. Mr. Gerald Fenton, a substantial land-surveyor and valuer, had built a commodious house upon the Caistor road, looking out upon a neat garden where gooseberry bushes grew with that severe trimness which implies a decent disregard for mere wild nature. His family had long been established, and his late wife’s connections had branched into the chief dairy-farms of the neighbourhood, giving them a root in the soil such as no recent professional man could boast. 

But the autumn of that year brought a shadow to the Fenton household, for Mrs. Fenton lay in the large front chamber, stricken by a slow, uncertain fever that baffled the conjectures of Mr. Phipps, the elderly surgeon who had brought half the parish into the world. It was in this atmosphere of suspended dread that Letitia Fenton, the elder daughter, found her days narrowed to the monotonous exactions of the sick-room. Letitia, or Letty, as she was called by those who had known her romping in the orchard, was a young woman of three-and-twenty, possessing a pale, clear complexion and dark hazel eyes, which had hitherto looked out upon the world with a placid expectation of its goodness—a disposition much encouraged by the general approval that had accompanied her quiet, uneventful girlhood. She had been courted, in that slow, unimpassioned fashion which passes for courtship in rural districts, for nearly two years by Mr. Jasper Cole, a neighbouring miller and maltster of considerable means. Jasper was a man of thirty, broad-shouldered, florid, and possessed of a practical, untroubled mind; he spoke of his prospects with the heavy satisfaction of a man who feels his own weight in the scale of worldly substance, and the Biresby world, including Mr. Fenton himself, considered the match as good as settled. For who, in a society where the chief aim was to keep the stocking full and the fireside warm, could dispute the fitness of a young woman exchanging the anxieties of her father’s declining years for the solid comforts of a prosperous mill?

Yet the human soul, even in its most provincial retreat, is not always governed by the arithmetic of the market-cross. Daily, almost hourly, in the shaded room where Mrs. Fenton’s laboured breathing marked the slow ebb of vitality, appeared Mr. Phipps’s assistant, Mr. Edgar Leyburn. He was a young man of five-and-twenty, the son of a ruined dissenting minister from the West Riding, who had come into the medical profession not through the broad gateway of inheritance, but by the steep, stony path of necessity. He was poor—offensively poor, Mr. Fenton thought, in a young man who handled the delicate vessels of life; his coat was worn at the seams, and his boots bore the permanent stain of the Biresby mud. But Leyburn had a head of singular alertness, and a clear, grey eye that seemed to pierce beyond the mere surface of things into the hidden mechanism of cause and effect. His speech, too, was marked by a precision that was almost foreign to the broad, drawling vowels of the Trent-side; he had read not only his anatomy, but his philosophy, and there was a certain restrained ardour in his voice when he spoke of the microscopic structure of the tissues, or the laws that governed the circulation, which made a strange contrast to the heavy, unhurried certainties of Mr. Phipps.

To Letty, whose mind had been fed chiefly on the poetry of the annuals and the mild moralizing of the village circulating library, this daily intrusion of eager, half-articulate science was like the opening of a window upon a wider horizon. She did not understand all that Leyburn said; indeed, she was often frightened by the swift, decisive movements with which he adjusted the bandages, or the intent, almost fierce, scrutiny with which he watched the changes in her mother’s face. But she felt the weight of his sympathy, which was not the soft, tearful pity of her Aunt Greenow, but a strong, silent companionship in suffering, which seemed to demand from her a corresponding courage. And so, while the December fogs hung thick over the river, and the bitterns boomed in the distant marshes, there grew up between these two young creatures, in the pauses of their ministrations to the dying woman, a subtle, unspoken communication of thought—a glance, a tone, a sudden pause in the reading of some volume of old voyages which Leyburn had brought to while away the midnight watches. They were not conscious of any peril in this; they were but two solitary souls, drawn together by the common pressure of sorrow, and the slow, stealthy convergence of human lots is often hidden from the actors themselves by the very simplicity of their immediate purpose.

But Destiny, which weaves its ironies out of the very threads of our indifference, was not asleep. One afternoon, when the frost had crisped the puddles in the Biresby lanes into plates of ruffled glass, Jasper Cole rode up to the Fenton gate on his heavy brown cob. He entered the sick-room with the heavy, considerate tread of a man who feels his own bodily soundness to be a sort of social offence in the presence of disease, and stood by the bedside, his broad face creased into an expression of awkward benevolence. 

“Aye, Miss Fent’n,” he said, in the deep, broad dialect that was the natural speech of the neighbourhood, “it’s a poor do for the old lady, an’ no mistake. My mother says as how she’ll send up a nice dish o’ chicken-broth; she allays says there’s nothin’ like chicken-broth for a faver. But the doctor’s a rare good ’un, I’ll be bound. You munna fash yourself, Miss Letty; you’re looking main peaked. There’s a deal o’ trouble in a house, to be sure.”

Letty murmured her thanks, feeling a sudden, unaccountable shrinking from the hearty, wholesome voice, which seemed to belong to a world so remote from the hushed, agonizing tension of the sick-room. And as Jasper turned to go, he laid a heavy, moist hand upon hers, with a kindly pressure that was meant to be infinitely reassuring, but which struck upon her senses with a dull, oppressive weight. It was the touch of the man whom Biresby had decided she must marry; the touch of the man who would carry her away, when this was over, to the great mill by the bridge, where the water-wheel turned with a ceaseless, deafening roar, and the air was thick with the white, mealy dust of grinding corn.

When he had gone, she stood for a long time looking out of the window upon the bare, black branches of the elm-tree, and the slow, sullen flow of the river beyond. The future, which had once seemed so smooth and uneventful, now stretched before her like a tangled, pathless wood. She knew that her mother’s recovery was doubtful; she knew that the coming spring would bring the crisis of her own fate. If Mrs. Fenton died, there would be mourning, and the question of marriage might be deferred; but if she recovered, Mr. Fenton, impatient of the expense of a prolonged illness, would press for an early settlement. And in either case, there was Leyburn—poor, ambitious Leyburn, with his worn coat and his flashing eyes, who had awakened in her a new, strange sense of her own existence, and who would, when the sickness was over, pass out of her life as suddenly as he had entered it. 

For she was not deceived as to the practical issue. She knew, with the clear, unflinching knowledge of a girl who has been bred in the wholesome atmosphere of provincial economy, that love without bread is a dangerous, disreputable affair. She had seen the fate of the baker’s daughter who had married the bankrupt artist, and the cold charity with which Biresby regarded a household where the teapot was kept locked. And yet, as she turned from the window and met Leyburn’s eyes, which were fixed upon her with a sudden, questioning intensity, she felt a rebellion rise within her—a vague, passionate protest against the tyranny of the stocking and the mill. It was only a momentary flash; the next instant she was bending over her mother, adjusting the pillow with the gentle, skilful touch of a practised nurse. But the seed had been sown, and the slow preparation of effects had begun.
