The invitation had come in the form of a note so flattering in its expressions that Miss Aurelia Thorne had been compelled to read it twice before she could determine whether the pleasure it gave her was of a legitimate kind, or whether it belonged to that order of gratifications which, like certain sweetmeats, are found upon examination to be compounded chiefly of air. Mr. Lionel Kettleby, whose critical essays in the *Westminster Review* were much admired for their combination of learning and vivacity, had been pleased to speak of her novel—published under the name of “Eustace Grey”—in terms that might have turned a steadier head than hers; and now he desired the honour of the author’s company at dinner, to meet a few friends whose names he mentioned with that air of conferring distinction which is peculiar to men who live much in the society of the distinguished. The difficulty was that “Eustace Grey” had no existence apart from herself; and Aurelia, who had chosen the masculine pseudonym not from any coquetry of concealment, but from a sober conviction that a woman’s book would be read with a prejudice she could not afford, now found herself entangled in the very web her caution had woven. To refuse the invitation would be to excite a curiosity that might end in discovery; to accept it as herself would be to confess the imposture. After a night of that anxious deliberation which persons of lively imagination mistake for thought, she resolved to attend as the author’s sister—a fiction so near the truth that it seemed to her almost a species of honesty—and to say that her brother was abroad, but that he had desired her to convey his thanks in person.

The house in Bloomsbury was of that solid, unpretending order which suggests a respectability founded on income rather than on ancestry; and the dinner, when it came, was such as a man of letters who values his digestion and his reputation for taste might be expected to provide. There were eight at table, and Aurelia, placed at her host’s right hand, had the satisfaction of finding that her assumed character of a provincial sister, diffident and observant, was accepted without question. She was a woman of that neutral exterior which is the best possible mask for a vivid inward life; her features were regular without being striking, her dress was correct without being fashionable, and her manner had the quietness of one who has learned to listen. She had the pleasure, if pleasure it could be called, of hearing her own book discussed with that freedom which men of letters reserve for the absent author; and though she had armed herself against criticism, she found that the severest strictures of the reviewers had not prepared her for the easy condescension of a dinner-table.

“I confess,” said Mr. Kettleby, when the soup had been removed and the conversation had taken that turn toward generalities which is the delight of the well-fed, “I confess I was taken in by the fellow—positively taken in. There is a breadth in the thing, a certain masculine grip of the subject, that I should not have expected. One would say he had been at school with the passions and had learned their Latin grammar.” He smiled, and applied himself to his fish with the air of a man who has said a good thing and knows that it will be appreciated.

“And yet,” said a young man with a pale forehead and a neckcloth of excessive whiteness, who had been introduced as Mr. Vivian Darke and was understood to be a poet, “and yet, do you not find something—I speak with submission—something a little strained in the pathos? It is the pathos of a man who has observed suffering rather than felt it. The tears are genuine, but they are shed at a distance.”

“That is very fine, Darke,” said the host, “very fine and very false. The man who feels his subject most is often the least able to shed tears over it. The surgeon does not weep at the operation. It is the bystander who faints.” He helped himself to wine with the complacency of one who has settled a question.

Aurelia, who had felt her heart beat faster at the poet’s criticism—for it had touched a truth she had half suspected—now experienced a curious sensation of relief. They were discussing “Eustace Grey” as they might have discussed a departed author, with that mixture of generosity and superiority which the living always accord to the dead. She was about to offer some remark, guarded and modest, when the conversation took a turn that made her pause.

“The remarkable thing,” said Mr. Kettleby, leaning back in his chair and addressing the table generally, “is that the book should have been written by a man at all. I mean no disrespect to the sex—Heaven forbid!—but one observes that when a woman takes up the pen, she is apt to take up the needle at the same time. She embroiders. She cannot help it. The domestic instinct is too strong for her. She gives us the little stitches of feeling, but not the large seams of life.” He paused, and looked round with the air of a man who has delivered a judgment.

“You are severe, Kettleby,” said a clergyman who sat opposite, and who had the comfortable appearance of one who has never been called upon to be severe himself. “I have known ladies whose letters are superior to many a sermon.”

“Letters—yes, letters. A woman’s letters are her natural province. She can be natural in them, because she is not writing for the public. But a novel—a novel is a public act, and the woman who writes one is in the position of a lady who should mount a platform. She loses the charm of her sex without acquiring the authority of ours. She is neither one thing nor the other—a sort of literary hermaphrodite.” Mr. Kettleby smiled again, and this time the smile was directed at Aurelia, as if to invite her concurrence in a pleasantry.

She felt the blood rise to her cheeks, and was conscious of a sudden impulse to declare herself. But the habit of concealment was strong, and the habit of politeness stronger. She said, with a composure that surprised her, “You speak, sir, as if the domestic instincts and the literary were necessarily opposed. I have known women whose minds were large enough to embrace both.”

“Ah, my dear Miss Thorne,” said Mr. Kettleby, with a condescending bow, “you speak as a sister should. It is charming to see such loyalty. But I speak of the general rule—the general rule. There are exceptions, of course; there are always exceptions. But a woman who writes as ‘Eustace Grey’ writes—with that grasp of character, that sense of the world’s hardness—must have unsexed herself in some measure. She must have looked on at life from behind a curtain, as it were, and taken notes. It is not natural. It is not—if I may say so—it is not womanly.”

The word fell on Aurelia’s ear with a peculiar sharpness. She had heard it before—from her father, who had discouraged her studies; from her brother, who had laughed at her ambitions; from the publishers who had returned her manuscripts with polite regrets. She had thought to escape it by taking a man’s name; and here it was, waiting for her at a dinner-table, spoken by the very man who had praised her book. She looked at Mr. Kettleby’s comfortable face, at his well-fed complacency, at the easy superiority of his tone, and she understood, with a sudden clearness, that his praise and his blame were of the same substance. He had praised “Eustace Grey” because he had believed it to be the work of a man; he would have condemned it had he known it to be the work of a woman. The book had not changed; only the name upon the title-page.

She rose from the table with the other ladies, and in the drawing-room, while the tea was being poured, she stood by the window and looked out at the lamplit street. She felt a strange mixture of emotions—a sense of triumph, because she had deceived them all; a sense of humiliation, because the deception had been necessary; and a sense of something like sorrow, because she had learned that the world’s judgment is not a balance but a bias. She thought of her novel, of the years of labour, of the characters she had loved into life, and she asked herself whether the approbation of such men was worth the price she had paid for it. The answer came to her slowly, as answers do to those who have learned to wait: that the work was her own, and that no critic could take it from her; that the name upon the title-page was a mask, but the mind behind it was a reality; and that the day might come when a woman could write without a mask, and be judged by her work and not by her sex. But that day was not yet; and in the meantime, she must go home and write another chapter.
