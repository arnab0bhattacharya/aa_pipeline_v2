The heat had entered the room and lay there, a pale, insistent presence, upon the shutters, the folded linen, the rim of the water-jug; and Helena, who had waked early, lay still and watched it climb the wall, thinking of the tomb—of the cool dark of it, and the little flame of the candle, and the dust that hung in the air like a veil not quite drawn. It was five days now since her father had spoken of going, and each morning the going had receded a little, as the shore recedes when one stands at the stern; but this morning he had said, at breakfast, with his knife poised above the egg, “We sail on Thursday,” and had not looked at her, and she had heard the words settle like stones into water.

She rose and went to the window and opened the shutter a hand’s breadth, and the light came in, and with it the sound of the river, which was not a sound so much as a pressure, a slow, brown, unending going-on, and the creak of a felucca’s yard, and a boy’s voice, thin and far, crying something in Arabic that rose and fell and was gone. Below, in the garden, the bougainvillea had dropped a little; the petals lay on the path, magenta against the white dust, and an old man was sweeping them, slowly, with a broom of palm, and the broom left lines in the dust like the lines the water leaves upon the sand. It seemed to her that everything this morning was busy with its own slow erasure.

At breakfast her father had been kind, had spoken of the cousin—of Edmund, who was to meet them at Brindisi, who had, her father said, a good head for the estate, and rode well, and would not, thank God, be forever grubbing in holes. He had laughed at his own phrase, and she had laughed too, and had felt the laugh go out of her like a breath, and had watched the flies settle on the sugar and rise again, and had thought: Thursday. And then, because she could not sit still, she had walked, and had come, by the long road that runs beside the river, to the place where the men were digging.

The shaft went down into the hillside, and the spoil was heaped about it, pale and crumbling, and the baskets went up and down, and the men’s voices came up out of the ground, hollow and strange, as though they spoke from another century. She stood at the edge and looked down, and it was like looking into the throat of the world. And presently he came up, out of the dark, blinking in the light, his shirt grey with dust, his hair grey with it, so that he seemed to have grown old in the earth and to be returning to her from a great distance; and he had in his hand a little jar, blue, with a line of black about its lip, and he held it out to her without a word, as one might hold out a flower.

“It was in the corner,” he said at last, “under the sand, where the light never came. Think of it—three thousand years, and no one has looked at it. And now it will go to a museum, and be put in a case, and people will pass it and say, ‘How pretty,’ and never know what it was to hold it in the dark.”

“And what was it?” she asked.

“A woman’s paint-jar,” he said. “For the eyes. She had put it down, perhaps, and meant to come back for it. And never came.” He turned it in his fingers. “There is a little of the paint still in it. Green. The colour of the river at evening.”

She looked at the jar, and at his hands, which were brown and cracked and careful, and she thought of the woman who had put it down, and of the three thousand years, and of the river going on and on; and it seemed to her that she stood at the very edge of something vast and quiet, and that if she were to step forward she would step out of her own life as out of a shallow boat into deep water. And she was afraid, and did not wish to step back.

“You will come again tomorrow?” he said. “We are to open the inner chamber. There may be—I cannot tell you what there may be.”

And she said, “I will come,” and knew, even as she said it, that on Thursday they sailed, and that her father’s kindness was a wall, and that she had no words to tell him of the jar, of the green paint, of the dark under the sand. She walked back along the river, and the water was the colour of the paint, and the sun went down, and the hills on the far side turned the colour of a peach, and then of a wound, and then of ashes; and she thought: He will hold the jar in his hand tomorrow, and I shall be packing. The words went round in her head with the slow persistence of the river, and would not be stilled.

That evening her father sat on the terrace with his cigar, and the smoke went up straight and blue into the still air, and he spoke of Edmund, of the house, of the autumn, and she listened, and said yes, and no, and watched a small boat with a lateen sail go down the river, and it seemed to her that she was in the boat, and that the boat was going from her, and that she stood on the bank and could not call out. And when at last she said, “Father, the tomb—they open the inner chamber tomorrow,” he had taken the cigar from his mouth and looked at the ash of it a long time, and had said, “My dear, we have seen quite enough of tombs,” and had put the cigar back, and the smoke had gone up, and the matter was finished.

She lay now in the hot room and heard the broom in the dust, and the boy crying on the river, and the slow, brown, unending going-on; and she thought of the inner chamber, of the dark and the dust and the little flame, and of the jar in his hand; and it seemed to her that all her life she would be standing at the edge of the shaft, looking down, and that the light would be behind her, and the dark before, and that she would never, never know what lay in the inner chamber. And the tears came into her eyes, not for herself, she thought, but for the woman who had put down the jar, and meant to come back, and never came.
