The chandelier in the Salon of the Ceiba had been looted from a merchant’s house in the lowlands, and its crystal drops, which had never been properly cleaned, held the candlelight in a brownish, smoky haze. General Anselmo Rojas, President and Liberator of the Republic of Montemar, stood beneath it with his back to the great open windows, through which the warm night breathed the smell of jasmine and, more faintly, of the distant barracks cook-fires. He was a heavy man in a white uniform with gold at the shoulders, and he held his glass not by the stem but in his fist, like a weapon he had decided not to use.

The foreign merchants had come in their stiff black coats, their collars high against the heat, and now they stood in a loose crescent around their host, smiling with the particular strained courtesy of men who have calculated the value of their warehouses against the cost of a gunboat and found the arithmetic discouraging. There was Mr. Halloran of the Liverpool house, red-faced and moist, who had lived in Montemar for thirty years and remembered four presidents; there was the little Frenchman, M. Berthier, who dealt in indigo and whose hands were never still; and there were the Germans of the Casa Wolfram, two brothers who spoke seldom and looked at everything, the way men look at a horse they are thinking of buying.

“You will find,” the General was saying, in the slow, emphatic manner of a man who has learned that his voice is his best instrument, “that the affairs of this country are now conducted with a regularity that was not always possible. In the past—you know as well as I—there was disorder. There were bandits who called themselves colonels. There were taxes levied three times on the same bale of cotton. That is finished.” He drank, and set the glass down on the marble console with a click that seemed to punctuate the sentence. “Montemar is open for business. Orderly business. That is what I have promised, and that is what I will keep.”

“We have never doubted your Excellency’s intentions,” said Halloran, in his rusty Spanish, and the others murmured agreement like a congregation.

Behind the General, at the sideboard, stood Lieutenant Emilio Cárdenas of the Presidential Guard, nineteen years old, with a bottle of Madeira in his hands. His duty was to pour, to step back, to be invisible. He was very good at it. He had been in the Guard six months, and in that time he had learned to keep his eyes fixed on the middle distance and his ears open, and he had learned that a man who is not looked at is often not remembered, and that a man who is not remembered may hear a great deal.

He moved along the crescent now, filling glasses, and as he bent toward M. Berthier he caught the Frenchman’s eye for a fraction of a second, and saw in it a flicker of something—amusement, or contempt, or fear; it was hard to say. The Frenchman’s hand, he noticed, was trembling, and a drop of wine fell from the lip of the glass onto the white marble floor, where it lay like a small dark coin.

“I have been in the interior,” the General continued, and now his voice had taken on a reflective, almost genial tone, as though he were about to tell a story. “I have been in the interior, gentlemen, and I have seen what the wars left there. Villages with no men in them. Fields gone back to scrub. A church at San Isidro where the roof has fallen in and the rain has been coming down on the altar for ten years.” He shook his head slowly. “That is what disorder costs. And it is the foreigner, very often, who pays the price. Your warehouses in Puerto Claro, Mr. Halloran—how many times were they burned? Twice? Three times? And the indigo in the Custom House at Santa Rosa, M. Berthier, that was requisitioned by Colonel Espinosa’s men in ‘eighty-nine and never seen again. These are the things that will not happen now.”

“We are grateful,” said Berthier carefully, “for your Excellency’s solicitude.”

“Solicitude,” the General repeated, as though tasting the word. “No. Not solicitude. I am not a sentimental man. I am a practical man. A country that cannot protect its merchants is a country that will have no merchants, and a country with no merchants is a country with no money, and a country with no money—” he smiled, and the smile did not reach his eyes—“is a country that cannot pay its soldiers. So you see, gentlemen, it is all one thing. Your interest and mine. Your prosperity and mine.”

Lieutenant Cárdenas poured for the German brothers, who said nothing, and then for Mr. Halloran, who said, “Your Excellency is very clear.”

“I am always clear,” said the General. “It is one of my few virtues.”

The dinner was served in the long gallery that ran along the eastern side of the palace, where the portraits of former presidents hung in a row, most of them dark with age, a few of them with their faces turned to the wall. The General sat at the head of the table, with Halloran on his right and the elder Wolfram on his left, and the lieutenant and two other guards stood at the far end, their backs to the shuttered windows, and watched the servants bring in the soup.

It was during the fish course that the General said, in a voice pitched for the whole table, “You will have heard, perhaps, of the business at Los Riscos.”

Halloran set down his fork. “We have heard something, Excellency. A disturbance.”

“A disturbance,” said the General. “Yes. That is a good word. A disturbance.” He wiped his mouth with his napkin, slowly, deliberately. “A garrison of my army, stationed at Los Riscos, which is a poor place in the hills where nothing grows but rocks and resentment—a garrison of my army rose against me. They had been listening to priests and to lawyers and to men who told them that I was a tyrant and that they were oppressed. They killed their captain. They opened the jail. They sent runners to the villages to raise the people.” He paused, and looked around the table, from face to face, and the lieutenant, at the far end, felt the look pass over him like a hand. “And then they waited. They waited, gentlemen, because they believed that the country would rise with them. They believed that the people were with them. They believed that I was alone.”

He picked up his glass and held it to the light.

“They were mistaken,” he said. “I went to Los Riscos myself. I had with me two hundred men of the Guard and four field guns. The rebels had barricaded the plaza and the church. I gave them one hour to surrender. They did not surrender. So I shelled the church, and then I shelled the plaza, and then I sent in the infantry, and when it was finished there were forty-one men of the garrison still alive, and I had them shot in the morning, in the plaza, against the wall of the church, and I made the villagers come and watch, so that they would understand what a disturbance costs.” He drank. “The priests I hanged. The lawyers I sent to the prison at Puerto Claro, where they are building a new road, and I am told the work agrees with them. And the people—the people who were going to rise with them—the people came and kissed my hand.”

There was a silence. The lieutenant stood with his back to the window and his hands behind him and his eyes on the far wall, and he thought of Los Riscos, because he had been there. He had been there with the Guard, and he had seen the church with its roof fallen in, and the plaza, and the wall, and the forty-one men, and he had seen the villagers, and he had seen the way they looked at the General when they came to kiss his hand. He had been sixteen then, and it was the first time he had seen men shot, and he had not slept well for a month afterward. He did not think about it now. He had learned not to.

“And so,” said the General, setting down his glass, “you will understand, gentlemen, when I say that Montemar is open for business. It is open for business because I have made it so. There is no one in this country who will trouble you, because there is no one in this country who does not know what I am capable of.” He smiled again, and this time the smile was almost warm. “You may rely upon it. You may rely upon me.”

Halloran cleared his throat. “We have always found your Excellency a man of his word.”

“I am a man of my word,” the General agreed. “That is another of my virtues.” He turned to the elder Wolfram. “You are a quiet man, Herr Wolfram. You have not said a word all evening. What do you think of my country?”

The German looked at him for a long moment. “I think, Excellency,” he said, in careful Spanish, “that it is a country with a great future.”

The General laughed, a short, barking laugh, and the table laughed with him, and the lieutenant poured more wine, and the servants brought in the roast, and the candles burned down in the looted chandelier. Outside, beyond the shuttered windows, the night was full of the sound of cicadas and, very faintly, the sound of a sentry’s boots on the gravel of the courtyard, pacing back and forth, back and forth, in the dark.
