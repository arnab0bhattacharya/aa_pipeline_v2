Sir Tristram Vandeleur had achieved the triumph of his life by doing nothing with an extraordinary amount of grace. He was universally regarded as the only man in London who had perfectly mastered the art of being idle. To him, exertion of any kind was a vulgarity, and to be useful was to be intensely middle-class. Nature had intended him for a purely decorative existence, and he had fulfilled her intention with an admirable fidelity. His cravats were whispered about in the clubs, and his tailor was a man who was spoken of only in hushed tones of reverence. 

It was, therefore, with a feeling akin to horror that he received Mr. Septimus Vane, his solicitor, one foggy morning in his chambers in Half Moon Street. Mr. Vane was a man who seemed to have been compounded entirely of legal phraseology and red tape. He had a profound respect for the late Sir Aloysius Vandeleur, Sir Tristram’s father, chiefly because the late baronet had possessed the excellent taste to be exceedingly wealthy.

“I am afraid, Sir Tristram,” said the solicitor, peering through his spectacles at a parchment, “that the condition attached to your late father’s will is perfectly unassailable. You must earn your own living for the space of one calendar year, or the estate, comprising some forty thousand a year, passes to your cousin, the Reverend Bartholomew Vandeleur.”

“But, my dear Vane,” murmured Sir Tristram, lighting a cigarette of surpassing slenderness, “I have been earning my living for the last ten years. I have been supporting the British opera, the French florists, and an inordinate number of Italian picture-decorators. I am the sole cause of at least three tradesmen retiring to the country. If that is not earning one’s living, what is?”

“The law, Sir Tristram, does not recognize aesthetic expenditure as industry,” replied the lawyer, with a slight smile that suggested he rather regretted the fact. 

“And what, pray, am I to do?” asked Sir Tristram, examining his perfectly manicured nails with an air of mild despair.

“You must enter some recognized profession, or accept some paid employment. The will expressly states that the remuneration must not be a mere nominal sum, but a living wage, earned by the actual performance of daily duties.”

Sir Tristram blew a delicate ring of smoke into the fog-laden air. “A living wage,” he repeated, with a delicate shudder. “It sounds terribly like eating chops in a basement. However, I suppose one must conform to the dying delusions of one’s ancestors. They always have such very bad taste in their final moments.”

When the news leaked out, as such news invariably does, society received it with a burst of delighted amusement. At Lady Windermere’s crush that evening, it was the sole topic of conversation. 

“It is the most amusing scandal of the season,” declared Lord Arthur Pendleton, adjusting his monocle as he surveyed the crowded salons. “It is like discovering that a piece of Sèvres porcelain has been ordered to cook a chop. One cannot help feeling curious to see how it will crack.”

“I think it is a most charming arrangement,” said the Duchess of Crowthorne, playing with her fan of spangled black silk. “Sir Tristram has been a purely ornamental figure for so long that it will be delightful to see him treated as a piece of useful furniture. I only hope he does not become a whatnot. A whatnot is so dreadfully lower-middle-class.”

“What is he going to do?” someone asked.

“He is going to be a private secretary,” replied Lord Arthur. “He has accepted a post with Lady Clementina Marchmont. She is the wife of the Cabinet Minister, you know, and requires someone to manage her correspondence, arrange her At Homes, and keep her husband’s political conscience in a state of elegant subjection.”

“But Lady Clementina is a woman of the most terrifying intellect,” cried the Duchess. “She is so dreadfully earnest. She believes in Reform, and other things that are quite unfit for discussion in a drawing-room. Poor Tristram! To be subjected to a woman who reads blue-books for pleasure! It is worse than the chops in the basement.”

Sir Tristram, however, accepted his fate with a perfect composure. He had early arrived at the conclusion that the only way to endure the vulgarity of life was to treat it as a comedy. On the morning he was to assume his duties, he arrayed himself in a frock-coat of exquisite cut, placed a gardenia in his buttonhole, and drove to the great political house in Westminster. 

Lady Clementina received him in her boudoir, a room decorated with a severity that was almost theological. The chairs were of stamped leather, and the tables were weighed down by pamphlets on social economy. She was a woman of high, angular features and an expression of perpetual disapproval. She wore a dress of sage-green silk that rustled like the leaves of autumn when she moved.

“I am given to understand, Sir Tristram,” she said, fixing him with her grey eyes, “that you have never done a day’s work in your life.”

“I have always considered work,” replied Sir Tristram, seating himself with an air of graceful ease, “to be the refuge of those who have nothing better to do. I have always had something better to do.”

“That is precisely the frame of mind,” said Lady Clementina severely, “which has brought our country to the deplorable state of moral apathy in which it presently languishes. I require my private secretary to be active, industrious, and entirely devoid of personal vanity.”

“I can be entirely devoid of personal vanity, Lady Clementina,” answered Sir Tristram, “provided I am allowed to retain my personal tailor. One must draw the line somewhere, and the line, I fancy, should always be drawn at the knee of one’s trousers.”

Lady Clementina frowned, but Sir Tristram’s manner was so entirely devoid of insolence, and so steeped in a languid charm, that she found it impossible to take offence. She handed him a large bundle of letters. 

“These require immediate answers,” she said. “They are from the wives of the constituents in my husband’s borough. You will find that they are mostly requests for patronage, advice on domestic economy, and inquiries into the character of cooks. I leave you to deal with them. I am going to a meeting of the Society for the Suppression of Superfluous Emotion.”

When she had departed, Sir Tristram looked at the mountain of correspondence. He took up his pen, a rather heavy instrument of gold and mother-of-pearl, and drew it thoughtfully across his forehead. Then he rang the bell. A grave-looking butler appeared.

“Pendragon,” said Sir Tristram, for he had already learned the names of the servants, a task he had found surprisingly easy, as servants were always much more interesting than their masters, “bring me sometea. And, Pendragon, if you have any influence with the kitchen, see that the muffins are toasted on the underside. A burnt muffin is an unforgivable sin, and I cannot write letters on an empty stomach.”

He applied himself to the task of answering the letters. He did not write in the style Lady Clementina would have approved of. To the wife of the Dissenting minister who had asked for a subscription to a charity, he wrote a charming epistle, full of delicate compliments and vague promises, enclosing a banknote from his own pocket, for he had not yet entirely abandoned the habits of his wealth. To the woman who complained of her cook’s intemperance, he replied that intemperance was always to be deplored, but that a cook who could make a good white sauce was, perhaps, to be forgiven much. 

He filled the letters with aphorisms and paradoxes, turning the dull complaints of the constituents into brilliant little essays on the absurdity of human existence. When Lady Clementina returned, she found him gazing out of the window at the river, his task completed.

“I have answered them all,” he said, turning his head with a slow, sweet smile. “I hope you will find my replies adequate. I have told the butcher’s wife that the true secret of happiness lies in a judicious disregard for the price of mutton. I felt it was a sentiment that would appeal to her.”

Lady Clementina read the letters with an expression of growing astonishment. They were entirely unlike the formal, chilling replies she usually dispatched. They were full of wit, of sympathy, and of a curious, delicate understanding of human foibles. 

“You have made a joke of the constituents’ sorrows,” she said, severely.

“On the contrary,” replied Sir Tristram, “I have elevated them to the level of art. Sorrow is only funny when it is someone else’s, but when it is one’s own, it is merely tragic. I have given them a little tragedy, tempered with a great deal of style. They will be charmed.”

To her own surprise, Lady Clementina found that she could not dismiss him. The replies, though entirely lacking in political gravity, had a certain undeniable quality. The constituents, starved of elegance, were enchanted. Letters of gratitude began to pour in. 

As the months passed, Sir Tristram became an indispensable figure in the household. He arranged Lady Clementina’s At Homes with the eye of a theatrical manager. He introduced a note of color into the severe drawing-rooms, substituting bowls of yellow roses for the usual aspidistras. He taught Lady Clementina that a well-chosen gown was as important as a well-drafted bill, and that a repartee was often more effective than a resolution. 

He earned his wage, not by any vulgar sweat of his brow, but by the mere exercise of his taste. He was paid for being himself. The Cabinet Minister found that his wife’s correspondence was suddenly witty, and that her entertainments were the most sought-after in Westminster. 

When the year was up, Mr. Septimus Vane called upon Sir Tristram with a document releasing him from the condition of the will. 

“You have fulfilled the requirements, Sir Tristram,” said the lawyer. “You have earned a living wage. The estate is yours once more.”

Sir Tristram looked at the parchment with a faint, melancholy smile. 

“I am afraid I must decline, Vane,” he said, lighting another cigarette. “I find that I have grown accustomed to the society of Lady Clementina. She is the only person in London who does not bore me. Besides, I have just discovered that the salary of a private secretary is just sufficient to purchase a new set of china, if one is careful. I shall stay.”

“But the estate, Sir Tristram! Forty thousand a year!”

“It is a great deal of money,” agreed Sir Tristram, gazing out at the grey river. “But then, it is a great deal of responsibility. I have no taste for responsibility. I prefer to be useful to Lady Clementina. It is so much more amusing than being rich.” 

And so it was that Sir Tristram Vandeleur, the man who had done nothing, became the man who did everything for a Cabinet Minister’s wife, and society, which had laughed at his downfall, was forced to admit that he had, after all, achieved the final triumph of style over circumstance.
