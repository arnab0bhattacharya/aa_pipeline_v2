I had been settled in my consulting rooms above a silversmith's in Greville Street for some three years when Miss Edith Vance knocked upon my door. It was a Tuesday morning in November of 1894, and a yellow fog had settled over Hatton Garden, dampening the clatter of the traffic and casting the gas-lamps in a sickly, haloed glow. I admitted her, noting as she entered the room that she was a young woman of artistic temperament—her left cuff was smeared with charcoal, and her hands bore the faint, indelible stain of cadmium yellow—but her bearing was composed, and her grey eyes held the steady directness of a person accustomed to observation.

She sat on the edge of the chair I offered and accepted neither tea nor fire, though the grate was newly lit.

"You are Mr. Gideon Fell," she said, in a low, brisk voice that betrayed a measure of education not entirely common to her trade. "I am a student of the Slade, lodging in the rooms above Mr. Josiah Bunce’s establishment. I face a matter which I cannot lay before the police, for I possess no evidence, only suspicions, and they would think me hysterical."

I had heard of Bunce. He kept a modest but respectable jeweller's shop on the south side of the street, dealing chiefly in second-hand pieces and minor repairs. I bade her continue.

"For six weeks," said Miss Vance, "I have been kept from my sleep by a persistent hammering beneath my floorboards. It begins at eleven o'clock at night, precisely, and ceases at three in the morning. The blows are measured and deliberate. I should not have minded it, had it not been for the singular hour and the singular nature of the sound. It was not the rapid tap of a clockmaker’s screwdriver, nor the whirr of a lathe. It was heavy, percussive, and always followed by a long, scraping hiss."

"And you raised this with Mr. Bunce?" I asked.

"I did. He is a small, fussy man, very bald, with a great gold ring upon his little finger. He appeared greatly put out by the inquiry. He told me he was a keen amateur horologist, that he took in clock-repair work after hours to supplement his income, and that the noise was merely the striking of his tools upon brass casings. He begged my indulgence and offered to reduce my rent by five shillings a month. I accepted."

"And the hammering continued?"

"Every night, sir, without fail. Until last Thursday. On Thursday, the noise ceased entirely. On Friday morning, the shop did not open. On Saturday, I observed that the shutters remained down. Mr. Bunce has not been seen since."

I steepled my fingers. "You entered the shop?"

"The constable from the local station forced the door. The interior was undisturbed. The glass cases were intact, the trays of rings and brooches untouched. There was, however, a quantity of white dust upon the counter, and a peculiar smell—a sharp, metallic odour, not unlike that of a dentist's surgery. The constable concluded that Mr. Bunce had fled to avoid his creditors. I am not satisfied."

She laid a small paper packet upon my desk. "I found this upon the staircase. It was caught upon a splinter of the banister, near the bottom step. I thought it might be of use."

I opened the packet. Inside was a small, irregular flake of pale, chalky matter. I examined it with my lens, held it to the light, and then, with my penknife, scraped a fragment into the flame of my spirit-lamp. A brief, vivid green flare answered.

"Miss Vance," I said, "you have done well. You have brought me the solution to your mystery, though you did not know it."

She leaned forward, her composure giving way to a sharp curiosity. "Then you will take the matter up?"

"I have already taken it up," I replied. "Pray remain here for one hour. I have an errand in Hatton Garden."

I made my way on foot through the fog. The shutters of Bunce's shop were still down, and a small knot of idlers had gathered to peer through the grille. I passed them by and descended the narrow alley to the rear, where the tradesman's entrance stood. The lock was a simple Ward. I had it open in under a minute.

The shop was as Miss Vance had described, down to the white dust upon the counter. I paid it no attention, but made my way to the back room, where a single gas-jet still burned low. The room was fitted out as a workshop. A bench stood against the far wall, littered with files and pliers. Upon the bench sat a small anvil, its face pocked and scarred with recent use. Beside it lay a heavy ball-pein hammer, its head bright where it had recently struck metal.

I knelt and examined the floorboards beneath the bench. They were loose. With the aid of the hammer, I pried up three of the boards and set them aside. Beneath, in a shallow cavity, I found what I expected to find: a quantity of grey, heavy powder, a small crucible blackened by intense heat, and a pair of iron tongs. But there was something else—a leather wallet, bulging and stiff, which had been thrust hastily into the far corner of the recess.

I drew it out and opened it. Inside was a thick sheaf of banknotes—French, to the sum of two thousand francs—and a folded paper bearing a wax seal. I did not break the seal. I replaced the wallet, the boards, and the bench, and returned to Greville Street within the hour, exactly as I had promised.

Miss Vance rose as I entered. "Well?"

"Your jeweller is not a clockmaker," said I, hanging up my greatcoat. "He is a smelter. The hammering you heard was the sound of metal being beaten flat upon an anvil, and the hiss which followed was the quenching of hot iron in water. The white dust upon his counter is borax, used in the reduction of ores. The green flare which my test produced tells me that the metal in question is copper, alloyed with a notable proportion of tin."

"You are telling me he was making bronze?"

"I am telling you, Miss Vance, that he was making something far more valuable. Do you know what French gold coinage is composed of?"

She stared. "Napoleons? Gold, of course."

"Twenty-franc Napoleon pieces are, in fact, an alloy of gold, silver, and copper. But there exist in circulation certain counterfeit Napoleons, of exquisite manufacture, in which the gold is replaced entirely by a bronze core, plated with a wash of the genuine metal. They pass the ring test, the weight test, and the eye test. They are, in short, the most dangerous counterfeits in Europe. And they are produced, Miss Vance, by exactly the method you have described to me."

"The hammering," she breathed.

"The hammering, the scraping, and the quenching. Bunce was not repairing clocks. He was stamping and finishing false coin. When the work was done, he would have needed to remove the evidence—the cores, the crucible, the tools. But something interrupted him. He was a careful man, we know, from the tidiness of his shop. He would not have left the wallet behind unless his departure was sudden and involuntary."

"You believe he was taken?"

"I believe he was taken by the very men who employed him. The French banknotes tell me the operation was Continental in its scope. The sealed paper, which I left in place for the authorities, will tell me the rest. I have already sent a wire to Inspector Harlow at Scotland Yard. He and his men will be at the shop within the hour. I should not be surprised if the trail leads them to a house in Limehouse, or a wharf at Wapping, by nightfall."

Miss Vance was silent for a moment. She gathered her shawl about her shoulders and looked at me with an expression I had come to know well in my years of practice—the mingled relief and wonder of a person who has seen a tangled skein suddenly and cleanly unravelled.

"I am very glad I came to you, Mr. Fell," she said.

"I am glad you had the wits to pick up that flake of borax, Miss Vance," I answered. "Without it, I should have had a very cold trail on a very foggy morning."
