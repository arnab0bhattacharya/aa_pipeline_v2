It is a curious thing that those who are born to sit upon thrones are never permitted to walk upon the earth. Their feet are kept perpetually removed from the common soil by means of velvet carpets and silver stilts, and their eyes are denied the wholesome spectacle of ordinary life. They are surrounded by courtiers who tell them only what is flattering, and by mirrors that reflect only what is agreeable; and as a result, they frequently go through the world believing themselves to be quite extraordinary, when in truth they are merely very well upholstered.

Such was the case with the Princess Valeria of Aurelia. She had been informed, from the moment she was old enough to understand language, that she was the most beautiful creature in the entire kingdom, and indeed in the three adjoining kingdoms as well. Her father, King Maximilian, was a man of immense wealth and very little imagination, who had accumulated a vast collection of rare and costly objects, and who regarded his daughter as the most valuable item in his collection. He had once been told by a visiting ambassador that her skin was like the petals of a white rose laid upon snow, and he had repeated the observation so frequently that the ambassador himself had grown quite tired of hearing it, though he was far too well-mannered to say so.

The Princess, having been told of her beauty so often that she had ceased to think about it altogether, had at last reached that dangerous age when one begins to suspect that what one has always been told may not be entirely true. She had observed, with some alarm, that the courtiers who praised her appearance most lavishly were precisely those whose own appearances were most irreparable. The elderly Countess von Schreckenhausen, whose face resembled a somewhat faded map of central Europe, had been particularly enthusiastic on the subject of the Princess’s complexion, and the Princess had begun to wonder whether beauty, like wealth, is most highly prized by those who possess the least of it.

One morning, therefore, she determined to visit the great market of Aurelia in disguise, in order to discover whether the common people, who had no reason to flatter her, would confirm what the courtiers had so long asserted. She put on the plainest dress she could find, which was still considerably more magnificent than anything worn by the actual populace, and covered her hair with a simple veil. Then she slipped out through a side gate of the palace, accompanied only by her maid, Lotte, who was terrified of being recognised and equally terrified of not being recognised, which is a condition familiar to many persons of modest social position.

The market of Aurelia was a vast and splendid confusion of colours, sounds, and smells. There were merchants from the southern provinces selling spices in small leather pouches, and merchants from the eastern provinces selling silks that shimmered like captured rainbows. There were old women selling bread, and young women selling flowers, and very young children selling nothing at all but simply running about underfoot, which is, after all, what very young children do best. The Princess moved through this scene with the quiet delight of someone who has been kept too long in a museum and has at last been permitted to enter a garden.

She paused at a stall where an astonishing array of flowers was displayed: crimson carnations, white lilies, blue irises, and a quantity of yellow roses that seemed to hold the very light of the sun within their petals. Behind the stall sat an old woman, dressed in a faded purple cloak, with a face so deeply lined that it appeared to have been folded many times, like an ancient letter that has been read and reread and put away at last in a drawer. The most remarkable thing about the old woman, however, was that her eyes were quite white and sightless, and she sat quite still, turning her face toward the sound of the passers-by as a flower turns toward the warmth of the sun.

The Princess stood before the stall for some time, examining the flowers with the careful attention of someone who has been taught that beauty is a serious matter requiring serious study. She selected a single white rose and held it for a moment against her cheek, which was a gesture she had seen her mother perform in a portrait, and which she considered to be very becoming.

‘How much is this rose?’ she asked.

The old woman turned her sightless eyes toward the sound of the voice. ‘For you, my dear,’ she said, ‘it is nothing. You have already paid me, though you do not know it.’

The Princess was somewhat disconcerted by this reply, which seemed to her to be unnecessarily mysterious, and she said, with a slight frown, ‘I am afraid I do not understand. I have not paid you anything.’

‘You have paid me in music,’ said the old woman. ‘Your voice, when you spoke just now, was like a song. It had in it the quality of a fine instrument played by someone who loves the instrument more than the melody. Most people speak in order to say something; you speak in order to make a sound, and the sound you make is beautiful.’

The Princess did not know whether to be pleased by this observation or offended by it. She had been told, all her life, that she was beautiful to look upon, but no one had ever suggested that she was beautiful to listen to. The compliment, if it was a compliment, seemed to her to be of a rather inferior sort, as though someone had praised a painting for the quality of its frame rather than for the genius of its composition. She was silent for a moment, turning the rose between her fingers.

‘You are very kind,’ she said at last, ‘but I did not know that I sang when I spoke.’

‘Everyone sings when they speak,’ said the old woman. ‘Most people simply sing out of tune. You sing in tune, which is rare, and you sing without caring whether you sing in tune, which is rarer still. The people who care most about their voices are usually the ones who have the least voice to care about. I have been blind for forty years, and in that time I have heard a great many voices. I have heard the voices of kings, which are generally loud and empty, like rooms that have been furnished but never lived in. I have heard the voices of beggars, which are sometimes soft and full of unexpected music, like the sound of wind passing through a ruined chapel. And I have heard the voices of those who are beautiful, who almost always speak as though they were apologising for something, though I have never been able to determine what.’

‘And what do the beautiful apologise for?’ asked the Princess, who could not resist the question, though she felt that she was perhaps revealing more than she ought.

‘For being beautiful, I suppose,’ said the old woman, ‘though that is rather like apologising for the sunrise. The sunrise does not require an apology, and neither does beauty, and yet both are frequently followed by complaints, which is perhaps the only thing that they have in common.’

The Princess placed a coin on the stall, despite the old woman’s protestations, and walked away through the market, carrying her white rose. She did not examine the faces of the passers-by as she had intended, nor did she pause to observe whether the common people stared at her with admiration or indifference. She walked in a kind of thoughtful silence, turning the old woman’s words over in her mind as one turns a gemstone, examining first one facet and then another.

It occurred to her, with something like a shock of recognition, that she had gone to the market to discover whether she was beautiful, and that she had returned having discovered instead that she possessed a beautiful voice, which was an entirely different matter, and one about which she had never previously given a moment’s thought. She felt a vague sense of disappointment, as though she had gone in search of a particular jewel and had been presented instead with a different jewel, of equal value but unfamiliar setting.

She thought also about what the old woman had said, that the beautiful apologise for their beauty, and she wondered whether this was true, and whether she herself had been apologising all her life without knowing it. She tried to recall whether she had ever spoken in a manner that suggested she was sorry for her appearance, and she found that she could not remember, which is perhaps the natural condition of those who have never had occasion to think about such things.

When she returned to the palace, she went at once to her mirror, which was a very large mirror framed in silver, and she looked at herself with the careful attention she had given to the flowers in the market. She observed that her skin was, indeed, like the petals of a white rose laid upon snow, and that her eyes were large and dark, and that her hair fell in golden waves about her shoulders. All of this she had observed many times before, and it had always given her a quiet satisfaction, but now she found that the satisfaction was somehow diminished, as though the mirror had failed to tell her something which she very much wanted to know.

She opened her mouth, as if to speak, and then closed it again, regarding her reflection with an expression of faint perplexity. The mirror, she reflected, could tell her that she was beautiful, but it could not tell her that she sang when she spoke, for mirrors are concerned only with surfaces, and sound belongs to the world of depths. She had spent her entire life being looked at, and she had never, until that morning, considered the possibility that she might also be listened to, which is, when one comes to think of it, a far more intimate form of attention.

She placed the white rose upon her dressing table, and as she did so, she said, quite aloud, ‘I wonder whether it is better to be seen or to be heard.’

And the mirror, as is the way with mirrors, returned no answer at all, but merely showed her, once again, the face that all the world had told her was the most beautiful in the kingdom, and which she now, for the first time, found to be somehow insufficient.
