It is a curious thing, perhaps, how readily a young man’s entire moral and intellectual compass can be warped by a sudden, desperate necessity and a girl in a white muslin dress. I had gone up to London in the spring with forty pounds and the assured intention of passing the Preliminary Scientific Examination. I returned to Seacombe, in Sussex, with empty pockets, a profound distaste for practical chemistry, and a mental state bordering closely upon panic. The examination had been a comprehensive disaster. I had confused the reagents, precipitated the wrong compounds, and demonstrated a grasp of inorganic chemistry that was, at best, purely theoretical.

My Uncle Josiah, who kept the large chemist’s shop opposite the parade at Seacombe, was a man of science in the strictly commercial sense. He was a stout, bald little man, much preoccupied with patent foods, sponges, and the compounding of cough lozenges. He took me into his employment at fifteen shillings a week, with board and lodging thrown in, upon the strict understanding that I was a useless fellow. "You’ll have to make yourself useful, George," he said, eying my despondent manner with a clinical detachment. "Wash the bottles, keep the counter clean, and don’t break the graduated measures. I’ll teach you the dispensing as we go." It was a humble enough beginning, but it served to keep me from absolute starvation.

It was in this capacity, standing behind a counter smelling strongly of carbolic soap and lavender water, that I first encountered Miss Vera Davenport. She had come into the shop to purchase some violet cachous and a small bottle of glycerine. She was staying with her family at the Royal Hotel, a circumstance which at once placed her in a social sphere entirely above my own. She was perhaps nineteen, dark-haired, possessed of a frank, engaging smile, and entirely oblivious to the devastating effect she had upon my sensibilities. She spoke to me with that cheerful, democratic friendliness which holiday visitors extend to shop assistants, and I, for my part, was entirely enslaved.

The deception began a week later. She had returned to purchase some court plaster, and we had fallen into conversation. She expressed an interest in the various bottles on the shelves, and I, fired by a sudden, reckless vanity, informed her that I was reading medicine. "Indeed?" she said, turning her fine eyes upon me. "My brother is reading medicine at Guy’s. He is down here for a fortnight to recover from overwork." I had not, at that moment, the faintest intention of passing myself off as a medical student. The lie simply fell from my mouth, born of a desperate desire to appear something more than a defeated examinee washing phials in a seaside shop. But having said it, I was bound by it. I hinted broadly at my recent studies in London, and she listened with an interest that was utterly intoxicating.

From that moment, my life at Seacombe became a feverish, elaborate fabrication. I constructed an entirely fictitious existence. I spoke learnedly, and entirely fraudulently, of the dissecting rooms and the lectures I was supposedly attending during the winter months. I carried a small notebook, in which I scribbled anatomical terms copied from a textbook I found in Uncle Josiah’s back room. I met her on the parade, by accident, and we walked together on the cliffs. She was vastly amused by my conversation, and I gathered that her brother, who was something of a prig, was regarded by the family as a person of immense intellectual weight. I walked the front at night in a state of emotional exaltation, planning absurd futures, and returning to my attic bedroom to stare at the ceiling and whisper "Vera!" in a thoroughly maudlin fashion.

The catastrophe, as is so often the way with these things, arrived with a brutal, mechanical swiftness. It was a Thursday afternoon. Uncle Josiah was in the dispensary, sneezing violently over a compound of ipecacuanha, and I was alone in the shop, polishing a glass percolator. The little bell above the door rang, and Miss Davenport entered, accompanied by a young man in a grey flannel suit, carrying a golf club. He was a lean, sandy-haired individual, with that aggressive, knowledgeable air which is the hallmark of the second-year medical student.

"This is my brother, Mr. Farringdon," she said. "I have been telling him about you."

Mr. Farringdon nodded with a curt, appraising nod. "Glad to meet you," he said. "Vera tells me you’re at the London. What year?"

I experienced a sudden, cold, sinking sensation in my thoracic region. "I’m—I’m doing my prelim," I said, which was, in a strictly technical sense, the truth, though not in the sense I intended him to understand.

"Ah," he said, leaning against the counter with an easy familiarity. "Rough show, that. I scraped through mine by the skin of my teeth. How did you find the inorganic? Old Baxter’s a terror on the practical, isn’t he?"

I said that old Baxter was indeed a terror, and that the practical had been a trial. I was sweating visibly. I mopped my brow with a handkerchief, hoping to convey the impression of a man oppressed by the heat rather than by guilt.

"Heavy on the group separations, isn’t he?" Mr. Farringford continued, cheerfully. "I remember I had a beast of a time with the arsenic group. How did you get on with the sulphuretted hydrogen precipitations?"

I said I had managed tolerably well, though the sulphuretted hydrogen had been a nuisance. I was, in truth, drowning in a sea of my own mendacity. I had not the faintest idea what the sulphuretted hydrogen precipitations were, or what old Baxter looked like. I had confused my reagents in the actual examination, and here I was, attempting to bluff a man who had presumably spent the better part of a year manipulating them.

"He’s very clever," Miss Davenport said, turning to her brother with a smile of such confiding warmth that I could have cheerfully sunk through the floorboards and perished in the cellar. "He knows all about the dissecting, too."

Mr. Farringdon regarded me with a sudden, sharp suspicion. "Dissecting?" he said. "In the prelim? You don’t do dissecting in the prelim. You don’t do that until the second year."

There was a horrible, frozen pause. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked with a loud, insolent clarity. I stood behind the counter, holding the percolator, and stared at Mr. Farringdon with the blank, uncomprehending stare of a trapped animal. Miss Davenport looked from her brother to me, and the smile faded from her face.

"I thought you said—" she began.

"He’s mistaken," I said, wildly. "I meant—I was referring to the osteology. The bones."

"Osteology isn’t in the prelim either," said Mr. Farringdon, with the grim, relentless satisfaction of a man who has detected a fraud. "What exactly are you doing here, if you’re a medical student? Why aren’t you up in London?"

"I’m—I’m down for a holiday," I stammered.

"Vera says you work here," said Mr. Farringdon, flatly.

There was no escape. The whole flimsy structure of lies collapsed about my ears with a sickening, inward crash. I put down the percolator very carefully upon the counter. "I am employed here," I said. "I failed my examination."

Miss Davenport drew a sharp breath. She looked at me with an expression of such profound, bewildered disappointment that it was infinitely worse than anger. "You failed?" she said, very quietly. "And you told me you were a student."

"I am a student," I said, miserably. "Or I was. I am an assistant here now."

"You told me you were at the London," she said. "You said you were going to be a surgeon."

"I know I did. I’m sorry. I—"

"I think we’ve heard enough," said Mr. Farringdon, briskly. He took his sister by the arm. "Come along, Vera. The fellow’s an impostor. A common shop boy."

They turned and went out of the shop, the bell ringing with a light, indifferent tinkling sound behind them. I stood quite still, staring at the percolator. Through the plate-glass window I watched them cross the road and disappear along the parade. The sea, visible beyond the promenade, glittered with a hard, unsympathetic brilliance. A small boy came in for a halfpenny worth of peppermint drops, and I served him with a mechanical precision. Then I went into the dispensary, where Uncle Josiah was grinding something in a mortar, and told him I had a headache. He told me to take a dose of phenacetin and not to loiter. I went upstairs to my attic and sat on the edge of my bed, staring at the wall.

It was, I suppose, a perfectly commonplace little tragedy of vanity. But it seemed to me, at that moment, that the entire fabric of human society was a cruel and mocking thing, and that the sea outside was laughing in a hollow, metallic voice at the folly of a young man who had pretended to be a medical student. I have recovered from it, of course. One recovers from everything. I am now a fully qualified pharmaceutical chemist, with a small establishment of my own in a suburb of Croydon. But I have never, from that day to this, made the slightest attempt to deceive anyone upon the subject of my qualifications. And I have a profound, unreasoning dislike of golf clubs.
