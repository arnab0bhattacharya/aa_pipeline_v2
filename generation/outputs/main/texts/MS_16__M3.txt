It is a melancholy truth, deeply engraven upon the tablets of my memory, that the seeds of our profoundest agonies are often sown in the very hour of our most cherished hopes. I had emerged from the obscurity of my mourning into the brilliant, feverish society of London, my heart, though seared by a grievous loss, still capable of the deepest attachments. My father, the sole author of my being, had perished in a foreign field, his life-blood staining the morning dew, the victim of a duel whose true origin and circumstances had been shrouded, by the artifices of his destroyer, in an impenetrable veil. I was left an orphan, wealthy, and unprotected; yet I possessed one inestimable treasure—my Seraphina. She was the star of my existence, a being of ineffable gentleness, whose soul seemed woven of light and sympathy. We had grown up together in the retirement of Valdegrave, and when I removed to London, it was to share with her the joys and the splendours which my newly inherited station seemed to promise.

It was in the early spring of 1822 that the intelligence burst upon me, crushing every springing bud of expectation, and plunging me into an abyss of unutterable wo. Seraphina was to be married. The man whose name she was to bear was Lord Valerius Devereux, a member of Parliament, a man whose eloquence charmed the senate, whose bearing commanded the reverence of the high and the powerful. The world worshipped him; but I—I possessed a terrible secret, which, while it tore aside the mask from his virtues, consigned my soul to the most frightful conflict. I held the proof of that fatal duel. I held the written confession of the second who had attended my father, a paper which proclaimed that Valerius, urged on by a dark, unappeasable hatred, had not only sought the encounter, but had denied his wounded adversary all aid, leaving him to bleed to death upon the damp earth, while he fled to cover his crime under the guise of an honourable quarrel.

Oh, what a night was that when this knowledge first forced itself upon my understanding! The elements without seemed to sympathize with the tempest of my soul. A storm of awful magnificence had gathered over the city; the thunder rolled in tremendous peals, and the lightning, red and vindictive, flashed through the casement of my chamber, illuminating the fatal scroll I held. I sat alone in the dead of night, the winds howling around the ancient walls of my dwelling, as if the spirits of the air were chanting a dirge over my departed peace. I read and re-read the damning lines, until each word seared my brain like molten iron. What right had I to keep silence? What right had I to let this man, this incarnate fiend, bind to his bosom the pure, the unsuspecting Seraphina? None! That I acknowledged. But to speak—to blast his name, and in so doing, to shatter the heart of her I loved, was a thought that filled me with unutterable anguish. I was torn by contrary impulses, each more agonizing than the last; and the storm within my breast raged with a fury that mocked the elemental war without.

I resolved to seek an interview with Valerius himself. I would extort from him a confession, a relinquishment of his claim, and if he proved adamant, I would denounce him. I went to his mansion, a stately pile in the heart of the metropolis. The day was sultry and oppressive, the sky lowering with clouds that seemed to portend a second burst of tempest. I was admitted to his presence. He stood before me, proud, collected, and surpassingly handsome; a man whom the world delighted to honour, but whose soul, if I read it aright, was a seething mass of guilt and selfish ambition. He received me with a cold, polished courtesy, which, while it concealed his surprise at my visit, failed to conceal the hardness of his nature.

"I know the purpose of your visit, Lady Eleanor," he said, his voice modulated to a deep, calm melody, which, in my then state of excited feeling, seemed like the hiss of a serpent. "You come to upbraid me with the past. I had hoped that time had buried all recollection of that unfortunate event."

"Unfortunate!" I echoed, my voice trembling with the passion that threatened to burst every bound of restraint. "You call the murder of my father unfortunate! My Lord, I possess the proof of your guilt. You left him to perish on the field of Marwood, a victim to your unrelenting vengeance. And now you would link your fate with that of Seraphina, the sister of my soul! By the God that made us, it shall not be!"

He started, a livid paleness overspreading his features; but the emotion was but momentary. The hardness, which in the ancient religions is deemed the immediate work of God, crept over his heart, turning its human misgiving to stony resolution. He drew himself up to his full height, and looking at me with a glance of mingled scorn and commiseration, replied, "You are frantic, Lady Eleanor. The tale you have heard is the fabrication of a diseased imagination. I slew your father in honourable combat, and no power on earth can fix the brand of murder upon me. As for Seraphina, she is mine by every tie of affection and honour. Seek not to cross my path, for you will only bring down ruin upon yourself."

His words, his cool, unblushing effrontery, acted upon me like some potent spell. I was silent; I could not speak. The very room seemed to swim before my eyes, and I felt as one who stands upon the verge of a precipice, fascinated by the depths below. I turned and left him, my heart swelling with a sense of impotence and wrong, which threatened to burst the frail tenement of clay.

For days I wandered about the streets of London, a solitary, despairing outcast. The busy hum of life, the gaiety of the throng, jarred upon my feelings like a hideous discord. I was alone in the midst of my kind; my secret was a canker that gnawed my vitals. I visited Seraphina. She was radiant with happiness; her eyes beamed with a light that went to my very soul, and her words, fraught with tender affection, pierced me with a thousand pangs. She spoke of Valerius in terms of rapturous admiration, dwelling on his virtues, his generosity, his noble aspirations. I listened in silence; I dared not undeceive her. The despair written on my brow, my sombre, mute, yet heart-broken mien, alone betrayed the conflict of my feelings.

"What ails you, my Eleanor?" she cried, throwing her arms around my neck. "You are changed; you are unhappy. Tell me your grief, and let me share it."

I withdrew from her embrace; her touch was fire to my tortured frame. "I am ill, Seraphina," I faltered; "the weather, the heat—forgive me, I must leave you." And I fled from her presence, lest the terrible truth should escape my lips in a torrent of accusation.

Surely there is no greater enemy to virtue and good intentions than that want of self-command, the exterior of which, though I had acquired, no portion existed in the inner substance of my mind. Calm, proud, and stern as I seemed to others, capable of governing the vehemence of my temper, within I was the same slave of passion I had ever been. I never could force myself to do the thing I hated; I never could persuade myself to relinquish the thing I desired. There is the secret of my misery; there the vice of my disposition, which produced for her I loved a desolate future, and for myself endless, unutterable wo. For a moment I had become virtuous and heroic. I had determined to speak, to reveal the truth, and save her from a union with her father's murderer. But the fear of wounding her, the dread of seeing the light die out of those beloved eyes, held me back.

The day of the bridal arrived. The sun rose in unclouded splendour, as if in mockery of my despair. The church was thronged with the gay and the great; the air was filled with murmurings of admiration. I stood apart, a silent, unseen witness of the ceremony. I saw Seraphina, in all the loveliness of her bridal robes, lean upon the arm of Valerius. I saw the smile of ineffable trust that played upon her lips. I saw the ring placed upon her finger, the holy words pronounced that bound them together for life. A devil at that moment whispered in my ear, a devil, who feeds on human crimes and groans, prompted my arm. Coward and dolt! to use such words—my own hellish mind was the sole instigator. In a moment it was done. I held my peace. I sealed my lips, and with that act, I consigned my soul to perdition.

The ceremony was over. The bride passed out, leaning on the arm of her husband. As she reached the portal, she turned and looked at me. One cry from him, one long, piercing shriek from her, and we were gone. With the swiftness of the winds I descended the steps of the church, and fled into the crowded street. The roaring in my ears, the blinding tears, the suffocating sense of a wrong unredressed, a duty unfulfilled, drove me onward, I knew not whither. I had sacrificed my father's memory to my friend's happiness; I had bartered my own peace for a silence that was to prove the grave of all my joys. The world went on around me, bright, festive, and indifferent; but I was alone, alone with my secret, alone with my despair; and the brand of Cain seemed seared upon my forehead, marking me out as an outcast and a fugitive upon the face of the earth.
