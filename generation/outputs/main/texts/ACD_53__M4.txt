It was on a raw November afternoon, with the yellow fog pressing close against the windows of my consulting-room in Clement’s Inn, that Mr. Josiah Barrowdale was shown in to me. He was a man of some sixty years, stout and florid, with the assured manner of one who has been accustomed to be listened to, yet I observed that his fingers worked nervously at the seals of his watch-chain, and that his eyes, though they met mine squarely enough, had in them a certain haunted look which belied the ease of his bearing.

“You are the investigator of whom I have heard so much,” said he, taking the chair I indicated. “I am told you are discreet and that you do not shrink from queer business. I have queer business for you, sir—the queerest, I think, that ever a man brought to another.”

“I shall be glad to hear it,” I answered, “though I make no promises until I have heard it all.”

He sat silent for a moment, as though marshalling his thoughts, and then he plunged into his story.

“Six weeks ago,” said he, “I was crossing Ludgate Hill, near the corner of Cobb’s Court, when a dray-horse took fright at a passing omnibus and came down the street at a gallop. I am not so active as I was, and I daresay I stood like a fool in the very path of the brute. There was a shout, a hand caught me by the collar and flung me clean across the gutter on to the pavement, and when I had picked myself up and found my breath again, the horse was gone and so was the man who had saved me. I had a glimpse of him only—a tall fellow, thin, in a shabby brown coat, with a grey comforter about his throat. I called out after him, but the fog and the crowd swallowed him up. I asked the bystanders, but not one of them had marked which way he went.”

“That was unfortunate,” said I. “But you have, I understand, some further clue?”

“I have had him sought for,” Barrowdale returned. “I am a rich man, sir, and I do not forget a debt of that kind. I set my solicitor’s clerk upon it, and afterwards a private inquiry agent, and I have myself spent many an hour in the neighbourhood of Cobb’s Court, asking questions of cabmen and porters and the keepers of the public-houses there. And the upshot of it is this, that I have traced my man to half a dozen places, and at every place he is a different man.”

“How do you mean?”

“I mean that I have five several descriptions of him, all of them tallying in the main—the tall thin figure, the brown coat, the grey comforter—and yet each of them vouched for by a credible witness who saw him at the very hour I am speaking of. On the day of the accident, at the very time when he was pulling me from under the horse’s hoofs in Ludgate Hill, he was seen by a pawnbroker’s man in the Borough, by a ticket-collector at Charing Cross Station, by a woman selling flowers in Covent Garden, and by a warder at the gates of Holloway Prison. There is no possibility of his having been in all those places, and yet I would swear to the truth of every witness.”

I confess that this recital interested me exceedingly. “You have, of course, compared the descriptions?”

“I have them written out,” said he, and he drew a bundle of papers from his pocket. “You will see that they are not identical. The pawnbroker’s man speaks of a gold ring upon the fellow’s left hand. The ticket-collector says that he wore no ring, but that he carried a small black bag. The flower-woman declares that he had a hare-lip. The warder noticed that he stooped a little in the left shoulder.”

I ran my eye over the papers. They were, as he had said, curiously alike and curiously different. “And what is your own impression of the matter?” I asked.

He hesitated, and I saw again that look of something very like fear come into his eyes. “I will be frank with you,” said he at last. “I am not a superstitious man, but I have begun to ask myself whether the man I am seeking is a man at all. There is a story in my own family—” He checked himself, and then went on with an effort. “My father, sir, was a merchant in the China trade, and he brought home with him from Canton a certain image of jade, which he always declared to be the likeness of a spirit that had done him a good turn in a time of peril. It was a little thing, no bigger than a man’s hand, and it stood upon his mantelpiece until the day of his death. I have it still in my study, and I would show it to you, but that it has been missing these three weeks.”

“Missing?”

“Stolen, or mislaid, or gone—I know not how. And it is since it went that I have seen the man in the brown coat. I have seen him, sir, in the street outside my house in Russell Square. I have seen him in the crowd at a public meeting. I have seen him in the fog at my own gate, standing quite still, with his face turned up to my windows. And when I have sent my man out to speak with him, there has been no one there.”

I rose and took down my hat and stick. “Mr. Barrowdale,” said I, “I will ask you to take me to your house at once, and to show me the place where the jade image stood.”

We drove through the thickening fog to Russell Square, and he led me into a handsome study upon the ground floor, where a fire burned in the grate and a lamp was lit upon the table. The mantelpiece was of black marble, and in the centre of it was a clean oval mark in the dust where the image had stood.

“You have had the room swept, I perceive,” said I.

“Not since the loss,” he answered. “I gave orders that nothing should be touched.”

I took out my lens and examined the mantelpiece and the surrounding carpet with some care. The dust upon the marble was undisturbed save for the oval mark, but upon the hearth-rug, close against the fender, I found what I had half expected to find—a few grains of coarse brown sand, such as is used by metal-workers in the casting of small figures. I straightened myself and looked at my client.

“You have had a visitor,” said I, “who came not to steal but to leave something behind him. Have you missed nothing else from this room?”

He stared at me. “Nothing—and yet, now that you speak of it, there was a paper-knife of mine, a common thing of steel and ebony, which I have not seen these three days.”

“Exactly,” said I. “And now, Mr. Barrowdale, I will ask you one question, and I beg that you will answer it without reservation. Is there any man living who has cause to wish you ill?”

His face went a shade paler, and he sank into a chair. “There is one,” said he, in a low voice. “My nephew, Martin Barrowdale. He is a clever, dissolute fellow, and I had intended to make him my heir, but I found him out in a piece of rascality last spring, and I told him plainly that not a penny of my money should ever come to him. He swore that I should repent it. He is a man of strange accomplishments, sir—a chemist, a modeller, a worker in metals—and he has been in Japan.”

“I think,” said I, “that we shall find your brown-coated friend before morning. Will you have the goodness to send your man for a constable, and to have the door in the garden wall watched?”

The affair ended, as such affairs will, in a very commonplace fashion. Martin Barrowdale had constructed, with no little skill, a figure of papier-mâché and wax, jointed and painted, which he could set up in any doorway or behind any pillar, and which, in the fog and the hurry of a London street, might well pass for a living man. The brown coat and grey comforter were the uniform, so to speak, of the phantom, and the several witnesses had seen the figure at the several places where the nephew had planted it, while he himself, in a different dress, had been busy elsewhere. His object was to haunt his uncle into a belief that he was pursued by some supernatural avenger, and so to bring him to a condition in which he might be persuaded to restore the nephew to his favour. The jade image he had taken as a model for the head of his puppet, and the paper-knife had served him for a tool. He was arrested that same night in a little workshop in Clerkenwell, with the waxen mask still upon his bench and the brown coat hanging upon a nail behind the door.

“I am glad to have been of service,” said I, when Barrowdale thanked me with tears in his eyes, “but I would counsel you to remember that gratitude is a dangerous emotion when it is mixed with fear. It was your fear that gave the rogue his opportunity, and it was your gratitude that made you look for a spirit where you should have looked for a man.”
