It was in the autumn of the year ’94, a season of mists and early frosts, that I find among my notes the singular affair of the Chalk Down postmistress. Holmes had been unusually silent for some days, for the summer had yielded no case of moment, and he had fallen into one of those moods of black depression which alternated in his strange nature with the fiercest energy. He sat now with his violin upon his knee, not playing, but staring at the fire as though the embers held the solution of some chemical problem.

“You have a letter, Watson,” said he, without turning his head. “It has lain upon the tray these two hours, and you have not opened it. That argues either a singular indifference to your correspondence or a preoccupation with my sulks, which I do not flatter myself you possess.”

“It is from a village postmistress in Hampshire,” I replied, breaking the seal. “She writes in a hand that is cramped with anxiety, and she begs that I will bring the matter to the attention of Mr. Sherlock Holmes, whose name she has seen in the illustrated papers.”

Holmes held out his hand for the letter, and I watched the quick transformation which always came over him when his mind was presented with a problem. His listlessness fell from him like a cloak. He read the epistle twice, then laid it upon the table and tapped it with his long forefinger.

“The lady is discreet, Watson. Observe that she gives no names, no place, no particulars which could compromise her should the letter fall into other hands. She speaks only of ‘a gentleman of position in our parish,’ of ‘certain communications which arrive in a condition not consistent with the ordinary course of the post,’ and of ‘a man in his employ who has not been seen these three weeks.’ That is the writing of a person who has learned caution—or who has had caution thrust upon her.”

“You will go down?”

“I will go down to-morrow. And you will accompany me, for I fancy the affair may prove to have a darker side than the good lady suspects. A steamed letter and a vanished gamekeeper—there is material there, if one knows how to read it.”

The village of Chalk Down lies some four miles from the nearest railway station, and we drove thither in a fly through a country of bare beech woods and grey, rolling fields. The post-office was a small brick building at the crossroads, with a garden of late cabbages behind it and a sign above the door which announced that Mrs. Hannah Tregellis was licensed to sell stamps and to receive letters for the Royal Mail. The lady herself met us at the threshold—a thin, sharp-featured woman of fifty, with grey eyes that had the quick, suspicious glance of one who has been long upon her guard.

“You are Mr. Holmes,” she said, with a little gasp of relief. “I had hardly dared to hope that you would come yourself. I have said nothing to anyone, sir—not to the rector, not to my own sister. I feared that if it were known I had written to you, the letters might cease to come—or worse.”

“You did wisely,” said Holmes. “Now, tell me everything from the beginning, and omit nothing, however trivial it may seem.”

She led us into the little room behind the shop, where a fire burned in a polished grate and a kettle sang upon the hob. When we were seated, she folded her hands in her lap and began.

“The gentleman I spoke of is Sir George Merridew, of Chalk Down Hall. He is a widower, sir, and has lived here these twenty years. His letters come through my hands, as all the letters of the parish do. About six weeks ago I noticed that the envelope of a letter addressed to him was damp—not wet, you understand, but clammy, as though it had been held over steam. The gum upon the flap had been re-moistened and pressed down again, and the paper was wrinkled at the edges. I said nothing, for I thought it might be an accident of the mail-bag. But the next week it happened again, and the week after that. Always the same—a slight dampness, a wrinkled flap, and once, sir, a faint smell of gum arabic that was not the gum of the Post Office.”

“You are an observant woman,” said Holmes. “Did you examine the letters closely enough to see whether they had been opened and resealed, or whether an attempt had been made and abandoned?”

“They had been opened, sir. I am sure of it. The flap had been lifted and laid down again, and the paper inside was not so crisp as it should have been. And there was another thing. Sir George’s letters come from his solicitor in Winchester, from his brother in India, and from a lady in London whose name I will not speak. But of late there had been one letter each week in a hand I did not know—a hand that wrote from London, with a foreign postmark. It was always among the damp ones.”

Holmes leaned forward. “You have the last of these letters?”

“I have, sir.” She rose and went to a drawer, from which she took a small packet wrapped in oil-cloth. “I kept it back. It came on the Tuesday, and I did not put it in the bag. I thought—I hardly know what I thought—but I kept it.”

Holmes took the envelope and held it to the light. It was addressed in a firm, sloping hand to Sir George Merridew, Bart., Chalk Down Hall, Hampshire. The postmark was London, and the date was the 14th of September. He turned it over, and with a penknife delicately lifted the flap. Inside was a single sheet of paper, covered with writing in the same sloping hand. Holmes read it, and his face grew very grave.

“Watson, this is a letter of menace. It speaks of a debt which must be paid, of a secret which has been kept too long, and of a reckoning which will come before the month is out. It is unsigned, but it is not the work of a common blackmailer. Observe the paper—it is of a quality used by solicitors and men of business. The ink is Indian, and the pen was a fine one. This is a man of education, and a man who knows Sir George Merridew intimately.”

“And the gamekeeper?” I asked.

“The gamekeeper, one William Rudd, disappeared on the night of the 20th of September. He was a quiet, steady man, sir,” said Mrs. Tregellis, “and had been in Sir George’s service for fifteen years. He was last seen at the Hall at six o’clock in the evening, when he went out to set his traps in the Home Wood. He never returned. His cottage was found locked, his supper untouched, and his gun still standing in the corner. There was no sign of a struggle, and no trace of him has been found from that day to this.”

Holmes sat for a long time in silence, his fingers pressed together and his eyes fixed upon the fire. Then he rose abruptly.

“We will go up to the Hall, Mrs. Tregellis. And you will oblige me by saying nothing of our visit to anyone. If Sir George asks why we are here, I shall tell him that I am a collector of old furniture, and that you have been good enough to recommend his cabinets. Watson, you will be my friend from London who is interested in Chippendale.”

The Hall was a long, low building of red brick, with a park of ancient oaks behind it and a drive of gravel that crunched beneath our feet. Sir George Merridew received us in the library—a florid, heavy man of sixty, with a red face and a nervous manner that ill-matched his bluff appearance. He spoke loudly and heartily of his furniture, but his eyes wandered to the window whenever a twig snapped in the wind, and once, when a door banged in the passage, he started as though he had been struck.

Holmes played his part admirably, praising the cabinets and asking questions about their history. But I saw his eye travel over the room—over the desk with its litter of papers, over the decanter of sherry upon the sideboard, over the muddy boots that stood by the hearth. And when Sir George turned to fetch a key from a drawer, Holmes stooped swiftly and picked up a small object from the carpet—a brass cartridge-case, such as is used in a rook-rifle.

He said nothing until we were in the fly again, driving back to the station. Then he took the cartridge-case from his pocket and held it up.

“This was not dropped by a sportsman, Watson. It is of a type used by the gamekeepers of this estate, and it has been recently fired. Sir George Merridew told me that he had not been out with a gun for a month, and that Rudd was the only man who used a rook-rifle upon the place. Yet this case was lying upon the library carpet, and there were muddy boots by the hearth which were not Sir George’s size.”

“You think the gamekeeper has been in the house since his disappearance?”

“I think, Watson, that the gamekeeper has not disappeared at all. I think he has been hidden—or has hidden himself—and that Sir George Merridew knows more of the matter than he chooses to tell. The steamed letters, the threatening note, the vanished man, and the cartridge-case—these are not separate threads. They are the strands of a single rope, and that rope is knotted about the throat of Sir George Merridew.”

He was silent for a mile, and then he added, in a lower voice:

“To-morrow we shall return, and we shall not go to the Hall. We shall go to the Home Wood, and we shall find what William Rudd was doing on the night of the 20th of September. And if I am not mistaken, we shall find it in the old ice-house which stands at the end of the beech avenue—the one building upon the estate which Sir George did not offer to show us.”

The train came in, and we were borne back to London through the gathering dusk. But I knew that the affair was not ended, and that Holmes, with the cartridge-case in his pocket and the letter of menace in his mind, was already upon the trail.
