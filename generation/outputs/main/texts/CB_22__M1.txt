The wind, a raw and restless spirit from the northern sea, had swept the narrow street of the Sint-Maartenswijk clear of all but its own whistle. It rattled the leaded panes of the house I had come to as Mrs. Eleanor Vance, housekeeper to the foreign doctor. The house was a tall, gabled thing, squeezed between a dyer’s shop and a guildhall gone to seed, and it held within its stone bones the chill of the ages. I had been a widow for two years, but the cold I felt now was not the familiar emptiness of my own hearth; it was the cold of a stranger’s land, a stranger’s tongue, and the stranger’s house I was now bound to serve.

Dr. Verhaegen was a man of great stillness. He possessed the fine, dark eyes and hawk-like profile of the old Flemish portraits, but his hands were the hands of a labourer, broad and capable, stained faintly with the chemicals of his trade. He ran a clinic for the poor from the ground floor of the house, and the scent of carbolic acid and suffering clung to the very woodwork. He had hired me with few words, his gaze seeming to read the lines of exhaustion and grief upon my face with a physician’s frankness. “You are English,” he had stated, not as a question. “They say the English are methodical. I require order, and I require compassion. Can you furnish both?” I had answered that I would endeavour to do so.

His sister, however, was a different matter. Mademoiselle Annelise Verhaegen was a creature of sharp angles and sharper glances. She moved through the house like a bird of prey, her black skirts rustling with a sound like dry leaves. She was the household’s priestess, and I, a Protestant, was an infidel in her sanctuary. She tested me daily, not with open accusation, but with a hundred small, probing checks. She would ask me the price of the fish, the state of my late husband’s soul, the name of my parish in England. To each, I gave a plain, direct answer, offering no more than was asked.

One evening, the storm that had been brewing all day broke with a violence that shook the very foundations of the town. Rain lashed the windows like thrown gravel. Dr. Verhaegen was late, summoned to the fever-ridden quarter by the Scheldt. Mademoiselle Annelise and I sat in the kitchen, the only warm room in the house, a great fire of peat and oak logs roaring in the cavernous hearth. The silence between us was a living thing, taut and watchful. I was mending a torn shirt of the doctor’s, my needle flying with a steady rhythm that belied the turmoil in my own breast.

Suddenly, a great crash of thunder, so close it seemed to split the sky directly above us, plunged the house into an abrupt and profound darkness. The fire, for a moment, seemed to shrink and cower. I heard Mademoiselle Annelise draw in a sharp breath. In the gloom, her voice, usually so controlled, held a tremor.

“The candles,” she said. “On the mantel. Be quick.”

I rose and felt my way along the wall, my fingers brushing against the cold stone. The wind howled like a banshee down the chimney, and the firelight cast wild, leaping shadows that made the familiar room a place of Gothic horror. I found the tinderbox and, with hands that would not shake, struck the flint. The spark caught, and I lit the first candle, then the second. As the small flames grew, pushing back the oppressive dark, I saw Mademoiselle Annelise’s face, pale and stark in the unsteady light. Her eyes were fixed upon me, and in them I saw not the usual disdain, but a raw, unguarded fear.

“You are not afraid of the storm,” she said. It was not a question. Her gaze was piercing, as if she sought to read my very soul. “Most women would have cried out. You are… very still.”

“I have known worse storms, Mademoiselle,” I replied, my voice low and even as I returned to my seat.

She took a step closer, the firelight glinting on the silver crucifix at her throat. “In England? I think there is a storm in you, Mrs. Vance. A storm you keep locked away. You are a widow, you say, yet you wear no ring. You have the manners of a gentlewoman, yet you seek a post of service. You answer my questions about God with the silence of a heretic. Who are you, truly? Why did you leave England?”

The force of her inquiry was like a physical blow. Every instinct screamed at me to retreat behind the wall of polite reserve I had so carefully constructed. But the fury of the storm without seemed to have broken the locks upon my own heart. A surge of hot resentment rose in my throat. I had fled one form of tyranny; I would not bow to another.

“I left England, Mademoiselle,” I said, my voice rising with a passion I could no longer suppress, “because the house I lived in was a tomb, and the man I was married to was its living ghost. I left because my conscience would no longer permit me to be a party to injustice. I am a Protestant, yes, but I believe my soul to be as precious in the sight of God as any Catholic’s. I came here to work, to live in peace, and to be judged by the labour of my hands and the truth of my character, not by the suspicions of a stranger!”

The words hung in the air between us, crackling like the fire. Mademoiselle Annelise stared at me, her lips parted. I had shown her a glimpse of the fire within, and I had expected her to recoil, to dismiss me on the spot. But she did not. Her gaze softened, and for the first time, I saw not a persecutor, but a woman as guarded and as wounded as myself. The front door opened then, letting in a gust of wind and rain, and Dr. Verhaegen stood there, a dark silhouette against the storm, his eyes finding mine across the room. The test, I knew, was not over. But for the first time, I felt I might not fail it.
