The drawing-room door at Thornecleave stood half ajar. I had come to seek a book of scripture Mr. Carris had lent me, but the sound of my own name, spoken in Mrs. Carris's cool, unembarrassed tone, arrested my step upon the Turkey carpet. I stood in the shadow of the heavy oak portière, neither seen nor heard.

"She is useful, Edmund," Mrs. Carris was saying, "and the children are attached to her in their rough way, but she is dreadfully plain. That grey gown of hers hangs like a sack. One cannot expect her to move through the rooms like a piece of furniture that one has grown accustomed to, but I confess I wish she were less visible."

"A piece of furniture," Mr. Carris repeated, with the low, easy laugh of a man who supposes himself witty. "A somewhat awkward article, I grant you, but solid enough. She does not creak, she does not demand polishing, and she costs remarkably little. Let us keep her, Helena."

I did not wait for more. I turned, and walked back down the long gallery that led to the schoolroom, my blood beating in my ears. The words followed me. *A piece of furniture.* I thought of my four years in this house. I thought of the nights I had sat beside little Arthur's bed when the fever was upon him. I thought of the Latin exercises, the mended frocks, the swallowed pride. I thought of God, who made me, and of these people, who used me. My hands trembled, and I pressed them hard against my bosom until the trembling ceased. I would not weep. I would not.

It was past midnight when the knocking came. Three sharp, frantic blows upon my door, and the voice of the housemaid, Martha, shrill with terror. I was out of bed in an instant, thrusting my arms into my shawl. "Miss Aird! Miss Aird, you must come! The young master!"

Young master was Master Philip, the youngest, a child of five, slight and pale and singularly loved. I found him in the nursery, and the sight of him struck through me like a blade. He lay across his nurse's lap, his small body convulsed, his face dark and suffocated. The croup had come on with a ferocity I had never witnessed, and never wish to witness again. Mrs. Carris knelt upon the floor in her dressing-gown, her hands gripping the counterpane, her face emptied of all vanity and purpose. Mr. Carris stood at the hearth, ashen, impotent.

"The doctor," I said. "Has someone gone?"

"Two hours since," Martha sobbed. "But the roads are flooded, Miss Aird. He cannot come."

I looked at the child. I knew what the child needed. I had seen this sickness before, in my mother's cottage, when my youngest brother had fought for breath against the same merciless grip. I knew the steam, the heat, the steady, unrelenting vigilance that could pull a small creature back from the edge of the dark. I knew, and they did not, and the knowledge sat in me like a stone.

"Bring water," I said. "Boiling water, every kettle in the house. Bring blankets, and the camp-stool from the library. I will take him."

For one moment Mrs. Carris looked up at me, and I saw her hesitate. I saw the proud woman shrink before the poor dependent she had called an awkward article. She knew, as I knew, that I had heard her. Something in my face told her. But her child was dying, and pride is a brittle shield against death.

She yielded. "Do what you can," she whispered.

I took the boy. He was burning, a small, rigid furnace of pain, and his breath came in that terrible, grating gasp that tears the heart of anyone who has heard it. I built the tent of blankets over the camp-stool, and beneath it I placed the basins of scalding water, and I sat with him in my arms inside that close, humid darkness, and I breathed, and I prayed, and I held on.

The hours passed. The wind threw itself against the nursery windows with a sound like the sea striking a cliff. The candles guttered and were replaced. Mrs. Carris sat on the floor outside the tent, watching, not speaking. Once, near dawn, I lifted the blanket and looked at the child's face, and saw that the convulsions had eased, that the terrible labour of his breathing had slowed to something nearer to sleep. I laid my hand upon his chest and felt the faint, rhythmic beating of his heart, steady now, persistent, a small stubborn flame that refused to be extinguished.

"He will live," I said.

Mrs. Carris made no answer. She put her face down upon her folded arms, and I knew she was weeping.

I did not sleep that night, nor the morning that followed. When the doctor arrived at last, muddy and weary, he found the boy resting and looked at me with a curiosity I did not trouble myself to answer. I went down to the schoolroom. I sat at my desk. I folded my hands. And I reckoned with myself.

I could go. I had saved the child, and I had earned my dismissal. I could pack my trunk, walk down the drive, and take the coach from Miller's Dale, and never look upon Thornecleave again. I had a little money saved. I had references. I had my youth, though it was a youth grown sober and watchful, stripped early of its illusions. I could go, and in going, I would preserve the one thing that no salary could purchase and no kindness could restore: my own sense that I was a soul, not an object.

But then I thought of Arthur, who would cry when he found me gone. I thought of Philip, who had clung to my hand in the dark. I thought of the scripture that had sustained my mother through her widowhood, the verse she had read to me on the night before she died: *I will not leave you comfortless; I will come to you.* I knew that God does not call us only to the easy roads. I knew that some lives are given over to a service that is unglorified, unacknowledged, and hard, and that this does not make them worthless. It makes them, rather, a kind of silent warfare, a stubborn standing of ground in a place where the adversary is not cruelty but indifference.

I thought of Mrs. Carris, kneeling on the nursery floor. She had called me furniture, and then she had watched me save her son, and she had not spoken a word of gratitude. But I had seen her face. I had seen the shame in it. And I knew that shame, however insufficient, was a crack in the wall through which something might one day enter.

I would stay. Not because I forgave them, and not because I forgot. I would stay because Philip needed me, and because I was not made for retreat. I would stay, and I would hold my head up in that house, and I would make them see me, not as the thing they had supposed, but as the woman God had made: poor, plain, and unowned, but standing.
