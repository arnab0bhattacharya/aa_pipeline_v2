The business of the Tonic was a curious thing. I have often thought it a perfect microcosm of the age, a distillation of our national genius for applying science to the service of credulity. My uncle, Mr. Josiah Pargiter, had built the entire edifice upon a foundation of newspaper columns and a bottle of faintly brown, faintly sweet liquid. He called it Pargiter’s Primal Nectar, and the advertisements, which he composed himself with a poet’s fury, declared it to be “The Elixir of Modern Vigour,” a restorative derived from a secret combination of Peruvian bark, rare mountain herbs, and what he vaguely termed “vital ozone.”

I, his nephew, Arthur, had been the Nectar’s first and most devoted salesman, its chief chemist—though there was little enough chemistry involved—and its accountant. I had walked the streets of Birmingport with a Gladstone bag full of samples, and I had learned to speak with a straight face of its miraculous properties to drapers and ironmongers. I knew, with the certainty of a man who has mixed the stuff by the barrelful, that its chief ingredients were water, sugar, a flavouring of gentian, and a cheap tincture of quinine. It did no harm, and it did a great deal of good to our bank balance. But as the business grew from a back-room enterprise into a national institution with its own offices and a printing press, a strange shame began to settle upon me. I admired my uncle immensely. His energy was a force of nature, his optimism a kind of weather. Yet I could not shake the feeling that we were engaged in a magnificent, audacious fraud.

It was therefore with a sense of impending absurdity that I learned of his new ambition. We were in the London office, a marvel of mahogany and brass, overlooking the Holborn Viaduct. Pargiter was standing before a large map of the Midlands, a pointer in his hand.

“Arthur,” he said, his voice ringing with the same conviction he used to sell a case of Nectar, “it is time we took our message to the very heart of the nation. The House of Commons!”

I confess I stared at him. “Parliament, Uncle?”

“Parliament!” he confirmed, jabbing the pointer at a smudged circle on the map. “The seat of Fenton-upon-Trent. They need a man of practical energy, a man who understands the modern world. They have had enough of these powdered-wig lawyers and land-owners. They need a manufacturer!”

I attempted a gentle objection. “But the Nectar, sir… the, ah, the political world is a rather different sort of business. It is not a matter of… efficacy.”

He laughed, a great booming sound that seemed to rattle the glass-fronted cabinets. “Nonsense, my boy! It is precisely the same! What is politics but the art of selling an idea to the public? We shall sell them the idea of Pargiter! Progress! Prosperity! We shall stand for the Self-Made Man against the privileged few! And,” he added, leaning in with a conspiratorial wink, “we shall have the finest advertising campaign this country has ever seen.”

And so we went to Fenton-upon-Trent. It was a town of soot and steam, a place of endless brick terraces and the ceaseless clangour of the great Piggott & Sons engineering works. The air tasted of coal. My uncle, however, saw none of the grime. He saw only opportunity. He was magnificent on the stump. Standing on a crate in the market square, with his tweed suit and his booming voice, he held the crowds spellbound.

“They tell you the Empire was built on British pluck!” he would roar. “I tell you it was built on British vigour! And where does vigour come from? It comes from a healthy constitution! It comes from a man who knows how to restore his vital forces after a long day’s labour! It comes from a man like me, who has made it his life’s work to provide that very thing!”

He never mentioned the Nectar by name, of course. It was all suggestion, all implication. He was the living proof of his own product. The working men, grimy and tired, looked up at him and saw a man who had wrested a fortune from the world through sheer force of will. They cheered him. They roared their approval. His opponent, a dull Liberal barrister named Finch, was drowned out by the sheer vitality of the man.

I stood at the back of the crowds, my collar pulled up against the Midlands damp, and felt a profound and complicated misery. I was proud of him. His command of an audience was an art form, a thing of genius. He was turning politics into a pantomime, and the people loved him for it. Yet every cheer felt like a fresh indictment of our enterprise. We were peddling a myth of vigour to men whose real vigour was being spent twelve hours a day in the ironworks. The irony was so thick you could have cut it with a knife.

The climax came at the grand hustings in the Fenton Corn Exchange. The hall was packed, hot, and smelling of wet wool and beer. Mr. Finch spoke first, a dry, logical discourse on tariffs and municipal reform. He was met with polite, intermittent applause. Then my uncle took the stage. He did not have notes. He did not need them.

“Fellow Englishmen!” he began, and the room fell silent. “They speak to you of fiscal policy! I speak to you of the fundamental policy of life! The policy of a man’s own body! Of his own spirit! What good is a tariff on corn if a man is too weary to eat it? What good is a municipal gas-works if a man has not the energy to read his evening paper by its light?”

It was nonsense, of course. It was a glorious, rousing, complete and utter non-sequitur. But it was delivered with such fire, such an earnest belief in its own truth, that it swept through the hall like a physical force. He spoke of the decline of the old aristocracy and the rise of the new man, the man of enterprise, the man who got on. He was a walking advertisement for himself.

“I have made my way in the world not by birth, but by force of character!” he concluded, his voice cracking with emotion. “And I tell you that the strength of this nation lies not in its blue blood, but in its red blood! In its vigour! In its capacity for work and for life! Give me your votes, and I will bring that spirit to Westminster! I will be your champion!”

The roar that went up was deafening. Men stamped their feet and waved their hats. It was a triumph. As the noise subsided, the returning officer called for questions. A small, bespectacled man in the front row stood up, looking like a schoolmaster.

“A question for Mr. Pargiter,” he said, his voice thin but clear. “Sir, you speak of vigour. You speak of national health. As a chemist myself, I have followed your remarkable career. I wish to ask you, as a man of science, to state for this assembly the precise physiological mechanism by which your… your tonic… is supposed to augment the vital forces you so eloquently describe.”

A sudden, absolute silence fell over the hall. Every eye turned from the little chemist to the great man on the stage. The question hung in the air, sharp and awkward and utterly devastating. It was the question I had never dared to ask.

My uncle did not flinch. He smiled, a broad, confident, magnificent smile. He looked from the questioner to the crowd, and then he leaned forward towards the microphone.

“Sir,” he boomed, his voice filled with a perfect imitation of scientific authority, “I am a manufacturer, not a metaphysician! I deal in results! And the result of Pargiter’s Primal Nectar is written on the faces of a million happy, healthy Britons!”

The crowd, released from its tension, erupted once more. They had not understood the question, and they did not care for the answer. They had their man. He had faced down a pedant with the same vigour he faced down the world. They carried him out of the hall on their shoulders that night, Josiah Pargiter, the next Member of Parliament for Fenton-upon-Trent. I followed in his wake, the scent of coal smoke and victory in my nostrils, feeling more ashamed of my uncle, and more proud of him, than I had ever thought possible.
