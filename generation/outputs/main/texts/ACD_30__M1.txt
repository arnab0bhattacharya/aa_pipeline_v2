It was in the autumn of the year '93 that my friend, Mr. Sherwood, was called upon to investigate a matter of a vexing and peculiar nature at the stables of Mr. Alistair Finch, a trainer of some repute upon the Newmarket Heath. The affair concerned a magnificent chestnut colt of the name of Windward Light, whose performances in public were as lamentable as his private trials were brilliant. The creature, a son of a Derby winner, possessed all the fire and speed of his lineage in the privacy of the gallops, but upon a racecourse he would run listlessly, his spirit seemingly extinguished, to the great chagrin of his owner and the bewilderment of all who saw him.

Mr. Finch, a man whose face was a roadmap of the weather and the turf, received us in his study. The room was furnished with sporting prints and the faint, pleasant aroma of good shag tobacco.

“I have had the feed tested, Mr. Sherwood,” he began, without preamble, “by the best chemist in Cambridge. It is pure. The water is from my own well. My lads have been with me for years; they would lie down in the mud for that colt. And young Perkins, his jockey, is an honest lad if ever there was one. He rides for the love of the horse, not the silver.”

“And yet,” Sherwood remarked, his gaze falling upon a framed photograph of the colt, “the horse fails. The problem, then, is not one of substance, but of circumstance. He is sound in body but not in mind. Tell me, what is the difference between a trial and a race?”

“Why, the crowd, the noise, the journey… everything!” Finch exclaimed.

“Precisely,” said Sherwood, a thin smile touching his lips. “It is in that difference that our answer lies. I should like to remain here for a week, if you would be so good. I shall pose as a gentleman with an interest in bloodstock. I will need a bed in the village and liberty to walk the stables at any hour.”

And so it was. For seven days, Sherwood was a fixture at the establishment. He watched the colt work upon the heath, its long, raking stride a picture of equine perfection. He saw the lads rub him down, their hands gentle and sure. He observed the feed, the water, the fresh straw in the stall. He spoke little, but his eyes, that grey and restless pair, missed nothing. His attention, however, seemed chiefly directed not at the horse, but at the comings and goings of the establishment after the sun had dipped below the horizon.

On the evening of the seventh day, he came to my rooms in the village. His face was set in that keen, predatory expression I knew so well.

“Tonight, Watson,” he said, pulling on his driving gloves, “we shall attend the theatre. Finch has entered Windward Light in the Two-Year-Old Plate at the Rowley Mile tomorrow. We must see the final act of the drama before the curtain rises.”

We took a trap out to the stables, arriving well after ten o’clock. The moon was a thin sliver, and a damp chill hung in the air. Sherwood led me not to the main yard, but around to a row of loose boxes at the rear of the property. He placed a finger to his lips and pointed towards a small, isolated box where the champion was kept.

For an hour we stood in the deep shadow of an elm, silent and motionless. The only sounds were the rustle of the wind in the trees and the occasional stamp of a horse. Then, a sound of footsteps on the gravel path. A figure emerged from the darkness, a man in a heavy overcoat and a bowler hat pulled low. He moved with a practiced silence, glancing about him before slipping the bolt on the colt’s stall and disappearing inside.

“Who is it?” I whispered.

“His name is Graves,” Sherwood breathed back. “A man of no fixed profession, but with a certain talent for animals. He was once a veterinary student, I am told, before he was sent down for some unpleasantness with a dissecting knife.”

We crept closer, treading softly upon the damp grass. A faint light glowed within the box. Peering through a crack in the timber door, we saw the man, Graves, holding a small, dark lantern. The colt stood quietly, its great head turned towards him. Graves moved to its side and, with a swift motion, produced a short, thick syringe from his coat pocket. He jabbed it into the soft flesh behind the colt’s ear. The horse flinched, but did not cry out. The man then produced a small vial and a dropper, and administered a few drops of some liquid into the horse’s mouth.

“Now,” Sherwood murmured, “for the final flourish.”

Graves took from his pocket a small tin box. He opened it, and with a pair of tweezers, he carefully extracted a tiny, dark object. It was a seed, no bigger than a grain of rice. He then lifted the colt’s tail and, with a motion that was both quick and horribly intimate, inserted the seed into the animal’s rectum. He wiped his hands on a rag, pocketed his instruments, and slipped out of the stall as silently as he had entered.

As the man’s footsteps faded, Sherwood pushed the door open and entered the box. The colt, now agitated, stamped and snorted. Sherwood held up his lantern, its beam falling upon the horse’s flank.

“There, Watson,” he said, his voice tight with triumph. He pointed to the spot where the syringe had been. “A puncture mark, fresh and clean. A mild sedative, enough to take the edge off his courage without leaving a trace by morning. But that is the lesser part of the villainy.”

He then turned his attention to the rear of the animal. “And this is the masterstroke. A seed of the *Datura stramonium*—the thorn-apple. A common weed, but its properties are most irritating. It will cause a burning, a desperate discomfort that will build throughout a race. The horse will not be lame, he will not be ill, but he will be consumed by a fire in his belly that no rider can soothe. He will be frantic to be rid of it, and in his agony, he will forget to run.”

The next day, the Rowley Mile was a sea of colour and noise. Windward Light was the favourite, his odds short. But as the horses cantered to the post, Sherwood, who stood beside me with a pair of field-glasses, gave a quiet exclamation.

“Observe Perkins, the jockey,” he said.

I looked. The lad sat his horse well, but his face was pale, and he kept glancing towards a particular spot in the crowd. Following his gaze, I saw the man, Graves, leaning upon the rail, a grim smile on his face.

“The poor boy is being watched,” Sherwood said. “The last link in the chain.”

The race began. For the first furlong, Windward Light ran true to form, his great stride eating up the ground. He took the lead with an ease that drew a roar from the crowd. But then, as they passed the half-mile, the change began. The colt’s head began to toss. His gait became ragged. He swerved violently to the left, then to the right, nearly unseating Perkins. The jockey fought to control him, but the horse was in a panic, his eyes wide with a terror that was not of the race. He finished a miserable last, lathered in sweat and trembling.

A roar of dismay went up from the stands. We, however, did not wait to see the aftermath. Sherwood was already moving, his long legs carrying him swiftly through the throng. He did not go to the winner’s enclosure, but straight to the stable entrance, where a constable, summoned by wire that morning, stood waiting.

They found Mr. Graves in the stable yard, a triumphant look upon his face. It turned to one of stark disbelief as the constable took him by the arm. Sherwood stepped forward, holding up a small, leather-cased hypodermic syringe.

“I believe this is your property, sir,” he said. “It was found in your rooms this morning. The puncture on the horse’s neck will match its needle, and I have no doubt that a chemist will find traces of chloral hydrate within it. As for the colt’s distress, a simple examination will reveal the cause.”

Graves, a hard man, said nothing. He merely glared at Sherwood with a hatred that was quite murderous. Later, in Finch’s study, the story was pieced together. Graves, it transpired, had made a fortune betting against the colt in every one of its public races. He had perfected a method to ensure its defeat, a method that left no permanent mark and was beyond the ken of the average veterinary surgeon.

“A cunning scheme, Finch,” Sherwood said, accepting a glass of sherry. “It played upon the trust between man and horse. The villain did not attack the animal’s body, but its spirit. He turned its own nervous temperament against it.”

Mr. Finch looked out at the yard, where Windward Light was being walked by his faithful lad. The colt’s head was low, but the wildness had gone from his eyes.

“The poor creature,” the trainer said softly. “To be betrayed in such a fashion. What happens to him now?”

“Now,” said Sherwood, a rare, genuine smile on his face, “he will run. And I fancy he will win. The only shadow upon the track today was the one cast by that rogue, and it has been lifted for good.”
