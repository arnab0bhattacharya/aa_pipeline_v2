The sign of The Jolly Cockerel, which for eleven months of the year displayed a bird of such swollen and self-satisfied aspect that it seemed to have fattened upon the subscriptions of the entire parish, now hung at a doleful angle, and its painted eye, encountering the fog, wore an expression of the deepest mortification. Within, the tap-room presented that peculiar desolation which attends upon betrayed expectations: the benches empty, the hearth cold, the pewter dull, and the great spigot of the beer-barrel turned inward upon nothing, like a man who has told his last story and found no one to laugh. Huddled at the far end of the room, about a table which bore no refreshment whatsoever—a circumstance without precedent in the annals of the club—sat some dozen members of the Goose Club of Camden Town, assembled upon Christmas Eve to divide the fruits of their year’s thrift, and discovering that the fruits had been gathered by another hand.

Mr. Obadiah Grubb, the publican, had been a man of expansive manner and a waistcoat of surpassing splendour, who shook hands with every customer as though conferring a knighthood, and who had kept the club’s monies in a drawer of which he alone possessed the key, observing, with a jovial wink, that a goose was safest in the goose-house. The goose-house, upon inquiry, was found to be empty; and Mr. Grubb himself, upon further inquiry, was found to be in Boulogne, or Calais, or some other foreign stew of wickedness, whither he had repaired with the club’s forty-seven pounds, twelve shillings, and sixpence, leaving behind him a wife in hysterics, a parrot that swore, and a reputation for hospitality which had been the wonder of the neighbourhood.

“Forty-seven pound,” said Mrs. Pellick, a laundress of small stature and immense indignation, who had washed for the club and been paid in promises, “and I stood godmother to his second, and lent him my mangle when his was broke! Forty-seven pound, and me with four to feed and a fifth expected!”

“It ain’t the money,” said Mr. Toby Vane, a journeyman cooper, in the hollow voice of a man rehearsing an argument he did not believe, “it’s the principle. The principle, Mrs. Pellick. A goose is a goose, but a principle—” He waved his hand, and finding nothing to wave it at, let it fall upon the table with a melancholy thump.

“Principle be blowed,” said Mrs. Pellick. “I want my goose.”

There arose a murmur of concurrence, in which the word “goose” recurred with the frequency of a bell in a fog, and with much the same effect of mournful and monotonous insistence. For these were poor people, every one; people to whom a goose was not a commonplace of the season, but a positive event, a thing dreamed of through November and counted upon through December; people who had denied themselves ounces and half-ounces, pints and half-pints, through fifty-two weeks, that they might sit down upon Christmas Day to a bird of their own, and not to the charity of a neighbour or the bounty of a soup-kitchen. And now the bird had flown, and taken the stuffing with it.

At the head of the table, or what would have been the head had the table possessed a head, sat Mr. Silas Quill, the lamplighter, a man of seventy years, whose face was so weathered by a lifetime of London fog and London frost that it resembled a much-thumbed map of a country where the roads were all wrinkles and the rivers all soot. He had lit the lamps of Camden Town for forty years, and had a way of looking at people as though measuring the length of their wick. He had said nothing during the uproar, but had sat with his chin upon his stick, his old eyes travelling from face to face with a certain speculative benevolence.

“Mr. Quill,” said Mrs. Pellick, rounding upon him, “you’re the oldest here. What’s to be done?”

Mr. Quill removed his chin from his stick, and his stick from the floor, and having established it in a new position, as though it were a lamp-post he intended to light, he spoke.

“When I was a boy,” said Mr. Quill, “which is a good while ago, and in a part of London that’s been pulled down twice since, there was a man as kept a pie-shop, and he ran away with the savings of a burial club. There was a mortial outcry, I recollect, and the members was for hanging him out of a window; but an old party as had the gift of the gab—a cobbler he was, with a squint and a wooden leg—he ups and says, ‘Neighbours, we can’t bury the man, but we can bury our grudge, and that’s cheaper.’ And what do you think they did?”

“Starved?” suggested Mr. Vane, gloomily.

“They made a coffin,” said Mr. Quill, “out of an old chest, and they put the club’s books in it, and they buried ’em in the cobbler’s back-garden, with a stone as said, ‘Here lies the Goose that laid no eggs.’ And then they had a supper of bread and cheese, and they sang ‘The Mistletoe Bough,’ and they was all the better friends for it.”

A silence followed, in which the fog seemed to press more closely against the windows.

“That’s all very well,” said Mrs. Pellick, “but bread and cheese ain’t goose.”

“No,” said Mr. Quill, “it ain’t. But it’s better than nothing, and it’s a good deal better than quarrelling. Now I’ll tell you what I’ll do. I’ve got a goose.”

Every eye was upon him in an instant.

“You’ve got a goose?” cried Mrs. Pellick. “Where?”

“In my basket,” said Mr. Quill, “and a fine fat fellow he is, and he cost me eighteenpence at Leadenhall, and I’ve been fattening of him these three weeks on the scraps of the parish. He was to have been my Christmas dinner, and I’ve looked forward to him, I don’t mind telling you, with a good deal of pleasure. But I’m seventy, and I’ve had a good many Christmas dinners, and there’s them here as has had fewer. So I’ll tell you what: let’s have a raffle for him. A penny a chance. Every member of the club as puts in a penny stands to win the goose, and the rest of us’ll go home and say we’ve had our fun, and no one the worse.”

There was a moment’s pause, and then a great clapping of hands and stamping of feet, and Mrs. Pellick was seen to be wiping her eyes with the corner of her apron, and Mr. Vane was heard to observe that it was the rummest go he had ever known, and that he would stand a penny for the sake of the principle. The pennies were produced—some from pockets, some from the lining of hats, one from the toe of a boot—and Mr. Quill, having set down his basket and produced from it a goose of such respectable dimensions that it seemed to fill the entire room with its promise, wrote the names of the subscribers upon scraps of paper, and put them into his old battered hat.

“Now,” said Mr. Quill, “who’ll draw?”

“You draw,” said Mrs. Pellick. “You’re the honestest man in Camden Town.”

Mr. Quill, with a solemnity befitting the occasion, thrust his hand into the hat, rummaged among the papers, and drew one forth. He unfolded it, and held it to the light of the one candle the club could afford.

“Mrs. Pellick,” said he.

The good woman stared, and then burst into tears, and then into laughter, and then into a fresh flood of tears, declaring that she had never won anything in her life, and that she didn’t know what to say, and that she would send Mr. Quill a slice of the breast, and that she hoped he would live to be a hundred. And Mr. Quill, having restored the goose to his basket with a gentle pat, observed that he had no doubt it would be a very fine bird, and that he had always said so, and that he was glad it had gone to a good home.

The club dispersed into the fog, and Mr. Quill, taking up his basket and his stick, went out to light the lamps of Camden Town, which stood in the murky evening like a row of old friends waiting to be waked. And as he went from post to post, touching them one by one into a warm and cheerful glow, he chuckled to himself, and said, “Eighteenpence, and a penny a chance, and twelve chances—why, I’ve made a profit of sixpence, and a good supper into the bargain.” Which was not, perhaps, the most exalted view of the transaction, but it was Mr. Quill’s view, and he was a lamplighter, and accustomed to look at things from the top of a ladder.
