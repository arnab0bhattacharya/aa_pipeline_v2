Mrs. Merriweather’s establishment in Clapham was, as she often observed to the gas-meter man, a ship upon a troubled sea. She was its captain, cook, navigator, and, most exhaustingly, its confessor. The house on Gables Road took in young men of the clerical profession, and since a clerk’s life is one of quiet desperation punctuated by small economies, they found in their widowed landlady a vast and sympathetic repository for their anxieties. It was a matter of professional pride with her that she could recite the salary, ambitions, and digestive ailments of every soul under her roof.

The two brightest stars in this particular firmament were Mr. Penhaligon of the Imperial & Colonial Shipping Co. and Mr. Finchley of Messrs. Gable, Finch & Thorne, Solicitors. They occupied adjacent rooms on the first floor and, until the autumn of 1903, had existed in a state of amiable competition over the quality of Mrs. Merriweather’s mutton broth and the speed of their respective promotions. This state of affairs was irrevocably altered by the arrival of a Miss Petunia Evans at the shipping office. Miss Evans, it was understood, operated a Remington typewriter with a speed and accuracy that bordered on the miraculous. More to the point, she possessed a laugh like the chiming of small bells and a way of looking up from her work that could, as Mr. Finchley put it in a moment of uncharacteristic poetry, “short-circuit a man’s entire logical apparatus.”

The trouble began, as it so often does, with a box of candied violets. Mr. Penhaligon, whose courage in financial matters was not matched in affairs of the heart, had left the confection upon Miss Evans’s desk with a note of such tortuous formality it might have been a bill of lading. He then spent the entire afternoon in a state of nervous collapse, which he confided to Mrs. Merriweather over his tea. He was just describing the precise shade of Miss Evans’s eyes when Mr. Finchley came down, his face a mask of thunderous gloom. He had, he announced, been rebuffed. Miss Evans, it seemed, had explained to him with the greatest kindness that her affections were already engaged.

“Engaged! To whom?” Mr. Penhaligon had squeaked, a dreadful suspicion dawning.

“To a gentleman of sterling character and considerable professional promise,” Mr. Finchley had replied, glaring at his rival. “A man, I might add, who has the good sense not to pester a lady with solicitor’s jargon.”

The atmosphere in the boarding house grew as thick as a London fog. The two clerks, who had previously shared the newspaper and opinions on the Boer War, now communicated only through Mrs. Merriweather.

“He tells you he is to be made a junior partner, I suppose,” Mr. Finchley would say to her in the hallway, loud enough to carry. “The man counts shipping manifests for a living. Where is the intellectual rigour?”

Mr. Penhaligon, for his part, would corner her in the kitchen. “He thinks because he can quote a legal precedent, he is a superior being. But has he ever had to calculate the displacement tonnage of a cargo steamer in a gale? I think not.”

Mrs. Merriweather, who had buried a husband with a far more practical turn of mind, felt a weary sort of pity for them both. She ladled out soup and dispensed platitudes, all the while managing the household’s small crises—a leaking cistern, a burnt-out gas mantle, the butcher’s boy who was getting fresh with the parlour maid. She was so occupied with the delicate diplomacy of the Penhaligon-Finchley war that the third great drama of the house crept up on her almost without her noticing.

This concerned young Mr. Abernathy of the Third Consolidated Bank. He was a quiet, mouse-like creature who kept to himself and was meticulous in his payments. His tragedy, therefore, was all the more shocking. It arrived in the form of two burly detectives from Scotland Yard, who stood in the hall one Tuesday morning, their wet boots sullying the linoleum. The charge was embezzlement. A sum of some four hundred pounds had vanished from the bank’s ledgers, and the trail, faint as it was, led to Mr. Abernathy’s desk. He was led away, pale and trembling, his spectacles askew, protesting his innocence in a whisper no one could hear.

The house was thrown into a profound gloom. The newsboys’ cries of “Bank Clerk in the Dock!” seemed to follow Mrs. Merriweather wherever she went. And then, to make matters worse, the two rivals for Miss Evans’s hand came to her with a new and terrible problem.

“It’s a question of her honour, you see,” said Mr. Penhaligon, pacing the small sitting-room. “Her name is in the papers. They are implying she was somehow… an influence.”

“A ‘feminine distraction,’ the *Standard* called it,” growled Mr. Finchley. “As if a woman’s affection is a criminal motive. It’s an outrage.”

They looked at her, these two earnest, foolish young men. They were no longer rivals, but allies in a new crusade. They wanted to issue a statement to the press. They wanted to hire a lawyer for Mr. Abernathy, whom they had suddenly decided was a victim of a vast conspiracy. They wanted, in short, to *do things*, hurriedly and dramatically.

It was then that Mrs. Merriweather felt the full weight of her captaincy. The ship was listing, the passengers were in a panic, and a man was overboard.

“Sit down, the pair of you,” she commanded, her voice cutting through their agitation. They sat. “You will issue no statements. You will hire no lawyers. You will do nothing of the sort. Do you want to make the girl’s life a perfect misery? Do you want to see your names in the papers beside a thief’s?”

They were silent, chastened.

“The world,” she continued, more quietly, “is not a play, with good fellows and villains. It is a boarding house. It is a place where people make mistakes, and pay their rent late, and fall in love with the wrong person. Mr. Abernathy… well, I don’t know what he did. But he was always quiet with the coal scuttle, and that counts for something.” She looked from one earnest face to the other. “Your duty is not to storm the Bank of England. It is to this house. You will go to your work. You will come home. You will eat your dinner. And you will stop this foolishness about Miss Evans. Let the girl breathe.”

The two men looked at their landlady, at her tired eyes and capable hands, and saw not a confidante, but a force of nature. They nodded, ashamed.

That evening, the house was quiet. The silence was not one of anger, but of exhaustion. Mrs. Merriweather served a shepherd’s pie, and the two clerks ate it without comment. The ship, for the moment, was on an even keel. Mrs. Merriweather looked at her lodgers, these boys playing at being men, and felt a surge of fierce, maternal protectiveness. The sea was still troubled, she knew. But for now, the lights were on, the boiler was working, and the house was holding together. That, she decided, was quite enough for one day.
