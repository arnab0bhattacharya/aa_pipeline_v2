# The Consultation

He came to me in the manner of all desperate men—without warning, without apology, and in the dark. I had been sitting late in my consulting room in Crispin Street, which is not a place where one expects visitors of quality after the lamps are lit, and indeed I had not expected visitors of any kind. The rain had been falling with a kind of sullen persistence since the afternoon, and the fog that came up from the river had crept through the gaps in the window frames so that the air of the room was thick and grey, like the air of a vault. I had been reading, or rather holding a book, for I cannot say that my attention was upon the page. My thoughts had been wandering in that direction which they too often took in those days—towards the contemplation of my own usefulness, which is a dangerous path for any man to tread, but especially for one who has chosen to labour among those for whom usefulness is the only justification for existence. I was, I believe, on the verge of closing the book and going to bed when I heard the knock.

It was not a confident knock. It had the quality of a man testing whether the door would betray him. I opened it and found nothing—or rather, I found a man who seemed to wish to be nothing, who had compressed himself into the shadow of the doorframe as though he might thereby escape the notice not only of me but of whatever eye he believed was fixed upon him. He was hatless. His hair was plastered to his forehead with rain or sweat, and his face had the grey-yellow pallor of a man who has lost blood or who is in the grip of a terror so complete that it has drained the colour from him. He wore a workman's coat, dark and wet, and he held his left hand against his chest in the manner of a man cradling a wounded bird.

"You are the doctor," he said. It was not a question. His voice was low and hoarse, and there was in it an accent I could not place—foreign, certainly, but of what country I could not say. He spoke English with the careful precision of a man who has learned the words but not their music.

"I am," I said. "Come in."

He did not come in at once. He stood in the doorway and looked past me into the room, and then behind him into the street, and I understood that he was not merely hesitating but calculating—estimating, perhaps, whether my room offered more than one exit, whether the street behind him was still empty, whether I was alone. These are not the calculations of an ordinary sick man. But I had learned by then that the poor of Whitechapel did not come to one as ordinary sick men. They came with their histories written upon their bodies, and one read those histories not in what they said but in what they feared.

He entered at last and sat in the chair I indicated, and when I lit the lamp on my desk the light fell upon him with a suddenness that made him flinch. It was then that I saw his hand. He drew it from his coat as a man might draw a weapon, slowly and with a kind of dread, and I saw that the fingers were wrapped in a cloth that had once been white but was now blackened and stiff. The smell reached me at the same moment—the sharp, chemical smell that I recognised, though I had encountered it only once before, in my student days, when a demonstration in the laboratory at Guy's had gone wrong. It was the smell of nitroglycerin, or of something very like it.

He watched me watching him, and I believe he saw the recognition in my eyes, for his own eyes—which were dark and very bright, the eyes of a man in whom some interior fire burned steadily—narrowed, and his mouth set itself in a line that was not quite a smile.

"You see," he said. "You understand."

"I understand that you are hurt," I said. "Let me see."

I unwound the cloth, and beneath it the hand was a ruin. The two smallest fingers were gone—not cleanly, not as a surgeon would take them, but torn away in a manner that spoke of violence, of an explosive force that had consumed what it touched. The remaining fingers were blackened, and the palm was a wound of such rawness that I marvelled he had walked at all, let alone carried himself with that compressed, cautious dignity to my door. I said nothing for a moment. I busied myself with the basin and the clean water and the dressings, and while I did so I was aware of him watching me with an intensity that was almost physical, as though he were pressing upon me the weight of his attention, willing me to understand something that he would not or could not say.

"This was not an accident in a workshop," I said, at last. I had begun to clean the wound, and he did not flinch, though the muscles of his jaw tightened.

"No," he said. "Not in a workshop."

He said no more, and I did not press him. There was in the silence between us something that was not yet a confidence but which had the shape of one—the outline of a truth that neither of us was ready to fill. I dressed the hand with what skill I possessed, and when I had finished he sat looking at the white bandage with an expression that I can only describe as one of bewilderment, as though he had not expected to find himself still whole, still breathing, still in possession of a body that could be repaired.

I gave him powder for the pain and told him to return in two days. He stood to leave, and at the door he paused and turned to me, and I saw that he wished to speak—that there were words pressing against the back of his teeth which he could not, for some reason, release. He said only: "You will not—" and then stopped, and I understood that the sentence he could not finish was the one in which he asked me not to betray him.

I did not answer him. I let him go, and I stood at the door and watched him disappear into the fog of Crispin Street, walking quickly, with his wounded hand pressed again to his chest, until the grey air took him and he was gone as completely as if he had never been there.

I closed the door and returned to my chair, and there I sat for a long time, looking at the bloodied cloth and the basin of water that had turned the colour of rust. I was aware—I was acutely, painfully aware—that I stood at a junction of two roads, and that neither was straight. I had taken an oath. I had bound myself by it as a man binds himself to a wife, for better or for worse, and the binding was not conditional upon the character of those who came to me in their need. This I understood. But I understood also that the man who had sat in my chair was not merely a man with a wounded hand. He was a man engaged in a work of destruction, and the hand I had dressed was a hand that would, if he had his way, dress others—wound them, tear them, reduce them to the same ruin I had seen upon his palm. I had repaired the instrument of a violence I could not foresee but which I knew, with the certainty of one who has looked into those bright, burning eyes, would come.

And yet the oath held me. It held me as a chain holds a dog—not because the chain is just, but because the dog has known nothing else and cannot conceive of breaking it. I sat and I reasoned, and the more I reasoned the more I saw that reasoning was itself a kind of betrayal—a betrayal of the simplicity of the obligation, which was not to judge but to heal. And yet was not silence itself a judgment? Was not the withholding of knowledge from those whose duty it was to protect a form of complicity, as quiet and as deadly as the chemical I had smelled upon his fingers?

I do not know how long I sat there. The lamp burned low, and the fog pressed against the window, and the room grew cold. I thought of the men I had seen that day—the children with their rickety limbs, the women with their hollow eyes—and I thought that the world they inhabited was already a kind of explosion, slow and grinding, and that the man who had come to me believed himself to be striking against it. I did not share his belief. I had seen too much of what violence accomplishes, which is only more violence, and more wreckage, and more of those grey faces pressing against the glass of my door. But I could not give him up. I could not walk to the station in Leman Street and say what I knew, because to do so would be to break the one thing that held me to my purpose—the one thin thread by which I was still, after all, a doctor and not merely a man.

He returned in two days, as I had told him. And in two days more. And each time he came, the silence between us grew deeper and more weighted, until it seemed to me that we were two men standing on opposite sides of a chasm, each holding his end of a rope that neither would pull and neither would release. I dressed his hand. I said nothing. And the world went on, as it does, indifferent to the small treacheries of conscience that consume a man from within like a slow fire, leaving the surface untouched and the interior ash.
