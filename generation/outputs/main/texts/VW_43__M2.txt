The fog had not yet lifted from the Thames, hanging low and grey over the water like a damp wool shawl, and Aldous stood on the bridge, watching the barges move with a sluggish, inevitable grace. It was a Tuesday, or perhaps a Wednesday; the days in London had lost their names, becoming merely durations of light and dark, of hunger and cold. He held his suitcase, a battered thing of brown leather, against his side as if it were a shield, or perhaps a child. He had come from the quiet, green valleys of the north, where the air tasted of pine and the hours were measured by the crowing of roosters, to this city of stone and steam, where the hours were measured by the chimes of Big Ben and the clatter of hansom cabs. He had come to write. He had come to find a room, a job, and a story, and he had found, so far, only the vast, indifferent machinery of the metropolis turning beneath his feet.

He walked on, down the steep slope towards the city. The pavement was slick with rain and the shadows of the buildings stretched out like long, dark fingers, grasping at the ankles of passersby. A woman in a black hat hurried past, her face hidden by a veil, and for a moment Aldous felt a pang of recognition, a kinship with her solitude, though her stride was purposeful while his was aimless. He watched her disappear into the crowd, a black drop falling into the grey sea of humanity, and wondered what story lay behind the veil. Was it a tragedy? A comedy? Or merely the mundane struggle of a woman trying to get to the shops before the rain started again? He tried to capture the feeling, the precise shade of her loneliness, but the words slipped through his mind like water through a sieve, leaving only a vague impression of black fabric and quick movement.

He turned into a side street, where the houses were tall and narrow, their windows dark and eyesless. A landlord’s sign hung crookedly above a door: *Rooms to Let, Single, Bath.* Aldous paused. The door was ajar, and from within came the smell of boiled cabbage and old dust. He pushed it open. The room was small, a box of dust and silence, with a single window overlooking a brick wall. The light that entered was pale and weak, struggling to penetrate the gloom. He sat on the edge of the bed, which was hard and unyielding, and felt the weight of his suitcase settle on the floor. It was a good room, in a way. It was quiet. It was empty. But it was not a home. It was not a place where stories could be born. He stood up, brushed the dust from his trousers, and left the door slightly ajar behind him, as if in apology.

He walked further, into the heart of the city, where the streets were wider and the noise was a physical force, a roar that seemed to vibrate in his teeth. The shops were closing, their windows glowing with artificial light, displaying arrays of goods that glittered and sparkled, mocking his poverty. He saw a display of silk scarves, blue and green and gold, and he thought of his mother, who had never owned a scarf, but who had worn the same grey cardigan for twenty years, mending it again and again, her fingers stained with ink from the books she read. He thought of the library in their village, with its high ceilings and the smell of old paper, and he felt a sudden, sharp pain in his chest, a longing for the simplicity of a life where the world was small and comprehensible. Here, the world was too big, too loud, too fast. He was a fish out of water, struggling to breathe in an atmosphere that was not his own.

He stopped at a café, not because he was hungry, but because he needed to sit down, to be still, to listen. The café was crowded with men in dark coats, their faces hidden by the smoke of their cigarettes. They spoke in low voices, their words blurred and indistinct, a murmur that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere. Aldous ordered a coffee, which arrived hot and bitter, and he wrapped his hands around the cup, feeling the warmth seep into his cold fingers. He watched the people around him. A man was reading a newspaper, his brow furrowed in concentration. A woman was writing a letter, her pen moving rapidly across the page. A child was crying, a high, thin sound that cut through the murmur of the room. Aldous tried to focus on one of them, to see the story behind their face, but the stories were elusive, hiding in the shadows of their eyes, behind the masks they wore. He realized that he was not seeing them, but only the surface of them, the way one sees the surface of a lake, without knowing what lies beneath.

The afternoon light began to fade, turning the windows of the buildings into dark rectangles, and the streetlamps flickered on, one by one, like stars descending from the sky. The fog was thickening again, wrapping the city in a cocoon of grey. Aldous walked on, his steps slower now, his energy spent. He passed a bookshop, and the window display caught his eye. A row of books, their spines lined up like soldiers, waited to be read. He paused, looking at the titles, and felt a sudden, overwhelming desire to buy one, to read it, to lose himself in the words. But he had no money. He had spent the last of his shillings on a sandwich that morning, and now his pockets were empty. He walked on, leaving the bookshop behind, and the books with it.

By evening, the city had transformed. The noise had died down, and the streets were quiet, save for the sound of his own footsteps. The fog was so thick that he could not see more than a few feet in front of him. The buildings were blurred, their shapes distorted, becoming ghosts of themselves. He walked on, not knowing where he was going, not caring. He had found no room, no job, no story. He had seen everything, and it had changed him. He felt lighter, somehow, as if the weight of his expectations had been lifted from his shoulders. He was no longer the young man from the north, full of hope and ambition. He was a part of the city now, a ghost in the fog, moving through the streets, searching for a light that might not exist. But he was alive. He was here. And that, perhaps, was enough. He turned a corner, and the fog closed in around him, swallowing him whole, and for a moment, he was no one, and he was everyone, and he was the city itself, breathing in the cold, grey air, waiting for the sun to rise.
