The letter had been a masterpiece of its kind, penned in a hand that trembled with practised frailty. *My dearest Clara*, it began, *I do not wish to be a burden, but the physician, Dr. Finch, is most concerned… a weakness in the chest… he fears the worst.* The rest was a litany of vague symptoms and dire prognostications, sufficient to send Clara into a state of immediate alarm. And so, within two days, she and her husband, Thomas, were rattling along the muddy Somerset lanes in their hired post-chaise, the familiar, damp green of the Quantock Hills closing in around them.

Her mother’s house, Fennel Manor, stood as it always had: a handsome but slightly sagging pile of grey stone, half-swallowed by ivy. The air, thick with the scent of wet earth and decaying leaves, felt as much a part of the place as the worn flagstones in the hall. Mrs. Albright was not in her bedchamber as Clara had feared, but in the morning room, propped upon a chaise longue by the fire. A cashmere shawl was drawn up to her chin, and her face, usually so sharp and animated, was a mask of pale resignation.

“Clara, my love,” she breathed, extending a hand that felt like a clutch of dry twigs. “You have come. I am so glad. And Thomas, dear boy, how good of you to leave your affairs.”

Thomas bowed over her hand, his expression one of perfect, unadulterated concern. “It is no trouble at all, Mrs. Albright. We would be nowhere else.”

Clara knelt, pressing her cheek to her mother’s hand. “Mama, what is it? Your letter was so frightful.”

“A trifling thing, the doctor says,” Mrs. Albright murmured, her eyes fluttering closed. “A congestion of the lungs. He has prescribed rest and quiet. But the quiet, you see, is the very thing that makes one so aware of one’s own failing. The ticking of the clock becomes a death knell.”

As if on cue, a small, dry cough escaped her lips. It was a delicate, apologetic sound. Clara felt a familiar surge of love and exasperation. She knew this performance, had seen it staged many times over the years, but the fear in her mother’s letter had been real enough to her. She resolved to be patient, to be the perfect, attentive daughter.

Thomas, however, surpassed all expectation. He did not merely sit and offer platitudes. He took charge. He adjusted the fire, not with a poker but with a gentle, precise touch on the logs. He tasted the broth the cook had sent up and, declaring it a trifle thin, went to the kitchen himself to speak with Mrs. Gable. He read to Mrs. Albright from the *Somerset County Gazette*, his voice a low, steady rumble that seemed to soothe her more than any draught. When she complained of the draft from the window, he fetched a screen without a word. When she found the shawl too heavy, he replaced it with a lighter one.

Clara watched him, a strange feeling coiling in her stomach. She had always known Thomas to be a kind man, a patient man. It was one of the qualities she loved most in him. But this was different. This was not mere patience; it was a kind of serene, focused attention, as if ministering to her mother’s imagined ailments was the most rewarding work in the world.

On the second day, the crisis deepened. Mrs. Albright declared the fire was giving her a headache, yet the moment it was banked, she shivered violently and claimed the room had turned to ice. Thomas spent a full quarter of an hour finding the perfect equilibrium, adding a log, then removing a coal, adjusting the screen, until a state of perfect, non-offensive warmth was achieved. He did it all with a slight smile, as if solving a delightful puzzle.

“Thomas,” Clara said to him later, as they stood in the cold corridor outside her mother’s room. “You are too good to her.”

He turned to her, his brow smooth. “She is unwell, my dear. And she is your mother.”

“She is not unwell,” Clara whispered, the words tasting of betrayal. “She has a cold. A sniffle. You heard her, her voice is as strong as a bell when she wants something.”

A flicker of something—amusement? understanding?—passed through his eyes before being carefully smoothed away. “Perhaps she feels unwell. The feeling is what matters. It is not for us to judge the validity of another’s suffering.”

His reasonableness was more infuriating than any argument would have been. He was so good, so impossibly, flawlessly good, that he made her feel like a shrew. Yet, a suspicion, unwelcome and sharp, began to take root. She began to watch him more closely. She noted the way his gaze would linger on her mother’s dramatic gestures, the almost imperceptible nod of appreciation when she pronounced a symptom particularly dire. When Mrs. Albright, in a fit of pique, refused the tisane he had carefully prepared, he simply smiled, set it aside, and began concocting another, this one with honey instead of sugar. He seemed… to be enjoying himself.

The thought was monstrous. Her husband, enjoying her mother’s hypochondria? It made no sense. And yet, the evidence of her own eyes was compelling. There was no weariness in his bearing, no sigh of frustration when Mrs. Albright’s demands became absurd. He met each one with the same unwavering, gentle resolve.

The breaking point came on the third evening. Mrs. Albright had been particularly trying, convinced that the portrait of her late husband, hanging opposite the chaise, was exerting a malevolent influence on her lungs.

“It watches me, Clara,” she’d moaned. “His eyes follow me. I can feel it, stealing my very breath.”

Clara had tried to reason with her, to explain the trick of the light. But Thomas had risen calmly. “Then we shall move it,” he said.

He and the footman had carefully taken the heavy gilt frame from the wall and carried it into the hall. Mrs. Albright had watched them, a beatific look of relief on her face. “Oh, thank you, Thomas. You understand.”

Later that night, unable to sleep, Clara crept from her room. A sliver of light shone from under the library door. She pushed it open softly. Thomas was there, standing before the portrait of her father, which now leaned against a bookcase. He was not looking at it with sorrow or nostalgia. He was studying it with an expression of intense, almost scholarly concentration, his head tilted. He ran a finger along the gilded edge of the frame, then stepped back to admire the effect of the candlelight on the oil paint.

Clara felt a cold dread, entirely separate from any worry for her mother’s health, steal over her. This was not kindness. This was not patience. This was a game. He was not humoring a frail old woman; he was curating an experience, staging a play with her mother as the leading lady and himself as the indulgent, all-capable director. The thought that he relished this—the endless small dramas, the constant, fussy demands, the chance to be the hero of a domestic tragedy—was so profoundly unsettling it made the floor seem to tilt beneath her feet.

She must have made a sound, for he turned. His face, in the dim light, was serene.

“Clara? Is everything alright?”

She looked at him, at this man she had married, this quiet, steady man from Oxfordshire who dealt in ancient books and had never raised his voice in all their years together. And she saw a stranger.

“Thomas,” she said, her voice barely a whisper. “Are you enjoying this?”

He did not pretend to misunderstand. The smile he gave her was gentle, and infinitely patient. “Enjoying it? My dear, I am simply glad to be of use.” He turned back to the portrait. “Your father had a remarkable likeness, did he not? The artist captured a certain… authority in the brow.”

Clara stood frozen in the doorway, the chill of the old house seeping into her bones. The crisis had been a sham, a cold in the head. But as she watched her husband, she felt she was only now beginning to understand the true nature of the illness in the house, and she had no physician, no tisane, and no cure.
