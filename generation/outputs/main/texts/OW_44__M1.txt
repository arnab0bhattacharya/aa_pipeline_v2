It was a truth universally acknowledged, though seldom confessed in the drawing-rooms of Richmond, that society is a conspiracy to prevent us from ever knowing one another, and a far more successful conspiracy to prevent us from ever knowing ourselves. Lady Beatrice de Vere had, however, achieved a singular triumph over this latter difficulty. For fifteen years, she had kept a record of her conquests, a slim volume bound in cream-coloured vellum and clasped with a turquoise, in which was inscribed, in her own elegant hand, every compliment she had ever received.

The book was not a diary of feelings, but a catalogue of effects. It was a museum of polished phrases, a herbarium of pressed and faded flatteries. Within its pages, her wit was a perpetual diamond, her eyes a constant sea of sapphire, and her smile a ceaseless dawn. She had been called ‘a sonnet in silk,’ ‘a paradox in pearls,’ and ‘the only argument for the existence of Providence in modern dress.’ The entries were dated and initialled, for a compliment, Lady Beatrice believed, was like a fine wine; it gained value from its vintage and the reputation of the house it came from.

The garden party at her cousin’s villa, ‘The Cedars,’ was, by all accounts, a triumph of the aesthetic. The roses were not merely pink, but the precise shade of a crushed strawberry. The lawn was a sheet of green velvet upon which the guests were scattered like fallen jewels. Conversation flowed as lightly and as insubstantially as the champagne. Yet beneath this placid surface, a tremor of anxiety had begun to spread. Lady Beatrice’s little book had been mislaid. She had been seen with it beneath the great copper beech, and now it was gone.

The news moved through the assembled company in a series of discreet, horrified whispers. Sir Marmaduke Ponsoby, a man whose face was a monument to good living and whose conversation was a desert of platitudes, grew perceptibly pale. He recalled, with a sudden, sickening clarity, a rainy afternoon in his own drawing-room when, in a moment of uncharacteristic poetry, he had compared Lady Beatrice’s complexion to ‘the inner petals of a white peony, untouched by sun or sorrow.’ The phrase, so charming at the time, now seemed a public declaration of his own florid sentimentality.

Near the strawberry ices, Mrs. Albina Finch, a woman whose soul was as tightly laced as her bodice, felt a cold dread congeal within her. Her contribution to the vellum-bound chronicle, made some three years prior, was the assertion that Lady Beatrice ‘possessed a moral grace so profound it was like watching a lily learn to read.’ To have such an absurdity read aloud, perhaps by some cynical young man, would be social annihilation. The very foundations of her carefully constructed world seemed to tremble.

Even the Vicar, the Reverend Mr. Abernathy, was not immune. He had once, in a moment of pastoral zeal, described Lady Beatrice’s presence at a charity bazaar as ‘a benediction in a bonnet.’ He now pictured this phrase being repeated by the young men from the Treasury, and felt the blood rush to his clerical collar. The book was no longer a private treasury of triumphs; it was a weapon of mass humiliation, and it was loose in the garden.

Into this state of quiet panic moved the only soul who was entirely at ease: Mr. Julian Vance. He was a young man of good family and no fortune, who had recently returned from Italy with a portfolio of sketches and a mind full of inconvenient truths. He was handsome in a way that society found slightly unsettling, for his beauty seemed less an accident of birth than a deliberate, critical statement. He had observed Lady Beatrice for years and had found her, like a too-perfectly polished mirror, to be a flawless reflection of the desires of others, but to possess no distinct image of her own. He had, consequently, never paid her a compliment. His silence, which she had at first found intriguing and then merely rude, was his own peculiar form of flattery; he had found nothing about her that he wished to praise in a language he considered bankrupt.

As the panic reached its zenith, Julian, who had been examining the intricate patterns of a lily pond, saw a flash of turquoise half-hidden in the moss beneath the copper beech. He knelt and retrieved the book. He did not open it. He simply held it, the cool vellum a stark contrast to the warm summer air.

He walked directly to Lady Beatrice, who was attempting to maintain an expression of regal detachment while her eyes darted about like trapped birds. He held out the book.

“I believe this is yours, Lady Beatrice,” he said, his voice calm and clear, cutting through the hushed terror.

A collective sigh of relief, so profound it was almost audible, swept across the lawn. Sir Marmaduke reached for his champagne. Mrs. Finch’s spine, for the first time all afternoon, relaxed. Lady Beatrice took the book, her fingers closing over it with a possessiveness that was almost painful.

“Oh, thank you, Mr. Vance,” she said, her voice regaining its customary poise. “My little book of thoughts. I should have been quite lost without it.”

“I confess I am curious,” Julian said, his gaze direct and untroubled. “All afternoon, it has seemed to be the most important object in the garden. And yet, I have never found a need for it.”

Lady Beatrice gave a little laugh, a sound like chinking ice. “It is merely a collection of… reminders. Of things people have been kind enough to say.”

“Ah,” said Julian, a faint smile playing on his lips. “A collection of other people’s opinions. How singular. I have always found my own to be quite sufficient, however disagreeable they may be.”

He bowed slightly and turned to walk away, leaving Lady Beatrice holding the key to all their shames. She looked at the book in her hand. The panic of the crowd had been replaced by a sudden, cloying gratitude. They looked at her not with fear, but with the fondness one reserves for a guardian of secrets.

Later that evening, in the quiet of her boudoir, Lady Beatrice opened the book. The familiar, elegant script swam before her eyes. ‘Sonnet in silk.’ ‘Paradox in pearls.’ ‘A lily learning to read.’ She read the words, but they were no longer jewels. They were brittle, dead things, the husks of moments that had passed. They were the compliments of a hundred other people, and not a single, solitary thought of her own.

She looked at her reflection in the silver-backed mirror on her dressing-table. The face that looked back was the same one that had been described in a thousand glittering ways. It was a beautiful face, a face that had launched a thousand flatteries. But for the first time, she saw it as Mr. Vance must see it: a perfect, polished surface, with nothing behind it. The book, which she had believed to be a testament to her existence, now seemed a monument to her emptiness. It was not a record of who she was, but a confession that she did not know.

She closed the vellum cover. The turquoise clasp clicked shut with a sound of finality. She had not lost her diary; she had found it. And in doing so, she had lost the one thing she had ever truly possessed: her ignorance.
