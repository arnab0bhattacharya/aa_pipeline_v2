It is a curious thing how a man’s life can be ruined by a diagram of a finch’s beak. Dr. Armitage, the Headmaster of St. Jude’s Preparatory School, called it ‘subversive naturalism’; I called it the foundation of modern biology. The boys, bless their cotton socks, thought it a ripping excuse to draw caricatures of Darwin looking like a bewildered monkey. But the governors were of a different opinion. My dismissal was swift, polite, and entirely devoid of scientific rigour. I was told my views were not in keeping with the character of a Christian gentleman, a charge I found both historically and personally questionable.

So it was that I found myself, a man of Huxley and Wallace, relegated to the dusty, gas-lit confines of Ashworth’s Cramming Establishment in the suburban sprawl of Blackheath. My new students were not the bright, curious lads of St. Jude’s, but a procession of dull-witted young gentlemen whose fathers possessed sufficient funds and insufficient sons to fill the officer corps of Her Majesty’s Army. They came to me with their minds as blank as a fresh slate, their only ambition being to memorise enough geography and rudimentary mathematics to pass the Woolwich examinations without disgracing their family name.

My classroom was a stuffy room overlooking a row of identical brick villas. It smelled of chalk dust, floor polish, and the faint, persistent odour of boiled mutton from the kitchen below. The young men—callow youths with fresh complexions and the vacant expressions of prize cattle—would slump before me, their eyes glazed over as I attempted to explain the intricacies of the Napoleonic campaigns or the principle of the parallelogram of forces. To them, the Battle of Austerlitz was no more real than the rings of Saturn, and considerably less interesting than the prospect of a game of billiards at their club. They were, in short, a profound disappointment to the cause of education.

It was into this intellectual wilderness that Miss Beatrice Finch began to intrude. She was the elder sister of young Cecil Pemberton, a boy of such remarkable obtuseness that he once mistook the isosceles triangle for a type of sea monster. She would arrive on Tuesday afternoons to collect her brother, and would invariably linger. At first, she would stand by the door, a tall, serious-looking girl with an intelligent face that seemed entirely out of place amidst the Pembertons’ bovine stupidity.

“Mr. Davies,” she said one afternoon, her voice clear and direct as she gestured to the book on my desk, “I see you are reading Mr. Wells. Do you find his account of a Martian invasion more plausible than the teleological arguments of the Paley school?”

The question was so unexpected, so utterly foreign to the discourse of my daily life, that I nearly dropped my chalk. I looked at her properly then. She had dark, thoughtful eyes and a mouth that seemed accustomed to firm opinions. “I find it a stimulating exercise of the scientific imagination, Miss Finch,” I managed to reply. “Mr. Wells has the great virtue of taking his premises to their logical, if uncomfortable, conclusions.”

From that day, her visits became a regular feature of my week. While Cecil struggled to distinguish between latitude and longitude, his sister and I would discuss the heat-death of the universe, the social implications of Malthus, and the breathtaking vistas of geological time revealed by Lyell. She had read everything, from Darwin’s barnacles to Herbert Spencer’s sociological tracts. She spoke of these things not with the timid deference of a schoolgirl, but with the passion and precision of a fellow enthusiast. Our conversations were brief, often interrupted by the need to correct Cecil’s spelling of ‘Metternich’, but they were a lifeline to the world of ideas I had lost.

“It seems such a waste, Mr. Davies,” she said one day, leaning against the doorframe while I explained the drainage of the Fens to a stupefied boy named Ponsonby. “Men like you, forced to pour knowledge into vessels that will not hold it, while the world rushes on.”

“It is a living, Miss Finch,” I said with a shrug I did not entirely feel. “And perhaps some of it may stick. A small dose of geography may prevent a future officer from invading the wrong country.”

She smiled at that, a sudden, brilliant thing that transformed her serious face. “You are a cynic.”

“I am a realist. There is a difference.”

Our acquaintance grew. She began to bring me books—a copy of *The Descent of Man* she had found in a second-hand shop, a pamphlet on electrical engineering from the Faraday Society. In return, I found myself explaining the principles of radioactivity or the strange new particles discovered in cathode ray tubes. The classroom became our little island of reason in a sea of mediocrity. I felt a stirring of hope, a sense that my intellectual life was not entirely extinguished, merely… on a peculiar hiatus.

The inevitable conclusion arrived on a damp Tuesday in November. A thin, querulous man with a face like a discontented pug introduced himself as Colonel Finch. He was her father. He did not sit down.

“I am given to understand, sir,” he began, his voice tight with a kind of military outrage, “that you have been filling my daughter’s head with a great deal of… scientific nonsense.”

“Sir,” I said, rising to my feet, “I have been engaged in conversation with your daughter on a number of topics of mutual interest. I was not aware that a knowledge of the natural world was considered nonsense.”

“It is when it leads to… to this!” he spluttered, gesturing vaguely at my desk, at the books, at me. “She speaks of humanity as a mere animal! Of the Earth as a speck of dust! Of the future as a mechanical process! It is not fitting. It is not womanly. It is not Christian.”

I felt a familiar heat rise in my face, the old battle-cry of the rationalist against the dogmatist. “Colonel Finch, the truths of science are not a matter of opinion or decorum. They are facts, discovered through observation and reason. To ignore them is to live in a state of deliberate ignorance.”

He stared at me, his face purpling. “You are a dangerous man, sir. You will not see my daughter again. And as for you, Cecil,” he bellowed towards the door, where the boy was peering in with a look of profound relief, “you will be attending Mr. Abernathy’s establishment in Croydon from now on. At least *he* is a man of sound principles!”

And with that, he swept out, dragging the bewildered Cecil with him. The door slammed shut, leaving me alone in the sudden silence. The gaslight hissed. The chalk dust settled. I looked at the empty chair where Beatrice Finch had so often sat, and at the books she had given me. The world, it seemed, had a powerful immune system against the virus of progress. It would expel the foreign body and return to its comfortable, unthinking slumber. I picked up a piece of chalk and, with a sense of grim finality, drew a diagram of the human eye on the blackboard. A perfect, intricate, and utterly pointless piece of engineering. The bell for the end of the period rang, a dull, tinny sound that echoed my own hollow victory.
