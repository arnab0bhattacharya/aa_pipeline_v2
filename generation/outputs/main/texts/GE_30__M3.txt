Among the various vehicles that dotted the slow-moving, deeply-rutted highways of Leicestershire in the year 1813, none was more punctual, or more thoroughly encompassed in its own quiet atmosphere of brown holland and mingled odours, than the carrier’s van of Mr. Silas Merton. The roads in those days were a severe trial to the patience of man and beast; the heavy clay of the district seemed to possess a malicious intelligence, gripping the wheels of commerce with a tenacious suction that turned a journey of a few miles into an epoch of bodily fatigue and philosophical reflection. Silas, however, was a man profoundly adapted to his environment. His broad, stolid countenance, shaded by a weather-beaten beaver, expressed a settled equanimity that might have been mistaken for stupidity by those who judge of mental capacity solely by the readiness of the tongue; but in truth, his slowness of speech was the natural result of a habit of weighing the moral bearings of things which, in a quicker temperament, might have been altogether overlooked. He was the silent confidant of the scattered parish of Kingley Cross. To sit beside him on the high box of the van was to enter into a tacit treaty of confidence; and the good folks who entrusted their parcels of butter, their cheques, and their family gossip to his charge, did so with the calm assurance that Silas was a man who respected the boundaries of his own vocation, carrying what he was given to carry, and asking no questions of the goods, whether they were alive in the shape of a nervous governess, or inanimate in the shape of a ham.

But the moral machinery of the simplest life is liable to be thrown out of gear by the sudden impact of an exceptional demand; and this adjustment of inward impulse to outward circumstance is a process that requires a far subtler philosophy than the mere heaping up of facts. It was on a damp Tuesday in late October that Silas pulled up his sober-paced mare, Bess, at the white gate of Thistledown Farm, the residence of the affluent and domineering Mr. Granger. He had expected to take on a consignment of eggs and perhaps a grumbled message regarding the quality of the previous week’s cheese. Instead, he found waiting for him by the gatepost the slender figure of Hetty Granger, the old farmer’s granddaughter. Hetty was a girl of nineteen, possessing that delicate, uncertain beauty which seems to require the shelter of ancestral walls, yet her face, usually placid and somewhat pale, was now flushed with a feverish agitation that lent it a startling brilliancy. She looked anxiously toward the farmhouse door before stepping close to the wheel, and drawing a sealed letter from beneath her grey stuff cloak.

“Silas,” she said, in a voice that struggled to suppress its tremors, “you are going to Lutterworth market today?”

“That I am, Miss Hetty,” Silas answered, gathering the reins with a mechanical precision, his eyes resting with quiet concern upon her agitated demeanour. “I shall be at the 'George' by noon, please God.”

“And you will be passing by the old Priory gates?” she pursued, her fingers nervously tightening on the paper she held. “You know the place, where the lane turns off to Ashby?”

“I know it, surely.”

“Will you—can you—leave this letter there? Under the great stone, just inside the ruined arch? It is of the utmost consequence, and I cannot trust it to the post. And, Silas,” she added, her breath catching as she pressed a shilling into his coarse, brown hand, “you will not speak of this to my grandfather, nor to anyone? It is a secret—a matter of business of my own.”

The nature of a secret is a thing imperfectly understood by those who have never felt the burden of one. To Silas, the shilling felt like a burning coal. He was a man whose moral constitution was of that robust, uncomplicated order which instinctively associates secrecy with impending transgression; and his attachment to the old farmer, though it was an attachment built more on the habit of years than on any effusive warmth of feeling, was strong enough to make him regard any clandestine proceeding at Thistledown Farm with a settled aversion. He looked at the letter, then at Hetty’s pleading eyes, and finally at the farmhouse windows, as if expecting to see the harsh, peaked visage of Mr. Granger glowering from the casement.

“I am a carrier, Miss Hetty,” he said, slowly, feeling his way through the sentence as he felt his way through the miry ruts. “I carry what I am paid to carry. But I am not over-fond of carrying things in the dark, so to speak. Is there aught wrong, Miss Hetty?”

“No, no, nothing wrong!” she answered, with a hurried impatience that only heightened his suspicion. “It is only—it is a letter to a friend. You will do it, Silas? You have always been kind to me.”

It is a curious evidence of the complexity of human motive that the appeal to his kindness should have been the very weight that turned the balance in favour of her request. Silas pocketed the shilling and placed the letter carefully in the breast of his greatcoat. “I will leave it where you say, Miss Hetty,” he said; and with a nod of gratitude she vanished back into the garden, leaving him to pursue his journey with a mind considerably less at ease than usual.

The road from Kingley Cross to Lutterworth was long, and the autumn mist hung heavily over the fields, wrapping the hedgerows in a damp, vaporous veil that seemed to reflect the mental obscurity of the carrier. Silas was not a man given to rapid analysis, but he possessed that invaluable faculty of moral imagination which enables a man to foresee the consequences of an act, not in the abstract, but in the concrete suffering of those he knows. He knew, with a certainty that needed no confirmation, that the letter was not intended for a female friend. The handwriting on the direction, hastily glimpsed, was large and masculine; and the Priory gates were within a mile of the lodgings of a certain young lawyer’s clerk from London, whose attentions to Hetty at the last parish races had been the subject of much scandalized whispering among the gossips of Kingley Cross. Silas revolved the matter in his mind as Bess plodded through the mud. He was a man of business, paid to carry what he was given; but he was also a man with a conscience, and that conscience now began to assert itself with a quiet but insistent force.

If he left the letter, he was, in effect, becoming a party to a clandestine correspondence which might lead to consequences that would bring down upon Hetty’s head the terrible wrath of her grandfather—a wrath which, in the rural society of that day, meant social disgrace and domestic misery. The old farmer was a man of rigid principles, and the idea of his granddaughter engaging in a secret courtship with a clerk of no fortune would seem to him little short of a felony. Silas felt the full weight of his position. To refuse to carry the letter would be to break his word to Hetty, and to expose her to the very danger she sought to avoid by appealing to him; but to carry it was to aid in a deception which his moral sense condemned. He was, as he had said, a carrier of goods, but he could not bring himself to be a carrier of ruin.

The mist had thickened into a steady, drizzling rain by the time he reached the cross-roads. The Priory gates loomed up before him, their grey stone pillars half-hidden by the dripping ivy. Silas pulled up Bess, and sat for a few moments in profound thought. The conflict between the dictates of worldly prudence and the higher law of moral duty is one that has agitated greater minds than his; but in its humble way, the carrier’s hesitation was as genuine a moral struggle as any that has perplexed the councils of philosophers. At last, with a heavy sigh that seemed to exhale from the very soles of his boots, he drove on past the gates. He would not leave the letter.

But neither would he betray the girl. To return the letter to her would be to abandon her to the consequences of her own rashness, and perhaps to drive her to some more desperate expedient. Silas felt that he must do something more than merely refuse; he must, if possible, avert the danger he foresaw. He would carry the letter to Lutterworth, but he would also carry a message of his own—a message that, if it did not directly interfere with Hetty’s plans, would at least put her on her guard against the consequences of her own folly. He would speak to her on his return, and tell her, in his blunt, homely way, that he had not left the letter, and that she must not ask him to do so again. It was a compromise, and like most compromises, it satisfied neither party; but it was the best that Silas could do in the exigency of the moment.

It was late in the afternoon when he drove back through the lanes, the shadows of the evening gathering thickly under the hedgerows, and the rain still falling with a quiet, persistent patience. He stopped at the white gate of Thistledown Farm, and Hetty came out to meet him, her face pale with suspense.

“Did you—did you leave it, Silas?” she asked, in a whisper.

“I did not, Miss Hetty,” said Silas, gravely, handing her back the letter. “I am sorry to disappoint you, but I could not do it. It is not for me to judge of your doings, but I am an old man, and I have seen a deal of trouble in my time, and I know that secrets of this kind are like the first cracks in a bridge—they widen with the frost. Your grandfather is a hard man, but he loves you in his way, and it would grieve him to the heart if he knew that you were carrying on a clandestine correspondence with a young man of whom he knows nothing. I cannot be a party to it, Miss Hetty. I am only a poor carrier, but I have a conscience, and I must answer for it as well as I can.”

Hetty’s eyes filled with tears, but they were tears of anger as much as of disappointment. “You have betrayed me!” she cried, snatching the letter from his hand. “You promised!”

“I promised to carry what I was paid to carry,” said Silas, with a quiet dignity that sat well upon him, “but I did not promise to carry away the peace of mind of a family that has trusted me for twenty year. The shilling I return to you, Miss Hetty, for the service was not done.”

He dropped the coin into her hand, and driving on, left her standing at the gate, a forlorn and passionate figure in the gathering dusk. The carrier’s van rumbled away through the mud, and Silas Merton, sitting solitary on his box, felt a melancholy satisfaction in the consciousness that he had done what he believed to be right. He had sacrificed the gratitude of the one human being who had lately treated him with a touch of confidence, but he had preserved his own integrity, and in the eyes of his own conscience at least, he was still a man of business, for the business he had chosen to transact was the preservation of a moral order.
