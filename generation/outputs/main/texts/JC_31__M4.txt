The room, which was on the third floor of a tall, narrow house in the Rue des Pâquis, had the proportions of a corridor and the atmosphere of a tomb. Its one window, opening upon a shaft-like courtyard, admitted a light so diminished and so grey that it seemed to have been strained through the very stones of the city, and it fell upon the faces of the seven men assembled there with a kind of impartial and hopeless scrutiny, as though it were the eye of a judge already weary of the case. The air was thick with the smell of tobacco, of damp plaster, and of the unwashed bodies of men who had sat too long in one place, and through the half-open door came, at intervals, the sound of a woman’s voice, remote and monotonous, singing some interminable lament in a dialect that none of them understood.

The young man—his name was Vassily Andreyevich Sokolov—stood with his back against the wall, his hands clasped behind him, and his eyes fixed upon a point somewhere above the heads of his accusers. He was thin, very pale, with the pallor of a student who has lived too long upon tea and bread and the smoke of his own thoughts, and his hair, which was of a light, dusty colour, fell in a disorderly lock across his forehead. He had the appearance of a man who has been awakened suddenly from a profound sleep and cannot yet distinguish the dream from the reality. His lips moved from time to time, but no sound came from them.

The president of the committee—a heavy, bearded man in a frock-coat too large for him, whose name was Ivan Petrovich Grach—sat at the head of the table, his hands spread flat upon its surface, and regarded the young man with a stare that was meant to be stern and was in truth merely uneasy. He had been a schoolmaster in his own country, and the habit of authority sat upon him awkwardly, like a borrowed garment. Beside him sat a woman—the only woman in the room—whose name was Anna Mikhailovna; she had a face of that peculiar hardness which is sometimes produced by the habit of suspicion, and she held a small pencil poised above a notebook, as though she were about to take down the prisoner’s words for some future and terrible use.

“You understand,” said Grach, in a voice which he endeavoured to make grave and weighty, “that we are not your judges. We are merely—we are merely the instruments of the common will. You have been accused. You have been accused by a man whose word, in former days, we had some reason to respect. You have the right to speak. You have the right to explain. But you must understand that we are not—that we cannot be—moved by mere protestations. We require—we require facts.”

The young man said nothing. He had heard these words, or words very like them, at least a dozen times in the course of the last three hours. They had ceased to have any meaning for him. He was thinking of the room in which he had lodged in Zurich—of the books upon the shelf, of the little stove that smoked so abominably in winter, of the letter which he had received from his sister, and which he had not answered. He was thinking of these things with a strange and painful intensity, as a man who is drowning is said to think of the whole of his life in a single instant.

“You do not answer,” said the woman, Anna Mikhailovna, without looking up from her notebook. “Why do you not answer?”

“I have answered,” said the young man, in a voice which was scarcely above a whisper. “I have told you that I am not a spy. I have told you that I do not know the man who has accused me. I have told you that I have never seen him before this evening. What more can I say?”

“You can tell us,” said Grach, leaning forward, “why this man—this Pavel Sergeyevich Voronov—should have named you. A man does not accuse another without reason. There must be a reason. What is the reason?”

“I do not know,” said the young man. “I tell you I do not know him. I have never spoken to him. I have never seen his face until tonight.”

There was a silence. The woman’s pencil moved slowly across the paper, making a small, dry, scratching sound that seemed to fill the room. One of the men at the far end of the table—a young man with a pale, eager face and a thin beard—coughed nervously and then said, in a voice which he tried to make casual and which was in reality strained and anxious:

“Perhaps—perhaps it would be well to send for Voronov. Perhaps—if he were confronted with the accused—”

“No,” said Grach, sharply. “No. That is not—that is not desirable. Voronov has already—he has already made his statement. He has already—he has already gone.”

“Gone?” said the young man with the thin beard. “Gone where?”

“He has gone away,” said Grach, and his voice was suddenly defensive. “He has—he has been sent away. For his own safety. It was thought—it was thought better that he should not remain. He is—he is in a place of safety.”

The young man against the wall raised his head slowly, and for the first time his eyes met those of the president. There was in them a look of such profound and weary comprehension that Grach, for all his assumed severity, dropped his gaze and began to fumble with the papers before him.

“He has gone away,” repeated Sokolov, in a voice that was quite without expression. “He has gone away, and I am left. That is—that is very convenient. For him.”

“What do you mean?” cried the woman, Anna Mikhailovna, lifting her head sharply. “What do you mean by that?”

“I mean,” said the young man, “that I have no way of proving my innocence, and he has no way of being questioned. I mean that I am to be condemned upon the word of a man who is not here to repeat it. I mean that this—this committee—has already decided. You have decided. You decided before I entered the room.”

There was a movement among the men at the table—a stirring, a shifting of feet, a murmur of protest. But no one spoke. The woman’s pencil had stopped. The distant voice of the singing woman in the courtyard rose suddenly in a high, wailing note, and then sank again into its interminable monotone.

Grach cleared his throat. He was a man who had spent his life in the presence of authority, and he had learned to imitate its gestures without possessing its substance. He said, with an attempt at dignity:

“You are mistaken. We have not decided. We are—we are deliberating. We are—we are weighing the evidence. But you must understand that the evidence—the evidence is against you. The man Voronov was—he was trusted. He was—he was one of us. He had no reason—no reason—to lie.”

“No reason,” said Sokolov, and his voice was very low. “No reason. Except—except that he was afraid. Except that he wished to save himself. Except that he found it easier to give up another man than to be given up himself. Is that—is that not a reason?”

The woman, Anna Mikhailovna, closed her notebook with a snap. She said, in a voice that was hard and dry:

“You are young. You are very young. You do not understand—you do not understand what we have suffered. You do not understand what it is to live as we live—in exile—in poverty—in fear. You do not understand what it is to be betrayed. We have been betrayed. We have been betrayed again and again. And now—now you come among us with your fine phrases—your talk of reasons—your——”

She stopped. She had risen to her feet, and she stood looking down at him with a kind of fury that was not altogether hatred, but was rather the fury of a woman who has been disappointed in her hope of finding a friend. The young man looked at her, and for a moment something like pity flickered in his eyes.

“I understand,” he said, quietly. “I understand very well. But I cannot—I cannot prove what is not true. I can only tell you that I am innocent. If that is not enough—if that is not enough—then there is nothing more that I can say.”

He turned away from them, and stood looking out of the window into the grey and narrow courtyard, where a single star was visible above the chimney-pots, trembling in the cold light of the evening. Behind him the committee sat in silence, and the woman’s pencil began again its small, dry, scratching sound.
