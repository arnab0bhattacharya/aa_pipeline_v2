The rain had not ceased for three days. It lashed the narrow gables of V— with a fury that seemed personal, driving through the ill-pointed mortar of our high brick walls and pooling in the uneven cobbles of the alley below. I stood at the unshuttered lattice of my bedchamber, watching the dark, oily canal below swallow the downpour, and felt the chill of the foreign climate sink into my bones. England, with its milder mists and its green, damp grave where my short, bewildering married life lay buried, seemed a thousand leagues away—not merely across the sea, but severed from me by a moral abyss I dared not peer into. I was a widow of twenty-three, penniless, and a Protestant in a Papist land. I had come to serve, and to hide.

A sharp knock shattered my reverie. It was not the doctor’s heavy, familiar tread, but a light, vindictive rap. I turned from the glass. "Enter," I said, collecting myself with a speed born of recent habit.

Frau Greta, Dr. Vance’s elder sister, thrust her spare frame into the room. She did not cross the threshold entirely, but stood framed in the doorway, her black bombazine gown rustling with a stiff, metallic whisper. Her face was narrow, deeply lined, and pale as tallow; her eyes, small and dark as Sloe berries, searched my own with a cold, suspicious hostility.

"The doctor has returned from the harbour clinic, Mrs. Maynard," she said, her English precise but heavily veiled in a guttural accent. "He is in the lower surgery. There is much blood, and the floor must be scrubbed. I am too occupied with the dispensary. You will go down immediately."

It was a task usually left to the charwoman, the old Fleming who came at dawn. I knew this. Frau Greta knew I knew it. The command was a probe, a test of my pride, an attempt to make me flinch from the grim realities of a surgeon’s household and flee back to my gentility.

"I will go directly," I replied, meeting her gaze without a tremor. I did not add "ma'am" or "madam." I let the blunt equality of my tone speak for itself.

Her thin lips compressed until they vanished entirely into the furrows of her face. "See that you do not faint at the sight of it," she added. "English ladies, I am told, are delicate. And you are not of our faith. The priests say suffering is a grace. Perhaps your Protestant heart cannot bear the grace of the flesh."

"My heart, Frau Greta," I answered, drawing my grey shawl about my shoulders, "was made in the same furnace as yours. It bears what it must. I am no stranger to blood."

She held my stare for a long, combative second, then stepped back, letting the door swing shut with a sharp click. I heard her retreating footsteps, rapid and hard, descending the oak staircase. 

I stood a moment in the grey twilight of my room. The fire in the small grate was nearly out, reduced to a single, angry crimson eye glaring from beneath a mound of white ash. I felt a sudden, fierce rebellion flare in my own breast—a hot, choking indignation against my fate, against the secrecy that bound me, against this foreign house and its jealous sentinel. Why was I here, scrubbing floors for a man I scarcely knew, enduring the malice of his kin? Because in England, to speak the truth of my late husband’s cruelties would have branded me a madwoman or worse; because silence was the only shield I had left. I pressed my hands to my eyes, forcing the tears back into their scalding source. I would not weep. I was hard and tough as an India-rubber ball, though, pervious through a chink or two still, and with one sentient point in the middle of the lump.

I gathered my heavy skirts and descended the winding stairs to the lower hall. The air grew thick with the scent of carbolic acid, laudanum, and the coppery tang of fresh bleeding. At the end of the vaulted corridor, the door of the surgery stood ajar. A faint, ruddy light spilled from within.

Dr. Vance was bending over a low pallet where a young Flemish labourer lay groaning, his leg swathed in crimson-soaked bandages. The doctor was a man of considerable height and formidable breadth, though he carried himself with a stoop that spoke of ceaseless labour. His hair was dark, heavily streaked with iron-grey, and fell over a brow that was massive and stern. His hands, as he worked to staunch the wound, were large, deft, and terribly steady. 

He looked up as my shadow crossed the threshold. His eyes were a deep, smouldering hazel—eyes that seemed to hold a perpetual, smothered fire, lit by some internal furnace of defiance or sorrow. 

"Ah, Mrs. Maynard," he said, his voice deep and resonant, stripped of ceremony. "Greta dispatched you, I suppose. She has a talent for finding the sharpest thorn and pressing it into the tenderest spot. You need not have come; I am nearly finished."

"The floor requires washing, sir," I said, my eyes taking in the dark, spreading stain on the stone flags. "I will fetch the pail."

"Do not be hasty," he replied, straightening to his full height and wiping his hands on a coarse towel. He looked at me with a probing directness that always disquieted me. "You are pale. You have not eaten today. I observed it at the morning meal. You pushed your bread about your plate as if it were a puzzle to be solved, not a sustenance to be swallowed."

"I was not hungry, sir."

"A lie," he said, not angrily, but with a blunt, clinical certainty. "You are starving yourself. For what purpose? To atone for a sin you did not commit? Or to punish those who forced you into exile?"

I started. The word "exile" struck too close to the hidden bone. "I am merely weary, Dr. Vance. The weather oppresses me."

He stepped closer, tossing the stained towel onto a wooden bench. The firelight from the hearth caught the harsh, rugged lines of his jaw. "You are a poor dissembler, Mrs. Maynard. You have a face like a closed book, but the cover is frequently flushed with the effort of keeping the lock shut. Greta believes you are a spy, or an apostate fleeing justice. She thinks you bring Protestant misfortune upon our Catholic charity."

"And you, sir?" I asked, a sudden, sharp anger giving courage to my tongue. "Do you also believe I am a harbinger of misfortune? If so, I shall pack my trunk and quit your house this very night, rain or no rain."

"By God, you have a temper," he murmured, a faint, grim smile touching the corners of his mouth. "It is well. I dislike passive creatures. No, I do not believe you are a spy. I believe you are a woman who has been struck a terrible blow by Destiny, and who is trying to stand upright beneath the weight of it. But I cannot help you, Mrs. Maynard, if you will not trust me."

"You pay me to keep your house, sir, not to confess my soul."

"I pay you to be a human being," he retorted, his voice suddenly rising, vibrating with a passionate urgency that filled the vaulted room. "I do not want a machine for ordering linen and counting laudanum drops. I have enough of machines in the dispensary. If you are in trouble—if there is a reason why the mere mention of England makes your lip whiten and your hands tremble—tell me. I am not a priest, but I am a doctor, and I have seen the ravages of the human heart. I will not judge you."

I looked at him. The surge of emotion in his voice, the fierce, benevolent fire in his eyes, seemed to melt the frost that had gathered round my spirit since I had crossed the Channel. I longed to unburden myself—to speak the name of my late husband, to tell of the slow, crushing tyranny that had driven me to feign my own death and flee under a borrowed name. But the fear was a physical thing, a hand at my throat.

"Dr. Vance," I said, my voice trembling but firm, "I cannot tell you. The secret is not mine alone to tell; it involves the living, and to speak it would ruin them, and perhaps me. I ask only to be allowed to serve you faithfully, and to be left in peace."

He stared at me for a long moment, the fire crackling loudly in the grate. The labourer on the pallet moaned softly in his stupor. 

"You ask for a heavy price," he said at last, quietly. "Peace is a rare commodity in a house of suffering. But I grant it. You shall keep your secret, Mrs. Maynard, until the day comes when you wish to break the seal yourself. Only promise me this: if the burden becomes too great—if the weight of it threatens to crush you—you will come to me, and not to the canal."

"I promise, sir."

"Good. Then go and fetch your pail, and wash the floor. But eat something first. I will not have you fainting on my clean stones."

He turned back to his patient, dismissing me as abruptly as he had detained me. I walked out into the cold corridor, my heart beating fast and hard against my ribs. The rain still beat against the high windows, but the chill in my blood had abated. I had found an ally—not a confidant, but a steadfast, rugged spirit that recognized the warfare in my own. And in that bleak, foreign house, amidst the scent of blood and rain, I felt for the first time a faint, stubborn spark of hope.
