It was a source of some surprise to Miss Helena Finchley that her mother, a lady of generally robust constitution and an avowed contempt for the fashionable ailments of the age, should have declared herself in need of the waters at Cheltenham. Mrs. Finchley spoke of a languor of the spirits, a want of tone in the system, which only the celebrated mineral springs could hope to remedy. Helena, who had witnessed her mother vanquish a week of rain with nothing more than a brisk walk and a sharp opinion, could not quite reconcile this new delicacy with the familiar figure of her parent. Yet, to Cheltenham they came, and took lodgings in a respectable house on the Montpellier Walk, where the air was said to be particularly salubrious.

Their days settled into a routine of prescribed healthfulness. They walked the Promenade in the morning, where Mrs. Finchley would greet their neighbours with a civil nod but decline all invitations to join walking parties, pleading the very fragility that had brought her there. They drank the waters at the Pump Room, a ceremony which Mrs. Finchley endured with the air of a martyr, declaring the taste to be that of a rusty nail dissolved in tepid disappointment. In the evenings, they would attend the Assembly Rooms, where Mrs. Finchley would sit in quiet observation, her fan moving with a slow, thoughtful rhythm that seemed at odds with the general gaiety. To Helena, the whole enterprise appeared a magnificent and rather expensive fiction; her mother was no more in need of the waters than a soldier in need of a parasol. She was, however, a dutiful daughter, and so she played her part, offering sympathy for the headaches her mother did not have and admiring the improved complexion that was, in truth, no different from before.

It was nearly a fortnight into their stay before the true motive of their visit began to reveal itself. The revelation came not in a single dramatic moment, but as a series of small, disconnected observations that slowly cohered into a startling picture. Helena first noticed a change in her mother’s attention at the Pump Room. The slow, sweeping gaze of the first week had narrowed, and now seemed to fix upon the door with a peculiar intensity, as if awaiting an arrival. Then, there was the matter of the morning walk. Mrs. Finchley began to suggest routes that took them past a particular crescent of houses, and her pace would slacken as they drew near, her eyes scanning the windows with an eagerness she took no pains to conceal.

The final piece of the puzzle was supplied by the landlady, a Mrs. Prattle, whose information was as abundant as her cap ribbons. In the course of inquiring about the best place to have a bonnet re-trimmed, Mrs. Finchley mentioned the crescent.

“Ah, yes,” said Mrs. Prattle, beaming. “A very fine address. Colonel Ainsworth has taken number seven for the season. A most distinguished gentleman. A widower now, poor man, but his first wife was a great beauty, I am told.”

Helena saw her mother’s hand, which had been resting on the counter, clench ever so slightly. The name was spoken with a reverence that had nothing to do with military rank. Ainsworth. The name seemed to hang in the air of the small parlour long after Mrs. Prattle had bustled away.

That evening, Helena resolved to understand the matter. As her mother sat by the fire, ostensibly reading a volume of sermons but in fact staring into the flames, Helena chose her moment.

“Mama,” she began, with a casualness that felt entirely false, “the landlady spoke of a Colonel Ainsworth. I do not believe we are acquainted with the family.”

Mrs. Finchley did not look up from her book, but a faint flush crept up her neck. “It is a common enough name, I dare say.”

“Perhaps. But it is a curious coincidence, is it not? That he should be here, of all places, at the very time we have come for your health.”

Her mother closed the book with a soft thud. She turned to Helena, and her expression was not one of guilt, but of a kind of weary triumph. “Helena, I have never pretended to you that I am a fool. You have known from the first day that I am not ill.”

“I confess I found it difficult to believe.”

“And you were right to do so. A woman of my age and sense does not travel fifty miles to drink foul-tasting water for a palpitation of the heart that is, in fact, only a restlessness of the mind.” She paused, and the flush on her cheeks deepened. “I came to Cheltenham because I had heard that Colonel Ainsworth was to be here.”

The frankness of the admission left Helena momentarily without speech. She had prepared herself for denial, for embarrassment, for a dozen other maternal defences. She had not prepared herself for this simple, startling confession. “Colonel Ainsworth?”

“A man I knew many years ago, before your father. He made me an offer of marriage, which I was obliged, for reasons of family, to refuse. He has been a widower for two years now. His wife, I understand, was a very amiable woman, but her health was always delicate.”

In this one speech, the entire architecture of their visit was laid bare. The illness, the waters, the languor—all had been a stage, set for a single actor. Helena felt a strange and conflicting rush of sensations. There was, first, a sense of the profound absurdity of it all; her mother, that pillar of propriety and common sense, had concocted this elaborate and rather undignified plot. It was mortifying to think of the subterfuge, the whispered consultations with Mrs. Prattle, the feigned headaches.

Yet, beneath the absurdity, another feeling stirred. It was something akin to admiration. To have carried such a hope in her breast for so many years, to have arranged this entire campaign with such quiet determination—it spoke of a depth of feeling, a romantic persistence, that Helena had never once suspected in her pragmatic parent. Her mother was not merely a mother; she was a woman who had once had a choice to make, a life that might have been different, and who now, at this late hour, was seeking to reclaim it.

She was uncertain whether to be shocked or delighted, and found she could not settle upon either. The conduct was undeniably shocking in its deception, yet the motive was so full of a quiet, desperate courage that it commanded a sort of delight.

“And what,” Helena asked at last, her voice softer than she intended, “do you mean to do now?”

Mrs. Finchley rose and looked out of the window towards the darkening crescent. “What any sensible woman would do,” she said, her voice regaining its customary crispness. “I mean to walk past number seven tomorrow morning at precisely eleven o’clock, when the Colonel is known to take the air. And I expect you, my dear, to walk beside me and look properly astonished should we happen to encounter him.”

Helena could not help but smile. The romance of the situation was entirely subsumed by the mechanics of its execution. Her mother was not a swooning girl, but a general planning a campaign. “And if he does not recall you, Mama?”

Mrs. Finchley turned from the window, and in the lamplight, her eyes held a spark of youthful fire. “My dear Helena,” she said, “a man does not forget the woman who refused him. He may forgive it, he may resent it, but he does not forget it. Now, be a good girl and ring for the tea. We have a very important morning ahead of us.”
