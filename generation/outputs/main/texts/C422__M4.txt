The assembly rooms at Brindlecombe were stifling, even with the long windows thrown open to the sea. Mrs. Araminta Vellacott plied her fan with a vigour that spoke less of the heat than of a simmering, strategic impatience. Her gaze, sharp as a heron’s, swept the crowded room, past the naval officers with their sunburnt noses, past the clutch of clergymen’s wives, and settled with predatory focus on a party just entering.

“There,” she breathed, her fan snapping shut. “Cordelia, Mirabel. Do not gape. That is he.”

Cordelia, the elder, possessed of a placid temperament and a tendency to see the best in everyone, looked with polite interest. The gentleman in question was a slight, fair-haired youth, his neck seemingly too slender for the elaborate knot of his cravat. He was accompanied by a tall, severe-looking woman in drab grey, who guided him through the throng with the practised ease of a sheepdog herding a reluctant lamb.

Mirabel, younger and of a more acerbic wit, squinted. “He looks as though he is on his way to be measured for a suit of armour he has no intention of wearing.”

“Hush,” their mother hissed, though her own expression flickered with a momentary doubt. “Lord Ashby-Whytcombe is but seventeen. A delicate age. He will fill out. And his title is one of the oldest in the county. His seat, Wren’s Hollow, is said to be magnificent. Now, smile. Both of you.”

The Vellacott sisters exchanged a glance. It was a glance laden with a season’s worth of unspoken commentary, a silent language honed by years of enduring their mother’s matrimonial campaigns. They smiled.

Mrs. Vellacott advanced, her daughters trailing in her wake like two elegant, well-dressed frigates. “Lord Ashby-Whytcombe,” she said, her voice a melodious chime. “What a pleasure. I am Mrs. Vellacott. We are lately of Bath, but the sea air is so restorative, is it not? May I present my daughters, Miss Vellacott and Miss Mirabel Vellacott.”

The young lord started, his pale blue eyes widening. He looked, for a moment, like a startled hare. “Ah. Yes. How do you do.” His voice had not yet broken; it emerged as a high, uncertain squeak. He bowed, a jerky, unpractised motion that threatened the stability of his cravat.

The governess stepped forward, her voice a low, firm monotone. “His lordship is fatigued from his journey. I am Miss Pettigrew. I have the care of his lordship’s education.”

“Of course, of course,” Mrs. Vellacott said, her smile never faltering, though her eyes had developed a slight, frozen quality. “A boy’s education is so very important. Cordelia, dear, perhaps you might fetch his lordship a glass of negus? The evenings can be so chill.”

Cordelia, ever obedient, moved to the refreshment table. Mirabel, left to her own devices, decided to probe. “Do you enjoy the sea, Lord Ashby-Whytcombe?” she asked. “I hear the bathing machines here are quite invigorating.”

The boy’s face flushed a deep, painful crimson. He looked desperately at Miss Pettigrew. “I… I have not yet… The weather has been…”

“His lordship is studying the principles of hydrostatics,” Miss Pettigrew interjected smoothly. “He finds the practical application of the science in the local tides a fascinating subject for his notebooks.”

“Ah,” said Mirabel. “Hydrostatics.” She pronounced the word with a certain relish.

Cordelia returned with the negus. The lord took it, his hand trembling so that a drop of the dark liquid splashed onto his pristine cuff. He stared at the stain in horror.

“Oh, allow me,” Cordelia said kindly, producing a handkerchief. She dabbed at the spot with a gentle, motherly air. The boy looked at her with an expression of profound gratitude, as a drowning man might regard a rescuer.

Mrs. Vellacott watched this tableau, and the light in her eyes, which had been a beacon of ambition, dimmed to a guttering candle. The title, the estate, the grand alliance—all of it seemed to recede behind the reality of a tongue-tied boy and his dragon of a governess. The disappointment was a physical thing, a sour taste in her mouth.

The first crisis came the following afternoon, on the promenade. Mrs. Vellacott, having rallied her spirits with a pot of strong tea and a review of Debrett’s, had decided that a public display of intimacy was required. She had manoeuvred her party to coincide with the Ashby-Whytcombe household’s constitutional.

“Lord Ashby-Whytcombe,” she cried, with the forced gaiety of a woman determined to enjoy herself. “What a delightful coincidence! We were just remarking on the bracing quality of the wind. Cordelia, my love, you are quite blown about. Do take his lordship’s arm for support.”

Before the boy could react, Mrs. Vellacott had deftly insinuated Cordelia’s hand into the crook of his elbow. The effect was immediate and disastrous. Lord Ashby-Whytcombe, startled by the sudden contact, stumbled. His free arm flailed, knocking his hat from his head. The wind, eager for sport, seized it and sent it bowling along the cobbled path.

“My hat!” he cried, his voice cracking with genuine distress.

“I shall get it!” Cordelia said, and before anyone could stop her, she was in pursuit, her own bonnet strings flying.

It was then that a large, friendly-looking dog, which had been ambling at the heels of a fisherman, decided to join the chase. It bounded after the hat with joyful barks, reached it just as Cordelia did, and in the ensuing scuffle, succeeded in trampling the hat into the dust and knocking Cordelia entirely off her feet.

The scene was one of pure pandemonium. Cordelia sat on the ground, her skirts in disarray. The dog was now worrying the mangled hat with gusto. Lord Ashby-Whytcombe stood frozen, his face a mask of utter horror. Mirabel, to her eternal credit, was the first to act. She strode forward, seized the dog by its collar, and retrieved the ruined hat.

“A valiant rescue, sister,” she said, helping Cordelia to her feet. “Though I fear the casualty is beyond saving.”

Mrs. Vellacott’s face was a study in conflicting emotions. Here was a public humiliation of the first order. But here, also, was an opportunity. She swooped down upon the young lord, who looked as if he might weep.

“The poor boy is quite overset!” she declared to the gathering crowd, her voice ringing with maternal solicitude. “The wind, the fright! Cordelia, my dear, are you hurt? No? Then let us get his lordship back to the hotel at once. He needs a restorative. Mirabel, take his other arm.”

The sisters, their faces carefully neutral, flanked the trembling youth. They formed a protective phalanx around him, a shield of Vellacott womanhood, and marched him away from the scene of the catastrophe. Miss Pettigrew, who had been watching the entire debacle with an impassive expression, followed a few paces behind.

Back in the hotel’s conservatory, a pot of tea was ordered. Lord Ashby-Whytcombe sat, still pale, clutching his teacup like a talisman. The ruined hat lay on a table like a fallen soldier.

“It was a very fine hat,” he murmured, more to himself than anyone else.

“It was,” Cordelia agreed gently. “But it is only a hat, my lord. And I am quite unharmed. You must not regard it.”

He looked up at her, his eyes full of a desperate, boyish sincerity. “You are very kind, Miss Vellacott. Kinder than I deserve. I am not… I am not very good at… at this.” He gestured vaguely at the world in general.

“At what?” Mirabel asked, genuinely curious.

“At being… a lord,” he confessed, his voice dropping to a whisper. “At being looked at. At being expected to know what to say. Miss Pettigrew says I must practise, but I always say the wrong thing. I would far rather be at home with my books.”

Mrs. Vellacott, who had been preparing a soothing speech about the trifling nature of mishaps, stopped. She looked at the boy—for he was a boy, she could no longer deny it—and saw not a title, not an estate, but a lonely, awkward child burdened with a name he did not yet know how to carry. The grand ambition that had propelled her from Bath to Sussex, that had dictated her daughters’ every smile and curtsy, suddenly felt hollow and ridiculous.

She thought of her own husband, a good, kind, unambitious man, and of the comfortable, unremarkable life they had built. Was this what she wanted for her daughters? A lifetime of managing a nervous, reluctant peer? A title bought at the price of a lifetime’s anxiety?

Cordelia, meanwhile, had taken the boy’s hand. “My lord,” she said, “I have a brother who is terribly shy. He finds that if he imagines everyone else is far more frightened than he is, it helps a great deal. And if that fails, he simply asks people about their dogs. Everyone likes to talk about their dogs.”

The young lord looked at her, a flicker of hope in his eyes. “Do they?”

“They do,” Mirabel confirmed. “It is a truth universally acknowledged.”

Miss Pettigrew, standing by the door, allowed herself a small, almost imperceptible nod of approval.

Mrs. Vellacott cleared her throat. “Lord Ashby-Whytcombe,” she said, her voice softer than it had been all week. “I wonder if you and Miss Pettigrew might join us for a picnic on the morrow? Nothing formal, you understand. Just a simple repast on the beach. Mirabel is quite the expert at building a fire for toasting cheese.”

The boy’s face lit up with a genuine, uncomplicated smile. “I should like that above all things,” he said. “I have never toasted cheese on a beach.”

Mrs. Vellacott smiled back. It was a different smile from the ones she had worn for the past week—less brilliant, perhaps, but infinitely warmer. She glanced at her daughters, who were now discussing the best method for achieving a perfect golden crust, and felt a strange sense of peace. The title might be lost, but the holiday, she suspected, was saved.
