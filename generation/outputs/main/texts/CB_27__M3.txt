The rain of a late November evening in Dunleath fell not in drops, but in long, sweeping, sorrowful lines, that lashed the grimy window-panes of my surgery and beat a heavy, muffled dirge upon the slated roof. The town below was a dark aggregate of towering chimneys and fever-dens; the air, even at this late hour, reeked of smoke and damp, and the ceaseless, grinding thunder of the great spinning-machines at Vane’s mill rolled through the streets like the pulsing of a monstrous, restless heart.

I stood by my deal table, wiping the blood from my hands. I had just set the broken arm of a child of nine. Her name was Martha. She was a piecer in the carding-room, and the relentless iron teeth of the machinery had caught her sleeve, dragging her arm into the cold, unyielding gears. The bone was fractured in two places. She had not wept. These children of the Lancashire mills learn early to stifle the cry of agony; tears are a luxury that buys no bread, and delays only bring the overlooker’s strap.

As I folded the clean linen over her small, thin, waxen arm, a hot, unquenchable fire burned in my own breast. I was but a young man, and a poor one, yet the fierce sense of an injustice that mocked the very heavens urged me to a reckoning. How long was this sacrifice of innocents to continue? The scripture saith, "Suffer little children to come unto me," but the masters of Dunleath seemed to have fashioned a grim, industrial gospel of their own, reading the text as "Suffer little children to toil unto death."

The following morning, the sullen dawn breaking pale and sickly through the factory smoke, I sought out Mr. Vane in his counting-house. He was a man of hard, dry, metallic features, his eyes the colour of cold slag, his mouth set in a line of inflexible commerce. He sat at his ledger, pen in hand, the very embodiment of that calculating spirit which weighs human flesh against the price of cotton.

"Mr. Vane," I said, my voice trembling despite my hard-won struggle for restraint, "I come not to intrude upon your business, but to discharge the solemn duty of my calling. I set Martha Greenwood’s arm last night. She is one of a hundred. The children are overworked; their frames are breaking under a toil that would tax a grown man. I must press upon you, sir, to shorten the hours of the carding-rooms, or the hospital will be filled with cripples before the winter is out."

He laid down his pen and looked at me with a slow, measured contempt that stung like a physical blow. "You are very zealous, Mr. Thorne. Pray, confine your zeal to your gallipots and your lancets. The hours are what the trade demands. If we shorten them, the orders go to Manchester, and Dunleath starves. Mind your trade, sir, and I will mind mine."

"My trade is to heal," I retorted, a sudden surge of passion breaking through my guarded speech, "and I tell you that your trade is murder! You are grinding the life-blood of these children into your yarn!"

"Enough," he said, his face darkening with a Napoleonic sternness. "You forget yourself. Leave my counting-house."

I left, burning with a bitter indignation that seemed to scorch the very air I breathed. I walked the damp, cobbled streets, seeing in every pale, hollow-eyed child that hurried past to the mill-bell a living accusation against the smug complacency of wealth. I felt the vast, unyielding weight of the world pressing upon my solitary head. I had no influence, no power, save the poor privilege of binding up the wounds that authority refused to prevent. 

That evening, as the damp chill of the factory fog crept into my solitary room, a knock came at my door. I opened it, expecting some new messenger of misery from the alleys. Instead, I found a figure cloaked in dark grey, the hood drawn closely about her face.

"Mr. Thorne," said a voice, low, yet vibrant with a clear, decided resonance. 

I started. It was Miss Clara Vane. I knew her by sight—the mill-owner’s daughter. She had been a shadow at the edge of my consciousness, moving through the gloom of Dunleath like a stray beam of sunlight in a subterranean vault. I had thought her merely a timid, delicate blossom, reared in the hothouse of her father’s wealth, deaf to the grinding of the machinery that paid for her silks.

"Miss Vane!" I exclaimed, vastly astonished. "Enter, I pray you. But the air is keen; you should not be abroad in this fog."

She stepped within, and throwing back her hood, revealed a countenance that struck me with its pale, searching earnestness. Her eyes, dark and profound, met mine with a directness that disarmed all suspicion. There was no simpering softness there, but the steadfast gaze of a soul awake to moral duty.

"I must be brief," she said, her tones combative, yet probing, as if testing the mettle she found in me. "I overheard your interview with my father this morning. I stood behind the partition. I heard his decree, and your protest."

"It was a fruitless protest," I said, the bitterness rising again to my lips. "He bids me mind my trade. I cannot stem the tide of this cruelty. I can only stand by and watch the victims bleed."

"And you call that fruitless?" she asked, a sudden flash of vehement feeling lighting her eye. "You think because you cannot move the mountain, the stroke of your pickaxe is in vain? I tell you, no! You have wounded his pride, and you have roused a slumbering conscience in at least one of his household."

She paused, and drawing from beneath her cloak a small, heavy purse, she laid it firmly upon my bare table. The metal clinked with a sharp, decisive sound in the quiet room.

"What is this?" I demanded, recoiling slightly. "I cannot take money from you—from Mr. Vane’s daughter."

"It is not Mr. Vane’s money," she answered, her voice dropping to a low, intense whisper, but losing none of its vigour. "It is mine. My mother left me an independence, small, but my own. I have seen the children, Mr. Thorne. I have looked into the alleys. I know what the mill does. You spoke of a dispensary for the mill-hands last month in the parish church. You wish to establish a place where they may be treated without losing a day’s wage. This is for that."

I looked at the purse, then at her. The contrast was sharp and strange: the daughter of the oppressor, standing in my poverty-stricken surgery, offering her private hoard to succour the oppressed. It was an anomaly that bewildered, while it moved me.

"You are risking much," I said, gravely. "If your father knew—"

"My father knows nothing of the motions of my heart," she broke in, with a fierce, proud defiance that thrilled me to the core. "Nor shall he. I am not come to ask his leave to do right. I am come to ask if you will accept this trust, and use it for the children. I cannot set their broken limbs, but I can arm the hand that does. Will you take it?"

There was a pressing urgency in her manner, a moral seriousness that demanded a candid answer. I felt the conflict within my own breast—the pride that shrank from accepting aid from a woman, and the overwhelming, imperative need of the suffering poor. The vision of Martha’s white, tearless face rose before me.

"I will take it," I said. "But I must accept it as a sacred trust. I will not have your generosity betrayed into a system of almsgiving that pauperizes. I will use this to build a place of healing, where the bruised and broken may find refuge."

"It is well," she replied, and a softening, a sudden, touching benignity, stole over her stern features. "I knew you would understand. I knew, when I first saw you bend over the fever-stricken in the wet streets, that you were a man to be trusted with the heavy burdens of this town."

She turned, drawing her cloak about her. "I must go. Expect no thanks, and breathe no word of this. The dispensary must seem to spring from your own exertions, or from public charity. My name must not appear."

"Miss Vane," I said, stepping forward, impelled by an irresistible impulse, "you are an unexpected guest in this house of mourning. You bring a light that I had thought extinguished in Dunleath."

"Do not say light," she answered, pausing at the door, her hand upon the latch. "Say rather a spark struck from the flint of a common sorrow. Keep it burning, Mr. Thorne. Good-night."

She was gone, and I was left alone with the heavy purse upon the table. Outside, the rain beat fiercely against the glass, and the wind howled down the narrow chimneys like a wandering spirit. But the chill had departed from my heart. I felt that I was no longer solitary in my contest with the grinding, pitiless machinery of the place. There was a hidden ally, a steadfast, fervent soul, allied to the cause of the weak and wounded. The furnace of my indignation still burned, but it was tempered now with a steadier, more enduring flame. The work was yet heavy, the adverse winds still blew, but the winter night had lost its utter darkness.
