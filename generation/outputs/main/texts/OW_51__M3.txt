In the northern kingdom of Valtzburg, where the winters are long and the consciences of men are proportionately short, there stood a tower of black iron and grey granite. It was built upon a jagged crag that overlooked the city, and in this tower lived Julian the Miser. Julian was a man of immense wealth and still more immense caution, for he had discovered in his youth that the only thing more dangerous than having no money was allowing other people to know you had it. He had therefore retired from the world, which he considered a very vulgar institution, and devoted his life to the contemplation of his possessions.

Every evening, when the shadows began to gather and the good citizens of Valtzburg were lighting their honest lamps, Julian would descend to his strong-room and count his gold. He counted it by the light of a single tallow candle, not because he could not afford wax—for he could afford anything—but because he maintained that avarice was a vice that required darkness to reach its full perfection. The candle was thin and economical, and it threw a meagre yellow glow upon the heaps of sovereigns and crowns, the chalices and the unset jewels, that lay upon the oaken table. To Julian, this flickering light was the sun of his private universe, and he found it entirely sufficient.

Gold, he would often say to himself, is the only thing that understands one. It does not talk; it merely glitters, and its glitter is the most eloquent language in the world. Julian had no friends, for he considered friendship an extravagance, and he had no relatives, for he had long since outlived them and felt their departure to be a proof of Nature's good taste. He was entirely alone, and he was perfectly content.

One winter evening, when the snow lay thick upon the crags and the wind was singing its wild northern songs through the crevices of the tower, Julian was seated at his table, counting. The candle burned low, and the gold gleamed with a sullen fire. He had just reached seven thousand three hundred and forty-two when a sudden sound disturbed him. It was a knock—a small, timid knock—at the great iron door of the tower.

Julian frowned. It was a rule of his establishment that no one should knock, for the simple reason that no one had any business to knock. He paused, wondering if it were the wind. Then the knock came again, a little louder, and a thin voice called out.

Julian rose, and taking the candle in his hand, he climbed the winding stair and drew back the heavy bolts. The door opened, and there stood a child.

She was a little girl of perhaps seven or eight, with a pale face and large dark eyes, and she was wrapped in a shawl that had once been blue but was now the colour of melancholy. The snow was falling fast, and it lay upon her shoulders like a white mantle. In her hand she held a small brass lamp, which was empty.

"Please, sir," she said, "may I have a light? My mother's lamp has gone out, and it is very cold, and she cannot see to finish her sewing."

Julian looked at her with an expression of profound annoyance. He disliked children on principle, for they were unproductive and noisy, and he disliked appeals to charity, which he considered a form of blackmail.

"I have no light to spare," he said, and made as if to close the door.

"But you have a candle," said the child, pointing to the thin flame in his hand. "It would take but a moment."

Julian hesitated. It was true that it would take but a moment, and yet he resented the intrusion. He looked at the child's lamp, and then at his candle, and he perceived that the giving of a light would cost him nothing. This was, in his view, the only circumstance under which giving was permissible.

"Very well," he said, and held the candle to the wick of her lamp. But the wick was damp, and the flame would not catch, and he was forced to hold it there for some time, while the child stood patiently in the snow. At last the wick caught, and a small steady flame sprang up, and the child's face was illuminated.

"Thank you, sir," she said, and smiled at him.

Then she turned and went away, down the steep path, into the darkness and the falling snow, and Julian stood in the doorway and watched the little point of light receding until it vanished.

He returned to his strong-room and resumed his counting, but he found that he had lost his place. This irritated him. He began again at seven thousand, but the figures would not arrange themselves in his mind. He kept seeing the child's face, pale and small, with the candlelight upon it, and her eyes, which were very large and very dark, and full of something that he could not name, for he had long since banished the vocabulary of emotion from his mental dictionary.

He put down the coins and tried to dismiss the matter. The child was nothing to him. She was merely a unit of the population, and the population was merely a nuisance. He had given her a light, which was more than she had any right to expect, and the transaction was concluded. There was no reason why he should think of her further.

But the reason, though he did not know it, was very simple. The child had smiled at him.

Julian had not been smiled upon for many years. The last person who had smiled at him was his banker, and that had been a professional smile, devoid of all sincerity. But the child had smiled without any motive whatsoever, and Julian found this incomprehensible. In his experience, nothing was done without a motive. Gold was hoarded because it was power; kindness was shown because it was policy; smiles were given because they were a means of obtaining something. But this child had asked for a light, had received it, and had smiled, and had departed. There was no logic in it whatever.

He spent the rest of the evening in a state of considerable irritation. He counted his gold three times, and each time he arrived at a different total, which had never happened before. He went to bed, but he could not sleep. He dreamed—or rather, he lay awake and refused to dream, which is the insomniac's version of the same thing—of the small flame in the brass lamp, and the face in the snow.

The next day he did not count his gold at all. This was unprecedented, and it alarmed him. He told himself that he was unwell, and that the cold had affected him. But he knew that he was not unwell. He was merely disturbed, and disturbance, to a man who has made a religion of tranquillity, is the most serious of maladies.

That night, when the darkness came, he did not light his candle. Instead, he sat in the window of his tower and looked down at the town below. He could see the lights of the houses, hundreds of them, twinkling like earthbound stars. Somewhere down there, in one of those poor and narrow streets, the child and her mother were sitting, and their lamp was burning with the flame he had given them. It was a very small flame, and yet it had entered his tower and disturbed his gold, which was the only thing in the world that he had thought inviolable.

On the third evening, Julian did a remarkable thing. He took his candle and descended the winding stair, and opened the great iron door, and went out into the snow. He had not left the tower for ten years. The cold struck him like a physical blow, and the wind plucked at his cloak, but he went on, down the steep path, into the town.

He found the street, for he had watched the child's departing light with the same precision he devoted to the counting of coin, and he found the house. It was a poor house, with broken shutters and a roof that sagged beneath the weight of the snow. He knocked, and the door was opened by the child.

She looked up at him with her large dark eyes, and Julian, who had not felt awkward in the presence of emperors, found that he had no words. He stood there, an old man in a black cloak, holding a candle that was rapidly guttering in the wind, and he felt as foolish as a man who has spent his life hoarding gold and has suddenly discovered that gold cannot keep him warm.

"I have brought you a candle," he said at last, for it was the only thing he could think of, and indeed the only thing he had brought.

The child took it and looked at it, and then she looked at him. "But we have a lamp now," she said. "You gave us the light for it."

"Then I shall come in," said Julian, "and sit by your fire, if you will permit me."

The child stepped aside, and Julian entered the poor house, and sat down upon a wooden stool, and looked at the fire. The mother was there, sewing, and the child sat beside him, and neither of them spoke. The room was small and bare, and there was no gold in it anywhere, and yet Julian found that he was not uncomfortable.

He sat there for a long time, watching the fire, and the face of the child, and the small steady flame of the lamp. And he perceived, with the clarity that comes to men only when they have ceased to be entirely rational, that he had spent his life in the worship of a false deity. Gold, he reflected, is a beautiful thing, but it is a cold companion. It glitters, but it does not smile. It accumulates, but it does not give. It is the perfect materialisation of selfishness, and selfishness, he now saw, is merely loneliness dressed in fine clothes.

He returned to his tower that night, and he climbed the winding stair, and he entered his strong-room. The gold was there, gleaming in the darkness. He looked at it for a long time, and then he blew out his candle.

In the morning, the people of Valtzburg saw a strange sight. Julian the Miser was standing at the door of his tower, and he was giving away his gold. He gave it to the poor, and the sick, and the cold, and the hungry. He gave it with both hands, and he did not count it, and he did not regret it. And when the gold was all gone, and the strong-room was empty, he went back into the tower and sat in his window, and looked down at the town, and saw the lights of the houses, which were brighter now, and warmer, and more numerous.

And he said to himself: "I have lost a fortune, and I have gained a candle."

Which was, perhaps, the most paradoxical thing he had ever said, and therefore the truest. For in the kingdom of Valtzburg, where the winters are long and the consciences of men are short, it was generally agreed that Julian the Miser had gone mad. But the child, who knew better, smiled when she heard of it, and went about her business with her little brass lamp, which burned very steadily, and gave a very sufficient light.
