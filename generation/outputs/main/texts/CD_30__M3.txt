If any well-regulated Englishman, of a peaceable disposition and a strictly moderate appetite, had been told that he would one day find himself panting in a dark Parisian doorway, grasping a brown paper parcel of unknown and possibly treasonable contents, while the bloodthirsty citizens of France howled for a printer’s head in the street directly beneath his boots, he would have laughed the prophecy to scorn. Yet, Mr. Ebenezer Pumblechook, of Puddleby-on-the-Marsh, a dealer in second-hand literature and a man who had never done a brave thing in all his forty-seven years, was precisely in this condition.

He had crossed the Channel with the modest intention of collecting a long-overdue debt from one Monsieur Beaumarchais, a printer of broadsides in the Rue de la Croix. Mr. Pumblechook’s nature was deeply opposed to sudden movements, loud voices, and any form of political enthusiasm. He liked his tea strong, his accounting neat, and his streets quiet. Paris, in this wet September of 1792, possessed none of these qualities. The city was a great, boiling cauldron of fury and wet wool, seething with a patriotic frenzy that smelled powerfully of garlic, damp plumes, and unwashed civic virtue.

Upon arriving at the printer’s shop, Mr. Pumblechook found not the orderly establishment he had imagined, but a scene of utter desolation. The windows were shattered, the press lay overturned in a puddle of spilled ink that mingled with the mud, and the shelves were bare. The only occupant was a boy, perhaps twelve years old, with a face of startling paleness and large, dark eyes. He was desperately cramming a collection of loose papers into a leather satchel.

“Where is Monsieur Beaumarchais?” demanded Mr. Pumblechook, adjusting his spectacles. “I am here on business of the utmost gravity. He owes my employer three pounds and sixpence.”

The boy looked up, his hands trembling. “Ah, Monsieur! He is gone! The patriots came for him an hour ago. They have dragged him to the Abbaye. He is arrested, Monsieur! He is lost!”

“Arrested?” echoed Mr. Pumblechook, a cold sweat breaking out upon his brow. “For debt? My dear boy, I am sure we can come to some arrangement. I do not wish to press charges. I only wish to return to England with a receipt, or at the very least, a promissory note.”

“For printing the wrong words, Monsieur!” cried the boy, whose name, it transpired, was little Philippe. “They say he printed lies against the Republic! They will cut off his head! They will do it tomorrow, or perhaps today, if the blade is sharp enough!”

Mr. Pumblechook’s heart, which was not made of stone but rather of a rather yielding, cautious material, quailed. He saw his three pounds and sixpence dissolving into the sanguinary mists of revolution. He turned to leave, suggesting that he would return to his inn and write a stern letter to the British Embassy.

“Wait, Monsieur! I beg of you!” The boy seized his coat. “You are English? You are leaving Paris today?”

“I had intended to,” said Mr. Pumblechook, nervously. “I perceive it is a very unhealthy climate for a man of business.”

“Then you must take these!” Little Philippe thrust a parcel into his hands. It was heavy, wrapped in coarse brown paper, and tied with a piece of string. “They are his papers. His lists. His true broadsides. If the patriots find them, they will say he is a spy for the Austrians, and they will execute him twice! But if they are in England, he is safe! He is only a printer who fled his debts! Please, Monsieur! You must carry them!”

Mr. Pumblechook stared at the parcel as if it were a live grenade. He had never been entrusted with anything more dangerous than a bad shilling. The very idea of smuggling political papers out of a city in uproar was repugnant to every fibre of his commercial being.

“No, no, my good boy,” he stammered, trying to push the parcel back. “Quite impossible. I am a respectable bookseller. I cannot involve myself in French politics. It is entirely against the rules. I have a schedule. I have a return ticket for the packet boat.”

“They will kill him!” shrieked the boy, his voice cracking. “They will put his head on a pike! Is that your schedule, Monsieur?”

A sudden, terrible roar rose from the street outside. The mob was returning. Mr. Pumblechook heard the tramp of heavy boots, the clatter of pikes, and the singing of the Carmagnole, sung with more enthusiasm than musicality. He looked at the door, then at the boy, and then at the parcel.

And so, driven by a motive he could not entirely explain, Mr. Pumblechook did the first brave thing he had ever done in his life. He snatched up the parcel, tucked it hastily beneath his greatcoat, and ran for his life.

He ran with the speed of a man who has just realized the full extent of his folly. He ran past the Café de la Paix, where citizens were dancing a frantic, leaping dance that seemed to involve a great deal of stamping. He ran past the Pont Neuf, where a fishwife was haranguing a crowd about the virtues of Liberty, Equality, and very cheap bread. He ran past a barricade of old furniture, which had been arranged with a certain artistic flair, but which struck him as a very inconvenient place to keep a wardrobe.

At last, he reached the courtyard of the coaching inn, the Cadran Bleu. The London coach was waiting, its horses steaming in the damp air. The driver, a surly Englishman named Mr. Grummitt, was already upon the box, shouting at a porter.

“Hold hard there, Mr. Pumblechook!” cried Mr. Grummitt, as the bookseller scrambled frantically into the coach. “You’re late! I was within a half-second of leaving you to the Jacobins!”

“I was detained,” gasped Mr. Pumblechook, clutching the parcel beneath his coat. “Drive on! For the love of all that is holy, drive on!”

The coach lurched forward, and Mr. Pumblechook was thrown back against the worn velvet cushions. He sat very still, his eyes wide, his hands gripping the parcel so tightly that his knuckles shone white beneath his skin. The other passengers regarded him with suspicion. There was a French merchant, who was trembling violently; an English spinster, who was reading a book of sermons; and a large gentleman in a red coat, who was eating a cold fowl.

“You look as if you’ve seen a ghost, sir,” observed the gentleman in the red coat, tearing off a leg with his teeth.

“I have seen a mob,” said Mr. Pumblechook. “And a printer. And a boy. And a parcel.”

“Ah,” said the gentleman, who was not listening. “Very bad for the digestion, mobs. Have a wing?”

Mr. Pumblechook declined the wing. He spent the entire journey to Calais in a state of mortal terror. Every jolt of the coach, every shout from the road, every inspection of papers at the town gates, filled him with a dreadful certainty that he would be discovered, dragged out, and made an example of. He imagined himself standing before a tribunal, a fierce-looking man in a red cap demanding to know why he, Ebenezer Pumblechook, a respectable Englishman, was carrying the treasonable correspondence of a French printer.

He imagined the blade falling. He imagined the crowd cheering. He imagined, with a sudden pang, that he would never see Puddleby-on-the-Marsh again, nor his shop, nor his cat, nor his small, neat ledger, in which three pounds and sixpence was now written off as a bad debt.

But the inspection at the gates was cursory. The guards, who were more interested in a wagon of wine that was trying to enter the city, glanced at his passport, noted his respectable appearance, and waved him through. Mr. Pumblechook, who had never been so terrified in his life, found himself, as the coach rattled across the cobbles of Calais, suddenly and inexplicably alive.

He stood on the deck of the packet boat, watching the grey waves of the Channel slap against the hull. The brown paper parcel sat on the rail beside him, damp with sea spray. He had done it. He had carried the papers. He had saved the printer from a second execution, though he could not save him from the first. He had done a brave thing, and he was surprised to find that it did not feel entirely unpleasant.

Mr. Pumblechook opened his small, neat ledger. He drew a line through the name of Monsieur Beaumarchais. Beside it, in his careful, clerkly hand, he wrote: “Bad debt. Paid in full.” He closed the book, tucked it into his coat, and turned his face toward England. The wind was cold, and the sea was rough, and he was thoroughly, completely, and entirely alive.

For perhaps the first time in his forty-seven years, Mr. Pumblechook was not thinking about his accounts. He was thinking about a boy with a pale face, and a printer who owed him three pounds and sixpence, and how strange it was that a man should travel all the way to Paris to do a brave thing, and find that the bravest thing of all was simply to go home.
