It was a truth very generally acknowledged in the parish of Ashcombe, that Mr. Edmund Hartley, being a widower of four years’ standing and possessed of a comfortable income, must soon look about him for a second wife. Miss Arabella Vane, who had formed a particular and intimate friendship with the late Mrs. Hartley, and who still continued her acquaintance with the family at Hartley Park, felt it her duty to see that he did not look about him to any disadvantage. She had long since fixed upon a very suitable person in her cousin, Miss Penelope Croft; and she had of late begun to suspect that a most unsuitable person had fixed herself upon Mr. Hartley.

This person was Miss Price, the governess to his two young daughters, a young woman of respectable family but no fortune, who had been with them for a twelvemonth. Miss Vane’s suspicions had been awakened by a number of small circumstances, which, taken together, formed a body of evidence that she considered conclusive. Miss Price, for instance, had lately taken to reading poetry aloud in the evenings, not, as was proper, for the instruction of her pupils, but in a low, expressive voice, that seemed designed to engage the attention of their father. She had been observed to linger in the drawing-room long after the children were abed; and, what was most alarming, Mr. Hartley had been heard to praise the neatness of her darning.

To Miss Vane, a woman of great discernment and quick judgement, these were not the actions of a mere employee. They were the calculated advances of a designing creature, and the soft, foolish compliance of a man about to be trapped. It was a situation that called for immediate intervention, and Miss Vane was not a woman to shirk a duty.

She called at Hartley Park on a Tuesday morning, full of a resolution that was only sharpened by the sight of Miss Price walking in the shrubbery with the children. Miss Price, on seeing her, curtsied with a quiet civility that Miss Vane could only interpret as the smugness of a confidence well-founded. After inquiring for Mr. Hartley and being told he was engaged on business in his study, she requested a few minutes’ private conversation with Miss Price. The request was granted with a look of mild surprise, and they adjourned to the morning-room.

Miss Vane began with some general remarks upon the weather and the parish, before coming, with a delicate but firm turn, to the subject at hand. “I am sure you will not think me impertinent, Miss Price,” she said, fixing the governess with a look of earnest concern, “for I speak as a friend to this family. Mrs. Hartley, dear creature, was my particular friend, and I feel a responsibility for the welfare of her husband and children.”

Miss Price’s expression was one of polite, but uncomprehending, attention. “You are very good, Miss Vane.”

“It is precisely because I am good, or at least endeavour to be, that I must speak plainly,” Miss Vane continued, warming to her theme. “A young woman in your situation, with your… advantages of person and manner, must be aware of the dangers that surround her. The line between a proper deference and an improper familiarity is a fine one, and once it is crossed, the consequences for a person of limited means can be quite ruinous.”

Miss Price remained silent, her hands folded in her lap. She did not blush, nor did she look away, but regarded Miss Vane with a steady, thoughtful gaze.

Miss Vane felt a small flicker of irritation at this lack of response. She had expected confusion, perhaps a tearful denial; this composure was unnerving. She resolved to be more direct. “There have been observations made,” she said, lowering her voice confidentially, “of a nature that cannot be mistaken. The reading of poetry in the evenings, for example. And your presence in the drawing-room after hours. It is not for me to say what intentions may lie behind such conduct, but I can assure you that it is not gone unnoticed by those who have Mr. Hartley’s best interests at heart.”

At this, Miss Price seemed to understand at last. A faint, almost imperceptible smile touched her lips. “My reading, ma’am, is for the improvement of my pupils. Mr. Hartley was so good as to approve the works I have chosen for them.”

“And the lingering?” Miss Vane pressed, her voice sharpening. “The… the darning? You must know, Miss Price, that a governess who sets her cap at her employer is a creature universally condemned. It is a form of mercenary conduct for which there can be no excuse. I am sure I need not say more.”

Miss Price’s composure did not falter. “No, ma’am,” she said, with a perfect and infuriating calm, “I believe you have made your meaning very clear. You are concerned that I am forming an attachment to Mr. Hartley.”

“I am concerned for Mr. Hartley’s peace and his daughters’ security,” Miss Vane corrected her haughtily. “An attachment of that kind would be a disaster.”

“Then you may set your mind at ease,” Miss Price replied, rising from her chair. The movement was graceful and final. “I have no such attachment, nor have I given the slightest encouragement for one. My only object is the proper discharge of my duties. If my conduct has been misunderstood, I can only regret it. Now, if you will excuse me, the children’s French lesson is due to begin.”

She curtsied and left the room, leaving Miss Vane in a state of considerable vexation. The interview had not gone at all as planned. Miss Price had not been put in her place; she had, in some indefinable way, put Miss Vane in hers. The woman was either a consummate actress or, more alarmingly, entirely innocent. But Miss Vane knew the world, and she knew that innocence in a governess was a fragile and temporary thing. She was more convinced than ever that the creature was a menace.

She was denied the satisfaction of venting her feelings to Mr. Hartley, who remained closeted with his steward. She left the house with a heavy heart, resolved to speak to him at the earliest opportunity and, if necessary, to write to her cousin Penelope to urge a more frequent presence at Hartley Park.

Within the study she had pictured as the scene of a future domestic bliss, Mr. Hartley was, in fact, wrestling with a problem of a different sort. His steward had just left, and the papers on his desk lay unheeded. His mind was not on rents or crop yields, but on the quiet woman who was at that very moment teaching his daughters the subjunctive mood.

He had been a widower long enough to have forgotten the easy intimacy of married life, and had grown accustomed to the ordered solitude of his house. Miss Price’s arrival had unsettled that order. He had hired her for her excellent references and her calm, sensible manner. He had not anticipated that he would come to find the sight of her calm, sensible manner the chief pleasure of his day. He admired the way she guided his daughters without ever raising her voice; he found himself listening to the sound of her voice reading to them, and had been foolish enough to comment on her darning, only because the sight of her patient, domestic skill had moved him in a way he could not easily name.

He was not a man of great passion, but he was a man of honour, and he knew that the situation was becoming untenable. He could not, in good conscience, continue to employ a woman whose presence caused him such distraction, nor could he offer for her when he knew that a governess in such a position could hardly refuse her employer without losing her situation. It was a dilemma that had kept him awake for three nights.

Just then, a note was brought to him from Miss Vane. It was filled with oblique warnings about the dangers of unguarded conduct and the necessity of protecting his children from unsuitable influences. He read it with a growing sense of grim irony. Miss Vane, with her worldly wisdom, saw a designing woman where there was only a dutiful one. She saw a trap where he saw a future he scarcely dared to hope for.

He rang the bell and asked for Miss Price to be sent to him.

When she entered, she looked as serene as ever, though he fancied he could see a new wariness in her eyes. He did not speak of Miss Vane’s visit. Instead, he came to the point at once.

“Miss Price,” he began, his voice a little unsteady. “I have asked you here to tell you that I find it necessary to make a change in your employment.”

A flicker of alarm crossed her face, but she said nothing.

“I cannot, with honour, continue to employ you under the present circumstances,” he went on, struggling to find the right words. “It would be unfair to you, and… and to myself. Therefore, I must ask you to leave my service.”

Her face became very pale. “May I ask, sir, if my conduct has been found wanting in some way?”

“No,” he said quickly. “On the contrary. Your conduct has been everything that is proper and admirable. It is my own conduct that is at fault. Miss Price—Amelia—” The use of her Christian name was a transgression, and they both felt it. “I cannot let you go without telling you the truth. I am asking you to leave my service, because I wish you to enter it on different terms. I am asking you to be my wife.”

He saw the surprise in her eyes give way to a look of such profound and gentle astonishment that he feared he had made a terrible mistake. But then, she smiled, a real smile this time, without a trace of irony or defence.

“I believe, sir,” she said softly, “that I should like that very much.”

At that moment, Miss Arabella Vane was at home, composing a letter to her cousin. She wrote of the necessity of constant vigilance, of the insidious nature of a governess’s ambitions, and of her own determination to see justice done. She was certain that with a little more time and a firm hand, the matter could be brought to a happy conclusion for all parties, save, of course, the unfortunate Miss Price. It was a pity, she reflected, but a woman in her position could not afford to be sentimental. She sealed the letter, well satisfied with her morning’s work.
