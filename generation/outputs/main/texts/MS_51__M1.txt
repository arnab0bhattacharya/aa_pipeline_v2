It is a truth, felt rather than known, that the heart is a fabric woven of many threads; some are of gold, and some of the palest silk, and some are stained with a darkness that no subsequent brightness can ever wholly expunge. For many months my own heart had seemed a tapestry of purest gold, illuminated by the sun of Edward’s love. He was all that my girlish dreams had painted of a noble and devoted partner, and the day of our union, appointed to be celebrated at Wychwood, my family’s ancient seat in the Sussex weald, approached with the swift, sweet certainty of a summer’s dawn. Yet, as is ever the way with human happiness, a shadow was preparing to fall, a shadow whose length and depth I could not then have conceived.

The agent of this unwelcome revelation was a quiet, sad-eyed woman, who had for some weeks occupied the post of governess to my younger sisters. Her name was Miss Eleanor Vance. She was a creature of gentle mien and profound melancholy; her movements were soft as a moth’s wing, and her voice, when she spoke, held the low, mournful cadence of a distant bell. I pitied her, for she seemed to carry a weight of sorrow far beyond her years, and I endeavoured, in the thoughtless bounty of my own happiness, to draw her from her despondency. It was on a day of particular brilliance, when the sun poured a torrent of golden light upon the ancient oaks of the park, that I found her seated by the window of the schoolroom, her hands idle in her lap, her gaze fixed upon some invisible, sorrowful vista.

“Miss Vance,” I said, my voice full of the untroubled cheer that was then my native tongue, “you look as though you have seen a ghost. Come, let us walk by the lake; the air will do you good.”

She started, and turned upon me a face of such startling pallor and distress that my own smile faltered. “Forgive me, Miss Armitage. I was lost in thought.” Her eyes, large and dark, fell upon the small, miniature portrait of Edward that I wore upon a ribbon at my throat. A tremor, a convulsive shudder, passed through her frame, and she averted her gaze as if the sight had wounded her.

“You are acquainted with my Edward?” I asked, a curious thrill of unease touching me. “You have met him before his coming to Wychwood?”

“I have seen him,” she replied, her voice barely a whisper, and then, with a sudden, desperate energy, she rose. “You must forgive me. I am unwell.” She fled the room, leaving me in a state of bewildered agitation. What was there in that beloved countenance to inspire such horror? A cold dread began to uncoil in my breast, a serpent of doubt where before there had been only the warmth of certainty.

That evening, as a storm gathered in the west, its distant thunder a low growl against the serenity of the twilight, I sought her out in her small chamber at the top of the house. The sky was bruised with purple and grey, and the wind moaned about the gables of Wychwood like a restless spirit. I found her not at her needlework, but kneeling before a small, locked trunk, her shoulders shaking with silent sobs.

“Eleanor,” I said, my own voice trembling, “you must tell me the cause of this distress. If I have offended you, or if there is any way in which I can serve you, I implore you to speak.”

She looked up, and in the flickering candlelight, I saw that her eyes were filled not with tears of sorrow, but with a terrible, burning anguish. “Serve me?” she cried, her voice rising to a pitch of almost hysterical passion. “Can you serve the dead? Can you call back the brother from his early grave? Can you wash the blood from the hands of the man you are to call husband?”

The words struck me as a physical blow. I reeled back, my hand flying to my throat. “Of what do you speak? What blood? My Edward is incapable of harm; he is the soul of honour and gentleness!”

A bitter, mirthless laugh escaped her lips. “Honour! It was for honour he died! My brother, my dear, brave Arthur, who would have defended my name against the whole world, was slain by him. By Edward Harwood.”

The room seemed to tilt and darken around me. The wind howled, and a flash of lightning illuminated her face, a mask of tragic fury. She then told me the tale, her words tumbling forth in a torrent of long-suppressed grief and rage. She spoke of a slight, a whispered slander against her name at a public assembly in London years before. She spoke of her brother, Arthur Vance, young and hot-blooded, who had confronted the author of the calumny, and of how the affair had escalated, as such affairs so often do, from words to threats, and from threats to the fatal field of honour.

“And he was killed,” she concluded, her voice dropping to a hollow monotone. “Run through the body by Mr. Edward Harwood, who fought with a coolness and a skill that spoke of a practised hand. He walked away from that field of blood, and my world ended. My brother was in his grave, my family ruined, and I was cast upon the world to earn my bread as a governess, while he… he has prospered, and is to marry you.”

I sank into a chair, for my limbs refused to support me. The world I had known, so bright and ordered, had crumbled into a chaos of ruin and despair. Every gentle word of Edward’s, every tender glance, now seemed a lie, a monstrous deception. The man I adored was a killer; the happiness I had prized was built upon a foundation of murder and a young woman’s desolation. I looked at Eleanor Vance, and I saw not a servant, but the living monument of my beloved’s crime. The house of Wychwood, which had seemed a paradise, was now a prison, and its walls echoed with the silent accusation of the dead. The storm broke then, and the rain lashed against the windowpanes, each drop a cold, hard tear for the love that had, in a single moment, withered and died within my breast.
