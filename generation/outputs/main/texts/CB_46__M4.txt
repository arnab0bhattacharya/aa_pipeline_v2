The letter arrived on a Tuesday, in the thin grey light of a Brussels morning that promised rain before noon. I had been three years in that city, teaching English to the daughters of merchants and magistrates, and in those three years I had received perhaps a dozen letters from England—none of them, I need hardly say, from anyone who signed herself as my correspondent had done.

*Your affectionate cousin, Eleanor Ashby.*

I read the name a dozen times before I would consent to believe it. My father had spoken of the Ashbys of Whitmore, of course—spoken of them with that bitter brevity he reserved for all who had, in his phrase, “prospered by the misfortunes of better men.” There had been a quarrel, a lawsuit, a sum of money that should have been my mother’s and was not. I had grown up with the Ashbys as one grows up with a legend of dragons: remote, fabulous, and wholly malevolent. And now here was one of them, in Brussels—in my Brussels, where I had thought myself as far from Yorkshire as the moon is from the sea—requesting the pleasure of my acquaintance.

She came on Thursday. I had resolved to be cold; I had rehearsed a manner of polite and glacial distance. But when the door opened and she stood there, small and dark and wrapped in a travelling cloak that had known better days, with the rain glistening on her shoulders and her eyes—my father’s eyes, I would have sworn it, that grey that is almost blue—fixed on me with a kind of eager timidity, my resolution faltered.

“Cousin Margaret,” she said, and her voice was low and warm. “I have come so far. You will not turn me away?”

I bade her enter. She sat by my little fire and held her hands to it, and she talked. She talked of Whitmore, of the old house with its crooked chimneys and its avenue of limes; she talked of my father as a boy, of his pride and his temper and his gift for languages; she talked of my mother, whom I had never known, and of a garden where she had walked, and of a song she had sung. How could she know these things? I asked myself. And yet she knew them—or seemed to know them, which is the same thing to a heart starved of its own history. I found myself leaning forward. I found myself, God forgive me, with tears standing in my eyes.

“You are so like him,” she said softly. “The very set of your head. I said to myself, when I heard you were here—Eleanor, I said, you must go to her. You must be the one to end this foolish estrangement. We are not many, cousin; we cannot afford to lose one another.”

The rain beat against the window. The fire hissed. I was aware of a warmth spreading through me that had nothing to do with the coals—a treacherous, longing warmth, the warmth of home, of kin, of belonging. I had been so long alone. I had been so long the schoolmistress, the foreigner, the woman who corrects exercises and is never invited to dine.

She stayed two hours. She told me of her own troubles—a husband dead, a small income swallowed by debts, a son at school in England who must be kept there or lose his prospects. She did not ask for anything. That was her cleverness; she did not ask. She only sighed, and looked into the fire, and said that she must soon be gone, and that she did not know how she would manage the journey back.

When she rose to leave, I pressed her to stay. She refused—with a pretty reluctance, with a glance at my little room that said, more plainly than words, that she would not be a burden. At the door she turned and took my hand in both of hers.

“Margaret,” she said, “I have a confession. I came to you partly for myself. I am in difficulties—small ones, but they frighten me. I need forty pounds to pay a debt that falls due next month. I have no one else to turn to. I would not ask it of a stranger—but you are not a stranger, are you? You are my cousin.”

Forty pounds. It was nearly half my yearly salary. I had saved it slowly, coin by coin, against the day when I might return to England, when I might set up a school of my own, when I might be something other than a dependent. I looked at her face—that grey-blue gaze, so like my father’s—and I thought: what is money, weighed against kinship? What is a dream of independence, weighed against the hand of a cousin held out in need?

“I will lend it,” I said. “Gladly.”

Her embrace was warm. Her thanks were fervent. She would repay me within the year, she said; she would write; she would come again. And then she was gone, and I stood in the doorway watching her descend the stairs, and the rain fell, and the wind rose, and I felt—I confess it—a happiness I had not known in years.

It lasted until the following Wednesday, when I received a letter from a solicitor in Leeds, a stranger to me, who wrote to inquire whether I had any knowledge of the whereabouts of one Eleanor Ashby, lately of Whitmore, who had left behind her in Yorkshire a considerable number of debts and a reputation for a charm that was universally described as fatal. He had heard, he said, that she had been seen in Brussels. He would be grateful for any information.

I sat down by my cold grate—for I had let the fire die, having no more coals to spare—and I read the letter twice. Then I laughed. It was not a pleasant laugh; it had in it more of rage than of mirth. I thought of my forty pounds, gone to a woman who had never been my cousin, who had learned my history from God knows what servant or what gossip, who had played upon my hunger as a skilful musician plays upon a string. And I thought of my father, who had warned me—who had said, with his bitter brevity, that the Ashbys were not to be trusted. He had been right. He had been right, and I had been a fool, and the loneliness that had driven me into her arms was a worse enemy than any Ashby could be.

I rose and went to the window. The rain had stopped; the sky was clearing; a pale sun struggled through the clouds. I looked out upon the city—upon the strange roofs and chimneys, upon the foreign streets where I had walked so long a stranger—and I said to myself, with a fierceness that surprised me: I will not be defeated. I will earn back what I have lost. I will go home, and I will face them all—the Ashbys, the gossips, the world—and I will not be ashamed. I have been a fool, but I am not a coward. And there is no shame in a heart that longs for home, even when that longing is betrayed.

The sun broke fully through. I stood a long time watching it, and I did not weep.
