There are, I am told, men who make a profession of war, and others who make a fortune from it; but Don Anselmo Vargas, who had kept the Hotel Miraflores for the better part of thirty years, belonged to neither class. His was a more ancient and a more fatalistic trade: he kept the peace, or rather, the appearance of it, within the four stuccoed walls that stood between the Cathedral square and the dark, slow-moving waters of the Rio Escondido. He had seen presidents enter his portals in a blaze of triumph and leave them in the back of a shuttered carriage. He had lodged rebels who swore they would hang the Minister of War from the nearest lamppost, only to find them, a month later, taking the very same Minister of War’s breakfast order. For Don Anselmo, the Republic was not an idea, nor even a government; it was a procession of guests, each with his own particular thirst, his own particular set of enemies, and his own particular hour of arrival.

His hotel was a creature of the climate. In the long, white afternoons, when the heat pressed down upon the city like a suffocating blanket, it was a tomb of silence and shadow. The polished mahogany of the reception desk gleamed in the gloom of the hall, and the air, heavy with the scent of wax and wilting gardenias from the patio, seemed to hold its breath. It was in this state of suspended animation that the two telegrams had arrived, carried by a sweating boy from the Compania Transatlantica, their flimsy paper already wilting in the damp. They were brief, formal, and utterly incompatible. General Valeriano, whose Liberal forces were said to be a day’s march from the northern gates, requested the entire second floor for the night. And General Mendoza, the staunch old Conservative holding the southern approaches, had secured, through a subordinate, the same floor for himself and his staff.

Don Anselmo read the two messages side by side on his desk, the silence of the hall broken only by the distant, metallic clang of a church bell marking the hour. It was not a problem of politics, of which he understood little and cared less. It was a problem of geometry, of space and time. The two men, who would sooner cut each other’s throats than share a bottle of wine, were to be separated by a single, carpeted corridor and a few inches of plaster. To refuse one was to invite his immediate and violent displeasure; to refuse the other was to do the same. To accept both was an act of madness, or of hospitality so profound it bordered on the suicidal. And yet, what else was there to do? A hotel keeper did not choose his guests. The river brought what the river brought.

He sent for his head waiter, a man named Ernesto, whose father had served in the same capacity and whose face was a mask of weary discretion. In a few low words, spoken as if they were discussing the vintage of a claret, Don Anselmo laid out the situation. Ernesto’s expression did not change, but a certain stillness came over him, the stillness of a man who understands he is about to walk a tightrope over an abyss. The plan, when it was formed, was one of elegant, terrifying simplicity. The east wing of the second floor would belong to General Valeriano. The west wing would belong to General Mendoza. In the centre, a small sitting room and a linen closet would serve as a no-man’s-land, its door to be kept locked. The staff were to be informed that a foreign dignitary of immense importance and delicate sensibilities was occupying one end of the hall, and that absolute silence was to be maintained in his vicinity. They were not to be told his name. Secrecy, Don Anselmo knew, was the most convincing lie.

General Valeriano arrived first, as the last of the sun bled orange and purple behind the mountains. He was a young man, or young for a general, with the restless energy of a caged panther and a uniform so bespangled with gold braid it seemed to generate its own light. His aides clattered up the stairs behind him, their spurs chinking on the marble, their voices loud with the easy arrogance of men who believe the world is theirs for the taking. Don Anselmo greeted him with the profound, unctuous bow he reserved for men who might one day sign his death warrant. He saw them to their rooms, the scent of expensive cigars and cologne already filling the east wing. An hour later, as a fine, persistent drizzle began to fall, turning the gas lamps in the square into hazy golden orbs, General Mendoza entered.

He was the very antithesis of his rival. A man carved from old wood and granite, with a grey moustache and eyes that held the profound weariness of a hundred lost causes. He did not clatter; he advanced. His staff was smaller, quieter, their uniforms faded and stained with the honest mud of the field. They moved with a sombre purpose that was more unsettling than Valeriano’s swagger. Don Anselmo performed the same deep bow, his heart a cold, heavy stone in his chest. He led them in the opposite direction, his voice a low murmur, apologising for the slight delay, for the draught in the corridor, for the very existence of the universe.

The long night began. It was a masterpiece of silent, frantic activity. Ernesto, moving like a ghost, directed the serving of two separate dinners, one of celebratory champagne and roast peacock, the other of simple stew and rough red wine. The clatter of cutlery from the west wing had to be disguised by the sudden, loud tuning of a piano in the parlour below. A valet from Valeriano’s party, seeking an extra blanket, had to be intercepted at the locked centre door and led on a long, bewildering tour to the far end of the building. The air in the hotel grew thick with a tension so palpable it was a physical presence, a third guest in every room. Don Anselmo sat at his desk, not reading, not sleeping, merely listening. He listened to the floorboards creak, to the muffled sound of voices, to the eternal, indifferent dripping of the rain in the gutters. He was a spider in the centre of a web of his own making, feeling for the slightest tremor that might signal its destruction.

The crisis, when it came, was almost absurd in its smallness. It was past midnight. A floorboard groaned in the east wing. A door opened. It was one of Valeriano’s young officers, a mere boy with flushed cheeks, seeking the water closet. He turned the wrong way. Don Anselmo, who had been dozing in his chair, was on his feet in an instant. He saw the boy, silhouetted against the dim light of a hallway window, take three steps towards the west wing. In that moment, the entire fragile peace of the Republic seemed to hang in the balance. But the boy, confused by the darkness and the unfamiliar layout, paused, turned, and disappeared in the correct direction. The door clicked shut. The silence returned, deeper and more profound than before.

Dawn came not as a relief, but as a slow, grey unveiling of the night’s exhaustion. The rain had stopped. The two generals departed within an hour of each other, one before sunrise, the other just after. They did not see each other. They left their payments—gold from the Liberal, silver from the Conservative—on the desk without a word. Don Anselmo stood on the portico and watched them go, their carriages rattling away in opposite directions down the wet, gleaming street. He had served them both. He had kept them apart. He had, for one night, held the whole fractured, violent soul of his country in balance within his hotel. And as the first rays of sun struck the dome of the Cathedral, he felt not triumph, but only the immense, hollow fatigue of a man who knows that tomorrow, or the day after, the river will bring another, and the whole desperate performance will begin again. The silence of the hotel was no longer peaceful. It was the silence of a stage after the actors have gone, waiting for the next scene to be set.
