The fever took hold on a Tuesday, creeping through Eleanor's blood while she dragged through lessons on the Pennine watershed and the principal exports of the Baltic. She remembered correcting Arthur's shaky Latin conjugation, and then she remembered nothing at all. When sense returned, it came in fragments: the rough weight of a blanket, the smell of camphor, a candle burning on the far side of the room.

She was in her narrow bedroom in the east wing of Briarlock Hall. Rain struck the window in diagonal lashes. She tried to call out but her throat was closed, her tongue thick. A shape rose from the chair beside the bed, and Mrs. Agnes Coulton's face materialized in the candlelight—sharp-boned, pale-eyed, unmistakable.

"Drink," the housekeeper said.

She held a cup to Eleanor's lips. The water was cool, tasting faintly of mint. Eleanor drank, and Mrs. Coulton's hand was steady, though the fingers that tipped the cup were rough and reddened. Eleanor tried to speak, to say she had not expected this, but Mrs. Coulton pressed her back against the pillow with a flat palm against her collarbone.

"You'll not talk. Not yet."

For three more days Eleanor drifted in and out. Each time she surfaced, Mrs. Coulton was there. She changed the damp cloths on Eleanor's forehead. She spooned broth between her lips. She said almost nothing, but her presence was constant and implacable, like the fells beyond the window.

On the fourth day Eleanor woke clear-headed, the fever broken, her body hollowed and strange. Mrs. Coulton sat mending a stocking by the weak grey light. She looked older than Eleanor had ever noticed—lines deepened around her mouth, her dark hair threaded with iron.

"How long?" Eleanor asked.

"Six days. You gave us a fright." She did not look up from her needle. "Mr. Thornbeck thought you might die. The children were in a state."

Eleanor had been at Briarlock for eight months. In that time Mrs. Coulton had spoken to her with nothing warmer than clipped civility—instructions about the children's schedules, the location of the linen stores, which corridors to avoid when the squire took his evening port. The other servants said the housekeeper ruled the downstairs with a tongue like a file. Eleanor had found her terrifying.

"Why are you doing this?" Eleanor asked.

Mrs. Coulton's needle paused. "Because there's no one else. Mrs. Thornbeck is in London. The maids haven't the sense." She resumed her stitching. "And because I know what it is."

She did not explain what she meant. But that evening, when she brought Eleanor a dish of bread soaked in warm milk and sat watching until she had eaten it all, Eleanor saw that her expression had changed. The old hardness was there, but beneath it, something else—something that looked like recognition.

Over the following days, as Eleanor regained her strength, Mrs. Coulton began to talk. Not much. A sentence here, a half-confession there, offered without preamble and never referred to again. It was as if the illness had loosened something in her, a valve that had been sealed shut for years.

She told Eleanor that she had come to Briarlock in 1819, at the age of twenty-three. Not as a housekeeper. As a governess.

Eleanor, propped against her pillows, felt a jolt. "You?"

"Miss Coulton, then." A thin smile. "I expect you find that difficult to picture."

She had been engaged by the previous squire, Arthur's grandfather, to educate his two daughters. The girls had been young—eight and ten—and Mrs. Coulton spoke of them with an unfamiliar softness, describing how the younger, Cecily, had struggled with her letters and wept with frustration, how she had sat up nights cutting out paper letters for the child to trace.

"I was good at it," she said. "I was patient. I thought it mattered."

The elder daughter, Harriet, had been clever and sharp-tongued. As she grew, she began to resent instruction from a woman she considered her inferior. She complained to her father—Miss Coulton was too strict, too dull, too presumptuous. The old squire listened to his daughter. He spoke to Miss Coulton in his study, his manner cold, warning her to remember her place.

"She was fourteen," Mrs. Coulton said. "And she knew how to wound. I'd given her everything I had, and she used it against me like a blade."

Then the younger daughter fell ill. A winter fever, much like Eleanor's, but the house was snowed in, the physician delayed. Cecily died on a January morning, and Miss Coulton held her hand at the end because the family could not bear to be in the room.

"They sent for me afterward," Mrs. Coulton said. "Mrs. Thornbeck—the present squire's mother—she thanked me. She said I had been kind, and that Cecily had been fond of me. Then she told me they would not require a governess any longer. Harriet was to be sent to a school in York. I was given a month's wages and a reference."

Eleanor stared at her. "But you stayed."

"I had nowhere else." Mrs. Coulton's voice was flat. "My father was dead. My mother had a sister in Carlisle who couldn't feed another mouth. The agency had no posts for months. So I asked Mrs. Thornbeck for work in the house. She looked at me as if I'd suggested something obscene. But the previous housekeeper was failing, and she was practical if nothing else. She gave me a trial."

She had learned the work quickly—the accounts, the suppliers, the management of servants. She had remade herself as the kind of woman who gave orders rather than received them. The hardness that Eleanor had feared was, she understood now, a carapace, grown over years the way a tree scars over a wound.

"The squire—" Eleanor began.

"Arthur's father. He never looked at me the same way after Cecily. As if I reminded him of something he couldn't face. When he died and Arthur inherited, Arthur kept me on. He's decent, in his way. He doesn't think about it." She paused. "Nobody thinks about it. I'm the housekeeper. I've been the housekeeper for twenty years. That's what I am."

She stood and collected the empty bowl. At the door she stopped, her back to Eleanor, and spoke so quietly that Eleanor barely caught the words.

"I was good with children, Miss Marsh. I was patient and I was kind, and it made no difference at all. So I stopped being those things, because they cost too much."

The door closed. Eleanor sat in the grey Cumberland light, listening to rain on glass, and understood that she had been given a warning, or a gift, or perhaps both at once—she could not yet tell which.
