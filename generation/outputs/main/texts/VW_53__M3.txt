The glasses clinked—how they clinked, like a shower of glass rain falling, falling, to break upon the earthenware—and the voices rose, swelled, and broke too, into pools of laughter that rippled across the drawing-room of the house in Heath Lane. Fifty years; half a century; it sounded, when one said it aloud, like a heavy book thudding shut, or a church bell striking a deep, unassailable note. Yet here was the room still festooned with the paper chains little Phyllis had strung, her fingers sticky with paste, and the chrysanthemums, burnt-orange and sulphur-yellow, nodding in the draught from the open window as if they, too, were listening to the speeches. Eleanor, standing a little back from the throng of children, grandchildren, and the inevitable cousins who surfaced on such occasions like fish rising to fly, felt the weight of the years not as a burden but as a kind of luminous sediment, clouding the glass through which she looked at them all—her life, her doing, her fifty years of Arthur.

Arthur was speaking now; she could see his beard—white now, but once reddish, she remembered, the colour of autumn beech-leaves—moving up and down, and his hand, broad and veined and rather stained with ink from the press, resting on the shoulder of their eldest son, James, who had come down from Leeds for the occasion and stood looking deeply, almost painfully, uncomfortable in his collar. What was Arthur saying? Something about the early days, the printing shop in Camden Town, the smell of ink and damp paper, the first press that jammed, the night they sat up until three in the morning to get out the parish magazine. She had set the type for him then, her fingers black, her back aching; she had set the type and she had made the tea, and the baby had cried in the basket by the stove. Eleanor felt a sudden rush of tenderness, but also, curiously, of distance, as if she were watching two strangers in a lantern-slide, flickering, pale, their mouths open in silent speech.

“I remember,” Arthur was saying, his voice thick with feeling and a little with the port James had brought, “I remember saying to my father—”

But Eleanor did not hear what he remembered saying to his father, for at that moment her granddaughter, little Iris, came tugging at her skirt with a hot, sticky face, wanting to know where the kitten was; and Eleanor, bending down, felt the blood rush to her head, and the room tilted, and the chrysanthemums swam, and she thought: I must go into the garden. I must go into the garden for a moment, just for a moment, to get some air.

“Run and ask your uncle James,” she said, straightening herself, and Iris, satisfied with this deflection, darted off, her white pinafore flashing among the dark skirts and trouser-legs. Eleanor slipped towards the French windows; no one noticed her go; they were all listening to Arthur, or pretending to listen, or looking at the food—how Margaret had piled the plates, sandwiches curling at the edges, a seed-cake, a trifle that slumped slightly and wept a little cream. The garden door was stiff; she had to lean her weight against it, and then it gave way with a sigh, and she was out.

The evening was blue. Not the hard blue of summer, but a soft, aqueous, autumnal blue that lay over the garden like a film of silk, pooling in the hollows, staining the brickwork of the garden wall, turning the last roses—Arthur always called them his last roses, though he never went near them—into dim, flushed faces that nodded but did not speak. The grass was damp; she could feel it through her thin shoes, a cold kiss on the soles of her feet, and the air smelled of wet earth and the faint, acrid sweetness of burning leaves from somewhere beyond the wall, where the neighbours were clearing their gardens. Eleanor walked to the bench beneath the pear-tree—the pear-tree that had been a sapling when they moved here, a mere switch of a thing, and was now gnarled and thick, its branches dark against the fading sky—and sat down, and folded her hands in her lap, and breathed.

Fifty years. She sat and breathed and let the years unspool, not in sequence, not in the orderly procession that a historian might demand, but in fragments, in shards of colour and sensation, the way memory truly moves: laterally, unbidden, slipping like water through the fingers. The printing shop; the smell of ink; the baby crying; the kettle on the hob; the winter the pipes froze and Arthur came in with his hair white with frost, laughing; James’s birth, long and terrible, the gaslight hissing on the bedroom wall; and then, later, the afternoons when the children were at school and she would sit with a book—she had always read, voraciously, secretly, as if it were a vice—and the house would be so quiet she could hear the clock in the hall and the crows in the elms and the far-off clatter of the High Street, and she would feel, in that quiet, a kind of space opening up inside her, vast and luminous, almost frightening, as if she might float away into it if she were not careful.

It was in one of those afternoons—the light falling slantwise across the page, dust motes swimming in it like tiny golden fish—that she had last thought of Hugh.

Hugh Merritt. The name came to her now, unbidden, floating up from some deep, undisturbed layer of the past, like a coin glinting at the bottom of a clear pool. Hugh, who had been Arthur’s friend before he was hers; Hugh, who taught mathematics at the school in Hampstead and had a way of looking at one—of looking at her—as if he were solving an equation in which she was the unknown quantity, the beautiful, unobtainable x. He had proposed to her—no, that was too formal a word; he had spoken to her, one evening, on the Heath, the city spread out below them in a haze of smoke and amber light, and he had said, in that quiet, precise voice of his, that he wished she had chosen differently. That was all. He had not pressed it; he had not begged; he had simply stated it, as one states a theorem, and then he had walked her home, and shaken Arthur’s hand, and gone away to his rooms, and she had not seen him again for twenty years, until one day she had passed him in the street and he had lifted his hat and his hair was quite white and his eyes, those searching, questioning eyes, had looked through her as if she were a stranger.

What if? The question hung in the blue evening air like a moth, trembling. What if she had chosen Hugh? Would she be sitting in a garden now, in Hampstead, on a bench beneath a pear-tree, while a husband made speeches and grandchildren ate trifle and the roses nodded in the dusk? Or would it have been different—quieter, perhaps; lonelier; a life of books and mathematical puzzles and long silences in which two people sat together but did not speak, because the things they had to say to one another had been said, once, on the Heath, and could not be unsaid? She did not know. She could not know. And yet the not-knowing was itself a kind of knowing, a dark, rich loam in which other lives put down roots and flowered, ghostly, hypothetical, their petals translucent as glass.

A sound behind her: the garden door opening, the murmur of voices spilling out, then retreating. Footsteps on the grass. Arthur, coming to find her, his figure bulky against the lighted room, his white beard catching the glow from the windows. He sat down beside her, heavily, with a little grunt, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The pear-tree creaked above them. A star appeared, very faint, very far away, like a pin-prick in the blue.

“Cold out here,” said Arthur.

“Yes,” said Eleanor.

He put his hand over hers, on her lap, and his hand was warm, and large, and familiar, and she felt the ghost of Hugh Merritt recede, not vanishing but folding itself away, neatly, like a letter replaced in its envelope, to be kept, to be taken out again, perhaps, on some other evening, in some other garden, in some other life. She turned her hand and laced her fingers through Arthur’s, feeling the ridges and grooves of his knuckles, the looseness of the skin, the stubborn, abiding strength beneath.

“They’re cutting the cake,” he said.

“Then we’d better go in,” she said; and they rose, together, and went back into the lighted house, where the voices still hummed and the glasses still clinked and the grandchildren raced up and down the passage, and the trifle, that beautiful, slumping, weeping trifle, waited to be eaten.
