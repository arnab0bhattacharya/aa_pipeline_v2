The Bell and Candle had stood at the crooked bend of Mill Lane for longer than anyone in Netherby Ash could rightly remember, its timbers gone the soft, dark colour of old tea, its sign creaking a patient rhythm in the wind off the wolds. Sarah Mottram had kept it for eleven years, since her husband's lungs had given out and left her with the licence, the cellar, and a debt that she had paid off one quart at a time. She was a woman of forty-six with strong arms and a habit of listening more than she spoke, and the village trusted her for it. A woman who keeps an alehouse learns the shape of a man's soul by the third pint, and Sarah had learned a good many souls.

None of them surprised her so much as Mr. Ellery's.

Edwin Ellery was churchwarden at St. Cuthbert's, a sidesman before that, a man whose word carried weight on the vestry and whose coat was always brushed. He had a wife, a pale silent woman named Dorcas, and a pew at the front where he sat with his hands folded and his chin lifted, and when the parson spoke of temperance — as the parson had begun to speak of it, that being the fashion — Mr. Ellery's head would nod with a slow and solemn gravity, as though he had never in his life smelled a malt.

But on Tuesdays, when the market crowd had drained away and the lane lay empty under a wet grey sky, the churchwarden came in by the back door.

He came at dusk, always alone, always with his collar turned up. He would take the corner seat behind the chimney-breast where the firelight did not reach, and Sarah would bring him a pint of the dark without being asked, and he would drink it in three long swallows and sit a while with his hands around the empty pewter, and sometimes he would talk, low and rambling, about the tithes and the roof of the nave and a brother in Nottingham who had done better than him. Then he would go out the way he came, and on Sunday he would pass Sarah in the porch with a nod so distant it might have been carved in stone.

She had never told a soul. It was not her business to tell, and besides, there was a tenderness in it. The man was thirsty and ashamed, and the Bell and Candle was the one place in the world where nobody required him to be a churchwarden.

Then the notice went up on the church door.

*Whereas the alehouse known as the Bell and Candle hath become a nursery of idleness and drunkenness, to the ruin of divers families and the scandal of religion, it is proposed that the said licence be opposed at the Michaelmas sessions.*

Mr. Ellery had drawn it up himself. Sarah read it twice, standing in the drizzle with her shawl pulled tight, and then she went home and sat by her own fire and laughed, a short and bitter laugh that had no mirth in it at all.

The campaign was patient and respectable, which was the worst kind. Mr. Ellery went from door to door with a petition, and the parson preached on the sin of strong drink, and at the vestry meeting it was resolved that the village would be the better for a decent house of coffee and reading. There were those who signed gladly, and those who signed to keep peace with the churchwarden, and a few who signed and then came sheepishly to the Bell and Candle to beg Sarah's pardon and drink a last pint beneath the beam.

"Tis not right," said Betsy Nunn, the blacksmith's widow, setting down her glass hard enough to slop it. "Him of all men, preaching. You ought to speak, Sarah. You ought to say what you know."

"And what do I know?" said Sarah.

Betsy looked at her sidelong. "You know what half the parish would give their eyeteeth to know."

Sarah wiped the counter and said nothing. But the thought had been planted, and it grew.

She was not a fool, and she was not a saint. She had a living to keep. The Bell and Candle was her roof and her bread and the whole of her small standing in the world, and if Mr. Ellery's petition carried, she would be left with a cellar of ale she could not sell and a house she could not keep, and she would go to her sister's in Loughborough and be a burden and a pity. She lay awake at night and counted the ways of it. She could write a letter to the bench, unsigned, naming what she had seen. She could let slip a word to Betsy Nunn and let the village do what villages do. She could stand up at the sessions and say, *Ask Mr. Ellery where he takes his Tuesday pint*, and watch his brushed coat and folded hands come apart like a wet paper bag.

She imagined it often. She imagined it too well, and that troubled her, because she found she could not imagine it without a sour taste.

On the Tuesday before the sessions, the rain came down in ropes. She had not looked for him, the village being full of talk and the sky full of water, but near seven there came the familiar scrape at the back door and the churchwarden stepped in, dripping, his collar up and his hat in his hands. He looked older than he had a month ago. He sat in the corner behind the chimney-breast, and Sarah poured the dark and set it before him, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"Thou'lt be glad to see the back of me, I daresay," he said at last, not looking up.

"I've never been glad to see the back of a customer," said Sarah.

He turned the pewter a quarter turn. "It is for the good of the village," he said. "There is too much drink. I have seen it. Men who cannot feed their children. I have seen it, Mrs. Mottram."

"So have I," said Sarah. "I've seen a man drink his wages and go home to a cold hearth. I've seen worse. I've also seen men who come in of an evening and are the better for a bit of company and a fire and a word that isn't a sermon. I've seen a man who works all week and is nobody at home and nobody at church and is somebody for half an hour in my corner. I don't reckon the drink makes the man. I reckon the man comes as he is."

Mr. Ellery was quiet a long time. The fire spat. The rain hammered the roof.

"I have prayed on it," he said, and his voice had gone thin. "I have prayed and prayed, and I cannot make myself clean. I think if the place were gone — if the temptation were taken out of my road —" He stopped. He put his hand flat on the table, and Sarah saw that it trembled. "I am not a wicked man, Mrs. Mottram. I am a weak one. There is a difference, though the Psalmist would not allow it."

Sarah looked at him. She thought of the sessions, and the petition, and her cellar, and her roof. She thought of the letter she had not written and the word she had not said to Betsy Nunn. It would be so easy. It would be so ruinous, and so easy.

"Mr. Ellery," she said, "I've kept thy secret eleven years and I'll keep it eleven more. Not for thy sake. For mine. I'll not have it said that Sarah Mottram saved her house by selling a man's shame. The house is worth more than that, and so am I."

He raised his head and looked at her, and there was something in his face she had never seen there before — not gratitude, exactly, but the nakedness of a man who has been seen and has not been turned away.

"Then I am in your debt," he said.

"Thou art," said Sarah. "And I'll collect. At the sessions, thou'lt speak. Thou'lt say what thou knowest of the Bell and Candle — that it is a decent house, kept by a decent woman, and that the evil thou wouldst mend is not in my cellar but in the hearts of men, and that thou art the first of them." She set her hands on her hips. "Thou'lt not name thyself. I'll not ask that. But thou'lt not lie, either. Thou'lt stand up and say a true thing for once in thy life, and let the village make of it what it will."

Mr. Ellery stared at her. Then, slowly, he lifted the pewter and drank the last of the dark, and set it down, and nodded.

"I will," he said.

He went out by the back door into the rain. Sarah bolted it behind him and stood a moment in the empty taproom, listening to the water in the gutters, and she did not know whether she had saved her house or lost it, and found, to her surprise, that she did not much care.
