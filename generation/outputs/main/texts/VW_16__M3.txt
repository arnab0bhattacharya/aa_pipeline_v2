The shutters were half closed against the glare, and in the room at the Hotel Mediterraneo the light fell in long, pale, dusty parallelograms upon the red-tiled floor. Outside, the noise of Palermo came in waves—the sharp cry of a vendor, the rattle of a carriage over uneven stone, and beneath it all, the vast, continuous murmur of the sea, which sounded to Eleanor, as she stood brushing her hair before the spotted mirror, like the breathing of some immense, indifferent animal. She laid the silver brush down. It clicked against the marble of the washstand, and the sound was minute, distinct, and final. She had done it; she was here. 

The money—three hundred and twenty pounds, left in a surprisingly firm hand on a faded blue sheet of writing paper—had been a shock, and then a door. Her aunt’s voice, thin and尖锐 in the drawing-room in Putney, had tried to make it a cage: *A young woman alone, Eleanor, in a Papist country; it is not suitable, it is not what dear Grandmamma would have wished, and the fogs there in autumn are quite deadly, they say.* But what had Grandmamma wished, lying there in the white bed for those six endless months, her fingers like small, cold claws resting in Eleanor’s palm? Not that Eleanor should sit in a dim London room and watch the damp creep up the curtains. She had wished for sun, for the heavy, bruised scent of dying hydrangeas, and for release. And Eleanor had taken the money and crossed the water, and now she was standing in a room where the air smelled of lemon peel and dust, and she was entirely alone.

She had been here for four days, and she had spoken to no one. The waiter at the pension, a dark young man with a napkin over his arm, merely bowed and placed the cup of black coffee before her; the chambermaid moved silently in the corridor, gathering up sheets that smelled of lavender and starch. It was an unspoken conspiracy of privacy, and it filled Eleanor with a lightness so acute it was almost painful. In London, she had been a figure in a tableau of illness; she had been the nurse, the watcher, the receptacle for the slow leak of a dying woman’s fears. Here, she was nobody. She had no attachments, no duties; she was a mere point of view, a glass through which the brilliant, careless world poured.

She went out into the street, pulling her grey veil down over her face. The sun struck the stones of the Via Vittorio with a blinding, metallic ferocity. Everything was sharp, angular, and bright. The carts drawn by mules were painted with violent scenes of saints and battles, the wheels rimmed with a scarlet that made the eye ache. She walked past the stalls in the market, where the fish lay on beds of crushed ice, their silver scales catching the light and throwing it back in tiny, fractured rainbows. There were heaps of purple aubergines, tomatoes split open and bleeding seeds, and the air was thick with the smell of overripe fruit and salt. She did not buy anything; she merely looked, letting the colour wash over her, feeling it soak into the grey fabric of her being. 

In London, they had said she would be lonely. *You will mope, Eleanor, you will have no one to speak to, and you will tire of your own company in a week.* But what they called loneliness was, in fact, the first true society she had ever kept. It was the society of her own mind, which had been suppressed for so long, crouching in the shadow of the sickbed, that now it sprang up, wild and rampant, like a plant reaching for the light. She thought of the orange trees in the courtyard of the cathedral, their leaves dark and glossy, the fruit hanging like heavy, dull gold against the blue plaster of the walls. She thought of the way the marble of the fountain was worn smooth by centuries of water, and how the water fell with a sound like the dropping of heavy jewels. She thought of nothing at all; she merely existed, a pulse in the hot, still air.

She walked down to the harbour. The water was a thick, oily blue, and the boats rocked gently, their masts describing slow arcs against the sky. A man sat mending a net, his brown hands moving deftly, rhythmically, over the twine. He did not look up as she passed. She was invisible, and the invisibility was a cloak of invulnerability. She sat on the stone wall, feeling the heat of the masonry through her skirts. A lizard darted across the stone, paused, its throat pulsing, and then vanished into a crack. The world was entirely populated, and yet it was empty of any demand upon her. 

She thought of her grandmother, not the frail, whispering woman of the last weeks, but the woman she had been forty years ago—a woman who had travelled, who had worn velvet and ridden horses, who had possessed a fierce, unquenchable energy that the illness had slowly, cruelly drained. *Go*, the woman in the bed had seemed to say, her eyes clouded with morphine and pain, *go and live.* And so Eleanor had come here, to this city of harsh light and barbaric colour, to this coast where the air tasted of salt and history, and she was living. She was not living in the way her aunt meant—making calls, attending services, exchanging trivial words over weak tea. She was living in the sense that the blood moved in her veins, and the sun warmed her skin, and the world, so vast and so indifferent, was yet entirely hers.

The afternoon wore on, and the light began to change. The hard, metallic glare softened into a golden haze, and the shadows of the masts lengthened upon the water. The sound of a church bell came across the city, a slow, measured tolling that seemed to mark not the hour, but the passage of some vast, impersonal time. Eleanor stood up. She was hungry. She would walk back to the pension, through the narrow streets where the balconies were hung with washing, and the children played in the dust. She would eat her dinner alone, at the little table in the corner, and the waiter would bring her a plate of fish, and a glass of rough red wine. And afterwards, she would sit on her balcony, watching the stars come out over the dark, heaving sea, and she would feel, for the first time in her life, that she was not waiting for anything. She was not waiting for the doctor, or the nurse, or the end. She was not waiting for a letter, or a visitor, or a change in the weather. She was simply there, a small, solitary figure in a vast, beautiful, and entirely uncaring world, and the happiness she felt was so profound and so still that it was almost like grief, and she knew, with a certainty that went beyond words, that she would never go back.
