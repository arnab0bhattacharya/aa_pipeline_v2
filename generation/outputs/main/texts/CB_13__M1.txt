I stood upon the landing of the second floor, where the draught is wont to whistle through the wainscoting like a complaining spirit, and I had halted there because I had heard my own name spoken. It is a strange and a cold sensation, to hear oneself discussed in the third person by those who hold the power of one’s daily bread. The door of the library stood ajar—perhaps an inch, perhaps two—and through that black slit, the voices of Mr. and Mrs. Vance flowed into the corridor, oily and precise.

"She is quiet, certainly," said Mr. Vance, and I could hear the rustle of the *Times* as he turned a page. "But her manner is severe. I do not like severity in a woman, Eleanor. It suggests a want of softness, a want of that feminine pliability which makes a home agreeable."

"She is a governess, Arthur," returned his wife. Her voice was like the chime of small, brittle bells. "We did not engage her for her conversation, but for her French and her water-colours. She is an instrument, is she not? A necessary piece of furniture, like the pianoforte in the drawing-room. As long as she functions and does not scratch the upholstery of our peace, she is adequate."

I leaned against the cold wall. Furniture. I, who had a heart that beat with a violence that sometimes terrified me; I, who had a soul that thirsted for the living God as the hart thirsteth for the water-brooks; I was to be rated no higher than a mahogany table or a stuffed chair.

"She is too proud," Mrs. Vance continued. "I saw her look at the Vicar’s wife last Sunday with a flash in her eye that was quite unladylike. Pride in a governess is a vice, Arthur. It is a rebellion against her appointed station. I shall have to check it. A few sharp words regarding her wages usually dampens the spirit of these learned women."

I did not wait to hear more. I turned and walked to my own chamber, the little room at the gable’s end where the roof slants low and the window looks out only upon the moor. I closed the door and leaned my forehead against the wood. A fierce heat rose in my cheeks—shame, yes, but something darker and harder beneath it. It was the indignation of the enslaved. I looked at my hands. They were roughened by the scrubbing of young Master Edward’s cuffs and the brushing of Miss Adeline’s hair. They were the hands of a worker, yet they were not the hands of a chattel.

"If I am furniture," I whispered to the empty room, "then I am a chair that will refuse to be sat upon."

But then, the wind rose. It came down from the heights of the Derbyshire hills with a sudden, savage howl, rattling the window-pane as if a giant hand demanded admittance. The moor turned from grey to a bruised purple under the storm clouds. And in that moment, the anger cooled into a hard, clear ice. I packed my small portmanteau. I would leave. Let them find another instrument to tune their children’s minds. I would go out into that storm rather than endure another day of their invisible chains.

I had fastened the last strap when the bell rang. Not the polite summons for tea, but a frantic, jangling peal that tore the fabric of the night. It was the nursery bell.

I hesitated. My hand was on the latch of my door. *Leave them*, said the voice of Self-Respect. *They think you are wood and horsehair; prove them right by vanishing.* But then came a cry—a thin, piercing sound that rose even above the gale. It was Edward. He was but five years old, a frail, hot-tempered child whom I loved with a fierce, protective tenderness despite his parents’ coldness.

I dropped the portmanteau. Duty is a stern mistress, and she brooks no argument when the innocent are in peril. I ran down the stairs.

The nursery was in an uproar. Mrs. Vance stood wringing her hands, her face white as chalk. "He is burning up! He is choking! Where is the doctor? Why does he not come?"

Mr. Vance paced the floor. "The road is flooded. The bridge is gone. We are cut off."

I pushed past them. Edward lay in his cot, his small body rigid, his breath coming in terrible, rasping gasps. His eyes were rolled back. I knew the look. I had seen it in the cottages of the poor. It was the croup, a strangling devil of a disease that steals the breath before one can say a prayer.

"Stand back," I said. My voice did not shake. It was the voice I used for the multiplication tables, hard and clear.

Mrs. Vance looked at me as if I had struck her. "Miss Thorne? What do you presume—"

"Your remedies are useless, Madam," I said, seizing the child and lifting him. He was light as a bird, and burning hot. "Hot flannels and mustard plasters will not open that windpipe. I know what to do. Boil water. Now. And bring vinegar."

They stared at me, paralyzed by their own useless terror. They were gentlefolk, bred to be served, and in this extremity, they were helpless as infants.

"Do as I say!" I thundered. The authority in my tone shocked me as much as it did them. For the first time, I was not the furniture; I was the house.

They moved. Mr. Vance rushed to the fireplace to stoke the flames; Mrs. Vance ran for the water. I sat in the rocking chair, holding the boy upright against my chest. I rocked him, not gently, but with a steady, rhythmic motion to keep him from collapsing. I dipped a cloth in the vinegar and held it to his face to sting him into breathing.

"Edward," I whispered into his ear, while the storm howled outside and the parents fumbled in the background. "Edward, fight. The Lord is thy shepherd; thou shalt not want. But you must fight."

The night stretched into an eternity. The clock struck twelve, then one. The boy’s breathing was a saw rasping through green wood. I did not waver. I poured the steam around him. I rubbed his back until my arms ached. I prayed silently, a prayer not of words but of will—a desperate wrestling with the angel of death.

Mrs. Vance approached me timidly. "Miss Thorne... is he...?"

"He is alive," I said. "And he will remain so, if you do not distract me."

I felt the change before I saw it. A loosening in the small, rigid chest. A deeper, wetter sound to the cough. The strangling seal of mucus had broken. He gasped, a great, tearing sound, and then his breath came smoother. He began to weep—a healthy, exhausted weeping that is the sound of life returning.

I slumped back in the chair, sweat pouring down my face. Edward fell into a heavy, natural sleep in my arms.

Silence returned to the room, save for the wind. Mr. Vance stood by the window, looking out at the blackness. Mrs. Vance sat on the edge of the bed, watching me. The dynamic of the room had shifted irrevocably. The mistress and master were now the supplicants, and I, the governess, held their world in my hands.

Mrs. Vance rose and came to me. She looked at her son, then at me. Her eyes were wet.

"You have saved him," she whispered. "We... we were helpless."

I looked at her. I thought of the voice in the library. *A necessary piece of furniture.* The words rose in my throat, bitter and burning. I could humiliate them now. I could demand a raise, an apology, a crown. I could remind them of their cruelty.

But looking at the sleeping child, I felt a strange pity for them. They were poor in spirit, even if rich in gold.

"Go to bed, Mrs. Vance," I said softly. "He needs quiet now. I will watch."

She hesitated, as if she wanted to say more—to thank me, perhaps, or to beg forgiveness—but the distance between us was too great for such bridges to be built in a single night. She nodded and withdrew, taking her husband with her.

I was left alone with the child and the dying fire. I rose and placed him in his cot, tucking the blankets around him with a tenderness that defied their definitions of me.

Then I went to the window. The storm was breaking. A pale, watery light was beginning to touch the edges of the moor. My portmanteau stood packed in my room upstairs. I could still go. The road would be clear by morning.

But I knew I would not. Not yet. To leave now would be to abandon the child to the clumsy hands of those who did not understand him. My self-respect demanded I be treated as a human being, but my conscience demanded I act like one.

"I am not furniture," I whispered to the dawn. "I am a fortress."

I pulled the shawl tighter around my shoulders. I would stay until the boy was strong. And then, when the time came, I would leave with my head high—not as a runaway servant, but as a woman who had conquered her own fear and proved her worth in the fire. Let the Vances keep their money; I possessed a dignity they could never purchase, and a strength they would never comprehend.
