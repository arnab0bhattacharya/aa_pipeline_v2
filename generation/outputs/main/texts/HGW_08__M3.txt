It is a curious thing to observe how readily the average country practitioner will accept the miraculous provided it arrives in a brown paper wrapper. I had been assistant to Dr. Verrall of Whitmore for the better part of two years before I stumbled upon my particular miracle, and in all that time I had learned far more about human endurance than about human nature. Verrall himself was a solid, pudding-faced man with a faith in the British Pharmacopoeia that amounted almost to idolatry, and a corresponding inability to perceive anything that lay outside its pages. I was left, therefore, to pursue my own lines of inquiry unmolested, which I did in the small brick laboratory behind the dispensary that had once been a washhouse.

My discovery, if I may call it that, came about through a sequence of investigations into the nature of lactic acid accumulation in muscular tissue, combined with certain observations I had made regarding the function of the suprarenal capsules. I will not weary the reader with the chemical particulars. It is sufficient to say that by the autumn of 1894 I had succeeded in compounding a fluid which, when injected beneath the skin of a guinea-pig, enabled that somewhat indolent creature to remain in a state of continuous muscular activity for a period of thirty-seven hours without any apparent diminution of its faculties, and—what was more remarkable—without the subsequent collapse that one might reasonably have expected. The animal merely ate with heightened appetite and resumed its normal existence. I had, in short, produced a serum that suppressed fatigue.

For a month I contented myself with animal experiments. Then, one evening in November, I administered the first injection to myself.

The effect was not dramatic, which is perhaps the highest compliment one may pay a physiological preparation. There was no rush, no exhilaration, nothing whatever of the kind produced by those vulgar stimulants upon which the tired London clerk depends. I simply found, as the clock crept past midnight and on toward morning, that I did not yawn. My hand remained steady. My eye remained clear. I went about the business of the following day—a Wednesday, I believe, with a full slate of morning consultations and a difficult midwifery case at Pidding Common—and felt nothing worse than a certain heightened alertness, a quality of attention that was almost pleasurable. I took a second injection that evening, and a third the next morning, and so, without any formal decision, I entered upon my new existence.

It is astonishing how much work a man may accomplish when the prison of sleep is removed from his days. I attended to the morning surgeries at Verrall's side. I made my afternoon rounds through the outlying farms. And then, when Whitmore had retired to its beds and the lamp in the surgery window burned alone, I returned to my laboratory. I read. I experimented. I wrote. I found that I could prepare a paper for the *Lancet* in a single night, an undertaking that had formerly consumed the better part of a week. Verrall, who had no great fondness for correspondence, was pleased to delegate his to me. The patients, too, began to notice the change. I was available at all hours. I responded to calls with a promptness that had previously been impossible, for the simple reason that I was never asleep. Old Mrs. Ffoukes at Number Four, Parchment Lane, who suffered from a stubborn bronchitis and a corresponding conviction that her symptoms were always worst at three in the morning, found me at her bedside within a quarter-hour of her bell. She was, I believe, more startled than grateful.

Within six weeks my reputation had grown to the point where Verrall, with characteristic generosity, raised my salary by ten pounds per annum. He also remarked, in his ponderous way, that I was looking remarkably well. And so I was—or so the shaving-glass assured me. My colour was good. My eyes were bright. There was, however, about my appearance one subtle alteration which the glass could not convey but which I was nevertheless conscious of: a certain immobility of the features, a stillness of the face that was not so much composure as the absence of those thousand minute muscular adjustments—the flickering, the faint contractions, the restless play of expression—which constitute the visible grammar of ordinary human life. I had become, I may say, a somewhat more efficient instrument, and somewhat less of a man.

I do not know when the patients first began to remark upon it. I have since reconstructed the chronology from scattered hints and half-remembered conversations. I believe it was the Pemberton woman who first articulated the unease. She had brought her son—a freckled, distressingly energetic child of six—to the surgery for a persistent cough, and I had listened to his chest with my stethoscope and written a prescription while she sat watching me. I recall that she fell silent in the middle of some remark about the weather, and when I looked up I found her staring at my face with an expression I did not at once comprehend.

"You never blink, do you, Mr. Penley?" she said.

I blinked, of course, at that. But the observation stayed with me. I had not, in fact, been blinking with anything like my former frequency. The serum, it appeared, had reduced the reflex to the bare physiological minimum required to preserve the moisture of the cornea. It was a small thing, but small things accumulate.

Then there was the matter of my hands. A country doctor's hands are accustomed to being looked at. They are expected to be steady and sure. But there is a difference between steadiness and rigidity, and the difference, I discovered, is one that patients perceive instinctively, without being able to name it. The farmer Grinstead at Marsh End—philosophical in most things, and a man of few words—remarked one afternoon that my fingers felt "like a pair of tongs" when I pressed his abdomen. He said it with a kind of rough, apologetic humour, but his wife, who was standing by the window, turned rather pale.

It was the cumulative effect of such observations, rather than any single incident, that brought me to a recognition of my predicament. I had become, in the phrase that I overheard young Mrs. Devereux use to the chemist's assistant—a phrase that reached me by way of three intermediaries and a Sunday church porch—"not quite right." The word that circulated with increasing openness through the drawing-rooms and market stalls of Whitmore was not "clever" or "dedicated" or even "eccentric." The word was "unnatural."

They were not wrong. I had not slept in eleven weeks. I had not, in all that time, experienced a single moment's drowsiness, a single involuntary yawn. I had not, I realised with a shock of something close to fear, dreamed. The dreaming part of me had simply ceased to function. And with the cessation of dreaming had come a slow, creeping alteration in my relation to the world—an alteration so gradual that I might have missed it entirely had I not been, by training and temperament, a man who observes. I found that I no longer felt impatience. I no longer felt sympathy. I observed my patients' suffering with the same detached, accurate interest with which I observed the progress of a reaction in a test-tube. I recorded their symptoms, diagnosed their ailments, prescribed their remedies, and felt—nothing.

The serum, I had begun to suspect, had done more than suppress fatigue. It had suppressed that periodic retreat from reality which sleep represents, and with it, all those faculties that depend upon retreat for their renewal. I was, in every sense that mattered, awake. But I was awake as a machine is awake—as a clock is awake—turning through its ordained motions with mechanical precision and mechanical indifference.

I sit now in the laboratory, writing this account by the light of a single lamp. It is, I believe, nearly four in the morning. The village is silent. Through the window I can see the faint grey suggestion of an approaching dawn over the rooftops of Parchment Lane. My hand is perfectly steady. My eyes do not ache. I have before me a hypodermic syringe and a small phial of the serum, and beside them, a phial of a rather different compound which I prepared this evening—a compound that I have every reason to believe will act as an antagonist to the first. I have not yet decided which to use. I find, indeed, that I am not altogether capable of decision, for decision requires something that I have misplaced: a preference, a desire, a leaning of the will toward one outcome rather than another. I have been so very efficient, and efficiency, I am beginning to understand, is the death of everything that matters.
