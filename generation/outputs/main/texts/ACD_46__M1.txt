The wind had come up from the south-west, bringing with it the smell of the sea and the fine, salt spray which the gulls were riding. It rattled the casements of Wychbury Hall and made the yew trees in the drive bend and whisper like conspirators. It was a night for the fire, and Sir Robert Wychbury sat in the deep leathern chair beside it, his face a map of eighty years, his hands, thin and spotted, resting on the arms.

Across from him, in a straight-backed chair that he occupied with a certain restless energy, sat the claimant. He was a man of perhaps forty, with a sun-browned face and the keen, watchful eyes of one who has looked upon wide horizons. His name was Mr. James Thorne, and he had arrived that afternoon with a portmanteau and a story as polished and plausible as the silver on the sideboard.

“I do not press you, Sir Robert,” Thorne was saying, his voice smooth and well-modulated. “I only lay the facts before you. My father, your brother Edmund, was lost when the *Hesperides* went down off the Cape in ’62. He had married a Dutch lady at Simon’s Town. I am their son. That is all.”

He had, in the course of the evening, proved his claim with an astonishing array of detail. He knew the childhood nickname Sir Robert had used for his brother, a name known to no one outside the family. He knew of the small, silver-cased compass their grandfather had given them, and of the exact spot on the library mantelpiece where it had stood for thirty years before being lost. He knew, too, of the private arrangement over the grazing rights at Nether Farm, a matter settled between the two brothers in a letter that Sir Robert was certain he had burned.

Sir Robert rubbed his brow. “It is a strange business, Thorne. A very strange business. Your knowledge is… remarkable.”

“Edmund was my father,” Thorne said simply. “He spoke of England often. Of you, sir. Of this house.”

It was at this moment that the door opened and Mrs. Gable entered with the tea-tray. She was the housekeeper, a woman of some sixty years whose starched cap and apron seemed to be a natural extension of her upright and unyielding character. She had served the Wychbury family since she was a girl, and her loyalty was a thing as solid as the foundations of the house itself. Her eyes, small and dark, took in the scene at a glance and rested upon Mr. Thorne with an expression of profound and settled disapproval.

“The wind is rising, Sir Robert,” she said, her voice as crisp as her apron.

“Indeed, Mrs. Gable.” The baronet waved a hand towards their guest. “You have met Mr. Thorne. He brings us news from a world we thought long closed to us.”

“I have met the gentleman,” Mrs. Gable said, placing the tray with a decisive click. Her hands, chapped and red from work, were steady. “He has been telling you of Mr. Edmund.”

“He has. He is Edmund’s son.”

The housekeeper’s lips thinned. “Begging your pardon, Sir Robert, that is a thing that cannot be. Mr. Edmund was my own cousin’s particular friend before he sailed. We had letters from him. Not many, but enough. He was a free man in Cape Town, he said, and had no thought of a wife or children. He was to make his fortune and come home. That was all he ever spoke of.”

“A man may change his mind, Mrs. Gable,” said Thorne, with a patient smile.

“He may change his shirt, sir, but he does not change his nature,” she retorted, her gaze unwavering. “Mr. Edmund was a wild lad, but his heart was in Sussex. He would not have settled out there. And he would have written of a wife. A man does not keep such a thing from his family.”

The silence that followed was heavy with conflict. Sir Robert looked from the calm, confident face of the claimant to the rigid, certain features of his old servant. He was an old man, and the prospect of an heir, of the name continuing, was a powerful lure. He had already spoken to his solicitor in Lewes about a codicil to the will.

It was then that the visitor was announced. I had been in Lewes on another matter, and the baronet’s note, sent by a groom on horseback, had reached me at my inn. I found the scene in the library exactly as I have described it.

“Ah, Doyle, thank you for coming,” said Sir Robert, rising with some effort. “You are the very man. We are in need of a cool head.”

I bowed and took the chair he indicated. I observed Mr. Thorne, who returned my gaze with an easy frankness, and Mrs. Gable, who stood by the door as if to guard the household from deception. I listened to the story, each speaker putting their case with economy and force.

When they had finished, I turned to the claimant. “Mr. Thorne, you say your father was lost at sea. The wreck of the *Hesperides*, I believe, was a matter of some note. There would be a passenger list, a record of the survivors.”

“There were no survivors,” said Thorne. “My father was officially presumed dead. But a man from the ship’s boat, a Lascar, made it to the coast. It was from him, years later, that I learned of my father’s fate. He had the compass in his possession.”

“A Lascar,” I mused. “A man difficult to trace.”

“My life has been one of travel, sir. I have learned to find those who wish to remain hidden.”

I turned to the housekeeper. “Mrs. Gable, you mentioned letters from Mr. Edmund. Are they still in your keeping?”

“Some are, sir. In my work-box.”

“And you are certain there was never any mention of a marriage?”

“As certain as I am of my own name.”

I rose and walked to the window. The storm was lashing the glass now. I had observed Mr. Thorne’s hands as he spoke. They were brown and strong, but the nails were impeccably kept, and there was no sign of the callouses or ingrained dirt that one might expect from a man who had spent his life in the rough trade of the Cape. More telling still, I had noted the small, almost imperceptible tremor in the fingers of his right hand as he lifted his teacup—a tremor I associated with a certain type of nervous energy, often found in those who live by their wits rather than their labour.

“The details you provide, Mr. Thorne, are indeed remarkable,” I said, turning back to the room. “The nickname, the compass, the grazing rights. A man would have to be intimately acquainted with the family to know such things.”

“As I said,” Thorne replied smoothly, “my father was a great one for stories.”

“Quite so.” I paused. “But there is one small point. You said the compass was lost. Sir Robert, when did you last see it?”

The old man thought. “It must be twenty years ago. It was on the mantelpiece one Christmas, and gone by the New Year. We thought a servant had taken it.”

“And you, Mrs. Gable? Do you recall its disappearance?”

The housekeeper’s brow furrowed. “I do, sir. It was the year the new footman was let go. For thieving.”

“A footman,” I said softly. “A man who would have had access to the library, who would have heard the family talk. A man who might have learned a nickname, or the details of a private arrangement.”

I looked at Thorne. His smile was gone. A watchfulness had entered his eyes, the look of a fox that has heard the hounds.

“It is a common enough story,” I continued, my voice even. “A servant dismissed for theft, who perhaps keeps some trifle and, years later, seeing an opportunity, uses the knowledge he gained in service to construct a claim. He would need to learn of the lost brother, of course. But a few well-placed questions in the local inns, a visit to the parish records, and the broad outlines of such a tragedy are easily obtained. The rest is invention, built upon a foundation of stolen fact.”

I turned to Sir Robert. “Your solicitor in Lewes is a man of great discretion. I spoke with him this afternoon. He mentioned that a gentleman had been making enquiries about the Wychbury estate, and about the provisions of your will, some three weeks ago. A gentleman whose description, I must say, tallies with Mr. Thorne’s.”

The silence in the room was absolute, broken only by the cry of the wind. Thorne rose to his feet. The veneer of the polished gentleman had cracked, and beneath it I saw a flash of something hard and desperate.

“This is an outrage,” he said, his voice tight. “Sir Robert, this man is insulting me.”

“I think,” said Sir Robert Wychbury, his own voice suddenly firm with the authority of his rank, “that you had better leave my house, Mr. Thorne. Mrs. Gable will show you to the door.”

The claimant looked from the baronet’s stern face to my impassive one. He knew the game was lost. Without another word, he picked up his hat and followed the housekeeper from the room.

Sir Robert sank back into his chair with a great sigh. “Good heavens, Doyle. How did you know?”

“The details were too perfect, Sir Robert. A true son would have had gaps in his knowledge, uncertainties. Mr. Thorne’s story was seamless, and a seamless story is usually woven. The tremor in his hand, the state of his nails, the early enquiries to your solicitor—these were the threads. And Mrs. Gable,” I added, with a nod to the housekeeper, who had returned and was quietly gathering the tea-things, “provided the most telling evidence of all. Her certainty. A woman who has lived in a house for fifty years knows its truths, and when she contradicts a plausible stranger, it is the stranger who is usually found wanting.”

Mrs. Gable allowed herself a small, tight smile. “The man was a liar, sir. I knew it the moment I set eyes on him.”

Outside, the storm began to abate. The wind dropped, and a pale moon broke through the scudding clouds, casting long, cold shadows across the lawn. The matter was ended. The name of Wychbury was safe, for a time at least, from the depredations of clever men with invented pasts.
