It is a curious thing that the English, who have produced so few artists of any real merit, possess an unerring instinct for the fine art of charitable hypocrisy. To raise funds for the destitute, they will gladly spend a thousand pounds on a supper, and to provide blankets for the poor, they will surely take off their own velvet cloaks, provided there are enough reporters present to admire the sacrifice. It was for this very reason that the matinée at the St. Cecilia Theatre had been organized, an event destined to be remembered less for the sums it gathered than for the social scandal it so elegantly dispersed.

The true sensation of the afternoon was to be Mrs. Vivian. She had been, until quite recently, the most celebrated actress of her generation, a woman whose voice held the precise timbre of weeping marble. But she had committed the unforgivable sin of marrying Lord Arthur Lonsdale, and had immediately, and with devastating composure, retired from the stage. Society had forgiven her her profession, entirely because she had abandoned it; it is only the sins that one gives up that the English upper classes are truly willing to pardon. 

Lord Arthur was a man of immaculate boredom. He possessed a superb collection of enamelled snuff-boxes and a profound contempt for any emotion that could not be framed and hung upon a wall. He was to be found that afternoon in his house in Mayfair, draped across a sofa of pale brocade, toying with a miniature of some deceased ancestor.

“I simply cannot permit it, Clara,” he was saying, his voice possessing the flat, polished surface of a mirror that reflects nothing. “The wife of a Lonsdale cannot appear upon a public stage. It is a thing entirely unknown.”

“But it is for charity, Arthur,” she replied, adjusting a magnificent cluster of hot-house orchids in a porcelain vase. The purple of the petals matched precisely the velvet of her afternoon gown. “One might do anything for charity. One might even be sincere, and still be forgiven, provided the cause is fashionable.”

“Charity is merely the excuse society gives for being tedious,” said Lord Arthur, examining a flaw in his fingernail. “I do not object to your being tedious, but I do object to your being public. The stage is a place for those who have failed in drawing-rooms. You have succeeded. It is a step backward. Besides, the Duchess of Wrexham will be there, and she has the most appalling taste in bonnets. I cannot have my wife associated with such a spectacle.”

Clara turned to him, her dark eyes alight with a sudden, dangerous brilliance. “I have always considered the stage a far finer place than the drawing-room, Arthur. In the theatre, the lies are written for one, and one is universally applauded for telling them beautifully. In society, one must invent one’s own falsehoods, and if they are believed, one is merely called a gossip. I shall act. I have given you my life, but I must keep my art. It is the only thing I possess that is not encumbered by your name.”

Lord Arthur sighed. He recognized the futility of arguing with a woman who had begun to quote herself. “If you go to the St. Cecilia,” he said, with icy slowness, “you will find the doors of my house closed to you. I cannot have my friends saying I have married a mountebank.”

“I am sure your friends will say whatever is most convenient to their digestion,” Clara answered, a faint smile playing about her lips. “I shall send the carriage back for my jewels.” 

She left the room with the majestic, unhurried grace of a woman who has just committed an unforgivable social crime and intends to enjoy the sunset before the execution. Lord Arthur returned to his snuff-boxes, feeling that he had been extremely decisive, and entirely unaware that his wife had already ordered her maid to pack her theatrical gowns.

That evening, the St. Cecilia Theatre was a stifling hothouse of perfume and presumption. The stalls were filled with women whose faces were pale, tragic masks of cosmetics, and whose silks rustled with the friction of mutual distrust. When Mrs. Vivian appeared, a profound silence fell over the house. She was dressed in white, a colour that suited her admirably, as it allowed the audience to project whatever tragedy they pleased onto her immaculate surface. She delivered her lines with the exquisite, hollow perfection of a broken vase glued back together with gold; one could see the fractures, but they only added to the value.

The audience was thrown into a paroxysm of uncertainty. To applaud was to insult Lord Arthur, whose boredom was considered a mark of high intellect; to remain silent was to appear unmoved by beauty, which was the one unforgivable sin in the higher circles. They compromised, as society always does, by applauding wildly while whispering to one another that her behaviour was utterly impossible. 

Lady Gwendolen Marsden, who prided herself on discovering people just before they were ruined, was seated in a private box. She tapped her ivory fan against her chin, a gesture she had perfected to indicate deep social contemplation. 

“It is terribly brave of her,” Lady Gwendolen murmured to the young Lord Pellington, who was sitting beside her, looking entirely bewildered by the complexities of the situation. 

“Brave?” Lord Pellington echoed. “I thought it was merely a charity performance.”

“My dear boy, there is no such thing as ‘merely’ in London,” Lady Gwendolen replied. “The question is not whether she acts well, but whether Lord Arthur will leave her. If he leaves her, she is a martyr to her art, and we must all visit her in a charming little cottage in Kensington. If he forgives her, she is merely a wife who has been allowed a tantrum, and we must cut her for being overdressed.”

“But surely,” Lord Pellington ventured, “if she is a great artist, she should be allowed to practice her art?”

Lady Gwendolen looked at him with the gentle pity one reserves for those who have not yet learned to read. “Art, my dear Pellington, is merely a method of escaping the conversation of people one despises. It is not a profession for a married woman. A married woman has already achieved her masterpiece. She has found someone to pay for her gowns. To return to the stage is to repaint a picture that has already been sold. It is dreadfully bad form.”

When the curtain fell, Mrs. Vivian bowed to the audience, her face a beautiful, impassive mask that concealed a profound, secret triumph. She had defied her husband, delighted the public, and committed the most exquisite of social errors. In the wings, she found her maid waiting with a heavy velvet cloak. 

“The carriage is here, my lady,” the maid whispered, trembling slightly.

Mrs. Vivian wrapped the cloak around her, the dark fabric swallowing the white gown like a shadow swallowing a ghost. “I shall return to Grosvenor Square, Alice,” she said calmly.

“But his lordship said the doors were to be closed, my lady.”

“Doors, Alice, are merely wooden things that men use to make their weaknesses seem like principles. I have the key, and I have the public’s applause. Between the two, I am entirely invincible.”

The next morning, London was divided into two distinct camps: those who said Mrs. Vivian had behaved with unpardonable vulgarity, and those who said she had behaved with unpardonable genius. In the end, society decided to applaud her, not because they admired her courage, but because Lord Arthur, upon her return, had merely yawned, complained of a headache, and retreated to his library. It was universally agreed that a man who could be so thoroughly defeated and remain so profoundly bored was a man of the very highest breeding.
