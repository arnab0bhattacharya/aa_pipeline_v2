Mr. Ezekiel Thimbleby, auctioneer and appraiser of the town of Marlow Green, was a man who carried his professional cheerfulness with him into all the by-ways of his existence, so that it had become a sort of second nature, a habit of the countenance that survived even the discovery of a bad debt or the contemplation of a neighbour’s ruin. He was a short, brisk man, with a bald forehead that shone like a well-polished shelf, and small, quick eyes that seemed to be perpetually taking an inventory of the world, appraising the value of a face, a coat, a horse, or a reputation, with the same practised and dispassionate glance. To be an auctioneer in a market town is to be a kind of public confessor, a lay-depositary of secrets, for into the sale-room come the scattered fragments of broken fortunes, and the hammer that knocks down a lot is often the knell of a family’s pride. Mr. Thimbleby knew the history of every piece of mahogany in the county, could tell you which sideboard had been bought with the profits of a lucky speculation and which had been sold to pay a gambling debt, and he could trace the descent of a set of Spode china through three generations of extravagance and thrift.

It was upon a bleak afternoon in March, when the wind came searching through the streets of Marlow Green with a raw, inquisitive edge, that he found himself standing in the long, low parlour of the old Grange at the end of the town, the residence of the lately deceased Mr. Ambrose Ferrers, a gentleman whose family had for two centuries been counted among the minor gentry of the district, but whose affairs, for the last twenty years, had been in a state of slow and dignified decay. The Grange itself, a solid, unpretending house of red brick, with a fine avenue of limes that had been allowed to grow into a tangled obscurity, spoke of a past prosperity that had dwindled into a shabby gentility. The furniture was of the era of the second George, heavy and good, but faded and worn; the carpet was threadbare in the centre of the room, where the feet of generations had trodden; and the portrait of a Ferrers in a periwig looked down from the wall with an air of supercilious surprise at the meanness of the surroundings.

Mr. Thimbleby was in his element. He moved among the effects with the cheerful alacrity of a man who sees in every dusty chair and tarnished mirror a potential item in his catalogue. He was attended by a young clerk, whose duty it was to note down the lots as they were indicated, and by the late Mr. Ferrers’s solicitor, a dry, cautious man named Ponder, who seemed to regard the auctioneer’s professional cheerfulness as a species of indecorum, if not a personal affront to the deceased.

“This bureau, now,” said Mr. Thimbleby, tapping a fine old piece of walnut-wood with his knuckles, “this is a very desirable lot. Genuine Chippendale, or I am no judge. It will fetch a good price in Loughborough. And these books—a very fair collection of divinity, with some curious tracts. There is a folio on the shelf yonder that would be worth a trifle to a collector. But we must look to the contents of the drawers and secret places. It is surprising what a family will leave in a bureau. I have known a will discovered in a work-box, and a fortune in bank-notes behind a drawer-bottom.”

The solicitor, Mr. Ponder, gave a dry cough. “I believe everything of a testamentary nature has been secured, Mr. Thimbleby. The drawers, I doubt not, contain only the usual miscellaneous accumulations of a man who has lived long in one place.”

But Mr. Thimbleby, whose curiosity was of that innocent, all-embracing sort which is the mainspring of his profession, had already pulled open the small drawers of the bureau, and was examining their contents with a rapid and experienced eye. There were old letters, bundles of receipts, a few trinkets, a lock of hair in a faded ribbon, and at the back of a small concealed drawer, which had been cunningly contrived in the woodwork, a packet of letters tied with a black ribbon, sealed with a seal that bore the Ferrers crest. The auctioneer held them up to the light. They were addressed in a delicate, sloping hand to “Ambrose Ferrers, Esq., at the Grange, Marlow Green,” and the superscription was in the handwriting of a woman.

Mr. Thimbleby’s cheerful countenance underwent a subtle change. He was a man of the world, and he knew that such a packet, falling into the wrong hands, might be a bombshell. He glanced at the solicitor, who was occupied in examining a case of stuffed birds, and then, with the instinct of a man who has handled many delicate matters, he slipped the packet into his pocket, resolving to examine it at his leisure.

It was not until the evening, in the privacy of his own little office, with its blazing fire and its walls adorned with notices of sales, that he untied the ribbon and read. The letters were from a lady, a Miss Harriet Comber, who had been, it appeared, the betrothed of the late Mr. Ferrers some thirty years before, and who had been cruelly deserted by him for a wealthier match. The letters were passionate, reproachful, and, in the light of the late gentleman’s subsequent career as a respected churchwarden and a model of decorum, utterly ruinous. They contained revelations of a private marriage, of a child that had died, and of a heartless desertion that had been the scandal of a distant town. The last letter was dated from a village in the north, and was written in a hand that trembled with a grief that had not yet learned to be bitter.

Mr. Thimbleby sat for a long time in his chair, the letters lying before him on the table. He was a cheerful man, but he was not a hard one. He had a wife and daughters of his own, and he knew the value of a good name. He thought of the late Mr. Ferrers, a pompous, portly man, who had been wont to sit in the front pew at church and to talk of the laxity of the age; he thought of the poor lady, who had perhaps died long ago, or who might still be living, a broken-hearted woman, in some obscure retreat; and he thought of the packet of letters, which, if sold, would fetch a few shillings from some curious collector, and would be the means of dragging a dead man’s name through the mire, and of bringing a blush to the cheeks of his innocent descendants.

The next morning, he called upon Mr. Ponder, the solicitor, and laid the packet before him. The solicitor read the letters with a face that grew longer and longer, and then he looked at Mr. Thimbleby with a gaze of profound and uneasy respect.

“This is a very serious matter, Mr. Thimbleby,” he said, in a hushed voice. “These letters, if they were made public, would—would be exceedingly detrimental to the character of the late Mr. Ferrers. The family would, I am sure, be prepared to—to make it worth your while to suppress them.”

Mr. Thimbleby’s cheerful countenance was for a moment clouded by a look of genuine disgust. “I am an auctioneer, Mr. Ponder,” he said, with a dignity that sat oddly upon his brisk little figure, “not a blackmailer. I did not come to you to sell the letters, but to ask your advice as to what is to be done with them. They are not mine to sell. They are not mine to burn. They belong, I take it, to the lady who wrote them, if she be alive, or to her representatives. It is my duty to return them to the person who has the best right to them.”

The solicitor was abashed. He had mistaken the auctioneer’s cheerful familiarity with the world for a want of principle, and he now perceived that beneath the professional manner there lay a solid core of honesty. It was arranged that the letters should be returned, through the solicitor, to the lady’s family, if any could be found, and that the matter should be kept a profound secret. Mr. Thimbleby walked home with a lighter heart than he had had for many a day. He had lost a good lot, but he had saved his conscience; and he was wont to say, in after years, when the subject of the Ferrers sale was mentioned, that the best day’s work he ever did in his life was the day he did not sell the letters. For, as he would observe, with a cheerful nod, “A man may make a fortune by his wits, but he cannot make a character by his hammer; and there is no lot so valuable as a quiet mind.”
