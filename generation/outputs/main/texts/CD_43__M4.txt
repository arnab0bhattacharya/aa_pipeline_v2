The morning was one of those soft, grey, weeping mornings peculiar to the suburbs of London, when the sky seems to have entered into a conspiracy with the soot to descend upon the earth in a fine, penetrating drizzle, and when the most sanguine of householders can find no comfort in his heart for anything but a good fire and a strong cup of tea. At No. 7, The Laurels, Clapham, a villa of stucco and pretension, standing in a garden of about a quarter of an acre, and looking as if it had been built by a speculative builder in a moment of reckless extravagance and furnished by his wife in a moment of even greater recklessness, there was no fire in the breakfast-parlour, and the tea-caddy was locked in the sideboard with a silence that spoke volumes.

Miss Clementina Vavasour, a young lady of some two-and-twenty summers, sat at the window of her own small sitting-room, gazing out upon the dripping laurels and the gravel walk, and thinking—as well she might—that the world had turned upside down. Her dress was of a sober grey merino, and her beautiful brown hair was drawn back with a simplicity that was almost severe; but no severity of attire could disguise the fact that she was a creature formed by Nature for the adornment of a drawing-room, and that her proper sphere was one of cushions, and china, and the tinkling of a piano-forte. She held in her hand a letter, which she read and re-read with a fixed and fascinated stare, as though it were a serpent that had stung her, and which, by some dreadful enchantment, she was powerless to drop.

The letter was from her uncle, Mr. Bartholomew Vavasour, of Vavasour and Vavasour, solicitors, of Gray’s Inn, and it was couched in terms of such profound and penitential gloom that it might have been written by a man who had just committed a murder, instead of one who had merely lost a fortune. It spoke of the Great Midland Junction and Continental Trunk Railway—a name that had been for years the very synonym of prosperity in the Vavasour household, and which had been wont to be pronounced by Mr. Vavasour with a flourish of the hand and a twinkle of the eye, as if he were the parent of the enterprise and the guarantor of its success. It spoke of calls, and shares, and the falling of stock, and the failure of a great contractor, and the flight of a defaulting secretary; and it concluded with the intelligence that the villa at Clapham, the carriages, the servants, the plate, and even the little pony-chaise in which Clementina had been accustomed to drive to the village of an afternoon, were all to be sold, and that the uncle and niece must henceforth seek a shelter in some humbler and less pretending quarter of the town.

Clementina let the letter fall upon her lap, and for a few moments gave herself up to a luxury of woe. She thought of the balls she had attended, of the partners she had danced with, of the bouquets she had received, of the young officers of the East India Company who had sighed and looked unutterable things, and of the Honourable Augustus Fitzgibbon, who had actually gone so far as to press her hand in the conservatory. And now—oh, the bitterness of it!—she was to be a poor relation, a dependent, a teacher of the young idea, a mere drudge in a school-room, at a salary that would scarcely keep her in gloves and shoe-ribbons. She wept—as who would not?—and her tears were the more poignant because they were shed in solitude, and because the laurels outside the window dripped and dripped, and the rain upon the glass made little zigzag rivers, as if the very heavens were weeping for her misfortunes.

It was in this condition of mind that she was interrupted by a knock at the door, and by the entrance of her uncle himself—a little, brisk, grey-headed man, with a face that was usually as cheerful as a winter fire, but which was now elongated to a most alarming extent, and wore an expression of such meek and melancholy resignation that it was impossible to look at him without a smile, had not the occasion been so very serious. He had his hat in his hand, and his umbrella under his arm, and he looked for all the world like a man who had been out in the rain and had found it not at all to his taste.

“My dear,” said Mr. Vavasour, sitting down upon the edge of a chair, and twirling his hat round and round with a nervous rapidity that was quite painful to witness—“my dear, I have been thinking—I have been thinking a great deal—and I have come to the conclusion that the best thing you can do is to accept the offer of your old governess, Miss Pinchbeck.”

Clementina started. Miss Pinchbeck! That excellent and energetic lady, who had once ruled over the nursery at The Laurels with a rod of iron, and who had since risen to the dignity of a school at Brixton, where she taught the daughters of small tradesmen and the orphans of dissenting ministers, and where she was understood to be making a very comfortable income out of the vanities and anxieties of parents. To be a teacher under Miss Pinchbeck! To be addressed as “Miss Vavasour” by a parcel of little girls in pinafores! To be obliged to wear a cap, and to correct exercises, and to hear the multiplication table, and to be thankful for a salary of twenty pounds a year! It was too much.

“Uncle,” said Clementina, “I would rather starve.”

“My dear,” returned Mr. Vavasour, “you would do nothing of the kind. You would only make a very uncomfortable and unbecoming fuss about it. I know you, my dear, better than you know yourself. You are a Vavasour, and the Vavasours have never starved yet, and I hope they never will; but they have a great deal of pride, and a great deal of obstinacy, and a great deal of nonsense, and I am afraid, my dear, that you have inherited a pretty fair share of all three. Miss Pinchbeck is a worthy woman, and she has offered you a home and a salary, and—and—in short, my dear, you must go.”

There was a tremor in the old man’s voice as he spoke, and Clementina, looking up, saw that his eyes were full of tears. She rose from her seat, and going over to him, put her arms round his neck, and laid her head upon his shoulder.

“I will go, uncle,” she said, with a little sob. “I will go, and I will try to be a credit to you. But oh, uncle, it is very hard!”

“It is, my dear,” said Mr. Vavasour, patting her hand; “it is very hard. But there is one comfort—we have still our health, and our appetites, and our—our—in short, my dear, we have still a great many blessings, though I confess I don’t at this moment distinctly remember what they are.”

And so it was settled. The next morning, a fly was ordered from the neighbouring livery stables—a vehicle of such a dusty and dejected appearance that it seemed to have been in mourning for the family misfortunes—and Clementina, with a small trunk and a large umbrella, was driven through the muddy streets of Clapham and Brixton to the door of Miss Pinchbeck’s establishment, which was situated in a narrow street near the church, and was distinguished by a brass plate, on which was engraved, in letters of a severe and uncompromising character, “Seminary for Young Ladies. Miss Pinchbeck.”

The door was opened by a little maid-of-all-work, whose face was as red as the fire, and whose apron was as white as the snow, and who informed the visitor, with a curtsey that was half a bob and half a courtesy, that Miss Pinchbeck was in the schoolroom, and that she would be with her in a minute. Clementina was shown into a parlour, where there was a strong smell of stuffy carpet, and a portrait of the Rev. Dr. Pinchbeck, in a black coat and a white cravat, looking as if he had been embalmed alive; and there she sat, with her heart beating, and her hands clasped in her lap, and her eyes fixed upon the portrait, until the door opened, and Miss Pinchbeck herself appeared.

She was a tall, spare woman, with a face that was all angles, and a nose that was all bridge, and a pair of spectacles that were all glare; and she had a way of putting her head on one side, and looking at you over her spectacles, that was exceedingly trying to the nerves. She was dressed in a black silk gown that was a little too tight for her, and a cap that was a little too large for her, and she carried in her hand a small cane, which she used for the purpose of pointing out the beauties of the English language to the young ladies, and occasionally for the purpose of enforcing her arguments upon their knuckles.

“My dear Miss Vavasour,” said Miss Pinchbeck, “I am very glad to see you. I have been expecting you. I hope your uncle is well? I see by the papers that the Great Midland Junction and Continental Trunk Railway is not quite so flourishing as it was. Ah! these speculations, these speculations! They are the curse of the age. I have a little money myself, my dear, in the three per cents, and I never touch anything else. But I am very glad to see you. You will find the young ladies a little noisy, perhaps, but they are very good girls, very good girls indeed; and I am sure you will be a great help to me. I have twenty-seven of them, my dear, twenty-seven, and I have only one assistant, Miss Crummles, who is very near-sighted, poor thing, and cannot tell a French verb from a German one. You will take the English and the geography, and the French, and the music, and the drawing, and the arithmetic, and the—in short, my dear, you will take everything that Miss Crummles cannot see, and that is pretty nearly everything. And now, if you are ready, we will go into the schoolroom.”

Clementina rose, and followed Miss Pinchbeck into the schoolroom, where twenty-seven young ladies, in brown holland frocks and white muslin collars, were seated at a long table, with their heads bent over their books, and where Miss Crummles, a little, faded, blinking woman, with a shawl over her shoulders and a pen behind her ear, was vainly endeavouring to impress upon a small girl the fact that the capital of France was not Paris, but Lyons, or perhaps it was Bordeaux—she was not quite sure which. And as Clementina stood there, with the eyes of twenty-seven young ladies fixed upon her, and the voice of Miss Pinchbeck ringing in her ears, she felt that the world had indeed turned upside down, and that the days of the pony-chaise and the conservatory and the Honourable Augustus Fitzgibbon were gone for ever.
