In the county of Loamshire, where the slow-moving waters of the Blythe reflect the gray western towers of Cloxham Cathedral, the inhabitants are much given to a quiet tenacity of opinion. It is a characteristic of such cathedral towns, where ancient stone and settled endowments breed a certain immovable self-satisfaction, that an alteration in the moral or financial landscape is met with a suspicion bordering on resentment. Thus, when the news leaked out that Miss Henrietta Albury was not, as half the close and the market-place had supposed, in possession of a fixed and unassailable income, but was indeed upon the very brink of ruin, the sentiment most immediately excited was not one of simple compassion, but rather a startled uneasiness as to how this unforeseen catastrophe might affect the liabilities of her neighbors.

Miss Albury, a spinster of fifty-seven, had long been the convenient reservoir into which the petty necessities of Cloxham had flowed. To her modest parlor in the precincts came the minor tradesmen, the impoverished curates, and the younger sons of the gentry, seeking to bridge a temporary gap between their ambitions and their accounts. She had lent money with a quiet absence of usury that was generally attributed to a lack of worldly wisdom, though the more astute might have perceived it was rooted in a mild disdain for the coarse mechanics of finance. It was therefore a profound shock to the Cloxham mind to learn that this dispenser of relief had herself been betrayed by the very machinery of commerce she had eschewed. Her small fortune, inherited from a brother in the East India Company, had been entrusted to a speculative mining concern in Derbyshire—a concern promoted by a distant cousin, whose plausible letters had woven a spell of guaranteed returns over Miss Albury’s otherwise exacting good sense. The mine had flooded, the shares were worthless, and Miss Albury’s forty pounds a year had vanished into the subterranean darkness.

Among those who felt the shock most acutely was Mr. Edward Phipps, a respectable draper with a shop in the high street. Mr. Phipps owed Miss Albury a sum of sixty pounds, contracted three years prior to stock his warehouse with silks, a debt she had never pressed, accepting his promise of repayment with a gentle nod that seemed to place the obligation in the realm of a mere moral understanding rather than a legal bond. Mr. Phipps was seated in his parlor above the shop, partaking of a dish of tea with his wife and his journeyman, Solomon Poole, when the news was brought in by his eldest daughter.

“Well, father,” said Miss Phipps, who had gathered the intelligence from the butcher’s boy, “they do say Miss Albury is quite broke down. Mrs. Trench says her cousin’s son came down from London, and the mine is gone, and she has scarce enough to pay her rent.”

Mr. Phipps, who was a man of a heavy, methodical countenance, paused with his cup halfway to his lips. He looked at his wife, and a slow, calculating relief spread through his features, though he instantly sought to mask it with an appearance of sympathetic gravity.

“Dear, dear,” he said, setting down the cup. “A very melancholy thing. A gentlewoman, too, and so kind to the poor. It is quite shocking how these speculations end.”

“But you owe her money, Edward,” said Mrs. Phipps, a woman of anxious virtues. “If she is in want, perhaps you should make it convenient to pay her back.”

Mr. Phipps cleared his throat, drawing his brows together in a way that gave his face the expression of a man wrestling with a profound ethical principle, though he was merely calculating the impact of sixty pounds on his forthcoming autumn purchases.

“That is a difficult point, my dear,” he replied, with a judicial slowness. “I am willing to acknowledge the debt. But the money was lent without note or bond. Miss Albury, in her kindness, has never asked for interest, and has never named a day. If I were to thrust sixty pounds upon her now, it might seem as though I took advantage of her misfortune to press a settlement she might not be prepared to receive. It would be a kind of affront to her pride.”

“Ay, it’s a puzzling thing,” said Solomon Poole, who was a lean, sharp-featured man, much given to whittling the edge of a table with his thumb. “But if her pride is like to go hungry, it’s a question whether she wouldn’t rather swallow it with a bit of bread and cheese. There’s them as says pride goes before a fall, but I allus thinks it follows after, when there’s nowt else to eat.”

“Hold your peace, Solomon,” said Mr. Phipps, with some asperity. “You do not understand the delicacies of feeling between a gentleman and a lady in Miss Albury’s position. The money is a debt of honor, and I shall keep it in reserve until she signifies, in some way, that she requires it. To force it upon her would be a kind of impertinence.”

Mrs. Phipps, who had less faith in her husband’s delicacies of feeling, pursed her lips but said nothing, resolving privately to call upon Miss Albury the next morning. Mr. Phipps returned to his tea, feeling that he had conducted the argument with a skill that did equal credit to his head and his heart, and reflecting that sixty pounds, safely retained, would buy a considerable quantity of new velvets for the winter trade.

The following day brought a subtle change in the atmosphere of Cloxham. Miss Albury’s misfortune became the property of the town, and was discussed with the eager solemnity that accompanies the troubles of the great. The Dean’s wife drove to the precincts in her carriage, and returned with a report that Miss Albury bore her affliction with a Christian fortitude that was quite affecting. The minor canons shook their heads, and several of them, who owed Miss Albury small sums, found themselves unexpectedly called away to distant parishes on urgent business.

But if the men of Cloxham were disposed to let delicacy shield them from the immediate inconvenience of repayment, there was one among the gentler sex who was not so afflicted. Mrs. Arabella Trent, the rector’s wife, who had borrowed twenty pounds to pay for her daughter’s schooling, walked into Miss Albury’s parlor on the second morning after the news, carrying a small canvas bag in her reticule.

Miss Albury was sitting by her window, which looked out upon the cathedral yard, mending a piece of lace with a quiet patience that might have deceived a casual observer into thinking her mind was at rest. She was, in truth, engaged in one of those painful mental revisions of the past, in which the injured party alternately upbraids themselves for their credulity and the world for its deceit. The sight of Mrs. Trent, however, roused her from her reverie, and she rose with a gentle, composed greeting that had in it no trace of the anxiety she had been feeling.

“My dear Miss Albury,” said Mrs. Trent, seating herself with an air of bustling purpose, “I have come on a very errand, I assure you. I have heard the news, and I am shocked beyond measure. It is a wicked world, and I have always said so. But I am not one to stand by and see a friend in want. Here is the twenty pounds you lent me for Lucy’s schooling. I had not thought to pay it for another six months, but I have gathered it together, and I insist upon your taking it.”

Miss Albury colored deeply. She had not expected this, and the sudden materialization of her need in the eyes of another was more painful than the want itself.

“My dear Mrs. Trent,” she said, in a low voice, “you are very kind. But I cannot take it. I know that you need it for Lucy, and I would not—”

“Nonsense,” said Mrs. Trent, firmly, pressing the bag into Miss Albury’s hand. “You must take it. It is your own money, and I am only returning what I borrowed. Do not be proud, my dear. Pride is a sin, and I shall tell the rector you said so.”

Miss Albury’s eyes filled with tears, and she grasped the hand of her friend. The bag of money was a small thing in itself, but it was a token that the world was not entirely composed of such calculating tenderness as Mr. Phipps’s.

Among the others, the resolution of conscience was slower. Mr. Phipps, discovering a few days later that Mrs. Trent had paid her debt, and that the Dean’s wife had been making inquiries as to the extent of Miss Albury’s liabilities, began to feel that his position was not so secure as he had supposed. The fear of being thought ungenerous by his social superiors is, in a provincial town, a force more potent than the dictates of conscience; and Mr. Phipps, after a week of increasing discomfort, called upon Miss Albury with a check for thirty pounds, explaining, with a face of pious concern, that he regretted he could not at present manage the full sum, but hoped to remit the balance at Christmas.

“I am sure, Mr. Phipps,” said Miss Albury, with a quiet smile, “that I am very grateful for your consideration. I did not wish to press anyone, and I had thought, perhaps, that my affairs were not generally known.”

“Oh, the town talks, Miss Albury, the town talks,” said Mr. Phipps, fidgeting with his hat. “But I have always said that a debt of honor is a sacred thing. I should not have slept at night, I assure you, had I not come.”

He departed with the consciousness of having performed a virtuous action, and Miss Albury sat down again by her window, looking out upon the cathedral, with a heart that was divided between gratitude and a mild, reflective amusement at the sudden activity of the Cloxham conscience. The great gray towers stood as they had stood for five hundred years, indifferent to the small tides of obligation and release that ebbed and flowed beneath their shadow; and Miss Albury, in the wisdom that adversity was beginning to teach her, perceived that human goodness, like the cathedral stone, is often most enduring when it is least conspicuous, and most readily called forth not by the pure light of duty, but by the more earthly illumination of a neighbor’s watchful eye.
