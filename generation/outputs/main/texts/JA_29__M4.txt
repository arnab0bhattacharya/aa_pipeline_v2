The dinner was fixed for half past five, and by a quarter before that hour Mrs. Ellingham had already surveyed the table three times, altered the disposition of the candlesticks twice, and desired the butler to assure himself that the second remove of game should not be wanting in the sauce which her cook, upon the authority of an aunt who had lived eleven years in Lord Seaton’s family, was pleased to call *cullis*. It was an occasion of more than common expectation. Mr. Ellingham, of Ashbury Park, had invited to his board no less a person than Sir John Ferrier, whose interest in the county was very great, and whose good opinion, it was reasonably supposed, might be of material service to a young man just entering upon the law. That the young man in question, his eldest son, was at that moment in his chamber, composing his countenance to the gravity proper to a future barrister, and rehearsing certain remarks upon the state of the nation which he designed to let fall with an air of easy reflection, was a circumstance highly gratifying to both parents.

The family were assembled in the drawing-room, and the Miss Ellinghams, in their best muslins, were disposed upon the sofa in attitudes of becoming composure, when the door was thrown open with a freedom that belonged to no servant of the house, and there entered a young man in a naval uniform, brown as a nut, with a countenance so open and so glad that it seemed to light the whole apartment.

“Tom!” cried the mother, rising. “Good heavens, Tom!”

“Why, mother, you look as though I were the French fleet,” said he, kissing her heartily. “I am not come to burn the house, I promise you. Only I am paid off at last, and here I am, and devil a bit of dinner have I had since Portsmouth.”

It was impossible to receive such a son with displeasure; yet it must be owned that his arrival was felt, by the elder part of the family, to be a thing of some inconvenience. He was the younger brother; he had gone to sea at twelve; he had never been expected to do anything but fight, and to be agreeable when he was at home; and he had a habit of speaking of the most tremendous occurrences in a tone of the most unguarded merriment, which, however it became a sailor, was not exactly what Mr. Ellingham desired to exhibit before a baronet whose politics were grave and whose digestion was delicate.

“You will dine with us, of course,” said Mr. Ellingham, with a look of some meaning at his wife. “We expect Sir John Ferrier.”

“Sir John Ferrier! Do we, indeed? And who is Sir John Ferrier, when he is at home?”

“A gentleman of very large property in this county,” replied the father, with dignity, “and one whose good word, Thomas, may be of the greatest consequence to your brother.”

“Then I shall be monstrous civil to him,” said the lieutenant. “I shall tell him the story of the *Ariel* — she was a tight little sloop, sir, and we lost her on the rocks off Ushant in a sea that would have frightened you out of your seven senses—and if that does not make a man of property take to me, why, I know nothing of human nature.”

Mr. Ellingham looked as though he would have given a good deal to know nothing of it either.

The dinner was served, and the party was not long in taking their places. Sir John Ferrier, a heavy gentleman of sixty, with a face that seemed to have been cast in a mould of formal civility, was placed at Mrs. Ellingham’s right hand, and made those observations upon the weather and the roads which are the natural preface to all country hospitality. The elder son, whose name was Charles, sat with a decent gravity, and threw in now and then a remark of studied moderation. The lieutenant, at the lower end of the table, ate with an appetite that did honour to the sea air, and looked about him with the frankest satisfaction.

For the first half hour, all went as well as could be desired. Sir John pronounced the soup to be excellent; Mr. Ellingham bowed; Mrs. Ellingham hoped the partridges were not overdone; and the Miss Ellinghams, who were three in number, said nothing at all, which is often the most prudent course for young ladies at a dinner of ceremony.

But the second remove had not been long upon the table before Sir John, with the condescension of a man who has seen the world, was pleased to ask the lieutenant a question touching his profession.

“And you have been upon the salt seas, sir, I understand?”

“Upon them and in them, Sir John,” returned Tom, with great good humour. “I have been in them up to my chin, and that not above three weeks ago. I shall never forget it. We were coming home with dispatches, and a foul wind, and a foul night, and the *Ariel* — for she was the *Ariel* then, poor thing — went upon the rocks off Ushant, and there she lay, beating and grinding, while the sea made a clean breach over her. We got the boats out, and the men behaved like men; but the long boat was stove, and there was nothing for it but to swim for it.”

“Good God!” said Mrs. Ellingham.

“And did you swim?” cried Sir John, with a stare.

“Swim! Lord bless you, sir, I swam till I was as sick as a dog, and then I swam again. There was a young midshipman alongside of me, a boy of fourteen, and he says to me, says he, ‘Mr. Ellingham, I can’t swim much longer;’ and I says to him, says I, ‘Then you must swim a little more;’ and so he did, and we came ashore together upon a piece of the wreck, and there we were, wet through, and the wind blowing great guns, and nothing to eat but a biscuit that had been in the pocket of a dead man, and that was so salt that we might as well have eaten the sea.”

The young ladies looked at one another; the eldest, whose name was Augusta, turned a little pale, and then a little red, and fixed her eyes upon her plate. Sir John Ferrier laid down his knife and fork, and regarded the narrator with a countenance in which astonishment struggled with a sort of unwilling admiration.

“And you tell this, sir,” said he, “with as much composure as if you were talking of a ride in the park.”

“Why, sir,” replied Tom, “if a man cannot laugh at his own misfortunes, he has no business to have any. I have been drowned, as one may say, twice, and I never could see the use of making a wry face about it.”

Mr. Ellingham here interposed, with an air of some anxiety, to observe that the partridges were getting cold; but the baronet, who had now got a subject, would not let it go.

“And what, sir, may I ask, is your opinion of the state of the navy at present?”

“My opinion, Sir John? Why, that it is the finest navy in the world, and that it would be a great deal finer if the people at home would send us a few more of the things we want, and not quite so many of the things we do not. But I am no politician. I only know that I have been in action twice, and that I would rather be in action than in a drawing-room any day of the week.”

This was said with so much honest heartiness that Sir John could not forbear a smile; and Mrs. Ellingham, perceiving that the danger was passing off, was beginning to breathe again, when her son, with the most unfortunate want of tact, turned to Augusta, who sat near him, and said, in a lower voice, but not so low but that half the table might hear:

“And how do you do, cousin? I have not seen you these five years. You were a little girl then, and now you are a great one. I declare you are grown handsome.”

Augusta coloured violently, and said, “Indeed, sir, you are very obliging,” with an air of great confusion.

“I am not obliging at all,” returned he. “I never pay compliments. I have no time for them at sea. But I have thought of you very often, I assure you, when I have been upon the watch at night, and the stars were out, and I have said to myself, ‘There is my cousin Augusta at Ashbury, and she is a monstrous fine girl, and I hope she is well.’”

Here Charles, with a look of great severity, observed that the stars were not a subject upon which he was accustomed to converse; and Sir John Ferrier, with a countenance of the most rigid gravity, desired to know whether the lieutenant had ever been in the Mediterranean.

“In the Mediterranean, Sir John! I have been in every sea that has a name, and in some that have none. I have been at Gibraltar, and at Malta, and at Naples, and at Constantinople, where the Turks wear turbans, and the women are kept in a sort of box, which I think a very odd way of treating a lady. I have seen the sun rise over the Pyramids, and I have seen a man hanged at the yard-arm, and I have seen the sea in a calm that was worse than a storm, and I have seen —”

“Thomas,” said Mr. Ellingham, “you will oblige me by taking a glass of wine.”

“With all my heart, sir,” said he, and drank it off. “And now, since we are upon the subject of obliging, I will tell you what I have been thinking of all dinner-time. I have been thinking that I should like to marry Augusta.”

The effect of this declaration was such as might be expected. Mrs. Ellingham screamed; the two younger Miss Ellinghams tittered; Charles pushed back his chair; and Mr. Ellingham, with a face of scarlet, desired his son to remember where he was.

“I do remember where I am,” said Tom, with perfect good humour. “I am at my father’s table, and there is no place in the world where a man may speak the truth more freely. I have three thousand pounds, prize-money, and my pay, and a heart as sound as a bell, and I have loved Augusta these five years, and I should like to know what anybody has to say against it.”

Sir John Ferrier here rose from his seat, with an air of the most profound solemnity, and observed that he had not the honour of being acquainted with the family’s private arrangements, and that he would not intrude upon them. Mr. Ellingham, in an agony, entreated him to be seated; but the baronet, with a bow that was intended to be polite, and was in reality very cold, declared that he had a long drive before him, and that he must wish them good evening.

He was gone; the door was closed; and the family were left to contemplate, in silence and dismay, the ruins of a dinner which had cost so much pains to arrange.

“Well,” said Tom, at length, with the most unruffled cheerfulness, “I am sorry the old gentleman is gone, for I liked him; but as for the rest, I say again, I should like to marry Augusta.”

And Augusta, though she said nothing, and though she looked as though she wished the floor would open and swallow her, did not, in her heart, entirely disapprove of the proposal.
