The parish of Lambeth, in that year of Our Lord eighteen hundred and forty-something, was a labyrinth of lanes and alleys that seemed to have been designed by a madman with a taste for smoke and a total disregard for the principles of straight lines. The houses, huddled together for warmth or perhaps for mutual protection against the very streets they made, leaned over the pavement with a gossipy, conspiratorial air, their upper stories whispering secrets to one another across the narrow chasms of air. Down below, in the perpetual twilight of the gutters, the world was a churn of commerce and complaint: the cry of the milk-girl, the rumble of the coal-cart, the percussive argument of a hundred different trades, all set to the grimy, ever-present bass note of the city’s breath.

Into this cacophony, there came a melody. It was a tune not of the city, but for it—a tune that spoke of country lanes and starlit nights, though its home was now the damp flagstones outside the sign of the Tumbledown Mare. Here, on most evenings, stood old Silas Croft and his granddaughter, Lottie. Silas, whose eyes had long since given up the thankless task of seeing the world, was a man built of shadows and sound. His face, a roadmap of sorrows etched by time and hardship, was perpetually tilted towards some celestial listener, and from the fiddle beneath his chin, he coaxed a music that was at once merry and profoundly mournful. He played for pennies, for pints of porter left half-full on a windowsill, and for the fleeting, unspoken gratitude of the weary souls who paused to listen.

And then there was Lottie. A creature of ten years, all sharp elbows and wide, watchful eyes that took in everything her grandfather could not. She was the keeper of the hat, the interpreter of the world, the fierce protector of their small, fragile enterprise. She would stand beside him, her hand sometimes resting on his worn coat, a small, pale anchor in the swirling tide of the street. She knew which faces meant a penny, which meant a curse, and which meant nothing at all. When the music was done, she would dip a quick, solemn curtsy and hold out the hat, her gaze as steady and unblinking as a sparrow’s.

But the fog, that great London thief that steals warmth from the bones and colour from the day, stole something more precious from old Silas. It settled in his lungs and would not be moved. It turned his merry tunes to a rattling cough and his strong bow-arm to a trembling reed. One evening, he played not a note, but leaned against the grimy bricks of the Mare, his fiddle silent in its case at his feet, while Lottie, with a courage that was far too large for her small body, took it up. Her small fingers, clumsy at first, found the strings. The tune she produced was a thin, fragile thing, a sparrow’s song where an eagle’s had been, but it was music nonetheless. The pennies, however, fell not into the hat, but into the hands of a man who had been watching from the pub’s warm doorway.

He was a spectacle in himself. A man of magnificent proportions, clad in a coat of a plum-coloured hue that seemed to have swallowed all the available light, with a waistcoat of a startling yellow, and a silver-headed cane that he tapped upon the stones with an air of delightful impatience. His face was a marvel of polished benevolence, his smile a masterwork of artifice, and his voice, when he spoke, was as smooth and rich as the cream atop a milk-pail.

“My dear child!” he boomed, sweeping off a hat that was a confection of silk and feathers. “Such a prodigious talent! Such a jewel, discovered in the gutter! It is an affront to the Muses! A veritable crime against art!” He tapped his cane for emphasis, as if to arrest the very notion of such a crime. Lottie, who had learned to be suspicious of grand gestures and grander words, simply stared, the fiddle held tight against her chest.

“I am Professor Phineas Plimpton,” the man continued, with a bow that seemed to encompass Lottie, her grandfather, the public house, and a significant portion of the county of Surrey. “Proprietor and Curator of Plimpton’s Peripatetic Panorama of Peculiar Persons and Prodigies. I seek the rare, the remarkable, the truly unique. And you, my little songbird, are a nightingale amongst sparrows.” He produced, from the plum-coloured cavern of his coat, a card, stiff and white, upon which his name was printed in letters of a most commanding nature.

Silas, roused by the booming voice, stirred and coughed. “Lottie,” he rasped, “who is it?”

The Professor’s smile did not waver; if anything, it broadened, becoming a thing of almost architectural solidity. “A friend, my good man! A patron! A visionary! I am offering your remarkable granddaughter a position of honour, of fame, of remuneration far beyond these paltry pennies! She shall be ‘The Lambeth Lark, the Blind Man’s Wonder’! She shall play before lords and ladies! She shall see the world!”

The world, to Lottie, consisted of the streets between the river and the Kennington Road. The idea of any other was as fantastical as the man himself. She looked from his glittering smile to her grandfather’s pale, sightless face, and then to the silent fiddle in her hands. The fiddle had always been her grandfather’s voice. To play it alone was one thing; to take it away from him, to sell its music to a stranger in a plum-coloured coat, felt like a betrayal of the deepest kind.

“Grandfather plays,” she said, her voice small but clear as a bell. “We play together.”

The Professor’s smile tightened by a fraction of a degree. “Of course, of course! A duet! An even greater marvel! The blind maestro and his prodigy! But the old gentleman, you see, is… indisposed. The rigours of the road would be too much for him. But for you, my little Lark, the road is a path to glory!”

He spoke of glory as if it were a loaf of bread one could simply purchase and eat. He spoke of money, of warm meals, of a bed that was not a pile of rags in a corner. He painted a picture with his words, a panorama of a life Lottie had never dared to imagine, and it was a beautiful, seductive picture indeed. She felt the pull of it, the temptation of a full belly and a roof that did not leak. But as she looked at the man, she saw that his eyes, though smiling, were as hard and as shiny as two new shillings. They were not looking at her, but at a reflection of her, a commodity to be polished and sold.

She looked down at her grandfather’s hand, lying still and grey on the cobblestones. It was a hand that had held hers every night on the long walk home, a hand that had wiped her tears and mended her clothes. This man offered her the world, but he offered it in exchange for that hand.

She shook her head, a slow, solemn gesture. “No, thank you, sir,” she said, and dipped a curtsy as she had been taught.

For a moment, the Professor’s magnificent façade seemed to crack. A flicker of annoyance, sharp and cold, darted across his features. But it was gone in an instant, replaced once more by that unwavering, theatrical beam. “A loyal heart! How charming! How utterly provincial!” He tapped his cane one last time. “The offer, my little Lark, is a standing one. A bird that will not sing in a golden cage may yet learn to regret its time in the rain.” And with another sweeping bow, he turned and melted back into the shadows of the Tumbledown Mare, leaving behind nothing but the scent of expensive pomade and the ghost of a promise.

Lottie knelt and carefully placed the fiddle back in its worn case. She took her grandfather’s cold hand in her own. The music was still there, in the wood and the strings. It was theirs. And as the gas lamps of Lambeth began to flicker to life one by one, casting long, dancing shadows on the wet stones, she knew it was enough. It had to be.
