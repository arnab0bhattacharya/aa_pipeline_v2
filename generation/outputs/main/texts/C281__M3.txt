The pain in Mrs. Letitia Callow’s hip was a solid, leaden thing, dragging her down into the horsehair mattress as though the bed itself were trying to swallow her. It had been three weeks since the cobblestones of Grafton Mews had reached up to claim her, her boot heel turning sharply on a piece of scattered coal, sending her sprawling across the filth of the gutter. The surgeon had set the bone, bound her tight, and left her with a bottle of laudanum and the unspoken certainty that she would not walk unassisted again. Letitia lay in the dim, cramped room above her shuttered shop, staring at the water stain on the plaster ceiling, listening to the relentless percussion of rain against the single pane of glass. 

She was entirely dependent, and the object of her dependence was a woman she had spent the last decade actively ruining. 

Below, in the narrow kitchen that smelled of tallow and boiled mutton, Mrs. Esther Roe was grinding mustard seed. The rhythmic crunch of the pestle against the mortar rose through the floorboards, a grating, domestic sound that set Letitia’s teeth on edge. Letitia closed her eyes, wishing the sound were a curse, a shout, or the slam of a door. She could have borne cruelty. She understood cruelty. Cruelty was the currency of Somers Town, the only language spoken fluently by landlords, debtors, and magistrates alike. 

Seven years ago, Esther’s husband, Thomas, had been crushed between a brewer’s dray and a brick wall in the Euston Road. He had lingered for three days, his chest filling with fluid, his lips turning blue. Esther had come to Letitia’s counter, her hands shaking, her cap askew, begging for a loan of five pounds to pay the surgeon who might drain the fluid and save Thomas’s life. Letitia had looked at the woman’s frayed cuffs, at the desperate, swimming panic in her eyes, and calculated the risk. Thomas was dying; the debt would die with him. Letitia had refused the loan outright, but she had offered a shilling for the man’s wedding ring, which Esther, in her distraction, had not yet removed from her own finger. 

Thomas had died. Esther had gone to the workhouse for a winter, scrubbing the paupers’ floors until her knees bled, before securing a position taking in washing. Letitia had thought of the matter exactly twice since then, each time concluding that she had acted as any sensible businesswoman must. The world was a river, and one did not weep for the drowning when one was safely on the bank.

Now, the river had shifted. Letitia was the one drowning, and Esther Roe was pulling her from the water with a terrible, unrelenting gentleness.

The door creaked open, letting in a draft of cold air and the smell of starch. Esther entered carrying a wooden tray. She was a stout woman now, her arms thick from years of wringing heavy linens, her face weathered but composed. She set the tray on the bedside table, adjusting the candle stub so the light fell across the bowl of broth.

"You must eat, Mrs. Callow," Esther said, her voice low and devoid of inflection. 

"I am not hungry," Letitia snapped, turning her face away to the wall. 

"The surgeon said you must keep your strength up if the bone is to knit."

"I know what the surgeon said. I am not a fool."

"No, Mrs. Callow. You are not a fool."

Esther did not argue. She never argued. She simply pulled a chair to the bedside, dipped the spoon into the broth, and held it patiently near Letitia’s lips. The silence stretched between them, thick and suffocating. Letitia wanted to scream. She wanted Esther to remind her of the ring, of Thomas, of the workhouse. She wanted the woman to spit in the broth, to tighten the bandages until the bone screamed, to take her revenge in a thousand petty, human ways. If Esther hated her, Letitia could hate her back. They could be enemies, locked in a familiar, honorable combat. But this steady, gray-eyed compassion was an armor Letitia could not pierce. 

"I can feed myself," Letitia muttered, reaching out a trembling, liver-spotted hand. 

"Very well." Esther passed her the spoon. Letitia’s grip slipped, and the spoon clattered against the bowl, splashing broth onto the counterpane. Letitia stared at the wet stain, her jaw working. She felt the hot sting of humiliation, a sensation far more acute than the throbbing in her hip. She was a woman who had held gold sovereigns in her hands, who had made grown men weep and plead at her counter. Now she could not hold a spoon.

Esther calmly took a rag, wiped the stain, took the spoon back, and resumed feeding her. Letitia submitted, swallowing the salty liquid, feeling it burn a path down her throat. She watched Esther’s face, searching for the microscopic twitch of a smile, the faint glimmer of satisfaction in the eye. She found nothing. There was only a profound, exhausting patience.

When the bowl was empty, Esther set it aside and began to arrange the blankets. "The boy is bringing up a scuttle of coals for the fire," she said. "It will be cold tonight."

"Mrs. Roe," Letitia said, her voice cracking before she could stop it. "Why do you not say it?"

Esther paused, her hands resting on the woolen blanket. "Say what, Mrs. Callow?"

"About Thomas. About the ring. About what I did to you."

Esther looked at her for a long moment. The rain lashed against the window, the sound like gravel thrown against glass. "What good would it do to speak of it now?"

"It would make sense of this," Letitia hissed, gesturing weakly at the room, at the tray, at Esther. "You play the saint. It is unnatural. You should hate me."

"I did hate you," Esther said quietly. She sat back in the chair, her hands folding in her lap. "When Thomas passed, and the workhouse master took my boy, I hated you more than I hated the drayman who dropped the barrel. I used to walk past this shop and look at the shutters and think of how I might set them alight."

Letitia felt a grim relief wash over her. Finally, the truth. "And now you wait for me to die. You keep me alive just to watch me suffer."

"No, Mrs. Callow," Esther said, shaking her head slowly. "I do not hate you anymore. I haven't for a long while."

"You lie."

"I am not a liar. I left the hate behind in the workhouse. It was too heavy to carry, and it wasn't doing me any good." Esther leaned forward slightly, her gaze steady. "You are a miserable, frightened old woman who has broken her hip and has no one in the world but the washerwoman she once spurned. You are suffering plenty. I do not need to add to it."

Letitia turned her face back to the wall, the words striking her with a force no physical blow could have matched. She had wanted to be a villain, to be despised. To be despised was to have power, to have left a mark upon the world. But Esther’s forgiveness was an erasure. It reduced Letitia’s lifetime of ruthless calculation, her hoarded shillings, her iron-clad ledgers, to nothing more than the actions of a frightened, miserable old woman. She was not a monster to be feared; she was merely a pathetic creature to be pitied.

"I will bring you a posset for the pain later," Esther said, rising from the chair. She picked up the tray and moved toward the door.

"Mrs. Roe," Letitia called out, her voice barely a whisper. 

Esther stopped, half-turning in the doorway. The candlelight cast a long shadow across the floorboards.

"The coals," Letitia said, struggling to find her breath, struggling to maintain the fiction of her authority. "Tell the boy to bank them well. I feel the damp."

"Yes, Mrs. Callow," Esther said softly. "I will tell him."

The door closed with a quiet click. Letitia lay in the darkening room, listening to the retreating footsteps on the stairs, the clatter of the tray in the kitchen below, the ceaseless drumming of the rain. She closed her eyes against the sting of tears she would never allow herself to shed, trapped entirely within the unyielding, silent prison of Esther’s grace.
