Mr. Arthur Penrose was a young man of such middling fortune and moderate ambition that he had long since ceased to expect that his life would be marked by any event of particular distinction. His estate, Ashworth Park, in the quiet county of Berkshire, was not large enough to command the awe of the gentry, nor so small as to invite the pity of the poor. It was, in fact, a place of perfect mediocrity, and Arthur, who possessed a nature not ill-tempered but distinctly passive, had settled into the rhythm of its hours with the ease of a cat in a sunbeam. He read, he walked, he attended to the modest requirements of his two sisters, and he waited for the world to happen to him, which, given his lack of initiative, seemed unlikely to occur without considerable prompting.

The prompting came, as it so often did, from Miss Eliza and Miss Clara Penrose, who resided with their brother and whose opinions on the management of his household and his heart were as numerous as they were unasked for. Eliza, the elder, possessed a face of severe correctness and a mind that catalogued every interaction in the ledger of social advantage. Clara, her junior, was less severe but equally determined, her eyes holding a sharp, acquisitive gleam that Arthur had learned to find uncomfortable. They had, over the course of the last month, subjected Arthur to a barrage of inquiries regarding his prospects, his debts, and his health, all of which were answered with a brevity that Eliza interpreted as evasiveness and Clara as sulking.

It was Eliza who finally delivered the blow, in the library, while the rain lashed against the windows and the fire burnt low. She placed a letter upon the table before Arthur, who was attempting to ignore the sound of her voice by concentrating on a volume of history that he had not read in three years.

"Here, Arthur," she said, her tone carrying the weight of finality. "You will read this. It is from Mrs. Halloway."

Arthur looked up, his eyes meeting his sister’s with a mixture of fatigue and dawning dread. Mrs. Halloway was a widow of forty-five, possessed of an income of three thousand pounds a year, a carriage that never left the park, and a reputation for being both generous and tedious. Arthur had met her twice, and on both occasions had suffered through conversations that ranged from the price of silk to the spiritual dangers of idle gossip, without a moment’s pause for breath.

"I am aware of Mrs. Halloway," Arthur said, keeping his voice level. "I am not aware, however, that I have requested any correspondence from her."

"You do not need to request it," Eliza replied, smoothing the corner of the letter. "You need only accept it. She is a sensible woman, Arthur. Sensible, and wealthy. Your income is insufficient for your station. I have calculated it. With Mrs. Halloway’s fortune, you could maintain the house properly, provide for us in comfort, and avoid the humiliation of relying on charity from distant cousins. Is that not worth a small sacrifice of your own romantic fantasies?"

Arthur did not look at the letter. He looked instead at the fire, where a log was cracking and spitting. "I have no romantic fantasies, Eliza. I have no desire to marry at all, sensible or otherwise."

"Such a noble sentiment," Clara interjected from the chair in the corner, where she had been pretending to sew but was in fact watching Arthur’s face with intense scrutiny. "But noble sentiments do not pay for the roof repairs, Arthur. And Mrs. Halloway is not merely a financial proposition. She is kind. She likes you. She has spoken of your quiet demeanor as a virtue in these days of excess."

"Quietness," Arthur said, "is often a mask for lack of interest. But I do not think Mrs. Halloway would find that objectionable. She has little interest in anything else."

Eliza’s lips tightened. "You are being ungrateful. We are trying to secure your future. To marry her is to secure our own. If you refuse, you leave us in a precarious position. You cannot expect us to live on your meager salary forever. We are not beggars, Arthur, but we are not rich, and we have expectations."

The word "expectations" hung in the air, heavy and suffocating. Arthur felt a sudden, sharp irritation, not at the widow, but at the mechanism of his own existence. He was a puppet, and his sisters were the ones holding the strings, pulling him left and right according to their own calculations of comfort and status. He liked Mrs. Halloway, or rather, he tolerated her with a degree of peace that he found rare. She did not ask for much, except for attention and the assurance of her own importance. She was safe. She was boring. And she was the key to the door that Eliza was holding open.

"Let me think about it," Arthur said, finally picking up the letter.

"Think quickly," Eliza warned. "Mrs. Halloway is visiting the Whitmores next week. A delay in your decision might be interpreted as a rejection."

Arthur walked out of the library and into the rain. The air was cold and damp, and the smell of wet earth was sharp in his nostrils. He walked for an hour, his thoughts turning over the proposition like a stone in a stream. To marry Mrs. Halloway was to surrender his independence to a woman who would likely dominate his home, and to acknowledge the authority of his sisters, who would forever hold the debt of his marriage over his head. He would be safe, comfortable, and utterly owned.

To refuse was to remain poor, to be dependent on the goodwill of others, and to face the quiet, persistent nagging of Eliza and Clara, who would never let him forget that his pride had cost them their comfort. He thought of Mrs. Halloway’s smile, which was wide and genuine, if slightly vacant. She had asked him once if he enjoyed reading, and when he said yes, she had nodded and said, "That is well. It is a quiet pleasure. I enjoy it too, though I find the plots often too complicated." It was a simple, honest observation. There was no pretense in her, no calculation. She was what she was: a woman with money and time to spare, looking for a companion who would not challenge her.

Arthur stopped walking and turned back toward the house. The lights in the windows were warm and inviting, a beacon of the domesticity that awaited him. He realized, with a sudden clarity, that he did not dislike being managed; he disliked being managed by people who did not care for him. Eliza and Clara cared for their own comfort, and they used him as the instrument to achieve it. Mrs. Halloway, on the other hand, cared for herself, and she would use him, but she would do so with a frankness that was almost refreshing. There was no hidden agenda, no subtle manipulation. It was a transaction, plain and simple.

He reached the house and entered the kitchen, where the housekeeper was putting the fire out.

"Mr. Penrose?" she said, surprised to see him so late.

"Yes. I have a decision to make."

"Does that concern the weather, sir?"

"No," Arthur said, a small smile touching his lips. "It concerns Mrs. Halloway."

The housekeeper’s eyebrows raised. "And what do you intend to do, sir?"

"I intend to write to her," Arthur said. "And I intend to accept."

He walked up the stairs to his room, his mind already turning to the phrasing of his letter. It would be polite, brief, and devoid of any passion. It would be, he decided, the most honest thing he had ever written. He sat at his desk and began to write, the scratch of the pen on paper the only sound in the house, as the rain continued to fall, washing the world clean of all but the simple, stark facts of his life.
