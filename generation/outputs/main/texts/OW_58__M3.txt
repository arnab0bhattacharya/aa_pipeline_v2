When Claude Marlow stepped into the pale, amber-tinted drawing room in Berkeley Square, the first thing he noticed was the heavy perfume of white violets. The second was that Beatrice was wearing pearls. They were not the modest seed-pearls he had threaded for her once, on a rainy afternoon in Kensington, but large, perfectly matched spheres that caught the fading November light. They rested upon her throat with the calm assurance of things that cost a great deal of money. Claude, who had spent three years breeding sheep in the dust of New South Wales, felt a sudden, profound respect for them.

She rose from the chaise longue, a vision of pale silk and undisturbed composure. Her golden hair was dressed high, after the new fashion, and her eyes held the cool, untroubled gaze of a Greek marble. She did not flutter, nor did she weep. She merely extended a hand, as cool as a lily.

“Claude,” she said, with a soft, musical inflection. “You have come back. How perfectly extraordinary of you.”

“Is it?” he asked, taking the hand and dropping it again. “I rather thought it was inevitable. The sea, you see, permits no permanent separations. It merely delays introductions.”

“Please, sit down,” she murmured, motioning him toward a chair. “You must be terribly fatigued by the journey. London is so trying in November. The fog is quite theatrical, is it not? It makes everything look like a bad etching.”

Claude sat down. He was a young man of remarkable composure, but his face, tanned by a fierce colonial sun, looked oddly out of place amidst the delicate Louis XVI furniture and the blue china. He had left London three years ago, a slender, pale youth given to quoting Keats and wearing cravats of a somewhat too violent a hue. He had returned a man of iron muscles, quiet eyes, and an absolute indifference to poetry. Australia, he had discovered, was a country that taught one the value of silence. It was a lesson, he reflected, that London society would do well to learn.

“I received your letter in Adelaide,” he said, his voice low and even. “It was forwarded to me by a man who deals in wool. He seemed to think it was a bill. He was not entirely wrong.”

Beatrice gave a gentle, melancholy smile. “I wrote it in a state of great distress,” she said. “I was in despair. We thought you were dead. The ship, you know, was reported lost.”

“Yes,” Claude replied, looking steadily at the fire. “The *Miranda*. She foundered off the Cape. But I was not on her. I had missed the sailing by a matter of ten minutes. I was detained by a man who insisted on reading me his views on the future of the colonies. The irony of it is quite terrible. Had I listened to only one more stanza of his rhetoric, I should undoubtedly have drowned. As it was, I took the next vessel, and spent three years in the Antipodes, learning to despise sheep and to admire sunsets.”

“We mourned for you,” Beatrice said, her eyes resting on a magnificent bowl of crimson roses that occupied the centre of the tea-table. “Arthur was quite broken by it. He wore a black band on his hat for a month. It was the only thing about him that was not entirely faultless.”

“Arthur,” said Claude, with a faint, amused flicker in his voice. “Yes. Arthur. My best friend, Arthur. I must not forget Arthur. He is, I presume, the reason for the pearls?”

Beatrice did not flinch. She possessed the invaluable gift of treating moral crises as matters of mere social etiquette. “Arthur and I were married last spring,” she said, with a gentle dignity. “We felt it was the only thing to be done. Mama was quite in favour of it. She said it was unseemly for me to go on wearing mourning for a man who was not even a relative.”

“And so you transferred your affections to Arthur?” Claude asked, leaning back in his chair. “Was the transition very painful? Or did it resemble the changing of a drawing-room curtain—merely a matter of selecting a new colour?”

“You are unkind, Claude,” she murmured, though without the slightest accent of reproach. “I was heartbroken. I genuinely believed you were at the bottom of the sea. Arthur was so consoling. He came to me every day during the terrible week after the news. He brought white lilies. He has an instinct for flowers. He sat with me, and he said nothing. It was exactly what I required. A man who says nothing in a crisis is a perfect gentleman. And then, one afternoon, the rain cleared, and he asked me to be his wife. I looked at him, and I saw that he was extremely handsome, and that his estates in Hertfordshire were quite unencumbered. I felt that Providence had spoken.”

“It is a charming trait in Providence,” observed Claude, “that it invariably speaks in the accent of the British aristocracy. But tell me, Beatrice, did you truly believe me dead? Or did you merely grow tired of waiting for a ghost?”

Beatrice looked at him. A faint colour touched her cheeks, not of shame, but of a delicate, artistic annoyance. She adjusted a pearl bracelet with slow, precise fingers.

“You have become dreadfully cynical, Claude,” she said. “The colonies must have ruined your manners. Of course I believed you dead. Why else would I have married Arthur? I am not a woman who changes her affections lightly. I change my gowns, my gloves, my reading matter, but my affections? Never. They are far too precious to be changed. They are merely re-directed.”

“Re-directed,” he repeated, tasting the word. “It is a masterpiece of phrasing. It has all the grace of a minuet and all the morality of a billiard ball. You believed me dead. That is one explanation. The other is that you grew bored. The period of mourning, I suppose, lacked entertainment. Black, after all, is only becoming to brunettes. You are essentially a blonde. You required a more colourful horizon.”

“If you mean to imply that I was weary of grieving, perhaps you are right,” she answered, with a crystalline candour. “Grief is so dreadfully monotonous. It has no variations. One cannot play Chopin etudes forever. One longs for a polka. Arthur was the polka. He was so bright, so exquisitely untouched by tragedy. He made me laugh. Laughter, after tears, is like a glass of champagne after a glass of ditch-water. It is a necessity.”

Claude rose from his chair. He walked over to the window and looked out at the square. The trees were bare, skeleton-like, and the fog was thickening, turning the gas-lamps into hazy, golden blurs. He felt a curious, detached amusement. He had crossed the world to find a tragedy, and he had found only a comedy of manners. It was, he thought, vastly more amusing.

“You know, Beatrice,” he said, turning back to her, “I ought to be furious. I ought to make a scene. I ought to curse you, and curse Arthur, and throw those magnificent roses into the fire. But I find that I cannot. I am too grateful.”

“Grateful?” she echoed, raising her eyebrows in genuine surprise.

“Yes. To discover that you believed me dead is flattering. It suggests a loyalty so profound that only the grave could terminate it. To discover, on the other hand, that you simply grew bored and married Arthur because he brought you lilies and possessed an unencumbered estate, is equally flattering. It suggests that I was a passion so overwhelming that ordinary life, without me, was entirely insupportable, and you were forced to seek any refuge, however humble, to escape the void.”

He paused, smiling at her with a tranquil, ironic charm.

“Both explanations,” he concluded, “are a tribute to my memory. I accept them. Indeed, I prefer them. A woman who weeps forever is a tedious companion. A woman who weeps until she sees a handsome man with a large income is a woman of taste and practicality. I congratulate you on your taste, Beatrice. And I congratulate Arthur on his income.”

She looked at him for a moment, her eyes wide, a faint, bewildered smile hovering on her lips. Then she laughed, a low, rippling laugh of genuine amusement. She understood him perfectly. She had always understood him. It was the one thing she had never found tedious.

“You are quite impossible,” she said. “You are worse than you were before you went away. You used to be merely sentimental. Now you are diabolical. Arthur will be delighted to see you. He is playing billiards at the club, but he returns at seven. You will stay for dinner?”

“I think not,” said Claude, taking up his hat. “I have an appointment with my tailor. A man who has worn corduroys for three years must approach his tailor with a humble and contrite heart. Besides, I find that I am suddenly very fond of sheep. I believe I shall return to them. They have an intellect that is much misunderstood. They never marry their best friends.”

He bowed, a trifle mockingly, a trifle tenderly. “Good-bye, Beatrice. Keep the pearls. They suit you. They have exactly the same cold, flawless perfection that you have. Only, do not let Arthur wear black for me. It would not be becoming. He has not the complexion for it.”

He left her standing by the fire, a beautiful, perplexed figure, the white violets scenting the air about her. As he walked down the stairs, he reflected that the only truly tragic thing in life is that one cannot be in two places at once. He had wished to be in London, and he was in London. He had wished to be in Australia, and he was not. It was the final, unforgivable flaw in an otherwise perfect universe. He stepped into the fog, and the night swallowed him up.
