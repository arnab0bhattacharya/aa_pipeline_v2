The *SS Morningstar* had been a bulk freighter in another life, hauling grain and timber across the North Sea. Now she rode low in the water, her hull streaked with rust and worse, her decks crowded with the huddled shapes of the living. She had anchored in the roads off Farrowgate at dusk, a grey silhouette against a darker grey sky, and the town had watched her come.

Dr. Elara Vance stood on the shingle of the town beach, the wind whipping her coat around her legs. She was thirty-two, the youngest physician Farrowgate had ever employed, and she had been in the town for less than a year. She had come from London, from a hospital that had been overwhelmed and then abandoned, and she had thought, foolishly perhaps, that a smaller place would be simpler. The sea was the colour of lead. The *Morningstar*'s single working engine coughed and fell silent. She could see figures along the rail, small and dark, and she could imagine their faces, though she could not see them.

Behind her, the town was gathering. Farrowgate was a town of four thousand souls, built around a harbour that had once been busy with fishing boats and now hosted a few pleasure craft and the occasional coastguard cutter. The council chamber was in the old town hall, a Georgian building with a clock tower that had stopped during the last great storm and never been repaired. By eight o'clock, the chamber was full.

Councillor Harold Finch, a retired solicitor with a face like a crumpled paper bag, sat at the head of the table. He had been mayor for eleven years, and he had never faced anything like this. The harbourmaster, a thin man named Pritchard, had brought the news: the ship had radioed for permission to dock, citing a medical emergency, a shortage of water, and a hundred and forty souls aboard, mostly from the drowned coasts of what had been Belgium and the Netherlands. The continent had been stricken for a decade, the seas rising, the crops failing, the cities burning. The refugees had been turned away from three other ports already.

"We can't let them land," said Councillor Miriam Shaw, a woman with sharp eyes and a sharper tongue. "We have no facilities. No hospital beds. No quarantine. We have a responsibility to our own people first."

"They're dying," said Elara. She had been invited to attend as an observer, a medical advisor, but she had not been asked to speak. She spoke anyway. "I saw them through the glass. They have children with them. They have a woman who appears to be in labour. They have no water."

"And if they bring disease?" said Shaw. "We have a hundred elderly residents in this town. We have a school with three hundred children. We have no way to test them, no way to isolate them. You're a doctor, Dr. Vance. You know what happens when infection spreads in a confined population."

"I know what happens when we refuse to help," said Elara. "I know that the plague that swept through the refugee camps on the continent last year was cholera, and cholera is treatable with clean water and salt. I know that the fever they call the grey cough is not airborne in the way we feared; it requires close contact. I know that if we leave them on that ship for another three days, we will be responsible for deaths that could have been prevented."

The chamber was silent. Finch rubbed his eyes. "We have protocols," he said. "The county council has issued guidance. Ports are to remain closed to unscheduled arrivals until further notice."

"The guidance is a piece of paper," said Elara. "It was written by people who have never stood on a beach and watched a ship full of human beings beg for help."

"You're young," said Shaw. "You haven't seen what I've seen. In the first wave, before you came here, we had a boat come in from France. They said they were clean. They weren't. We lost forty people that winter. Forty people in a town of four thousand. I buried my own mother."

Elara met her gaze. "I'm sorry," she said. "I am. But I've also seen what happens when we treat every stranger as a threat. I worked in a camp outside Calais for six months. I saw people die of treatable infections because no one would touch them. I saw a mother hand her baby over a fence because she knew the child would have a better chance on the other side. The fence was electrified. She didn't care. She just wanted the baby to live."

The council debated for hours. They discussed quarantine measures, the possibility of anchoring the ship offshore and ferrying supplies, the legal implications of refusing entry, the moral implications of accepting it. Pritchard pointed out that the harbour was too shallow for the *Morningstar* to dock directly; they would need to use the lifeboats. Finch pointed out that the town had a small clinic, two nurses, and Elara. Shaw pointed out that the nearest hospital was thirty miles away, and the roads were flooded.

At midnight, a coastguard report came in: the *Morningstar* had launched a small boat. It was heading for the harbour. The council dispatched Pritchard and a constable to the pier. Elara went with them.

The boat was a fibreglass dinghy, overcrowded, with a single outboard motor that sputtered and died twenty yards from the pier. The constable, a young man named Davies, waded into the water and pulled it in. There were six people in the boat: a man with a gash across his forehead, a woman clutching a bundle that turned out to be a sleeping infant, two teenage boys, and an elderly woman who was barely conscious. The man with the gash spoke in broken English. He said his name was Pieter. He said the woman with the baby was his sister. He said the elderly woman was his mother. He said there were others on the ship who were worse off.

Elara knelt beside the elderly woman. Her pulse was thready, her skin cold and clammy. Dehydration, probably, and something else, something respiratory. Elara looked up at Pritchard. "She needs fluids," she said. "She needs warmth. She needs a bed."

Pritchard looked at the constable. The constable looked at the town, the dark windows, the silent streets. "The council hasn't decided," Pritchard said.

"The council can decide while I work," said Elara. "I'm a doctor. I have a duty of care. That duty doesn't stop at the edge of the harbour."

She helped Pieter lift his mother out of the boat. The woman weighed almost nothing. Davies took the baby from the sister, who was crying now, silently, the tears running down her face. The teenage boys stood on the pier, shivering, and Pritchard, after a moment, took off his coat and put it around one of them.

By dawn, the council had reached a decision. It was not a unanimous decision, and it was not a generous one. The refugees would be allowed to land, but they would be housed in the old fish market, which had been disused for years, and they would be kept there under guard for a period of fourteen days. They would be tested for infection. They would be given food and water and medical care. They would not be allowed into the town proper. It was a compromise, Finch said, between compassion and caution.

Elara, standing in the fish market with a stethoscope around her neck and a clipboard in her hand, watched the first of the lifeboats come in. The sun was rising over the harbour, pale and weak, and the *Morningstar* sat at anchor, still and silent. Pieter's mother was asleep on a camp bed, her breathing easier. The baby was awake, looking around with wide, dark eyes. The teenage boys were eating bread and drinking tea, their hands still shaking.

It was not enough, Elara thought. It was not nearly enough. But it was something. It was a start. She picked up her clipboard and went to meet the next boat.
