The arrival of the ——shire Militia in the town of Wetherby had produced, as was entirely to be expected, a very general and agreeable agitation among the families of the neighbourhood. It was not merely that the streets were rendered more lively by the sight of scarlet coats, nor that the assemblies, which had before been but thinly attended, were now crowded to an almost inconvenient degree. The true consequence of the officers’ presence lay in the sudden and universal revival of hope in the breasts of every mother who had a daughter to dispose of; and in the no less sudden conviction of those daughters themselves, that a red coat and a tolerable figure were the very essentials of happiness.

Among the officers thus happily quartered at Wetherby, Lieutenant Frederick Ashby was generally allowed to be the most agreeable. He was not, perhaps, strictly handsome; but he had a manner which pleased, and a person which set off his regimentals to advantage. He was, moreover, the younger son of a baronet, a circumstance which, in the eyes of the Wetherby matrons, more than compensated for any deficiency in his features. He was lively, self-assured, and possessed of that happy facility of conversation which enabled him to address every lady in the room with an air of particular attention, without committing himself to any one in particular. He had, in short, all the qualifications necessary to make him the object of universal pursuit, and he enjoyed the distinction with a complacency that was entirely his own.

The Misses Harringtons, the Misses Cole, and the two Miss Prestons had all, in their several ways, exerted themselves to attract his notice; and Mrs. Harrington had gone so far as to invite him to supper three times in the course of a fortnight, a mark of favour which had not been bestowed on any other officer in the regiment. But Lieutenant Ashby, though he received these attentions with the utmost good humour, and returned them with a gallantry that was very becoming, had not yet shown any disposition to attach himself to any one of the young ladies who were so liberally set before him.

There was, indeed, one young woman in Wetherby who appeared altogether insensible to his merits. Miss Eleanor Whitmore was the daughter of a clergyman, a man of small fortune but great respectability, who had died some years since, leaving his widow and two daughters to the careful economy of a very narrow income. The elder Miss Whitmore was now the wife of a neighbouring farmer, and Eleanor, the younger, lived with her mother in a small house at the end of the High Street, where they kept no carriage, gave no suppers, and were, in consequence, considered by Mrs. Harrington and her friends as persons whom it was hardly necessary to notice.

It was this very indifference, however, which first drew the attention of Lieutenant Ashby. He had been introduced to Miss Whitmore at the assembly, and had been struck by the composure with which she received his compliments. She had not blushed, nor simpered, nor looked down, nor done any of those things which he had been accustomed to expect from young ladies on such occasions. She had merely answered him with civility, and had then turned to speak to her mother. He had watched her for the rest of the evening, and had observed that she danced only with a young man in a plain coat, whom he afterwards learned to be a Mr. Edward Ferrers, the son of a neighbouring squire, and that she seemed perfectly content to sit out the remaining dances in conversation with her mother.

“I cannot conceive,” said Lieutenant Ashby to his friend Captain Wentworth, as they walked home together, “what there is about Miss Whitmore that should make her so particular. She is not handsome—at least, not remarkably so. She has no fortune, I am told, and no connexions worth speaking of. And yet she carries herself as if she were a duchess.”

“Perhaps,” replied the captain, who was a man of more observation than his friend, “she is not conscious of any reason to be otherwise.”

“Nonsense,” said Ashby. “Every woman is conscious of her own value, and expects to be admired. I have never yet met with one who did not.”

“You have never yet met with Miss Whitmore,” said the captain, drily.

The lieutenant was not a man to be discouraged by a rebuff. He determined to pay his addresses to Miss Whitmore with the same assiduity which had succeeded so well with the other ladies of Wetherby, and to see whether a little perseverance might not produce the same effect. He accordingly called at the house in the High Street the very next morning, on the pretext of inquiring after Mrs. Whitmore’s health, and was received by that lady with a politeness which was perfectly civil, but which had in it nothing of the flutter of gratified vanity. Miss Whitmore was sitting at the window, employed in some needlework, and she rose to receive him with an ease that was almost provoking.

“I hope I do not interrupt you, ma’am,” said he, with his most engaging smile.

“Not at all, sir,” replied Eleanor. “My mother and I were just speaking of the weather. It is very fine for the season.”

“It is indeed,” said he, seating himself without ceremony. “I have been walking on the parade, and the air is quite delightful. I am sure you must find it so, Miss Whitmore. You are fond of walking, I dare say.”

“I am fond of walking, sir, when I have an object in view,” she replied. “But I cannot say that I walk for the mere pleasure of being seen.”

There was a something in this speech which the lieutenant did not quite understand. He was not accustomed to be answered with so much point, and he was not sure whether he was pleased or offended. He resolved, however, to take it as a compliment, and to proceed in his usual strain.

“You are severe, Miss Whitmore,” said he. “I am sure you would not deny that a walk in good company is one of the pleasantest things in the world. I have often thought that a pleasant companion is the best part of a fine day.”

“I agree with you entirely, sir,” said Eleanor. “But I think a pleasant companion is not always to be found in a red coat.”

Mrs. Whitmore here interposed, with a remark on the price of bread, and the conversation became general. But the lieutenant went away more perplexed than ever. He had not been used to such treatment. He had expected to be received with smiles and blushes, and he had been met with a composure that was almost contemptuous. He could not understand why his charm should fail with this one young woman, when it had succeeded so well with all the others.

He repeated his visits, and he repeated his compliments, but with no better success. Miss Whitmore was always civil, always composed, and always, in some way which he could not exactly define, perfectly indifferent. She did not avoid him, but she did not seek him. She listened to his conversation with attention, but she did not seem to be impressed by it. She was, in short, the only young woman in Wetherby who did not appear to think him a person of consequence, and the discovery was one which he found it very difficult to forgive.

It was not long before the whole town had taken notice of his attentions. Mrs. Harrington was of opinion that Miss Whitmore was a very artful young woman, and that her indifference was all a pretence. The Misses Cole were of opinion that she was a very odd creature, and that she did not know what was good for her. The two Miss Prestons were of opinion that she was proud, and that she gave herself airs. But Mr. Edward Ferrers, who had known Eleanor from childhood, and who had long been attached to her, was of opinion that she was the most sensible woman in the county, and that Lieutenant Ashby was a fool.

The event justified his opinion. Lieutenant Ashby, after a perseverance of some weeks, was obliged to confess that he had made no impression whatever on the heart of Miss Whitmore. He had, indeed, made a considerable impression on the hearts of several other young ladies, who were ready to console themselves with the reflection that he had never been really attached to Miss Whitmore, and that she had always been a very disagreeable sort of person. But the lieutenant himself was not so easily satisfied. He had been accustomed to conquest, and he could not endure defeat.

“I cannot conceive,” said he to Captain Wentworth, as they sat together at the mess, “what there is about that woman that should make her so difficult to please. I have done everything in my power to recommend myself to her. I have been attentive, I have been civil, I have been—I may say—assiduous. And yet she treats me as if I were a common acquaintance.”

“Perhaps,” said the captain, “she does not think you worth the trouble of being pleased with.”

“That is very uncivil,” said the lieutenant, colouring.

“It is very true,” said the captain. “You have been used to being admired, Frederick, and you cannot bear to be neglected. But Miss Whitmore is not a fool, and she sees through you. She knows that you have no more real regard for her than you have for the Miss Prestons, and she will not give you the satisfaction of thinking that you have made her unhappy.”

“I never wished to make her unhappy,” said the lieutenant, somewhat sulkily.

“No, but you wished to make her admire you, and you cannot forgive her for not doing it. That is the whole secret of your vexation.”

The lieutenant was silent. He was not convinced, but he was not prepared to argue the point. He had, indeed, a vague suspicion that the captain was right, and the suspicion was not agreeable to him. He had never before met with a woman who did not think him irresistible, and he had never before been obliged to consider whether he was really so. He was not a bad-hearted young man, but he was a vain one, and vanity is not easily reconciled to defeat.

Miss Whitmore, in the meantime, was perfectly unconscious of the agitation she had occasioned. She had never regarded Lieutenant Ashby as anything more than a very common young man, with a very good opinion of himself, and she had treated him with the civility which was due to any acquaintance, without the slightest idea that she was doing anything extraordinary. She was not insensible to the merits of a red coat, but she was not disposed to think that a red coat was a substitute for good sense, or that a gay manner was a guarantee of a good heart. She had seen too much of the world, even in her quiet retirement, to be dazzled by the first appearance of gallantry, and she had a very just estimation of her own value, which was not to be lowered by the attentions of a man who had no real regard for her.

The militia at length removed from Wetherby to another quarter, and Lieutenant Ashby departed with the rest. He left behind him a great many broken hearts, and a great many promises of correspondence, which were not very strictly kept. He did not leave behind him any very deep impression on the heart of Miss Whitmore, who, in the course of a twelvemonth, was married to Mr. Edward Ferrers, and settled in the neighbourhood, where she lived in great happiness, and was never heard to express any regret that she had not been more favourably disposed towards the handsome lieutenant.
