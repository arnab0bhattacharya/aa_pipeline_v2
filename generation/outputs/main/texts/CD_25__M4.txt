The works of Messrs. Grimsby and Groat, Soap Boilers and Tallow Chandlers, stood at the muddy verge of Battersea Reach, where the river, at low tide, exposed a broad and slimy shore that seemed to have been laid down expressly for the convenience of cats, old boots, and despair. It was a place of perpetual steam and perpetual smell; a smell that was not precisely fish, nor yet precisely fat, but a rich and clinging compound of the two, which insinuated itself into the very wool of a boy’s jacket and the very lining of his soul. Here, in the great, gloomy, echoing shed, with its vats of bubbling grease and its reek of soda and rosin, a boy named Edmund Vane—known to all upon the premises simply as Ned—found himself, at the age of twelve, employed in the turning of a great wheel, from which position he could watch the sluggish river and the barges and the chimneys of the distant town, and dream.

For Ned was a dreamer, and his dreams were of a magnificent and sustaining nature. His father, a small chandler in a small way in a small street, had been visited by that sudden and complete ruin which, in those days, seemed to lie in wait for honest men at the corner of every ledger. The shop was gone, the stock was gone, the very chairs and tables were gone, and Ned’s father, a broken man, had taken a clerk’s position in a distant counting-house, while Ned was sent to the soap boilery to earn his bread. But Ned, being of a proud and romantic turn, could not endure to be the son of a bankrupt chandler; it was a fact too mean, too sordid, too much in keeping with the tallow and the steam. And so, in the long, monotonous hours by the great wheel, he had invented for himself a father of a very different stamp.

“My father,” he would say to the other boys, in the dinner-hour, when they sat upon the steps of the drying-shed, gnawing their bread and cheese, “my father was a sea-captain. Captain Vane, of the brig *Aurora*—a fine, tall man, with a face like a mahogany table, and a voice like a speaking-trumpet. He was lost at sea, my father was, in the great gale off the Cape. The ship went down with all hands, and my father, he stood upon the quarter-deck, giving his orders as calm as a Sunday morning, and the last thing they heard of him was his voice, a-calling out, ‘Steady, my lads, steady!’ through the roaring of the wind.”

The boys listened with open mouths, and the men, too, would sometimes pause in their labour to hear the tale; for there is nothing a working-man loves so well as a story of the sea, and nothing so fine as a gentleman who has come down in the world. Ned told it so often, and with such a wealth of detail—the brig’s cargo of indigo and spices, the captain’s silver-mounted pistols, the parrot that swore in Portuguese—that he began to believe it himself. He would lie awake at night in his little garret and see the *Aurora* plunging through the black water, and hear his father’s voice above the storm; and his eyes would fill with tears, not for the father he had lost, but for the father he had invented. It was a beautiful grief, and he hugged it to his heart.

Now, at the corner of the street, a little way from the works, there stood a public-house called the *Three Bells*, much frequented by watermen and sailors, and here, of an evening, the men of the soap boilery would repair to wash the taste of soda out of their mouths. And here, too, came an old sailor named Ben Bracegirdle, a man with a face like a worn-out rope, and a pair of eyes that had looked at so many horizons they seemed to have contracted a permanent squint from the effort. He had a wooden leg, a tarpaulin hat, and a habit of sucking at a short black pipe, and he was known to all the riverside as a man who had been everywhere and seen everything, and who could tell you the price of a cargo of rum at Rio or the colour of the mud at the mouth of the Amazon.

One evening, when Ned was sent to the *Three Bells* with a message for one of the foremen, the old sailor, who was sitting by the fire with his pipe, looked up and fixed him with that squinting eye.

“Here, boy,” said he, “you’re the young ‘un from the soap works, ain’t you? The one as has the sea-captain for a father?”

Ned, with a proud heart, said that he was.

“And what was his name, my lad?” said Ben Bracegirdle, taking his pipe from his mouth and leaning forward with an appearance of great interest. “What was the captain’s name?”

The question fell upon Ned’s ear like a bucket of cold water. For a moment, his invention, which had never been thus put to the test, seemed to fail him utterly. He had told the story so often, but he had never been asked for the name. He had called his father Captain Vane, but that was his own name, and it sounded too plain, too much like the chandler’s son. He stood there, with the eyes of the old sailor upon him, and the eyes of the landlord, and the eyes of a couple of watermen who had turned round to listen, and he felt the whole fabric of the *Aurora* and the gale off the Cape and the voice like a speaking-trumpet trembling to its fall.

“His name,” said Ned, with a desperate effort to keep his voice steady, “was Captain—Captain—Mortimer.”

“Mortimer?” said the old sailor, slowly. “Mortimer? I knowed a Mortimer once—Captain Mortimer, of the *Mary Anne*, trading to the Levant. But he warn’t lost at sea. He was drowned in a dock at Wapping, a-falling off a ladder. Was your father a tall man, my lad, with a cast in his eye?”

“No,” said Ned, “he was a very tall man, with a face like a mahogany table, and a voice like a speaking-trumpet.”

“Ah,” said Ben Bracegirdle, “then it warn’t him. But Mortimer’s a common name enough. What was the name of the brig, my lad?”

“The *Aurora*,” said Ned, “of London.”

“The *Aurora*,” repeated the sailor, and he sucked at his pipe with a meditative air. “I knowed an *Aurora* once, a brig, in the fruit trade, from the Azores. She was lost, she was, in the Bay of Biscay, with all hands, in the year ‘twenty-eight. But her captain’s name warn’t Mortimer. It was—it was—why, what was it? It was Captain—Captain—Trelawney. Yes, that was it. Trelawney. A little man, with a red face and a squeaky voice. Warn’t your father, was he?”

“No,” said Ned, “my father was a very tall man, with a face like a mahogany table.”

“Well,” said the old sailor, “it’s a strange thing, but I don’t seem to remember a Captain Mortimer, of the *Aurora*, of London. And I’ve been in the trade, man and boy, for fifty year. But there, it’s a big ocean, and a many ships goes down as never comes up again. What was the cargo, my lad?”

“Indigo,” said Ned, “and spices.”

“Indigo and spices,” said Ben Bracegirdle. “From the Indies, then?”

“From the Indies,” said Ned.

“And the gale off the Cape,” said the sailor, “was it the gale of ‘twenty-nine?”

“It was,” said Ned.

“Ah,” said the sailor, “I was in the gale of ‘twenty-nine, and I don’t remember no *Aurora* being lost. But there, I was in the *Betsy Jane*, and we was all so busy saving our own lives we hadn’t no time to look after other people’s. Well, my lad, here’s your good health, and your father’s, too, though I don’t remember him.”

And he took a pull at his beer, and Ned, with a burning face, went back to the soap boilery. But from that night, the story of the *Aurora* and Captain Mortimer began to lose its savour. The boys asked him questions he could not answer, and the men looked at him with a knowing eye. And Ned, as he turned his great wheel, would look out at the river and the barges, and think of the real Captain Vane, the chandler, who was not lost at sea at all, but was sitting on a high stool in a distant counting-house, and who, if he had no mahogany face, had at least an honest heart. And the tears that came into his eyes were for a father who was alive, and who was his own, and who had never been upon the quarter-deck of any *Aurora* whatsoever.
