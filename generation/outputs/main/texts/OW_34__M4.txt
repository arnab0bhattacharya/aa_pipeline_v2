The first thing that anyone said of Julian Vane—and it was said so often, and with such an air of pleasant finality, that it had come to seem less an observation than a christening—was that he was the very picture of his poor father. Lady Vane herself said it, in the modulated voice of one who has learned to make grief graceful, and she would touch the boy’s hair with a hand that was all rings and resignation, and add that it was a comfort, a great comfort, though her eyes, as they travelled over the young man’s features, wore the peculiar fixedness of a painter examining a copy of a picture he had never much admired. The world, which is always charmed to agree with a beautiful woman about anything, agreed with her about this, and so Julian grew up in the reflected glory of a man he could not remember, a man of whom there remained in the house only a portrait by a fashionable hand, and a reputation for a certain careless charm that had made him beloved at White’s and forgiven everywhere.

The portrait hung in the morning-room, and Julian, who had a turn for the fine shades of things, had spent a good many of his idle hours before it. It represented a slender gentleman in a green coat, leaning against a pillar with the air of one who has just said something clever and is waiting, with perfect confidence, for the laugh. The face was frank and rather beautiful, with the wide, untroubled eyes of a man who has never been obliged to think. Julian, studying it, had always felt a little flattered and a little sad; it was like looking into a mirror that flattered and remembered.

It was in his twenty-fourth year, and in the second week of a June that had decked London out in its most expensive colours, that the mirror cracked. He had been rummaging—for he was of an inquisitive temper, and the rain had kept him indoors—in the drawers of the old bureau in the library, and had come upon a bundle of letters tied with a ribbon that had once been pink. They were his father’s letters, and they were not to his mother. They were to a woman whose name he did not know, and they were written in a hand that shook with feeling, and they spoke of a love that was not the calm, well-bred affection of a married man, but a wild, secret, breathless thing, full of rendezvous and concealment and the fear of discovery. The last of them, dated a month before his own birth, contained a sentence that struck him with the coldness of a douche: ‘She suspects—I am sure she suspects—and if she knows, she will never forgive; but I cannot give you up, not though the heavens fell.’

Julian sat for a long time with the letter in his hand. The heavens, it appeared, had not fallen; they had merely arranged themselves into a very proper blue, and his mother had gone on being a great lady, and he had gone on being the image of his father. He looked up at the portrait, and it seemed to him that the green-coated gentleman had grown suddenly embarrassed, and that the clever thing he had just said had been, after all, in rather poor taste.

He went to his mother that very afternoon. She was in her boudoir, among her china and her ferns, a woman of fifty who had the art of looking ten years younger than her sorrows, and she received him with the gentle irony that was her substitute for conversation.

‘My dear Julian,’ she said, when he had told her what he had found, and had asked her, with a directness that surprised himself, whether the letters were true, ‘you have been reading old papers, which is a habit of the ill-regulated and the under-bred. Your father was a man of feeling, and men of feeling write a great deal of nonsense. I beg you will not make a scene; scenes are so fatiguing, and the carpet is new.’

‘But, mother,’ said Julian, ‘the letters speak of a woman—’

‘The letters,’ she said, arranging a spray of maidenhair with a hand that did not tremble, ‘speak of a great many things, and none of them, I fancy, of the slightest consequence to you or to me. Your father was devoted to me; the world knew it; I knew it. If he chose to write to some—person—in a strain of romantic exaggeration, it is not for his son to publish the fact. I have burned a great many of his letters; I regret that I did not burn these.’

‘Then you know who she was?’

Lady Vane looked at him for a moment with an expression that was almost one of pity, and then she smiled her slow, fine, terrible smile.

‘I know nothing,’ she said, ‘except that you are my son, and that you are exceedingly like your father, and that I have a headache. Ring for my maid, like a good boy, and go and dine somewhere. There is a new chef at the Marlborough, and I am told he makes a soufflé that is almost a religious experience.’

But Julian did not dine. He went out into the streets, which were full of the scent of lilac and the rustle of silk, and he walked until the lamps were lit, and he asked himself, with the fatuity of the young, whether he had ever known anything at all. The world, which had been so solid, had become a series of drawing-rooms, and behind the drawing-rooms there were other rooms, and behind those, perhaps, other rooms still, and in none of them, it seemed, was there any truth that a man might hold in his hand.

His search, which was to last through the season, took him into every drawing-room in London that had a memory and a secret. He began with his godmother, the Countess of Merrow, who had known his father at Brighton in the year of the Exhibition, and who received him among her fans and her photographs with a cordiality that was a little too bright.

‘Your father, my dear Julian,’ she said, ‘was the most charming man in England, and the most faithful. He was devoted to your mother—devoted. There was never a breath—never. Of course, there was that foolish business with the little singer—but that was nothing, absolutely nothing; a man of the world, you know, must have his little distractions. She died, I think, in Naples. Or was it Nice? It was somewhere with an N.’

‘And her name?’

‘My dear boy, how should I remember? I never remember the names of people who are not in the peerage. It was something Italian, I fancy—or perhaps it was not. Have you seen the new dancer at the Alhambra? She is quite divine, and so respectable that it is almost a scandal.’

From the Countess he went to old Lord Ambermere, who had been his father’s friend, and who sat in his library among his books, which he had never read, and his wine, which he had, and who became, at the first question, so profoundly and elaborately deaf that Julian was obliged to shout his inquiries, and received, for his pains, a lecture on the impiety of raking up old stories, delivered with a fluency that suggested long practice.

It was at the house of Mrs. Delamere, a lady of exquisite taste and no reputation, that he at last found a woman who was willing to be indiscreet. She was standing by the fireplace, in a gown of peacock-blue, with a great black fan in her hand, and she listened to him with a smile that was not unkind.

‘You are looking for the truth about your father,’ she said. ‘How charmingly old-fashioned! Nobody looks for the truth nowadays; they look for a good cook and a bad conscience, and they generally find both. But I will tell you what I know, because I am the only person in London who is not afraid of your mother. Your father was not the man they pretend. He was weak, and he was lovely, and he was unhappy, which is the worst of the three. The woman in the letters was a Miss Erskine—a governess, my dear, a governess with red hair and a great deal of feeling—and she died, as the Countess told you, in Naples, of a broken heart, or a fever, or perhaps of both. And the child—’

‘The child?’ said Julian, and his voice was strange in his own ears.

‘The child,’ said Mrs. Delamere, closing her fan with a little snap, ‘was a boy, and he was brought up by his mother’s people in the north, and he is now, I am told, a very respectable young man in the City, with a wife and a house at Streatham. You have his eyes, you know—the wide, untroubled ones. It is the only thing your father gave you, and it is the only thing he gave him. I should not tell your mother that I told you. She is a great lady, and great ladies do not like to be reminded that their husbands were men. And now, my dear, you must go, for I have a hundred people coming to dinner, and I have not yet decided which of them I shall cut.’

Julian went out into the twilight of the park, where the lamps were beginning to shine like yellow flowers among the leaves, and he walked for a long time, and he thought of the green-coated gentleman in the morning-room, and of the governess with red hair, and of the respectable young man at Streatham, and of his mother, who had known everything and had said nothing, and had gone on being a great lady with a new carpet. And he understood, at last, that the world is not a mirror but a masquerade, and that the only truth is the mask that is worn longest.
