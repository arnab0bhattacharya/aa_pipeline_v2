The rain had beat against the long windows of the counting-house since dawn, and now at four o’clock it ceased suddenly, as if a hand had been withdrawn from a drum, leaving only the sound of water dripping from the eaves and the sullen roar of the beck swollen brown and furious in the valley below. I stood at the glass and looked out upon the mill-yard, where the hands were filing through the gate—men with their caps pulled low, women with shawls drawn tight about their heads, and the little children, the piecers and scavengers, dragging their feet as though the very mud clung to them in pity. My father sat at his desk behind me, and I heard the scratch of his pen as he struck out one figure and wrote another in its place.

“You are early at your ledgers this morning, sir,” I said, for I could not keep silence, though I knew the temper in which he sat.

“Orders fail,” he answered, not lifting his eyes. “The Americans will not buy, and the French have their own looms now. I must either close the mill or bring down the wages. I have brought them down.”

“By how much?”

“A third.”

I turned from the window. “A third! Father, they cannot live on two-thirds of what was already starvation’s pittance. I have seen the children in the yard, and their faces are the colour of the clay. There is a woman—Hannah Thwaite—her husband broke his arm at the loom, and she has four bairns and nothing but what she earns at the carding. A third less will kill them.”

He looked up then, and his face was grey and hard as the millstone. “And if I do not bring them down, the mill closes, and they are all turned off together, and the village starves in earnest. Do you think I take pleasure in it, Catherine? I have built this mill from a wheel and a shed. I have fed these people for twenty years. But I cannot feed them on air, and the banks will not take my notes for their bread.”

I knew the truth of it; I knew it as I knew the rain upon the glass, and yet the truth did not warm me. I went out into the yard, and the wet wind took my hair and whipped it across my eyes, and I stood there like a fool, with my hands empty at my sides.

It was there that Ralph Ferrand found me—Ralph, the son of the banker at Stanbury, who had ridden over on some errand of his father’s, and who never failed to find an excuse to linger at the mill. He was a handsome man, and he knew it; he had a fine colour and a fine coat, and he dismounted with the air of one conferring a favour.

“Miss Hargrave! You will catch your death in this damp. Come within, I beg you.”

“I am not afraid of the damp,” I said. “I am thinking of the people who have no dry roof to go to.”

He glanced at the retreating backs of the workers with a mild and tolerant eye. “Ah, the hands. My father says your father has done the only prudent thing. Wages must follow the market, you know. It is a law of nature.”

“It is a law of nature that men should starve, then?”

“It is a law of nature that the fittest survive,” he said, smiling, and offered me his arm. “You are too tender-hearted, Miss Hargrave. It does you credit as a woman, but you must not let it spoil your complexion. Come, I have brought you a book from Leeds—a volume of Mrs. Radcliffe’s, which I am told is all the rage.”

I looked at his arm, and at his pleasant, vacant face, and something rose in me that was near to hatred. “Mr. Ferrand,” I said, “there is a child in that cottage yonder who has eaten nothing but water-gruel these three days. Can your Mrs. Radcliffe give her a dinner?”

He laughed, as at a jest, and drew back his arm. “You are severe, Miss Hargrave. But I like you the better for it. You have spirit.”

“I have no spirit,” I answered. “I have only eyes.”

He rode away at last, and I went in and up to my chamber, and sat down on the edge of the bed with my bonnet still on, and my heart beating thick and hot against my ribs. For I had no money. That was the bitter core of it. My father was not a cruel man, but he was a close one; he gave me board and clothes and a small allowance for ribbons, and he kept the purse-strings in his own fist, and he would not hear of my touching the capital that was settled on me at my mother’s death. “You will marry, Catherine,” he said, “and your husband will manage it.” And so I sat in my father’s house, a woman grown, with a woman’s will and a woman’s pity, and not a penny in the world that I could call my own to put bread into the mouth of a dying child.

The next day I went down to the village. The rain had gone off, and the sun shone cold and bright on the wet slate roofs, and the beck was still high and loud. Hannah Thwaite’s cottage stood at the end of the row, and I knocked and went in, and the smell of it was damp and close—a smell of wet ashes and unwashed linen. She was sitting by the hearth with a babe at her breast, and the fire was little more than a handful of sticks, and the two eldest children sat on the floor picking over a heap of coal-dust to find the cinders.

“Miss Catherine!” she said, and made to rise, and I put my hand on her shoulder and pressed her down.

“Do not get up. I have brought you a little tea and sugar, and a loaf. It is not much.”

“It is a great deal,” she said, and her eyes filled. “The Lord will reward you.”

“The Lord has nothing to do with it,” I said, and my voice was sharper than I meant. “It is my father who has taken your wages, and it is my father who should make it good.”

She looked frightened then, and glanced at the door. “Hush, miss. He is a hard man, but he is not a bad one. He gave my husband work when no one else would.”

“He has given you a third less this week,” I said. “How will you live?”

She was silent, and the babe cried, and she put it to her breast again, and the tears ran down her cheeks and fell upon the child’s head. I stood there and looked at her, and I felt the shame of my own warm cloak and my own full stomach, and I felt the rage of my own helplessness, and I knew that if I stayed a moment longer I should weep too, and that would be of no use to her.

I went out and walked up the lane towards the mill, and the wind came down off the moor and smelled of peat and heather and the far-off clean places of the world. I thought of Ralph Ferrand and his fine coat, and of the other one, Thomas Ashworth, who had come courting in the spring with a gig and a pair of greys, and who had told me that a woman’s place was in the parlour, and that I should leave the mill and the village to the men. I thought of my father at his desk, striking out figures. And I thought of Hannah Thwaite’s face, and the cinders on the floor, and the child’s thin hands.

I stopped in the middle of the lane, and I clenched my fists at my sides, and I spoke aloud, though there was no one to hear me but the wind and the beck.

“I will not marry a man who cannot see a starving child,” I said. “I will not sit in a parlour and be pretty while the village goes hungry. I have no money, but I have a brain and I have a will, and if my father will not give me my own, I will earn it. I will teach. I will write. I will do something. And if the world says a woman cannot, then the world is a liar, and I will prove it so.”

The wind took my words and scattered them, and I stood there a moment longer, trembling, and then I turned and walked back towards the mill, and my step was lighter, and my heart was still fierce, but it was no longer hot and blind. It was cold and clear as the winter sun on the wet roofs. I had no power yet. But I had a purpose, and I had a tongue, and I would use both, and let the devil take the man who stood in my way.
