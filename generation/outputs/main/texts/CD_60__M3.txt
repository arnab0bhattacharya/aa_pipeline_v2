To say that the town of Clinkerbottom, in the riding of Yorkshire, was in a state of profound agitation on that frosty morning in November, eighteen hundred and fifty-something, would be to state the fact with a mildness that would do grievous injustice to the truth. The great mill of Mr. Obadiah Grume—erected, as a carved stone over the principal gate most boastfully informed the beholder, in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and forty—was a mere shell of blackened, smoking brick; a monument to the devouring element which had, in the space of three short hours, swallowed up the livelihood of three hundred human creatures.

The fire had broken out at midnight. How it began, no man could say with certainty; but the flames had found a firm friend in the cotton-dust and the oily machinery, and they had danced their merry, merciless dance until the very river seemed to boil, and the skies above Clinkerbottom glowed a fierce, unnatural orange, as if the heavens themselves had been heated in a colossal crucible. And now, beneath a cold and cheerless dawn, the ruins smoked and hissed, sending up little spiteful jets of steam that took the shapes, to a fanciful imagination, of hungry spectres mocking the ruin they surveyed.

If Clinkerbottom was in agitation, the High Street was the very focus and centre of the commotion. It was thronged with pale-faced men in fustian jackets, and haggard women in shawls so thin and patched that they seemed to have been woven by the spiders of despair. They clustered round the gates of the blackened yard, looking through the iron bars with hollow eyes, as if they expected the skeleton of the mill to rise up and offer them their daily bread. But the skeleton offered nothing save the smell of wet ashes, and the melancholy dripping of water from broken beams, which the engines had played upon the conflagration too late to be of any earthly use.

Out of this crowd, pressing his way with a sturdy, determined tread, emerged Mr. Josiah Mudge, the head-overseer of the late establishment. Mr. Mudge was a man of fifty, or thereabouts; broad-shouldered, square-jawed, with a pair of shrewd gray eyes that looked out from beneath two bushy eyebrows like two glimmering lights from under two penthouses. He had been a faithful servant to the Grume dynasty for twenty years, and he possessed that strict, unyielding adherence to routine which is the glory and the plague of the English overlooker. Under his left arm, pressed closely to his fustian waistcoat, he carried a bulky, charred volume, bound in what had once been calf-skin, but was now a species of scorched parchment. It was the mill’s principal ledger.

“Make way there! Make way, my good people, make way!” cried Mr. Mudge, shoving the crowd gently but firmly aside, with the air of a man who carried the fortunes of the nation in his hat. “What’s the use of standing here catching cold in your chests, and staring at a burnt brick-kiln? It’ll neither give you work nor fill your porridge-pots. Stand back, I say!”

“Oh, Mr. Mudge!” cried a pale woman, catching the skirt of his coat, “is it true the mill is gone clean, and we are all on the parish?”

“Gone clean, Mrs. Slogger, as clean as a Sunday-school boy’s conscience. But I’ve saved the books, I have; and where the books are, there’s the master’s property, and where the property is, there’s the law, and where the law is, there’s a way out of the workhouse—or I’m a Dutchman, which I am not.”

Mr. Mudge, having thus delivered himself of a string of propositions that were more remarkable for their sound than their logic, marched sturdily up the High Street, and turned into the parlour of the Black Ram, where he knew Mr. Grume was to be found.

That gentleman was seated before a roaring fire, with a glass of hot brandy-and-water on the table beside him, and a countenance which, if one might judge by its expression, was not utterly inconsolable. Mr. Obadiah Grume was a large, pale, flabby man, with a double chin that overlapped his cravat, and small, twinkling eyes that seemed perpetually engaged in a secret calculation of pounds, shillings, and pence. He possessed a singular talent for looking unhappy when he was perfectly satisfied, and for appearing charitable when he was meditating an act of peculiar self-interest.

“Ah, Mudge!” said Mr. Grume, in a thin, reedy voice, which contrasted oddly with his large person. “You have come. I knew you would. You are a faithful creature, Mudge. It does my heart good to see you, Mudge. A sad calamity, Mudge. A most melancholy visitation!”

“Very sad, sir,” returned Mr. Mudge, depositing the charred ledger on the table with a heavy thump. “But I’ve saved the books, sir. The men are without work, and the women are crying in the streets, but the books are safe, sir. The books are safe!”

“The books!” cried Mr. Grume, snatching at the volume as a hungry dog snatches at a bone. “Bless my soul, Mudge! You don’t say so! The books! The principal ledger! Why, you’re a hero, Mudge—a positive hero! I shall not forget this, Mudge. I shall not forget this.”

And indeed, the small eyes of Mr. Grume sparkled with a satisfaction that was altogether out of proportion to the melancholy tone of his discourse. He turned over the leaves of the ledger with a fondling, caressing motion, as if he were stroking the back of a favourite child, and his pale face flushed with a species of rapture.

“And how are the poor hands, Mudge?” he inquired, after a long and loving gaze at the figures. “How are the poor, poor hands?”

“Bad enough, sir,” said Mudge, drily. “They are like a flock of sheep without a shepherd, and a very hungry flock at that. Three hundred of them, sir, counting the women and children that depend on them. What is to be done, sir? Shall we begin to clear away the rubbish, and set up the looms again?”

Mr. Grume sighed—a long, rippling, watery sigh, that seemed to start from his boots and travel upward, like a bubble in a water-pipe. He shook his head slowly, and looked into the fire.

“No, Mudge, no,” he said, in a tone of gentle sorrow. “I fear that is not to be. I fear, Mudge, that the ruin is too complete. I fear that the capital is too much impaired. I fear—”

He paused, and took a sip of his brandy-and-water.

“I fear, Mudge, that I shall be compelled to rebuild elsewhere.”

“Elsewhere, sir!” cried Mudge, starting. “Not in Clinkerbottom?”

“No, Mudge, not in Clinkerbottom. I have been thinking it over, and I find that Clinkerbottom is not the place for me. The site is damp, the water-supply is uncertain, and the railway is too far off. I shall rebuild in the south, Mudge—in the south, where labour is cheaper, and coals are nearer, and the railway runs right up to the door. It is a painful necessity, Mudge, but it must be done.”

“And the three hundred hands, sir?” said Mudge, a dark scowl gathering on his brow.

Mr. Grume raised his hands in a gesture of deprecation. “The hands, my good Mudge, must look out for themselves. I am sorry for them—deeply, painfully sorry. But the laws of trade are inexorable. Capital must flow to the most profitable channel. I cannot be expected to ruin myself for the sake of a parcel of idle, intemperate—”

“Come, sir,” interrupted Mudge, “they are neither idle nor intemperate. They have worked for you, sir, fourteen hours a day, and they have not been paid over-well for it. And now you are going to throw them over, like so many empty bobbins, and carry your capital to the south, where you can grind the faces of the poor a little cheaper. That is what you call a painful necessity, is it?”

Mr. Grume’s face assumed a look of pained surprise. He gazed at his overseer with an expression of injured innocence. “Mudge,” he said, “you are excited. You are carried away by your feelings. You do not understand the laws of political economy. You do not comprehend the beautiful, the harmonious operation of capital and labour. It is a pain to me to part with you, Mudge; but if you cannot see this thing in the right light, I fear we must part. I fear we must part.”

“I think we must,” said Mudge, grimly. “But before I go, Mr. Grume, I have a word or two to say to you. I saved the books, sir, but I saved something else. I saved a sight of the inside of your office, and I saved a sight of your desk, and I saved a sight of a certain drawer in that desk, and I saved a sight of a certain paper in that drawer. I know, sir, that you took out a policy of insurance on this mill for ten thousand pounds, only one week ago today!”

Mr. Grume’s face, which had been pale before, became now a livid white. The glass of brandy-and-water trembled in his hand, and a drop of the liquid fell upon his waistcoat, like a tear of contrition. He stared at Mudge in silence for a moment, and then, with a great effort, recovered his composure.

“Well, Mudge,” he said, with a sickly smile, “and what then? What then? Is it a crime to insure one’s property? Is it a sin to provide against the accidents of fire? I am a cautious man, Mudge—a cautious man. I saw that the old policy was running out, and I renewed it. That is all. That is all.”

“That is a lie, Mr. Grume,” said Mudge, sternly. “There was no old policy. The mill was uninsured for two years before last week. I know it, for I heard you say, only last month, that the premiums were a robbery, and that you would not pay them. And then, all of a sudden, you change your mind, and take out a policy for ten thousand pounds; and a week later, the mill burns down. It looks queer, sir. It looks very queer.”

Mr. Grume sprang to his feet, and his flabby face quivered with anger. “How dare you!” he cried. “How dare you, sir! Do you mean to insinuate—do you mean to hint—”

“I mean nothing,” said Mudge. “I only state facts. I leave the insinuations to the magistrates, and to the insurance company. They may be able to draw them, and they may be able to prove them. As for me, I have done with you.
