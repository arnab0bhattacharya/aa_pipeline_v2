In the kingdom of Valerion, where the palaces were of white marble and the follies of black granite, there reigned a princess whose beauty was a matter of state concern, and whose arrogance was a triumph of art. She had refused every suitor who presented himself at her crystal gates. Princes were offered to her as one might offer a fine vintage, and she discarded them with the same fastidious carelessness. She held the singular theory that marriage is the one event that makes a romance impossible, and she preferred to keep her romance in the ideal. 

Her court was a symphony of decorative perfections. There were tapestries wrought with threads of spun silver, and petals of bruised violets scattered daily upon the floors. She herself moved through her chambers like a painted fan, exquisite in her silks of moonlight blue, her fingers heavy with jewels that seemed to trap the very sun within their facets. Yet, for all the opulence that surrounded her, she was consumed by the most refined of modern diseases: a terrible, aching curiosity for novelty.

"I will marry," she announced one morning to her chief courtier, leaning back among her cushions of pale amber, "the man who can bring me something I have never seen."

The courtier, a man who had survived three reigns by systematically refusing to have an opinion, bowed and inquired whether the condition was meant to include the abstract as well as the material.

"Everything," said the Princess, adjusting a comb of carved jade in her heavy, dark hair. "But I warn you, I have seen a great deal. I have seen the hypocrisy of the righteous and the treachery of the beautiful. It is a very dull world, and it has exhausted its surprises."

The proclamation went forth, and the kingdom of Valerion prepared to be entertained. The great forest that bordered the marble city whispered its secrets to the wind, but the wind was too well-bred to repeat them, and so the court relied upon the vanity of men.

Kings arrived from distant lands, their camels laden with chests of rubies that burned with a dull, heavy fire, and sapphires that held the perilous blue of a summer lightning flash. They brought pearls from the southern seas, grey and sombre as the eyes of a dead saint, and opals that shifted colour like a woman shifting her vows. The Princess examined them all through her tortoiseshell lorgnette, and found them merely expensive.

"Gems," she murmured, tossing a flawless emerald onto a velvet cushion, "are merely the tears of a materialist. I have seen them before. They are the apologies that wealth makes to beauty, and I find them entirely inadequate."

Wise men came next, bringing with them the wonders of their philosophy. There was an astronomer who presented her with a mirror of polished obsidian, in which he claimed one might see the death of the stars. There was an inventor who brought a mechanical bird, wrought in gold and enamel, that sang the precise moment of one's birth. The Princess listened to the bird, which sang a rather tedious and metallic air, and dismissed the inventor.

"Mechanics," she said, with a faint, weary smile, "are the attempts of the clumsy to imitate the graceful. As for the stars, they are merely the gossip of the night, and I never listen to gossip."

The courtiers began to despair. They feared she would die a virgin of the intellect, unsullied by reality. But the world, which is always anxious to provide a fool when a paradox is required, sent forth a shepherd from the depths of the great forest.

He was a youth of remarkable and almost tragic plainness, clad in the coarse, undyed wool of his trade. He possessed none of the graces of the drawing-room, and his hands, though gentle enough to soothe a frightened ewe, were entirely unacquainted with the handling of crystal or jade. He carried nothing in his hands but a shallow basin carved from a block of clear ice, which he held before him with the solemnity of a priest bearing the sacrament.

The guards, who are always the first to recognize a social inferior, attempted to turn him away, but the Princess, who was leaning over the marble balustrade of her balcony, was intrigued by the sheer impertinence of his poverty.

"Let him approach," she commanded. "A man who has nothing is the only man who has nothing to hide."

The shepherd was brought before her. He did not bow, for his pride was of the earth, and hers was of the marble. He looked at her with the quiet, unblinking gaze of one who is accustomed to watching the slow, deliberate movements of the seasons.

"What have you brought me?" she asked, her voice touched with a delicate, patronising amusement.

"I have brought you something you have never seen, Highness," he answered. His voice was low and cadenced, carrying the hushed simplicity of the forest.

He knelt upon the floor of polished porphyry and set the basin of ice before her. It was already beginning to weep in the warmth of the palace, its edges softening, the water within it settling into a perfect, still clarity. It was not a gem, nor a mechanical toy. It was merely a pool of water, caught in the frail, melting vessel of winter.

"Look into it," he said.

The Princess, moved by some strange impulse she did not quite understand, stepped down from her dais. She bent over the basin, her dark hair falling in heavy, perfumed coils about her face. She looked into the water, and she saw a face looking back at her.

It was her own face. But it was a face she had never seen before. The mirrors of her palace were made of silver, and silver, being a noble metal, is compelled to flatter. It smooths the lines of cruelty about the mouth and softens the hard brilliance of the eyes. But this pool of water, held in a vessel of ice, was entirely indifferent to her rank. It showed her the truth, and the truth was something she had never seen.

She saw the subtle, creeping corruption of her own vanity. She saw the thin, hard line of her lips, pressed together in the perpetual disdain of a woman who has judged the world and found it wanting. She saw the cold, calculating glare of her eyes, eyes that had looked upon so many wonders and felt nothing but the chill of their own superiority. She saw a face that was beautiful, certainly, but it was the beauty of a closed door, of a sealed vault, of a winter that refuses to yield to spring.

For a moment, a profound and terrible silence held the room. The courtiers held their breath, watching the scene as one might watch a tragedy unfold upon a stage. The shadows lengthened across the marble floor, and the light in the room seemed to weary of its task and fade.

Then the Princess straightened herself up. Her face, which had been momentarily touched by a great and tragic revelation, hardened once more into the mask of marble she had spent her life constructing. She was afraid of the truth, for the truth is the one thing that cannot be made to serve our vanity.

"Throw him out," she said, turning her back upon the shepherd and the melting ice. "He is an impostor. He has brought me nothing but a common pool of water."

The guards seized the boy and dragged him towards the great doors. He did not resist. He looked back once at the Princess, who was already examining a new silk from the Orient, and he smiled the sad, forgiving smile of one who knows that the blind will always resent the gift of sight.

The basin of ice was left upon the floor. It continued to weep, spreading a small, dark stain across the porphyry, a stain that no one bothered to wipe away. It was the only trace of the truth that had been offered to her, and it was left to evaporate, as all truths do, in the stifling, perfumed air of the palace.

The Princess never married. She lived out her days among her jewels and her mirrors, surrounded by the elaborate and empty flatteries of the court. She was universally admired, and universally feared, and she was entirely alone. She had demanded to see something new, and when the world had shown her her own soul, she had closed her eyes, preferring the comfortable darkness of her ignorance to the blinding, unforgiving light.
