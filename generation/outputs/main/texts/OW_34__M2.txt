Julian Ashworth sat in the shadow of a mahogany bookcase, watching the dust motes dance in the slanting afternoon light, and thought that if one’s face were a mirror, it was a poor servant to reflect the truth. He had been told, with the fervor of the devout and the precision of the jeweler, that he was a perfect copy of his late father, Lord Aldous. This was a burden of vanity, not lineage. One’s features were merely the architecture of a house one did not own, built by a mason whose name had been carefully chiselled from the foundation stone.

His mother, Lady Ashworth, was a woman of such formidable elegance that she seemed less a member of society than a critical member of the furniture. She had a habit of smiling at people as if they were amusingly dressed dogs, and she possessed the rare virtue of never being surprised by anything, which made her the most dangerous woman in London.

"You look well, Julian," she said, not looking up from her embroidery. The needle moved with the mechanical regularity of a metronome, ticking out the seconds of their conversation. "You have your father’s chin. It is a rather aggressive shape for a young man, but it carries a suit admirably."

"Mother," Julian said, keeping his voice light, as one keeps a glass of champagne from spilling, "I have been told I do not look like Lord Aldous."

Lady Ashworth’s needle stopped. The silence that followed was so complete it felt audible, a sharp, bright thing in the air. "By whom?" she asked, her tone devoid of curiosity, yet vibrating with the tension of a plucked string.

"By Dr. Harker," Julian replied. "He was examining the old portraits in the gallery. He said the resemblance was uncanny, but that the eyes were wrong. He said Lord Aldous had eyes like wet slate, cold and indifferent. Mine, he said, are too warm. Too human."

Lady Ashworth resumed her stitching. "Dr. Harker is a man who wears his spectacles too tightly. It constricts the blood supply to the brain and leaves him with an excess of critical faculty. He sees what he expects to see, which is rarely what is there. You have your father’s face, Julian. Do not be so childish as to doubt it because a man with a microscope cannot comprehend the soul."

"But who did I resemble, then?" Julian asked. He had rehearsed this question in the mirror, polishing it until it gleamed with the promise of scandal. "The man who died in the carriage accident? Or the man who was merely the name on the door?"

Lady Ashworth looked up. Her eyes were the color of old ice, clear and utterly without mercy. "You resemble your father, Julian. And your father was a good man. He died as he lived: loudly, expensively, and with a great deal of dignity. Now, please do not be dramatic. It is unbecoming of a man with your ears."

But the seed had been planted in the rich, dark soil of Julian’s suspicion. He knew his mother’s silence better than he knew his own pulse. It was a wall built of velvet bricks, soft to the touch but impenetrable to the eye. He left the room, the floorboards creaking under his feet like old bones, and stepped out into the grey London afternoon.

The city was a theatre of masks. In the drawing rooms of Mayfair, people wore their faces as they wore their clothes: as a disguise for the body, or a costume for the play. Julian walked to the club where he knew the truth-tellers gathered, not because they were honest, but because they were bored. Boredom, in high society, is the only reliable engine of confession.

He found Lord Pemberton in the corner, nursing a glass of brandy that had been forgotten, and Mrs. Vane, who was playing solitaire with a deck of cards that seemed to have lost its interest in winning.

"Julian," Lord Pemberton said, his voice thick with the syrup of old wine. "You look as if you have been told the rent is up. Which, in this economy, is a tragedy."

"I have been told," Julian said, sitting down, "that I am a counterfeit."

Mrs. Vane looked up, her eyes sharp as a razor. "Counterfeit to what? Your mother’s affection? I thought that was a limited edition."

"To my father," Julian said. "Or rather, to the man who signed the letters and wore the face. Dr. Harker says the face is wrong. The eyes are wrong."

Lord Pemberton laughed, a dry, rattling sound. "Ah, the eyes. The eyes are the only part of the face that tells the lie. The rest is merely decoration. But tell me, Julian, what does your mother say? Does she weep? Does she faint? Or does she simply change her gloves?"

"She changes her gloves," Julian said. "She says Dr. Harker is foolish. But she does not look at me. She looks through me, as if I were a window and not a son."

"Then she is guilty," Mrs. Vane said, laying down a card with a snap. "In our world, innocence is loud. Guilt is quiet. Your mother is not quiet, Julian; she is silent. There is a difference. Silence is a scream that has been edited."

"Who was he, then?" Julian asked. "If he was not Lord Aldous, who was he?"

Lord Pemberton leaned forward, his face changing from amusement to a sudden, stark seriousness. "There was a man," he said softly. "A poet, they called him. Or a musician. I forget. He was tall, and he had hands that looked as if they had been made for holding a lute, or a knife. He stayed in the East Wing of the house. Your mother kept him there. We thought he was a servant. Or a ghost. Or a dream."

"A ghost?" Julian asked.

"A living one," Lord Pemberton corrected. "He died before Lord Aldous. Or so we were told. But I remember the smell of him. It was not the smell of tobacco and leather, but of violets and ink. And I remember that your mother used to go to his door at night. She would stand there, just standing, for an hour. And when she came out, she would be weeping. But she would wash her face immediately. She never let the tears dry. She hated the salt."

Julian felt a chill run through him, colder than the London fog that pressed against the windows. He thought of his own eyes, which he had always been told were too warm, too full of life. He thought of the poems he had found in the attic, written in a hand that was not his father’s, but which he recognized with a shock of intimacy. They were addressed to *E.*, his mother’s initial.

He left the club without saying goodbye. The night was falling, and the gas lamps were beginning to glow, like the eyes of watchful cats in the dark. He walked back to the house, his steps quick and irregular. He knew what he had to do. He had to go to the East Wing. He had to open the door that had been painted over, or hidden behind a tapestry, or perhaps merely forgotten.

When he reached the house, the lights were out, save for a single glow in the library where his mother was likely to be, reading or pretending to read. He did not go to her. He went to the service entrance, the one he had used as a boy to sneak out to the stables. The door was unlocked. It was always unlocked. The servants were asleep, and the house was breathing its slow, heavy sleep.

He found the East Wing. The air was stale, smelling of dust and time. There was a door at the end of the corridor, painted the same shade of green as the walls, so that it disappeared into the architecture. He touched the handle. It was cold.

He turned it.

The door opened onto a small room. It was furnished with a single chair, a table, and a window that looked out onto the moonlit garden. On the table lay a single object: a locket, open, containing a miniature portrait. It was not Lord Aldous. It was a young man with dark eyes and a sad, beautiful smile.

Julian picked up the locket. His hands trembled. He looked at the portrait, and then at his own reflection in the dark glass of the window. The eyes were the same. The sorrow was the same. The truth was not a scandal to be shouted, but a secret to be kept. It was a jewel, cut and polished, hidden in the dark.

He put the locket in his pocket and closed the door. He did not go to his mother. He went to his own room and sat in the dark, listening to the clock on the mantelpiece tick away the minutes. He knew he would never ask her again. The answer was in his blood, in the shape of his face, in the warmth of his eyes. He was not a copy. He was the original. And the original was dead.

He lay back on the bed and stared at the ceiling. The moonlight came through the curtains, painting silver stripes on the floor. It was a beautiful night. It was a terrible night. It was the first night of his life.
