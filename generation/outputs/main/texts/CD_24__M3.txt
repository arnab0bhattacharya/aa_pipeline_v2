If any mortal man in the metropolitan district of Clerkenwell laboured under the grievous, the stupendous, the perfectly overwhelming misfortune of being acquainted with the true value of a rusty nail, a shattered boot-heel, or a fragment of soiled and mildewed calico, that man was unquestionably Mr. Ephraim Muck. His yard—a dreary and unwholesome wilderness, situated in that delectable and aromatic locality known to the initiated as Grubber’s Mews—was a very Aladdin’s cave of unconsidered trifles. Not the trifles of poetry or parlour-rooms, but the hard, unyielding, uncompromising trifles of squalor. Rags of every texture, from the tattered velvet of a decayed gentleman’s coat to the coarse, perspiration-steeped hemp of a crossing-sweeper’s apron, festooned the walls like the melancholy banners of a defeated army. Bones, bleached and yellow, lay piled in mighty ossuaries that would have furnished materials for a dozen catastrophes. Old iron, twisted, rusted, and disfigured, writhed in tortuous heaps, as if in silent agony at its own uselessness. The yard itself was a dank, muddy basin, into which the sun appeared to penetrate only by some unfortunate accident, and where the very fog seemed to take a denser, dirtier, and more opaque consistency than in any neighbouring thoroughfare.

Mr. Muck was a miser. Let the philanthropist weep over him, let the political economist analyze him, let the beadle denounce him; he was a miser of the most uncompromising and unmitigated description. His residence was a crazy, crazy old tenement that leaned against the neighbouring brewery as if for support, and seemed to reproach it with its own frailty. In this mansion, Mr. Muck lived, and starved, and hoarded, and muttered, and shivered, attended only by a small apprentice girl named Tilly Toop. Tilly was a wisp of a creature, pale as a turnip, with eyes that seemed perpetually apologizing for their own brilliancy, and a pair of legs that, in their thinness, bore a painful resemblance to the very canes Mr. Muck refused to sell. Mr. Muck’s conversational powers were limited, and principally consisted of the monosyllable “Umph!” delivered with various inflections—sometimes of anger, sometimes of contempt, and generally of both. He would not part with a penny if his life depended upon it. “Umph!” he would say, when appealed to by the parish; “umph! let the parish pay for the parish’s poor! What’s the ratepayers for, if not to pay? Umph!” And having delivered himself of this comprehensive view of political economy, he would shut the door in the face of the applicant, and retire to count his coppers.

It was upon a raw, damp, creeping evening in November, when the fog seemed to crawl along the ground, when the gas-lamps in Grubber’s Mews were just struggling into a sickly, yellow life, and when the very bones in the yard appeared to be shivering, that Tilly Toop, returning from an errand to the chandler’s shop, found her master lying in the mud. He was stretched out at full length, with his head resting against a pile of ancient and unsavoury rags, and his right hand clutching, with a dying grasp, a rusty door-nail, which he had probably been in the act of picking up when the hand of Death arrested his thrift. He was quite dead. The damp had stolen into his old bones, and the cold had stopped the sluggish current of his miserly blood. Tilly, with a scream that penetrated the surrounding gloom, and brought half the Mews to her assistance, stood gazing at the corpse. The neighbours gathered round. There was Mrs. Gamp, the laundress, with her arms akimbo; Mr. Sweedlepipe, the bird-fancier, with a canary in his hand; and a score of other denizens of the Mews, all in a state of prodigious excitement. “Lor’ bless us!” cried Mrs. Gamp, “he’s gone, sure enough! The old bone-grubber’s gone at last! And serve him right, too, for keeping such a shocking bad yard!” “Poor old cove!” said Mr. Sweedlepipe, “he was a rum ’un, he was! I wonder what he’s left behind him?” This question, naturally enough, excited the curiosity of the assembled multitude. The yard was ransacked, and the discovery that was made beggared all description. In the very centre of the largest bone-heap, carefully concealed beneath a layer of ox-skulls, was discovered a bag containing no less than three hundred pounds in gold and silver. In the middle of a pile of rags, stitched up in an old pillowcase, was found a quantity of bank-notes, damp, mildewed, and rotten, but still recognizable. In an old kettle, buried in the mud, was a hoard of copper coin, amounting, by actual computation, to nearly fifteen pounds. And in the lining of his old coat, which Tilly had often washed and patched, was found a bundle of securities, bonds, and mortgages, representing a fortune of many thousands a year. The excitement of the neighbours was tremendous. They rummaged, and dug, and scratched, and searched. They emptied bags of rags into the mud, and scattered bones in every direction. The yard of Mr. Muck was a very Golconda, a very Pactolus, a very California. They overturned the old iron, and found sovereigns; they ripped open old mattresses, and found mouldy bank-notes; they broke old boxes, and found bright new shillings. The yard was a scene of wild, bewildering, and perfectly indescribable confusion.

And where was Tilly all this while? Tilly, poor Tilly, with her pale face and her brilliant eyes, stood apart, gazing at the scene with a strange, scared, bewildered look. She was thinking—thinking of the many nights she had sat up with the old man, nursing him through his rheumatics, and listening to his ravings. She was thinking of the night he had been delirious, and had talked of a certain spot beneath the hearthstone of the kitchen, where, he said, the “biggest plum of all” was hidden. She knew—none better—where that hearthstone was. It was in the dark, damp, miserable kitchen, where she had cooked his scanty meals, and washed his few plates, and where she had often sat shivering over the expiring embers, while the old man slept upstairs. She crept away, unobserved, from the excited crowd, and slipped into the house. The kitchen was dark and gloomy. The feeble light of a tallow candle, which she had lighted, flickered and flared in the draught, casting gigantic shadows on the smoke-begrimed walls. She knelt down by the hearth, and with trembling hands, removed the loose brick. Beneath it was a hollow space. She thrust in her hand, and drew forth a small iron box, heavy and rusted. She opened it. It was full of gold! Bright, yellow, beautiful gold, that gleamed and glistened in the candlelight, and seemed to fill the miserable kitchen with a glory that was not its own. Tilly gazed at it with astonishment. She had never seen so much money in her life. She had never dreamed of such a thing. She was poor, friendless, and alone; and here was wealth—wealth enough to make her comfortable for life. But a thought struck her. She remembered the old man’s words, “It’s for you, Tilly—you’ve been a good gal—a good gal, Tilly.” Had he really said it? Or was it the fancy of a disordered brain? She could not tell. But the words rang in her ears, and she felt a strange, wild, overpowering sensation of joy. She would take it; she would keep it; she would be rich. She clutched the box to her breast, and looked round, as if apprehensive of being discovered. But she was alone—quite alone—with the dead man’s gold. The noise of the neighbours in the yard continued. She could hear their shouts, their oaths, their laughter, and their quarrels. They were fighting over the spoils. The strong were robbing the weak; the cunning were cheating the simple; and the greedy were grasping at everything. It was a hideous, a disgusting, a deplorable spectacle. But Tilly was safe. She had the best of the plunder, and she would keep it. She would fly; she would leave the miserable old house, the miserable old yard, the miserable old Mews, and all their wretched associations. She would go out into the world, and begin life anew. She replaced the brick, blew out the candle, and with the box clasped tightly to her breast, crept softly upstairs to her little garret, to pack up her few belongings, and to await the coming of the morning. The morning came, cold, grey, and cheerless. The fog hung over Grubber’s Mews like a pall. The neighbours had dispersed, carrying with them such spoils as they had been able to secure. The yard was a scene of desolation. The bones were scattered, the rags were torn, the old iron was piled in heaps, and the mud was trampled into a thick, black, unctuous paste. The body of Mr. Muck had been removed by the parish authorities, and the house was left to silence and to Tilly. She came down, pale and trembling, but with a new light in her eyes, and a new resolve in her heart. She passed through the kitchen, and out into the yard. She looked at the old house, the old yard, and the old Mews, and she shuddered. She turned away, and walked rapidly out of the Mews. She never returned. The house was pulled down, the yard was built over, and Grubber’s Mews is now a respectable thoroughfare, inhabited by respectable people, who know nothing of Mr. Muck, or Tilly Toop, or the hidden treasure beneath the hearthstone. And Tilly? Tilly went away, no one knew whither. Some said she emigrated; some said she married a wealthy tradesman in the city; and some said she entered a religious house, and devoted her fortune to the poor. But whether any of these rumours were true, I am unable to say. This only I know, that somewhere in this great London of ours, there is a woman who was once a poor, friendless, starving servant-girl, and who is now, perhaps, one of the happiest and most respected of her sex. And if ever you should happen to meet her, give her my kindest regards, and tell her that her old friends in Grubber’s Mews remember her with affection, and wish her well.
