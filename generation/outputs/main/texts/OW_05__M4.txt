The Duchess of Merrowvale received her nephew in the morning-room, where the fire was permitted to burn only when the thermometer stood below forty, and where the photographs of her three deceased husbands regarded one another across the chimney-piece with the air of men who had agreed, at last, upon a truce. She was eighty-one, and her face was like a piece of old parchment upon which beauty had once been written in a hand so fine that only the discerning could still read it. She wore grey, as she had worn grey for thirty years, and her rings were few and enormous.

“Ah, Bertram,” she said, without turning from the window. “You have come about the book. Sit down. There is cake, if you are young enough to want it, and tea, if you are old enough to need it.”

Bertram, Lord Ashwicke, who was forty-four and looked upon himself as a man of the world chiefly because the world had never asked him to be anything else, sat down and declined both. He had rehearsed several openings in the cab, and discarded them all, for the truth was that his aunt had a way of making one feel like a schoolboy who has been sent for about a window.

“I have read the manuscript,” he said.

“Of course you have read it. I sent it to you to be read. You are my editor, Bertram. It is a position of considerable dignity, and of no remuneration whatsoever. You should be proud of it. Editors are the only people in England who are permitted to know everything and say nothing, and they are consequently the best company in the world.”

“Aunt Caroline, it is not a manuscript. It is a bomb.”

“It is a memoir,” said the Duchess, with a certain severity. “A bomb is an explosive device. A memoir is an arrangement of facts in the order most flattering to the author and most ruinous to everyone else. I have merely followed the conventions of the genre.”

He laid his hand upon the pile of pages as though it were a creature that might bite. “You describe the Christmas at Ravensholt in ‘sixty-two. You describe it in some detail.”

“I describe everything in some detail. It is what distinguishes a memoir from a railway timetable.”

“You describe my mother.”

The Duchess turned from the window at last. Her eyes were the colour of weak tea and quite as clear, and there was in them a look that Bertram remembered from his boyhood, when she had caught him in some small, satisfactory wickedness and had declined, on principle, to punish it.

“I describe your mother,” she said, “as she was. She was my sister, and I loved her, and she was the most accomplished flirt in the county of Norfolk, which is saying a great deal, for the county of Norfolk has never been deficient in either accomplishment or flirtation. She was also, for eleven days in the December of that year, the particular friend of a young attaché from the Austrian embassy whose name I have taken the trouble to spell correctly. There were mistletoe and a good deal of champagne and a conservatory. I was there. I remember the conservatory. It was heated to a most improper temperature, and so, I may say, was everyone in it.”

“She is my mother.”

“She was my sister. You will observe that the one relation does not cancel the other. On the contrary, it intensifies it. I am aware that the world has lately decided that a woman’s virtues belong to her children and her faults to her biographer, but I have never subscribed to the arrangement, and I am too old now to be converted to it. Besides, Bertram, you are to inherit. There is nothing like an inheritance for reconciling a man to the truth about his family. It is the only form of consolation that is also a form of income.”

He rose and walked to the fire, which was giving out a thin, apologetic warmth, like a curate venturing an opinion. “If this is published,” he said, “the Marlowe connection will not receive me. Lady Marlowe has already declined to dine. She sent a note so cold that the ink had frozen on it. And there is the question of my own position. I am asked to stand for the division. A man cannot stand for a division with a mother like—like that, in a book.”

“My dear Bertram,” said the Duchess, “a man who cannot stand for a division with a mother like that in a book is a man who has never read any history. Half the peerage is descended from people who behaved far worse and wrote far better. Consider the great houses of England. Consider the Howards. Consider the Church. You are not asked to be proud of your mother; you are asked to be patient with her. Those are the two orthodox attitudes towards one’s relatives, and they have served the English family for six hundred years.”

“I am asked to edit the book.”

“You are asked to edit it. There is a difference. An editor does not approve of his author; he corrects her commas. I have never known a comma to ruin a reputation. It is the adjectives that do the damage, and I have been very sparing with them. Read the passage again. You will find that I have said nothing against your mother that a jury of her own dinner-guests would not have said in the smoking-room, and said with less charity. I have merely put it where it can be read by people who were not invited to dinner.”

Bertram stood a long moment looking into the fire. He was thinking, as men of his sort do think at such moments, not of his mother but of himself; and he was ashamed of it, and continued to think it. “And if I refuse?” he said.

“Then the book will be published without an editor, which is to say it will be published as I wrote it, with all the adjectives I have been sparing of. I am an old woman, Bertram, and I have outlived three husbands and a great many opinions, and I have come to the conclusion that the only immortality available to my sex is to be talked about after one is dead. I intend to be talked about. I have arranged for it. It is the last arrangement I shall ever make, and I mean to make it thoroughly.”

“You are not afraid of what people will say of you?”

“Afraid?” The Duchess smiled, and for an instant the parchment was illuminated, and one saw what the young men of 1835 had seen. “My dear boy, I have been talked about for sixty years. It is the only thing that has never tired me. And I have observed that the world forgives a woman everything except being dull, and forgives her that only when she is dead and cannot contradict the eulogy. I do not intend to be dull, and I do not intend to be eulogised. I intend to be read.”

She rose, and took up her stick, and came across the room with the slow, deliberate dignity of a woman who has never in her life been hurried by anyone. She laid her hand for a moment upon the manuscript, as a gardener might lay his hand upon a wall that he has planted and intends to see covered with roses.

“You will edit it, Bertram,” she said. “You will correct my commas, and you will leave my sentences alone, and you will discover, in the course of the winter, that a family scandal is like a fire in a grate: it is dreadful in the drawing-room, but it is very pleasant in the library, and it keeps the whole house warm. Now ring for Finch and tell him to bring the cake. You look as though you had been thinking, and that is always a mistake before luncheon.”
