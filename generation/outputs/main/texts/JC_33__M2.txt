The heat in the Red Sea was not a thing that touched you; it was a substance that entered you, a heavy, stagnant weight that settled in the marrow and clouded the mind with a dull, yellow haze. It hung over the deck of the *Aldebaran* like a damp cloth, thick and suffocating, pressing down on the hull, on the rigging, on the men who moved through it with the slow, deliberate grace of shadows. I stood at the chart table in the wheelhouse, the air still and motionless, the only sound the faint, rhythmic creak of the wood and the distant, muffled thrum of the engine deep below. It was the third watch, that dead, unyielding hour when the sun had not yet risen to judge the world, and the stars were beginning to fade into the grey mist that gathers on the horizon.

I looked at the course I had plotted. It was a line of ink, straight and confident, cutting across the blue paper, leading us toward the port of Jeddah. It was a line that should have been real, solid, anchored in the truth of the sea and the mathematics of the stars. But as I traced it with my finger, a cold sensation, sharp and unwelcome, began to spread through my chest. It was not a chill of temperature, but a chill of doubt, a sudden, terrifying awareness that the line was a lie. I looked up at the window, where the darkness was beginning to lighten into a bruised purple, and I saw the silhouette of Elias Thorne standing there. He was leaning against the frame, his face turned away from me, his profile sharp against the dim light of the cabin lamp. He was drinking. I could not see the bottle, but I could smell it, a faint, sweet, cloying scent of gin that hung in the stagnant air, cutting through the smell of salt and oil.

Thorne was a good man. He was a precise man, a man who could read the sea as a book, who knew the moods of the wind and the temperament of the current. He had been my chief officer for ten years, through storms and calms, through the narrow straits and the open, indifferent oceans. He was a man who had earned my trust, and my respect, and the respect of the crew. He was a man who did not lie. Or so I had believed. But now, standing in the shadow of the wheelhouse, I knew that he was lying. He was lying to himself, and he was lying to the ship. He had been drinking for a week. I had known it, in the way one knows a sickness in the body, a vague unease, a wrongness in the way he moved, in the way he spoke. I had seen the tremor in his hands, the glazed look in his eyes, the way he avoided the chart table. I had told myself it was fatigue, the strain of the voyage, the heat. I had told myself many things, but I had not told the truth. I had let him lie.

I looked at the chart again. The line was still there, but it was no longer a line of certainty. It was a line of error. We were not where we thought we were. We were off course. Not by a mile, not by a degree, but by a drift, a slow, insidious drift that had accumulated over days, a drift that had carried us away from the safe waters and into the unknown. I did not know exactly where we were. I did not know what lay ahead of us. I only knew that we were lost, and that the man who was supposed to guide us was drunk.

The implications of this were terrible. To report him was to destroy him. It was to take the man I had trusted, the man who had saved my life in a storm off the Cape, and to cast him out into the world of disgrace and ruin. It was to break the bond that held us together, the bond of professional duty and personal loyalty. But to say nothing was to condemn the ship. It was to let the *Aldebaran* sail on into the darkness, into the rocks, into the deep, until it was too late to turn back. It was to accept the possibility of disaster, to allow the fate of two hundred men to hang on the whim of a man who had lost his way.

I felt a great weight settle on my shoulders, a weight that was not physical, but moral, a weight of responsibility that I had never before felt so keenly. I was the captain. I was the man who had to decide. I had to choose between loyalty and duty, between the man and the ship. It was a choice that could not be undone, a choice that would define the rest of my life. I looked at Thorne again. He was still standing there, his face in shadow, his body still. He did not move. He did not speak. He was waiting for me to speak, or perhaps he was waiting for the sun to rise, to bring with it the clarity of day, the clarity of judgment. But the sun did not rise. The darkness held us, and the silence held us, and the sea held us in its cold, indifferent embrace.

I knew what I had to do. I knew it with a certainty that was almost painful. I could not let him continue. I could not let the ship be lost. I had to report him. I had to save the ship, even if it meant destroying the man. It was a terrible thing, a thing that went against every instinct of my nature, every fiber of my being. I did not want to do it. I did not want to be the one to break the bond, to be the one to deliver the blow. But I had to. It was my duty. It was the only thing I could do.

I straightened my uniform, buttoning the top button with a steady hand, though my heart was beating fast, a frantic, irregular rhythm that I could feel in my throat. I turned to face him. He turned to face me. We looked at each other, two men in the dark, two men who knew the truth, two men who could not look away. There was no need for words. The look in his eyes said everything. It said regret, it said shame, it said resignation. It said that he knew he had failed, and that he was ready to accept the consequences.

I nodded, a slow, solemn nod. It was a gesture of acknowledgment, of understanding, of farewell. I turned back to the chart table. I picked up the pencil. I drew a new line. It was a line of correction, a line of redemption, a line that led us back to the safety of the known. I did not look at Thorne again. I did not need to. I knew what I had done. I knew what it cost. And I knew that I would carry it with me, always, a heavy stone in my chest, a weight that I could not set down. The sea was still, the air was still, and the ship moved on, into the light, into the uncertainty, into the future that we had earned by the sacrifice of the past.
