I must confess that when I first accepted the position of Junior Clerk in the Subsistence Bureau of the Fourth District, I did not anticipate that the fate of the modern social order would rest upon my shoulders. The Bureau was, after all, the supreme triumph of our age. The World Government had, at last, eliminated poverty by the simple and devastatingly logical expedient of universal rationing. Every citizen was assigned a daily quota of calories, clothing, and lodging, issued directly from the central depots. It was a vast, perfectly oiled machine, and my part in it was merely to tally the weekly consumption reports of District Four.

It was a dull, methodical task, perfectly suited to a man of regular habits. I sat in my small, well-lit office in the Administrative Pylon, adding figures from morning till night. The machinery of the Bureau was so flawless, and the public so meticulously categorized, that discrepancies simply did not occur. Or at least, they had not occurred until the third week of October, when I noticed a peculiar friction in the gears of our utopian distribution.

The matter presented itself in the most prosaic fashion. I was checking the carbohydrate tallies against the population rolls. Every kilogram of synthetic bread and every tin of proteid paste must, by the very nature of our system, correspond to a registered mouth. Yet, I found that District Four was drawing from the central granaries a quantity of foodstuffs exactly seven tons in excess of its official requirements. I checked the figures again. I verified the population count. The math was quite correct. We were feeding exactly three thousand, four hundred persons who did not officially exist.

I reported the anomaly to my superior, Mr. Blenkinsop, a man of immense administrative lethargy and a remarkable talent for finding explanations that required no further action.

“A clerical error, Mr. Wedderburn,” he said, not looking up from his desk. “Doubtless a decimal point misplaced by some half-asleep clerk in the lower echelons. You may adjust the balance sheet.”

“But, sir,” I protested, for I had a stubborn, perhaps foolish, attachment to the integrity of my ledgers, “I have checked the lower echelons. The requisitions are stamped and countersigned. The food is leaving the depots.”

“Then it is being wasted,” Blenkinsop muttered, impatiently. “Spoiled in transit, thrown into the sea, or consumed by rats. Make the adjustment, Wedderburn, and let us have no more of it.”

I made the adjustment in the ledger, but I did not let the matter rest. I was possessed by that irritable curiosity which is the chief affliction of the scientifically minded. I determined to find out where those seven tons of food were going.

My opportunity came two nights later. I had made a point of acquainting myself with the routing of the provision lorries. The food was dispatched from the main depot on electro-magnetic trams, running deep beneath the city in the old utility tunnels. According to the schedules, the trams for the southern sectors made a loop that passed directly beneath the ruins of the old cathedral before ascending to the surface distribution points. I took it upon myself to walk that subterranean loop.

It was a foolish, perhaps dangerous, thing to do. The tunnels were dimly lit and strictly out of bounds to unauthorized personnel. But I carried a pocket torch and a certain righteous anger. I walked for perhaps a mile and a half, the damp air smelling of ozone and old chalk, until I came upon a junction where the tram lines diverged. Here, I found something that had no business existing in our perfectly ordered society. A secondary track, unmarked on any official survey, had been laid, branching off into the profound darkness of an older, pre-Government drainage system.

I followed it. The tunnel sloped gently downwards, and the air grew noticeably warmer. After another half-mile, I heard the hum of an electric generator, and around a bend, I found a heavy steel door, painted a dull, institutional green, which stood slightly ajar. I pushed it open and stepped through.

I found myself on a metal gallery overlooking a vast, cavernous space. It had the appearance of a subterranean warehouse, or perhaps a forgotten railway terminus. Stacked along the walls were crates upon crates of Bureau rations, marked with the very stamps I had been tallying in my office. And in the centre of this space, beneath suspended electric bulbs, were long trestle tables, crowded with people.

They were eating. I stood there, utterly astounded, watching this clandestine feast. They were not the sleek, well-regulated citizens of the upper world, clad in the standard grey of the Government. Their clothes were a patchwork of oddments, ill-fitting and faded, though clean enough. They were talking, too, in a lively, colloquial hum that struck me as utterly alien to the hushed, efficient queues of the surface depots.

“Pass the paste, will you, Hargreaves?” I heard a stout woman say. “There’s a good fellow. The synthetic stuff’s a bit dry tonight, but it’s better than the sawdust they used to push in the old days, eh?”

“Right you are, Mrs. Botley,” a lean man replied, tossing a tin across the table. “Though I’d give my right arm for a bit of real cabbage. Still, we can’t complain. The Invisible Man provides.”

“The Invisible Man?” I muttered to myself, utterly baffled.

“You’re a bit early for the second sitting, aren’t you?” said a voice behind me.

I spun around. A man had emerged from the shadows of the gallery. He was a short, sturdy fellow with a red, cheerful face and a manner of immense practical competence. He wore the white coat of a Bureau depotman, but over it he had slung a curious array of tools and a heavy ring of keys.

“Who are you?” I demanded, with what I hoped was official severity. “I am Wedderburn, Junior Clerk of the Fourth District. I demand to know what is going on here.”

The man looked me up and down, and a slow, ironic smile spread across his face. “So you’re the chap who’s been chasing the missing tonnage,” he said, not in the least put out. “I wondered how long it would take one of you upstairs to notice. I’m Grimes. I run this show. Come on down, Mr. Wedderburn. You’d better meet the Invisible Man.”

He led me down a spiral staircase to the floor of the cavern. As we approached the tables, the people looked up. I braced myself for hostility, but found only a kind of amused tolerance. They looked at me as one might look at a stray dog that has wandered into a private garden.

“Here’s the Bureau man, Mrs. Botley,” Grimes announced cheerfully. “The one who found the hole in the bucket.”

“Poor lamb,” said Mrs. Botley, wiping her mouth. “He looks half-starved himself. Sit down, dear. Have a tin of paste. You look as though you could do with a square meal.”

I sat down, my official severity quite deflated. “But who are you all?” I asked. “You aren’t on the rolls. You aren’t registered.”

“Bless you, of course we aren’t,” said Hargreaves, a man with a remarkable shock of grey hair and a pair of bright, argumentative eyes. “That’s the whole point. We’re the unregistered. The surplus. The people the Bureau forgot to count.”

“But that’s impossible,” I said. “Every citizen is registered at birth. The system is absolute.”

“The system is a machine,” said Hargreaves, leaning forward with the air of a man who enjoys a good argument, “and like all machines, it has its friction. People fall through the cracks. Children born to unregistered parents, people who lost their identity cards in the early reorganizations, hermits, vagabonds, and those who simply found the grey, regular life of the upper world too crushingly dull to endure. We are the dust beneath the machine, Mr. Wedderburn. And we are surprisingly numerous.”

“And you feed them?” I asked, turning to Grimes. “You divert the rations?”

“Redistribute,” Grimes corrected me, tapping his nose. “The Bureau allows a certain margin for spoilage in transit. I simply widen that margin a little. The surface dwellers get their rations, and we get the surplus. It’s a perfectly natural economy. It’s the friction that keeps the machine from overheating.”

I looked around the cavern. There were hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, living in this underground network of old tunnels and forgotten crypts. They had built a society of their own, a kind of shadow city, hidden beneath the gleaming, ordered metropolis. They had their own customs, their own slang, their own strange, vibrant life. And they were, to a man, remarkably well-fed.

“But this is a fraud,” I said, though my conviction was already wavering. “You are stealing from the Government.”

“We are stealing from a mathematical abstraction,” said Hargreaves. “The Government has eliminated poverty by defining it out of existence. If you do not exist, you cannot be poor. Therefore, we do not exist, and therefore, we are not poor. It is perfectly logical, Mr. Wedderburn. You, of all people, should appreciate the logic.”

I sat there, staring at my tin of proteid paste, and for the first time in my life, I doubted the absolute virtue of the Bureau. The system above was perfect, but it was a sterile perfection. It was a world of grey clothes and quiet queues, where everyone had enough, but no one had too much, and no one, perhaps, had quite enough of life. Here, below, in the damp and the dark, these unregistered people had built something messy, inefficient, and utterly human. They had a warmth that the upper world lacked.

“Well, Mr. Wedderburn,” said Grimes, leaning back in his chair. “What’s it to be? Will you go back to your Pylon and report us? You can bring the constables down here and clear us out. You’ll be a hero of the Bureau. They’ll probably give you a commendation and an extra half-ration of meat-extract.”

I thought of Blenkinsop, and the hushed corridors of the Administrative Pylon, and the endless, mindless adding of figures that would never again quite balance. I thought of the grey, regular life that awaited me above.

“No,” I said, slowly. “No, I don’t think I will.”

“Wise man,” said Mrs. Botley, pushing a tin of synthetic biscuits towards me. “Have a biscuit. They’re a bit dry, but they’re real enough.”

I took the biscuit. It was, as she had said, a bit dry. But it was, I reflected, as I chewed it, the most genuine meal I had ever eaten. The great machine of the Bureau would continue to grind on, above us, distributing its rations with mathematical precision to the registered and the regular. And down here, in the forgotten dark, the Invisible Man would continue to feed his people. The figures would never add up, and I, for one, was glad of it.
