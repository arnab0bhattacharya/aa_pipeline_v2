The first sign of the trouble, as far as Albert Phipps was concerned, was not the screaming, but the silence. For twenty years he had known the specific, rhythmic clatter of hooves upon the granite setts of the Euston Road, a sound as constant and reliable as the ticking of the great clock at St. Pancras. On that Tuesday morning, the silence fell upon the city like a sudden, heavy snow.

Then came the noise. It was a sound he had never heard before—a chorus of equine terror, a screaming that had nothing of the animal’s natural voice in it. It was the sound of a mind tearing itself apart.

Phipps was on the box of his hansom, the reins loose in his hands. His horse, a sturdy grey cob he had named Jupiter, stood trembling, his great dark eyes rolling with a white-rimmed panic. All around them, the orderly chaos of the London morning had dissolved into a scene from Bedlam. A fine pair of chestnuts belonging to a brewer’s dray were locked in a death struggle, biting and kicking at one another, their harnesses snapping like thread. A young thoroughbred, escaped from a carriage, was galloping in a mad, blind circle, its hooves striking sparks from the pavement before it crashed through the plate-glass window of a milliner’s shop.

“Whoa, Jup. Steady, old son,” Phipps murmured, his voice a low, calming rumble. He patted the horse’s neck, feeling the muscles corded like iron beneath the sweat-soaked coat. Jupiter was not mad, not yet. He was frightened, deeply and profoundly frightened, but he knew his master’s voice.

A policeman, his helmet askew, ran towards Phipps, waving his arms. “Get that animal shot, cabby! Orders are to shoot any horse that shows the sign! It’s the contagion!”

Phipps looked down at the man from his perch. He saw the sweat on the policeman’s brow, the wildness in his eyes. “He’s not sick,” Phipps said, his voice flat and hard. “He’s just scared. Like the rest of us.”

“Don’t be a fool, man! They’re turning! They’ll kill someone! There’s a veterinary from the Board of Agriculture down at the corner with a rifle. Bring him over!”

Phipps looked at Jupiter. The horse had been with him since it was a foal. He had nursed it through colic, had trimmed its hooves in the biting cold of winter dawns, had shared his own bread and cheese with it on long waits at the railway stations. Jupiter was not merely a tool of his trade; he was the other half of his life, the silent, faithful partner in the long, slow, grinding business of survival. The thought of a rifle ball in that intelligent, trusting head was a physical sickness in his gut.

“No,” Phipps said.

He gathered the reins. With a sharp click of his tongue and a flick of the whip—not to strike, but to startle—he turned the hansom sharply, away from the policeman, away from the veterinary with his rifle, and into the labyrinth of the side streets. Jupiter, trusting the familiar command, plunged forward.

The chaos was everywhere. A man in a silk top hat was weeping in the gutter beside the twitching body of his prize stallion. Two dray horses, their eyes rolling like mad things, were backing a heavy wagon of coal through a brick wall. The sound of breaking glass and splintering wood was punctuated by the awful, human-like shrieks of the animals. It was as if the entire city’s engine of transport and commerce had suddenly and violently broken down.

Phipps knew he had only minutes before the authorities cordoned off the districts. He could not go to the mews behind his lodging house; it would be the first place they would look. He needed somewhere else, somewhere forgotten. He steered Jupiter down a grimy alley off the Gray’s Inn Road, a place that smelled of stale beer and despair. There was a building there he knew well, a derelict warehouse that had once held fabrics, now empty and awaiting demolition. Its great double doors for wagons were boarded up, but he knew a smaller entrance around the back, a door to a flight of stairs leading down to a vast, dry cellar.

He brought the hansom to a halt. Jupiter was lathered in sweat, his breath coming in ragged snorts. “Easy now, Jup. Easy,” Phipps soothed, his hands moving with a lifetime’s practice as he began to unbuckle the harness. The clinking of the brass fittings sounded obscenely loud in the unnatural quiet of the alley.

He led the trembling horse to the cellar door. It was a struggle to get Jupiter down the narrow stone steps, the animal’s iron-shod hooves clattering and slipping on the damp stone. But Phipps spoke to him constantly, a low murmur of encouragement and nonsense, and at last they were down in the gloom.

The cellar was cavernous, its ceiling lost in shadow. The air was cool and thick with the smell of damp earth and dust. A single, grimy window, high up in the wall, let in a shaft of weak, grey light. Phipps tethered Jupiter to a stout iron pillar. He found an old horse blanket in a corner and threw it over the animal’s back. Then he sat down on an empty crate, his back against the pillar, and listened.

Above, the sounds of the city were muffled and strange. He heard a distant crack of a rifle, then another. Shouts. The rumble of a heavy wagon with no horse to draw it, men pushing it, their voices strained with effort. The great metropolis, that marvel of the age, was coming apart at the seams because its sinews had failed. All the cleverness of mankind, the steam engines and the electric lights and the telegraph wires, was as nothing without the patient, powerful creatures that had, until this morning, drawn its loads and carried its people.

He looked at Jupiter, who had lowered his great head and was sniffing at the dusty floor. The horse seemed calmer now, lulled by the darkness and the familiar presence of his master. Phipps reached into his coat pocket and pulled out a slightly squashed apple. He held it out on the palm of his hand. Jupiter’s soft, velvety lips tickled his skin as he took it.

For Phipps, there was no grand theory about the madness, no talk of microbes or miasmas as the newspapers would soon have it. There was only this: a man and his horse in a cellar, while the world above went mad. The cabman’s life was a hard one, a life of cold and wet and the endless indifference of the public, but it had its certainties. One of them was the creature before him. He would not give him up. Not for the police, not for the Board of Agriculture, not for the whole of London. Let them have their chaos. He would keep his small, sane world down here in the dark.

He patted Jupiter’s neck again, the coarse hair warm beneath his hand. “Right then, old son,” he whispered to the darkness. “We’ll just wait it out. We’ll just wait it out together.” And in the profound silence of the cellar, broken only by the horse’s breathing and the distant, frantic cries of a city losing its mind, Albert Phipps began his vigil.
