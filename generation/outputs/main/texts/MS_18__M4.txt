I had thought that the fairest climes of Italy might restore to my spirit somewhat of that peace which had been denied me in my native land, and that the contemplation of her ancient glories, her palaces and her ruins, might serve to lift my thoughts from the private sorrow that had so long oppressed them. Yet I have learned, in the bitterness of experience, that the mind which is once diseased carries its own climate with it, and that no change of scene can cure a heart which is the author of its own misery. It was in the autumn of the year 182— that we came to Milan, my father, my sister, and myself, and took up our abode in a modest lodging near the Porta Orientale, whence we could see, at evening, the distant peaks of the Alps, crowned with eternal snows, and glowing with the last light of the sun. I was then in my two-and-twentieth year, and I had known enough of grief to make me serious beyond my years, though not enough, alas, to arm me against the passion which was soon to become the tyrant of my existence.

It was at the house of a certain Countess Malvezzi, a lady of ancient family and liberal opinions, that I first beheld Lorenzo Aldini. The recollection of that evening is even now, after so many years, indelibly impressed upon my memory. The salons of the Countess were adorned with the choicest productions of Italian art, and the conversation, which was animated and eloquent, turned upon the wrongs of our unhappy country, and the hopes, still cherished in secret, of her deliverance from the yoke of the stranger. In the midst of this company I remarked a young man of a noble and melancholy countenance, whose dark eyes, kindling with an enthusiasm that seemed to consume him, and whose voice, now tremulous with emotion, now swelling with a lofty indignation, commanded the attention of all. He spoke of the ancient liberties of Italy, of the great men who had shed their blood for her, and of the sacred duty of every son to labour for her regeneration; and as he spoke, I felt my heart burn within me, and I forgot, for the moment, that I was a stranger and an alien, and that the cause of which he discoursed was not my own.

From that night Lorenzo Aldini sought my society with an ardour which I was too inexperienced to discourage, and too ingenuous to conceal. We walked together in the public gardens, beneath the shade of the acacias and the plane-trees; we visited the vast and gloomy cathedral, whose pinnacles seem to pierce the very heavens; and we stood side by side upon the ramparts, watching the sun go down behind the distant Apennines, and speaking, in low and earnest tones, of those high and perilous designs which filled his soul. He told me that he was the last of a family which had been impoverished and proscribed for its devotion to the cause of freedom; that he had devoted his life to the deliverance of his country; and that he knew not what fate awaited him, but that he was prepared to meet it, whether in the dungeon or upon the scaffold. I listened, and I loved. I loved him for his enthusiasm, for his courage, for the very desperation of his fortunes; and I dared to hope that my affection might be to him a solace in his trials, and a reward for his sacrifices.

But my father, who was a man of cautious temper and of strict, though not illiberal, principles, soon observed the intimacy which had sprung up between us, and, having made inquiries concerning the character and designs of Lorenzo, he commanded me, in terms which admitted of no dispute, to renounce the acquaintance. He represented to me the madness, the guilt, and the inevitable ruin of all such enterprises; he painted in the most alarming colours the fate which must attend a young man who had engaged in a conspiracy against a power so vigilant and so relentless as that of Austria; and he bade me reflect upon the dishonour which would attach to me, and to my family, if I were to link my destiny with one who was already, perhaps, marked out for destruction. I wept, I entreated, I pleaded the worth and the misfortunes of Lorenzo; but my father was inexorable. He forbade me to see him, to write to him, or to hold any communication with him; and I, who had been accustomed from childhood to yield an implicit obedience to his will, dared not disobey. I wrote to Lorenzo a letter, bathed in tears, in which I acquainted him with the commands I had received, and bade him an eternal farewell; and I implored him, by the love he bore me, to abandon his perilous designs, and to seek safety in flight.

I received no answer to my letter, and for some weeks I remained in a state of the most agonizing suspense. I knew not whether Lorenzo had received my communication, or whether he had disdained to reply to it; and my heart was torn by a thousand conflicting emotions. At length, one evening, as I sat at the window of our lodging, watching the moon rise over the spires of the city, my sister entered the room, pale and trembling, and informed me that Lorenzo Aldini had been arrested, with several of his associates, and was confined in the fortress of the Castello, whence few, if any, had ever returned.

The intelligence fell upon me like a thunderbolt. I was for a time deprived of speech and motion; and when at length the power of weeping returned, I threw myself upon my knees, and prayed, with an earnestness which I had never before felt, that the life of him whom I loved might be preserved. I recalled to mind all the noble qualities of Lorenzo, his high spirit, his disinterested patriotism, his tenderness for me; and I shuddered to think that a being so gifted, so generous, so full of promise, should be cut off in the flower of his age, and consigned to a dungeon or a scaffold, by the cruelty of a foreign tyrant. I reflected, also, with a bitterness which I cannot describe, that it was perhaps my own letter which had betrayed him; that my father, in his anxiety for my safety, might have communicated with the police; and that I had thus become, however innocently, the cause of his destruction.

In this extremity I formed a resolution which, I am aware, will appear to many to have been inconsistent with the duty which I owed to my father, and with that modesty which is the ornament of my sex. I determined to go myself to the Governor of the city, to throw myself at his feet, and to implore the pardon of Lorenzo. I knew that the attempt was desperate; that I, a young and unprotected female, could have but little influence with a man hardened in the exercise of arbitrary power; and that I might even expose myself to insult and to danger. But I felt that I could not be guilty of a greater crime by any act of imprudence than by suffering the man whom I loved to perish, without an effort to save him. I therefore, without the knowledge of my father, and attended only by my faithful servant, an old and trusty domestic who had accompanied us from England, presented myself at the palace of the Governor, and demanded an audience.

I was admitted into a vast and gloomy apartment, where the Governor, a man of a stern and haughty aspect, sat surrounded by his secretaries. I threw myself at his feet; I besought him, with all the eloquence which despair could inspire, to spare the life of Lorenzo Aldini; I represented to him his youth, his talents, his noble birth, the services which his family had formerly rendered to the state; I implored him, by all that he held sacred, by his own hopes of happiness here and hereafter, not to stain his hands with the blood of a man whose only crime was that he loved his country too well. The Governor listened to me with an air of cold and contemptuous indifference, and, when I had ceased, he replied, in terms of studied politeness, that he was not accustomed to interfere with the course of justice; that the prisoner had been guilty of a crime of the deepest dye; and that his fate was already determined. He then dismissed me, with a hint that I would do well to confine my sorrows to my own bosom, and to remember that the solicitude which I had manifested for a state criminal might itself be construed into a proof of complicity.

I returned to my lodging in a state of mind bordering upon distraction. I had failed in my attempt; the last hope of saving Lorenzo was extinguished; and I was now compelled to endure the additional torture of reflecting that my rash and unauthorized step might have compromised my father, and involved my whole family in ruin. I passed the night in an agony of tears and self-reproach; and when the morning dawned, I arose, pale and exhausted, to learn the fate of him for whom I had ventured so much. The intelligence which awaited me was such as I had but too much reason to apprehend. Lorenzo Aldini, with two of his associates, had been condemned to death, and the sentence was to be executed on the following day.

I have no words to describe the feelings which overwhelmed me when I received this fatal news. I felt as if the last tie which bound me to existence was about to be severed; as if the sun himself would be extinguished, and the world become a blank and a desolation. I thought of Lorenzo, in his dungeon, awaiting the dawn of that fatal day; of his youth, his genius, his love for me; of all that he might have been, and of all that he was about to suffer; and I prayed, in the wildness of my despair, that I might be permitted to exchange places with him, and to die in his stead. But the decree was irrevocable; and I could only weep, and pray, and wait, in the bitterness of my soul, for the hour which was to consummate my misery.
