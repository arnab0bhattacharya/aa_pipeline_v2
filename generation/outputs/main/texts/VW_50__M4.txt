The letter came at breakfast, and Mrs. Ashworth, holding it against the teapot’s steam as if the warmth might thaw its news, thought how thin the envelope was, thinner than the others, and how the ink had run a little at the corner where the censor’s stamp had come down. Gerald was at the front—she never said *somewhere in France*, she said *at the front*, as though there were only one, a single muddy acre where her son stood—and his letters had grown shorter, more like telegrams, more like the notes a man leaves for the servants. *Dear Mother, I am well. The weather is foul. Give my love to Father and to Nora.* That was all. She read it twice, three times, and then she did what she always did: she looked out of the window at the plane tree, at the sparrows quarrelling in the wet leaves, and she thought, with a violence that startled her, *I cannot bear another winter of this.*

In the hospital at Rouen, Nora was folding a man’s shirt—not his shirt, the hospital’s shirt, stiff with carbolic—and thinking that she had not slept properly in nine months, and that the war had done something to her hands, made them older, redder, a nurse’s hands, and that when she looked at them she could not remember what they had looked like before. She had written to her mother that morning, a cheerful letter, a letter full of the funny things the orderlies said, because one did not write the truth from a hospital; the truth was a man of twenty-three with his jaw shot away, and the truth was that she had held his hand while he died and had felt nothing, nothing at all, and had gone to her supper afterwards and eaten it. *You are so brave,* her mother wrote back. *We are so proud of you.* And Nora, reading it under the dim bulb of the sisters’ sitting room, had wanted to tear it into pieces, and had folded it instead, and put it in her apron, and gone to see whether the boy in bed nine had taken his beef tea.

Mr. Ashworth, in his office in the City, where the light fell grey through the tall windows and the clerks bent over their ledgers as though the war were a rumour from another planet, kept his son’s letters in the left-hand drawer, under the box of rubber bands. He did not read them often. He read them once, quickly, and then he put them away, because there was work to be done, because the firm had lost two of its young men already and he had taken on a boy of seventeen who could not add, because at fifty-eight one discovered that grief was a thing one carried about with one like a stone in the pocket, and that the stone did not prevent one from signing one’s name. Sometimes, at the end of the day, he would take out the last letter and hold it, not reading, only holding, and think of Gerald at seven, in a sailor suit, on the beach at Whitstable, digging with a wooden spade. Then he would put the letter back and lock the drawer and take his hat from the peg.

They met, the four of them, in the December of 1916—Gerald on leave, with a wound in the shoulder that was healing and a look in his eyes that his mother could not name; Nora home for a fortnight, thin, brisk, with a way of finishing other people’s sentences that set Mrs. Ashworth’s teeth on edge; and the house, the house in Highgate with the plane tree and the good china, seemed to shrink around them, seemed to hold them too closely, as though it had forgotten how to breathe. They sat at dinner. The soup was hot. The fire crackled. And no one could think of anything to say.

“How long,” said Mrs. Ashworth at last, “how long have you got, dear?”

“A week,” said Gerald. “Six days.”

“And then back to—?”

“Back to the mud,” he said, and smiled, and his mother saw that his smile had changed, had become a thing he put on and took off, like a cap.

Nora said, “The mud isn’t so bad. It’s the lice.”

“Nora,” said Mrs. Ashworth.

“Well, it is. One gets used to the mud.”

“One gets used to anything,” said Gerald, and for a moment their eyes met across the table, and Mrs. Ashworth, watching them, felt a door close somewhere, a door she had not known was open. They had a language now, these two, a language of lice and mud and beef tea, and she was not admitted. She was the mother. She was the one who stayed at home and knitted and wrote cheerful letters and did not know.

“Your father,” she said, “has been made a partner in the—the thing. The thing with the—”

“The amalgamation,” said Mr. Ashworth. “It’s nothing, really. A matter of papers.”

“It’s not nothing,” said Mrs. Ashworth. “It’s a great honour.”

Gerald said, “I’m glad, Father.” And Mr. Ashworth said, “Thank you, my boy.” And then the silence came down again, the awful silence of people who love one another and have nothing left to say, and the fire spat, and the clock on the mantelpiece—the clock that had been a wedding present, that had ticked through measles and Latin verbs and the first long trousers—ticked on, indifferent, measuring out the seconds of a week that would not come again.

It was after dinner, when the women had gone up and the men sat over their port, that Mr. Ashworth said, “Your mother worries about you.”

“I know,” said Gerald.

“We all do. But she—she feels it more.”

Gerald turned his glass. “There’s nothing to feel, Father. I’m all right. I’m one of the lucky ones.”

“Yes,” said Mr. Ashworth. “Yes. Of course.” And he wanted to say something more, something about the beach at Whitstable, about the wooden spade, about how proud he was, how terribly proud, but the words would not come, and so he said, “Have another glass,” and Gerald said, “I won’t, if you don’t mind,” and they sat in silence until it was decent to go up.

The war ended in November, and the bells rang, and Mrs. Ashworth stood at the window and wept, and Mr. Ashworth came home early and said, “Well. Well, well,” and they had a glass of sherry together, and it was over. Gerald came home in the spring, demobilised, thin and quiet, with a civilian suit that did not fit him and a habit of smoking in the garden. Nora came home in the summer, and took a room in town, and said she was going to train as a masseuse, and Mrs. Ashworth said, “But you’ve only just come back,” and Nora said, “I’ve been away four years, Mother. I don’t think I can come back.” And so there were three of them at dinner now, and then two, and then—when Gerald took a post in the north, in Leeds, in a shipping office, and wrote once a month—two who sat at the long table and looked at the empty chairs and found that they had become, without noticing, a couple again, an old couple, with their habits and their silences and their memories that did not quite meet.

One evening in October, Mrs. Ashworth took out the bundle of Gerald’s letters and read them, one by one, from the beginning. *Dear Mother, I am well. The weather is foul.* She read them all, and then she sat very still, and the plane tree moved in the wind outside, and she thought: *We have all come through, and we have not come through. We are here, and we are not here. We are strangers, and we are the same people who sat at this table in 1913, and I do not know what has happened, only that something has happened, and that it cannot be undone.* And she put the letters back in the drawer, and went to see about the supper, because the supper would not see about itself, and the clock on the mantelpiece ticked on, indifferent, measuring out the seconds of a life that would not come again.
