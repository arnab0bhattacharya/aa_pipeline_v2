It was a night of such profound stillness that even the stars seemed to hold their breath, suspended in the velvet canopy like diamonds set in onyx. In the gilded chambers of the Palace of Aurelia, where the air was perfumed with jasmine and the silence was bought with gold, the Duke’s heir was born. Simultaneously, in a damp cellar beneath the tanner’s quarter, where the smell of lye and rotting straw hung heavy in the air, the son of a beggar entered the world. The moon, that great pale eye which watches all human vanity, looked down upon both cradles with an expression that might have been pity, or perhaps mere amusement.

At that hour, the Fairy of Fates, dressed in robes of twilight purple and wearing a crown of woven spider-silk, descended from the clouds. She carried two gifts, wrapped in the thin, translucent skin of an egg. To the rich child, she gave Beauty. It was a terrible beauty, of the kind that stops the heart and turns the blood to ice; it was the beauty of a marble statue, flawless, cold, and utterly devoid of warmth. To the poor child, she gave a Kind Heart. It was a humble gift, invisible to the eye, beating with a slow, steady rhythm that cared nothing for rank or reward, only for the alleviation of another’s suffering.

Twenty years passed like the turning of a great wheel. The rich son, Lord Valerius, grew into a youth of such dazzling aspect that the women of the city whispered his name as if it were a prayer and a curse combined. He moved through the streets of Aurelia as a comet moves through the sky—brilliant, fast, and leaving only a trail of ash in his wake. He knew that he was beautiful, and he used this knowledge with the precision of a surgeon. He understood that beauty is the only talent that is never wasted, yet he squandered it on the most trivial of things: the arrangement of his hair, the shade of his silk, the angle of his chin. He believed that because he was beautiful, he was also wise, a common error of the vain, who mistake their reflection for their soul.

The poor son, whom the city knew only as Elias, grew in obscurity. He worked in the gardens of the wealthy, pruning roses and weeding paths. He was plain of face, with eyes that held the dull, patient sadness of a winter lake. But when a bird fell from its nest, he caught it; when a flower wilted in the heat, he watered it. He did not ask for thanks, nor did he expect it. He knew, with the quiet certainty of the poor, that kindness is the only currency that does not devalue with inflation.

On a spring evening, when the air was sweet with the scent of orange blossoms and the sun was setting behind the hills in a blaze of crimson and gold, the two young men met at the palace gate. It was a chance encounter, or so it seemed, for in a city of fates, chance is merely another word for destiny.

Valerius was leaning against the black iron gate, adjusting his gloves, which were white as the milk of a doe. He watched the crowd below with the detached interest of a man observing ants. Elias was walking past, carrying a basket of apples he had gathered for the palace kitchen. The apples were red and round, and they looked, in the fading light, like small hearts.

Valerius turned his head. His eyes, blue as the sea before a storm, fixed upon Elias. There was a moment of silence, a vacuum in the air where sound went to die.

“You are new here,” Valerius said. His voice was soft, like the rustling of leaves, but it carried the weight of a command. “I have never seen you in the gardens before.”

Elias stopped. He looked up, not with fear, but with a gentle curiosity. “I have been here for many years, my lord. Perhaps you have not looked at the workers before.”

Valerius smiled. It was a beautiful smile, sharp as a blade and cold as the snow. “I look at everyone, I assure you. But some are worth looking at twice. You have a strange face. It is not handsome, but it is... memorable. There is a quality of sadness in it that I find quite charming. Do you know that sadness is the only emotion that is true? Joy is a lie told by the body, but sadness is the truth of the soul.”

Elias tilted his head. “I suppose so. But I find that joy is also true, when it is shared. A single drop of water is just a drop, but when it joins the stream, it becomes part of the sea.”

Valerius laughed, a low, musical sound. “You speak in riddles. It is a common trait among the poor; they use words to hide their poverty. Tell me, what is your name?”

“Elias,” the young man said simply.

“Elias,” Valerius repeated, tasting the name. “A biblical name. It suggests a history of suffering. I like it. It fits the drabness of your clothes.”

Elias did not flinch. He looked at Valerius, and for the first time, the rich young man felt a strange unease. It was not fear, but a sense of being seen, of being stripped of his silks and jewels and left naked before a mirror that did not lie.

“And you?” Elias asked. “What is your name?”

“Valerius,” the rich man said, with a bow that was more a performance than a courtesy. “But that is of no importance. Names are just labels we put on things to make them seem real. You are the gardener, I am the master. That is all that matters.”

“Is it?” Elias asked. “I think it matters more that you are the one who can give, and I am the one who can receive. But who is richer, the one who has the apple, or the one who has the hunger to eat it?”

Valerius’s smile faltered. For a second, a shadow crossed his face, a flicker of something ancient and weary. He looked at Elias, really looked at him, and saw not a beggar, but a man. He saw the kindness in the other’s eyes, a kindness that was so profound it seemed to glow like a lantern in the dark.

“You are dangerous,” Valerius whispered. “You look at me as if you know what I am. You do not see the beauty, you see the emptiness.”

“I see a man who is very tired,” Elias said softly. “Beauty is a heavy burden, my lord. It is a mask that cannot be removed. I am only a man with a heart that beats. It is not much, but it is enough.”

Valerius straightened his spine. The mask of vanity slipped back into place, smooth and flawless. “Enough,” he said, his voice turning icy again. “You are not enough for me. I need grandeur, I need passion, I need fire. You are water. Water is good for washing, but it cannot warm the soul.”

He turned on his heel and walked into the palace, the doors closing behind him with a finality that echoed like a gunshot.

Elias stood alone in the street. The sun had set, and the first stars were appearing in the sky. He looked at the closed doors of the palace, then down at the basket of apples in his hands. He picked up one, red and perfect, and ate it. It was sweet, and it was warm.

He walked away, into the shadows, carrying his small, invisible treasure. He did not know that he had won the day, nor did he care. He knew only that the heart he had been given was still beating, steady and sure, like a drum in a distant land, keeping time with the pulse of the world. And in the palace, behind the velvet curtains, Valerius sat alone in the dark, staring at his reflection in the mirror, wondering why the face that looked back at him seemed so far away, and so terribly alone.
