In that forgotten hollow of the earth’s crust, known to the maps of surveyors and the ledgers of the wealthy as the village of Slaterton-in-the-Bog, the smoke hung thicker than a London fog, and the soot settled upon the faces of the living as if to prepare them early for the shroud. It was a place where the sun was a rumor, where the pit-wheels turned with a melancholy screech, and where the great engines puffed and snorted like dragons of an unwholesome mythology. The cottages, built of a brick so blackened that it seemed to have been steeped in the very essence of despair, huddled together in rows, as if for warmth against the biting winds that swept across the Durham moors.

In one of these cottages, Mrs. Pippin sat by the dying embers of a fire, her face drawn and pale, her hands clasped in her lap, and her eyes fixed upon a pallet in the corner of the room, where her son, Silas, a boy of eleven, lay groaning. Silas was a pithead boy, one of those small, soot-stained creatures who sit at the mouth of the mine, sorting coal, and whose fingers, by the time they reach the age of ten, are as calloused as the hoofs of a beast of burden. He had been at his post on the previous day, perched upon his sorter’s bench, when a great iron chain, snapping with a report like a pistol-shot, had sent a cascade of coal and machinery down upon him. He had been dragged from beneath the ruin, senseless and bleeding, with a leg bent at an angle that made the strongest of the pitmen turn away their heads.

Mrs. Pippin was a widow. Her husband, Tom Pippin, had been killed in the very same pit three years before, when a fall of the roof had crushed him as flat as a tallow candle beneath a drayman’s wheel. Since that time, she had existed upon the earnings of Silas, supplemented by such charity as the parish might extend, which was never much, and seldom given without a lecture upon the moral duty of industry. She was a thin woman, with the look of one who had been dried out by grief and hard fare, and whose tears, had she any left to shed, would have been as acrid as the smoke that drifted through her broken window-pane.

She was roused from her miserable reverie by a heavy step upon the threshold, and looked up to see Mr. Grout, the pit agent, standing in the doorway. Mr. Grout was a man of substance, which is to say, he was stout, red-faced, and wore a greatcoat with a velvet collar. He carried a cane, which he used not for walking, but for emphasis, and he had a manner of speaking through his nose, as if the air he drew in were too precious to be wasted upon the common purposes of speech. Behind him came a thin, furtive gentleman in spectacles, carrying a leather portfolio, who was, as Mrs. Pippin knew from bitter experience, a creature of Mr. Grout’s, employed to write down whatever Mr. Grout wished to have written, and to see that it was binding.

"Good morning, Mrs. Pippin," said Mr. Grout, entering without invitation, and seating himself upon the only chair in the room that was not broken. "A sad business, this. A very sad business. How is the boy?"

"He’s bad, sir," said Mrs. Pippin, in a voice that trembled. "The surgeon says the leg’s broke in two places, and—"

"Tut, tut," interrupted Mr. Grout, waving his cane as if to dismiss the surgeon’s opinion along with all other inconvenient facts. "Boys mend. Boys always mend. I’ve seen boys in the pit with worse hurts than that, and they’ve lived to be old men. The young are remarkably resilient, Mrs. Pippin. It is one of the mercies of Providence."

He paused, and looked about the room, as if taking an inventory of its contents, which might have been done in a very few seconds, there being nothing to inventory except a table, three chairs, a fractured looking-glass, and a kettle.

"Now, Mrs. Pippin," he continued, drawing a paper from the portfolio which the thin gentleman had opened upon the table, "I am come from Sir Jasper Muckleton, the proprietor of the colliery. Sir Jasper is a humane man, Mrs. Pippin—a most humane man. He has been deeply affected—deeply affected—by the account of your son’s misfortune. He desires me to express his sympathy, and to offer you this."

He held up a bank-note, which fluttered in the draught from the broken window like a white flag of surrender.

"Five pounds," said Mr. Grout, with the air of one announcing a legacy. "Five pounds, Mrs. Pippin. It is a handsome sum. It is more than the boy would earn in half a year. Sir Jasper wishes you to have it, as a token of his regard, and in consideration of the accident. All you need do is to sign this paper, which acknowledges the receipt of the money, and agrees that the matter is settled—quite settled—between you and the colliery. There, you see? Nothing could be simpler."

Mrs. Pippin looked at the note, and then at the paper, and then at Silas, who had opened his eyes and was staring at the ceiling with the glassy, unseeing stare of a creature in pain. She put out her hand toward the note, and then drew it back, as if the paper upon the table had been a snake coiled upon the board.

"I don’t know, sir," she said. "I can’t read. What does it say, exact?"

"It says what I have told you, my good woman," said Mr. Grout, with a patience that was obviously costing him a great deal. "It says that you have received five pounds, and that you are satisfied. Sign it, and let us have done."

It was at this juncture that the door, which had been left ajar, was pushed open with a creaking protestation of its hinges, and there entered a young man, very thin, very pale, and very earnest, wearing a black coat that was threadbare at the elbows, and a cravat that appeared to have been tied in a spirit of defiance rather than neatness. This was Mr. Ebenezer Tetch, the chapel preacher, a man of two-and-twenty, who had come to Slaterton from a theological college in York, and who had brought with him a conscience as troublesome as a sore tooth, and a habit of saying things that nobody wished to hear.

"I beg your pardon," said Mr. Tetch, standing in the doorway with his hat in his hand, and his eyes travelling from Mrs. Pippin to Mr. Grout, and from Mr. Grout to the bank-note, and from the bank-note to the paper, with a rapidity that suggested he had already divined the nature of the transaction. "I heard of the accident, and I came to see if I could be of any assistance. I perceive that I am in time."

"You are in the way, sir," said Mr. Grout, rising from his chair with an air of considerable hauteur. "This is a private matter—a private matter between Mrs. Pippin and the colliery. I must ask you to—"

"I shall not go," said Mr. Tetch, quietly, and sat down upon the broken chair nearest the door, as if to establish his presence by the act of sitting. "Mrs. Pippin, do not sign that paper."

Mr. Grout’s face, which had been red, became a shade purple. "Sir!" he exclaimed. "You are interfering in a matter that does not concern you. I am Sir Jasper Muckleton’s agent, and I am here upon Sir Jasper’s business. The woman is to sign the paper, and there is an end of it."

"What is the paper?" asked Mr. Tetch, turning to Mrs. Pippin, and ignoring Mr. Grout as completely as if that gentleman had been a piece of furniture. "Mrs. Pippin, I beg you to tell me what you understand it to be."

"They say it’s a receipt," she faltered. "For the five pounds. Sir Jasper’s agent says it’s a token of regard."

"A token of regard!" repeated Mr. Tetch, with a bitter smile. "Mrs. Pippin, it is a release. It is a document which, when you sign it, will bar you from any further claim upon the colliery for your son’s injury. It will mean that Sir Jasper Muckleton, whose machinery was faulty, whose chain was worn and rusted, whose neglect has brought your boy to that pallet, may go free, and that you and Silas will have nothing—nothing but those five pounds, which will be spent before the winter is out, and then you will be where you were before, only with a crippled boy and no way to feed him."

"Upon my honour," said Mr. Grout, turning to the thin gentleman with a look of aggrieved innocence, "I have never heard such a tissue of impertinence. The chain was not faulty. The chain was sound. It was an act of God."

"It was an act of Sir Jasper Muckleton," said Mr. Tetch, firmly. "And I will prove it, if Mrs. Pippin will have me."

Mr. Grout drew himself up to his full height, which was considerable, and looked down upon the young preacher with an expression that was intended to be terrible.

"Mr. Tetch," he said, in a voice that was low and dangerous, or as low and dangerous as a voice can be that speaks through the nose, "you are a clergyman, or something of the sort. I would advise you to confine yourself to your prayers, and to leave the affairs of the colliery to those who understand them. I would advise you, further, to remember that the cottage in which Mrs. Pippin resides belongs to Sir Jasper Muckleton, and that Sir Jasper Muckleton is not a man to tolerate interference. If Mrs. Pippin does not sign that paper, I cannot answer for the consequences. The cottage may be required. There are many who would be glad of it."

"You hear that, Mrs. Pippin?" said Mr. Tetch. "If you sign, you get five pounds and a crippled boy. If you do not sign, you get no pounds, a crippled boy, and no roof over your head. Such is the generosity of Sir Jasper Muckleton. Such is the humanity of the man who is, as his agent says, deeply affected."

Mrs. Pippin looked from one to the other, and then at Silas, who had turned his head upon the pillow and was watching the scene with eyes that were wide and fearful. She wrung her hands together, and the tears, which she had thought were long since dried up, came at last, and ran down her thin cheeks, cutting channels through the soot that had settled there.

"I can’t," she whispered. "I can’t sign it, sir. Not if you turn me out into the road. My boy’s hurt, and it wasn’t his fault, and it wasn’t God’s fault, and I won’t say it was."
