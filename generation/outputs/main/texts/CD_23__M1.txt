In the year of grace one thousand seven hundred and ninety-two, in that most fashionable of London’s districts, Mayfair, there existed a temple dedicated to the god of hair, and its high priest was Monsieur Henri de la Fontaine. This establishment, which bore the rather grandiloquent name of “La Coiffure des Dieux,” was a marvel of mirrors and mahogany, of scented pomades in crystal jars and silver-backed brushes that gleamed like captured moons. And Monsieur de la Fontaine himself was no less a marvel. He was a man of slender build and exquisite posture, with a face as pale and finely drawn as a poet’s sonnet and hands that moved with the swiftness of sparrows and the delicacy of angels. He had come to England from a small town in Normandy with nothing but a pair of shears and a soul overflowing with flattery, and he had built his empire upon the powdered heads of the aristocracy. His genius lay not merely in the architectural marvels he wrought upon human hair—those towering edifices of curls, those powdered clouds that defied gravity and reason—but in his uncanny ability to soothe the vanity of the great. To the Duke of Abernathy, whose nose was a veritable eagle’s beak, he would murmur, “Ah, your Grace, a nose of such noble character! It gives the face its magnificent profile, like a Roman emperor.” To the Countess of Wexford, whose chin receded as if in modest retreat from the world, he would sigh, “But Madame, such delicacy! Such refinement! It speaks of a gentle spirit, too fine for this coarse age.” And so, with his scissors and his silken tongue, he had trimmed and teased his way into the very heart of the fashionable world, amassing a fortune that clinked in a strongbox beneath his bed and a reputation as the indispensable artist to the ton.

His shop, on a Tuesday morning, was a veritable salon of whispers and rustling silks. The air was thick with the scent of lavender and bergamot, a cloying sweetness that clung to the velvet curtains and seemed to emanate from the very walls. Lady Seraphina, a creature of such ethereal lightness that one feared a sudden draught might carry her to the heavens, was having her morning’s coiffure attended to. “Oh, Monsieur Henri,” she trilled, her voice like the tinkling of tiny bells, “they say in Paris the heads are simply tumbling off! One can scarcely keep track of them all! The guillotine, they call it. Is it not the most dreadful thing?”

Monsieur de la Fontaine, whose fingers were engaged in the delicate business of pinning a single, perfect rosebud into a cascade of powdered hair, did not falter. “Madame,” he said, his voice a smooth, calming balm, “we must not allow the follies of the French to disturb the serenity of the English. It is a storm in a teacup, a tantrum of the mob. Here, in London, we have order, we have reason, we have… hair.” He pronounced the last word with a reverence usually reserved for the Deity, and Lady Seraphina was duly mollified.

It was at this precise moment of tranquil artifice that the bell above the door jangled with an unceremonious violence that made every powdered head in the room turn. A boy, grimy with the soot and mire of the city, stood there, holding a letter. “For Monsieur Henri de la Fontaine!” he shouted, as if the name were a proclamation of war. The name, so usually pronounced with a soft, French flourish by the clientele, sounded brutally plain and foreign on the boy’s tongue. Henri felt a prickle of unease. He took the letter, its paper coarse and cheap, a stark contrast to the vellum and gilt of his usual correspondence. The handwriting was a familiar scrawl, urgent and jagged.

He retreated to a small back room, leaving his aristocratic flock to wonder at this sudden intrusion of the real world. His hands, so steady with a curling iron, trembled as he broke the seal. The letter was from his brother, Luc. His younger brother, Luc, whom he had left behind in their sleepy home town of Beaumont-sur-Mer, a boy with a poet’s soul and a revolutionary’s heart.

*Henri,* it began, without ceremony or affection.

*The great work proceeds. The aristocrats tremble in their chateaux, for the people have found their voice, and it is a voice of thunder! We are building a new world upon the ruins of the old, a world of liberty, equality, fraternity! But a revolution, like any great engine, requires fuel. It requires coin. You, my brother, have grown rich by flattering the very parasites we seek to crush. Do not think I do not know of your ‘Coiffure des Dieux.’ I laugh at its pretension. But I do not laugh at its profits. Send money. Five hundred louis d’or. It is not for me, but for the cause. For the new dawn that is breaking over France. Fail us, and you will find yourself on the wrong side of history. The people have long memories, and they are not fond of those who dance with their oppressors.*

*Your brother,*
*Lucien de la Fontaine,*
*President of the Jacobin Club of Beaumont-sur-Mer.*

Henri sank onto a stool, the letter falling from his nerveless fingers. Five hundred louis d’or! It was a king’s ransom, the very sum he had been saving to purchase a small estate in the country, away from the cloying perfumes and endless chatter. But it was the signature that chilled him to the bone. President of the Jacobin Club. His gentle, bookish brother, a leader of the very men who were sending heads rolling in the Place de la Révolution.

He returned to the salon in a daze. The world had changed. The gentle cooing of Lady Seraphina now sounded like the shrieking of a doomed aristocrat. The admiring glances of the Duke of Abernathy, who was now in the chair, felt like the suspicious stares of a jury. “A fine day, Monsieur,” the Duke said, his eyes narrowed. “Though the news from abroad is… unsettling. One hears of Frenchmen, even here in London, with dangerous sympathies. One must be careful who one trusts.”

Henri’s hand, as he raised a comb, gave a slight, almost imperceptible twitch. “Indeed, your Grace,” he murmured, his voice suddenly thin. “One must always be careful.” He felt their eyes upon him, no longer seeing the artist, the master coiffeur, but a foreigner, a Frenchman, a man whose brother might be sharpening a blade. The mirror before him, which had always reflected his own proud and prosperous image, now seemed to show a stranger, a man caught between two worlds, a powdered wig sitting uneasily upon a head that might, any day now, be parted from its shoulders. The sweet scent of the pomades turned sour in his nostrils, and the gentle rustle of silk sounded like the whisper of the tumbril, rolling ever closer.
