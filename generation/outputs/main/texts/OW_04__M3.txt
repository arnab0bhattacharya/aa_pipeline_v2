It is a curious thing, but the only people who are entirely without secrets are those who have nothing worth concealing. The Duchess of Harley, who was a woman of much wisdom and fewer scruples, had known this for a great many years. She lived in the pale and perfect house at the north corner of Grosvenor Square, and her dinners were the only events in London where the conversation was more carefully prepared than the cuisine. 

She had grown weary of the ordinary gatherings of the season, where one is invariably seated next to someone whose ancestors one has already insulted, or whose novels one has failed to read. "Society," she had remarked to Lord Amberdale, whose sole virtue was his capacity to listen, "is merely a vast conspiracy of acquaintances to prevent us from making any new friends. To be entirely familiar with a person is the first step toward finding them completely intolerable."

Thus it came about that she resolved to give a dinner of perfect strangers. She sent out cards of invitation to four persons, none of whom had, to her certain knowledge, ever exchanged a single word with any of the others. 

The first to arrive was the Right Reverend Cyprian Vance, Bishop of Bexhill, a man whose silver hair and melancholy eyes gave him the appearance of a saint who had grown somewhat weary of his miracles. The second was Mrs. Vane, an actress whose beauty was a matter of public debate in every smoking-room in London, and whose private life was a matter of public knowledge. Then came Captain Reginald Thorne, an explorer who had recently returned from the interior of Africa, bringing with him a sunburnt complexion, a beautiful collection of Benin bronzes, and a total inability to sleep before dawn. The last was Mr. Julian Ashe, a very young poet who had just published a book of verse so scandalous that no respectable bookshop would display it in the windows, and no unrespectable bookshop could keep it in stock.

They assembled in the pale green drawing-room, eyeing one another with the cautious delight of wild beasts meeting at a waterhole, uncertain whether to drink or to devour. 

"I am told," said the Bishop, addressing the actress with a gentle smile, "that the modern stage is a powerful force for moral instruction. I confess I have rarely had the leisure to attend the theatre. It is a great misfortune, for I am told your own performances are quite irresistible."

"Your lordship is very kind," she answered. "But I fear the stage is less a school of morals than a mirror of fashionable sins. We merely show the world the vices it already practises, and charge five shillings for the privilege of looking at them."

"A most just observation," murmured the explorer, who had been examining a small Egyptian statuette upon a mantelpiece. "In the wilds of the Congo, the natives worship a deity of fire. I have often thought we English worship a deity of respectability, which is far more demanding, and requires far greater sacrifices."

"Ah, but respectability is merely the mask that vanity wears when it wishes to be admired," said the young poet, who was dressed in a coat of an almost aggressive perfection. "Vanity, at least, is a sincere passion. Respectability is only a borrowed one."

"You are very young to be so cynical, Mr. Ashe," said the Bishop.

"I am young enough to believe what I say, your lordship. That is the penalty of genius. As one grows older, one merely repeats the errors of one's youth, and calls it experience."

At this moment dinner was announced, and the Duchess led the Bishop to the table, leaving the others to follow as Fate, or the hostess, decreed. 

It is a terrible thing to seat four strangers at a round table. The round table is the only truly democratic institution in England, for it allows no head, and consequently permits no one to feel superior to anyone else, which is the chief misery of the English temperament. 

By the time the second course, a magnificent turbot in a rich and fragrant sauce, had been placed before them, an extraordinary change had come over the company. The ice, which had been merely thin, had been broken entirely, and beneath it lay waters of a surprising depth. 

It was the explorer who first cast the stone. He had been staring intently at the Bishop, who was delicately separating a morsel of fish from the bone. 

"Your lordship," said Captain Thorne, "has a remarkably familiar air. I cannot rid myself of the notion that we have met before. But I am sure I should remember a face so distinguished."

"I have rarely travelled beyond my own diocese," replied the Bishop, with a faint sigh. "Unless it was in the South of France. I spent a winter in Mentone many years ago, for my health."

The explorer started. "It was in Mentone!" he cried. "At the Hotel de Paris. I was there with a shooting-party. But surely—forgive me—your lordship was not then in episcopal attire. You were, if I am not mistaken, in the company of a very beautiful woman, and you were weeping."

A profound silence fell upon the table. The Duchess, who was drinking a glass of champagne, did not spill a drop, though her eyes sparkled with a dangerous pleasure. 

"I was weeping," said the Bishop, very quietly, "because I had just learned that the woman I was to marry had betrayed me. I did not become a bishop until a month afterwards. It is an odd thing, Captain, how a broken heart often leads directly to the altar of God. The Church is the only institution that welcomes the utterly disillusioned."

"Then you know the pain of a secret exposed," said the poet, leaning forward with an eager, destructive joy. "I, too, have a secret. My book of poems, which society pretends to find so shocking, was not written by me at all. I wrote only the preface, which is a defence of immorality. The poems themselves were written by my grandmother, a lady of strict Puritan virtue, who left them to me in her will upon the condition that I should publish them under my own name. She felt it was time the family had a scandal, as they had been tediously respectable for four generations."

"How perfectly delightful!" exclaimed the actress. "I have always maintained that the only true artists are the middle-aged and the elderly, for they alone have suffered enough to know what art is. Youth is merely a form of bad taste."

"And yet, Mrs. Vane," said the explorer, turning to her with a sudden gravity, "is it not true that your own art has recently been financed by a gentleman whose collection of African antiquities is quite unmatched in London? I refer to my late employer, Sir Henry Marlow. I brought him the Benin bronzes myself. I was told you were his closest friend."

The actress did not change colour, though she smiled a little bitterly. "Sir Henry was a very generous patron of the drama," she said. "He paid for the production of my last play. I did not know he had sent you to Africa to gather bronzes. He told me he sent you to find a particular kind of yellow orchid."

"He sent me to find gold," said the explorer, with a short laugh. "But I found only fever, and a great many mosquitoes. It is the fate of explorers to seek one thing and find another. I returned with nothing but the bronzes, and Sir Henry, in a fit of disappointment, dismissed me. He then, it appears, spent the money he had saved on financing your play."

"Then we are all connected by a web of ironies," said the Bishop, looking round the table with a gentle melancholy. "I am a bishop because I was jilted; the poet is a scandal because he is the grandson of a saint; the actress is famous because an explorer failed to find gold; and the explorer is here because he brought back something he did not seek."

"It is exactly like a French farce," said the Duchess, "only with better conversation. But I must tell you that I, too, have a secret. I did not invite you here by chance. I knew every one of your histories before I sent out the cards. I wanted to see if strangers, when placed in a room together, would take longer to discover each other's secrets than friends do. Friends, of course, know everything already, and have spent years pretending they do not. Strangers, I find, are merely friends who have not yet had the time to be hypocritical."

"You are a very dangerous woman, Duchess," said the poet.

"I am merely a very bored one," she answered. "In London, to be bored is the supreme social sin, and to relieve it is the supreme social virtue. But now that we all know the worst about one another, we can begin to enjoy the dinner. The turbot is getting cold, and a cold turbot, like a discovered secret, is a very unappetising thing."

So they all laughed, and ate their turbot, and talked of other things. But the Bishop looked at the actress, and the explorer looked at the poet, and they all knew that the most exclusive dinner of the season was, in reality, the most intimate. For there is no bond so strong as the bond of shared disgrace, and no intimacy quite so perfect as the intimacy of strangers who have nothing left to hide.
