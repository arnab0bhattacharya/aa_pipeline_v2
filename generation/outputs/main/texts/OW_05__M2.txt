The town house in Belgravia possessed that particular quality of architectural indifference which is found only in structures that have survived too many generations of wealth. The walls, papered in a faded damask of deep burgundy, seemed to absorb the light rather than reflect it, while the air hung heavy with the scent of old beeswax and the faint, cloying perfume of decayed lilies. It was a house that did not breathe so much as it waited, holding its silence like a miser holds a coin, waiting for the right moment to spend it.

Lady Althea Montrose sat in the window seat, a figure of such exquisite and brittle elegance that one might have mistaken her for a sculpture of alabaster left too long in the rain. She was eighty-three, a fact that her face had long since ceased to acknowledge, for wrinkles are merely the lines that time draws when it is bored, and Lady Althea had never been bored. Her eyes, pale as winter ice and equally hard, fixed themselves upon the young man standing by the fireplace, who was attempting to look as though he were merely arranging the logs, though he had not touched a single one in the last ten minutes.

"Arthur," she said, her voice light as a feather, yet carrying the weight of a verdict. "You are standing in the way of the fire. And the fire is doing nothing to warm you, I suspect. You are trembling."

Arthur Montrose, the Duke’s nephew and the man who had been granted the tedious privilege of editing the memoirs, turned with a smile that was all practice and no comfort. "I am merely shivering, Aunt. The draft from the door is dreadful. London air is so unkind to the constitution these days. One feels one is inhaling the city’s sins rather than its oxygen."

"Sins are the only things that keep the air fresh," Lady Althea replied, adjusting the silk of her glove. "And you, my dear, are here to sift through mine. I trust you have read the first volume? The part where I married the first husband? He was a bore, but he had excellent teeth. The second was a fool, but he bought me a ruby necklace that still hangs in the jewel box. And the third..." She paused, a small, cruel smile playing on her lips. "The third was a villain, but he had the decency to die young. It is a relief, isn’t it, to outlive one’s mistakes?"

Arthur straightened his tie. "I have read it, Aunt. It is... remarkably candid. Most people of our standing prefer to leave the rough edges of their lives to the imagination. The imagination is a much more forgiving editor than the truth."

"Imagination is for the poor and the young," Lady Althea said, waving a hand dismissively. "The rich and the old must deal in facts. Facts are the only currency that does not devalue. Now, about Volume Two. I have marked the pages in red. I require you to remove them. Not because they are untrue, but because they are too true. There is a difference. The truth is a garment that fits too well; it exposes the lumps and the bruises beneath. I want you to smooth them out. I want you to make the truth look like a rumor. A rumor is charming because it is distant. A fact, when it is close enough to touch, is merely ugly."

Arthur picked up the manuscript from the side table. The leather was worn soft, the pages yellowed with age, but the ink was still black and sharp, like a wound that would not heal. He opened it to the marked section. His eyes scanned the lines, and a coldness settled in his stomach that had nothing to do with the draft.

This was the chapter concerning his mother, the late Duchess of Alderley. Lady Althea had written of her with the dispassionate clarity of a jeweler describing a flawed stone. She spoke of the night of the ball at the Opera, of the whispered conversation in the box, of the letter that had been found in the glove box of the carriage. She did not accuse; she merely observed. She noted that the Duchess’s eyes had widened, that her lips had gone white, and that she had laughed—a sound like glass breaking.

"She was a very brave woman," Lady Althea said, watching Arthur’s face with the intensity of a hawk watching a field mouse. "Brave enough to lie. Most women are brave enough to weep. Lying requires a certain amount of art. It is the only art that is never appreciated, and yet it is the one we all practice most skillfully."

Arthur closed the book. "You are asking me to destroy this," he said. It was not a question.

"I am asking you to edit it," Lady Althea corrected. "To take the sharp edges off the knife. I am not asking you to burn it. Fire is vulgar. I want you to rewrite it. Make her a victim of circumstance, not a participant in it. Make her weak, Arthur. Weakness is a shield. If she was weak, she is innocent. If she was strong, she is guilty. And I would not have you inherit a name that is stained by the guilt of your own mother. It would be so very inconvenient for your future marriages."

Arthur looked at her, and for a moment, he saw not the old woman in the window, but the creature she had been in the drawing rooms of the previous century: sharp-tongued, cold-hearted, and utterly devoid of the mercy that society so loudly preached and so rarely practiced.

"You are a monster, Aunt," he said softly.

"Monsters are merely men and women who have refused to lie about themselves," Lady Althea replied. "And I have never refused to tell the truth, Arthur. I have simply chosen the audience. You are my audience now. And I find you a poor one. You are too sentimental. You still love her. That is your weakness. You think love is a bond. It is merely a chain. And I have just handed you the key to break it, or to keep it tight. Which will you choose?"

Arthur stared at the closed book. The silence in the room was no longer empty; it was full of the weight of unspoken words, of the things that had been hidden in the shadows of the past for decades. He thought of his mother, who had died with a secret in her heart and a smile on her face. He thought of the inheritance, the name, the house. He thought of the lie, which was a comfortable coat, and the truth, which was a naked skin in the winter wind.

"I will edit it," he said at last.

Lady Althea smiled, and it was a terrible thing, beautiful and hollow. "Good," she said. "Now, pour me some wine. The claret. The 1888 vintage. It pairs well with a good lie."

Arthur moved to the cabinet, his hands steady now, the trembling gone. He poured the wine, the dark liquid swirling in the glass like blood in water. He handed it to her, and she took it with a grace that belied her years.

"One last thing, Arthur," she said, as he turned to leave. "Do not make it too smooth. If it is too perfect, they will know it is a lie. Leave a small crack in the porcelain. Let them see the seam. That is where the charm lies. In the imperfection. In the hint that something was there, and something is gone. That is the only mystery that remains worth keeping."

Arthur bowed, a low and precise movement, and left the room. The door clicked shut behind him, a sound final and absolute. In the room, Lady Althea drank her wine, watching the dust motes dance in the slant of light from the window, and felt, for the first time in years, a profound and satisfying peace. The past was a thing that could be managed, like a garden, if one had the right shears and the right heart. And she had both.
