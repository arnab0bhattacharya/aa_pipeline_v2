Julian Ayscough had achieved the remarkable feat of becoming the most talked-about barrister in London precisely by talking the least in court. His specialty was the silent, devastating cross-examination, relying instead on a mere arching of his dark eyebrows, a gentle sigh of dismay, or a deliberate adjustment of his lavender kid gloves to make a witness crumble into a tearful confession of perjury. Charm is the great enemy of truth, as Julian frequently observed; a maxim that had served him exceedingly well, for society will always forgive a man for being wrong, provided he is exquisitely right in his waistcoats. 

It was precisely this devastating sartorial elegance, coupled with a mind that moved through legal loopholes like a silver fish through still water, that had captured the attention of Lady Augusta Vandeleur. Lady Augusta was a woman who had survived three political administrations and two husbands, emerging each time with her complexion unblemished and her cynicism perfectly intact. She held one of those rare, unassailable salons in Mount Street where the destinies of empires were casually diverted over a plate of cucumber sandwiches.

Julian loved Lady Vandeleur. He loved her as one loves a perfect piece of Italian sculpture—coolly, reverently, and with a profound appreciation for the flawless cruelty of her profile. She was forty, which is the dangerous age for a woman of intellect, for it is then she begins to realize that she possesses one, and decides to use it against men. To Julian, she was the only person in London worth listening to, and, what is infinitely rarer, the only person worth talking to.

One wet Tuesday afternoon, Julian found himself alone with her in her amber-draped drawing room, surrounded by the heavy scent of Gloire de Dijon roses and the soft ticking of a French enamel clock. 

"I have been watching you, Julian," she said, pouring out the tea with a graceful precision that suggested she was measuring out political careers rather than Darjeeling. "You are wasting your intellect upon the law. The law is merely a trade for gentlemen who lack the imagination to be blackguards. Politics is the true art, for it is the art of keeping one’s crimes entirely out of the newspapers."

"I fear I am too sincere for Parliament," Julian murmured, adjusting a single rose in a porcelain vase. "Sincerity is a fatal flaw in public life."

"Then you are perfectly suited," she replied, a thin smile playing on her lips. "I can offer you the seat for West Bletchley. The present incumbent is dying, though he does not yet know it. He is a Whig, and thinks he is a Conservative. By the time the election is called, he will be a memory, and you, my dear Julian, will be the candidate."

Julian looked at her, his heart beating with a rhythm that had nothing to do with ambition. "And what is the condition of this political salvation?"

"I am not a woman who offers something for nothing," Lady Vandeleur said, leaning back against the sapphire velvet of the sofa. "One must pay for one’s convictions, and the currency is always matrimony. You must marry my niece, Clarice."

At the mention of the name, a shadow seemed to pass over the room. Clarice Waring was a girl of nineteen, possessed of a complexion like a bruised peach, eyes the color of weak tea, and an income that would have rendered a Rothschild envious. She was entirely good, entirely healthy, and entirely uninteresting. She had the rare misfortune of being perfectly virtuous, and like all perfectly virtuous people, she was profoundly dull.

"Clarice?" Julian repeated, allowing a slight tremor of theatrical horror to enter his voice. "But Lady Vandeleur, she is an angel."

"She is," Lady Augusta agreed, with a chilling serenity. "And angels, my dear Julian, make the very worst companions in marriage. They are entirely unyielding, and they have an irritating habit of looking upward when one wishes to look at them. However, she is an heiress, and her money will fund your constituencies, and her name will sign your invitations. I have arranged it."

Julian set down his teacup, the delicate porcelain rattling faintly against the saucer. "And what, may I ask, do you get out of this arrangement?"

"I get you," she said, and for a moment, a flash of something—power, possession, perhaps even a fleeting hunger—crossed her beautiful, weary eyes. "I get to keep you near me, Julian. If you marry Clarice, you will be my nephew, and I shall have the pleasure of your company every evening of my life. If you refuse, you will remain a clever barrister, and I shall be forced to find another pawn."

Julian rose from his chair and walked to the window, where the rain was streaking the glass like tears on a marble cheek. The paradox was exquisite. To gain the woman he loved, he must bind himself to the girl he could not love. To be near Lady Vandeleur forever, he must be chained to Clarice. It was a cruelty so elegant, so perfectly devised, that he could not help but admire it. It was a masterpiece of manipulation, and like all masterpieces, it demanded a sacrifice.

"And if I were to refuse?" he asked, though he already knew the answer.

"Then you would be a fool," Lady Vandeleur said, "and I have no use for fools. I have a carriage waiting to take you to Clarice. She is in the conservatory, watering the orchids. She is very good with orchids. They require a steady hand and a cold heart."

Julian turned from the window and looked at her one last time. She was smiling, a small, triumphant smile that played about the corners of her mouth, and he realized, with a sudden, terrible clarity, that she knew. She knew that he loved her, and she was using that love as a leash.

"You are a wicked woman, Lady Vandeleur," he said softly.

"I am a practical one," she corrected. "Wickedness is merely practicality misunderstood by the public. Go to Clarice, Julian. Parliament awaits, and so does my niece. And remember, marriage is the one adventure that requires no courage, only patience."

Julian bowed, a slow, graceful inclination of his head, and left the room. As he walked down the long, flower-scented corridor toward the conservatory, he felt a strange, melancholy joy settling over him. He was selling his soul, but he was selling it for a seat in Parliament and the daily sight of the woman who had engineered his ruin. It was, he reflected, a very handsome price.

He found Clarice among the orchids, as Lady Vandeleur had promised. She was dressed in white muslin, her pale hair coiled simply about her head, and she looked up at him with those weak-tea eyes, full of a gentle, trusting innocence that made his heart sink like a stone in still water.

"Mr. Ayscough," she said, her voice soft and colorless. "Aunt Augusta said you wished to speak to me."

Julian looked at her, this pale, perfect creature who was to be his bride, and then he thought of Lady Vandeleur, sitting in her amber drawing room, smiling her triumphant smile. He thought of Parliament, of the speeches he would make, of the power he would wield. And he thought of the endless evenings he would spend in Mount Street, sitting opposite the woman he loved, watching her pour tea with that graceful, devastating precision.

"Miss Waring," he said, taking her hand in his, "I have come to ask you to be my wife."

She blushed, a faint, becoming pink that spread over her bruised-peach cheeks, and she murmured something about being so surprised, and so honored. Julian watched her, and he felt nothing but a vast, echoing emptiness, and a profound, terrible admiration for the woman who had made him what he was: a man who had traded his heart for a political career, and called it love.

As he left the house an hour later, an engaged man, he paused on the steps and looked up at the sky. The rain had stopped, and a thin, pale sun was breaking through the clouds, casting a watery, golden light over the wet pavements of London. 

"To be perfectly happy," Julian murmured to himself, adjusting his collar against the damp air, "one must be perfectly deceived. I, it seems, am to be the architect of my own deception, and Lady Vandeleur is to hold the blueprint." 

He smiled then, a thin, bitter smile, and stepped into his hansom cab, leaving the amber light of Mount Street behind him.
