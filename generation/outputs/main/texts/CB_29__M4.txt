The fever had burned in me seven days before I knew I was ill. I remember the schoolroom at Ravenscar, the fire smoking sullenly in the grate, and the faces of my two charges swimming before me like faces seen under water; I remember setting down the grammar-book because my hands could not hold it, and then the world tilting, and the cold flags of the corridor rising up to meet me. After that, nothing—until I woke in a narrow bed in a room I did not know, and saw Mrs. Garth seated at the window with her sewing, her profile hard against the grey light of a Cumberland afternoon.

She did not look up at first. She was a woman of fifty, spare and upright, with hands like a knot of roots and a mouth that had learned to close upon itself. In three months at Ravenscar she had never spoken to me save in the way of instruction or reproof, and I had set her down in my heart as one of those whom life has dried to hardness, without imagining that life could ever have done otherwise. Yet when she turned and saw my eyes open, something crossed her face that was not displeasure—a kind of grim relief, as though a debt she had not wished to owe was now discharged.

“You are to lie still,” she said. “You have had the fever, and you are not to talk.”

I was too weak to disobey. For three days more I lay and watched her move about the room—her economy of motion, her silent feet, the broth she brought and made me drink, holding the cup with a hand that never trembled though her eyes were weary. She would not let the housemaid near me. “The girl has no more sense than a moth,” I heard her say at the door. “I will do it myself.” And she did, night after night, sleeping in the chair by the window with her shawl about her shoulders, so that when I woke in the dark I saw her shape against the casement and was strangely comforted.

On the fourth day I found strength to speak. “You have been very good to me, Mrs. Garth,” I said. “I did not think you liked me.”

She looked at me a long moment. “Liking has little to do with it,” she answered. “You were put in my charge, in a manner of speaking. I do not leave my charge to perish.”

“Still, I thank you.”

“Thank me by getting well,” she said, and her voice was sharp; but I saw her hands, folded on her apron, grip one another as though she held something back.

It was on the sixth day that the truth came out. She had brought me tea, and the evening light was red behind the fells, and something in the quietness loosened her tongue. She asked me where I was born, and I told her—a village in the West Riding, a father dead, a mother dead, a school where I had been both pupil and drudge. She nodded as though I had confirmed a suspicion.

“I was a governess once,” she said. “Did you know that?”

I confessed I had not.

“At a house in this county. Ellerbeck, it was called. I was twenty-four, and I thought myself fortunate. The family were gentlefolk, and I had the teaching of two girls, and I believed that if I did my duty faithfully I should be treated as a person and not as a piece of furniture.” She paused, and her jaw set. “I was a fool.”

“What happened?”

“What always happens,” she said, “when a governess forgets her place. The eldest girl took a fancy to me—children will, sometimes, if you are patient with them—and she would run to me with her troubles instead of to her mother. The mother did not forgive it. I was told I was presuming; I was told I gave myself airs; I was told, in the end, that I had set the child against her own blood. I had done nothing but love the child, and teach her her lessons, and keep her from the fire when she was small. But a governess’s love is a dangerous thing, it seems. It is either servility or insolence, and they will call it by whichever name suits them.”

Her voice had not risen, but it had hardened to iron. I lay still and listened.

“They dismissed me in the winter,” she went on, “with a month’s wages and a character that damned me. The mistress wrote that I was ‘not suited to the station.’ I walked six miles to the village inn, and the snow was up to my knees. I had a trunk and a Bible and nowhere to go. I thought of the river, I do not mind telling you. I thought of it very calmly.”

“Mrs. Garth—”

“Do not pity me,” she said, almost fiercely. “I did not drown. I came here, to Ravenscar, because they wanted a housekeeper and asked no questions. I have been here twenty years. I have kept my place, and I have kept my tongue, and I have never once let a child of this house love me, nor loved it, for I learned what comes of that.” She looked at me then, and her eyes were bright and terrible. “And then you came, with your books and your soft ways, and the little one began to follow you about as she never followed me. I was angry. I will not deny it. I thought, she will be broken as I was broken, and it will serve her right.”

“And now?” I asked.

She was silent a great while. The fire sank; the red light faded from the fells. “Now,” she said at last, “I find I am not so hard as I thought. I sat by you seven nights, and I prayed—I, who have not prayed since Ellerbeck—that you might not die. It seems I have some foolishness left in me yet.”

I reached out and took her hand. It was dry and rough and cold, but it did not withdraw. “You were not broken,” I said. “You were only wounded, and you have carried the wound so long you forgot it could heal.”

“You are young,” she answered, “and you talk like a book. But I will tell you this: I have watched you with the child, and you have done what I could not do—you have kept your kindness and your place together. I do not know how you do it. I only know I am glad you are not dead.”

She rose then, and busied herself with the fire, and would not look at me. But when she brought my supper she had put a sprig of winter jasmine on the tray, and I understood that in her own stern language she had asked my pardon, and given me her friendship, and that both were the more precious for being so long withheld.
