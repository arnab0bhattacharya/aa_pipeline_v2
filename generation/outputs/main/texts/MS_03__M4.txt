The letter lay upon the table before me, its seal broken, its few lines of typewritten characters possessing a power to freeze the very blood in my veins. I had read it a dozen times, and yet my eyes were drawn to it again, as if by a dreadful fascination, as a man might gaze upon the instrument of his own destruction. It was a report from the Colonial Commission, a dry, statistical document, yet every figure was a death-knell, every projection a prophecy of woe. The harvests in the Veldt Provinces had failed for the third successive year; the blight, which we had been assured was a passing evil, had returned with a virulence that defied all our science. The great grain ships that were the life-blood of our island nation would, by the Commission’s most hopeful estimate, make but half their accustomed voyages in the coming year. The famine, they concluded with a cold finality that struck a chill to my soul, would reach our shores within twelve months.

I am Arthur Pendennis, and I had devoted the full flower of my manhood to the service of the English Republic. To me, the state was not a mere abstraction of power, but a sacred trust, a vast and complex mechanism whose every cog and wheel was a human soul. I had entered the Senate chamber as a young man, my heart filled with the sublime, if perhaps naive, conviction that reason and virtue might be made the twin pillars of a new golden age. I had laboured, I had debated, I had poured forth the earnest eloquence of my spirit in a thousand speeches, all in the service of a vision of a just and prosperous commonwealth. And now, at the pinnacle of my influence, I found myself standing upon the edge of an abyss.

I summoned my closest colleagues to my chambers, the men with whom I had shared so many trials and triumphs. There was the Chancellor, old and weary, his face a map of the nation’s cares; and with him, the young and fiery Home Secretary, whose zeal for the Republic’s glory was a flame that often threatened to consume all caution. I placed the report before them, and watched their faces as they read. A heavy silence fell, broken only by the crackling of the fire in the hearth and the distant, indifferent hum of the city.

“It is unthinkable,” whispered the Chancellor, his voice trembling. “A famine here, in the heart of the civilised world? It would be a catastrophe beyond parallel.”

The Home Secretary, however, was of a different mind. His eyes, which had widened in alarm, now narrowed with a fierce and desperate resolve. “It is a report, Pendennis, not a decree of fate. Such predictions have been wrong before. To announce this to the public would be to court madness. The people would riot; the markets would collapse; the very fabric of our society would be torn asunder by panic. We must hope, gentlemen. We must work in secret to procure what supplies we can, and we must trust that Providence will not forsake us.”

“Hope?” I cried, my voice rising with an indignation I could not suppress. “Is hope a policy, then? Is it a shield to ward off the hunger of a child? You would have me stand before the Senate and the people and speak of our prosperity, of our strength, while I hold in my hand the certain knowledge of their doom? It is a monstrous deception! It is a betrayal of the very principles upon which this Republic was founded!”

The debate that ensued was long and bitter. They spoke of the need for stability, of the fragility of public order, of the danger of premature alarm. They painted vivid pictures of bread queues turning into mobs, of factories silent, of the delicate machinery of commerce grinding to a halt. Their words were not without a terrible logic; they were the words of men who governed the world as it was, not as they wished it to be. But to my ears, they were the counsels of despair, the sophistry of fear. I saw in their eyes not wisdom, but a terror of the storm, a desperate, vain desire to hide from the lightning that was already gathering on the horizon.

I left them and walked out into the night. The London of my time is a marvel of order and light, its broad avenues illuminated by the cold, steady glow of electric lamps, its air kept clean by the great engines of the sanitation towers. Yet on this night, it seemed to me a city of ghosts. The people I passed, laughing in the theatres, hurrying home from their labours, conversing in the brightly-lit cafes—they knew nothing of the shadow that was falling upon them. I felt a gulf, sudden and profound, open between myself and my fellow citizens. I was the sole guardian of a dreadful secret, a prophet of calamity in a city of blissful ignorance.

I ascended to the roof of my apartments, and looked out over the vast, sprawling metropolis. A wind, cold and biting, swept from the east, and I fancied it carried with it the first faint, phantom breath of the coming winter. The sky was a deep, starless indigo, and the city’s lights spread beneath me like a sea of earthly stars, a testament to human ingenuity and spirit. And I thought of the Veldt, of the parched earth and the dying crops, of the silent, empty granaries. I thought of the long, perilous voyage across the oceans, and of the ships that would return with holds half-empty, or not at all. The sheer, inexorable physics of it was a weight upon my soul more crushing than any moral dilemma.

What was my duty? Was it to my colleagues, to the fragile government I had helped to build, to preserve its stability at all costs? Or was it to the truth, and to the people whose servant I was? To speak was to risk anarchy; to remain silent was to become an accomplice in a lie that would inevitably lead to a greater and more terrible chaos when the truth could no longer be hidden. The people would remember who had warned them, and who had not. They would remember the man who had spoken of hope while the spectre of hunger sharpened its scythe.

I recalled the great orators of antiquity, whose words I had studied with such ardour in my youth. I thought of Cicero facing the conspiracy of Catiline, of Demosthenes rousing the Athenians against the might of Macedon. They had not counselled hope in the face of ruin; they had sounded the clarion call of truth, however perilous, and had trusted to the courage and the reason of their people. Was I, then, so much lesser a man? Was the Republic I served so weak that it could not withstand the shock of a hard truth?

A strange and terrible exaltation seized me, a feeling akin to that which the martyrs of old must have felt as they faced the flames. It was a sublime and awful moment, a moment of perfect clarity. I saw that my whole life, my every speech, my every effort, had been but a preparation for this single, dreadful choice. I could not be a party to a lie. I could not build a future upon a foundation of deceit. If the Republic was to fall, it would fall with its eyes open, and I with it.

I descended from the roof, my heart now calm with a terrible resolve. I went to my study and began to write. I wrote not for my colleagues, nor for the Senate, but for the people. I wrote with a hand that was steady, though a tremor of awe ran through my entire being. I described the blight, the failing harvests, the dwindling ships. I did not seek to soften the blow, nor to embellish the horror. I spoke of the famine to come, and of the trials that would attend it. But I did not end there. I spoke of the strength of our nation, of our capacity for sacrifice and for fellow-feeling. I spoke of a great and common purpose, a crusade against the coming darkness, in which every man, woman, and child would have a part to play. I called upon them not to despair, but to prepare; not to panic, but to unite.

The morning sun, pale and cold, began to illuminate the windows of my chamber as I set down my pen. The document lay before me, a declaration of truth and of war. I knew not what it would bring—whether I would be hailed as a saviour or reviled as a traitor, whether the Republic would be steeled by my words or shattered by them. The future was a dark and uncertain sea. But I had chosen my course. I would stand before the people and deliver my message, and whatever tempest might follow, I would face it with the consciousness that I had been true to the light of my own reason, and to the sacred trust of my office.
