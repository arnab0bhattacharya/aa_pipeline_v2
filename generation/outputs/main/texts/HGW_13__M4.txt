Mr. Albert Pringle, a second-class clerk in the statistical branch of the Board of Health, had a mind of a purely numerical cast. He was not a man given to fancies. His world was one of columns, of averages, of the steady, reassuring rhythm of birth and death as recorded in the Registrar-General’s returns. He liked the smell of the ledgers—a dry, leathery, slightly dusty smell—and the comfortable scratch of his pen. He was, in short, a man of facts, and he believed in them as other men believe in a creed.

It was this very faith that was now so sorely tried.

The facts, as they came to him week by week, were these. In the parish of St. Sepulchre’s, in the Borough of Southwark, there had been a sudden and unaccountable rise in the mortality figures. Not a great one, you understand; not a plague-like leap that would have set the headlines screaming. Just a steady, insidious increase. A dozen deaths in the first week, twenty in the second, thirty-five in the third. And the cause of death, certified by the overworked parish medical officer, was invariably the same: “Influenza, with complications.” It was the “complications” that troubled Pringle. They were of a peculiar and distressing nature. A profound lethargy, a slow, creeping paralysis of the limbs, and finally, a strange, dusky pallor of the face, as though the patient were being slowly suffocated from within.

Pringle had drawn a little chart of it, for his own satisfaction. The line, which had been wobbling along the bottom of the graph for months, now began to climb with a most alarming steadiness. It was a neat, precise, and utterly damning line. He took it to his superior, Mr. Horace Wainwright, a man of a more political turn of mind.

“Ah, Pringle,” said Wainwright, leaning back in his chair and viewing the chart with the air of a man examining a mildly interesting curiosity. “Most industrious, I’m sure. But you mustn’t let these little local eddies alarm you. St. Sepulchre’s is a low-lying district. Drainage, you know. And the people there—well, they are not our sort, are they? It is the season for influenza. The Local Government Board has made no special report. The newspapers have said nothing.”

“The newspapers,” Pringle retorted, with a boldness that surprised himself, “are full of the wedding of the Duke of Mersham’s daughter. They have no space for a few dozen deaths in Southwark.”

Wainwright smiled a thin, official smile. “My dear Pringle, our business is to collate facts, not to create panics. The figures are, I grant you, anomalous. But until we have a clear diagnosis, a specific contagion, we are not justified in making a stir. It would be… premature. Let us wait for the next week’s returns.”

The next week’s returns were worse. The line on Pringle’s chart now had a distinct upward curve, like the first swell of a wave. The deaths had spread beyond St. Sepulchre’s, appearing in the crowded courts of Clerkenwell and the mean streets of Bethnal Green. And now, the newspapers had got wind of it. But they had got hold of it in their own peculiar fashion. A paragraph appeared in a popular evening sheet under the heading “THE MYSTERY MALADY.” It spoke of a “strange and terrible scourge,” of “victims struck down as if by a blow,” and hinted darkly at a “foreign origin.” The next day, a more respectable morning paper, in a leading article, deplored the “alarmist rumours” and assured its readers that there was “no cause for undue apprehension,” while simultaneously devoting three columns to the “mysterious outbreak,” complete with a map of the affected districts and a gruesome interview with a publican whose entire family had been “laid low.”

The effect was precisely what one might have expected. The public, bewildered by the contradictory reports, did what it always does: it panicked. The chemists in the affected areas sold out of every quack remedy on their shelves. People took to wearing camphor bags around their necks and burning pastilles in their parlours. The rich fled to the country; the poor, who could not flee, simply waited. And all the while, the official voice of the Board of Health remained a studied, maddening silence.

Pringle, in his dusty office, felt the weight of that silence like a physical thing. He was not a man of action. He was a clerk, a creature of ink and paper. But the figures on his chart were no longer mere numbers. They were people. He saw the face of a little girl he had once seen playing in the gutter of St. Sepulchre’s, a girl with a grimy face and a shrill, happy laugh. He imagined her now, pale and still.

He began to spend his evenings in the reading-room of the British Museum, poring over old medical journals. He found what he was looking for in a volume of the *Lancet* for 1848: a description of an obscure and, at the time, little-understood condition called “progressive pernicious anaemia,” characterised by a peculiar dusky pallor and a slow, creeping paralysis. The symptoms were identical. The disease, the article noted, was not contagious in the ordinary sense, but seemed to be associated with certain insanitary conditions, with overcrowding and bad drainage. It was, in short, a disease of the slums, a disease of neglect. And it was almost invariably fatal.

Pringle sat back, his heart thumping a dull tattoo against his ribs. This was it. This was the “complication.” It was not a new plague from the East; it was an old, forgotten enemy, bred in the very heart of the greatest city on earth. The authorities were not merely negligent; they were criminally so. They were waiting for a “clear diagnosis” while people were dying in the courts and alleys of Southwark.

He thought of his chart, of the neat, climbing line. He thought of Wainwright’s thin smile. And he thought of the little girl in the gutter.

The next morning, he did not go to his office. Instead, he put on his best—which is to say, his least shiny—suit, and took a ’bus to Fleet Street. He had a name in his pocket, a name he had copied from the bottom of a particularly sensational and, he suspected, particularly unreliable article in a weekly paper. It was the name of a man who, it was said, would print anything if it were true, and a good deal that was not. His name was Cornelius Gubbins.

Pringle found him in a cluttered, smoke-filled room above a public house, a small, ferret-like man with a sharp nose and ink-stained fingers. Gubbins listened, at first with the weary cynicism of his trade, then with a growing, professional interest. Pringle, with the meticulous care of a statistician, laid out his facts: the mortality figures, the symptoms, the forgotten article from the *Lancet*. He did not exaggerate. He did not need to. The plain, unvarnished truth was more terrible than any fiction.

When he had finished, Gubbins was silent for a moment, his sharp eyes fixed on Pringle’s face. “And you can prove all this?” he asked, his voice a low rasp.

“The figures are in the public records,” said Pringle. “The article is in the Museum. The deaths are in the cemeteries.”

Gubbins nodded slowly. He picked up a pen. “Right,” he said. “Now, let’s have it all again, from the beginning. And this time, give me the names. The streets. The numbers. Everything.”

That evening, Pringle walked home through the gas-lit streets. He felt a strange lightness, as though he had set down a heavy burden. He knew, of course, what would happen. There would be a terrible row. Questions would be asked in the House. Wainwright would be furious. His own career, that neat, orderly progression of years and increments, was probably finished. He would be a clerk who had broken the rules, who had gone outside the proper channels. He was, in the eyes of his world, a traitor.

But as he looked at the faces of the people hurrying past him in the gloom, he found that he did not care. He had seen the figures. He had seen the line on the chart. And he had decided that a column of numbers, if it were true enough, could be a more powerful weapon than any politician’s speech. He was a man of facts, and he had just let one loose upon the world.
