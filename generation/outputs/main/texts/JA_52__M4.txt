The Misses Ashby of Fordwell Park were understood, in the neighbourhood of Wrenbury, to be the most affectionate of sisters; and as their affection was chiefly exercised in the regulation of their brother’s conduct, it was perhaps fortunate for their own comfort, and certainly for their reputation, that Mr. Edmund Ashby was of a temper rather to submit to their government than to dispute it. He was the only son of a gentleman whose estate, though respectable in appearance, was found at his death to be so encumbered as to leave the heir a very slender independence, and a very lively sense of the importance of money. His sisters, who were older than himself, and who had long considered the settlement of his future as the peculiar province of their care, had fixed upon Mrs. Delacourt almost before that lady had been three months a widow.

Mrs. Delacourt was the mistress of a handsome jointure, a house in Berkshire, and a disposition so universally allowed to be amiable, that it was difficult to say whether her friends admired her most for her fortune or her temper. She was not yet thirty, had no children, and had been heard to speak of the married state with a moderation which her own experience might have justified, but which the Misses Ashby chose to interpret as a delicate preference for the single life, and a consequent reluctance to be persuaded out of it. To persuade her, therefore, into a second marriage with their brother became the occupation of their lives; and as Mr. Ashby liked her, and was aware that his sisters had resolved he should like her, he found himself in that most disagreeable of situations, in which a man’s inclinations and his opposition are equally enlisted on the same side, and he can neither gratify himself without appearing to obey, nor resist without seeming to be obstinate.

The affair had been conducted with all the formality which the Misses Ashby thought due to so important a negotiation. Mrs. Delacourt had been invited to Fordwell Park; Mr. Ashby had been instructed when to hand her into dinner, and with what degree of warmth to press her to take a second glass of wine; and the sisters had so arranged the walks and the card-tables, that their brother and the widow were never long together without being at the same time so manifestly observed, that the very servants must have discerned the design. Mrs. Delacourt, who was neither blind nor foolish, discerned it perfectly; and though she was too well-bred to resent, she was too sensible to be flattered by it. She had liked Mr. Ashby from the first; she liked him still; but she had no taste for being the object of a family manoeuvre, and she began to think that a man who could be so managed by his sisters might, in the end, be managed against his wife.

Mr. Ashby, on his side, was not without his own reflections. He was a man of good understanding and of a humour rather inclined to indolence than to decision, who had been so long accustomed to hear his sisters speak of his interests as if they were entirely their own, that he had almost learned to consider his marriage as a matter to be settled for him, like the lease of a farm or the choice of a steward. But there were moments when the recollection of his own independence returned to him with some force, and when he felt that to marry Mrs. Delacourt because his sisters wished it would be a very different thing from marrying her because he wished it himself. He had no doubt of her merit; he had very little doubt of his own regard; but he began to suspect that if he proposed, he should never be quite certain whether the proposal was his or theirs, and that the consciousness of having yielded to management would embitter even the possession of the fortune which was to have set him above it.

Matters were in this state when an evening conversation in the drawing-room brought them to a point. The elder Miss Ashby had been discoursing on the happiness of a well-assorted marriage, with a particularity which left no doubt as to the parties she had in view; the younger had observed that Mrs. Delacourt’s jointure was reported to be no less than eight hundred a year; and both had concluded with a gentle reproach to their brother for the coldness of his manner, which they were pleased to call a want of spirit.

‘I am sure,’ said Miss Ashby, ‘if I were a man of Edmund’s family and expectations, I should not hesitate a moment. Mrs. Delacourt is everything that is amiable, and her fortune is exactly what is wanting to make Fordwell what it ought to be.’

‘And she is so attached to the family,’ added the younger sister. ‘I never saw anyone so little disposed to give herself airs. She has not once spoken of her own house since she came.’

‘That is very true,’ said Mr. Ashby, with great composure. ‘She has spoken only of ours. I have remarked it.’

‘And you, Edmund, who might be so happy, sit there as if you had no more feeling than a post. I protest I have no patience with you.’

‘You have a great deal of patience, sister,’ replied he, ‘whenever it is necessary to bring me to a sense of my duty. But I would ask you one question. If Mrs. Delacourt had been poor, and her jointure had been nothing, would you have been so earnest for my marrying her?’

The sisters were silent for a moment, and then declared that they hoped they were not so mercenary as to be influenced by fortune alone; but that, as the world went, a man in their brother’s situation could not be justified in marrying without it.

‘That is exactly what I thought,’ said he. ‘And now, let me ask another question. If I were to marry her tomorrow, would you be content to leave the management of my household to her?’

‘To her!’ cried both sisters at once. ‘To a woman who has no more notion of economy than a child!’

‘Then you would still wish to direct it yourselves?’

‘We should wish to see you happy, Edmund, and to prevent your being imposed upon.’

‘I am much obliged to you,’ said he, ‘but I begin to think that I am imposed upon already. I have been so long accustomed to have my happiness arranged for me, that I scarcely know what it is I want. I like Mrs. Delacourt, but I will not marry her to please you; and if I marry her, it must be because I please myself. I am afraid that is a distinction you will not allow.’

The sisters were greatly offended, and retired to their own apartments to lament the perverseness of a brother who could not be brought to see his own interest. Mr. Ashby, left alone, felt the satisfaction of having spoken his mind, mingled with the uncomfortable reflection that he had spoken it to very little purpose. He was not a man to be happy in a quarrel, and he was aware that his sisters would make him suffer for his independence in a thousand small ways, which, though they could not alter his resolution, would certainly disturb his comfort.

The next morning he walked to Mrs. Delacourt’s house, with a determination to say something decisive, though he had not yet fixed what it should be. She received him with her usual ease; and when they were alone, she spoke of his sisters with a kindness which made him ashamed of his own irritation.

‘I am afraid,’ said she, ‘that I have been the occasion of some little disagreement among you. I hope you will not think me impertinent if I say that I have observed it.’

‘You are very good,’ replied he, ‘and very quick-sighted. But you are not to blame. My sisters are excellent women, but they have a passion for managing, which I have not always the courage to resist. I have been thinking a great deal of late, and I have come to a resolution which I ought to have made sooner. I will not marry to please them; and I will not refuse to marry to please them either. If I ever marry, it must be with the consent of my own judgment, and not by their direction.’

Mrs. Delacourt looked at him with an expression which he could not quite interpret. ‘And have you any reason to think,’ said she, ‘that your judgment would be against me?’

‘No, indeed,’ said he, with some warmth; ‘I have every reason to think it would be for you. But I will not ask you to accept a man who has been recommended to you by his sisters as a sort of family arrangement. If I could be sure that you would have me for my own sake—’

‘You are very delicate, Mr. Ashby,’ said she, smiling, ‘but I am not so nice. I have a great regard for you, and I have no objection to your sisters, though I do not think I should like to live with them. If you can promise to keep them at a proper distance, I believe we may agree.’

Mr. Ashby was surprised, and not a little pleased, to find the negotiation so easily concluded. He had expected difficulties, and he found only good sense. He returned to Fordwell Park with a countenance so much happier than he had worn for some time, that his sisters, who had prepared a lecture for him, were disconcerted into silence. When he told them what had passed, they were at first inclined to be angry; but as they had the satisfaction of reflecting that the marriage would take place, and that they had been the means of bringing it about, they soon recovered their good humour, and were pleased to say that they had always thought Mrs. Delacourt a very sensible woman.

Mr. Ashby, however, was not without his private reflections. He had gained his point, but he had gained it by a concession which he did not quite approve. He had been determined not to marry to please his sisters, and he was now going to marry with their full concurrence. He was not certain whether he had been independent or only fortunate; and he resolved, for the future, to keep his own counsel, and to let his sisters understand that a man may be guided by his own judgment even when it happens to agree with theirs.
