If any honest chronicler of human folly were to take it upon himself to chart the precise geographical and moral coordinates of the town of Biddleham-in-the-Marsh, he would be compelled to state that it lies in a damp hollow of Kent, where the cathedral spire stretches upward like a gloomy finger attempting to scratch the undersides of the clouds, and where the collective gravity of human respectability settles into the gutters and stays there. It was a town of ancient stone, of creeping moss, and of an air so heavily saturated with Sunday that a man could scarcely draw a breath on a Tuesday without feeling as though he were committing a minor impropriety.

Into this venerable and damp enclosure, in the autumn of the year eighteen hundred and fifty-something—for the exact date is of no consequence to the moral arithmetic of our tale—came Dr. Tobias Pinch. He had returned from the baking, fever-ridden plains of Bengal, bringing with him a trunk of worn leathers, a cache of admirable medical experience, and a heart so completely battered by the sun and by sorrow that it appeared to beat with a dry, hollow sound. He had gone out to the East a bright-eyed assistant; he returned a sunburnt, independent master of his art, only to find that the suns of India were as nothing to the freezing chill that struck him when he heard the news of Clemency.

Clemency Verner. She had been the daughter of his former master, the apothecary of Biddleham, and she had promised, in the days before Tobias’s departure, to wait for him. But the warm winds of Bengal had blown for seven long years, and human promises, like human constitutions, are sadly subject to the damp and draughts of the English climate. She was now engaged to be married to Mr. Verner himself; not the old apothecary, who lay beneath a handsome slab of Biddleham marble in the churchyard, but to his son, Mr. Gideon Verner, who had inherited the business, the brass mortar, and seemingly, the affections of Clemency.

Tobias took a poor practice in the lane behind the cathedral, a practice so poor that the patients who frequented it seemed to pay their medical bills in coughs and rheumatic groans rather than in sterling. He established himself in two rooms above a dealer in second-hand spectacles, and he pinned his professional shingle to a doorpost that leaned against the wall as though it, too, were suffering from a chronic indisposition.

It was in this narrow,spectacled lane that the younger brother of Clemency, a boy of thirteen named Sylvester, was brought to him. Sylvester was a pale, sharp-featured youth, with a chest that seemed desirous of escaping from his waistcoat at every breath, and a constitution so delicately strung that it appeared to have been spun from glass and wet paper. He was afflicted with a lingering fever of the lungs, which the new Mr. Verner, being a dealer in tinctures and boluses rather than a physician of the chest, had been entirely unable to arrest.

"You must not suppose," said Gideon Verner, standing in Tobias’s consulting room with his hat held before him in a defensive posture, as if to ward off any medical or romantic implications, "that I am come here, Pinch, to revive any old—hem—misunderstandings. Not at all. I am a man of business. The boy declines. Clemency frets. You are a doctor. You are, I hear, a very clever doctor. The connection is natural."

"My practice is small, Mr. Verner," said Tobias, looking at the wall.

"Small!" cried Gideon, who had a habit of repeating the last words of a sentence as if they were a species of foreign currency he was unaccustomed to. "Small! I should think so. A man must live, Pinch. I have no doubt you wish to live. The boy is the sole concern. Sole concern. What say you?"

Tobias said nothing, but he took the case. He visited the great, damp house on the rise, where the elder Verner’s brass mortar still stood as a monument to the family trade, and there he found Clemency. She was as beautiful, and as tearful, and as grateful as a good angel in a crinoline.

"Oh, Dr. Pinch," said Clemency, for she insisted upon the formal title, as though the familiar Tobias had died somewhere between Calcutta and Dover. "If you could but save him! He is all we have. He is so delicate. He is so dear!"

"He is in the hands of Providence now, Miss Verner," said Tobias, though he thought to himself that Providence seemed to have remarkably cold hands in Biddleham. "And in mine."

And so began a long, tedious, and agonizingly intimate illness. For six months, Tobias walked the lane behind the cathedral, up the rise to the Verner house, and sat by Sylvester’s bedside. Sylvester, for his part, was a boy of an inquiring and sarcastic turn of mind, given to making observations that were far too shrewd for his years.

"You are very patient, Dr. Pinch," Sylvester observed one evening, his voice rattling like a dry leaf in a tin cup. "You sit and you sit. Do you sit because you like sitting, or because you like the view from my window?"

"The view is tolerable," said Tobias, adjusting the boy’s pillows. "I have seen worse in India."

"Ah, India!" coughed Sylvester. "My sister talks of India. She says you were very brave there. She says you fought a tiger."

"I fought a fever, mostly," said Tobias. "And the fever very nearly won."

"And now you fight my fever," said the boy. "Do you expect to win, Dr. Pinch?"

"I expect," said Tobias, pouring a dark, bitter draught into a spoon, "that you will swallow this without complaint."

"A physician’s evasion," said Sylvester, swallowing the draught with a grimace. "You are all alike."

The weeks turned into months, and the gratitude of the Verner household settled upon Tobias like a thick, suffocating blanket. Clemency would bring him tea, and her eyes would fill with a profound, agonizing thankfulness that was worse, far worse, than any coldness or contempt. She thanked him for sitting; she thanked him for standing; she thanked him for the wind that blew through the casement, as though he had personally arranged the weather for Sylvester’s benefit. Gideon Verner thanked him in a more commercial fashion, pressing a fee into his hand with a brisk, businesslike air that implied Tobias was being granted a rare and lucrative franchise in the Verner lungs.

"Good work, Pinch! Good work!" Gideon would say, counting out the coins. "The boy mends. Clemency is a new creature. We are all your debtors. Deeply indebted. Deeply."

But Tobias did not wish to be their creditor. He wished, in his darker hours, to be anything but the man who had cured the brother so that the sister might marry the apothecary with a quiet mind. The gratitude was a chain. Every spoonful of medicine he administered to the boy seemed to bind him more firmly to the family, and to the inexorable machinery of their impending marriage.

One evening, when the boy was sitting up in bed and the fever had finally broken, Clemency entered the room with a letter in her hand. Her face was pale, and her eyes were swollen.

"It is the license," she said, holding it out as though it were a sentence of transportation. "Gideon has proc it. We are to be married in the cathedral, next month. I wished to tell you myself, Dr. Pinch, because—because you have been so good to us. So very good. We should not have had Sylvester with us, if not for you. You have given us back our joy."

Tobias looked at the license. He looked at Clemency. He looked at the boy in the bed, who was watching him with a shrewd, knowing, and entirely too-perceptive gaze.

"You are very welcome, Miss Verner," said Tobias. "I am glad the boy will be well enough to see you married."

"He will stand up with us," said Clemency. "He is to hold the ring. It will be a beautiful day, Dr. Pinch. I only wish—I only wish that everyone could be as happy as we are."

At this, Sylvester let out a cough that might have been a laugh, and might have been a symptom, but was certainly not a sound of unmixed joy.

"Happiness, sister," said Sylvester, "is a very complex prescription. I do not think Dr. Pinch would prescribe it in your exact dosage."

"Sylvester!" cried Clemency. "You are unwell again! You must not excite yourself!"

"I am perfectly well," said the boy. "I am the only one in this room who is perfectly well. It is the rest of you who are suffering from a chronic inflammation of the conscience."

Tobias gathered his things. He placed his stethoscope in his bag, and he closed the clasp with a sharp, decisive click. He looked at Clemency, and he thought of the warm winds of Bengal, and of the cold stones of Biddleham Cathedral, and of the long years that stretched ahead of him in the lane behind the spectacles.

"I will call tomorrow," said Tobias. "The boy is out of danger. My attendance, henceforth, is merely a formality."

"Dr. Pinch—" Clemency began.

"Good evening, Miss Verner," said Tobias.

He walked down the stairs, past the brass mortar that stood in the hall like a grinning, metallic idol, and out into the damp air of Biddleham-in-the-Marsh. The cathedral spire was lost in the fog, and the lane behind the cathedral was dark, and the doorpost of his practice leaned against the wall as though it were waiting for him to come and lean beside it. He put up the collar of his coat, and he walked into the fog, which closed around him like the gray and ghostly arms of a gratitude that would not let him go.
