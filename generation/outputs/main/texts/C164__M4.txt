The Hall of Saint Gudula smelled of beeswax and damp wool. Rain had fallen since morning, and the dignitaries seated along the front row—the burgomaster in his chain, two aldermen, a canon from the cathedral, and a scattering of fathers whose coats were better than their manners—had brought the weather in with them, so that the whole room seemed to breathe out a fog of wet cloth and pomade. Mademoiselle Thérèse Vandevelde stood at the side of the platform with her hands folded, watching the last of the parents file into the back benches, and told herself, as she had told herself every quarter for eleven years, that it would be over in an hour.

The examination was the school's great ornament. Once each season the pupils of the Institut Lemaitre were paraded before the city to demonstrate their penmanship, their catechism, their geography, and—the director's particular pride—their elocution. Monsieur Désiré Lemaitre himself had composed the programme, ordered the chairs, and engaged the town's only piano tuner to ensure the instrument in the corner would not offend. He stood now at the foot of the platform in his black tailcoat, one hand resting on the rail, smiling at the company with the serene confidence of a man who had never in his life doubted that the room admired him.

Thérèse did not dislike him. This was the difficulty. A dislikable man would have been simpler. He was vain, certainly, and he took the school's successes as his own with the appetite of a man who had never learned to share a table; but he had also hired her when she was twenty-three and plain and had no other prospect, and he had kept her through the cholera year when two other teachers fled to Ghent, and he had never once been unkind to her in the way that schoolmasters were often unkind to the women who taught their younger classes. He simply believed, with the completeness of a man who had never examined the belief, that whatever was good in his school had begun in him.

The elocution came third on the programme. Thérèse had spent six weeks preparing the girls for it, and four of those weeks had gone to a single pupil: Louise Verhoeven, twelve years old, the daughter of a linen merchant, who had a voice like a small bell and a memory that held whatever was put into it. Louise would recite a poem. The poem was called "The Lantern-Bearer," and it was about a child who carries a light through a dark street so that others might find their way home, and it was sixty lines long, and Thérèse had written it.

She had written it in the spring, in the evenings, after the day-scholars had gone home and the dormitory girls were at their mending. She had not intended it for the school. She had intended it for herself, which was a luxury she allowed herself twice a year, and she had put it in the drawer of her desk with the other things she wrote and did not show anyone. Then Louise had needed a piece for the examination, and the pieces in the recitation book were stale and English and full of nightingales, and Thérèse had thought: it is only a schoolroom. It is only a child saying lines. No one will know, and no one will care, and it will do her good to have something that is not about a nightingale.

She had not reckoned with Louise. The girl did not merely recite the poem; she inhabited it. She had asked, in her grave twelve-year-old way, what the lantern-bearer's name was, and when Thérèse confessed she had not given him one, Louise had named him Pieter and had thereafter spoken of him as a person she knew. She had asked why the fourth stanza turned cold, and Thérèse had said, because the child is afraid, and Louise had nodded and said, yes, I thought so, and had gone away and come back with the stanza delivered in a voice that made the hairs stand up on Thérèse's arms.

On the day of the examination, Thérèse stood at the side of the platform and listened to the catechism and the geography, and then Monsieur Lemaitre stepped forward and announced, in the rounded voice he kept for public occasions, that the next item would be a recitation, and that he was sure the company would agree that the Institut Lemaitre was fortunate in its little scholars. Then he said something else. He said it lightly, as a man says a thing he expects to be admired for, and he said it while looking at the burgomaster and not at her.

"An original composition," he said. "Written for the occasion. I am told it has some merit."

Thérèse felt the sentence land in the room and settle. There was a small polite murmur from the front row. The burgomaster inclined his head. And Monsieur Lemaitre, having said it, stepped back and folded his hands behind him and smiled at the platform, and Thérèse understood, with a clarity that was almost physical, that he had not meant to lie. He had meant to be gracious. He had meant to offer the school's little ornament to the city with his own hand, and in the offering he had absorbed it, as he absorbed everything, into the great warm weather of his own self-regard. He probably did not know that he had done it. That was the worst of it. He probably did not know.

Louise came up the two steps to the platform. She wore her best frock, blue, with a white collar, and her hair was plaited so tightly that her eyebrows were lifted. She found her place, folded her hands, and looked out at the room. Thérèse saw her find her father in the fourth row, and saw her chin come up a fraction, and then Louise began.

She was better than she had ever been. The bell of her voice carried to the back benches without strain. She made the dark street dark and the small light small, and when she came to the fourth stanza she did the thing with the cold that she had worked out for herself, the thing Thérèse had not taught her, and the room was quiet in a way that rooms are rarely quiet. Thérèse, standing at the side, felt the poem leave her. That was the strange part. She had written it, and she had heard it forty times in the practising, and yet standing there she heard it as the burgomaster heard it, as the canon heard it, as the linen merchant in the fourth row heard it—a thing made, a thing that had gone out into the world and no longer belonged to the drawer of her desk. And she thought: it does not matter whose name is on it. It has done what it was for.

Then Louise reached the last stanza, and her voice did not falter, and she said the final line, and the room drew a breath and clapped. The burgomaster clapped with the flat of his hands. The canon nodded as though he had expected it. And Monsieur Lemaitre stepped forward and took Louise by the shoulder and turned her to face the company, and said, "The Institut Lemaitre thanks you," and then, turning, with his hand still on the child's shoulder and his eyes moving pleasantly over the front row, "I am glad the little piece found favour. One writes these things for the young."

Thérèse looked at the floorboards. They were old and scuffed, and there was a knot in the board near her foot that looked like a bird. She thought about the drawer of her desk, and the other things in it, and the eleven years, and the cholera year, and the plain face she had been given, and the fact that a woman who claims a thing in a room like this is not a woman who has written a poem; she is a woman who has made a scene. She thought about Louise, who had named the lantern-bearer Pieter, and who was standing on the platform with her hands folded and her eyebrows lifted, waiting to be told to go down.

The clapping faded. Monsieur Lemaitre was already turning to the piano, to the next item, to the rest of his programme. In a moment it would be too late to say anything at all, and the moment after that it would be too late forever, and no one would ever know, and it would not matter, and it would matter.

Thérèse stepped forward. She did not step onto the platform; she stepped only to the edge of it, and she did not raise her voice. She said, "Monsieur le Directeur."

He turned, courteous, a little surprised.

"The poem," she said. "Louise should not be praised for the writing. Only for the saying. The writing was mine."

The room did not gasp. Rooms rarely do. There was a small movement along the front row, the canon's head turning, the burgomaster's chain shifting as he leaned back. Monsieur Lemaitre looked at her. For a moment his face was entirely blank, and then something crossed it—not anger, she thought afterward, but a kind of bewilderment, the look of a man who has reached for a thing on a shelf and found it not there.

"Ah," he said. "Yes. Of course." And then, recovering, with a smile that was almost his old smile, "Mademoiselle Vandevelde is too modest. She assists me in these little matters. The credit is shared."

He turned back to the piano. The room settled. The next item began. Thérèse stood where she was, and Louise, coming down the steps, paused beside her and looked up, and said nothing at all, and went to her seat.

Afterward, in the empty hall, Thérèse stacked the programmes and blew out the candles, and Monsieur Lemaitre came back in his coat with the collar turned up and said, without looking at her, "You might have spoken to me first." And she said, "Yes. I might have." And he stood a moment, and then went out into the rain.
