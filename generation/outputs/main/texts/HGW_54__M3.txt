It is a curious thing to observe the profound silence of a perfectly ordered society. I had come to Sarnis, that immense and gleaming metropolis of the twenty-third century, prepared to marvel at its architecture or its conveyances, but I found myself instead continually struck by the absolute, almost terrifying, efficiency of its social machinery. The Sarnisians had solved the ancient, wasteful problem of vocation. They had built the Allocator.

I was conducted to the great Hall of Allocation by my appointed guide, a brisk, cheerful functionary named Varron, who wore the grey and silver of the municipal civil service. He explained the mechanism to me with the pride of a man showing off a particularly well-oiled engine. "We do not," said Varron, tapping his fingers upon a polished brass railing, "leave the happiness of the citizen, or the efficiency of the State, to the haphazard accidents of birth and circumstance. There is no scrambling for professions, no disappointed ambition, no tragic misfitting of the man to the task. The Allocator decides."

The machine itself was a vast, humming construction of crystal and spun copper, occupying the entire central well of the hall. It was, as Varron explained, an extraordinary assemblage of analytical engines. It measured the precise electrical resistance of a hundred different nerve centres, the quickness of ocular reaction, the associative capability of the brain, and, by means of a series of subtle pneumatic tests, the fundamental disposition of the applicant. It did not merely test knowledge, Varron insisted, but the inherent, structural capacity of the individual. At birth, every child was brought before the Allocator, and again at sixteen, and the machine, with an infallible mechanical prescience, assigned them their lifelong trade. It was the supreme achievement of a scientific civilisation. 

I expressed some mild wonderment at this. "And the machine never errs?" I asked. "A man is entirely satisfied with his lot?"

"The machine is mathematically incapable of error," Varron returned, with a touch of impatience at my ignorance. "It does not err, because it measures the physical facts of the brain and body. Satisfaction is a secondary matter; a properly adjusted citizen is necessarily a useful one. But as for satisfaction, the physical exercise of one's natural aptitudes produces a corresponding neurological contentment. We have eliminated the misfit. The man fitted to dig is made happy by the act of digging. The man fitted to govern is made happy by governing."

It was a remarkably neat philosophy, and I might have accepted it entirely had I not, later that afternoon, wandered alone into the lower terraces of the city. I had walked perhaps a mile from the Hall of Allocation, descending by a series of broad, marble stairways into the service quarters where the humbler mechanisms of the city's daily life were carried on. The streets here were scrupulously clean, washed by a perpetual shower of fine water-spray that played from hidden conduits in the walls. I was watching a woman engaged in the sweeping of one of these lower promenades. She was a slight, pale woman, dressed in the dark red tunic of the street cleaners. She was handling her long, fibre-bristled brush with a certain rhythmic, listless precision, pushing the damp dust into the gleaming gutters.

As I passed, I noticed she had paused in her work. She was staring at a large, iridescent beetle that had somehow blundered up from the lower drains and was now struggling on the smooth, wet pavement. She was observing it with an extraordinary intentness, her lips moving slightly, as though she were framing the creature's struggle into some form of words. 

I am not a naturally intrusive man, but the scientific curiosity of the traveller overcame me. "A remarkable insect," I ventured, stopping a few paces away.

She started, and her hand tightened on the shaft of her brush. For a moment she looked at me with the quick, apprehensive glance of a startled animal. Then, seeing I was a stranger and unarmed, she relaxed. "It is a water-beetle," she said. "But it does not belong to the drains. It belongs to the light. It is fighting the water."

"It is fighting the smoothness of the stone," I corrected, smiling at her poetic phrasing.

"Smoothness," she repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. Smoothness is the great enemy of the water-beetle. And of everything that wishes to climb." 

I looked at her more closely. There was an odd intensity in her pale, grey eyes, a smouldering, unspent energy that seemed entirely at odds with the dull, mechanical rhythm of her sweeping. I sat down upon a stone bench beside the promenade. "You do not find your work congenial?" I asked, bluntly enough.

She leaned upon her brush, looking cautiously up and down the empty terrace. "The Allocator has decided it," she said, in a low, measured tone. "My nervous reactions, my muscular adaptations, the exact degree of my ocular focus—all these were measured when I was sixteen. The machine decreed that my hands were steady, my lungs strong, and my patience considerable. Therefore, I am a sweeper. I am a part of the city's sanitation. It is a highly necessary function. Varron himself told me so when he read me my classification."

"Varron is a very practical man," I said.

"He is a machine," she returned, with a sudden, surprising bitterness. "He is a part of the Allocator, just as I am a part of the gutter. But the Allocator does not measure everything. It measures the hands, and the nerves, and the brain. It does not measure the thing behind the brain."

"What is the thing behind the brain?" I asked, genuinely curious.

She looked at me as if I were very dull. "The thing that wishes to speak," she said. "The thing that sees the water-beetle fighting the smoothness of the stone, and feels a great, swelling, unutterable need to put that struggle into words. Into beautiful, ordered words. I wish to be a poet."

I confess I was somewhat taken aback. It was not the ambition itself—every age has its dreamers—but the stark, hopeless irony of it, set against the infallible background of the great machine. "A poet," I repeated. "But surely the Allocator, if it measured your associative capabilities, would have detected a linguistic aptitude?"

"It detected an aptitude for the arranging of things," she said, and a faint, tragic smile touched her lips. "It found I had an excellent sense of pattern, and a steady, tireless hand. Therefore, it reasoned, I am fitted to arrange the dust upon the pavements. The machine is infallible. It sees that I have a genius for sweeping. It does not see that I have a genius for weeping."

She resumed her sweeping for a moment, pushing the wet dust into a neat, ordered line. "They have a library here," she continued, speaking low and rapidly, as if afraid the very walls might hear. "A great, domed building on the upper terraces, where they keep the histories and the approved verses of the past centuries. I go there on my day of rest. I read the old poets, the men who wrote before the Allocator was built. And I think of words, all day, as I sweep. I make verses in my head. I make them about the water-spray, and the beetles, and the grey faces of the people in the lower terraces. But there is no profession of poet in Sarnis. The Allocator has decided that poetry is an inefficient use of the associative faculty. It produces nothing. It moves nothing. It cleans nothing."

"It is a luxury," I suggested, mildly. 

"It is a necessity!" she flashed, with a sudden, fierce energy that startled me. "It is the only necessity! To take the world, and the pain of it, and the wonder of it, and put it into sounds that ring true! But the machine has measured my brain, and my brain is suited to the brush. And so I sweep. And the verses stay in my head, and they die there, like insects in the drain."

I sat there for some time, watching her work. The smooth, wet pavement gleamed under her brush, immaculate and perfectly ordered. The great city of Sarnis hummed about us, a marvel of scientific arrangement, a triumph of mechanical prescience. Every man, woman, and child in its vast, glittering expanse was exactly fitted to their task. There was no poverty, no crime, no disappointed ambition. There was only the steady, infallible, humming perfection of the Allocator.

And yet, as I watched this pale, slight woman methodically pushing the dust from the marble, I could not shake a profound and singular unease. I thought of the vast, intricate machine in the Hall of Allocation, with its crystal valves and its pneumatic tests, measuring the physical facts of the brain with an infallible precision. It was a magnificent engine. But it could not measure the soul. It could not measure that stubborn, irrational, unscientific spark that looks at a water-beetle on a wet stone and sees a tragedy. 

I left her at last, still sweeping, her lips moving silently to some unspoken, unwritten verse. As I climbed the broad, marble stairways back to the upper terraces, the gleaming perfection of Sarnis seemed suddenly oppressive. The city was a marvel of efficiency, a monument to the triumph of science over the chaotic miseries of the past. But I could not help but feel that in hamstringing the misfit, in eliminating the wasteful, unprofitable dreams of mankind, the men of Sarnis had somehow hamstrung humanity itself. They had built a perfect State. But they had cleaned the streets with the souls of their poets.
