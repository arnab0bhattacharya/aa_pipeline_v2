It was a strange and wonderful thing to see Mr. Ambrose Finch, who had gone out of Little Petherick a bare-footed boy with a turnip in his hand and not a halfpenny in his pocket, come back into it again with the dust of the Australian gold-fields upon his boots and the weight of a considerable fortune in his pockets. He came not by the stage-coach, which rattled and banged its way through the village twice a week with an importance quite out of keeping with its destinations, but in a chaise of his own hiring, which was a far grander affair, with a pair of horses that seemed to snort their contempt at the Suffolk mud and a driver who cracked his whip at the geese in the road as though he were a lord of the manor.

Ambrose himself, as he stepped out before the sign of The Wheatsheaf, was a sight to make the good people of Little Petherick stare. He was brown as a berry and broad in the chest, with a coat of a strange, rough-cut cloth, and a waistcoat of a colour that no man in the village had ever dared to wear. He had a gold watch-chain that was thick enough to moor a small boat, and he looked about him with a kind of bright, eager wonder, as a man might look upon a picture he had half-forgotten. He was Ambrose Finch, and yet he was not; he was a memory made flesh, and a prosperous one at that.

His first thought was for his old friend, Tom Tully. Tom, who had shared his bread with him, and with whom he had poached rabbits on the Squire’s land, and sworn a boy’s oath of everlasting friendship. He asked for him at the bar, where the landlord, a man grown fat on small beer and smaller gossip, polished a glass with a slow, deliberate motion. “Tully?” said the landlord, without looking up. “Ah. Young Tom Tully. He’s not here, sir. He went for a soldier. Or was it a sailor? One of the two. And never came back. Or if he did, he came back with the ague and went out again in a wooden box. There’s a stone for him in the churchyard, under the yew. A plain one.” The landlord resumed his polishing, and the bright gold in Ambrose’s pocket suddenly felt as cold and heavy as a stone in a coffin.

His next inquiry was for the miller’s son, Ned Corbet, a merry, red-faced boy who could whistle any tune in Christendom. He found the mill, but the wheel was still and a strange, sad quiet hung about the place. A woman with a child on her hip told him, with a shake of her head, that the Corbets were all gone; the mill had failed, the father had died of a broken heart, which is a malady the doctors cannot physic, and Ned himself was in the workhouse at Bury, ashamed to be seen. And so, the friend of his boyhood was a name on a tombstone, and the friend of his youth was a ghost in a pauper’s coat. The village, which had seemed so bright and welcoming from his carriage, now looked upon him with a thousand indifferent eyes, and its silence was a judgment.

It was with a heavy heart, and with the strange, new feeling that his fortune was a kind of curse, that he made one last inquiry. He asked a small girl, who was skipping with a bit of rope, where he might find Miss Pringle, the schoolmistress. The child pointed him down a narrow lane, where the cottages huddled together for warmth, to a little building of brick and tile, with a bell that looked too heavy for its frame. This was the dame-school, where he had learned his letters, not from a book at first, but from a hornbook that Miss Pringle, a lady then as old as the hills and as kind as the summer, had held in her patient hand.

He pushed open the gate, which creaked a familiar welcome, and stood for a moment in the small, gravelled yard. Through the open window, he could hear the droning of small voices repeating their tables, and the gentle, yet firm, voice of the mistress saying, “Now, Silas, a seven is not a six, however much you may wish it to be. Attend to the figure before you.” Ambrose felt a sudden rush of tears to his eyes. Here, in this humble place, was the one sound of his childhood that had not changed.

He entered the schoolroom, and a dozen small faces turned to stare at him, their mouths agape. And there, at her desk, sat Miss Pringle. She was smaller than he remembered, and her hair, which had been a pepper-and-salt grey, was now as white as the chalk on her slate. But her eyes, when she looked up at him, were the same. They were eyes that had seen a thousand boys come and go, and they held a deep, unwavering kindness.

She did not cry out his name in surprise. She did not point to his fine coat or his gold watch-chain. She simply looked at him, and a slow, sweet smile spread over her face, a smile that smoothed away the years as a hand smooths a creased page. “Ambrose,” she said, and her voice was the same gentle, patient voice that had taught him that A was for Apple and B was for Boy. “My dear boy. You have come home.”

She rose and came to him, and took his hands in her own thin, dry ones. She did not look at his clothes, or his fortune, or the hard lines the sun and the world had drawn upon his face. She looked only at his eyes, and in them she found the little boy who had struggled with his letters and triumphed over the multiplication table. He fell upon his knees before her, the great Mr. Finch from the gold-fields, the man with the horses and the gold, and he wept as he had not wept since he was a child. And Miss Pringle laid her hand upon his head, and said, “There, there, my child. It is a hard world. But you have learned to read its lessons, and that is the greatest fortune of all.” And in the little schoolroom, with the smell of chalk-dust and warm children about him, Ambrose Finch knew that he was, at last, truly rich.
