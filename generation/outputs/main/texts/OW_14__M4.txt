The Reverend Mr. Alistair Pomfrey was a man of advanced views, a fact which he announced to the world not with the trumpet-blast of the zealot but with the discreet cough of the man who has discovered a new and superior form of etiquette. His church, St. Perpetua’s, in that pleasant, well-upholstered quarter of Kensington, was a masterpiece of modern convenience, with electric bells for the pews and a heating system that murmured, in the winter months, like a well-fed cat. It was his proudest boast that he had made religion, if not exactly attractive, at least profoundly comfortable. It was this very ambition that led him to invite Mr. Julian Vane to occupy his pulpit.

Mr. Vane was a celebrated aesthete, a man whose life was a series of beautifully arranged tableaux, and whose conversation was a collection of epigrams strung together on a thread of exquisite boredom. He had been heard to remark that he could resist everything except temptation, and that the only way to get rid of a temptation was to yield to it. The Reverend Pomfrey, who understood such remarks as little as a cow understands the binomial theorem, nevertheless perceived in them a certain drawing-power. A scandal, he reasoned, was merely a sermon that had been reported in the newspapers, and a fashionable scandal might well fill his empty pews. He wrote to Mr. Vane, proposing a Sunday sermon on the theme of ‘The Modern Conscience.’ Mr. Vane, who had a profound contempt for the modern conscience, and a still more profound appreciation for the aesthetic possibilities of a well-dressed congregation, accepted with a telegram that read simply: *Delighted. Will bring my own lilies.*

The Sunday arrived, a day of such limpid, golden perfection that it seemed to have been specially ordered from the florist. The church was full. The front pews were a blaze of silk hats and the more subdued, though no less costly, millinery of ladies who had come to see a scandal and perhaps to be seen in one. The air was heavy with the scent of Mr. Vane’s lilies, which, arranged with a profusion that bordered on the indecent, threatened to obscure the altar entirely. Mr. Vane himself, in a suit of dove-grey, with a buttonhole of a single, enormous orchid, ascended the pulpit with the air of a man mounting a stage. He looked down upon the congregation, a faint, fastidious smile playing upon his lips.

He began, as was his custom, with a paradox.

“My dear friends,” he said, his voice a beautifully modulated instrument, “we have been told, and told often, that the soul is a battleground between good and evil. I have always felt this to be a great error in tactics. A battleground is a vulgar thing, a place of noise and dust and disagreeable casualties. The soul, I am convinced, is not a battlefield but a garden, and the flowers that grow there are not the pale virtues we are so often exhorted to cultivate, but the more exotic blooms of what the world, in its clumsy way, calls sin.”

The congregation stirred. The silk hats turned, one to another, like a field of sunflowers seeking a new sun. Mr. Vane continued, undeterred.

“Consider, for a moment, the virtue of humility. It is, I grant you, a very worthy thing, and very cheap. It requires no imagination. It is the refuge of the dull and the consolation of the ugly. But think, on the other hand, of the sin of pride! Pride is the very engine of civilisation. It was pride that built the towers of Oxford and the follies of our own dear Kensington. It is pride that makes a man refuse to wear a cheap coat, and in so doing, supports the entire trade of Savile Row. To be humble is to accept the world as it is; to be proud is to insist that it might be a great deal more beautiful.”

He paused, allowing the words to settle like dust on a polished surface. The Reverend Mr. Pomfrey, in his stall, felt a coldness at the base of his spine. This was not quite what he had had in mind.

“Then there is the sin of covetousness,” Mr. Vane went on, warming to his theme. “A dull, grasping vice, you may think. But no! It is the mother of art. The desire to possess a beautiful thing is the first step towards its creation. The covetous man is not a miser; he is a collector. He is the man who knows that the only way to resist a beautiful object is to acquire it. He is the patron of the painter, the inspiration of the jeweller, the defender of the antique. Without covetousness, our museums would be empty, our galleries silent, and our ladies would have nothing to wear but last season’s fashions, a thought too dreadful to contemplate.”

The ladies in the front pews exchanged glances. Mrs. Algernon Trench, a woman of formidable piety and even more formidable diamonds, clutched her prayer book as if it were a life raft. Mr. Vane’s voice, meanwhile, had taken on a dreamy, lyrical quality.

“And what of the sin of luxury, the one we are all, at this very moment, engaged in committing? We are told that it is a snare. But luxury is simply the art of living. It is the difference between a crust and a *brioche*, between a hansom and a brougham, between a sermon and a conversation. It is the appreciation of the finer shades, the subtler textures of life. The truly luxurious man is not a glutton; he is a connoisseur. He knows that a cigarette is the perfect type of a perfect pleasure. It is exquisite, and it leaves one unsatisfied. What more can one want?”

He leaned forward, his eyes, which were the colour of pale sherry, sweeping over the sea of scandalised faces with a kind of affectionate contempt.

“The greatest of all sins, however, is the one we are most afraid to name. It is the sin of beauty. For beauty is a thing that cannot be argued with. It is a tyranny, and the world, which is ugly, resents it. It is the beautiful man who is suspected of wickedness, the beautiful woman who is believed to be frivolous. The world would have us all be plain and good, for it is only the plain who have the time to be good. It is a terrible price to pay for doing so, but we are overcharged for everything nowadays. I should fancy that the real tragedy of the poor is that they can afford nothing but self-denial. Beautiful sins, like beautiful things, are the privilege of the rich.”

A low murmur, like the rustle of silk, ran through the church. The Reverend Mr. Pomfrey had turned the colour of a well-ironed surplice. Mr. Vane, however, was in his element.

“And so, my dear friends, I urge you not to be good. Goodness is a negative quality, a mere absence of temptation. Be, rather, magnificent. Be splendid. Let your sins be like your jewels, worn with style and worn with pride. For the only way to resist a temptation is to yield to it, and the only way to be forgiven is to have done something worth forgiving. The soul that has never sinned is like a garden that has never been planted. It is neat, no doubt, but it is also terribly dull. Go home, I beg of you, and commit a beautiful sin. You will find it an experience of the utmost aesthetic value, and I can assure you, from personal experience, that the remorse is simply exquisite.”

He descended from the pulpit in a silence so profound that one could hear the electric bells humming in the vestry. He gathered up a single, perfect lily from the altar, and, with a slight, graceful bow to the congregation, walked down the aisle and out into the sunshine, leaving behind him a silence that was more eloquent than any prayer.

The Bishop’s letter arrived on Tuesday. It was, Mr. Pomfrey noted with a sinking heart, a masterpiece of ecclesiastical fury, denouncing Mr. Vane as a ‘pagan, a sybarite, and a corrupter of youth,’ and the sermon as ‘a blasphemous rhapsody that would have made the angels weep and the devils applaud.’ The church, the Bishop concluded, was not a music-hall, and he would be obliged if Mr. Pomfrey would remember it.

But the strangest thing of all was the collection. It had never been so large. And the following Sunday, the church was fuller than ever.
