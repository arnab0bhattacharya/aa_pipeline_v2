The rain lashed the windows of the omnibus as it lurched heavily past the dripping trees of the mall, and inside, the air was thick, condensing on the glass, blurring the great wet world outside into a smear of green and grey and glistening black. They were thrown together, these strangers, in a heaving, rattling box of metal and wood, inhaling the same damp wool, the same odour of wet macintosh and steaming umbrellas, yet each was utterly alone, walled in by the glass of their own consciousness. The conductor rang his bell, a sharp, insistent sound that cut through the hum of the engine and the drumming of the rain, but it seemed to Eleanor, sitting with her gloved hands folded tightly in her lap, to be ringing somewhere far away, miles beneath the sea of her own preoccupation. She was thinking of Charles; she was always thinking of Charles now, so that the whole city, its sprawling, chaotic vastness, its stones and steeples and rushing water, seemed but a faint shadow cast by the immense, suffocating fact of his absence.

He had said, "I will write on Thursday," and Thursday had come and gone, dissolving into Friday, and now it was Saturday, and the letter-box in the hall of her boarding-house had gaped empty, a dark square mouth swallowing her hopes. She looked at the back of the man sitting in front of her; he wore a damp bowler hat, and the collar of his coat was frayed, revealing a sliver of pale, reddened skin beneath. Perhaps he too was waiting for a letter; perhaps the whole world was merely an assembly of people waiting for letters, their lives suspended over the terrible chasm of silence. She saw, reflected in the wet pane, her own face, pale and anxious, and she thought how strange it was that she should look so composed, so much like a woman merely going about her business in the rain, when inside, her thoughts were a tangle of accusations and justifications. He is busy, she thought; he works so terribly hard in that grim office by the river, where the fog comes in at the windows and the ink freezes in the wells. And yet, if one loves, does one not find a moment? A scrap of time, torn from the rush of hours, to say, "I think of you"? The bus jolted over a rut in the road, and she was thrown forward, catching the strap to steady herself. The physical motion seemed to shake her thoughts loose from their fixation; she looked out and saw a woman standing under a shop awning, holding a child by the hand, and the child was crying, its face screwed up, its small fist clenched in the wet fabric of the mother's skirt.

It was the child’s face that struck Mrs. Fyfe, sitting on the opposite seat, with a sharp, painful recognition. She held her own little boy close against her shoulder, feeling the damp heat of his forehead through her blouse, the shallow, rapid flutter of his breath. He had been feverish since morning, his small body radiating a heat that seemed to her terrifying, unnatural, a fire consuming him from within. She had wrapped him in her shawl, and she was taking him to the dispensary in the City, where the doctor would see him, where they would give her a bottle of medicine, and he would be well again. He must be well again. She looked at the other passengers, at their oblivious faces, at the typist with her tight gloved hands and her staring eyes, and she wanted to cry out, "Look at him! Look at how he breathes! Tell me he will be well!" But they would not understand; they were wrapped in their own concerns, their own private despairs. The rain beat against the roof of the bus, and it sounded to her like the ticking of a clock, measuring out the seconds of her son’s life. She remembered the baby she had lost two years ago, the terrifying stillness of its face, the silence that had fallen over the house like a shroud, and a cold terror gripped her heart. She pressed her lips to the boy's hot temple, murmuring a fragment of a lullaby, a meaningless succession of sounds that she hoped would soothe him, though he was too deep in his feverish stupor to hear.

Opposite her sat an old man, his hands trembling slightly as he counted the coins in his palm. He moved his lips, forming the numbers without sound, his brow furrowed in intense concentration. He had been to the pawnbroker in Hammersmith, and he had redeemed his wife’s brooch, a small, worthless thing of tarnished silver and false pearls, which now lay heavy in his waistcoat pocket. It had cost him three and sixpence, which was three and sixpence more than he could afford, but he had done it, and now he felt a strange, exultant pride, mingled with a profound, weary sadness. Eliza would have been sixty today, he thought; she would have sat beside him on this jolting bus, holding his arm, commenting on the passers-by, her voice a low, gentle murmur in his ear. He saw her face, pale and smiling, as it had been in the photograph taken on their wedding day, and he felt the brooch in his pocket, a cold, hard lump of memory. The world was full of young people, rushing past, their faces bright with purpose, their umbrellas raised against the rain, and he felt himself to be a relic, a fragment of a bygone era, adrift in a world that had no use for him. He counted his change again; it came to one and twopence halfpenny, which would have to last him until Monday. He closed his fingers over the coins, pressing them into his palm, as if by holding them tight he could hold onto the world, onto the past, onto the fragile, fading image of Eliza's face.

At the front of the bus, gazing with unseeing eyes at the wet road unwinding before him, sat the Reverend Arthur Benson. He was composing his sermon for tomorrow morning, and the phrases were forming and dissolving in his mind, like clouds gathering and dispersing in a stormy sky. "The peace that passeth all understanding," he thought; but what did that mean, really, to these wet, hurrying people, to the woman with the sick child, to the old man counting his pennies? He had spoken those words so often from the pulpit, and they had sounded hollow to him, empty vessels clashing together. He looked at the rain, at the grey sky, at the grimy facades of the houses sliding past, and he thought, there must be something more; there must be some thread of meaning running through this tangle of lives, this ceaseless procession of birth and death and suffering. He thought of his own life, of the quiet vicarage in the suburbs, of the roses in the garden, of the clock striking in the hall, and he felt a sudden, sharp sense of his own inadequacy, his own inability to reach out and touch these lives, to offer them anything more than words, empty words. The bus stopped, and a young woman got on, shaking the water from her umbrella, and for a moment, their eyes met, and he saw in her face a look of such profound, unutterable loneliness that he felt a physical pang, a sharp contraction of the heart. He wanted to say something, to reach out, to bridge the gulf that separated them, but the moment passed, and she moved down the aisle, and he was left alone with his sermon, with the words that would not come.

The omnibus reached the terminus. The engine shuddered and died, and the conductor called out the stop in a bored, sing-song voice. They rose, these strangers, these solitary travelers, and filed out into the rain. Eleanor stepped onto the wet pavement, her umbrella unfurling with a snap, and she walked quickly away, her heels clicking on the slick stones, her face set towards the river, where Charles was. Mrs. Fyfe wrapped her shawl tighter around her child and hurried towards the dispensary, her head bowed against the rain. The old man shuffled off, his hand in his pocket, clutching his coins and his brooch. The Reverend Benson descended heavily, his hat brimming with water, and turned towards the rectory, where his tea would be waiting, and his fire, and his books. They scattered, they dissolved into the grey, wet vastness of the city, each bearing their own burden, their own secret grief, their own small, stubborn hope. The omnibus stood empty, its windows streaked with rain, and the conductor leaned against the platform, smoking a cigarette, watching the smoke curl up into the grey air. The rain continued to fall, washing the streets, blurring the outlines of the buildings, dissolving the world into a mist of grey and silver, a world of shadows and reflections, where the living and the dead walked together, and the city, vast and indifferent, endured.
