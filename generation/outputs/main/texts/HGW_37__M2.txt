The man who called himself Aldous stood in the center of the Plaza of Stasis, his hands trembling not from the chill of the air, which was regulated to a perpetual and sterile twenty-two degrees, but from the sheer, crushing weight of their stares. He was an oddity, a grotesque, a thing of nightmare in a world of polished porcelain. His hair, once black and thick, was now a chaotic thicket of white and silver, a crown of frost that seemed to scorch the eyes of the onlookers. His skin, mapped with the fine, intricate lines of seventy years, looked to them like cracked parchment, dry and brittle, ready to shatter.

In this city, Veridia, no one aged. The serum was injected at the first whisper of puberty, locking the body in the prime of thirty. There were no wrinkles, no grey hairs, no creaking joints. To see a man with a beard, even a stubble of it, was enough to cause a child to cover their mouth in revulsion. But Aldous was worse. He was old. He was a ghost, they whispered, a spectral remnant of the times before the Cure, a dead man who had somehow forgotten to dissolve.

He tried to speak, to explain that he was merely a traveller from the Outer Colonies, where the old ways had not been fully scrubbed away. He wanted to say that he had come to trade data crystals, to buy water, to find a hotel. But when he opened his mouth, the sound that emerged was not the bright, clear tone of the citizens, but a raspy, gravelly thing, deep in the chest.

"Please," he said, the word thick in his throat. "I am not a ghost. I am flesh and blood."

A woman in a sleeveless tunic of iridescent blue stepped forward. She was beautiful, terrifyingly so, with the smooth, unblemished skin of a doll. Her eyes were wide, dilated with a mixture of horror and morbid curiosity. She did not step closer, but she did not retreat. She pointed a slender finger at his face.

"Look at the damage," she said, her voice sharp and incredulous. "Look at the decay. How do you still stand? How does the heart keep pumping with such... such ruin?"

"Asthenia," Aldous said, using the medical term he hoped they might recognize, though he suspected they had forgotten the word for sickness. "It is just time. It is the accumulation of moments."

The woman recoiled as if he had spat at her. "Time," she repeated, the word tasting like poison in her mouth. "You speak of time as if it were a substance you could carry. We have mastered time. We have stopped it."

"You have stopped the body," Aldous corrected, his voice gaining a little strength, a stubbornness born of decades of being told he was obsolete. "You have not stopped the mind. You have not stopped the weariness. I see it in your eyes. You are all so young, and yet you look so tired."

A murmur rippled through the crowd. It was a dangerous thing to say. To accuse the perfected of being tired was to accuse them of failing. A man in a grey suit, his face a mask of identical perfection to the woman’s, stepped in beside her. He was taller, broader, his posture rigid.

"You are a freak," the man said, not with anger, but with the cold, clinical disdain of a biologist examining a mutated specimen. "You are a relic. You should be contained. The Airship Wardens will come for you. They will scan you for pathogens. They will put you in a stasis cell until you... until you are no longer a threat."

"I am not a threat," Aldous said. He looked around at the sea of identical faces, the same jawline, the same bright eyes, the same empty, polished smiles. It was a hall of mirrors, a parade of clones who had never known the pain of a broken bone or the ache of a bad back. "I am just old. And old things are valuable. They have memory."

"Memory is a defect," the man said. "It is a clutter. We have optimized our lives. We have removed the redundancy."

"Redundancy is life," Aldous said, and he felt a sudden, fierce pride rise in him. He straightened his back, ignoring the protest of his lower spine. "Look at my hands," he said, holding them out. They were spotted, the nails thick and yellowed, the veins bulging blue beneath the skin. "These hands have held children. They have built things. They have buried friends. They have felt the sun on a winter morning and known that it was good because it was cold. You have no winters. You have no mornings. You have only the same afternoon, forever."

The woman in blue made a sound of disgust, covering her nose. "It smells of decay," she said. "It smells of death."

"It smells of living," Aldous said. "Yes, I will die. That is the dignity of it. Because I will die, the moment I am in now has weight. It has value. Your moment has no weight because it is infinite. You are not living, my friends. You are merely existing. You are a painting that never dries, a song that never ends. It is not music. It is noise."

The crowd was silent now. The air in the plaza was still, the artificial breeze holding its breath. The man in the grey suit looked at his companion, then back at Aldous. There was a flicker of something in his eye, not fear, but a confusion, a crack in the glass of his certainty.

"You speak strangely," the man said. "You speak with a gravity we do not possess."

"Gravity is what keeps you on the ground," Aldous said. "You have lost it. You are floating. And floating is a lonely thing. It is a void. I am grounded. I am heavy. I am here."

He took a step forward, his cane tapping against the pristine white tiles. The sound was a sharp, rhythmic clack that seemed to pierce the silence. *Clack. Clack. Clack.* It was the sound of time passing, measured out in footsteps.

The woman in blue flinched. The man in grey did not. He watched the cane, watched the foot, watched the old man advance.

"Stop," the man said, but his voice was less commanding. "You are close to the edge of the sanitation zone. You will be cleaned."

"Cleaned," Aldous laughed, a dry, rattling sound. "You want to scrub the years off me? You want to bleach the wisdom out of my skin? You cannot. It is not on the surface. It is in the bone."

He stopped in the center of the plaza, surrounded by the young, the unaged, the terrified. He looked at them, really looked at them, and saw not monsters, but children who had been denied the right to grow up. He saw their fear, which was not of him, but of themselves. They were afraid that if they looked at him too long, they might see their own potential for decay, and they were not ready for that.

"I will go," Aldous said, turning slowly. "I will go to the Outer Colonies. I will find my people. They have wrinkles. They have grey hair. They have bad knees and good stories. And they die. And because they die, they love. Do you understand? They love because it is brief. You do not love. You tolerate. You coexist. You are a garden of plastic flowers. Beautiful, yes. But dead."

He began to walk away, his cane tapping out the beat of his own heart, irregular and strong. The crowd parted before him, not in deference, but in awe, as one parts for a storm. They watched him go, their perfect faces tight with a new, unsettling emotion. It was not hatred. It was envy. For the first time in the history of Veridia, they looked at the old man and did not see a ghost. They saw a man who had lived, fully and completely, and who had paid the price for it. And in the silence that followed his retreating figure, the air seemed to grow heavier, as if the weight of time itself had returned to the city, pressing down on their smooth, unblemished shoulders.
