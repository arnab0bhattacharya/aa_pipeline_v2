The sea, that day, was a vast, grey, tumbling expanse of wrath. The wind, a raw and boisterous spirit from the north, tore at the shawls of the few brave souls upon the sands and flung the spray of the churning waves far up the esplanade. I stood with my young charges, the Misses Harcourt, under the dubious shelter of the pier’s ironwork, watching the spectacle. Little Clara, her bonnet strings whipped into a frenzy, clung to my hand, while her elder sister, Jane, laughed at the wind’s assault. For them, it was a grand adventure. For me, it was a trial of endurance, not of the elements, but of my own spirit, which felt as bleak and besieged as the shore.

We were at Scarborough, a place of prescribed gaiety and restorative airs for the Harcourt family, who had come to escape the maladies of a London spring. For me, it was but a change of scenery in the unceasing performance of my duties. The Harcourts were not unkind people; they were merely oblivious, as the rich so often are to the internal weather of those who serve them. Mrs. Harcourt, a lady of delicate nerves and pronounced tastes, required my constant presence to manage her daughters, to read to her in the afternoons, and to endure her complaints about the quality of the sea-bathing. Mr. Harcourt, a man whose world was composed of ledgers and shipping schedules, saw me as a useful piece of furniture, well-mannered and efficient.

It was on the third day, as we walked along the crowded sands, that I saw her. A vision of silks and feathers, a bonnet of such elaborate construction it seemed a small architectural marvel, stood in stark relief against the drab backdrop of the sea. She was laughing, a light, tinkling sound, as a gentleman of prosperous appearance shielded her from the worst of the wind. As we drew nearer, her gaze fell upon me, and her laughter faltered. A look of dawning recognition, followed swiftly by a shadow of what I could only interpret as pity, crossed her fine features.

“Agnes? Agnes Leyton? Can it really be you?”

It was Isabella Croft. Isabella, who had sat beside me at Miss Wycliffe’s Academy for Young Ladies, whose ribbons I had mended and whose sorrows over a lost locket I had comforted. Now she was Mrs. Croft, and the evidence of her good fortune was manifest in every thread of her gown.

“Isabella,” I said, my voice sounding stiff and formal in my own ears. I curtsied, a habit ingrained by my position, which felt absurd before a former schoolfellow. “What a surprise.”

“Oh, you poor thing, you must not curtsy to me!” she cried, seizing my hands. Her own were gloved in soft kidskin; mine were bare and chapped from the salt air and the constant washing of childish hands. “We are old friends! But look at you, Agnes. In service? I had heard… well, one hears things. But I did not know it had come to this.”

Her words were not spoken with malice, but with a frank, unthinking candour that was infinitely more wounding. She drew me aside, her husband looking on with a benevolent, slightly bored air. “You are a governess? To these children?” She glanced at the Harcourt girls, who were now attempting to build a sandcastle against the wind’s assault.

“I am,” I replied, my chin held high. “I find the work agreeable.”

“Agnes, you were always so proud,” she said, her voice dropping to a confidential whisper. “So clever. You were head of the class in everything. It is a dreadful shame. Life can be so cruel. To think of you, tied to these… duties… while others…” She left the sentence unfinished, but her gesture, taking in her own fine attire and her waiting husband, completed the thought with brutal clarity.

A hot flush of shame and indignation rose in my cheeks. I felt myself to be an object of charity, a cautionary tale whispered about in drawing-rooms. The injustice of it stung me. I had worked for my bread, had not stooped to deceit or degradation to obtain it. My position, though humble, was one of honour. Yet in her eyes, I saw not a fellow woman earning her keep, but a fallen creature, a relic of a shared past that had diverged so painfully.

Before I could frame a reply that would not betray the tempest within me, Mrs. Harcourt called. “Agnes! The children are getting soaked. Do attend to them at once.”

The summons was like a whiplash. It was my place, my reality. I was the governess. I turned to Isabella. “If you will excuse me.”

“Of course, of course,” she said, her face a mask of sweet commiseration. “We are staying at the Grand Hotel. You must call on me, Agnes. We must talk. I might… I might be able to help you in some way. To find a situation less… taxing.”

I did not answer. I simply curtsied again, this time to her husband as well, and hurried back to my charges, my heart a cold, hard knot in my chest. The rest of the afternoon was a blur of enforced cheerfulness. I wiped Clara’s tears when a wave stole her pail; I fastened Jane’s bonnet for the tenth time; I listened to Mrs. Harcourt’s lamentations on the ruinous effect of the damp air on her complexion. All the while, Isabella’s words echoed in my mind: *You poor thing. A dreadful shame.*

That evening, after the children were abed and the house was quiet, I stood at my window, looking out at the dark, heaving sea. The moon had broken through the clouds, casting a cold, silver light upon the waves. The pity I had endured all day now turned inward, forcing me to a merciless examination. Was any part of it deserved? Was I not, in some measure, a figure of pity? I was twenty-six years old. My youth was passing. I had no home of my own, no prospect of marriage, no fortune. My world was circumscribed by the four walls of a nursery and the whims of my employers. I was, in the eyes of the world, and perhaps in the eyes of God, a woman who had failed to secure the chief end of her sex: a husband and a home.

A surge of self-pity, bitter as gall, rose within me. But as I watched the relentless, powerful waves crash against the shore and fall back, only to gather themselves and crash again, a different feeling took hold. It was not despair, but a fierce, quiet defiance. What was Isabella Croft’s pity worth? It was a sentiment that cost her nothing and gave her the satisfaction of her own benevolence. She saw my life and saw only lack. She did not see the strength it required, the daily discipline, the quiet satisfaction of a duty done well. She did not understand the fierce, protective love I felt for my charges, nor the integrity of my own mind, which I had kept, against all odds, uncrushed.

Let her pity me. Let the whole world pity me. My life was my own. My conscience was clear. I had not sold my soul for silks and a comfortable name. I stood at my post, poor and plain and alone, yet I was not broken. The wind howled outside, a wild and lonely sound, but within me, a small, steady flame of self-respect burned, unquenchable and bright. And in the face of that cold, majestic sea, I felt not shame, but a strange and solemn strength.
