It was in the spring of the year 1894 that my friend Mr. Penhaligon, whose remarkable gifts for the elucidation of obscure problems are already widely acknowledged in the west of England, received a letter which provoked in him that quiet but intense interest which I had learned to recognise as the precursor of an investigation. I was seated opposite him in his consulting room in Worcester when the post brought the missive, and I observed that he read it twice before laying it down.

"You recall, I dare say, the little parish of Amberley in Worcestershire," he remarked, turning to me. "I see from your expression that you do not. It is a scattered village some seven miles from here, remarkable for nothing except a rather fine Norman church. It appears that the church has been robbed of its silver communion chalice, a vessel of some antiquity and considerable value, and that the blame has been laid at the door of a family of gypsies who were encamped upon the common."

"And you think otherwise?" I asked, for I knew his habits.

"I think nothing until I have seen the evidence," he replied, with some asperity. "But the letter is from the vicar, a Reverend Morley, and he is a man of parts. He puts before me three facts which appear to have escaped the local constabulary. In the first place, he doubts the guilt of the gypsies. In the second, he has observed that his churchwarden, a Mr. Brazier, has within the fortnight paid off a debt of long standing which had been a heavy burden upon him. In the third, the bell-ringers, who were practising in the tower late on the night of the theft, are prepared to swear that they heard a horse upon the road at midnight."

He folded the letter and placed it in his pocket. "We will go to Amberley this morning," said he.

It was a fine morning in April, with a brisk wind blowing from the west and the hedgerows just beginning to show their green. We drove out in a light trap, and Penhaligon spoke little during the journey, sitting with his chin upon his breast and his keen grey eyes fixed upon the road ahead. It was not until we had turned into the lane which leads to Amberley that he roused himself.

"You will observe, doctor," said he, "that the common lies to the south of the church, and that the road to Worcester runs past the north gate of the churchyard. If the gypsies were concerned in this business, they must have carried the chalice across the open common and through the lych-gate, a proceeding which would have exposed them to observation from any of the cottages which face upon the green. It is not impossible, but it is improbable."

We drew up at the vicarage, a solid stone house adjoining the churchyard, and were shown into a study where Reverend Morley awaited us. He was a tall, thin man, elderly, with a face of that refined and scholarly cast which one associates with the better class of country clergyman. His hand, when I shook it, was the hand of a man accustomed to rowing or to some such open-air exercise, for the palm was hard and the grip firm.

"I am very much obliged to you for coming, Mr. Penhaligon," said he, in a quiet, measured voice. "This affair has caused a great deal of excitement in the parish, and I confess that the readiness with which people have condemned these gypsies troubles me. There is a prejudice against them in the countryside which is not always founded upon evidence."

"Tell me the facts, as briefly as you may," said Penhaligon.

"The chalice was kept in the vestry, in a locked cupboard. The vestry window was found open on Thursday morning, and the cupboard forced. The key to the vestry door was hung upon a nail inside the church, and the thieves—or thief—evidently knew where to find it. The gypsies had arrived on the Monday and left on the Wednesday, the day before the discovery. They were gone before the theft was known, which was taken as evidence of guilt. The constable from the village has been to their last known camp and found nothing."

"What of your churchwarden, this Mr. Brazier?" asked Penhaligon.

Reverend Morley's brow clouded. "Brazier is a farmer of substance, or was until these last hard years. He has been in debt to the tune of three hundred pounds, a sum which has pressed upon him grievously. I learned from his own son, who is frank in a way that the father is not, that the debt has been cleared within the week. Brazier himself does not know that I am aware of this, and I beg of you, Mr. Penhaligon, that you will treat the information with discretion."

Penhaligon nodded. "I should like to see the vestry, and I should like to speak with the bell-ringers."

We walked across the churchyard, Penhaligon noting, as we went, the position of the gates and the lie of the land. The vestry was a small stone chamber built onto the east end of the church. The window was of ordinary size, secured by a catch which might easily have been forced with a knife. Penhaligon examined the sill with his lens, then turned his attention to the floor within.

"Has the vestry been swept since the theft?" he asked.

"It has not," said the vicar.

"Then I am fortunate," said Penhaligon, and pointed to a faint mark upon the stone floor, a smudge of reddish clay such as might be brought in upon the sole of a boot. "There is a clay pit to the north of the Worcester road, is there not?"

"There is," said the vicar, with surprise. "Brazier's farm lies beyond it."

Penhaligon said nothing, but led us out through the north gate of the churchyard and along the road for some fifty yards. Here the hedgerow was broken by a farm gate, and the track beyond it led through muddy ground towards a cluster of buildings. "The bell-ringers heard a horse upon this road at midnight," he observed. "A man coming from Brazier's farm would naturally take this road. A man coming from the gypsy camp would not."

We returned to the church, where the bell-ringers, three stout fellows of the village, were assembled in the tower. They were plain men, and their testimony was simple. They had been ringing until half past eleven, and had lingered in the tower afterwards, smoking their pipes. At midnight, or very near it, they had heard a horse upon the road, proceeding at a walk towards Worcester. They had thought nothing of it at the time.

Penhaligon questioned them closely as to the sound. "One horse?" he asked.

"One horse, sir," said the eldest, a man named Grubb. "And a light cart, by the sound of it. I know a cart from a carriage, having driven both in my time."

"That is very helpful," said Penhaligon, and thanked them.

We drove then to Brazier's farm, which we found in some disorder, for Brazier was a widower and his household managed by a daughter who was not yet twenty. Brazier himself was in the yard, a heavy man, red of face, with the look of one who has borne anxiety and drink in equal measure. His coat was of rough tweed, and his boots, I could not help but observe, were caked with the same reddish clay which Penhaligon had remarked upon in the vestry.

Penhaligon introduced himself as an inquirer into the church robbery, and Brazier received him with a bluster which was in itself suggestive. He had nothing to do with it, he said, and was glad the gypsies had been found out, for they were a thieving lot. His eyes, however, shifted as he spoke, and his hands, which were large and coarse, plucked incessantly at the lapel of his coat.

"You were at home on Wednesday night?" asked Penhaligon, in his mildest manner.

"I was not. I was in Worcester, on business."

"You drove in?"

"I did."

"And returned at what hour?"

Brazier hesitated. "It was late," said he. "Past midnight, I should think."

"Past midnight," repeated Penhaligon. "And you drove in a light cart?"

"I did."

"And your business in Worcester—it was successful, I take it? You were able to settle your affairs?"

The man's face went white, then red again. "What do you mean by that?" he cried, with an oath.

"I mean," said Penhaligon, drawing himself up with a sudden severity, "that the chalice was taken from the vestry by a man who knew where the key was hung, who entered by the north gate, and who left reddish clay from the pit beside your farm upon the vestry floor. I mean that you were heard returning at midnight with a light cart, and that you have within the week paid a debt which you could not have discharged from your farming profits. I mean that you carried the chalice to Worcester and sold it, and that the gypsies had no more to do with the matter than I had."

Brazier stared at him, his mouth working, and then, with a groan, he sank down upon a bench and buried his face in his hands. The confession which followed was complete, and was afterwards confirmed in every particular. He had taken the chalice on the Wednesday evening, after the gypsies had departed, knowing that the blame would fall upon them. He had carried it to Worcester in his cart and sold it to a Jew in the back streets of the city for a sum which cleared his debts. The key, the clay, the cart, the hour—all were as Penhaligon had deduced.

We returned to the vicarage in time for a late dinner, and Reverend Morley, who was much moved, pressed us to stay the night. Penhaligon declined, but accepted a glass of wine.

"It was the clay that settled it," he said to me as we drove back in the dusk, the spring wind fresh upon our faces. "The gypsies had come from the south, where the soil is chalk. The clay was from the north, and from one farm only. There was the horse, too, which the bell-ringers heard, and which Brazier's own confession confirmed. I confess I have some sympathy for the man, for he was sorely pressed; but the law must take its course, and the gypsies must be cleared."
