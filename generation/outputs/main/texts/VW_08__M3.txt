The sun struck the courtyard at a slant, breaking itself into shards of white light against the paved stones, and Mrs. Thrale, sitting in the wicker chair beneath the great, glossy leaves of the magnolia tree, felt the warmth penetrate the thin stuff of her blouse, and penetrate further, into the still, dull ache of her bones. She had been sitting there for half an hour, holding a book she was not reading, while Edward slept upstairs behind the slatted shutters. He slept so much now; his breathing was a faint, dry rustle like the turning of thin paper. She had left the door of their room ajar, and the sound, or rather the absence of sound, floated down into the courtyard and sat beside her. He was a man who had dwindled, as if the damp of the English autumn had eaten into his very substance, leaving behind only the delicate, fretwork of his mind, which still turned out its precise, ironic observations, though in a voice that seemed to come from an immense distance.

She looked up, and there was the girl again, crossing the courtyard with her basket of lace. She moved with a kind of dark, unhurried grace, her bare feet making no sound on the hot stones. She was perhaps twenty, with the clear, olive skin of the island, and eyes that held the deep, liquid brown of the chestnuts that roasted in the streets below. Her name was Senhora Almeida—no, simply Almeida, for she was not married. Mrs. Thrale had spoken to her twice before, buying a collar of exquisite needlework that she did not want, and which lay folded in tissue paper in her dressing-case. The lace was very fine, almost as fine as the web of life itself, Mrs. Thrale thought, spread out and waiting to be broken.

"Good morning," said Mrs. Thrale.

The girl paused, and a slow, shy smile illuminated her face. She set down the basket on the edge of the fountain, where a thin, cool thread of water fell perpetually into a stone basin, making a sound like the ticking of a clock in an empty room.

"The English lady is well today?" she asked, in her slow, precise English, which had the curious effect of making each word seem isolated, a stone set apart from the next.

"Quite well, thank you," said Mrs. Thrale. She did not say that Edward had coughed blood again that morning, a bright, alarming stain on the white handkerchief that he had folded and hidden beneath his pillow, thinking she had not seen. She had seen it, and had gone out onto the balcony and looked at the town, the red roofs, the harbour, the sea, and had felt nothing, except a kind of distant surprise that the world should still be so brightly coloured.

She looked at the lace, spread out in the basket. It lay there like white foam, like the froth of the sea that one saw from the hotel terrace in the late afternoon. There was a scarf, a long, delicate thing, with a pattern of flowers and trailing leaves worked into it.

"That is very beautiful," she said, touching it.

"My mother made it," said the girl, with a sudden, proud directness that struck Mrs. Thrale like a physical force. "She works at it all night, by the lamp. She is going blind, but her fingers still know the way. They have always known the way."

Mrs. Thrale looked at the girl, and then down at the lace. She felt, suddenly, the immense distance between her own life and this girl's life; a distance not of miles, nor of wealth, but of something more fundamental, more inward. She thought of her own mother, in the drawing-room in Cheltenham, pouring tea, adjusting the antimacassar, her days measured out in small, precise rituals that had seemed, at the time, so important, so unassailable, but which now, in the bright, foreign light, appeared as insubstantial as the foam on the fountain.

"She must be a remarkable woman," Mrs. Thrale said.

"She is," said the girl simply. "She says that every stitch is a prayer. When she cannot see to pray with her lips, she prays with her fingers."

Mrs. Thrale felt something tighten in her throat. She thought of Edward, upstairs, with his thin, transparent hands folded on the counterpane, and his lips moving silently over the Greek text he still persisted in reading. He did not pray. He had never prayed. He believed in the mind, in the clear, cold light of reason, and now the mind was flickering, like a lamp in a draught, and there was nothing to take its place.

"You live with her, your mother?" Mrs. Thrale asked.

"Yes, in the house of my grandmother, up there," the girl pointed, beyond the hotel gardens, to the steep, terraced hillside where the houses clung like swallows' nests to the rock. "It is a long walk. But the air is good. And we have the garden, with the lemons, and the view of the sea. My brother, he works on the boats. Sometimes he is gone for weeks, and my mother waits, and makes lace, and prays. But I—" she paused, and the smile flashed out again, transforming her face, making it vivid, almost fierce, "I wish to go away. To Lisbon. To learn. To be something more than a woman who sits and waits."

Mrs. Thrale looked at her. She saw the whole life of the island in that glance; the dark house, the flickering lamp, the blind woman's fingers, the waiting, the lace, the sea, always the sea, stretching away to the horizon, promising everything, delivering nothing. And she saw, too, her own life, her safe, comfortable, English life, with its seasons and its servants and its illnesses and its small, unspoken accommodations, and she felt, for the first time, a kind of envy, a kind of shame. For she had never wanted anything so fiercely as this girl wanted to go to Lisbon. She had never wanted anything at all, perhaps.

"We might all of us wish to be something more," Mrs. Thrale said, and her voice was gentle, and a little sad.

The girl looked at her, and for a moment their eyes met, and Mrs. Thrale had the curious sensation of being seen, not as the English lady, the woman with the invalid husband and the money and the wicker chair, but as herself, as Eleanor, as the woman who had once, long ago, wanted to paint pictures, and had not painted them, because it had not seemed worth while, because there had been other things to do, other claims, other duties, and the years had passed, and the desire had shrivelled up, like a leaf in a fire, leaving only a little grey ash.

"You are sad," said the girl, not as a question, but as a statement.

"No, not sad," said Mrs. Thrale. She looked down at her hands, lying in her lap. They were still smooth, well-kept, the hands of a woman who had never done any rough work. But there was a faint brown stain on her forefinger, the ghost of a stain left by the silver nitrate she had used, years ago, in the darkroom she had set up in the linen cupboard at home, before Edward had fallen ill, before everything had changed. She had been clever at it; she had had a sense for the way light fell, for the moment when the image emerged, ghost-like, from the blankness of the paper. She had given it up. She could not remember why.

"I will buy the scarf," she said, suddenly, and the girl's face lighted up.

She paid for it, and the girl folded it and wrapped it in paper, and they sat for a while in silence, listening to the fountain. The water fell, and fell, and fell, each drop distinct, and yet merging, in the ear, into one continuous sound. Mrs. Thrale thought of the lace, and the blind woman's fingers, and the long nights on the hillside, and the sea below, dark and immense. She thought of Edward, sleeping, and of the Greek text, and of the blood on the handkerchief. She thought of her mother in Cheltenham, and of the drawing-room, and of the tea. And she thought, too, of the girl, going home in the evening, up the steep path, through the lemon-garden, to the dark house and the blind mother and the waiting, and of the girl's fierce, bright wish to go to Lisbon, to learn, to be something more.

The sun moved across the courtyard, and the shadows of the magnolia leaves shifted on the stones, making a pattern, like lace, like the foam of the sea, like the web of life itself, spread out and waiting. And Mrs. Thrale sat in her wicker chair, and held the parcel of lace in her lap, and felt the warmth of the sun on her face, and knew that she was, for this moment at least, alive, and that this was enough.
