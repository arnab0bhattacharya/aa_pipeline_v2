Mr. Arthur Pym, a clerk in the Employers’ Liability Department of the Eagle and Anchor Insurance Company, was a man whose life was governed by the principles of order and predictability. His bowler hat hung on its designated peg, his morning paper was ironed and read at seven-fifteen, and the 7:42 train to Waterloo was as fixed a point in his universe as the North Star. It was therefore with a sense of profound and dislocating shock that he awoke on the Tuesday of that fateful week to find the world had been rendered illegible.

The first sign was the silence. The 7:42, a punctual rumble that usually punctuated his shaving, was absent. In its place was a strange, wet stillness. Pym, a man who trusted his senses as little as he trusted an unsolicited offer, put down his razor and went to the window. The tidy privet hedge that marked the boundary of his garden was gone. In its place was a vast, shimmering expanse of brown water, lapping gently against the sill of the ground floor. The neat line of semi-detached villas on Cedar Avenue, each with its little lawn and its little pride, now resembled a chain of islands in a muddy, continental lake.

By mid-morning, the entire street had assembled on the upper balconies and at the bedroom windows of the more fortunate houses. There was old Mr. Henderson, the retired chartered accountant, who kept insisting it was merely a "fluvial anomaly" and that the municipal authorities would have it sorted by luncheon. There was Mrs. Gable, a widow of robust sensibilities, who had already organised a rota for the sharing of tinned goods and was loudly proclaiming the whole affair a "damned nuisance." And there was young Mr. Finch, a junior clerk at the bank, who was possessed of a nervous energy and a subscription to *The Strand Magazine*, and who saw in the rising tide not an inconvenience, but the beginning of a grand and terrible adventure.

"It's the Thames, Pym!" he called across the water, his voice unnaturally loud in the quiet air. "The whole valley must be under! They'll have to send the army, you mark my words!"

Pym, who had managed to retrieve his morning paper from the letterbox—now a floating receptacle—found it sodden and useless. The world of dividends, shipping forecasts, and parliamentary squabbles had been washed away. In its place was a single, overwhelming fact: the water. It was not the clean, sparkling water of the river he knew, but a thick, opaque soup of silt, debris, and drowned things. It carried a fence post, a child's hoop, and once, a bloated and unidentifiable parcel that drifted past with an air of grim purpose.

The first day passed in a state of bewildered inactivity. The novelty of the situation provided a certain grim entertainment. People rowed between houses in washtubs and on doors, exchanging news and rumour. A boat from the Hampton Waterworks, its engine chugging ineffectually, passed down the centre of what had been the High Street, its crew shouting that the main pumping station was flooded and that the situation was "in the hands of the Committee." This Committee, it transpired, was a vague and distant body in Kingston, and its hands, it seemed, were as full as everyone else's.

By Wednesday, the novelty had curdled into anxiety. The water had climbed another foot. The ground floors were now entirely submerged, and the damp, chill air was a constant reminder of the creeping, insidious nature of the flood. Pym had salvaged his ledgers and a tin of biscuits, and he sat in his bedroom, trying to make sense of his household accounts, as if the double-entry system might somehow impose order on the chaos outside. It was a futile exercise. The world no longer balanced.

The Committee, when its first official communication arrived, did little to restore confidence. It was not a telegram, or a proclamation, but a man in a rowing boat with a megaphone, who paddled slowly down Cedar Avenue shouting platitudes. "Remain calm! Do not attempt to travel! Help is being organised! The situation is being assessed!"

"Assessed!" bellowed Mr. Henderson from his balcony. "My cellar is being assessed by a foot of foul water! What is being done, man? What is being done?"

The man with the megaphone merely shrugged and paddled on, his voice fading into the general dampness. The failure of authority was a more profound shock than the failure of the weather. They were accustomed to a world of rules, of inspectors, of regulations that governed everything from the height of a garden wall to the proper procedure for a train derailment. But there was no rule for this. The officials who arrived in their own boats were as helpless as anyone else, their faces pale with the same dawning realisation that the great, intricate machine of British civilisation had seized.

Young Finch, however, was thriving. He had constructed a raft from a door and two oil drums, and he spent his days ferrying supplies and gossip, his face alight with the thrill of the unprecedented. "It's like the last days of Pompeii, Pym!" he declared, pulling alongside Pym's window. "Or a story by Mr. Wells! We are witnessing the collapse of the old order!"

Pym, shivering in his damp flannel shirt, did not share his enthusiasm. He saw no grandeur in the spectacle, only waste and ruin. He thought of the Eagle and Anchor, of the neat stacks of policies in his office, each one a small, man-made bulwark against misfortune. What policy could cover this? What actuary could have calculated the premium for the complete and utter submersion of the Thames valley?

On the third day, the rain began again, a steady, miserable drizzle that made the world a uniform grey. The water was now halfway up the ground-floor windows. A dead sheep floated past, its wool matted and dark. The smell was no longer just of mud, but of decay. The cheerful bustle of the first day was gone, replaced by a grim, watchful silence. The sharing of tinned goods had become a more serious business, and Mr. Henderson had ceased his pronouncements on municipal engineering.

Pym stood at his window, looking out at the drowned world. The certainty of his life—the 7:42, the ironed paper, the bowler hat on its peg—was a memory from another age. He was no longer an insurance clerk; he was simply a man, marooned in a bedroom, his future as murky and uncertain as the water below. He saw Mrs. Gable being helped into Finch's raft, her face set in a mask of grim determination. They were abandoning the houses, heading for the higher ground of the common. The Committee, it seemed, had finally organised something.

Finch looked up at Pym's window. "Coming, Pym? We're making for the church! It's on the hill!"

For a moment, Pym hesitated. His house, his possessions, his orderly life, were all he had. To leave them felt like a final, irrevocable surrender. But then he looked at the water, now lapping at the very glass of the window, and he understood. The order was gone. The world he knew was submerged. He gave a short, sharp nod, gathered his tin of biscuits and his insurance ledgers—some habits die hard—and began to climb down the trellis towards the waiting raft. There was no longer any question of assessment or procedure. There was only the water, and the slow, uncertain struggle to rise above it.
