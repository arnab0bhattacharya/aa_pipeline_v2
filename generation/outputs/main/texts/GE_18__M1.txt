There are few spectacles more instructive to the philosophic mind than the slow displacement of an old human order by a new one, provided the spectator has the leisure to observe it without the inconvenience of participating in the change. Such a displacement was in progress at Harefoot Down in the year 1814, where the ancient and almost geological relationship between a man and the soil he had trodden for forty years was being dissolved by the sharp solvent of a commercial lease. The old tenant, Mr. Pyke, a man whose grazing had been conducted on the principle that a good shepherd is never hurried, had died in the winter; and the farm had been taken by Mr. Grimes, a grazier from the Vale, whose mind worked with the rapidity of a calculating machine and who measured every living thing—sheep, horse, or man—by its immediate utility to the balance sheet.

It was Grimes who stood now in the yard of the stone farmhouse, confronting the shepherd, Amos Harker. Amos was a man of seventy-two, whose figure had acquired the stoop of the Downland hills themselves, and whose face was a map of the seasons he had weathered. He stood with his hands clasped behind his back, holding a crook that was as much a part of him as his own shinbone, while the new master, a stout man in a coat of London cloth, delivered the verdict that was to uproot him.

"You be too old, Harker," said Grimes, not unkindly, but with the firmness of a man stating a mathematical fact. "The Down is no place for a man that can't run the collar round a ewe in a wet gale. I wants a young man with legs to him. You've had your day."

To the observer, it might seem that the injustice lay in the dismissal of a faithful servant; but the deeper tragedy, as is often the case in the economy of human suffering, lay in the sudden severance of a man from his own identity. Amos had not merely worked on Harefoot Down; he was the animating intelligence of its flocks. He knew the temper of the ewes, the treachery of the drifts, and the history of every stone in the walls. To be told he was "too old" was not merely to be told he could no longer run; it was to be told that his memory, his experience, and his very soul were obsolete commodities.

"I've seen the sun rise on this Down afore you was breeched, Master Grimes," Amos said, his voice low and roughened by the wind and the dialect of the vale. "I knows the sheep better'n they knows theirselves."

"Knowing don't put meat on the bone, old man," Grimes replied, turning to look at a stack of hurdles. "I can't have you falling in a ditch and me liable. You'll have your month's wage, and a shilling over. But come Lady Day, I wants the cottage for the new man."

Amos said no more. He knew the futility of arguing with a man who viewed the world through the lens of a ledger. He turned and walked slowly toward the low stone cottage that abutted the farm buildings, where the smoke rose thin and grey against the April sky. It was here that the second actor in this small drama awaited him: his grandson, Thomas.

Thomas was a boy of twelve, sharp-featured and bright-eyed, possessing that quick, restless intelligence which is often born in the shadow of labor. He had been taught his letters by the curate’s wife, who saw in him a vessel worth filling, and he had seized upon reading with the hunger of a starving man at a banquet. When Amos entered, the boy looked up from a tattered volume of *Robinson Crusoe*, and seeing the set of his grandfather's jaw and the dullness in his eyes, he knew at once that the battle was lost.

"He've turned me off, Tommy," Amos said, sinking into the Windsor chair by the hearth. "Says I be past it."

Thomas closed the book. "Past it? You know more about the ewes than he'll ever learn if he lives to be a hundred."

"Aye, but knowledge don't run, lad. And I be a burden now. We be to leave at Lady Day."

The boy’s face flushed with a heat that was not of the fire. It was the indignation of the weak against the strong, sharpened by a dawning sense of the world’s mechanics. He knew, from his reading, of kings and landlords and the great chain of command. He knew that Grimes was not the ultimate master; there was a man in London, a Sir William Ellesmere, who owned the soil, and who, so the talk went, had never once set foot upon it since he inherited it.

"He can't do it," Thomas said, his voice hard. "Not without the Squire says so. The Squire is the landlord. He's supposed to look after the tenantry. It's in the books."

"Books be for gentlefolk, Tommy," Amos sighed, weary and beaten. "The Squire don't know Amos Harker from a gate-post. He knows Grimes pays the rent. That's all the Squire knows."

"Then we must tell him," said the boy. "I'll write a letter."

Here we see the collision of two worlds: the ancient, oral world of the shepherd, who accepts fate as he accepts the weather, and the new, literate world of the boy, who believes that words on paper can alter the axis of reality. Amos looked at his grandson with a mixture of pride and pity. He knew the boy was clever—too clever for the plough, perhaps—but he also knew the height of the wall they were trying to climb. A letter to a baronet? It was like shouting at the moon.

"What's the use, lad? The paper won't stop the rain, nor Grimes neither."

But Thomas was already at the deal table, pulling out a sheet of precious paper and the ink. His mind was working rapidly, analyzing the situation not with the slow wisdom of experience, but with the quick logic of literacy. He knew he must write not as a complaining child, but as a man of business. He must use the right words—words that would catch the eye of a gentleman who dealt in property and obligation.

For an hour, the only sound was the scratching of the quill and the wind rattling the casement. Thomas wrote with a furrowed brow, pausing to think of the grandest words he knew. He wrote of "forty years of faithful service," of the "ancient custom of the Down," and of the "injustice of age." He folded the paper, directed it to *Sir William Ellesmere, Bart., Grosvenor Square, London*, and sealed it with a blob of candle wax, impressed with a button.

"I'll take it to the post-mistress," the boy said. "She owes Grandmother for the eggs."

"It's a fool's errand, Tommy," Amos said, though he reached out to touch the boy's shoulder. "But you've a good heart."

"A fool's errand is better than no errand at all," Thomas replied, with a precocious gravity.

We may smile at the boy's presumption, at the naive belief that a sheet of paper can bridge the chasm between a stone cottage on the Downs and a mansion in Mayfair. And yet, if we look closely at the machinery of history, we often find that great changes are set in motion by such small, desperate levers. The letter traveled by the stagecoach, bumped over the rutted roads, and arrived in London three days later. It sat on the hall table of Sir William Ellesmere for a week, buried under invitations to dine and notices of parliamentary votes.

But Sir William was a man of some conscience, if of little industry. One rainy Tuesday, finding himself bored and looking for a distraction, he picked up the child’s scrawl. He read it through once, then again. He was touched, as men of leisure often are, by the pathetic details of labor they never see. He was also annoyed, as men of property are, by the thought of a grasping tenant displacing a family that had held the land for a generation.

"Forty years," Sir William murmured to his wife. "And this new man, Grimes, wants to turn him out for a younger leg. It is very unfeeling. Very unfeeling indeed."

He sat down at his own mahogany desk, far grander than the deal table at Harefoot Down, and wrote a note to his agent. He did not mention the boy’s letter directly, for he thought it beneath his dignity to be swayed by a child; but he instructed the agent to inquire into the treatment of the old shepherd at Harefoot Down, and to make it clear that the landlord preferred to see long service rewarded rather than discarded.

The wheels of the universe ground slowly, but they ground. The agent wrote to Grimes; Grimes, fearing the loss of his lease, swallowed his irritation. The young man who was to take the cottage was sent away, and Amos Harker was told he might stay on to mind the gates and the lambs, though he could no longer walk the high ridges.

Thomas watched the letter go and watched it return in the form of his grandfather’s reprieve. He learned a lesson that no book could teach him: that while the world is ruled by power and money, it is also susceptible to the pressure of a well-placed word. He returned to his *Robinson Crusoe* with a new understanding of the island he inhabited—a world where a boy with a pen might yet carve out a place for the old and the weary. And Amos, sitting by the fire, looked at the boy and felt the strange, sweet sorrow of a man who knows that his own time is passing, but that the future is in hands younger and quicker than his own.
