Miss Harriet Ashby was not, by nature, a woman of profound sorrows, but she possessed a sensibility that could be easily wounded by the misfortunes of her friends, and a private attachment that made those misfortunes her own. It was with a heavy heart, therefore, that she received the summons from her dearest Miss Sophia Waverley, whose note, penned in a hasty hand, requested her presence at Waverley Park with a urgency that admitted of no refusal.

Upon her arrival, Harriet found her friend in a state of agitation quite foreign to her usually cheerful temper. Sophia led her to a small, unoccupied parlour, where the fire was low and the afternoon light struggled through the windows.

“Harriet,” she began, seizing her hands, “I have terrible news, and I have a terrible request to make of you. It is of my brother.”

Harriet felt her own colour rise, a sensation she hoped was attributed to the suddenness of the summons. “Of Mr. Waverley? Is he unwell?”

“He is unwell in fortune, if not in body,” Sophia replied, her voice dropping to a near-whisper. “He has been in town this fortnight past, and… he has been unfortunate at cards. Terribly, ruinously unfortunate. The sum is one he cannot possibly honour.”

The floor of the parlour seemed to tilt beneath Harriet’s feet. The image of Mr. Edmund Waverley—his quiet humour, his thoughtful brow, the easy grace with which he moved through a room—was suddenly shadowed by this intelligence. “But surely,” she managed, “a loan can be procured? An arrangement with his creditors?”

Sophia shook her head, a single tear tracing a path down her cheek. “The gentlemen with whom he played are not of a patient disposition. There is a man, a Mr. Croft, who holds the largest note, and he has made it plain that he will not be trifled with. There is but one recourse. My father has arranged for him to sail for Barbados on the packet from Portsmouth, to take up a position in the family’s sugar concerns. He leaves us in a sennight.”

“Barbados!” The word was a shock. It spoke of distance, of heat, of a life utterly removed from the gentle routines of Sussex. It spoke of an end.

“He will be gone for years,” Sophia continued, “and in that time, we may hope to settle his affairs. But Harriet, you must understand the delicacy of our position. If it were to become known that he is fleeing his debts, his name would be utterly ruined. He would be thought a common gamester, a man without honour. My father insists upon the matter being presented as an opportunity, a venture for a young man of spirit.”

She paused, her grip on Harriet’s hands tightening. “We are to have a small dinner this evening, to see him off. Our aunt, Lady Beatrice, will be there, and the Penfolds from the vicarage. I beg of you, dearest Harriet, for the sake of our friendship and for Edmund’s future prospects, you will not betray, by look or word, that you know the true nature of his departure. You must treat it as a happy, if sad, occasion. Can you do this for me?”

What could Harriet say? To refuse would be to add to her friend’s distress; to consent was to enter into a conspiracy of silence that felt like a betrayal of all the warm, unspoken regard she had held for Edmund Waverley. She could only press Sophia’s hands in return and murmur her assent, her own heart a cold and heavy stone in her breast.

The evening was a masterpiece of polite deception. The table was laid with the Waverley’s finest silver, the candles cast a flattering glow, and Mrs. Waverley, a lady whose fortitude was her most notable quality, spoke of the West Indies as if it were a fashionable resort.

“Edmund has always had a taste for adventure,” she declared to the assembled company, her voice ringing with a false cheerfulness. “And the climate, they say, is exceedingly beneficial for a certain constitution.”

Lady Beatrice, a formidable woman whose opinions were delivered as pronouncements, nodded sagely. “Indeed. A change of scene is the making of a young man. It will cure him of the languor of this English winter.”

Harriet sat opposite Edmund, and found it almost impossible to meet his eye. When she did, she saw not the adventurer his mother described, but a young man held captive by circumstance. His smile was a strained and painful thing, and he spoke little, contributing only when directly addressed. His usual wit was absent, replaced by a profound and mortified politeness.

“Miss Ashby,” he said, when the conversation turned to her, “you are very quiet this evening. I trust you are not unwell?”

“I am quite well, thank you, Mr. Waverley,” she replied, the lie tasting like ash in her mouth. “I was merely thinking of the vastness of the ocean you are to cross.”

“It is a sobering thought,” he agreed, his gaze dropping to his plate. “But one must go where one’s prospects lead.”

The conversation was a tightrope walk of euphemism. His creditors were ‘unforeseen circumstances’; his flight was ‘a sudden resolution’; his ruin was ‘a change of fortune’s wheel’. Sophia, at the other end of the table, would catch Harriet’s eye with a look of desperate gratitude, a silent communication that was more painful than any words. Harriet felt a strange pity for them all—the parents performing their roles, the friend bound by loyalty, and the man at the centre of it all, trapped in a prison of his own making.

At last, the gentlemen did not join the ladies, the hour being late and Edmund’s departure early the next morning. The party broke up, with much talk of fair winds and prosperous voyages. As Harriet prepared to leave, Edmund approached her.

“Miss Ashby,” he said, his voice low so that only she might hear. “I thank you for your presence tonight. It has meant a great deal to me to have our friends here.”

His sincerity was so genuine that it nearly broke her resolve. She wanted to say, *Do not go. Stay and face them. We will find a way.* She wanted to tell him that his honour was not in his purse, but in his character.

Instead, she could only offer the paltry comfort of convention. “We shall all miss you, Mr. Waverley. Sussex will be a duller place without you. I wish you every success in your new venture.”

He looked at her then, and in his eyes she saw a flicker of something—gratitude, yes, but also a deep and abiding sadness, as if he knew the inadequacy of the words they were forced to speak. “Thank you, Miss Ashby,” he said simply. “Your good wishes are the kindest I have received.”

He bowed, and turned away. Harriet was left with the sound of the door closing, the scent of the snuffed candles, and the terrible, unspoken truth hanging in the air between them, a truth as vast and impassable as the ocean that would soon separate them. She had held her tongue, as she had promised, but in the silence, she felt she had lost a part of herself.
