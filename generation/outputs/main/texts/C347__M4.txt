The fever had passed, leaving Halloran hollowed out and light-headed, and it was in this state of queer clarity that he first noticed the discrepancy. He was crouched in the stern of the pirogue, his surveyor’s theodolite packed away in its greasy canvas, a map case balanced on his knees. The map was the work of a man named Pemberton, who had died of blackwater fever two years prior, and whose careful, spidery hand had laid out the course of the Kivari River like a promise. It showed, at a bend where the river widened into a sluggish brown lake, a village called Saint-Éloi-sur-Kivari, complete with a mission, a trading post, and a grid of native huts. A little further on, another, called Nduku, and then a third, Mbala, with a cross-hatched symbol that Pemberton’s legend identified as a fortified stockade.

The sun was a white-hot hammer on the water. The air was thick with the smell of mud and rot and the sweet, cloying perfume of some unseen blossom. Halloran raised his head and looked at the bank. There was no mission. There was no trading post. There were no huts. There was only the unbroken wall of the forest, a green immensity that seemed to press down on the river, and a single, skeletal palm tree leaning out over the water like a drunken sentinel. He looked back at the map. According to Pemberton, he should be looking at the spire of a church. He wiped the sweat from his brow and called a halt.

His head man, a coastal man named Kwame who had been up this river before with Pemberton, brought the paddlers to a stop. The canoe drifted, turning slowly in the current. “Bwana?” Kwame said, his voice flat.

“Kwame, look here,” Halloran said, jabbing a finger at the map. “Pemberton’s chart. It says there is a village here. Saint-Éloi. Where is it?”

Kwame squinted at the paper, his face a mask of patient indifference. He had learned to read a little, enough to recognize the shapes of words, but he did not trust paper. He trusted the river. “The river has many faces, bwana,” he said. “It changes. The water eats the bank. The forest takes it back.”

“A whole village, Kwame? A mission? The forest does not eat a church in two years.”

Kwame shrugged, a gesture that was both deferential and dismissive. He spoke to the paddlers in their own tongue, a rapid, clicking language that Halloran could not follow. They laughed, a short, sharp sound that was quickly swallowed by the vast silence of the forest. Halloran felt a flush of anger, but it was a weak, distant emotion, lost in the fog of his convalescence. He made a note in his logbook: *No sign of St-Éloi. Enquire at next settlement.* He was a man of precision, of triangulation and measurement, and he did not like mysteries. He liked them even less when they were scrawled in another man’s hand across a landscape that refused to corroborate them.

They pushed on. The river narrowed, the current quickening. The following day, they came to the place where Nduku should have been. Halloran had the men paddle close to the bank, and he walked the shoreline for a hundred yards in each direction, his boots sinking into the black mud. He found nothing. No post-holes, no shards of pottery, no blackened stones of a hearth. Nothing but the primal, indifferent forest. He felt a cold knot tighten in his stomach that had nothing to do with the fever. Pemberton had been a careful man. His notes on the river’s depth, on the sandbars, on the treacherous currents, were all accurate to the inch. Why would he invent villages?

The third, Mbala, was the most detailed on the map. Pemberton had noted the stockade was built of timber from the forest, that the chief was a man named Osei, and that the village was the center of a thriving trade in rubber and ivory. When they reached the designated bend, a place where the river curled around a great, grey rock shaped like a sleeping animal, Halloran was not surprised to find nothing. He had half-expected it. The men beached the pirogue and made a fire, their earlier amusement replaced by a quiet unease. They too, it seemed, felt the wrongness of the place. The forest was too quiet here. There were no birds.

That night, Halloran sat by the fire, his map case open on his lap. He held Pemberton’s chart and his own, still largely blank, sheets of paper. The company’s instructions were clear. He was to verify and update Pemberton’s survey. The directors in London, he knew, had already used Pemberton’s map to float a new issue of shares, promising investors a river lined with friendly villages and lucrative trade. He had seen the prospectus himself, a grand document with a lithograph of a bustling Saint-Éloi-sur-Kivari, its church spire pointing to a civilized heaven. He was the company’s man on the spot, the sole arbiter of the truth. The truth was that there was nothing. The river was a vein of brown water cutting through a continent of silence. The villages were a fiction.

He thought of Pemberton, a man he had never met, a man whose bones were likely bleaching somewhere upriver. Why had he done it? Had he been mad? Had the fever taken his mind before it took his life? Or had he been a liar, a fabulist who had found it easier to draw a world than to map the one he was in? Halloran imagined him, hunched over his table in a tent, the heat and the insects and the loneliness pressing in on him, drawing his own ghosts, his own hopes, into the empty space of the map. He had drawn a river that was not empty, but full of people, of commerce, of the comforting shapes of home. He had drawn a lie, but perhaps it had been a necessary one, a shield against the crushing nullity of the place. And now the lie had become a fact, a financial instrument, a promise sold to men in warm, well-lit rooms in Europe who would never smell the mud of the Kivari.

The decision lay before him, as tangible as the paper and the ink. He could draw his map truthfully. He could mark the long, empty stretches, the failed settlements, the absence of trade. He could write a report that would reach London and cause the shares to collapse, ruining the directors, and perhaps ruining himself. He would be the man who had unmasked the fraud, but who would hire a man who unmasked frauds? He would be the bringer of bad news, the destroyer of a beautiful, profitable dream.

Or he could corroborate. He could take Pemberton’s map and copy it, adding a few of his own flourishes, a note about a promising tributary here, a new sandbar there. He could give the directors what they had paid for. He could make the lie true by putting his own name to it. The river would still be empty, but the map would be full. The villages would live on paper, a testament to the power of a line drawn in ink. Who was to say which was more real? The muddy, empty bank, or the neat, hopeful settlement on the chart? One was the truth of the world; the other was the truth of the men who owned it.

He looked at Kwame, who was staring into the fire, his dark face unreadable. “Kwame,” he said softly. “The villages on Pemberton’s map. Did you see them? When you came here with him?”

Kwame did not look at him. He picked up a stick and poked at the embers. “We saw what Bwana Pemberton saw,” he said, after a long silence. His voice was quiet, but it carried the weight of a profound and ancient wisdom. He was telling Halloran that a man sees what he needs to see, and that there was no profit in seeing otherwise.

Halloran nodded slowly. He understood. He took the cap from his pen, a small, decisive movement. He dipped the nib in the ink and, with a steady hand, began to draw. He drew the bend of the river, the great rock, and then, with a few careful strokes, the outline of a stockade. He drew the neat grid of a village and marked it Mbala. He worked through the night, the fire his only light, his map a ghost story he was writing for the living. The ink was black and final, and it smelled of the future.
