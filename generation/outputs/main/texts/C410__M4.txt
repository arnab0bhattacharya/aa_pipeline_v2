The mail coach set her down at the edge of Lichfield at four in the afternoon, in a thin, persistent rain that seemed to have been falling since the beginning of the world. Ellen Marsh stood beside her corded trunk and watched the coach wheel away, its lamps already lit against the gathering dark, and felt the last of her old life go with it. Her father’s failure had been swift and total, a collapse of bills and promises that had left them with nothing but a small annuity from a distant cousin and the necessity of her removal. She was to keep house for her uncle, Mr. Thomas Barrow, a solicitor of some repute in this cathedral town, a man she had met but twice in her life and remembered only as a tall, grey silence.

His house was in a close off the main street, a narrow, three-storeyed brick building that leaned its shoulder against its neighbour as if for support. The maid who answered her knock was a girl no older than herself, with a raw, reddened face, and she took Ellen’s trunk with a grunt and led her into a parlour that was tidy to the point of severity. A fire burned low in the grate. The air smelled of coal and old paper. Her uncle rose from a chair by the window, a newspaper folded precisely on his knee. He was as she remembered him, dry as a husk, his face all planes and angles, his hair the colour of ash.

“Ellen,” he said, and offered his hand. His grip was brief and cool. “You will be tired. Susan will show you to your room. We dine at six.”

That was the whole of her welcome. Her room was small and clean, with a window that looked out over a jumble of roofs to the pale, improbable bulk of the cathedral, its three spires piercing the mist like needles. She unpacked her few things and sat on the edge of the bed, listening to the rain and the distant clang of a bell, and thought that she had never been so alone. She had expected misery, and here it was, punctual as the dinner hour.

The days that followed established a pattern of such unvarying dullness that Ellen felt she might scream from it. Her uncle rose early, breakfasted on toast and tea in a silence broken only by the rustle of his newspaper, and departed for his office in the cathedral close. He returned at five, ate a dinner of plain roast mutton or boiled fowl, and then retired to his study, where he remained until eleven, when he would ascend the stairs with the same measured tread and close his door. He spoke to her of household matters only: the price of candles, the need for a new scuttle, the advisability of airing the linen. He never asked her a question about herself, and she soon ceased to offer any information. She felt herself becoming invisible, a ghost in a house of quiet, deliberate shades.

She took to walking in the afternoons, when her duties were done. She walked the length of the close, past the cathedral’s great west front, its stone saints weathered to featureless nubs, and down by the millpond where the water lay flat and brown under the winter sky. She wrote letters to her mother that were cheerful lies, and she read the few novels she could find in her uncle’s scant library, books of sermons and a battered copy of *The Seasons*. She was lonely with a loneliness that had a taste, like copper on the tongue.

It was in January, in the deep, dead heart of the winter, that the first thing broke. She had been sent to her uncle’s study to fetch a ledger he had forgotten, a room she entered only under strict instruction. It was a cold, square room, lined with books that looked as though they had never been opened. His desk was a battlefield of papers, all neatly ordered. And there, on the mantelpiece, propped against a tarnished clock, was a small, slim volume, its spine cracked with use. She picked it up. It was a copy of Cowper’s poems, and it fell open in her hand to a page marked with a slip of paper. The poem was “The Castaway.” She read a few lines, and then a few more, and felt a strange, cold finger trace her spine. The book was worn soft as cloth at the corners, the pages thumbed grey. It was not a book of sermons. It was a book of secrets.

She put it back exactly as she had found it, but the discovery lodged in her like a splinter. Her uncle, the dry, silent lawyer, read poetry. He read it in secret, in this cold room, while she sat alone in the parlour. The knowledge changed the shape of the house. It was as if she had found a hidden door in a familiar wall.

A week later, she heard him. She had risen in the night for a glass of water and was passing the study door, which stood ajar. A candle burned within, and by its light she saw him, not at his desk, but in his chair by the dead fire, a book open on his knee. He was not reading to himself. He was murmuring, his voice a low, rough thread, the words just audible in the stillness. He was reciting, his head bowed, one hand pressed to his brow. She caught a line, something about a “darkness visible,” and then he stopped, and was silent for a long time, and she crept away to her bed, her heart beating hard.

She began to watch him. She noticed how his hand would sometimes stray to his waistcoat pocket, where a small, flat object bulged. She noticed how, on certain days—the anniversary of something, perhaps—he would be even more silent, his face a mask of stone. She noticed that he never spoke of his past, and that no letters ever came for him but those of business. He was a man who had been unhappy for so long that his unhappiness had become a kind of architecture, a structure he lived within.

One evening in February, a fierce gale blew up from the west, rattling the windows and sending the rain in sheets against the glass. The fire in the parlour smoked, and Susan was weeping in the kitchen over a letter from her sweetheart. Ellen, unable to bear the close, unhappy air, took a candle and went to the study door. It was open. Her uncle sat as she had seen him before, a book in his lap, but his head was in his hands. He did not hear her. She saw the book was not Cowper this time, but a smaller, older volume, its leather cover worn to suede. She saw the object from his pocket lying on the table beside him: a miniature portrait, oval, in a plain gold frame. The face was a woman’s, young, with dark hair and a sad, sweet smile.

She must have made a sound, for he looked up. His face was not angry. It was stripped bare, naked with a grief so old and so deep that she felt herself flush with shame at having seen it. He did not speak. He simply looked at her, and then at the portrait, and then back at her.

“She was your aunt,” he said, his voice a dry whisper. “She died. A fever. Thirty years ago this month.” He paused, his fingers tracing the edge of the little frame. “I was to have been a different man.”

He said no more. He closed the book and put the portrait in his pocket and wished her good night. But something had shifted. The next evening, he left the study door open, and she sat in the parlour with her sewing, and from the other room came the low, steady murmur of his voice, reading aloud—to himself, or to the ghost of her, or perhaps, she thought, to her. He read from Cowper, and from Burns, and once, hesitantly, from a poem of his own composition, a clumsy, tender thing about a lark in a summer sky. She never told him she heard. She never spoke of it. But she stopped writing cheerful lies to her mother. She began to write the truth: that the winter was long, and the house was quiet, and that she was learning that silence has many rooms, and that in one of them, a man was reading poetry to a girl who was no longer quite so miserable, or quite so alone.
