‘The proper basis for all society,’ said Mr. Evelyn Rooth, examining the ice in his champagne with the air of a jeweller appraising a flawed diamond, ‘is a mutual misunderstanding. We call it manners.’

‘And love?’ asked Lady Constance, fanning herself with a programme from the Royal Academy.

‘Love,’ replied Mr. Rooth, ‘is merely the most expensive misunderstanding of all. It is the only error that people are willing to pay for with their liberty.’

The drawing-room of the Duchess of Belgravia was a riot of orchids and expensive ennui. It was the hour of the evening when the conversation had turned from the scandalous to the merely frivolous, and the guests, like the flowers in the tall Sèvres vases, looked slightly overblown in the heat. Rooth stood apart from them all, a pale, cynical figure in a perfectly cut evening suit, regarding the world with the detached amusement of one who has long ago discovered that life is a farce, but lacks the energy to leave the theatre.

‘You are a terrible man, Evelyn,’ said Lord Fitzroy, a young gentleman whose chief virtue was that he had inherited a fortune and whose chief vice was that he had not yet spent it all. ‘You have never been in love.’

‘On the contrary, my dear Fitzroy,’ Rooth corrected him, ‘I have never been in love *with anyone else*. That is the distinction. To love another person is to surrender the sovereignty of one’s own soul. I prefer to be a despot of the heart.’

‘Then you cannot possibly understand Lady Helena Vane,’ said Lady Constance, lowering her voice as if to speak of something dangerous.

At the mention of the name, the room seemed to grow a degree colder. Lady Helena Vane was the great mystery of the season. She was beautiful, certainly—beautiful as a statue of Diana is beautiful, with a cold, white purity that forbade the touch of mortal hands. She was the widow of a diplomat who had died in the East, and she returned to London with a reputation for an intellect as sharp as a diamond and a heart as hard as the stone itself. She collected critics the way other women collected fans, and she had broken the spirit of three poets and a bishop without ever raising her voice.

‘I understand her perfectly,’ Rooth declared, setting his glass down on a marble table. ‘She is a woman who has mistaken her own vanity for virtue. She is cold because she has never met anyone interesting enough to warm her.’

‘She would freeze you where you stand,’ warned Fitzroy.

Rooth smiled. It was a charming smile, though it did not quite reach his eyes. ‘I shall make a wager with you, Fitzroy. I will bet you the pair of greys from your stable—and you know how I covet them—that I can make Lady Helena Vane fall in love with me within a month.’

A gasp of delight rippled through the small circle. ‘You are mad!’ cried Lady Constance.

‘Madness is merely reason that has found its proper object,’ said Rooth. ‘If I fail, I will give you my collection of first editions. If I win, I have the horses and the satisfaction of proving that the coldest heart in London is merely a locked room waiting for the right key.’

The month that followed was a masterpiece of calculated emotion. Rooth did not pursue Lady Helena; he merely happened to be wherever she was, dressed in colours that complimented her mood, speaking of things that interested her mind. He was not the passionate lover who pleads at the gate; he was the subtle poison that seeps through the window.

He found her one afternoon in the conservatory of the Duchess’s country house, tending to a rare white orchid.

‘It is a difficult flower, Lady Helena,’ Rooth said, leaning against a trellis of jasmine. ‘It demands so much, yet gives so little scent. It is the paragon of modern women.’

‘And yet, you admire it,’ she replied, not looking up.

‘I admire its reserve,’ said Rooth. ‘It is so tiresome to be understood by everyone. I prefer the company of things that keep their secrets.’

He spoke to her of art, not with the enthusiasm of a lover, but with the discernment of a connoisseur. He told her that her indifference was a sign of her superiority, and that her solitude was a sanctuary for a soul too fine for the rough handling of the crowd. He flattered her intellect until she began to believe that he was the only man in London who possessed one.

He played the part of the cynic so well that she believed he was the only man honest enough to be trusted. By the third week, the ice around her began to crack, not with the heat of passion, but with the slow, insidious thaw of vanity. She began to dress for him. She began to laugh at his epigrams. She began to look at him with eyes that held a question.

On the last night of the wager, they stood on the balcony of the Vane residence, looking down at the fog-choked streets of Mayfair. The gaslights flickered below like fallen stars.

‘You have won, Mr. Rooth,’ she said softly. She turned to him, and for the first time, the mask of the marble goddess slipped. She looked young, and frightened, and terribly vulnerable. ‘I thought I was safe. I thought my heart was a ruin that no one would ever wish to inhabit.’

Rooth felt a sudden, sharp sensation in his chest. It was not triumph. It was something far more uncomfortable. He looked at her face, pale in the moonlight, and realized that he had done something unforgivable. He had taken a living thing and broken it just to prove he could.

‘Helena,’ he said, and his voice was rough, stripped of its usual polish.

‘I love you, Evelyn,’ she whispered. ‘God help me, I love you.’

She leaned forward, offering him the victory he had so craved. But as he looked at her, he saw not the conquest, but the cost. He saw the greys he had won, and they seemed suddenly like gaudy, useless toys. He saw his reputation as a breaker of hearts, and it seemed a shabby, tawdry thing.

He had intended to kiss her and claim his prize. Instead, he took her hand, raised it to his lips, and held it there for a moment too long.

‘You are too good for me, Helena,’ he said quietly. ‘Far too good.’

He left the balcony and walked out into the London fog. He walked all the way to his club, where he found Lord Fitzroy playing billiards.

‘Well?’ asked Fitzroy, chalking his cue. ‘Did the Ice Queen melt?’

Rooth sat down in a leather armchair and stared into the fire. He felt a strange hollowness, a sense of loss for something he had never possessed. He had won the bet. He had proved his point. He had demonstrated that the coldest woman in London was as susceptible to flattery and art as any other.

‘She loves me,’ Rooth said tonelessly.

‘Good heavens!’ Fitzroy cried. ‘Then the horses are yours! You are the cleverest dog in London. You have made a fool of the finest woman in the city.’

Rooth looked at his friend, and for the first time in his life, he had no witty reply. He had set out to conquer a heart and had succeeded, only to find that in the moment of his victory, he had lost his own. He had made a fool of the finest woman in London, yes. But he had made a cynic of himself, and that was a far heavier price to pay.

‘Send the horses to the stables,’ Rooth said, taking a glass of brandy from a passing servant. ‘I find I have no use for them.’

‘But you won them,’ Fitzroy insisted.

‘I know,’ said Rooth, drinking deeply. ‘That is the tragedy. I have got exactly what I wanted.’
