It was upon a raw November morning in the year 1894 that I first set eyes upon Mr. Jonas Amberley. He had come to my rooms in Duke Street upon the recommendation of a mutual acquaintance, and he bore himself with the stiff, unyielding bearing of a man more accustomed to the command of subordinates than to the seeking of advice. He was lean and sunburnt, with a harshly cut face and a pair of remarkably keen grey eyes that belied his sixty years. His attire was careless in the extreme, consisting of a frieze overcoat much worn at the cuffs and a soft tweed hat pulled low upon his brow.

"You will pardon the early hour, Mr. Marlow," said he, sinking into the chair I had placed for him. "I am a man who has lived half his life in the open, and early rising has become a disease with me. I was for thirty years a tea planter in Ceylon, and I have been retired these five years. I had thought my affairs concluded, but it appears that the past is not so easily buried."

I begged him to be seated and to lay his trouble before me with as much detail as possible.

"It is a singular business," said he, speaking in the sharp, staccato manner of one who grudges every unnecessary syllable. "Ten days ago I received a communication from a shipping agent in this city. He informed me that a man answering precisely to the description of my former partner, Silas Vance, had been seen in the docks. I need not tell you that Vance has been accounted dead these twelve years. He perished, or so I had every reason to believe, in the great flood of '82, which swept away an entire portion of our plantation near Dimbula."

"You are certain it was he?"

"I had the word of a man who had known him well in the old days. The agent sent a written description. There is the scar upon the left cheekbone, and the peculiar trick of carrying the right shoulder higher than the left. I cannot doubt it. But that is not the worst of it. Within a week of this intelligence, I found that my house in Aigburth was being watched."

"Watched by whom?"

"By a lascar. A Malay or a half-caste, I cannot say which. A short, thick-set man, with a yellow face and a singularly malignant pair of eyes. He first came to my notice on Tuesday last, when I observed him standing under the lamp-post at the corner of my road. He made no attempt to conceal himself, but stood there for the better part of two hours, looking steadily at my windows. The next day he was there again. On the Thursday he followed me into the city, keeping always some fifty yards behind. I am not a man given to fancies, Mr. Marlow, but I confess that the business has unsettled me."

"You have no notion of what this man wants?"

"I have no notion of what Vance wants," said Amberley, with a sudden harshness that was very marked. "Nor am I disposed to speculate."

There was a finality in his tone which closed that particular line of inquiry as effectively as a slammed door. I had observed, during the brief time he had been in my room, that his hands, which were brown and muscular, were clenched hard upon his knees, and that a certain pallor showed beneath the weather-beaten hue of his cheeks. Whatever the nature of the connection between himself and this dead man, it was one which he had no intention of disclosing.

"I must ask you to be plain with me, Mr. Amberley," said I. "If you wish me to get to the root of this matter, you must tell me how your partnership with Vance was dissolved."

"I will tell you nothing of that," he answered, rising abruptly from his chair. "It is a chapter which I have closed. I have come to you to find out what this man wants, not to open old wounds. You have my permission to use any means you see fit, short of compelling me to speak of Dimbula. I will pay you whatever you consider a fair recompense, and I will expect you to report to me at my house this evening."

He laid a couple of sovereigns upon the table, drew his coat about him, and was gone before I could frame a reply. I was left with the impression of a man driven by fear, but dominated by a pride which would not permit him to confess the cause of it.

It was not until the afternoon that I was able to set about the business in earnest. I made my way to the Aigburth road in which Amberley resided, a quiet street of substantial villas, bordered by a strip of barren common land upon the farther side. The house itself was a tall, narrow building of red brick, set back from the pavement behind a low wall. I had not been in observation more than ten minutes when I perceived the lascar.

He was standing in the lee of a broken palisade upon the common, a spot which commanded a clear view of Amberley's front windows. He was a short, thickly built man, dressed in a shabby blue jacket and a pair of canvas trousers. His face was of a sallow, sickly hue, and there was about him that indefinable air of the Eastern seas which marks the man who has spent his life in the forecastle. He was smoking a cheroot, and his eyes never wandered for an instant from the house.

I crossed the common and approached him from the side, walking with the careless gait of a loiterer. As I came abreast of him, I observed that he held in his left hand a slip of paper, which he thrust hastily into his pocket as I passed. His right hand, I noticed, was curiously maimed, the two middle fingers being entirely wanting. He shot a single, venomous glance at me, and then resumed his watch.

I passed on, but I had seen enough to convince me that this was no casual loiterer. The man was stationed there for a purpose, and the purpose was connected with Amberley. I walked round by a circuitous route to the rear of the common, where I found a small public-house of a somewhat dingy character. The landlord was a communicative fellow, and I soon learned that the lascar had been a frequent visitor to the neighbourhood during the past week. He had been seen in conversation with a second man, a person of better appearance, who had the look of a ship's officer.

"He come off a vessel in the Alexandra Dock," said the landlord, wiping a glass as he spoke. "The _Surabaya Queen_, a tea-clipper just in from Colombo. The yellow man is her bosun, or some such rating."

Here was a clue worth having. If the lascar was attached to a vessel newly arrived from Colombo, and if that vessel lay in the Alexandra Dock, the matter began to wear a more definite shape. I made my way at once to the northern end of the city, and by half-past five I was standing upon the quay of the Alexandra Dock, where the _Surabaya Queen_ lay at her moorings.

She was a small, dingy steamer of the type that haunts the Eastern trade routes, her plates rust-red, her rigging slack and ill cared for. I made inquiry of the mate, a surly Scotsman, who confirmed that his vessel had indeed come from Colombo, and that he had among his crew a half-caste Malay named Ah Sing.

"He's gone ashore," said the mate, in answer to my question. "Gone up Aigburth way, on some business of the skipper's. The skipper, he's a man named Cardew, and he's had Ah Sing in his service these fifteen year and more. Ah Sing was in the Ceylon trade before ever he shipped with Cardew."

"You speak of Captain Cardew," said I. "Is he aboard?"

"He's gone up to the town," said the mate. "But you'll find his papers in the cabin, if it's cargo you're after."

I had no need to press the point further. The name of Cardew was a new one to me, but the connection was plain enough. A vessel from Colombo, a Malay sailor who had served his master for fifteen years, and a watch kept upon the house of a retired tea planter. The threads were beginning to draw together.

I returned to Aigburth, and arrived at Amberley's house as the dusk was falling. I found him pacing his narrow hall in a state of considerable agitation. He had drawn the blinds of every window, and the house had the air of a beleaguered fortress.

"Well?" he demanded, as I entered. "What have you learned?"

"I have learned that your watcher is a half-caste named Ah Sing, attached to a steamer called the _Surabaya Queen_, which arrived from Colombo some ten days ago. He is acting upon the orders of the vessel's master, a Captain Cardew."

The effect of these words upon Amberley was extraordinary. He reeled back as though he had been struck, and his face, already pale, went the colour of grey ashes. He clutched at the hall table to steady himself, and for a moment I thought he would fall.

"Cardew!" he gasped. "Cardew!"

"You know the name?"

He mastered himself with a visible effort, and his jaw set in the hard, rigid line which I had marked at our morning interview.

"I know it," said he. "It was the name Silas Vance took when he left Ceylon. He was born Cardew. You have found him, then. He is alive."

"He is in Liverpool, if your information is correct. The question remains, Mr. Amberley, what does he want with you?"

Amberley stood silent for a long moment, his eyes staring before him into the shadows of the hall. When at last he spoke, his voice had lost all its briskness and was grown hoarse and uncertain.

"He wants what I took from him," said he. "He wants the plantation. And he is welcome to it, if he will only take his lascar away from my door."

He turned abruptly and led the way into his dining-room, where a lamp burned upon the table. As he did so, I observed that his hand, which rested upon the back of a chair, was trembling violently. Whatever had passed between Jonas Amberley and the man who called himself Silas Vance in the Dimbula hills twelve years before, it was clear that the reckoning was now at hand, and that the retired tea planter, for all his bravado, was a man who lived in the grip of a terror which the passage of years had done nothing to diminish.
