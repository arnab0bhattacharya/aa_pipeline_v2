The rain came down on Gimmerdale with a sullen, ceaseless fury. It beat against the narrow lattice of the counting-house, driving through the ill-fitted frames, and gathering in beads on the stone sill. I watched it; I had nothing else to watch. The valley below was a drear expanse of mist and turbulent water, the Gimmle roaring over its bed of shattered slate, while the tall chimneys of my father’s mill pierced the vapor like black, blighted trunks of trees stripped by lightning. The air in the room was thick with the smell of damp wool and stale ledgers. My father sat opposite me at the desk, his pen scratching furiously, his brow contracted into a groove that seemed cut there by a chisel. He was a man of iron and clay—iron to the world, clay only to his appetites and his pride.

“Another sixpence off the piece,” he muttered, not to me, for I was merely a piece of household furniture to him, but to the ledger that lay open beneath his inky fingers. “Let them growl. The cloth lies dead in the warehouse at Hull; there are no orders, and if the looms stand idle, they may thank the French, not me. A cut in wages is better than a lock on the gate.”

I looked at the fire in the grate. It was a small, niggardly fire, choked with damp coals, sending out more smoke than heat. I thought of the weavers in their damp-stone cottages up the sides of the valley—of Seth Kitching’s wife, who had been brought to bed last week, and the child wailing in a room where the plaster fell from the walls. I thought of the thin, hollow faces I had seen that morning at the mill gate, and a slow, heavy anger kindled in my chest. It was not the anger of rebellion, for I was but a woman, and my father’s unmarried daughter, bound to obedience by every law of scripture and society; it was the anger of impotence, which is perhaps the most bitter of all angers.

“If the wages are cut, they will starve,” I said. My voice was low, but it rang with a hard distinctness in the close air of the counting-house.

My father looked up, surprised, as he might be surprised if the poker had spoken. “Starve? They will not starve. They have oatmeal, they have the parish. Do you think I am made of gold, Margaret? The orders fail, the cloth rots, and I am to bleed my veins to feed a pack of ungrateful curs who would let the mill burn to the ground if they thought it would bring them a loaf of bread?”

I made no answer. Argument with my father was like casting stones into a bottomless morass; they sank without a ripple. I turned again to the window. The rain had abated somewhat, and through a sullen gap in the clouds a pale, livid gleam of afternoon light fell upon the wet road below, illuminating the figure of a man making his way up the valley side. It was Seth Kitching himself, his shoulders bent under the weight of a sack of wool, his feet slipping on the wet stones. He was going to his loom, to earn the sixpence my father had just docked from his piece.

A spasm of something akin to agony contracted my heart. I had no money of my own; my mother’s small legacy was in my father’s hands, swallowed up in the mill’s accounts. I had no power; I could not order the wages paid, nor could I give bread from my father’s store without his leave. I was a cipher, a dependent, a woman—worse than the meanest hand in the mill, for they at least could sell their labor, while I could only sell my soul, and only if some suitor saw fit to buy it.

That evening we had visitors. The Hon. George Leighton, a younger son of a nearby landowner, and his friend, Mr. Thwaites, rode over from Thwaite Hall, their horses splashing through the mire of the yard. They came in with a great clatter and a jingling of spurs, shaking the water from their coats, their faces flushed with the cold and the high spirits of men who have never known a want they could not satisfy.

My father received them with a grim, pleased smile. He liked the company of gentlemen; it flattered his vanity to see the heir of the Leightons drinking his ale and praising his port. I sat at the hearth, my hands busy with a piece of needlework, my eyes downcast, listening to their talk. It was all of foxes, of rents, of the price of wool at Halifax market, and not one word of the misery that gnawed at the very heart of the valley.

It was Mr. Leighton who turned to me at last, with that easy, half-mocking gallantry which was his way with women. “And how does Miss Marsden endure this dismal weather? I vow, the rain in Gimmerdale is enough to damp the spirits of a saint. You must long for a livelier scene—the assemblies at York, perhaps, or the theaters of London.”

I looked up. His face was handsome, with clear blue eyes and a fresh complexion, but it was a face that seemed to me like a painted mask, behind which there was neither depth nor substance. I felt a sudden, fierce impulse to strip the mask away, to lay bare the indifference beneath.

“I endure the weather, Mr. Leighton, as the people of this valley endure it,” I said, and my voice had an edge I did not try to soften. “They have no assemblies to fly to, no theaters. They have only their looms, and now, a cut in their wages. I think their spirits are more dampened by hunger than by rain.”

A silence fell. My father shot me a furious glance. Mr. Thwaites shifted in his chair and looked into his glass. But Mr. Leighton only laughed, a light, careless laugh that grated on my ear like the scrape of a file.

“Come, Miss Marsden, you are too severe. The lower orders are used to hardship; it is their natural condition. They do not feel it as we should. A full belly and a warm fire are luxuries they do not miss, because they have never known them.”

I looked at him, and the anger that had smoldered in my breast all day blazed up suddenly into a fierce, scorching flame. I saw, as in a vision, the hollow eyes of Seth Kitching’s wife, the wailing child, the damp-stone walls; and I saw, set against them, this man’s sleek, indifferent face, his fine coat, his easy laugh. The injustice of it struck me like a physical blow, and I felt my cheeks burn and my hands tremble in my lap.

“You are mistaken,” I said, and my voice, though low, shook with the force of what I felt. “They feel it. They feel it as acutely as you would feel the loss of your horse, or your dinner. They are not beasts, Mr. Leighton, though you may treat them as such. They have souls, and hearts, and I believe God will require an account of those who grind them to dust.”

My father struck the table with his fist. “Enough, Margaret! You forget yourself. Mr. Leighton, I beg pardon for my daughter’s uncivil tongue. She has read too many books and learned too little manners.”

Mr. Leighton waved a hand, still smiling. “Not at all. I admire Miss Marsden’s spirit, though I think she wastes it on an unworthy subject. The poor will always be with us, as Scripture says.”

He quoted Scripture with the easy confidence of a man who has never opened the book in earnest. I turned my face away, back to my needlework, and sewed in a silence that was more eloquent than speech. The talk resumed, the laughter flowed, the port went round, but I heard nothing. I was thinking of the mill, of the cut wages, of the hunger that was creeping into the valley like the mist from the Gimmle. I had no money, no power, no voice that would be heard. I was nothing. And yet, in the fierce, surging tide of my indignation, I felt something that was not quite despair—a stubborn, unquenchable spark, a resolve that burned in the darkness. I could not feed them, I could not save them, but I would not, by my silence or my smiles, become an accomplice to their oppression. I would not sell my conscience for a husband, nor barter my soul for a home. Better to be a lonely woman in a lonely valley, better to face the storm alone, than to sit by a warm fire and laugh while the world outside starved.
