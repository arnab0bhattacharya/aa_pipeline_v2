It was upon a raw, foggy morning in the late November of 1894 that I found myself seated opposite my friend, Mr. Silas Faulkner, in his snug chambers off Whitehall. The yellow fog had crept in through the ill-fitting windows, and Faulkner was hunched over his chemical apparatus, engaged in one of those precise experiments which formed his chief relaxation from the labours of his profession as a consulting detective. A sharp ring at the bell broke the silence, and presently Mrs. Hudson, his landlady, ushered in a visitor.

She was a woman of middle age, dressed in the deepest mourning, her face drawn and colourless. Her hands, encased in black kid gloves, were clasped tightly before her. Faulkner surveyed her with that quick, comprehensive glance which missed no detail of dress or manner.

"Pray be seated, madam," said he. "I perceive you have come from Richmond, and that you are in some great trouble."

She started. "You are right, Mr. Faulkner. I have come direct from Richmond. But how you know—"

"It is a simple matter. The yellow clay upon your boots is peculiar to the Richmond lanes, and your agitation is too pronounced to be anything but the result of a sudden shock. I am entirely at your service. What is the nature of your trouble?"

"My husband, Mr. Faulkner. Mr. Arthur Pendennis. He returned three weeks ago from Mexico, where he has been engaged in mining. Last evening, upon reading a letter, he was struck down. He is unable to speak—the doctor says it is apoplexy. But it is what was upon his desk that has driven me to you."

Faulkner leaned forward, his grey eyes intent. "What was upon the desk?"

"A letter, written in Spanish, and a rough sketch of a church. I know no Spanish, and the sketch told me nothing. But I am convinced, Mr. Faulkner, that the contents of that letter were the cause of his seizure. He was a strong man, and in excellent health."

"Have you the letter and the sketch about you?"

"I have." She produced a folded sheet and a torn piece of paper from her reticule.

Faulkner spread them upon the table. The letter was written in a cramped, foreign hand. He read it through swiftly, then turned his attention to the sketch. It was a rough outline of a substantial building, with a square tower and what appeared to be an enclosure marked behind it.

"You say Mr. Pendennis was in mining in Mexico?" said Faulkner.

"Yes. In the state of Guerrero. He had been there for two years."

"And the name of the place?"

"San Mateo, I believe. A small town in the mountains."

Faulkner nodded slowly. "I see. And your husband was alone when the seizure occurred?"

"He was. I found him in his study at half-past nine. He was seated at his desk, his face purple, his eyes staring. He could not utter a word, though he seemed to recognise me. The doctor was sent for at once."

"Has anyone else been in the study?"

"No one but the doctor and myself."

"Has your husband had any visitors since his return?"

"Only one. A Mr. Leyland, a former associate in Mexico, called upon him two days ago. They were closeted together for an hour. I did not see Mr. Leyland, but my husband seemed disturbed after his departure."

Faulkner rose and took up his hat. "I will return to Richmond with you, Mrs. Pendennis. There are certain details I must observe for myself."

The house was a solid, comfortable villa, set back from the road. Faulkner passed swiftly through the hall and into the study. It was a plainly furnished room, with a broad writing-table at the window. He examined the desk with minute care, running his long fingers over its surface, peering into the ink-well, and inspecting the blotting-pad.

"You have the letter in its envelope?" he asked.

"It was lying open upon the desk."

"Where is the envelope?"

Mrs. Pendennis hesitated. "I did not observe one."

"No envelope," Faulkner muttered. "That is significant. And this sketch—where was it?"

"Beside the letter. It had been torn from a larger sheet."

Faulkner held the sketch to the light. "It is drawn upon paper of a peculiar texture—coarse, with a faint yellow tinge. Such paper is common in Mexico. You see these marks at the corner? They are the impressions of a rubber stamp, now nearly illegible. But with a lens—ah! The words are 'Oficina de Correos.' This sketch was torn from a sheet of Mexican official postal paper."

"But what does it mean, Mr. Faulkner?"

"Patience, madam. Now, this letter. It is dated from the City of Mexico, ten days ago. It is signed by one Carlos Mendez. The substance of it is this: Mendez writes that he has discovered the truth regarding the affair at San Mateo. He speaks of a sum of money—twenty thousand pounds—which was entrusted to your husband for the building of a church. He states that the church was never built, and that the money has disappeared. He threatens to lay the matter before the British Consul unless an explanation is forthcoming."

Mrs. Pendennis had sunk into a chair, her face ashen. "It is false!" she cried. "Arthur would never—"

"One moment," said Faulkner, raising his hand. "There is more. Mendez goes on to say that he has in his possession a document, signed by your husband, which proves that the money was diverted to the purchase of a mining concession. He encloses, as he says, a sketch of the church which was never built, as a memento."

"The villain!" Mrs. Pendennis whispered. "It is a lie!"

"The letter," Faulkner continued, "is written in a hand which trembles with excitement. The ink is of a brownish hue, such as is used in Mexico. The paper is thin and of poor quality. These details are consistent with its having been written under stress in that country."

He turned to the sketch again. "You observe this enclosure behind the church? It is marked with a cross. Now, I happen to know something of Mexican villages. The enclosure behind the church is usually the cemetery. The cross marks a grave. Whose grave, I wonder?"

Mrs. Pendennis stared at him. "I do not understand."

"I think I do," said Faulkner, quietly. "Tell me, did your husband ever speak of a man named Leyland?"

"Mr. Leyland? Yes. He was his partner in Mexico."

"Was there any trouble between them?"

"Not that I know of. But my husband was very reticent about his affairs."

Faulkner was silent for a moment, his eyes fixed upon the sketch. Then he turned abruptly. "Mrs. Pendennis, I must ask you to trust me. I believe that this letter, though it accuses your husband, is itself a weapon of blackmail. Mendez is either a tool or a principal. The money was, I suspect, honestly invested, but in a manner which gave rise to suspicion. The church sketch is the key. If your husband had built the church, the money would have been accounted for. He did not build it. Why? Because he built something else—a school, perhaps, or a hospital—which the local authorities preferred. But there is a graver possibility."

He paused, and his voice dropped. "The cross in the cemetery. Mendez writes that Leyland can tell the truth. I think Leyland is dead, and that his grave is marked. I think your husband knew of his death, and that this letter, threatening exposure, implied that he was responsible. It was the shock of that implied accusation which struck him down."

Mrs. Pendennis had risen, her hands gripping the chair-back. "But Arthur never spoke—"

"He would not, if he feared suspicion. But we must have facts. I shall wire to the British Consul in Mexico City. Meanwhile, I must find this Mr. Leyland."

"You said he was dead."

"I said I believed so. But the Mr. Leyland who called here two days ago was very much alive. It was he who brought the letter."

"The man who called himself Leyland was not Leyland," Faulkner continued, his eyes kindling. "He was Mendez, or his agent. He came to prepare the ground, to see what Mr. Pendennis's state of mind was, and then to send the letter. The real Leyland is in Mexico, in that grave behind the unbuilt church."

He turned to the writing-table again and took up the blotting-pad. Holding it to the glass, he read off the reversed impression. "See here, Mrs. Pendennis. Your husband, in his last conscious act, wrote a few words upon this pad. The impression is faint, but legible. It reads: 'Leyland dead. My hands are clean. God help me.'"

The room was very still. Mrs. Pendennis stood motionless, her lips parted.

"Your husband," said Faulkner gently, "was not a thief. He was a man who had witnessed, perhaps caused, the death of his partner, and had carried the secret in silence. When this letter came, accusing him of theft and hinting at worse, the accumulated weight of years broke upon him. It was not guilt which struck him down, but the sudden, terrible resurrection of a memory he had thought buried."

He folded the letter and sketch and placed them in his pocket. "I will take these. I will lay the matter before the proper authorities, and I have no doubt that the truth will be established. Your husband, when he recovers speech—and I trust he will—will be able to clear himself. Meanwhile, you need have no fear. The man who called as Leyland will not trouble you again."

Mrs. Pendennis extended her hand. "How can I thank you, Mr. Faulkner?"

"By keeping this matter quiet until I have finished. And by believing in your husband."

We passed out into the fog-shrouded street. Faulkner walked briskly, his chin buried in his coat-collar.

"A curious case," said he. "The sketch was the clue. A church that was never built, a grave that was dug instead. Money, guilt, silence—and then the letter. It is a strange world, Watson—"

He checked himself, with a dry smile. "I beg your pardon. I was forgetting that you are not my biographer. But the case is not without interest, and I shall see it through."
