The village of Cheadle Magna lay in a hollow of the Staffordshire moorlands, where the grey stone of its church seemed to have drawn its colour from the sky itself, and the vicarage garden, in wet weather, sent down a stream of clay-coloured water that stained the path to the lych-gate. It was a place where the past was not so much remembered as felt—a weight in the air, a dampness in the walls—and where the dead, having been buried in their due degrees, were expected to remain quietly beneath their stones, troubling no one with inconvenient recollections.

But it is the nature of the dead, when their monuments are meddled with, to stir up the living.

The restoration of St. Chad’s had been the project of the new vicar, Mr. Thurstan Beale, a man of earnest and somewhat anxious piety, who had come to Cheadle Magna with the conviction that a house of God should not be allowed to moulder, and that the souls of his parishioners were best reached by way of their eyes. He had therefore set about raising subscriptions, and had been gratified beyond measure when the principal contribution came from Cheadle Hall itself—from Mrs. Agatha Vane, widow of the late squire, who had been gathered to his fathers some three years since, and whose memory, in the opinion of his tenantry, was better left to the mercy of heaven than committed to the skill of any stonecutter.

The mason engaged for the work was one Jonas Rigg, a man of fifty years and more, whose family had hewn and set the stones of that parish for three generations. He was a broad-shouldered, slow-spoken fellow, with a face like the rock he worked, and hands that could split a block of millstone grit along its natural bed with the same ease that another man might tear a sheet of paper. He was not a man of many words, but those he used were weighed, and his neighbours had learned that when Jonas Rigg said a thing was so, it was so—whether they liked it or not.

It was on a Tuesday morning in March, when the rain had ceased and the wind had shifted to the east, bringing a dry cold that made the chisels ring sharper than usual, that Mr. Beale came picking his way among the fallen stones and the scaffolds, with a paper in his hand and a look of pleasant importance on his face.

“Rigg,” said he, “I have here the inscription for the memorial tablet to the late Mr. Vane, to be set in the north aisle. Mrs. Vane has been so good as to compose it herself, and I think you will agree it is very handsomely done. I shall want you to begin upon it as soon as the tablet itself is dressed.”

Jonas took the paper in his horny fingers and held it at arm’s length, for his eyes were not what they had been. He read it slowly, moving his lips, and when he had come to the end he folded it again and returned it to the vicar without a word.

“Well?” said Mr. Beale, smiling. “It is a fine tribute, is it not? ‘A devoted husband, a generous landlord, a true friend to the poor.’ I confess I did not know Mr. Vane had so much in him. But death, you know, Rigg, death softens our judgments. We see our neighbours more clearly when they are removed from the dust of daily life.”

“I knew him,” said Jonas.

“Yes, yes, of course. You have lived here all your life. You would have known him better than most.”

“I knew him,” Jonas repeated, and there was a something in his voice that made the vicar look at him more narrowly.

“You do not seem pleased, Rigg. Is there aught amiss with the lettering? I thought it plain enough.”

“The lettering’s well enough. It’s the meaning I mislike.”

Mr. Beale’s smile faltered. “I am afraid I do not understand you.”

Jonas laid down his mallet and stood upright, wiping his hands upon his leathern apron. “I’ll not cut them words, sir. I’ll cut any other words you please—the Psalms, the Commandments, the whole Book of Common Prayer if you’ve a mind—but I’ll not cut lies into a stone that’s to stand in God’s house till the Day of Judgment.”

The vicar coloured. “Rigg, this is very extraordinary language. Mrs. Vane is the best of women—a widow, moreover, and your landlord’s widow. She has paid for the tablet out of her own pocket. It is not for you or me to sit in judgment upon the dead.”

“I’m not sitting in judgment, sir. I’m refusing to set my hand to what I know to be false. There’s a difference.”

“And what, pray, do you know?”

Jonas was silent for a moment, looking out over the churchyard to where the roofs of the village clustered below the hill. “I know that when my father was a young man, he built the wall round the Hall plantation—a wall of dressed stone, as fine a piece of work as any in the county. And when it was done, Mr. Vane—the old squire, his father—paid him with a promissory note that was never honoured. My father died in debt, sir, and his children after him. I know that when the smallpox came through this parish in ‘ninety-seven, the squire shut up the Hall and went to Bath, and left his tenants to bury their own dead. I know that when my brother Will was taken up for poaching—and poaching a rabbit, sir, a single rabbit, because his children were crying for bread—it was Mr. Vane himself who swore the information, and Will was transported, and died on the voyage. I know these things, sir, because I lived them. And I’ll not carve ‘generous landlord’ over the bones of the man who did them.”

Mr. Beale had listened with an expression that passed from astonishment to distress, and from distress to a kind of troubled perplexity. He was not a hard man, nor a dishonest one; but he was a man who had been bred to the belief that the social order was a part of the divine order, and that the rich and the poor alike had their appointed stations, and that it was the business of the clergy to reconcile the one to the other by gentle ministrations and the avoidance of unpleasant topics.

“Rigg,” he said at last, “I perceive that you have suffered—that you have been deeply injured. But consider: is this the place for such recollections? The tablet is not a history; it is a memorial. It speaks of what Mrs. Vane remembers—of what she wishes to remember. She was a good wife to him, and she is a good woman now. Would you have her grief embittered by the revival of old grievances? Would you have her think that her husband’s memory is to be blackened in the very church where she has come to pray for his soul?”

“I would have her think the truth, sir, if she can bear it. And if she cannot, then let her put up no stone at all. Let his name be writ in water, for all I care. But I’ll not be the man to set a lie in letters of brass.”

“But truth, Rigg—truth is a delicate thing. It is not always the same as fact. A man may be a hard landlord and yet a loving husband. He may be cruel in one relation and kind in another. Who shall say which is the truer picture?”

“God shall say, sir, when He comes to judge. And until then, I’ll not anticipate His verdict.”

The vicar was silent. He had a great respect for Jonas Rigg—a respect that was not diminished by this encounter—and he felt, though he would not have confessed it, that there was a force in the mason’s plain speech that his own more cultivated reasonings could not altogether withstand. He looked at the paper in his hand, and then at the church around them—the pillars that Jonas’s grandfather had set, the windows that Jonas’s father had repaired—and he was aware of a great and uncomfortable truth pressing upon him: that the stones of the church were not merely stones, but the record of the hands that had shaped them, and that those hands had their own testimony to bear.

“I will speak to Mrs. Vane,” he said at last. “I will put the matter before her—not, perhaps, in all its particulars, but in such a way as she may understand. And I will ask her to consider whether some other inscription—something more general, more—more charitable—might not be substituted.”

“I’ll cut ‘A sinner saved by grace,’” said Jonas, “and mean it. Or ‘He rests from his labours.’ That’s true enough for any man.”

Mr. Beale smiled faintly. “You are a strange fellow, Rigg. You will not lie for a widow, but you will cut a text for a knave.”

“I’ll not lie for any man, sir. But I’ll cut the truth for all men, if it’s only the truth of their dying.”

The vicar went away with the paper in his pocket, and Jonas took up his mallet again and went on with his work, and the sound of his chisel rang out across the churchyard, clear and sharp and inexorable, like the voice of a man who has made his peace with his own conscience and will not be moved from it.
