The Rev. Arthur Pemberton had been at Fettleworth some four months before he ventured to propose the lending library, a period long enough for the bloom of his newness to rub off, and for the town to have settled, as it were, upon him, like the fine dust that rose from the market square on a dry day. He was a man of earnest but not aggressive temperament, whose faith was of the kind that seeks to express itself in practical benevolence; and having observed the men of the town—the weavers from the silk-mills, the journeymen smiths, the labourers who drifted from one odd job to another—gather of an evening at the Red Lion or the Wheatsheaf, he conceived a vision of a room where they might find wholesome intellectual occupation, a substitute for the fumes of ale and the futility of coarse gossip. His scheme, when he unfolded it to Mr. Silas Croft, was simplicity itself: a room in the Mechanics’ Institute, a few shelves, and a collection of books on history, science, and travels, to be lent out for a penny a week, the sum being a mere token to encourage a sense of ownership.

Mr. Croft, whose great silk-mill dominated the eastern end of the town and whose philanthropy was as well-organised as his business, listened with a sharp, grey eye and a smile that did not quite reach the hard lines about his mouth. He was a man who had risen by his own exertions and who held a profound, if unspoken, belief that the lower orders were a species of raw material to be improved for the general good. “A capital notion, Vicar,” he said, stroking his chin. “Knowledge is power, eh? And a sober man is a better workman. I will not only subscribe to the thing, I will stand surety for the first hundred volumes. But we must have it managed properly. No loose talk in the reading-room. I shall nominate a committee. And the books must be of a right sort—nothing to unsettle men’s minds about their station.”

Pemberton, in his eagerness, saw only the handsome gesture, the immediate realisation of his dream. He did not pause to consider the character of the books Mr. Croft might deem ‘of a right sort’, nor the nature of the ‘proper management’ he envisioned. He felt a rush of gratitude, a warm sense of alliance with the great man of the town. It was only later, when he mentioned the project to his curate, Mr. Grimes, that he encountered the first current of opposition.

Mr. Grimes was an old man, the very incarnation of immovable habit. He had served the parish for forty years under a series of vicars, and viewed any novelty with the deep, instinctive dread of a creature whose shell has been its sole protection. “A lending library?” he wheezed, his watery eyes blinking in the dim light of the vestry. “Books for the weavers? And what, sir, will they read but Tom Paine and the Rights of Man? You are opening a floodgate. You will have them all turning Methodist, or worse. They have their Bible and their prayer-book. It is what their fathers had before them. This is not a time for stirring up the pot, Mr. Pemberton. Stirring up the pot.”

Pemberton tried to reason with him, to speak of self-improvement and the dignity of labour, but his words fell upon the old man’s prejudice as upon a stone wall. Grimes saw the world as a fixed hierarchy, ordained by God, in which each man had his allotted place, and any attempt to educate the lower orders beyond their station was an act of presumption, a defiance of Providence. The curate’s opposition, born of no personal malice, but of a genuine and profound terror of change, was to Pemberton more disheartening than any open hostility. It was like trying to argue with the very geology of the place.

Yet the library, buoyed by Mr. Croft’s subscription, went forward. A room was secured, shelves were erected, and a fine selection of books arrived—histories of England that dwelt lovingly on the glories of the monarchy, biographies of self-made men, and volumes of travels that depicted the heathen in their ignorance as a salutary contrast to the English labourer. The committee, as Mr. Croft had promised, consisted of himself, two of his foremen, and the local doctor; Pemberton found himself its nominal chairman, but his voice was a minority one. He soon discovered that the gift of the books had been merely the first instalment of a debt. The library was not to be a free-thinking republic of letters, but an instrument of order. A man named Higgs, a weaver who had once been a Chartist sympathiser, was quietly refused a ticket on the grounds of his ‘discontented’ character. Books on political economy, which Pemberton had hoped for, were deemed ‘unsettling’ and excluded.

The great test came at the opening. Mr. Croft, in a speech of magnificent paternalism, spoke of the library as a ‘privilege’ and a ‘trust’, reminding his hearers that the duties of a workman were as sacred as the rights of a man. Mr. Grimes, in a flutter of anxiety, had distributed tracts on the sin of idleness at the door. Pemberton, looking out at the rows of faces—some curious, some sullen, some simply blank—felt a chill of disillusionment. He saw that he had not given the men a tool for their own liberation, but had merely gilded their cage. The obligation was now upon them to be grateful, to be sober, to be improved in the precise manner their betters had prescribed.

Walking home through the twilit market square, Pemberton met the old curate. Grimes was leaning on his stick, watching the last of the crowd disperse. “Well, sir,” the old man said, his voice less combative now, merely weary. “You have had your way. They have their books.” He paused, and a flicker of something almost like pity crossed his features. “But you will find, Mr. Pemberton, that in a town like this, you cannot give a man a thing without fastening a string to it. And it is always the giver who holds the end of the string.” The vicar stood silent, the truth of the words settling upon him with the weight of the falling dusk. He had come to Fettleworth to sow seeds of enlightenment, but he was beginning to understand that the soil itself was owned, and the harvest, too, was spoken for.
