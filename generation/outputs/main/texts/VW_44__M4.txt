The train, which had been sliding through the outskirts for some time, now came to a standstill with a sigh, and the name of the station, spelled out in white letters on a blue ground, seemed to her to have the curious unreality of a word repeated too often until it loses its meaning. Victoria. She said it to herself in French, *Victoria*, and it sounded like a woman’s name, a queen’s name, something ceremonial and remote, not at all like the grimy, echoing cavern into which she now stepped, her bag heavy in her hand, the smell of soot and damp wool and something else—coal smoke, was it?—filling her nostrils with a sensation so piercingly familiar that she stood still for a moment, arrested, while the porters hurried past and the crowd flowed round her as water flows round a stone.

Twenty years. She had crossed the Channel perhaps a dozen times in twenty years, but always to Dover, always to Folkestone, and then the train had carried her through the green tunnel of the Kentish countryside to this same London, and she had stepped out upon this same platform, and yet—and yet she had never before felt this particular dizziness, this sense of having alighted in a dream from which she might at any moment be awakened by the sound of her husband’s voice calling from the garden, *Hélène, Hélène, the roses need water*. For now there was no garden, no roses, no voice to call her; there was only the flat grey light of an English afternoon pressing against the glass roof, and the immense, indifferent machinery of arrival and departure, and somewhere, in a house in Highgate, a coffin, and two sisters whom she had not seen for seven years, and a mother who was dead.

She had become, somewhere between Calais and Dover, a foreigner. It was the oddest thing. She had felt it first upon the boat, when the stewardess had addressed her in English and she had answered in French, instinctively, before she could stop herself, and the woman had looked at her with that particular expression—not unfriendly, but puzzled, faintly superior—which the English reserve for those who have abandoned, as they see it, the decencies. And then at Dover the customs officer had asked her how long she intended to stay, and she had said, *I don’t know*, and he had looked at her passport, at the name, *Hélène Marchand, née Ashworth*, and had seemed to find something irregular in it, as though the hyphen between the two names were a kind of equivocation, a failure of loyalty.

The cab, when she found one, smelled of leather and petrol and the faint, sweetish odour of some previous passenger’s cigarettes. She gave the address in Highgate, and the driver, a man with a red face and a cloth cap, said something which she did not catch, and she said, *I beg your pardon?* and he repeated it, and she understood, and then she did not know what to answer, for the words had been a pleasantry, a small joke about the weather, and the machinery of pleasantry, which had once been as natural to her as breathing, seemed to have rusted in her throat. She smiled, and looked out of the window, and the streets unrolled themselves, grey and brown and endlessly repetitive, and she thought of the street in Lyon where she had lived for twenty years, the narrow street with its tall houses and its shutters and its smell of coffee and garlic and the dark, rich smell of the river in summer, and she thought how strange it was that this city, which she had known before she knew any other, should now seem to her like a photograph of a place she had once visited, flat and without depth, a place where the light fell differently, where the very stones had a different colour, a different weight.

Her sister Margaret met her at the door. Margaret, who was fifty now, and grey, and who had never married, and who had stayed at home with mother, and who looked at Hélène with eyes that were like their mother’s eyes, and said, *Well, Hélène*, and then, after a pause, *You’ve come then*, and stepped aside to let her in. The hall was dark and smelled of furniture polish and something else, something medicinal, something that spoke of illness and the long, slow closing of a life. There were flowers on the table, lilies, white and heavy-headed, and their scent was so thick, so cloying, that Hélène put her hand on the wall to steady herself, and Margaret said, *Are you all right?* and she said, *Yes, yes, it’s only the flowers*, and Margaret said, *They were her favourite*, and there was a silence in which the word *her* hung like a shape in the air, a shape which neither of them could quite bring themselves to touch.

Her other sister, Beatrice, came down the stairs then, and Beatrice was forty-five and stout and had married a solicitor and had three children, and she kissed Hélène on the cheek, a quick, dry kiss, and said, *You look well, Hélène. France suits you*, and there was something in the way she said *France* that made it sound like a disease, a chronic condition, something to be pitied rather than envied. And Hélène, who had meant to say that she was tired, that the journey had been long, that she was glad to be here, found that she could say nothing at all, that the words which had formed themselves so readily on the boat, on the train, in the cab, had now dissolved, leaving only a kind of blankness, a waiting.

They sat in the drawing-room, the three of them, and the clock on the mantelpiece ticked, and the lilies in the hall sent their scent through the open door, and Margaret said, *The funeral is on Thursday. The vicar will say a few words. Mother wasn’t one for a fuss*, and Beatrice said, *No, she wasn’t*, and Hélène said, *No*, and the word sounded in her mouth like a pebble, smooth and foreign. She looked at the room, at the photographs on the piano, at the china dogs on the shelf, at the worn places in the carpet, and she remembered—she remembered suddenly, with a violence that made her catch her breath—the garden at home, the garden in Lyon, the garden that was hers now, hers and Jean’s, with its roses and its lavender and its view of the hills, and she thought of Jean, who had stayed behind, who had said, *I will come if you need me*, and she had said, *No, no, it is better I go alone*, and now she wondered whether she had been wrong, whether she had been afraid, whether she had wanted to come alone so that she might see, with her own eyes, what she had become.

*You’ve changed*, Beatrice said, and it was not an accusation, exactly, but it was not a compliment either. *Your clothes, your hair—you look different. French.*

*I am French*, Hélène said, and the words came out more sharply than she had intended, and Beatrice flushed, and Margaret said, *Of course, of course you are, we only meant—* and then stopped, because there was nothing, really, that she could mean, nothing that could be said without opening the door to all the years of absence, all the letters that had grown shorter and shorter, all the Christmases that had passed without her, all the small, accumulating distances that had turned her, in their minds, into someone else, someone they did not quite know, someone who had chosen, deliberately, to live among strangers.

The clock ticked. The lilies sent their scent. And Hélène, looking at her sisters, at their familiar, unfamiliar faces, felt the strangeness of it all settle upon her like a weight, like a coat that did not fit, and she thought: I am here, and I am not here; I am Hélène Marchand, and I am also Hélène Ashworth, and neither of them is quite real, and the house of my childhood is a house I no longer know, and the country of my birth is a country where I speak the language like a native but dream in another tongue. And she thought of the garden in Lyon, waiting for her, the roses needing water, and she thought of her mother, lying somewhere in the dark of the house, and she thought: This is what it is, then, to be a foreigner; it is to carry your home within you, and to find, wherever you go, that it does not quite fit the shape of the world outside.
