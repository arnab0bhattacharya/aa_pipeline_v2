The heat lay upon the valley of El Robledal like a woollen blanket, thick and still. For Eleanor Vance, who had spent twenty-two years in the perpetual grey damp of Somerset, the Andalusian sun was a revelation and an assault. It bleached the terracotta walls of the Villa Solana to the colour of old bone and turned the dusty olive groves into a shimmering, silver-green mirage. It was the source of her family’s constant, fretful misery, and the very thing that had, against all odds, begun to anchor her to this place.

Her family, the Ashworths, were a study in English discomfort. Mr. Ashworth, a man whose moustache seemed to wilt in the heat, had brought them here on doctor’s orders for his wife’s nerves. Mrs. Ashworth, a creature of lace and fragility, spent her days on the shaded veranda, fanning herself with a palm-leaf fan and complaining of the dust, the insects, the unnerving silence. The children, twelve-year-old Beatrice and ten-year-old Leo, were pale, bored creatures, accustomed to the manicured lawns and predictable rains of home. They missed the scullery maid, the predictable meals, the sound of English rain on English slate. They missed, in short, everything that Eleanor did not.

They depended on her utterly. Eleanor was their bridge, their interpreter, their shield against the bewildering foreignness of their own chosen exile. Her Spanish, learned from a governess from Seville in her youth and polished with a dogged determination in the weeks before the voyage, was now the most valuable currency in the household. It was she who haggled with the grocer in the village, she who calmed the irascible cook, she who explained to the gardener, old Mateo, exactly which roses Mrs. Ashworth wished to have by her window.

“Tell him, Eleanor,” Mrs. Ashworth would sigh, gesturing vaguely with her fan, “that these are not the roses I requested. They are… common.”

Eleanor would turn to Mateo, who stood patiently, his hat in his hands, his face a roadmap of sun and time. She would soften the words, of course. “Doña Ashworth is very particular, Mateo. She wonders if these are the *Reina de España*? She admires them, but she believes she asked for the pale pink *Isabel*.” Mateo would nod, a slow, understanding smile touching his lips. He never took offence. He would simply touch the brim of his hat and say, “*Por supuesto, señorita*. Tomorrow. It will be done.” In these moments, Eleanor felt a quiet thrill, a sense of being not merely a translator, but a diplomat, a necessary fulcrum upon which the small, strange world of the Villa Solana turned.

Her walks to the village of El Robledal were the highlight of her day. The path wound down the hill, through the scent of wild thyme and sun-baked earth. The village itself was a cluster of whitewashed cubes around a small plaza, dominated by a church with a bell tower that housed a population of storks. Here, she was not “Miss Vance, the governess.” Here, she was “*La Señorita Inglesa*,” a figure of quiet curiosity and growing affection.

They trusted her. She would sit on a cool stone bench in the plaza while the village women, their dark hair coiled at their napes, would gather to talk. They spoke of the price of olive oil, the health of their children, the hope for rain. Eleanor listened, her English reserve slowly melting in the shared, unhurried warmth of their conversation. They would offer her a slice of a tomato, still warm from the sun, sprinkled with coarse salt and olive oil, and the simple, vibrant taste was more nourishing than any meal served in the silent, tense dining room at the villa.

One afternoon, she found Señora Isabella, the baker’s wife, weeping quietly outside her shop. Her small son had a fever, a bad one, and the doctor in the next town was away. The boy was delirious, and the local *curandero*’s remedies were not working. Eleanor’s heart clenched. She knew the Ashworths’ medicine cabinet was stocked with every English remedy imaginable.

“Wait here, Isabella,” she said, her voice firm with a confidence she did not entirely feel. “I will see what I can do.”

She ran back up the hill, her heart pounding in time with her steps. She slipped into the villa’s cool, dark hallway and retrieved the brown leather case of medicines. She found the quinine, the fever powder, the soothing balm. She did not ask permission. She simply took what she needed and hurried back down the path.

For the rest of the afternoon, she sat by the little boy’s cot in the cool, shuttered room above the bakery. She bathed his forehead with cool water and administered the fever powder as the label instructed, her Spanish clumsy but clear as she explained the dosage to the frantic mother. She did not leave until the child’s breathing had steadied and the terrible heat had begun to recede from his skin.

When she finally walked back to the Villa Solana as the sun was setting, painting the sky in strokes of rose and violet, she felt a profound sense of peace. She had not been a governess or a translator. She had simply been a woman helping another woman’s child.

The next morning, she found a small, wicker basket on the doorstep of the villa. Inside was a round loaf of bread, still fragrant and warm, a pot of honey, and a single, perfect sprig of rosemary. There was no note. There was no need. The gesture was as clear and as warm as the Spanish sun. She understood it as an offering, a thank you, and something more—a welcome.

She carried the basket into the kitchen, where the cook, a large, taciturn woman named Dolores, raised a questioning eyebrow. “*De Isabella*,” Eleanor said simply. Dolores’s stern face softened. She took the bread, sliced a thick piece, and drizzled it with honey. She pushed the plate towards Eleanor.

“*Come, señorita*,” she said. “*Usted es de la familia ahora*.” You are one of the family now.

Eleanor stood in the cool, dark kitchen, the scent of bread and honey and herbs filling the air. Outside, she could hear Mrs. Ashworth calling for her, a thin, querulous sound that belonged to another world. But in here, in this quiet moment, with the taste of honey on her tongue and the weight of belonging settling gently upon her shoulders, Eleanor felt the truth of the cook’s words. She was no longer just passing through. She was home.
