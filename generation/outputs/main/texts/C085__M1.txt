The prospect of a new governess arriving at Ashworth Park had been a matter of some excitement for Miss Amelia Croft. At nineteen, with her own season in London concluded in a flurry of chaperones and cards, she had returned to the quiet of her family’s estate with a sharpened sense of the world’s machinations. She had seen how fortunes were sought and alliances were made, and she viewed the arrival of a Miss Eleanor Vance with a keen, if entirely imagined, understanding of her purpose.

Mr. Alistair Ashworth, the master of the park, was a man of thirty-two, possessed of a handsome fortune and a disposition so gentle and scholarly that it was, to Amelia’s mind, a kind of vulnerability. His late wife had left him with two small children, Thomas and Clara, and a library of books he preferred to the company of his neighbours. He was, Amelia concluded with the absolute certainty of youth, the perfect target for a fortune-hunter with a pretty face and a sad story.

Miss Vance arrived on a Tuesday. She was not, as Amelia had feared, a dashingly beautiful creature of the sort found in Gothic novels. She was, in fact, rather plain, with mousy brown hair that would not hold a curl and a dress of grey bombazine that spoke of genteel poverty. Her only striking feature was her eyes, a startlingly clear shade of hazel, which seemed to observe everything with a calm, unflappable intelligence. This, Amelia decided, was even more dangerous. A woman of obvious beauty could be dismissed; a woman of quiet intelligence could insinuate herself. She was a serpent in a governess’s weeds.

Amelia’s campaign began immediately. She took it upon herself to be Miss Vance’s guide and confidante, her every observation a fishing line cast into the governess’s true intentions. “My dear Miss Vance,” she said one afternoon as they walked in the rose garden, the governess’s charges chasing a butterfly nearby. “You must find the country very quiet after… well, after all you have been through.” She let the vague reference to the governess’s fallen circumstances hang in the air.

Miss Vance’s gaze followed her young charges. “It is peaceful,” she replied, her voice even. “The children are a great joy. Their minds are like fresh wax, ready to take an impression. It is a great responsibility.”

“Indeed,” Amelia pressed, lowering her voice. “And Mr. Ashworth is so very kind. He is quite taken with you, I think. He has not had a governess of such… quality before.” She watched for a blush, a flicker of triumph. There was nothing. Miss Vance simply nodded thoughtfully.

“Mr. Ashworth is a most considerate employer. He has provided me with an excellent set of atlases for young Thomas’s geography lessons. It is a relief to have the proper materials.”

This was maddening. The woman spoke of atlases when the subject was the master of the house! Amelia tried another tack, this time with the children. She found little Clara in the nursery, arranging a set of wooden blocks.

“Clara, my dear,” Amelia said, sitting beside her. “Do you like your new governess?”

“Oh, yes, Miss Croft!” the child chirped. “She tells us stories about the stars. And she doesn’t make us do sums on Sundays.”

“And your papa? Does he like her too?” Amelia asked, her voice a conspiratorial whisper.

Clara looked up, her brow furrowed. “Papa likes her very much. He says she is sensible. He stood in the doorway for ever so long yesterday just watching her teach us our letters. He said she made the nursery feel… orderly.”

Orderly. The word was a cold splash of water. Amelia had imagined sighs and meaningful glances, not a man appreciating good order. But she could not be dissuaded. She was a protector, a guardian of the Ashworth name and fortune. She began to watch Mr. Ashworth, too, trying to see him through the governess’s calculating eyes.

What she saw was a man slowly, almost imperceptibly, coming out of a long, quiet grief. He began taking his tea in the schoolroom. He asked Miss Vance’s opinion on a new translation of Homer. He was seen in the village purchasing a new set of watercolours, which he presented to Miss Vance for the children’s lessons. Each small kindness was, to Amelia, a stone laid in the path to the altar. She saw the trap closing. She had to act.

The opportunity came during a rare dinner party, a small affair with only the local vicar and his wife. Amelia, seated beside her host, decided the time for subtlety was over. She had to save Mr. Ashworth from himself.

“Mr. Ashworth,” she began, her voice laced with a manufactured concern. “I feel I must speak to you, as a friend.”

He turned to her, his expression open and mild. “Of course, Miss Croft. Is something the matter?”

“It is about Miss Vance,” Amelia said, leaning closer. “I fear her designs may not be as… pure as you imagine. A woman in her position, with her prospects… one cannot be too careful. I have observed her, and I believe she is angling for a position far more permanent than that of governess.”

Alistair Ashworth’s gentle face did not cloud with anger or shock. Instead, a slow, warm smile spread across it. He looked at Amelia as one might look at a clever but misguided child.

“Her designs, you say?” he repeated, his voice soft.

“Yes,” Amelia insisted, emboldened. “She is a fortune-hunter, sir. She means to trap you. I only wish to protect you from such a mercenary creature.”

He set down his fork and gave her his full attention. “Miss Croft,” he said, his tone impossibly kind. “Your concern for me is touching, and I thank you for it. But you have misread the situation entirely. In the matter of designs, it is not Miss Vance who is the architect.”

Amelia stared, uncomprehending. “I do not understand.”

“It is I,” he said simply. “I have been designing to marry my governess for the past three months. I have been attempting to court her with atlases and watercolours and conversation in the schoolroom. I have been waiting for the right moment to speak, and for her to perhaps come to see me as something other than her employer.” He glanced towards the door, where Miss Vance could be seen through the crack, patiently listening to the vicar’s wife tell a long story. “Her only design, so far as I can tell, is to ensure my children learn their Latin declensions and do not fall into the pond. She is, as you say, a most sensible woman. It is her sensibility, and her kindness, and her quiet integrity that has captivated me. Not her prospects.”

Amelia felt the blood drain from her face. The world tilted on its axis. She, with her grand theories and her self-appointed role of saviour, had been a complete and utter fool. She had mistaken a man’s quiet, earnest courtship for a woman’s predatory ambition. The serpent was not in the nursery; it was in her own imagination.

The rest of the evening passed in a haze of mortification. When the vicar and his wife departed, Amelia felt she must make some sort of apology. She found Mr. Ashworth in the library, not with a book, but staring into the fire.

“Sir,” she began, her voice barely a whisper. “I am so very sorry. My behaviour was unpardonable.”

He turned, and his expression held no mockery, only a deep, gentle sympathy. “Think no more of it, Miss Croft. Your intentions were good. In a world full of artifice, it is a rare thing to meet with such earnest, if misguided, protectiveness.” He paused. “I have spoken with Miss Vance. She was… surprised. And flattered. It seems she had not noticed my attentions at all. She thought me merely a man who valued a well-run nursery.”

A small, hopeful smile touched his lips. “But she has agreed to consider my proposal. It seems we are both rather sensible, after all.”

Amelia retreated to her room, her cheeks still burning. She looked out of her window at the darkened gardens of Ashworth Park. She had set out to protect a man from a predator, only to discover he was a hunter of a different kind, and that his quarry was not his fortune, but his own quiet happiness. She had been so certain of the world’s villainy that she had failed to see its simple, gentle truths unfolding right before her eyes. The quiet of the Hampshire night settled around her, no longer a backdrop for intrigue, but a vast, peaceful emptiness, waiting to be filled with things she did not yet understand.
