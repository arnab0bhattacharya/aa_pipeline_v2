The Cathedral of Saint Aurelius in the city of Velledore stood as a monument to the pious vanity of the dead and the fashionable devotion of the living. Its great western gate, carved with the agonies of martyrs and the ecstasies of angels, was a marvel of intricate stone, but it was merely the background to the morning’s true spectacle. Here, upon the worn marble steps, sat Marcellus. He was a beggar by trade, and like all true artists, he had brought his craft to a state of flawless perfection. His rags were of the most becoming melancholy, his blindness a poetic fiction, and his begging bowl a masterpiece of simulated misery. Every morning the great ladies of Velledore dropped silver into his bowl, feeling quite beautifully charitable as they did so. Charity, after all, is merely a fashionable method of purchasing a good opinion of oneself at the expense of the poor.

It was shortly after the hour of Prime, when the square was still bathed in the pale, liquid gold of the morning sun, that a stranger approached the gate. He moved with a silent grace that was entirely foreign to the heavy, practical tread of the citizens of Velledore. His garments were of a white so dazzling that it seemed to reproach the weathered grey of the cathedral stone, and his hair had the exact tint of beaten bronze. 

He stopped before Marcellus and looked into the beggar’s bowl. It was empty, for the morning was yet young.

"I perceive," said the stranger, in a voice that had the cadence of distant music, "that the world has not yet awakened to its duties."

"The world is asleep, sir," answered Marcellus, turning his sightless, beautiful eyes towards the sound. "It dreams of its own importance, and forgets to drop its coppers."

"I am an angel," said the stranger, with a serene simplicity that precluded all argument. "I have been sent from the celestial kingdoms to offer you a crown."

Marcellus did not start. He had long ago learned that the unexpected is the only thing one can reasonably expect. "A crown of gold, I presume?" he asked.

"Of purest gold," replied the angel, "fashioned with a skill that the silversmiths of Velledore cannot comprehend. It is heavy with sapphires, and the clasp is a single ruby. It is a crown for a king."

"It sounds excessively beautiful," murmured the beggar. "And what, may I ask, is the condition of this magnificent generosity?"

"Merely that you surrender your begging bowl," said the angel. "Break it upon the stones, and the crown is yours. You shall be a king, and sit at feasts, and command the armies of the earth."

Marcellus smiled, a slow, delicate smile that played about the corners of his pale mouth. "I am afraid," he said, after a moment of thoughtful silence, "that I must decline."

The angel’s radiant countenance clouded, a passing shadow of perplexity. "You decline a king’s crown for a wooden bowl? It is but a coarse thing, half-broken, and stained with the dregs of sour wine."

"It is the source of my power," returned Marcellus, stroking the chipped rim affectionately. "If I were a king, I should be forced to wear crowns. I should have to sit upon thrones, which are notoriously uncomfortable, and dispense a justice I do not believe in. People would bow to me, but they would not see me. They would see only the crown. But with this bowl, I am the master of the universe. Every man who passes, be he a prince or a prelate, is forced to acknowledge that he is richer than I, and in that acknowledgment, he pays me tribute. The bowl is the mirror of their vanity; the crown would be but the cage of my freedom."

"But you are in rags, and you hunger," urged the angel, to whom the logic of suffering was still a sacred mystery.

"Rags are the only garments that are never out of fashion," answered Marcellus. "And hunger is an exquisite sauce that the rich can never purchase. No, my celestial friend, I will keep my bowl. It is far more agreeable to receive than to rule."

The angel looked at him with a profound wonder, and then, without another word, vanished into the morning mist, leaving behind only a faint scent of lilies and a sense of unresolved theology.

From the deep shadow of the cathedral porch, the Bishop of Velledore, the Right Reverend Father Anselm, had overheard the entire colloquy. He was a man of immense pomp and profound silks, who carried his piety before him like a heraldic banner. He stepped forward now, his jeweled crook clicking sharply against the marble.

"What means this insolence?" demanded the Bishop, his voice trembling with a righteous wrath that was, in reality, merely wounded authority. "You refuse the gift of Heaven, you lazy and ungrateful wretch? I command you to tell me why you have cast aside the grace of God for the leavings of men."

Marcellus turned his blind eyes towards the prelate, and a faint, ironical smile touched his lips. "Your Grace," he said, "I refused the crown because I prefer my bowl."

"Prefer a beggar’s dish to a king’s diadem? You are mad! Or worse, you are possessed of a sinful pride that is an abomination in the sight of the Lord. To refuse gold is to refuse the blessing of Providence."

"Gold is merely a yellow metal," said Marcellus, with the gentle insolence of a philosopher. "It is the supreme conceit of Your Grace’s religion that it mistakes glitter for grace. The angel offered me a crown, but he did not understand the true nature of power. A king must justify his crown by his virtues; a beggar justifies his bowl by his needs. Virtues are excessively difficult to maintain, and are, as Your Grace knows, often terribly inconvenient. Needs are a permanent and unanswerable argument. With a crown, I should be the servant of the state, the slave of ceremony, and the target of every assassin’s knife. With my bowl, I am the critic of humanity. Every coin that drops into it is a confession of the giver’s guilt. I do not beg for alms, Your Grace; I collect taxes on the consciences of the rich."

The Bishop’s face grew purple, a colour that clashed horribly with his violet vestments. "This is mere sophistry! The devil speaks through your mouth. You wallow in your filth because you love the idleness of sin."

"I wallow, as Your Grace elegantly phrases it, because it is the only position from which one may look up at the world without straining one’s neck," replied Marcellus. "You, in your silks and your jewels, are forced to look down. It is a most tiring posture, and it gives one a false impression of the ground. I see the true nature of men. I see them when they drop their masks to drop their pennies. The saint and the miser are exactly the same to me; they both desire to be rid of a burden. You preach from your pulpit that the poor are blessed. I merely test that blessing. And I find it very profitable."

"You are impious!" cried the Bishop, raising his crook as if to strike.

"I am practical," returned the beggar, unmoved. "Impiety is merely the irreverence of the poor. When Your Grace refuses the truth, it is called theology. When I refuse a crown, it is called madness. But I know that in this bowl lies the only magic that works. The angel wished me to give up my art. For begging, Your Grace, is an art. It is the art of making something out of nothing, which is precisely what your priests claim to do with their sacraments. Why should I trade my miracle for a piece of metal?"

The Bishop stood speechless, his anger choking the usual flow of his ecclesiastical rhetoric. He gazed at the beggar, at the torn cloak, the sightless eyes, and the battered bowl. He saw in them a reflection of his own elaborate ceremonies, stripped of their velvet and gold.

"You are a fool," he said at last, but his voice had lost its thunder. It was the voice of a man who has been confronted with an uncomfortable truth and does not know where to put it.

"Perhaps," agreed Marcellus, holding out his bowl with a graceful, practiced gesture. "But even a fool must live. And as the morning is advanced, and the charity of the righteous is yet wanting, perhaps Your Grace, in his infinite kindness, would condescend to drop a coin into the bowl of a fool. It is, after all, the least a bishop can do for a king who has abdicated."

The Right Reverend Father Anselm hesitated. He looked at the empty wooden bowl, and then, with a sudden, nervous movement, he drew a golden florin from his purse and let it fall. It clinked with a bright, metallic sound that echoed in the quiet air.

Marcellus smiled his slow, delicate smile. "I thank you, Your Grace," he said. "You see, the crown would have been a most terrible burden. But the coin is just heavy enough to remind me that the world is still very foolish, and that the Cathedral of Saint Aurelius is still very good for business."

And the Bishop, murmuring a hasty prayer for his own soul, swept up the marble steps and into the fragrant gloom of the cathedral, leaving the beggar alone in the sunlight, the master of his gate and the keeper of his truth.
