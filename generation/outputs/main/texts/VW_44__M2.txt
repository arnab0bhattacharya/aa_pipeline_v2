The train had shed its coat of coal dust upon the platform at Victoria, and the air, though cool, carried a heaviness that felt less like atmosphere and more like a lingering memory of smoke. Elara stood, not quite certain if she was stepping forward or backward, her luggage a single, battered valise that seemed to pull at her arm with a gravity distinct from her own. London. The word, when she had spoken it in the carriage, had sounded flat, dead, a stone dropped into a dry well. Here, however, it was a living thing, a thrumming, grey beast that breathed around her, indifferent to the fact that she had left its heart twenty years ago to beat in the slower, sun-drenched rhythm of Provence. She looked at the faces around her—sharply dressed, hats tilted with a certain aggressive certainty, eyes scanning for threats or opportunities—and felt a sudden, sharp prickle of displacement. She was not a stranger here, not legally, not in the eyes of the law; she was a citizen, a wife, a woman with a passport and a history. Yet, as she moved through the crowd, she felt transparent, a ghost passing through the solid matter of the living.

Her mother had died on a Tuesday, she recalled, or perhaps it had been a Wednesday; the days had blurred in the weeks leading up to the funeral, a continuous wash of grey rain and white flowers in the village square. But here, in London, time was punctuated by bells and the sharp click of heels on pavement. She needed a cab. She raised her hand, the gesture automatic, trained by twenty years of Parisian streets, but the drivers in London were larger, their coats darker, their expressions harder. One stopped. The driver looked at her, then at the valise, then back at her, his eyes lingering on the faint trace of sunburn on her neck, a tan that seemed unnatural in the pallid light of the city.

"Where to, miss?"

"Number 14, Hartley Street," she said, and the voice that came out was strange to her own ears, accented, slightly higher than she remembered, carrying a softness that the London wind had not yet stripped away.

The cab swerved into traffic. The city rushed past the window, a kaleidoscope of brick and iron, of people hurrying with purpose, of newspapers fluttering like wings. Elara watched them, these people who had not changed, who still wore their mourning veils and their stiff collars, and she thought of her sister, Clara. Clara, who had stayed. Clara, who had kept the house, the routines, the silence. There was a quality to English silence that Elara had once admired, a clean, crisp absence, like a cut of crystal. But now, returning, it felt suffocating, a heavy quilt draped over the face of the world, smothering the breath. In France, silence was different; it was a space filled with the sound of cicadas, or the rustle of leaves, or the distant clatter of a bicycle. It was a silence that invited thought. The English silence was a wall.

As the cab turned onto Hartley Street, the houses rose on either side, tall and narrow, their windows dark eyes staring out at the street. Number 14 was there, as it had always been, but it seemed smaller, hunched under the weight of the years. Elara paid the driver, her fingers trembling slightly as she counted the coins. The money felt heavy, foreign in its own way. She stepped out, and the door of the cab clamped shut behind her with a finality that made her flinch.

She walked up the path. The gate was ajar. Inside, the garden was overgrown, the roses she had planted as a girl long dead, the grass high and unkempt. It was a mirror of her own interior state, she thought, a tangle of neglect and decay. She reached for the latch, and as she did, she saw a figure in the window upstairs. Clara. She was standing with her back to the glass, her silhouette sharp against the dim room, arranging flowers, perhaps, or simply standing. They did not look at each other. The moment hung, suspended, charged with a tension that was not anger, but something more complex, a recognition of the chasm that had opened between them. Elara let go of the latch. She could not go in yet. She could not face the stillness of that house, the dust that would be on the furniture, the smell of lavender and old paper that would greet her.

Instead, she turned and walked down the street, away from the house, away from the grave that was already dug, away from the sister who had remained and the self that had been left behind. She walked until the street changed, until the houses gave way to larger, more sprawling buildings, until the air itself felt different, less confined. She stopped at a corner, where a street musician was playing a violin. The music was thin, reedy, but it was a thread of sound in the grey void. She listened, closing her eyes, letting the notes wash over her. She thought of her husband, Pierre. He would be in the garden, perhaps, pruning the olive tree, his hands stained green, his face turned up to the sun. He would not understand this. He would not understand the weight of the fog, or the sharpness of the cold, or the way the people here moved with a purpose that seemed so utterly devoid of joy.

"I suppose you’ll be staying at the hotel?" a voice said behind her.

Elara turned. It was a woman, young, with a hat pinned low over her eyes. She looked at Elara with a curiosity that was not unkind, but was certainly foreign.

"No," Elara said, and the word was barely a whisper. "I am staying with my sister."

The woman nodded, as if this were a matter of great interest, and then turned away, her heels clicking on the pavement. Elara watched her go, a small, dark shape disappearing into the crowd. She felt a sudden, fierce longing for the smell of pine resin, for the blue of the sky over the hills, for the simple, unadorned truth of a life lived in the open. Here, in London, everything was layered, hidden, draped in veils of propriety and protocol. She was a stranger in her own skin, a woman who had forgotten the language of the street, the rhythm of the city, the unspoken rules that governed the movement of bodies through space.

She turned back toward Hartley Street. The house was there, waiting. The window upstairs was empty now. The silence had returned, but it was no longer a wall; it was a question. Elara adjusted her shawl, tightened her grip on the valise, and began the walk back. She did not know what she would say to Clara, what words could bridge the twenty years of absence, the twenty years of sun and shadow, of wine and bread, of a life lived in a different key. Perhaps she would say nothing. Perhaps she would simply stand in the doorway, letting the light of the hallway fall upon her, letting the years settle into her bones. She would wait. She would listen. And in the waiting, she would find, perhaps, a way to speak again, not in the language of the past, but in the language of the present, which was silent, and vast, and entirely her own.
