The wind that came down from the Iron Peaks was a blade, and it had been sharpening itself on the stones of Valerius’s tower for a week. Inside, the only warmth was a small, stubborn fire in a hearth and the single beeswax candle that stood sentinel on his table. Its flame threw his shadow, a vast and stooped thing, against the rough-hewn wall where the maps of his dominions—the town of Oakhaven and the surrounding lands—were pinned. But Valerius was not looking at the maps. His world was contained in the small, velvet-lined chest before him.

His fingers, thin and yellow as old parchment, moved with the practiced reverence of a priest handling holy relics. One by one, he lifted the coins. The heavy, silver marks of the Northern Kingdom, stamped with the grim-faced king. The smaller, bronze obols from the southern trade routes. And the crowning glory, the gold sovereigns of the lost Valerian Empire, each one a small, perfect sun he could hold in his palm. He would hold each to the candlelight, watching the glint, feeling the cool, dense weight of it, before placing it back in its designated slot. The soft *clink* of metal on metal was the only music he had ever loved. This was the ritual, the nightly affirmation of his existence. The world outside could freeze and starve; in this room, with his gold, Valerius was a king.

A sound, sharp and alien, cut through the wind’s howl and the whisper of coins. A knock. Not the frantic hammering of a beggar, nor the peremptory rap of a tax collector. It was a small, hesitant tap, so quiet he almost dismissed it as a branch tapping against the shuttered window. But it came again. Three soft taps.

Valerius froze, a sovereign clutched in his hand. No one came to his tower. No one had a reason to. He was Valerius the Miser, the Dragon of the Spire, a fixture of the landscape as much as the dead oak on the hill. Annoyance warred with a sliver of cold curiosity. He placed the gold back in the chest, his ritual shattered. He rose, his joints cracking in protest, and crossed the stone floor. The heavy oak door was barred with an iron bolt he had forged himself. With a grunt of effort, he slid it back. The groan of the metal was a lament.

He opened the door just a crack, and the wind immediately tried to force its way in, carrying with it the scent of snow and pine. Huddled on the narrow landing was a child, no older than eight. She was swaddled in a threadbare shawl, her face pale and pinched with cold, her eyes wide and dark in the gloom. Snowflakes were melting in her matted hair.

“What is it?” Valerius’s voice was a rustle of dry leaves.

The girl flinched but held her ground. In her hands, she cupped a small, clay oil lamp, its wick a black, dead thing. “Please, sir,” she said, her voice a thin whisper that the wind tried to steal. “Our lamp has gone out. My mother is ill. We… we have no fire.”

Valerius stared at her. He saw not a child, but a problem. A disruption. A drain on resources. “Go to the baker. The smith. They have fires aplenty.”

“They are all shuttered for the night, sir. And the snow…” She gestured vaguely at the swirling darkness behind her. “The only light we saw was from your window. A single candle.”

His candle. His one extravagance, his tool for his nightly counting. The thought of parting with any of its light was physically painful. It was like asking him to give away a piece of his own flesh. “I have nothing for you,” he said, and began to close the door.

“Please,” she said again, and this time there was a tremor in her voice that was not from the cold. “Just a light. Just a moment of your flame.”

He paused. The word ‘flame’ echoed in his mind. It was true. It was all she wanted. Not his food, not his coin, not his shelter. Just a flicker, a temporary thing that would cost him nothing but a moment. With a sigh that seemed to dredge up dust from the depths of his lungs, he stepped back and jerked his head, a gesture for her to enter.

The girl slipped inside, bringing the winter with her. She stood shivering on the rushes, her gaze falling immediately on the open chest on the table. Her eyes widened, not with greed, but with a kind of awestruck wonder, as if she were seeing a hoard of fallen stars. Valerius felt a bizarre, possessive shame and slammed the lid of the chest shut. The sound was like a gunshot in the quiet room.

He took a slender twig from the hearth, lit it from his precious candle, and held it out to her. The flame sputtered and danced. The girl carefully brought her lamp forward, and he touched the twig to her wick. It caught, a small, brave light blooming in the clay dish. The warm, oily smell of the lamp filled the space between them.

“Thank you, sir,” she whispered. “Thank you.”

“Go,” he said, his voice gruff. “And bar the door behind you.”

She gave him a small, solemn nod and was gone. The door groaned shut, the bolt slid home, and the tower was silent once more. But it was not the same silence. It was a silence that held the echo of her voice, the scent of her lamp oil, the memory of her wide, dark eyes. Valerius returned to his table and opened the chest. The gold sovereigns gleamed in the candlelight. But their warmth seemed to have fled. The *clink* as he dropped one back into its slot sounded hollow, meaningless.

He tried to resume his count, but his mind would not obey. He kept seeing the child’s face, pale against the dark. He found himself wondering her name. Did she have bread for the morning? Was the fire in her home truly out? The thought of the mother, ill and cold in the dark, was a splinter under his skin. It was an absurd, irrational thought. He owed them nothing. The world was a place of scarcity, and he had secured his portion. That was the only law that mattered.

But the thoughts came anyway, unbidden and persistent. He saw the tower not as a fortress, but as a cage. He saw the gold not as a treasure, but as a pile of cold, dead metal. The child had asked for a light, and in giving it, he felt as though she had taken something else entirely—something he could not count or lock away. She had given him a glimpse of a world outside his own, a world of shared warmth and shared suffering.

Valerius sat back in his chair, the single candle casting his lonely shadow on the wall. He looked at the gold, then at the door. For the first time in forty years, he did not think of his wealth as a shield against the world, but as a wall that kept him alone within it. The wind howled, and in its voice, he thought he could hear a child’s whisper, asking not for gold, but for light. And he, the Dragon of the Spire, had given it away.
