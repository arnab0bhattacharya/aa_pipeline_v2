The light had gone from the room—a pale, spent light it had been, the colour of weak tea—and now the gas-jet hissed its small blue hymn against the darkness, and the fire, which had been sulking all afternoon, took heart and began to crackle, throwing its flickering orange over the heap of clothes upon the floor. Sylvia sat among them, her legs folded under her, her hands moving with a slow, absent-minded tenderness over the stuffs—the wool, the serge, the one good velvet that had been for best—as though she were reading a language she had almost forgotten. The flat was very quiet. From the street below came the muffled clop of a horse, the distant cry of a newsboy, sounds that seemed to belong to another London, a London of gaslight and fog and hansom cabs, and she thought, with that queer doubling of the mind that comes in the midst of grief, how strange it was that the city should go on, that the wheels should turn, when her mother lay three days dead and the world had lost its centre.

She had meant only to sort, to divide, to make those neat piles which are the arithmetic of loss—this for the ragman, this for the hospital, this for herself, though she knew she would keep everything, that she would sit among these emptied sleeves and bodices until the fire died and the gas guttered, and that even then she would not have begun to let go. The coat she held now was a dark green, the colour of a holly leaf, with a collar of fur that had gone bald at the edges, and it was heavy, heavier than it looked, as though it had absorbed the weight of years. She turned it over, felt in the pockets, out of habit, out of that superstition which makes us search the garments of the dead for some last word, some sign, and her fingers closed on something stiff and small—a card, a ticket, a scrap of paper that had been folded and refolded until it was soft as cloth.

She held it to the light. A theatre ticket. The Adelphi, it said, in faded red ink, and a date—the twelfth of December, 1897—and a seat, a stall, no less, which was odd, for her mother had never been one for stalls, had always said the pit was good enough for the likes of them, had laughed her dry laugh and said she liked to see the faces of the people in the boxes, to guess what they were thinking. But there it was: a stall, row F, seat twelve, and the play—the play was called *The Silver Cord*, or something like it, a name that meant nothing to Sylvia, that had slipped out of the world's memory as so many things slip, as her mother herself had slipped, quietly, between one breath and the next, on a Tuesday afternoon in a room in Bloomsbury, with the rain coming down and the fire not yet lit.

She turned the ticket over. Nothing on the back. No name, no message, no clue. And yet it seemed to her, as she sat there with the fire warming her knees and the gas-jet singing its thin song, that the ticket was a key, and that if she could only turn it in the lock the door would open on a night twenty years gone, a night before she was born, when her mother was not her mother but a woman named Ellen, a woman with a waist and a laugh and a secret or two, a woman who had put on her best coat and gone out into the December dark to sit in row F, seat twelve, and watch a play called *The Silver Cord*.

She tried to see it. The fog, first of all—there would have been fog, there was always fog in those days, a yellow fog that swallowed the lamps and made the streets mysterious, made them a stage set for something. Her mother would have walked, of course; she never took a cab if she could help it, said the walking was half the pleasure, said it gave her time to think. She would have walked from—from where? From the rooms in Lamb's Conduit Street, perhaps, where she had lived before she married, a street of dingy houses and respectable landladies, where the milk came at dawn and the organ-grinder came on Saturdays. She would have walked, and the fog would have wet her face, and she would have held her skirt up a little, out of the wet, and perhaps she would have been humming something, a tune from the halls, something with a bit of a swing to it.

And who was with her? For a woman did not go alone to the Adelphi in those days, not to a stall, not in her best green coat with the fur collar. There must have been someone—a man, surely, a man who had bought the ticket, who had handed it to her with a little bow, who had sat beside her in the dark and perhaps had taken her hand, or perhaps had not, had been too shy, had talked too much about the play, had said the leading lady was no good, had said the whole thing was rubbish, and she had laughed and told him to be quiet, to let her enjoy it. What was his name? Sylvia could not imagine. Her father—no, it could not have been her father; her father had been a man of habits, of regular hours, who thought the theatre a waste of money and the evening a time for the newspaper and the pipe. This was someone else. Someone with a bit of dash to him, perhaps, someone who wore his hat at an angle and had a way of looking at a woman that made her feel she was the only person in the room.

The fire shifted, sent up a little shower of sparks, and Sylvia, watching them die against the dark, felt the old ache of it—the ache of all the lives her mother had lived before her, the lives that had been folded away like the ticket in the pocket, forgotten, or nearly forgotten, until a daughter's hand should find them. She thought of her mother's hands, those capable hands that had peeled potatoes and darned socks and held her own small hand on the way to school, and she thought how those same hands had once held a programme, had once clapped, perhaps, at the end of an act, had once been kissed in a hansom cab. It was unbearable, somehow, and it was beautiful, and she cried a little, quietly, among the clothes, and then she stopped and wiped her face with the back of her hand and looked again at the ticket.

The twelfth of December. A Friday, she thought, though she could not be sure. A Friday in winter, with the shops lit up for Christmas and the streets full of people and the smell of chestnuts in the air. Her mother would have come out of the theatre into the cold, her cheeks flushed, her eyes bright, and she would have stood a moment on the pavement, pulling on her gloves, and perhaps she would have said, "Well, that was something," and perhaps the man would have said, "Shall we have a bite of supper?" and perhaps she would have said yes, and they would have gone to an oyster bar or a chop-house, somewhere warm and steamy, and she would have eaten with a good appetite, for she had always had a good appetite, and she would have laughed at something the man said, and the man would have thought himself in love with her, and perhaps he was, and perhaps it came to nothing, and perhaps it came to something, and she never told, she never said a word, she kept it all in the pocket of the green coat, and the green coat hung in the wardrobe year after year, and the ticket stayed there, a little stiff scrap of paper, a little piece of a night in December, 1897.

Sylvia folded the ticket carefully and put it in her own pocket. The fire was dying now, the gas-jet still hissing, and the room was cold. She would keep the coat, she thought; she would keep everything. She would hang the green coat in her own wardrobe and perhaps one day, when she was old, she would put her hand in the pocket and find the ticket again, and she would sit on the floor among the clothes and try to imagine the woman she had been before she was born.
