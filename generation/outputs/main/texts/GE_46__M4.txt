The cottage at Bradecote End stood where the lane, having climbed from the mill, thought better of its ambition and subsided into a cart-track; so that the house, with its roof of heavy thatch and its walls washed the colour of wet sand, seemed always to be turning its back on the world and its face towards the little garden where the marigolds grew rank and the bees kept up their summer-long disputation. Here lived Reuben Hallowfield, thatcher and hedge-cutter, with his wife Sarah and their daughter Lucy; and it was to this door, upon a Saturday in the October of the year 1823, that Lucy came back, not as she had gone away three years before—a heavy-footed girl in a cotton gown, with her box on her shoulder—but stepping down from the carrier’s cart with a bandbox in her hand and a bonnet that had been bought in a town the name of which her mother could never remember, and a manner so composed, so delicately reserved, that it put a distance between herself and the family like a sheet of glass.

For Lucy had been in service at Marchmont Hall, where the ladies wore lace at breakfast and the footmen spoke in whispers, and where she had learned to hold her tongue and to arrange flowers and to move through a room without seeming to disturb the air of it. She had learned also—though of this she said nothing—to feel the weight of a look from a gentleman, and to understand that a promise made in a summer-house is not the less a promise because it has no witness but the bees.

Her mother saw at once that something was amiss. Sarah Hallowfield was a woman of slow speech and quick eyes, and she had carried Lucy in her arms through the measles and the whooping-cough, and had known the child’s face in every weather of its temper. Now she saw a face that smiled at the right moments and was silent at the wrong ones; that ate its bread and butter with a fine appetite and then sat staring at the fire as if the fire had asked it a question it could not answer. She said nothing for a week. She said nothing for two. But on the Sunday evening, when Reuben had gone down to the Holly Bush to hear what the men were saying about the price of wheat, she put down her knitting and said:

“Lucy, my wench, thou’rt not happy.”

Lucy looked up quickly, and then, with that new manner of hers, looked down again. “I am very well, mother,” she said. “The air here is better than at the Hall. I sleep well.”

“Thou sleepest,” said Sarah, “but thou dost not rest. I know the difference. I have watched thee these three Sundays, and thou hast a trouble on thee. Is it the place? Wast thou not well used?”

“I was well used,” said Lucy. “The ladies were kind. Mrs. Ellwood gave me a shawl when I left, and Miss Caroline cried.”

“Then it is not the place. It is something thou hast brought away with thee.” Sarah’s needles clicked again, slowly. “There is no need to tell me, if thou canst not. But I am thy mother, and I have a right to the sorrow that is in my own house.”

Lucy was silent a long while. The clock ticked; the fire fell together with a soft crash; a moth that had got in blundered against the lamp. At last she said, in a voice that was carefully steady:

“Mother, I have nothing to tell. Only I am not the same. I have seen things—I have seen how people live—and I cannot be content with Bradecote any more. It is like a dream here. It is like being asleep.”

“And at the Hall thou wert awake?” said Sarah, not unkindly.

“I was awake,” said Lucy. And then, because the truth was pressing at her lips, she added: “There was a gentleman—a gentleman who came to the house—but I cannot speak of it. Do not ask me. It is done with. It is like a book that is shut.”

Sarah said no more that night. But she lay awake, and she thought of many things; and in the morning she said to her husband, as she set the bacon before him:

“Reuben, thou must speak to Lucy about Thomas Whitlock.”

For Thomas Whitlock, the blacksmith at the cross-roads, had waited three years. He was a broad-shouldered man of thirty, with a face that seemed to have been hammered into shape on his own anvil—honest, plain, and a little dull—and he had loved Lucy Hallowfield since she was a girl in short petticoats. Every Sunday he came to the cottage after church, and sat on the settle with his great hands on his knees, and said little; and every Sunday Lucy was kind to him, and said little; and the little that was said was as far from the truth as the moon is from the weathercock. He had asked her before she went to the Hall, and she had said she was too young. He had asked her again when she came back, and she had said she must think. And now he waited, patient as a horse at a gate, while the autumn went on and the leaves came down.

“I will speak to her,” said Reuben. “But thou knowest, Sarah, that Lucy has a tongue of her own now, and it is not the tongue of Bradecote. She says ‘father’ as if it were a word in a book.”

“She is thy daughter,” said Sarah, “and she is in trouble. Speak to her as her father, not as a man who is afraid of her bonnet.”

So it came about that on a Tuesday, when the mist lay low on the fields and the rooks were noisy in the elms, Reuben Hallowfield walked with his daughter down the lane towards the mill, and, having cleared his throat several times, said:

“Lucy, Thomas Whitlock is a good man.”

“He is a good man,” said Lucy.

“He has waited three years.”

“I know it.”

“And he has a house with a good roof—which is more than most men can say—and a bit of land, and his trade is steady. He would make thee a good husband, and thou wouldst make him a good wife, and thy mother and I would be glad to see thee settled before we are too old to dance at the wedding.”

Lucy walked on in silence. The mist was in her hair, and her hands were clasped before her, and her face was pale and composed.

“Father,” she said at last, “I will tell you the truth. I cannot marry Thomas Whitlock. I am not worthy of him.”

Reuben stopped in the lane. “Not worthy? Thou—not worthy? Thou hast been in a great house, and thou hast learned manners, and thou art the prettiest girl in the parish, and thou sayest thou art not worthy of a blacksmith?”

“I am not worthy,” said Lucy, “because I have a secret in my heart that would make him unhappy. I cannot tell it to you. I can only tell you that it is so. And I would rather live here all my life, and be a burden to you, than marry a man I could not love as a wife should love.”

There was a long pause. The rooks had settled; the mist was thickening; a cow lowed somewhere in the distance, and the sound seemed to belong to another world.

“Lucy,” said Reuben at last, and his voice was rough, “I am not a scholar, and I cannot talk to thee as thy fine ladies would talk. But I know this: a secret that keeps a woman from her duty is a bad secret. It will grow in the dark, like a mushroom. Thou hadst better tell it, and have done with it.”

“I cannot,” said Lucy. “Not yet. Perhaps never.”

“Then,” said Reuben, and he turned and looked at her with a strange mixture of anger and tenderness, “thou must not blame thy mother if she frets, and thou must not blame me if I am silent. We are plain folk, and we cannot follow thee into thy fine troubles. But I will tell Thomas Whitlock that thou wilt not have him, and I will tell him why—that thou hast a secret, and art not worthy of him—and he must make what he can of it.”

He walked on, and Lucy followed him, and neither spoke again. But when they came to the gate of the cottage, and Reuben had his hand on the latch, Lucy said, in a low voice:

“Father, I will tell you one thing. The secret is not a sin of mine. It is a sorrow. And if I could tell it to you, you would not think me wicked—only unhappy.”

Reuben looked at her for a moment, and then he did a thing that he had not done since she was a child: he put his arm round her shoulders and drew her to him.

“Well, well,” he said, “thou art my wench, sin or sorrow. Come in and have thy dinner. Thy mother has made a pie.”

And they went in together, and the door closed behind them, and the mist went on thickening over the fields, and the rooks were silent, and the blacksmith’s hammer, far off at the cross-roads, rang on through the afternoon, patient as the man himself, beating out the iron of a future that no one could foresee.
