The letter which reached me at my Baker Street rooms upon a raw November morning was written in a hand which, though firm, betrayed a certain tremor of agitation. The paper was of an expensive quality, and the ink a deep purple, but the address was penned with such haste that the final letters of my name had almost run off the edge of the sheet. It came from one Miss Eleanor Vane, and it begged my immediate assistance in a matter of the utmost gravity affecting her inheritance and her personal safety.

My companion, Sherlock Holmes, had been engaged in an abstruse chemical experiment involving the distillation of some foul-smelling alkaloid, but he looked up with keen interest as I read the missive aloud. The narrative it contained was of a singular nature. Miss Vane, the sole survivor of the Vane family of Kelsworth Manor, had travelled to Northumberland to take possession of her estate following the death of her father. Upon her arrival, however, she had been met not with the deference due to a mistress of the house, but with a stony and uniform hostility.

The household, consisting of a butler named Grimes and a housekeeper of the name of Mrs. Lyle, refused to acknowledge her identity. They maintained that the true Miss Eleanor Vane had perished at sea some months previously, and that the young woman before them was an adventuress seeking to impose upon the estate. The confusion was compounded by the sudden death of Mr. Jarvis, the family solicitor, who had been the only man capable of verifying her identity. He had been found dead in his study at Kelsworth only two days before her arrival, his demise attributed by the local doctor to a sudden apoplexy, though the letter hinted at darker suspicions.

"It is a capital mistake to theorize before one has facts," Holmes remarked, blowing a cloud of smoke toward the ceiling. "However, the facts as presented are sufficiently curious. A dead solicitor, a hostile staff, and a claimant who is denied by those who should know her best. And the portrait?"

"The portrait," I read on, "hangs in the Great Hall. It is a likeness of my grandmother, painted some fifty years ago. The household admits that I resemble the woman in the picture, yet they claim this resemblance is the proof of my guilt, asserting that I have studied the canvas to perfect my disguise. I am alone in the house, for they have retired to the servants' quarters and refuse me service. The wind howls about the eaves, and I confess I am afraid."

"We must go to Northumberland, Watson," said Holmes, rising and knocking the ashes from his pipe. "The drama is already in progress, and we are late upon the scene."

The journey north was accomplished in a series of jolting carriages and a slow train that rattled through the industrial grime of the Midlands before emerging into the wilder, greener country of the North. By the time we reached the coast, the weather had turned to a biting gale that drove the grey waves of the North Sea into a frenzy of foam against the black rocks. Kelsworth Manor stood upon a headland, a stark silhouette of grey stone and slate, looking out over the abyss with the grim endurance of a fortress.

We were admitted not by Grimes, but by a terrified local girl who had been sent to attend Miss Vane. She led us through a cold, stone-flagged corridor into the Great Hall. The room was cavernous, lit by a fire that struggled against the draughts. Above the mantelpiece hung the portrait of which the letter had spoken. It was a striking work, depicting a woman in the dress of the thirties, with dark hair and eyes of a penetrating grey. The resemblance to the young woman who rose to greet us was indeed remarkable.

Miss Vane was pale, her features drawn, but there was a resolute set to her jaw that spoke of good blood. She wore a travelling dress of dark serge, and her hands, which clasped ours in greeting, were icy cold.

"Mr. Holmes, Dr. Watson, you are come like angels of mercy," she said, her voice low and steady. "I have been besieged in this house for three days. Grimes and Mrs. Lyle have locked the larder, and I have subsisted upon the few biscuits I brought in my portmanteau."

"A pretty state of affairs," said Holmes, casting his keen eyes rapidly about the room. "And the servants?"

"They keep to the wing," said she. "They watch me. I feel their eyes upon me whenever I move."

Holmes strode to the portrait and examined it closely with his lens. "A fine hand," he murmured. "And the likeness is exact. Tell me, Miss Vane, this Mr. Jarvis—where did he die?"

"In the study, at the end of the gallery. The door is locked, but I have the key."

"Let us proceed," said Holmes.

The study was a small, book-lined room, still bearing the musty smell of old paper and stale tobacco. The desk was bare, save for an inkwell and a heavy brass candlestick. Holmes dropped to his knees and began to scrutinize the carpet, moving with the agility of a hound. He ran his fingers over the surface of the desk, the chair, and the window sill.

"Apoplexy, they say," Holmes muttered. "Yet observe, Watson, the window latch. It is a patent fastener, yet it is scratched and abraded. Someone has forced it, though clumsily."

He turned his attention to the fireplace. It was swept, but Holmes plunged his hand up the flue and withdrew it coated in soot. From the debris, he extracted a small, charred fragment of paper.

"Ah," he cried. "What have we here?"

He smoothed the scrap upon the desk. It was too burnt for legible script, but a wax seal remained partially intact.

"The seal of the Vane family," said Miss Vane. "But what does it mean?"

"It means," said Holmes, "that Mr. Jarvis was engaged in correspondence that he deemed necessary to destroy. But he was interrupted."

Holmes rose and paced the room. "Let us consider the facts. Mr. Jarvis dies suddenly. The servants, who have been in the house for years, deny the rightful heir. They claim she died at sea. Yet here she stands. If she were an impostor, why would the servants deny her? An impostor would likely have bribed them, or they would have exposed her immediately to the authorities. Instead, they lock themselves away and treat her as a ghost. It is illogical."

He stopped before the window, peering out at the darkening sky. "Unless," he said softly, "they are not trying to expose an impostor, but to displace a rightful owner. If Miss Vane is proved to be who she says she is, she has the power to dismiss them. If she is an impostor, the estate falls to... whom?"

"To my cousin, Richard Vane," said Miss Vane. "He is the next of kin. But he is in India."

"Is he?" Holmes turned sharply. "Watson, fetch Grimes. I wish to ask him a question regarding the painting of the portrait."

I went to the servants' quarters and summoned the butler. He was a heavy-set man with a face like a bulldog, and he entered the study with a surly reluctance.

"You wish to see the picture, sir?" he grunted, as Holmes indicated the direction of the hall.

"I do," said Holmes. "Specifically, I wish to know who commissioned the restoration of the frame last year. The gilding is fresh."

Grimes shifted his weight. "Mr. Jarvis arranged it, sir. Before he died."

"Indeed. And the restorer?"

"I believe he came from Alnwick. A Mr. Hogg."

Holmes smiled—a thin, dangerous smile. "A fiction, Grimes. There is no restorer by that name in the district. The frame was regilded by a man named Timpson, who resides in the village. I saw his mark upon the ladder in the hall, which you neglected to put away."

Grimes paled. "I... I do not know what you mean."

"I think you do," said Holmes, his voice cutting like a whip. "You and Mrs. Lyle have conspired to deny Miss Vane in the hope of securing the estate for yourself, or for a third party. You murdered Mr. Jarvis when he discovered your scheme, perhaps by placing a noxious substance in his snuff, and staged the scene to look like apoplexy. You then sought to drive the young lady away by psychological torment."

"You have no proof!" the butler snarled, his composure cracking.

"On the contrary," said Holmes, stepping aside to reveal the window latch. "Your boot, Grimes. The heel is chipped in a manner that matches the abrasion on the metal. You forced the window to simulate a burglary, or perhaps to gain entry after Jarvis locked himself in. And the charred paper? A draft of the will which named Miss Vane, which Jarvis was preparing to verify."

Grimes made a sudden, violent lunge toward the door, but Holmes had anticipated the movement. With a swift, judo-like throw, he sent the heavy man crashing to the floorboards. I rushed forward to assist, and together we secured the fellow with the bell-pull cords.

The confession came later, in the cold light of the drawing-room. Mrs. Lyle, confronted with the evidence and the sight of her accomplice in irons, broke down completely. They had indeed poisoned the solicitor, fearing he would expose their embezzlement of the estate's funds. They had spread the rumor of Miss Vane's death to pave the way for a fraudulent claim by a distant relative who had promised them a share of the spoils.

"The portrait," said Holmes, as we prepared to leave the next morning, "was the one true thing in the house. It showed the blood of the Vanes, and it was that blood which Grimes feared. He could deny the woman, but he could not deny the likeness in the hall, and so he sought to destroy the woman herself."

Miss Vane stood upon the steps of Kelsworth as our carriage pulled away, the morning sun finally breaking through the coastal mist to light up the grey stone.

"A singular case, Watson," said Holmes, settling back into his cushions. "The Northumberland air is bracing, but I confess I prefer the smog of London. At least there, one knows that the villains are lurking in the alleys, rather than serving the tea."

"And the portrait?" I asked.

"A mere coincidence of genetics," he replied, closing his eyes. "But in this trade, Watson, one must never underestimate the value of a good likeness."
