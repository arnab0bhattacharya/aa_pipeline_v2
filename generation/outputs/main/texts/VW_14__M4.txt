The train, which had stood so long in the throat of the station that it seemed to have taken root there among the signals and the greasy rails, gave at last a shudder—a long, reluctant sigh of steam—and began to move; and Margaret, settling her black gloves more firmly in her lap, watched the platform slide away with that curious detachment of the bereaved, for whom the world’s ordinary departures have become a kind of insult, a bustle of porters and barrows and waving hands that goes on, and on, indifferent to the one who is no longer there to wave. The glass of the window was cold against her forehead. London, in its slow recession, offered up its backs of houses, its washing hung in sooty yards, its church spires pricking a sky the colour of a shell—all of it slipping backwards, backwards, into the past, as if the whole city were being drawn down some immense corridor of memory; and she thought, with that sudden lurch of the heart which comes when the mind, having held itself rigid, lets go, *Clara is dead; and I am going to her; and she will not know that I have come.* The thought had no edges. It was a weight, rather, a heaviness in the breast, like a stone dropped into still water, and the circles of it widening, widening, until they touched everything—the worn plush of the seat opposite, the brass rack overhead, the little framed print of Whitby Abbey, askew, upon the partition.

She had chosen the corner seat by the window, as one does when one wishes to be alone with a grief that is not yet shaped into words; but at the last moment, when the guard’s whistle had already thinned into silence, the door of the compartment was wrenched open, and a young man swung himself in, and the door banged to, and there he was—breathless, apologetic, a khaki sleeve brushing the dust from his cap—installed in the seat opposite, and the train, gathering speed, carrying them both northward into the gathering afternoon.

He was very young. That was her first thought, and it came with a pang that was almost maternal, for he could not have been more than nineteen, and his face had the unfinished look of a boy who has been thrust too early into a man’s coat, the collar of it standing away from his neck, the belt drawn tight to a waist that was still boyish. His hands, she noticed, were red and rough, as if he had been handling ropes or soil; and there was a smear of something—oil, perhaps—along the ridge of one knuckle. He sat with his knees apart and his cap upon them, turning it round and round, and he looked at the floor, and then at the rack, and then at the window, and then, with a kind of furtive courtesy, at her, and away again, as if he feared to intrude upon her silence and yet could not bear his own.

The fields began. They came in long pale washes of green and grey, hedgerows stitching them together, and here and there a copse of beeches, still bare, their branches fine as ink upon the sky; and the sky itself, which had been a shell, was opening now into a paler, wider light, a light that seemed to lie upon the land rather than to fall from above, as though the afternoon were a substance, a thin and luminous water, in which the farms and the far-off spires were drowned. Margaret watched it, and did not see it. For the fields were the fields of her childhood, and the hedgerows were the hedgerows she and Clara had run beside, on the road to the village, their skirts catching in the brambles, their laughter—Clara’s laughter, which had been like a handful of bells thrown into the air—ringing out across the meadow. *Clara, who could never be still; Clara, who had danced at her own wedding with her hair coming down; Clara, who had written, only last month, in that slanting, hurried hand of hers, “The daffodils are out, and I wish you were here to see them, for they are not the same without you.”* And now the daffodils would be out in the churchyard, and Clara would not see them, and the letter—the last letter—was in Margaret’s bag, folded and refolded until the creases had worn white, as if by handling it she could keep her sister alive.

“Beg pardon, ma’am.”

The voice was so low that at first she thought she had imagined it. But the boy was leaning forward, his cap now motionless in his hands, and his face—his face was flushed, and his eyes, which were very blue and very clear, were fixed upon her with an anxious politeness.

“Beg pardon,” he said again, “but would you mind—that is, would it disturb you if I was to put the window down a little? Only it’s that close in here, and I’ve been travelling since—since—”

He stopped, as if the number of hours were a thing he could not quite bring himself to name.

“Of course,” said Margaret. “Please do.”

He wrestled with the strap, and the window dropped with a rattle, and a gust of cold air, smelling of wet earth and distant smoke, swept into the compartment, lifting the corner of her veil. She put up her hand to still it, and he, seeing the gesture, said quickly, “I’m sorry, ma’am—I’ll put it up—”

“No, no,” she said. “The air is good. It is—it is good to breathe.”

And she smiled at him—a small, mechanical smile, the smile of one who has learned to do the civil thing even when the heart is elsewhere; but he took it gratefully, as a dog takes a word of kindness, and his shoulders, which had been hunched, relaxed a little against the plush.

They were silent again. The train ran through a cutting, and the walls of it, red and raw, streamed past the window like something bleeding; and then they were out again, into the wide, pale, watery afternoon, and a village church appeared and was gone, and a mill, and a line of willows trailing their branches in a stream that was the colour of pewter. The boy looked at it all with a hunger that was almost painful to see—looked and looked, as if he were trying to store it up against some future emptiness; and Margaret, watching him from beneath her veil, felt the first loosening of her own grief, that strange, involuntary turning of the mind toward another’s pain, which is, perhaps, the only true consolation.

“You are going home?” she asked. It was not a question, really; it was an offering.

He turned to her, and his face lit—actually lit, as if a lamp had been kindled behind it. “Yes, ma’am. To my mother. I’ve got—I’ve got seven days.” He said it as one might say a prayer. “Seven days. It don’t seem real, somehow. I keep thinking I’ll wake up in the trench and hear the—the noise.”

He stopped. The word *noise* hung in the air between them, a poor, thin word for what he meant, and she saw him struggle with it, saw the boy’s mind groping for some way to say the unsayable, and failing, and taking refuge in the ordinary.

“It’s quiet here,” he said, at last. “That’s what I noticed first. The quiet. You can hear the—the wheels, and the—the birds, and that’s all. It’s a good quiet.”

“Yes,” said Margaret. “It is a good quiet.”

And she thought: *He has heard things that would crack the world open, and he calls it noise, and he is glad of the wheels and the birds.* And she thought: *Clara would have liked him. Clara would have drawn him out, would have made him laugh, would have—* But she could not go on with that; the stone in her breast had shifted, and the circles were widening again, and she turned her face to the window to hide the trembling of her mouth.

The afternoon deepened. The light upon the fields grew longer and more golden, and the shadows of the hedgerows stretched across the meadows like bars of a cage; and the boy, lulled by the rhythm of the wheels, had fallen into a doze, his head against the corner of the seat, his cap still in his hands. He looked, in sleep, even younger than before—a child, really, with his lips slightly parted and his lashes lying dark upon his cheek. Margaret watched him, and her heart ached with a tenderness that had no object, or rather with an object that was twofold: for it was Clara she mourned, and it was this boy—this stranger, this soldier—whom she wished to shelter, to fold in her arms, to keep from the noise that waited for him at the end of his seven days.

And then, without warning, he spoke. His eyes were still closed; his voice was thick with sleep.

“She’ll have the kettle on,” he said. “She always has the kettle on. And the dog’ll bark, and she’ll say, ‘Is that you, Tom?’—and I’ll say, ‘It’s me, Mother,’ and she’ll—she’ll—”

He stopped. His eyes opened. He looked at Margaret, and for a moment there was in his face a confusion, a shame, as if he had been caught in some secret weakness; and then he smiled, a little ruefully, and said, “I was dreaming. I beg your pardon.”

“You were dreaming of home,” said Margaret. “There is no need to beg my pardon for that.”

He looked at her then—really looked, for the first time, as if he saw her, not as a lady in black, a stranger in a railway carriage, but as a human being, with a history and a grief of her own; and something passed between them, some current of understanding, that neither could have put into words.

“You’ve had bad news, ma’am,” he said, quietly. It was not a question.

“Yes,” said Margaret. “My sister. She died on Tuesday.”

The words were out before she could stop them; and having spoken them, she felt a strange lightness, as if she had set down a burden she had not known she was carrying. The boy said nothing. He did not offer condolences; he did not say, “I am sorry for your trouble,” in the way that people said it, meaning nothing. He simply sat and looked at her with his blue, clear eyes, and his silence was more eloquent than any speech.

“She was my younger sister,” Margaret went on, and her voice was steady now, steady and low, as if she were telling a story that had happened long ago. “She was always—always the lively one. She could never sit still. When we were children, she used to run ahead of me on the road to the village, and I would call after her to wait, and she would turn and laugh—she had a laugh like—like bells, Mr.—”

She stopped. She did not know his name.

“Tom,” he said. “Tom Ellery, ma’am.”

“Tom,” she repeated. “I am Margaret Ashby.”
