The dinner party at the Harlowes’ was of that comfortable, well-appointed kind which promised nothing beyond an agreeable evening, and threatened, therefore, no greater evil than an hour of dullness before the cards were introduced. Miss Celia Ashby, seated between a clergyman of grave deportment and a gentleman whose chief accomplishment was a talent for carving, had resigned herself to the pleasures of observation, and was acquitting herself with the quiet good sense which distinguished her, when her mother, from the opposite end of the table, raised her voice upon a subject that made her daughter’s heart stand still.

“You are very kind, Mrs. Harewood, to enquire after my girls,” said Mrs. Ashby, with that complacent smile which precedes a communication of importance. “Celia, indeed, is the occasion of some bustle in our household at present. We expect to be very gay before the summer is out. There is to be a wedding, I believe, before Michaelmas.”

A general murmur of congratulation arose. Forks were suspended; every countenance turned, with the lively curiosity that a wedding in a country neighbourhood is sure to inspire, first upon Mrs. Ashby, then upon her daughter. Celia felt the blood mount to her cheeks, and, what was infinitely worse, felt it mount with the consciousness of guilt, though of what guilt she could not have told. She had no engagement; she had received no offer; she had never, in the whole course of her acquaintance with Mr. Ellingham, been given the smallest right to suppose that he intended one.

“And who is the happy gentleman?” cried Mrs. Harewood, whose delight in a match was equalled only by her impatience to have it named.

“Oh, I shall say nothing more,” returned Mrs. Ashby, with the arch look of a mother who believes herself to be delightfully mysterious. “Young people do not like to be forestalled. But I think I may venture to say that the gentleman is among us.”

Every eye now sought the company of Mr. Ellingham, who sat at Mrs. Harewood’s right hand, and who, at this moment, exhibited a countenance of such rigid astonishment, such profound and speechless dismay, as must have undeceived the most sanguine mother in England. He coloured; he opened his lips and closed them again; he looked at Celia with an expression in which entreaty and reproach were strangely blended; and then, finding the eyes of the whole table upon him, he took refuge in his wine.

Celia would gladly have sunk beneath the mahogany. The situation was exquisitely painful. Her mother, she knew, was not a woman to be contradicted with impunity; she had a fondness for managing her daughters’ affairs, and a happy confidence in her own penetration, which made her see engagements where others saw only civility, and hear declarations in every speech that contained a compliment. To contradict her before Mrs. Harewood and the clergyman and the gentleman who carved, would be to expose her to a humiliation from which her pride would never recover; to sit silent was to confirm a falsehood, and to fix upon Mr. Ellingham, before the eyes of his neighbours, an obligation he had never incurred.

She was a young woman of too much sense to be long without a resource. “Mamma,” said she, with a composure that did her infinite credit, “you speak, I am sure, of my sister Lucy’s prospects, which are indeed the occasion of much agreeable speculation in our family. You know I have always said that Lucy’s partiality for the army was likely to end in a red coat before the year was out.” And, turning to the clergyman with an air of the most natural ease, she enquired whether he did not think the militia had been uncommonly gay that winter.

The clergyman, who had no interest in the matter beyond a general love of gossip, was perfectly willing to discuss the militia. Mrs. Harewood, though disappointed, was too well-bred to press a subject that had been so dexterously turned aside; and the conversation, after a moment’s pause, flowed into its accustomed channels. But Celia, though she had saved herself, was not at ease. She had spoken of Lucy’s prospects, and Lucy was at that moment in Devonshire, and had never in her life exchanged two words with an officer. The falsehood, though a small one, and spoken in self-defence, lay heavy on her conscience; and she was moreover perfectly aware that Mr. Ellingham was looking at her with an expression she could not interpret, and that Mrs. Harewood was watching them both with an attention that boded no good.

When the ladies withdrew, Celia sought the shelter of a window-seat, and endeavoured to compose her spirits. She had not been long alone before Mr. Ellingham entered the room. He came towards her with an air of grave embarrassment, and, after a pause of some moments, said, in a low voice, “Miss Ashby, I fear I have been the occasion of some uneasiness to you. I cannot imagine how your good mother should have formed the idea she was pleased to express. I have never, I assure you, given her the smallest reason to suppose——”

“Pray, sir,” interrupted Celia, “say no more. The mistake was my mother’s, and I am only concerned that you should have been made uneasy by it. She is apt to be sanguine; she sees what she wishes to see. I am sorry you should have been exposed to so awkward a moment.”

“You are very good,” said he, “very good indeed. But I am not sure that I understand. Your mother spoke of a wedding before Michaelmas. If it is not to be mine, whose is it to be? I had thought—I had hoped—but I see I have been premature. Forgive me, Miss Ashby. I will not intrude upon you further.”

He bowed, and was about to withdraw. Celia, who had listened to this speech with a beating heart, and who now perceived, with a mixture of vexation and relief, that her mother’s officiousness had done what her own modesty never would have done, called him back. “Mr. Ellingham,” said she, “you must not go away with a wrong impression. My mother’s zeal outran her knowledge; but I would not have you think that I—that is, that my friends—that anyone who wishes me well would be—would be sorry——” She stopped, confused, and then, with a smile that was half reproach and half encouragement, she added, “You see, sir, I am no better at explaining myself than mamma.”

Mr. Ellingham, who was not a dull man, understood her. He came back; he sat down beside her; he said a great deal in a very few words; and when the gentlemen joined them an hour later, it was observed by Mrs. Harewood that Miss Ashby and Mr. Ellingham were conversing together in the window-seat with an air of great particularity. Mrs. Ashby, from the other side of the room, beheld the pair with a countenance of triumphant satisfaction, and remarked to Lady Mordaunt that she had always known how it would be. Celia, catching her mother’s eye, could not forbear a smile; but whether it was at her mother’s vanity or at her own happy escape from the consequences of it, she did not think it necessary to explain.
