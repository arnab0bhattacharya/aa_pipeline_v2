The house at Harrowgate had always possessed a certain dignity that was difficult to shake, largely because it was built of stone that had no intention of crumbling and occupied a site that commanded a view of the valley which, while not spectacular, was sufficiently broad to discourage the notion of petty confinement. Yet, in the autumn of 1815, that dignity was threatened not by the elements, but by the ledger of Sir Julian Thorne. His only son, Arthur, had returned from Oxford with a complexion improved by the air of the university and a character improved by the absence of supervision, but with a set of accounts that would have turned the face of a more prudent father the color of a brick. The debt was not merely a sum of money; it was a moral indictment, a testament to the failure of paternal vigilance and the success of youthful indulgence.

Sir Julian’s method of addressing this catastrophe was direct, if not entirely without a tinge of the theatrical. He proposed the sale of the Oakwood, the finest timber on the estate, a tract of trees that had stood for a century and were valued not only for their worth in the market but for their role in the picturesque symmetry of the grounds. "It is a sacrifice," he declared to his wife, Mrs. Thorne, as they sat in the drawing-room, the fire low and the room dim, "but it is a noble one. We shall sell the timber, pay the debts, and preserve the rest of the estate intact. It is a loss, certainly, but a loss that secures our honor."

Mrs. Thorne, who was a woman of considerable tact and a mind that preferred to solve problems by adding people to the equation rather than subtracting assets, looked up from her embroidery with an expression of polite skepticism. She did not agree with the sale of the Oakwood. To her, the trees were not merely financial instruments; they were the backdrop to every conversation, the shadow under which every young lady had once walked. To cut them down was to admit that the Thorne family had been reduced to a state of desperation.

"And what of the heir?" she asked, her voice light, as if the question were a simple matter of etiquette rather than a crisis of inheritance. "Arthur is of age. He has the benefit of a considerable fortune, if one looks at it in the long view. Surely, there is no need to resort to such drastic measures if we can secure a wife of sufficient consequence. A match with Miss Elphinstone, for instance, or perhaps the daughter of Lord Cavendish, would bring in a settlement that would make the Oakwood seem a triviality."

Sir Julian frowned. "You speak of marriage as if it were a financial transaction, Harriet. Arthur is my son. He has his own sentiments."

"Sentiments," Mrs. Thorne repeated, with a smile that did not reach her eyes, "are a luxury we can ill afford. Sentiments do not pay for rent. Nor do they settle debts. If Arthur is to remain the heir, he must be secured. And the best way to secure a man is to tie him to a woman with a purse."

Arthur, meanwhile, was not in the drawing-room. Nor was he in the library, where he might have been expected to sit and contemplate the severity of his father’s censure. He was in the field, with his gun over his shoulder, walking the line of the river. The air was crisp, and the geese were flying south in long, loose lines, their cries echoing off the water. Arthur felt a profound sense of relief. The woods were silent, save for the rustle of leaves and the occasional snap of a twig. There were no ledgers here, no demands, no expectations. There was only the rhythm of his own footsteps and the quiet satisfaction of being alone.

He had heard the conversation, or at least the gist of it, for the servants were quick to repeat the words of their masters, and the butler had been in the hall when Arthur had been summoned. He had heard the word "timber," and the word "heiress," and he had felt a strange mixture of irritation and amusement. His father wanted to sell the trees. His mother wanted to sell him. He wanted to go shooting. It was a simple equation, and he was the variable that both parents sought to manipulate.

He stopped by a large sycamore, leaning against the rough bark, and watched a hare dash across the open ground. It was a beautiful creature, quick and sure, moving with a purpose that he envied. He had no purpose. He had no plan. He had only the day, and the gun, and the freedom to be idle without being called lazy.

When he returned to the house, the sun had set, and the candles were lit in the windows. The house looked warm and inviting, but Arthur knew that within those walls, there was a tension that was as palpable as the smoke in the air. He entered the hall and saw his father standing by the fireplace, his back to him. Sir Julian was looking at the fire, his face hidden in shadow.

"You are back," Sir Julian said, without turning.

"Yes, father."

"I see you have been in the woods."

"I have."

"Did you find what you were looking for?"

Arthur considered the question. He had been looking for peace, for a moment where he was not a debt, a son, or an heir. He had found it, briefly, in the silence of the trees. But now, he was back in the house, and the silence was broken by the presence of his father.

"I found the air fresh," Arthur said, keeping his voice neutral.

Sir Julian turned then, and his face was stern. "The air is fresh, but the situation is not. I have spoken to your mother. We have a plan. The timber will be sold. It is the only way."

Arthur felt a flush of anger rise in his chest. It was not the anger of a man who has been denied a pleasure, but the anger of a man who has been treated as a piece of property to be managed. "And I?" he asked.

"You will marry," said Mrs. Thorne, who had entered the room. She was smiling, but it was a tight, controlled smile. "We have identified a suitable candidate. Miss Elphinstone. Her family is wealthy, and her character is said to be excellent. It is a match that would do credit to the Thorne name."

Arthur looked at his mother, then at his father. They stood there, united in their purpose, their faces set in a grim determination. He realized then that he was not a person to them, but a problem to be solved. They had tried to solve it with trees, and now they were trying to solve it with a woman.

"I will not marry Miss Elphinstone," Arthur said. His voice was quiet, but it carried a weight that silenced the room.

Mrs. Thorne’s smile faltered. "Arthur, you must consider—"

"I have considered," Arthur interrupted. "I have considered the state of the house, the state of the family, and the state of my own mind. I will not marry to pay a debt. Nor will I let you sell the Oakwood to pay for my idleness. If you wish to sell the timber, sell it. If you wish to find a wife, find one. But do not ask me to be the instrument of your financial salvation."

He turned and walked out of the room, leaving his parents standing in the silence. The fire crackled, and the shadows lengthened on the walls. Sir Julian looked at his wife, and for the first time in their marriage, he saw in her eyes not just the calculation of a bargain, but the fear of a man who has lost control of his own house.

"He is gone," Mrs. Thorne said softly.

"Yes," said Sir Julian. "He is gone. And we are left with our debts, and our timber, and our pride. I suppose we must decide which of these we can afford to lose."

Outside, Arthur walked into the night. The air was cold, and the stars were bright. He did not know where he was going, or what he would do tomorrow. But for the first time in days, he felt that he was alive. He was no longer a son, or an heir, or a debtor. He was only a man, walking alone in the dark, with the freedom to choose his own path. And that, he thought, was worth more than any timber, and more than any wife.
