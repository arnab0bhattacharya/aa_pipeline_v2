The pine logs, stacked with a Swiss exactness against the stone wall, smelled of resin and a dark, damp earth, and the firelight, throwing long, shivering shadows across the rafters, caught the glass beads at her throat. She was standing by the window of the inn, looking out at the mist, and she had lifted her hand to push the heavy curtain aside; and in that gesture, so slight, so entirely unselfconscious, Stephen felt the whole world tip and reassemble itself into a shape that was entirely hers. He was standing by the hearth, a mug of hot cider growing cold in his grasp, and he thought how extraordinary it was that one could live for twenty-four years in a state of suspended animation, crossing oceans and reading books and walking through alpine meadows under the delusion that one was alive, only to be struck into being by the mere angle of a woman’s wrist against a faded red curtain. Her name was Theresa. He had heard the innkeeper’s wife call her that, earlier, when she had come down the stairs with her hair still damp from the rain, smelling of pine and wet wool; Theresa, who had smiled at the old woman, a quick, flashing smile that seemed to Stephen to contain all the warmth of the valley, and had taken the bread from her with both hands, as if it were a gift of immense importance.

And there she was, still looking out at the rain, which was falling now in a steady, grey sheet over the village of Grins, blurring the massive shoulders of the mountains so that they seemed not to be made of rock at all but of cloud, of mere vapor, of the sighs of the earth. He watched the reflection of the fire play across her cheekbone, turning her skin to a substance that was neither flesh nor marble but something in between, and he thought, if I were to speak to her, if I were to cross the three feet of worn wooden floor that separate us, what would I say? He had a vision of himself approaching, of his lips parting to form some triviality about the weather, the pass, the state of the paths, and the words turning to ash in his mouth, because how could one speak of the weather when what one was really seeing was the entire arc of a future life together, the two of them walking on a shingle beach somewhere, or sitting by another fire, older, the firelight caught in her hair which would have faded to a softer brown? He did not move. He took a sip of the cider, which had grown entirely cold, and tasted only the sharpness of the apples, which was the taste of his own timidity.

Julian, sitting in the deep alcove with a book open on his knee that he had not been reading for half an hour, saw all of this. He saw Stephen’s back, the rigid set of his shoulders, the way his head was tilted, and he knew, with a precision that was almost surgical, exactly what Stephen was feeling, because he felt it too, but for Stephen. It was like watching a man drown in a shallow stream, standing on the bank with a rope in one’s hand and finding that one’s arm would not lift. Julian looked down at the page of his book. The words ran together. He read a sentence about the governance of the Tyrol, something about the Emperor Maximilian, and the words were entirely meaningless, mere black marks on a page, because his whole consciousness was fixed on the back of Stephen’s neck, on the way the lamplight fell on the fair hair above his collar, and he was thinking of that afternoon on the mountain path, when Stephen had slipped on a wet stone and Julian had caught his arm, and for a moment, a single moment, the whole weight of Stephen’s body had leaned against his own, and he had felt the warmth of him through the rough serge of their jackets, and it had been as if a door had opened onto a vast, sunlit plain, a plain that was immediately shut again when Stephen laughed and regained his footing, saying something about clumsy boots.

Now Stephen was watching the woman, and the woman was watching the rain, and no one was watching Julian, which was as it should be, he thought, shutting the book with a soft snap; for who watches the watcher? He was the frame, not the picture; the glass, not the wine. And yet, he thought, turning his head to look at the fire, which was collapsing in on itself in a shower of orange sparks, was that not a kind of freedom, to be the one who sees, who records, who feels the pulse of the moment without being subject to its terrible demands? He did not know. He knew only that the sight of Stephen’s face, illuminated by that helpless, hungry wonder, made him feel as if he were being slowly, methodically, hollowed out, like a gourd left in a frost. And the woman—Theresa—she was oblivious. She turned from the window at last, letting the curtain fall, and crossed the room to the table where the innkeeper’s wife had left a bowl of late cherries, and she took one and put it between her lips, tasting the tart skin, the dark juice, and she was thinking, perhaps, of nothing at all, or perhaps of the cow she had heard lowing in the byre as she came in, or of the letter she must write to her aunt in Innsbruck. She did not see Stephen. She did not see the way his hand, holding the mug, trembled slightly, spilling a drop of cider onto the boards. She was entirely sufficient to herself, standing there in the lamplight, a creature of flesh and habit and small, unremarkable thoughts, and it was this, Stephen realized, this utter self-containment, that made her so beautiful; that she did not need him, that she would never need him, that her life would continue to unspool with or without him, a thread of a color he could not name woven into the vast, indifferent tapestry of the mountains.

The rain intensified, drumming against the leaded panes, and the fire settled with a groan of settling logs, sending up a flare that illuminated all of them at once—the woman by the cherries, the man by the hearth, the other man in the shadows—and for a moment they were held there, suspended in the amber light, three separate solitudes, three unmoving figures in a painting no one would ever see. Then Theresa picked up another cherry and turned away, towards the stairs, her footsteps light on the worn wood, and she was gone. The room seemed to contract, to grow colder, to be merely a room again, with its smell of damp and smoke, its scarred tables, its faded curtains. Stephen set down his mug. He said, without turning, his voice thin and strange in the quiet room, “She has gone up.” It was a statement that required no answer, a mere marking of time, of an absence, and Julian, looking at the fire, said, “Yes,” and the word hung in the air between them, small and heavy and unanswerable as a stone dropped into a deep, dark pool, sinking down and down through the black water, turning over and over, while the rain beat on the roof and the mountains stood outside in the night, immense and uncaring, their white peaks lost in the cloud, their roots sunk deep in the ancient, indifferent rock.
