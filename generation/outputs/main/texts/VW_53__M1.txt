For fifty years, she had kept the key to the garden door in the pocket of every dress she had ever worn, and now, in the heat and the noise, she felt for it again, a little piece of cold iron against her thigh. The noise was a great wave that broke and broke again against the walls of the drawing-room, a wave of voices—her son’s, which was deep and certain and talked of the new motor-cars; her daughter’s, which was bright and thin as a new spoon and talked of the suffragettes and the march through London; the laughter of the grandchildren, which was a spray of sound, catching the light. And above it all, his voice, her husband Edward’s, a comfortable rumble, the sound of a fire that has been burning so long one forgets it is there. He was telling the story of the house again, the story of how he had won it at auction, his white hair a nimbus in the late afternoon sun that slanted through the tall windows, catching the dust motes in a slow, golden dance. Fifty years. The words were a drumbeat under the chatter. Fifty years, fifty years. She smiled, a smile that felt stretched and thin over her face, and her eyes, moving over the familiar, dear, crowded scene, saw not her family but a series of surfaces: the gleam of the silver teapot, the soft fur of her granddaughter’s collar, the way the light fell through the glass and made a long, pale rectangle on the Persian rug. It was all a kind of beautiful, suffocating pattern, and she felt a sudden, sharp need for air, for the unpatterned dark.

She moved towards the door, not with any great decision, but as a leaf is moved by a current, and no one seemed to notice. They were all caught in the web of Edward’s story, their faces turned towards him. She slipped through the hall, where the grandfather clock ticked with immense, hollow patience, and let herself out into the garden. The air was cool and smelled of damp earth and the last of the roses, a scent that was both sweet and faintly decaying. The party was a bright, loud box behind her, and she was suddenly, blissfully alone. The sky above the trees was a deep, velvety blue, and the first stars were pricking through, not brilliant, but shy, as if unsure of their welcome. She walked to the old stone bench beneath the copper beech, its leaves a dark, rustling canopy overhead, and sat down, the stone cold through the thin silk of her dress. The dampness of the garden seeped into her, a different kind of reality, quiet and enduring.

She had not thought of him for years. Not really. Not as a presence. He was a name in a drawer, a ghost in an old photograph, his face turned away from the camera. But now, in the quiet, he came to her, not as a memory of what had been, but as a possibility, a life that had run parallel to this one, shimmering just beyond a veil. Leo. The name itself was a small, sharp stone in her mind. He had not had Edward’s steady gaze; his eyes had been restless, full of a light that was always looking towards the next thing, the next idea. He would not have stood in the centre of a room telling a story about a house. He would have been at the edge, by the window, watching the light change on the leaves, or he would have taken her hand and pulled her out of the room entirely, saying, ‘Come, Margaret, the moon is up.’ But the moon was not up tonight. It was a half-moon, like a sliver of fingernail, and it cast a faint, watery light that made the familiar shapes of the garden strange and new.

What would it have been like? Not better, she told herself, the word a small, hard defence. Just different. A different set of rooms, a different set of silences. There would have been no long, quiet evenings with Edward’s newspaper rustling by the fire, a sound so constant it had become a kind of silence itself. There would have been no children with Edward’s serious brow and her own quick temper. There would have been other children, perhaps, with Leo’s restless eyes, or perhaps none at all. They might have lived in a small, cluttered flat in Chelsea, with books piled on the floor and the sound of a violin from upstairs. They might have been poor. They might have been unhappy. But they would have been… awake. Yes, that was the word. There had been a kind of wakefulness in his presence, a feeling that every moment was a threshold. With Edward, one knew the shape of the day, the month, the year. It was a comfort, a great and solid comfort. But it was also a kind of sleep. A long, untroubled sleep. Was it a sleep she had chosen? Or had she simply drifted into it, borne on the current of a love that was real and true and steady?

A sudden burst of laughter from the house, louder this time, and her son’s voice calling, ‘Mother! Where have you got to?’ She did not move. The sound was from another world, a world of certainties. She looked down at her hands, folded in her lap. They were her mother’s hands now, the skin thin as paper, the veins a faint blue map. These hands had held her children, had stitched Edward’s shirts, had held the hand of the man who lay dying in the room above. They were hands that had done the work of a life. Leo’s hands had been different—long-fingered, stained with ink or charcoal, always gesturing, always reaching for something that was not there. Had she been afraid of those hands? Afraid of the reach? She thought of a single afternoon, years and years ago, by the Serpentine. The sun had been hot on the water, and he had been talking about going to Italy, about seeing the frescoes, about the colour of the light. ‘It is not like our light, Margaret,’ he had said, his voice full of a desperate, beautiful longing. ‘It is a light that makes things real.’ And she had looked at him, and she had felt a terror so profound it had made her silent. His light was too bright. It would burn everything away. Edward’s light was a steady, domestic glow, the light of a lamp by a bedside. It kept the darkness at bay. It was enough. It had to be enough.

A moth, large and soft as grey velvet, fluttered against her cheek and then was gone, a frantic, silent thing in the twilight. She watched it disappear into the dark mass of the beech tree. It was gone. It had never been. The life with Leo was a ghost story she told herself in the dark, a story with no ending because it had never had a beginning. This was the beginning. And the middle. And the end. This house, these trees, this cool stone beneath her. She took a deep breath, and the air filled her lungs, the smell of the damp earth and the dying roses, the smell of the world as it was. The key in her pocket was just a key. She stood up, smoothing the silk of her dress, and felt the stiffness in her joints. She walked back towards the house, towards the rectangle of golden light that spilled from the drawing-room window, and the sound of her family, which was the sound of her life, calling her home.
