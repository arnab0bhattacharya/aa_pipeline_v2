The house was called Fairhaven, a name that had always struck Clara as an optimistic overstatement. It was a squat, grey-bricked thing perched on the low cliff above the Norfolk beach, its windows perpetually filmed with salt spray. Every August, for the last six years, she had come here with her husband, Thomas, and their three children. It was, Thomas said, good for the constitution. The sea air, the brisk walks, the simplicity of it all. Clara suspected he simply liked the excuse to smoke his pipe on the veranda without her complaining about the smell in the drawing room.

This year, her sister Edith had come with them. It was the first time. Edith, three years Clara’s junior, was unmarried. In the hushed, pitying tones of their mother, she was “not yet settled.” She had a small flat in Bloomsbury and worked in a library, a fact that their mother treated as a polite fiction, a way for Edith to pass the time until a suitable man appeared. Clara had invited her out of a sense of duty, a vague hope that the sea air might do for Edith what it had failed to do for her—blow some sense of reality into her head.

The first morning, Clara woke to the sound of gulls and the thin, reedy cry of her youngest, Arthur. The day unspooled in its familiar, tangled fashion. There was the porridge to be made, the stockings to be found, the endless, minor skirmishes between the two older children, Nell and George, to be mediated. Edith appeared at nine, looking impossibly rested in a simple linen dress that Clara would have ruined with jam and sea-sand within the hour.

“You look well,” Clara said, a little breathlessly, as she wrestled Arthur into his smock.

Edith smiled. “I slept like a stone. The air is so quiet here.”

Quiet was not a word Clara would have used. To her, the house was a cacophony of demands. But she merely nodded and handed Edith a bowl of porridge. “Perhaps you could take them down to the shore this morning? I have a mountain of linen to see to.”

And so, a pattern was set. Each morning, Edith would take the children to the beach. From the veranda, Clara would watch them go, a small, neat figure in her linen dress, trailed by two skipping children and one who toddled, a bright red bucket clutched in his fist. She would watch Edith sit on the sand, her knees drawn up, a book open on her lap, while the children scrabbled for shells and shrieked at the waves. She never seemed to mind the sand in her hair, or the way Arthur would inevitably tip the contents of his bucket down her back.

Clara, watching from above, felt a strange and unsettling pang. It was not jealousy, not exactly. It was more a kind of bafflement. Edith’s life was, by any measure their mother could conceive, a failure. She had no husband, no home of her own, no children. She earned a modest wage and lived in a set of rooms that smelled of dust and old paper. Yet, as Clara scrubbed porridge from a pot, she saw Edith throw her head back and laugh at something Nell had said, a full, unconstrained sound that carried on the wind. Clara could not remember the last time she had laughed like that.

One afternoon, Thomas took the older children to see the fishing boats, leaving Clara with a sleeping Arthur and Edith, who was sketching on the veranda. Clara sat down opposite her, the exhaustion of the day already settling deep in her bones. The silence between them was comfortable, punctuated by the distant shush of the waves.

“You seem to be enjoying yourself,” Clara said, a touch of something sharp in her voice she hadn't intended.

Edith looked up from her drawing. “I am. It’s a lovely change. Your children are wonderful, Clara. Nell is so curious, and George has such a serious little face.”

“They are a great deal of work,” Clara replied, the words coming out flatter than she’d hoped. She looked at Edith’s sketchbook, at the delicate, confident lines that captured the swoop of a gull. “It must be nice,” she continued, “to have the time to do that. To just… sit.”

“It is,” Edith said simply, and there was no pity in her eyes, only a calm acceptance that Clara found infuriating. She wanted to shake her, to tell her that her calm was an illusion, that life was about duty and sacrifice and the endless, grinding reality of other people’s needs.

That evening, after the children were finally asleep, Clara found herself alone in the kitchen, washing the last of the teacups. She felt a sudden, overwhelming urge to weep. Thomas was on the veranda with his pipe, and Edith was in the sitting room, reading. The house was quiet, but it was the quiet of held breath, of things unsaid. She looked at her own reflection in the dark windowpane. The lines around her eyes, the slight downturn of her mouth. She thought of Edith’s flat in Bloomsbury, of the freedom to read until midnight, to eat nothing but bread and cheese for supper if she chose, to spend a whole day sketching without a single interruption.

The realisation was not a lightning strike, but a slow, cold seep. She did not envy Edith her small rooms, her modest salary, her lack of a husband. She did not envy her the pitying looks from their mother or the awkwardness at parties. But she envied her the silence. The profound, uninterrupted silence of a life that belonged only to oneself. She envied the space to be bored, to be lonely, to be anything at all without it being a reflection on a husband or a child. She envied the simple, radical freedom of not being indispensable.

A week later, on their last morning, Edith was packing her small case. Clara came into the room, holding a small, wrapped parcel. “For the train,” she said. “Some sandwiches.”

Edith took it, her fingers brushing Clara’s. “Thank you. It’s been a lovely week. Truly.”

Clara watched her sister, so neat and self-contained. She thought of the week ahead, of Thomas’s return to the city, of the school term starting, of the relentless, loving, suffocating tide of her life. For a moment, she felt a surge of something so powerful it almost choked her. It was not resentment, nor was it pity. It was a raw, unvarnished envy, sharp as a new pin. She envied Edith her departure. She envied the train journey back to her own quiet rooms, her own life, her own self.

“It has been,” Clara said, her voice tight. She wanted to say something more, to bridge the chasm that had opened between them, but the words would not come. She could only stand there, her hands full of nothing, and watch her sister, who had nothing she wanted, prepare to leave a place that had given her everything.
