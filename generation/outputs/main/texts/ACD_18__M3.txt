I had remarked to my companion, Mr. Martin Hewitt, that the November fog seemed to bring out the very worst in human nature, as we sat in his comfortable chambers in the Inner Temple that evening. He smiled, relighting his pipe.

"It is not the fog, Brett," he replied. "It merely provides a convenient cloak for those already inclined to do evil."

No sooner had he spoken than our landlady announced a visitor. Miss Eunice Vance was shown into the room, a young woman of respectable bearing, though her eyes were red with recent weeping. She wore a plain grey serge skirt and jacket, and her gloved hands trembled slightly as she sat down.

"You are Mr. Hewitt, the detective?" she asked. "My brother, Thomas Vance, is dead. The police say his heart failed him. I say he was murdered."

Hewitt regarded her with quiet attention. "Pray tell me the facts, Miss Vance, from the beginning."

She composed herself with an effort. "Thomas was valet to Count Reval, of the Austro-Hungarian Embassy. He had held the position for four years, and was devoted to his master. Three days ago he was found dead in a hansom cab in Belgrave Square. The cabman said Thomas had hailed him in Pall Mall, given an address in Pimlico, and then fallen silent. When the cab reached its destination, Thomas was slumped against the side, quite dead. The police surgeon found no mark of violence and pronounced it heart failure."

"And what makes you suspect otherwise?"

"Two things," she said. "First, Thomas wrote to me the morning before he died. He was not a literary man, but the letter was strange. He said: 'I have seen something I was not meant to see, and I fear I am watched. If anything happens to me, remember the blue dispatch box.' Second, within a day of his death, Count Reval caused a quantity of papers to be burned in the embassy yard. I have a friend in the household who told me this."

Hewitt nodded. "And what do you know of this blue dispatch box?"

"Nothing. Thomas mentioned it only in that letter."

"And your brother's health?"

"Excellent. He was thirty-two, active, and had never complained of his heart."

Miss Vance produced the letter, which Hewitt examined carefully before handing it to me. The handwriting was cramped but steady, and the words were precisely as she had quoted.

"I will take the case," said Hewitt. "Leave me your address, Miss Vance, and say nothing of my involvement to anyone."

After she had gone, Hewitt sat for some time in thought. "There is a great deal here, Brett," he said at last. "The Count is either a guilty man or a frightened one. Both are useful to us."

The following morning we called at the Austro-Hungarian Embassy in Belgrave Square. It was a fine white-stuccoed building, and the hall porter, an imperious Hungarian, attempted to deny us access. Hewitt, however, was not easily turned aside. He presented his card to the Count's secretary, and after a delay of some minutes, we were shown into a small, elegantly furnished office.

Count Reval was a man of perhaps fifty, tall and grey-bearded, with the easy assurance of the career diplomat. He received us with a thin smile.

"I am afraid, Mr. Hewitt, that I can tell you nothing about the unfortunate affair. Vance was an excellent servant. His death was a shock, but the doctor's verdict was clear."

"Yet you burned papers the following day," said Hewitt mildly.

The Count's smile faded. "That was routine. Diplomatic correspondence is regularly destroyed. I fail to see the connection."

"Of course," said Hewitt. "Tell me, Count Reval, did your valet have occasion to handle your dispatches?"

"Certainly not. He laid out my clothes and attended to my personal effects. He had no access to official materials."

Hewitt glanced about the room. "You have a fine example of the Kremnietz school there," he said, nodding toward a small landscape painting on the wall. "I observed another in the hall. The varnish is distinctive."

The Count looked faintly surprised. "You are well informed, Mr. Hewitt."

"Not at all. But I make a point of noticing varnishes." He paused. "One further question. On the night before Vance's death, did you receive visitors?"

The Count's composure held, but I observed that his right hand, resting on the desk, tightened almost imperceptibly. "I received no visitors of any consequence."

We took our leave. Outside, Hewitt walked briskly to the corner of the square and stopped.

"The Count is lying, Brett. He received someone that night, and Vance saw that someone. The blue dispatch box is the key. I noticed that the Count's desk bore a faint ring-mark consistent with a box of approximately fourteen by ten inches, recently removed. The varnish on that painting in the hall was fresh, and I could smell turpentine. Something has been concealed behind it, or was until very recently."

We spent the remainder of the day making inquiries. Hewitt visited the cab-stand in Pall Mall, where he found the cabman who had driven Vance. The man remembered his fare clearly.

"He was nervous, gentlemen," said the cabman. "Kept looking back over his shoulder, he did. And before he got in, he brushed down his coat, like he was shaking something off. I thought perhaps he'd been in a scuffle."

Hewitt asked whether anyone else had been near the cab. The cabman thought for a moment. "There was a gentleman in a heavy ulster. Walked past just as my fare got in. Foreign-looking, I'd say, from his beard."

"Did this gentleman speak to your fare?"

"Not as I saw. But he looked hard at him."

That evening, Hewitt called upon Dr. Pemberton, the police surgeon who had examined the body. Pemberton was a blunt, cheerful man.

"Heart failure, beyond question," he said. "The organ was in a peculiar state of degeneration, though the man was young."

"Could such degeneration have been induced?" asked Hewitt.

Pemberton hesitated. "There are substances that can produce cardiac distress over time. But I found no trace of poisoning."

"Did you examine the clothing?"

"Routinely. There was dust on the coat, nothing remarkable."

Hewitt produced a small phial. "I took the liberty of collecting a sample of that dust from the cab itself. May I ask you to have it analysed?"

Pemberton agreed, and two days later the results confirmed what Hewitt had suspected. The dust contained traces of a rare alkaloid, employed in certain Continental apothecaries, which could be absorbed through the skin over a period of weeks, slowly weakening the cardiac muscle until any sudden exertion or alarm might prove fatal.

"He was poisoned systematically," said Hewitt, laying out the evidence before me. "The Count, or whoever acted for him, administered the compound through Vance's clothing—perhaps through his valet's livery, which the Count would have had ample opportunity to treat. When Vance saw something he should not have seen—the visitor, the dispatch box—he was already a dead man. A sudden fright completed the work."

"But why not simply dismiss him?"

"Because he knew too much to be trusted at liberty. Dismissal might have led to disclosure. Murder was safer, and a death by heart failure would arouse no suspicion."

Hewitt laid the evidence before the authorities, and the matter was taken up discreetly through diplomatic channels. Count Reval was recalled to Vienna within the week, and the blue dispatch box, recovered from behind the painting where it had been hastily concealed, was found to contain correspondence of a nature that would have caused considerable embarrassment to more than one government. Whether the Count faced formal justice I never learned, but Miss Vance received word some months later that her brother's death had not gone unmarked, and that those responsible had answered for it in their own country.

"It is a poor sort of satisfaction," Hewitt remarked when I commented on the outcome. "But the truth, once uncovered, has a way of finding its own justice."
