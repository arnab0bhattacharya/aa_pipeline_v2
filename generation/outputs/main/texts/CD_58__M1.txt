The town of St. Brévin-les-Écluses had, in the autumn of the year 1793, the appearance of a herring which has been left too long in the sun: it was shrivelled, it was pungent, and it was possessed of an eye that stared with a fixed and glassy apprehension. The very stones of the quay seemed to hold their breath, and the gulls, that were wont to wheel and scream over the harbour-masters’ office with a brazen disregard for human affairs, now flew low and silent, like feathered emissaries of the Republic, bearing news of the least dissension to the Committee of Public Safety.

It was into this atmosphere of sour wine and sourer suspicion that Captain Silas Thrum—a man whose face was as weather-beaten as the hull of his lugger, the *Sea-Serpent*, and whose heart was, by his own frequent admission, as hard as the biscuit he served to his crew—made his way. He was a smuggler of brandy, of lace, of theological pamphlets, and, on occasion, of human souls; but he held that souls were the most cumbersome and least profitable of all contraband, being apt to weep, to pray at inconvenient hours, and to develop a thirst for fresh water that seriously impinged upon the brandy-kegs.

His business, on this particular evening, was with a certain Monsieur de Valois, a gentleman of the old school who had lately discovered that the new school had a very short recess indeed. Thrum found him in a shuttered room above a chandler’s shop, looking for all the world like a portrait of himself that had been left out in the rain. Beside him stood a boy of perhaps five years, silent as a mouse in a trap, and a woman of formidable aspect whom Thrum took to be the child’s nurse.

“Captain Thrum,” said the Marquis de Valois, his voice the rustle of dry leaves, “you are known to be a man of discretion. My wife and I—we are to be guests of the Republic at the Conciergerie. We do not expect to return. This is my son, Étienne. You will take him to England. Here is the gold.”

Thrum took the pouch. It was heavy, which was good; but it was accompanied by the woman, which was bad. She was tall, angular, and possessed of a bosom that seemed to have been designed as a shelf for the display of righteous indignation. Her name, she informed him in a voice like a saw cutting through knotty pine, was Madame Vionnet.

“I go with the child,” she declared, not as a request, but as a geological fact. “He is of a delicate constitution. He requires his tisane. He requires his stories. He requires the hand that has known him since the hour of his birth.”

“He requires to be in England before the tide turns,” growled Thrum. “Madame, my lugger is not a nursery. It smells of fish, bilge, and brandy. It is no place for a lady.”

“Then it is no place for the boy,” she retorted, her eyes, which were the colour of a winter sky, fixing him with a stare that had turned better men than he to jelly. “Unless I am there to see to his comforts.”

Thrum looked at the gold. He looked at the boy, who was staring at a crack in the floorboards as if it held the secrets of the universe. He looked at Madame Vionnet. She stood with her hands clasped before her, a monument to duty and a fortress of starch. There was something about her that Thrum did not like. It was not her severity, for he respected severity; it was the *precision* of it. She was too neat. Her cap was too white. In a town where the very rats looked dishevelled from fear, she was as crisp as a new banknote. And in Thrum’s experience, a crisp banknote in a dirty pocket usually meant trouble.

“Very well,” he said at last, with the air of a man agreeing to carry a lit fuse. “But you will do as I say, and you will not make a sound. Not a whisper, not a sigh, not a prayer that lasts longer than a cat’s sneeze.”

They crept through the sleeping streets, the boy’s hand in the woman’s firm grip. The *Sea-Serpent* lay at the end of a dilapidated pier, rocking gently in the black water. Thrum’s mate, a dwarf of a man named Barnaby Crumpet, who had a face like a withered apple and a soul of pure contraband, was already casting off.

“Sharp’s the word, Captain,” whispered Crumpet. “The patrol boat is late, which means it is early. Or the other way round. It’s never good.”

They bundled the passengers into the tiny, suffocating cabin beneath the deck. It was a space that smelled of old rope and older secrets. The boy sat on a coil of hawser, his eyes wide. Madame Vionnet sat beside him, her back as straight as a belaying pin.

As the lugger slipped out of the harbour, catching the first breath of the offshore wind, Thrum remained on deck, his eyes scanning the horizon. But his mind was in the cabin. He was a man who trusted his nose for brandy and his eye for a revenue cutter, and his senses were now prickling.

It was the silence that unnerved him. A woman of Madame Vionnet’s vocal power, shut in a dark hole with a frightened child, should have been murmuring comfort, reciting the rosary, or at the very least, complaining about the damp. But there was nothing. Absolute, profound silence.

He crept to the hatch and lifted it a crack. The smell of bilge wafted up, and with it, a scent that did not belong. It was not the lavender water of a nurse; it was something sharper. Soap. Hard, chemical soap.

He looked down. In the dim light of the ship’s lantern, he saw Madame Vionnet. She was not comforting the child. She was wiping her hands. She wiped them methodically, finger by finger, with a cloth that she then folded into a perfect square and tucked into her pocket. She looked at the boy, Étienne, not with the soft gaze of a guardian, but with the assessing look of a butcher inspecting a side of beef.

Then she spoke, and her voice was different. It was lower, flatter, stripped of its theatrical indignation.

“The wind is favourable, *Citoyen*,” she said to the darkness.

Thrum froze. There was no one else in the cabin.

“We should make the English coast by dawn,” she continued, addressing the air itself. “The Committee will be pleased with the recovery of the emigrant’s gold. The boy is the proof of the transaction. Once we are ashore, he is to be disposed of. He is a loose thread.”

Thrum felt a coldness in his gut that had nothing to do with the Channel spray. It was not the nurse’s warmth that had compelled her to come; it was the gold. She was not a protector; she was a retriever. She had been sent by the very men who had arrested the parents, sent to ensure that the gold—and the heir—did not escape the Republic’s justice. She was a spy, a wolf in a starched cap.

He looked at the boy. Étienne was watching her. He was not looking at the floorboards now. He was looking at the woman’s hands, and in his small, silent face, Thrum saw a terror that was ancient and knowing. The child knew. He had always known.

Thrum lowered the hatch. He stood for a moment on the rolling deck, the salt wind stinging his face. The *Sea-Serpent* was cutting through the waves, a smuggler of souls, carrying a cargo of gold, a child, and a devil.

“Barnaby,” Thrum called softly to the mate.

“Aye, Captain?”

“Break out the heavy brandy,” Thrum said, his voice hard as iron. “And bring up the spare anchor chain. We have a passenger who requires... airing.”

He looked back at the dark shape of France, receding into the mist. He was a smuggler, a rogue, and a thief. But he was not a murderer. And as the lugger plunged into the darkness of the Channel, Captain Silas Thrum resolved that before the sun rose over the white cliffs of Dover, Madame Vionnet would learn the true meaning of the word ‘disposal’.
