I was in my twenty-third year, and the pride of my studies lay heavy upon me, when I conceived the design that was to prove the undoing of my peace. In the ancient university town of Altenberg, where the spire of the church rises above a labyrinth of crooked streets and the river murmurs through the meadows beyond the walls, I had devoted myself, with an ardour that bordered upon obsession, to the mysteries of chemistry. My preceptors spoke of me with approbation; my fellow students regarded me with a mixture of admiration and envy; and I, poor fool, believed that I stood upon the very threshold of a discovery that should render my name immortal.

My grandfather, old Wilhelm Hartmann, had for seven years dwelt beneath our roof, a living monument to the ravages of time. His mind, once so vigorous and penetrating, had become a house of shadows; he knew neither his son nor his daughter-in-law, nor even me, whom he had dandled upon his knee in infancy. He would sit for hours by the window, gazing upon the garden with eyes that saw nothing, and when we spoke to him he would answer with a courtesy that was more terrible than rudeness, for it was the courtesy of a stranger who knows not whom he addresses. My father bore this affliction with a resignation that I could not comprehend; he would sometimes sit beside the old man and hold his hand in silence, as though the mere contact of flesh could bridge the gulf that separated them. But I, who had been bred upon the audacious speculations of the new philosophy, could not accept so passive a surrender. I resolved to penetrate the veil that had fallen upon my grandsire's spirit, and to restore to him, by the agency of science, the memories that disease had stolen.

For two years I laboured in my little chamber, surrounded by retorts and alembics, by jars of strange salts and phials of volatile essences. I studied the writings of the alchemists, not for their mystical fancies, but for the hints they afforded of the subtle operations of nature; I corresponded with learned men in Leipzig and in Jena; I experimented upon animals, and upon myself, with a temerity that I now shudder to recall. At length, after many failures and some perilous accidents, I compounded a tincture of exceeding potency, a limpid fluid of a pale golden hue, which, when I had tested it upon a dog that had been rendered senseless by a blow, appeared to restore the creature to a consciousness that was almost human in its intelligence. I hesitated long before I dared to administer it to my grandfather; but at last, upon a dreary afternoon in November, when the rain beat against the windows and the wind howled in the chimneys, I resolved to make the trial.

My father was absent upon business in Dresden; my mother had retired to her chamber with a headache; and I was alone with the old man in the library. He sat in his accustomed chair, his hands folded upon his knees, his eyes fixed upon the fire that flickered in the grate. I approached him with a beating heart, and, under the pretext of offering him a cordial, I presented the phial to his lips. He drank mechanically, as he did all things, and for a moment I feared that the potion had lost its virtue. But presently a change came over him; his eyes, which had been dull and vacant, began to kindle with a strange light; he raised his head, and looked about him as one who wakes from a long and troubled dream.

"Where am I?" he cried, in a voice that was no longer the feeble treble of age, but the firm and manly tone that I remembered from my childhood. "What has happened? Why is it dark? Have I been ill?"

"Grandfather," I exclaimed, seizing his hand, "you are at home, in Altenberg, in the house of your son. I am Konrad, your grandson, whom you have not known these seven years."

He gazed at me with an expression of bewilderment that gradually gave place to recognition. "Konrad!" he murmured. "The child that used to play with my watch-chain? Yes, yes, I remember. But why do you weep? Why do you look at me so strangely? Have I been mad?"

I could not answer him for the emotion that choked my utterance. I fell upon my knees beside his chair and wept like a child, while he, with a tenderness that I had never known, laid his hand upon my head and blessed me. For an hour we talked together of a thousand things—of my studies, of his youth, of the changes that had come upon the town; and I listened with a delight that I cannot describe to the recital of adventures that I had heard from his lips when I was a boy, but which had long since faded from his memory. It seemed to me that I had achieved a triumph beyond the dreams of the alchemists themselves; that I had conquered death, and wrested from oblivion the treasures of the past.

But even as I exulted, a shadow crossed his countenance. He had been speaking of his early manhood, of the friends of his youth, of the days when he had been a student at this very university; and suddenly he paused, and a shudder passed through his frame.

"Konrad," he said, in a low and altered voice, "there is something I must tell you—something that has lain upon my conscience these forty years, and that I have never dared to reveal to any living soul. I had thought that I should carry it with me to the grave; but now that my memory has returned, I cannot be silent. It is a crime—a dreadful crime—and you, who have restored me to myself, must hear it."

I entreated him to be calm, to spare himself the pain of so distressing a recital; but he would not be dissuaded. He told me, with many pauses and with a voice that trembled with remorse, how, in the year 1753, when he was a young man of two-and-twenty, he had been concerned in the death of a fellow student, a youth named Ernst Vogel, who had been his rival in love and in scholarship. They had quarrelled, he said, over a trifle—a question of precedence at a festival, a jest that had been misconstrued—and in the heat of passion he had struck the young man with a heavy candlestick. Vogel had fallen, and had never risen. My grandfather, in an agony of terror, had concealed the body in a disused cellar beneath the college, and had fled from the town, giving out that he was called away upon urgent business. The disappearance of Vogel had been attributed to various causes; some said that he had gone to sea, others that he had been murdered by robbers; but no suspicion had ever attached to my grandfather, who had returned after a year, had completed his studies, and had married the woman over whom they had quarrelled. The secret had remained buried in his breast, and the burden of it had, perhaps, contributed to the very malady that had deprived him of his reason.

As I listened to this dreadful confession, a cold horror crept over me. I had sought to restore my grandfather to his family, and I had restored him only to the consciousness of a guilt that should embitter his remaining days. I had unlocked the door of memory, and there had sprung forth a spectre that I had not the power to lay. The fire burned low upon the hearth, and the rain still beat against the windows, and the wind still howled in the chimneys, as though nature herself were in sympathy with the misery that filled my soul. I looked upon the old man's face, furrowed with the lines of age and sorrow, and I knew not whether to curse the science that had wrought this miracle or to bless it for the truth it had revealed.

At length my grandfather rose from his chair, and, taking my hand in his, he said, with a solemnity that I shall never forget: "Konrad, you have given me back my memory, and with it the knowledge of my sin. I do not know whether to thank you or to curse you. But this I know—that I cannot die with this secret in my breast. To-morrow I shall confess all to the magistrate, and whatever punishment the law may inflict, I shall bear it as the expiation of my crime. Do not seek to dissuade me; it is the only atonement I can make."

He spoke with a firmness that I had not expected, and I saw that his resolution was not to be shaken. That night I did not sleep; I sat by my window, watching the rain that ceased not to fall, and listening to the wind that sighed among the eaves. I thought of the young man who had perished so many years ago, and of the family that had mourned him, and of the old man who had carried the burden of his guilt through half a century of silence. And I thought of myself, who had brought this misery to light, and I asked myself whether the knowledge of the truth was worth the anguish it had cost. The morning came, grey and cheerless, and with it came the officers of justice, to whom my grandfather, with a calmness that was more affecting than any tears, made a full confession of his crime. He was conveyed to the prison of the town, where he died within the year, having received the consolations of religion, and having expressed a hope that his penitence might be accepted as a partial atonement for his offence.

As for me, I have never since entered the laboratory where I achieved my fatal triumph. The phials and the retorts stand there still, covered with dust; but I have not the heart to destroy them, for they are the monuments of a discovery that has taught me a lesson I shall never forget—that there are secrets which it is better to leave hidden, and that the memory of man is a sacred thing, which the hand of science should approach with reverence and with fear.
