Mr. Solomon Sniffit was, by profession, a mourner; by temperament, a man of profound and settled gloom. He was what is known in the trade as a mute, and he pursued his sorrowful calling with an assiduity that bordered upon the artistic. His countenance, which Nature had moulded in a moment of deep despondency, was his principal capital. It was a face of long, drooping lines, like a melting waxwork, with a nose that seemed to weep in sympathy with the general lugubriousness of his features, and eyes which, at a moment’s notice, could assume an expression of such unutterable bereavement as to make the chief mourner at a funeral feel himself an amateur in the business of grief. He had a particular way of holding his hat, a rusty, tall, black beaver, against his chest, as if it were a sacred relic containing the last will and testament of his own happiness, which never failed to draw a sympathetic sigh from the assembled populace.

On this particular Tuesday in the month of November, Mr. Sniffit was engaged for the interment of a Mr. Barnaby Flinch, a gentleman of immense and mysterious fortune, whose demise had occasioned a considerable stir in the fashionable parish of Kensington. The firm of Dismal & Sons, Undertakers to the Nobility and Gentry, had been commissioned to conduct the obsequies, and Mr. Sniffit, as one of their most reliable and melancholy assets, was posted at the lychgate of the ancient church of St. Jude’s-in-the-Green. His duty was simple: to stand, to weep silently if the occasion demanded, and to represent the pinnacle of professional sorrow.

As the long, black procession wound its way up the gravel path, a carriage of state drew up, and from it descended a young man in a state of the most violent and unbecoming agitation. He was a Mr. Peregrine Pennyfeather, a distant cousin of the deceased, whose expectations were as vast as his composure was slight. He stumbled over a gravestone, righted himself, and, in his flustered state, seized upon the first figure of authority he saw. This, of course, was Mr. Sniffit, standing in his blackest broadcloth, his face a perfect mask of funereal dignity.

‘Sir!’ gasped Mr. Pennyfeather, his voice a frantic whisper. ‘A terrible day! A day of blackest clouds and darkest sorrows! You are one of the family, I perceive, from your look of such profound and personal loss!’

Now, Mr. Sniffit was a man of few words at the best of times, and at a funeral, he considered words to be an impertinent intrusion upon the sanctity of silence. He therefore merely inclined his head in a manner so full of pathos, so heavy with unspoken grief, that it conveyed not only assent but a whole history of shared suffering and common blood. He had been mistaken for a mutesman, a coachman, and on one memorable occasion, a bailiff; but never before for a relative. The mistake was so grand, so flattering to his professional vanity, that he lacked the moral courage to correct it. To do so would be to shatter the beautiful, sorrowful tableau he had so carefully constructed.

‘I knew it!’ cried Mr. Pennyfeather, clapping him on the shoulder with a force that nearly dislodged the hat from Mr. Sniffit’s chest. ‘I see the family likeness! The Flinch nose! The Flinch melancholy! We are brothers in this woe, sir! Brothers!’

The service was a magnificent affair. The organ groaned, the choir wailed, and the parson delivered a sermon on the vanity of earthly riches that was so moving it made the footmen cry and the silver-plated coffin-handles seem to weep with a dull, metallic sorrow. Mr. Sniffit, playing his part to perfection, stood by the grave with a single, perfectly timed tear tracing a path through the dust on his cheek. It was, he felt, his masterpiece.

As the mourners departed, Mr. Pennyfeather once again seized upon him. ‘You will accompany us to the house, of course!’ he declared. ‘The will is to be read at once. It is the family’s wish, and the solicitor, Mr. Vulture, is a man of punctuality that borders on the ferocious. Come! My carriage is here.’

To be invited to a will-reading was an honour undreamt of in the annals of the Dismal & Sons’ mute-department. It was a glimpse into the very heart of the mystery he was paid to mourn. Terror and curiosity waged a terrible war within the bosom of Mr. Sniffit, and curiosity, being the more garrulous of the two, won the day. He allowed himself to be bundled into a carriage that smelled of lavender and old leather, and was borne away to the late Mr. Flinch’s townhouse in Kensington Square.

The house was a grand, silent tomb of a place, where the clocks had been stopped and the mirrors shrouded in white linen, as if in fear of catching a glimpse of their own mortality. The relatives were assembled in the great drawing-room, a collection of faces as long and as greedy as a row of cormorants. They eyed Mr. Sniffit with suspicion, for they did not know him, but seeing him in the company of the heir-apparent, Mr. Pennyfeather, they presumed he must be some obscure, and therefore unimportant, branch of the family tree.

At the head of a vast mahogany table sat Mr. Vulture, the solicitor. He was a gentleman of such extreme emaciation that he appeared to be constructed entirely of dry parchment and sharp angles. His voice, when he spoke, was like the rustle of dead leaves in a forgotten churchyard.

‘We are assembled,’ he began, adjusting his spectacles, ‘to hear the last will and testament of Barnaby Flinch, Esquire. A man whose fortune was as large as his heart was… complicated.’

A nervous titter went through the room. Mr. Sniffit, finding a chair in a shadowy corner, did his best to become one with the wallpaper, which depicted a scene of weeping willows that suited his mood admirably. He felt a cold sweat on his brow. This was no place for a man of his station. He was an artisan of grief, a hired sorrow-monger, and here he was, surrounded by the genuine article, who were about to divide the spoils of the very death he was pretending to lament.

Mr. Vulture cleared his throat, a sound like a snake shedding its skin. ‘To my cousin, Agatha Flinch, who always reminded me of a vulture in a bonnet…’

‘Sir!’ shrieked a formidable woman in black bombazine.

‘…I leave the sum of one hundred pounds, and my sincere hope that she finds a more agreeable perch,’ continued Mr. Vulture, without a flicker of emotion. ‘To my nephew, Barnaby Flinch the Younger, who has the business acumen of a startled rabbit…’

And so it went. Bequests of small sums, of snuffboxes, of portraits of disagreeable ancestors, were parceled out with a dry, cutting precision that left the recipients speechless with indignation. Mr. Sniffit listened, fascinated. This was a funeral of another sort, a slow and painful execution of hopes and expectations.

Finally, Mr. Vulture came to the bulk of the estate. ‘And to my most distant and unexpected relative,’ he droned, peering over his spectacles at the assembled company, ‘whose face I saw only in my final moments, but whose profound and silent sorrow touched me more deeply than all the clamorous grief of my nearer kin…’

A hush fell over the room. Every eye was fixed on the solicitor. Mr. Sniffit felt his heart, an organ he usually kept in a state of professional suspension, begin to beat a frantic tattoo against his ribs.

‘…to this individual, who stood like a silent monument to my mortality at my own funeral,’ Mr. Vulture concluded, his gaze sweeping the room and, to the universal horror of the Flinch family, landing directly upon the weeping wallpaper in the corner, ‘I leave the entirety of my residual estate. To the mute.’

There was a moment of perfect, crystalline silence. Then, as one, the heads of the Flinch family swivelled, their eyes, wide with disbelief and rage, fixing upon the small, terrified man in the corner who was trying, with all his might, to look like a willow tree. Mr. Solomon Sniffit, the man who was paid to look sad, found that for the first time in his life, his sorrow was entirely, and terrifyingly, his own.
