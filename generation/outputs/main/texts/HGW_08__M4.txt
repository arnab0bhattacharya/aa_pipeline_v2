Dr. Merridew’s establishment in the little market town of Cuckfield consisted of a modest brick house with a surgery attached, a brass plate upon the door, and myself, Albert Finch, as his assistant and dispenser. I was a young man of five-and-twenty, with a fair knowledge of pharmacy and a constitution that had hitherto seemed adequate to the demands of a country practice. Hitherto, I say, for in the autumn of that year—it was the year of the great frost, when the roads were like iron and the hedgerows stood black and naked against the pale sunsets—I found myself increasingly unequal to the work. There were confinements that kept one from one’s bed for two nights together, there were the interminable rounds through miry lanes, and there was Dr. Merridew himself, a man of sixty, whose temper had soured with the years and who regarded any sign of fatigue in his assistant as a personal affront.

It was in the matter of a certain nerve tonic, a preparation of my own devising, that the whole curious business began. I had been experimenting with the alkaloids of the coca leaf, not with any notion of a patent medicine, but in the hope of finding something that might relieve the more distressing symptoms of the gout, with which the squire at Pelling Manor was afflicted. The serum I produced was of a pale amber colour, and when I first injected a small quantity into the vein of my own arm—a practice I had adopted from a paper in the Lancet, and which I do not recommend to the general reader—I experienced a sensation of extraordinary clarity. The fatigue that had been lying upon me like a wet blanket was rolled away as if by magic. My thoughts moved with a precision that was almost mechanical. I could hear the ticking of the clock in the hall with a distinctness that was positively painful, and the pattern of the wallpaper seemed to stand out in relief.

For three days and three nights I worked without sleep. I attended to the squire, I delivered a child in a cottage at Wivelsfield, I dressed the leg of a carter who had been crushed by his own wagon, and I wrote up the books with a neatness that I had never before achieved. Dr. Merridew, who was himself suffering from a bronchial catarrh, was pleased to remark that I seemed to have found my second wind. I did not tell him of the serum. I kept the little phial in a locked drawer in the dispensary, and each morning, before the first patient arrived, I would draw up a measured dose and inject it into my forearm.

The effects were cumulative. By the end of the second week I had ceased to require food in any regular fashion. A biscuit and a glass of water at noon sufficed. My face, I observed in the glass, had taken on a certain gauntness, but my eyes were bright and my hands were steady. The patients, who at first had been merely gratified by my promptness, began to exhibit a curious uneasiness in my presence. Mrs. Pargiter, the wife of the corn merchant, a woman of a voluble and confiding disposition, remarked to me one afternoon that I had a way of looking at her as if I were reading the inside of her head. I laughed at the time, but I confess the observation lingered.

It was the children who first put their finger upon the truth, as children will. The little Hoskins boy, who had been brought to me with a whitlow, sat upon his mother’s knee and stared at me with round eyes throughout the dressing. When I had finished, he whispered something to his mother, who coloured and hurried him out. I heard her say, as she went down the steps, “Hush, Tommy, it’s not polite.” But I never learned what it was he had said.

The change, when I look back upon it, was gradual and yet inexorable. I found that I could no longer sleep, even if I wished to. I would lie upon my bed in the dark, listening to the mice behind the wainscot, and my mind would race through the problems of the practice with a lucidity that was almost painful. I could recall the exact dosage of every prescription I had written for a month past. I could remember the precise tone of voice in which Dr. Merridew had addressed me on the morning of my arrival, three years before. But I could not remember what it felt like to be tired.

The patients began to fall away. It was not a sudden desertion, but a steady thinning, like the leaves of a tree in autumn. The farmers who had once come from miles around now sent their wives to the chemist in Haywards Heath. Mrs. Pargiter, encountering me in the street, crossed to the other side. The squire, whose gout I had cured with a single injection, wrote a letter of thanks that contained a note of something that was almost alarm. “You have a remarkable gift, Finch,” he wrote, “but I confess I find your manner altered. You seem to have outgrown the ordinary weaknesses of our flesh.”

Dr. Merridew summoned me to his study on a bleak afternoon in November. He was seated in his armchair, wrapped in a plaid shawl, and his face was grey with the effort of the interview. “Finch,” he said, “I have had complaints. Not of your work—your work is beyond reproach. But there is something about you that disturbs the patients. Mrs. Hoskins tells me that her boy cried for an hour after you dressed his finger. She says you looked at him as if he were a specimen under a microscope.”

I attempted to explain that I had been experimenting with a tonic that had greatly improved my efficiency. He listened in silence, his old eyes fixed upon my face with a scrutiny that I found disconcerting. “You have not slept,” he said at last. “Not for weeks, I should judge. Your eyes are like glass. Your skin is dry. You move too quickly. I have seen men in this condition before, Finch, in the hospitals. They were not always to be trusted.”

That night I sat alone in the dispensary, the phial of amber serum before me on the table. The lamp cast a yellow circle upon the wood, and beyond it the shadows seemed to press in with a curious intimacy. I thought of the patients who had fled, of the child who had cried, of the old doctor’s warning. I thought of the work I had accomplished, the lives I had saved, the nights I had conquered. And I thought of the thing I had become—a creature of unremitting vigilance, a machine of flesh and blood that knew neither weariness nor rest.

I did not take the serum again. But the effects did not leave me. For three days I waited, expecting the fatigue to return, expecting to feel the old human heaviness in my limbs. It did not come. I sat in the surgery, listening to the silence of the empty waiting-room, and I understood at last what the child had seen. I was no longer quite a man. I was something else—something that had stepped out of the ordinary current of human life and could not step back in.

The practice dwindled and then ceased altogether. Dr. Merridew took on a new assistant, a fresh-faced young man from Guy’s, who yawned over his ledgers and complained of the damp. I remained in Cuckfield for a time, living quietly in lodgings, and then I removed to London, where I found work in a laboratory. I do not sleep. I do not tire. I sit at my bench through the small hours, and the watchmen who pass the window on their rounds have grown accustomed to the sight of my shadow against the blind. Sometimes, in the grey light of morning, I catch a glimpse of my face in the glass of the window, and I see what the patients saw—a thing that is almost human, but not quite. And I wonder, with a curiosity that has long since ceased to be painful, what the end of it will be.
