The rain had been falling in Portsmouth for three days, a cold, driving sleet that turned the cobblestones of the High Street into a treacherous mirror and turned the air into a damp, grey shroud. It was in this atmosphere of chill and decay that I first encountered the peculiar case of the ivory token, a matter that would soon draw me from the safety of my study in Baker Street to the humid, salt-stung corridors of the Seamen’s Hospital.

It was a case of simple mechanics, or so it appeared at first glance, but it possessed that certain irregularity, that jagged edge, which suggests that the surface of things is not what it seems. The deceased, a dock worker named Arthur Penrose, had been admitted with a severe case of pneumonia. He had died in the night, and it was the Reverend Mr. Thorne, the hospital chaplain, who discovered the anomaly. Penrose, in his final delirium, had not been calling for his wife or his children, nor for the mercy of God. He had been clutching a small, carved object—a token of ivory, no larger than a shilling, depicting a coiled serpent swallowing its own tail. And he had been whispering a name: “Halloway. The debt. Halloway in Ashby.”

Mr. Thorne, a man of precise habits and limited imagination, had done the only sensible thing. He had consulted the parish records and the directories. There was only one Halloway of note in Ashby-de-la-Zouch, a prosperous alderman and magistrate, a man whose name was synonymous with civic virtue and industrial success. Thorne sent a polite, if rather stiff, letter to the alderman’s residence, informing him of the death of a man who seemed to have a grievance, and asking if the deceased had ever been in his employment.

The reply came back within the hour, delivered by a footman who looked as though he had been scrubbed with a pumice stone. It was a single sheet of heavy, cream-colored paper, bearing a wax seal. The note was brief, written in a hand that was cramped and angular, lacking any warmth. It stated that the alderman had never heard of Arthur Penrose, nor did he know of any such debt. It was signed, “R. Halloway.”

I read the note to Holmes as we sat by the fire, the coal glowing with a dull, red heat. He had been silent for a long time, his long, thin fingers drumming a rhythmic pattern against the arm of his chair.

“Cramped and angular,” I repeated. “You note the handwriting?”

“I note everything, Watson. But it is not the handwriting that interests me. It is the speed. A man who has never heard of a dock worker, who is likely busy with the affairs of the corporation, does not reply to a stranger’s letter within the hour. He files it. He delegates it. He ignores it. To reply so quickly, and so coldly, is a reaction. It is the snap of a trap.”

Holmes rose and began to pace. His eyes were bright, fixed on some distant point in the room. “The serpent swallowing its own tail. Ouroboros. A symbol of eternity, or of self-destruction. But in the context of a sailor’s rumor, it is likely a mark of a specific guild, or perhaps a secret society. Or, more likely, it is a personal emblem. A brand of ownership. If Penrose was clutching it, it was not a souvenir. It was a receipt. Or a threat.”

“Do you think the alderman is guilty?” I asked.

“I think the alderman is afraid,” Holmes said, stopping abruptly. “And fear, my dear Watson, is the greatest of all witnesses. It testifies where no other can. We must go to Ashby. Not as detectives, for that would be too obvious, but as investigators of a maritime claim. I have a letter of introduction to the local magistrate, a friend of the alderman’s. We shall present ourselves as agents for the deceased’s widow, seeking a pension or a settlement for alleged unpaid wages. It is a reasonable pretext. It will allow us to observe Halloway under the strain of suspicion.”

We traveled by train the following morning, the landscape sliding past the window in shades of brown and grey. The Midlands in late autumn was a place of quiet, heavy industries, where the smoke from the chimneys hung low and thick, blending with the fog until it was impossible to distinguish the earth from the sky. We arrived in Ashby-de-la-Zouch as the sun was setting, casting long, distorted shadows across the market square.

The alderman’s house was a large, stone-built mansion on the edge of the town, surrounded by a high wall and a gate of iron bars. It looked less like a home and more like a fortress. As we approached, I noticed a carriage standing in the courtyard, its horses shifting uneasily. A man was standing by the gate, looking out. He was tall, thin, with a face that seemed to have been carved from pale wood. His eyes were fixed on us, and I saw that they were wide, darting, and terrified.

He was not Halloway. I knew this instinctively. Halloway would be a man of substance, of authority. This man was a shadow, a whisper.

As we rang the bell, the gate creaked open. The man stepped forward. He did not ask who we were. He did not ask our business. He simply looked at Holmes, and then at me, and his face drained of all color.

“You are the agents for Penrose,” he said. His voice was high, reedy, and trembling.

“We are,” Holmes replied, his tone cool and detached. “We have come to speak with Mr. Halloway.”

The man’s hand went to his throat, as if he could not breathe. “He is not here. He is... he is away. On a journey.”

“Where to?” I asked.

“I do not know. I do not know. Please, sir, go. Mr. Halloway is not in a state to receive visitors. He is unwell.”

Holmes stepped forward, his eyes narrowing. “You are not the butler, I presume. You are a servant. A private secretary, perhaps?”

The man flinched. “I am... I am a friend. A clerk.”

“A clerk,” Holmes mused. “Who stands at the gate in the rain, wearing a coat that is too fine for the weather, and whose eyes hold the terror of a man who has seen a ghost.”

The man made a sound, a small, strangled cry, and turned to run back toward the house. But Holmes was faster. He stepped aside and raised his hand.

“Stop,” he said. It was not a shout, but the word hung in the air with the weight of a command. The man froze.

“I have a question for you,” Holmes continued. “Did Arthur Penrose die owing a debt to Mr. Halloway? Or did he die because he knew too much?”

The man’s face contorted. He looked from Holmes to me, and then his gaze dropped to the ground. He was shaking, his knees buckling.

“I don’t know anything,” he whispered. “I swear I don’t know. He... he was here. Last week. He came to the library. He was drunk. He was talking about the ship, the *Elara*. He said Halloway had sold the cargo. He said Halloway had killed the captain. He said the ivory was the proof. He said if he didn’t pay, he would come back. And then... and then he left. And the next day, Halloway sent for the police. He said the man had stolen the token. He said it was a family heirloom. And he said if the man came back, he would have him hanged.”

Holmes turned to me, a faint smile playing on his lips. “The token, Watson. It is not a receipt. It is a key. Or perhaps, a confession. The alderman is not just lying. He is stalling. He is waiting for the police to arrive, or for the man to disappear. But he is afraid. And he has made a mistake.”

“What mistake?” I asked.

He pointed to the man. “Look at his hands.”

I looked. The man’s hands were hanging at his sides, trembling. But it was not the trembling of fear that caught my eye. It was the color. The tips of his fingers were stained with a dark, reddish-brown substance. Ink? Or something else?

“Copperas,” Holmes said softly. “Or blood. It does not matter. What matters is that he is sweating. And he is wiping his hands on his trousers. He is nervous. He is lying. And he is not the only one in that house who is lying.”

We stepped over the threshold. The air inside was cool and smelled of lavender and old paper. We were not alone. Behind the man, in the shadow of the hall, another figure was watching us. It was Halloway. He was sitting in a chair, his face pale, his eyes fixed on us with a look of absolute, frozen horror. He did not move. He did not speak. He simply watched us, as if he were waiting for a verdict.

Holmes walked past the trembling clerk and stopped in front of the alderman.

“Mr. Halloway,” he said. “I believe that Arthur Penrose did not die of pneumonia. I believe that he died because he was a witness. And I believe that you are the man who owed the debt.”

Halloway’s lips moved, but no sound came out. He was not a man of words, I saw. He was a man of actions. And he had just made his last one.
