The noise from the hall was the first sign. It was not the measured tread of our butler, Benton, nor the light step of my mother, but a clatter of boots and a boyish shout that echoed up the stairwell and into the morning room where I sat with my needlework. A moment later, the door burst open and my brother, Frederick, stood on the threshold, his face flushed from riding and his hair a windswept mess.

“Lizzy!” he cried, his voice cracking on the second syllable. “Lizzy, I have seen an angel!”

I set down my embroidery hoop, a small smile already forming. He was but seventeen, and prone to fits of dramatic fervour that our father, a man of sober habits, found trying. “An angel, Fred? Has the vicar taken to hiring them for the Sunday service?”

“Don’t be absurd,” he said, striding into the room and throwing himself into a chair opposite me. He was all long limbs and earnest energy. “It is the new governess at Marchmont Hall. Miss Fairweather. I saw her in the village, and then again walking in the lane by the old mill. She has hair the colour of spun gold and eyes that hold the very secrets of the deep.”

“The deep?” I queried, raising an eyebrow. “You mean the sea?”

“I mean the soul, Lizzy! The very depths of feeling!” He leaned forward, his hands clasped. “I am utterly, irrevocably in love.”

I confess my first reaction was a profound and unseemly amusement. To see Frederick, who only last month had been in agonies over a mare that had gone lame, now declaring eternal devotion to a woman he had barely glimpsed, was a comedy of the highest order. I managed to keep my countenance, however, for his expression was one of such genuine torment that to laugh would have been cruel.

“And what does this paragon’s name have to do with her character?” I asked, attempting a more practical tone. “Fairweather. It suggests a certain placidity, does it not?”

“It is a name of pure poetry,” he declared, missing my point entirely. “A name to be sighed in the dark watches of the night.”

The dark watches of the night, in our household, were generally interrupted by our father’s snoring, but I did not say so. Frederick’s infatuation, I decided, was a fire of straw; it would blaze brightly for a week and then be forgotten. I was wrong.

At dinner, he was worse. He spoke of nothing but Miss Fairweather, her grace, her wit, the charming way she had smiled at a child in the village. Our father, Mr. Ellsworth, listened with a stony silence that was far more alarming than any outburst. My mother, a gentle woman whose life was a series of small anxieties, looked as though she might require her smelling salts.

“A governess, Frederick?” my father finally said, his voice low and dangerous. “You speak of a governess as if she were a lady of quality.”

“She is a lady,” Frederick insisted, his chin jutting out. “A lady in every way that matters.”

“She is a paid companion to the children of the Ashworths,” my father retorted, laying down his knife and fork with a decisive click. “She is an employee. You are the son and heir of this estate. I will not have you make a spectacle of yourself and this family over a woman who is, by her station, entirely unsuitable.”

The word ‘unsuitable’ hung in the air like a physical blow. Frederick’s face went white, then red. He pushed back from the table, his chair scraping against the floor. “You would deny me the only true happiness I have ever known!” he shouted, and stormed from the room, the slam of the door rattling the china on the sideboard.

My mother began to weep softly. My father, his face grim, poured himself a large glass of port. “He will get over it,” he said, more to himself than to us. “He is a boy. It is a passing fancy.”

But it was not a passing fancy. For the next fortnight, Frederick was a ghost in his own home. He neglected his studies, refused to join us for cards, and spent his days riding his horse towards Marchmont Hall, only to return at dusk, disconsolate. He had, he told me in a moment of confidence, managed to exchange a few words with Miss Fairweather over the garden wall. She had been kind, but distant. She had called him ‘Master Ellsworth’, a title that seemed to wound him more deeply than any insult.

“She thinks me a child, Lizzy,” he said, his voice thick with misery.

And here I must confess my own part in this drama. I had met Miss Fairweather, whose Christian name was Anne, on several occasions. The Ashworths are our nearest neighbours, and she had accompanied their daughters on calls to our house. She was, as Frederick had rightly observed, a lady of considerable grace and intelligence. She was not beautiful in a conventional, striking way, but there was a warmth in her grey eyes and a quiet dignity in her manner that I found deeply admirable. We had shared a pot of tea and a conversation about the novels of Mrs. Radcliffe, and I had felt an instant kinship with her. She was, I suspected, a woman of feeling and good sense, trapped in a position that offered her little scope for either.

My loyalty, of course, was to my brother. His suffering was real, and my parents’ anxiety was palpable. The house was thick with unspoken tension. My father saw Frederick’s behaviour as a threat to the family’s standing; my mother saw it as a prelude to social ruin. I was torn. I loved my brother and hated to see him in pain, yet I could not help but feel that his pain was born of a boyish romanticism that had more to do with the idea of Miss Fairweather than the woman herself. And I liked Anne Fairweather. I liked her far too much to see her become the object of a family scandal, the governess who had bewitched the young heir.

The climax came on a Tuesday. Frederick, in a fit of desperate bravado, had ridden to Marchmont Hall and asked to see Miss Fairweather. He had, he later told me, intended to declare himself and offer her his hand, regardless of the consequences. He was shown into the library, where he found not only Anne, but also Mrs. Ashworth, a formidable woman with a voice like a foghorn.

I can only imagine the scene. Frederick, stammering his affections, his face the colour of a beetroot. Anne, standing by the window, her expression a mixture of pity and embarrassment. And Mrs. Ashworth, her eyes narrowing with a cold, assessing fury.

Frederick returned home in a state of utter despair. He had been dismissed, he said, with a few well-chosen words from Mrs. Ashworth that had stripped the skin from his bones. Miss Fairweather had said nothing at all, but had looked at him with a sadness that he found more devastating than any reprimand. He had been made to feel, he sobbed, like a foolish, presumptuous boy.

That evening, as I sat with him in the library, trying to offer what comfort I could, Benton entered with a letter. It was addressed to me, in a neat, feminine hand. I recognised it at once. It was from Anne Fairweather.

I read it in the privacy of my room. She wrote with a frankness that I found both painful and deeply moving. She was sensible, she said, of the great honour young Mr. Ellsworth had done her, but she was also sensible of the impossibility of the situation. She was a governess, and he was a gentleman. She had her position and her reputation to consider, and she could not, in good conscience, encourage his attentions. She asked me, as his sister, to help him to forget her. She ended by saying that she valued my friendship and hoped that this unhappy business would not put an end to it.

I sat for a long time with the letter in my hand. My first instinct was a fierce, protective anger towards her. How dare she be so calm, so rational, when my brother was in torment? But as the evening wore on, my anger cooled, replaced by a profound sense of pity for them both. They were both victims of a world that measured a person’s worth by their birth and their fortune. He was trapped by his name; she was trapped by her lack of one.

The next morning, I found Frederick in the stables, saddling his horse. His eyes were red-rimmed, but his jaw was set.

“I am going for a long ride,” he said, without looking at me.

“Fred,” I said gently, “I had a letter from Miss Fairweather.”

He froze, his hands still on the leather strap. Slowly, he turned to face me. “What did she say?”

I told him, not everything, but enough. I told him that she thought him honourable, but that she could not see him again. I told him that she had asked me to help him forget. He listened in silence, and when I had finished, he nodded once, a short, sharp movement.

“I see,” he said. He finished saddling his horse, mounted, and rode away without another word.

He was gone for three hours. When he returned, he was calmer. He did not speak of her again, not that day, nor for many weeks. The fever had broken. The family breathed a collective sigh of relief. My father pronounced him ‘a sensible lad, after all’, and my mother’s anxieties turned to the more manageable matter of the autumn ball.

As for me, I wrote to Anne Fairweather that same week. I told her that I understood, and that I hoped she was well. I did not mention Frederick, but I think she knew. A few days later, a small parcel arrived for me. It was a book of poetry by Mr. Wordsworth. Inside, on the flyleaf, she had written a single line: ‘For the friend who understands.’

I smiled, and placed it on my shelf. The world, I reflected, was a complicated place, full of impossible choices and quiet heartbreaks. And sometimes, the only thing a person could do was to offer a little understanding, and a book of poetry, to a friend.
