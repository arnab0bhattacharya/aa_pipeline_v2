It was one of those crises which, in the history of a town like Whetstone, are at once singularly important and singularly petty; a crisis in which the large forces of commercial rivalry and social ambition are concentrated into a single act of laying a stone, an act which, in the minds of the principal actors, will bear the weight of a generation's honour. The new chapel in the Lower Wharf district was to have its foundation stone laid by one of the two great families of the town, and to refuse this distinction to one while granting it to the other was to declare a victory in a war that had been waged for thirty years in the counting-houses and drawing-rooms of Whetstone.

On one side stood the Mardens, whose prosperity was as solid and unadorned as the iron they wrought. Mr. Silas Marden was a man whose thoughts ran in grooves as deep and direct as the channels his furnaces cast. He had risen from the shop-floor, and he held a deep, unspoken contempt for any distinction that was not founded upon the tangible evidence of a ledger-book. His rival, Mr. Josiah Fell, represented the older, more genteel form of mercantile power, his family having made its fortune in wool and banking a generation before the first steam-engine had ever been erected in the valley. Fell’s wealth was more diffuse, his influence more subtle, and he held the position of chief magistrate with an air of one to whom such honours are a natural birthright.

Between these two formidable pillars of the community stood the Reverend Mr. Arthur Penrose, a man whose constitution was better suited to the quiet contemplation of patristic theology than to the bruising commerce of a manufacturing town. He had been presented to the living of St. Jude’s by the Fell family, a fact which he felt not as a burden of obligation, but as a living, breathing presence that sat beside him in the vestry and watched him from the pews. Yet it was to Silas Marden that he owed the very substance of his moral life. In his youth, before the Church had claimed him, he had been a clerk in a Marden warehouse, and it was Silas’s father, a stern but righteous man, who had seen the lad’s hunger for learning and had provided him with the books and the small stipend that allowed him to escape the counter for the university. To the Mardens, therefore, he owed not his place, but the possibility of having a place at all; he owed his conscience.

The decision had been forced upon him with the simple, crushing weight of a practical necessity. The foundation stone—a great block of Derbyshire gritstone—lay waiting in the builder’s yard, and the day for the ceremony was fixed. Both Mr. Fell and Mr. Marden had, in their different ways, made their expectations known. Fell had spoken of the chapel as a “fitting monument to the spiritual and civic leadership of this town,” his words smooth and rounded as river pebbles. Marden had been more blunt, declaring to the chapel committee that “a thing of this sort wants a solid man at the bottom of it, and there’s none more solid than me.”

Penrose, in his small, book-lined study, felt the pressure of these two wills as a physical constriction of the chest. He saw that the decision was not a matter of preference, but of definition. To choose Fell was to affirm that leadership was a matter of inherited status and established authority; to choose Marden was to declare that it was a matter of industrious merit and present power. The chapel, a building intended for the worship of an impartial God, was to be founded upon a deeply partial act of man. He felt the irony with a painful acuteness. His own position was a testament to the same tangled web of patronage and principle; he was a man raised by one family to serve, in a sense, the spiritual needs of all, yet he could not escape the feeling that he was merely a steward of the Fells’ religious property.

The slow growth of his anxiety had been like the spreading of damp in an old wall, unnoticeable at first, but now a source of undeniable decay. He had sought a compromise, suggesting that the stone be laid jointly, a solution which both parties had rejected with a contempt that was, for once, identical. For the honour was indivisible; it was a banner to be seized by one army or the other, not a flag of truce to be shared.

His decision, when it came to him, was not a sudden flash of resolution but a quiet, weary concession to the nature of the world he inhabited. He saw that the conflict was not one of right against wrong, but of one right against another; the right of established authority against the right of self-made power. The Fells had built the town’s institutions, the Mardens had built the town itself. In the end, the choice must fall to the man whose claim was rooted in the present, whose hands were stained with the very soot and iron-dust that gave Whetstone its life. To deny Marden would be to deny the essential character of the town and, in a way, to deny the very source of his own humble beginnings. It was a painful choice, for he felt he was slighting the family to whom he owed his professional life, but it was the only one that felt true to the world as it was.

He sent a note to both men, requesting their presence at the vestry. They arrived within the same hour, each man’s silence a form of eloquent expectation. Penrose, his face pale and his manner one of deep humility, spoke not of his decision, but of the chapel’s purpose.

“Gentlemen,” he began, his voice quiet but clear, “this chapel is to be a house of prayer for all sorts and conditions of men. It is built not for one family, or one interest, but for the poor weaver, the iron-puddler, the clerk, and the master alike. Its foundation must therefore be laid by one whose life and work are a testament to the very industry and effort that sustain this town. It is therefore my solemn conviction that this honour belongs to Mr. Marden.”

A stillness fell upon the room, a silence so profound it seemed to hum. Mr. Fell did not move a muscle, but a cold, hard light entered his eyes, the light of an insult received and meticulously filed away. He gave a stiff, formal bow. “I see, Mr. Penrose, that you have made your choice. I trust you will not have cause to regret the… particularities of it.” He turned and left without another word, his footsteps echoing on the flagstones like the final, definitive strokes of a pen closing an account.

Silas Marden, who had been standing with his hands clasped behind his back, now let out a long, slow breath. He looked at Penrose, and for the first time, his gaze was not one of demand, but of a deep, unvarnished respect. “You’re a bold man, Reverend,” he said, his voice a low rumble. “More bold than I took you for. You’ve cut your coat according to your cloth, I’ll say that.”

Penrose felt a great weariness settle upon him, the exhaustion that follows a fever. He had chosen, and in choosing, he had affirmed one kind of truth while wounding another. He knew that his relationship with the Fell family was irrevocably altered, that a subtle chill would now attend his every interaction with them. But he had also, he felt, honoured the memory of the old Marden, the man who had seen a future for a poor boy that the boy could not yet see for himself. The foundation stone would be laid on Saturday, and in the slow, heavy work of building the chapel, he hoped to find some measure of peace, a peace founded not on the favour of men, but on the difficult, necessary compromises of a conscience at work in the world.
