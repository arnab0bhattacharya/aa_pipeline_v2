If you would know the whole of it, you must first picture to yourself a certain narrow street, not a stone’s throw from the clamour of Holborn, where the houses lean so close together that they seem to be whispering secrets, and where the sun, if he visits the place at all, comes in like a debtor who is ashamed to be seen, slipping down the chimneys and departing by the gutters before any man can clap a hand upon him. In this street—Maggot’s Rents it was called, though no maggot ever throve there half so well as the soot—there stood a shop, shuttered these many years, over whose door there yet lingered the faded ghost of a sign, upon which the words “MR. ABEL CRUMBLE, PLEDGES” might still be spelled out by any person of strong eyes and stronger patience. And above this shop, in two rooms that smelt of dust and old ledgers and the ghost of departed tea, there dwelt the said Mr. Crumble himself, a retired pawnbroker of seventy years, who trusted nobody, and had good reason, as he would tell you, to trust nobody, for had he not spent his life in the taking of other men’s treasures, and the watching of them rot?

It was to this gentleman, his grandfather, that there came one bleak November afternoon a boy of ten, by name Nathaniel—Nat, as he was called—who had never in his life beheld the old man, his mother having quarrelled with her father in a manner that need not here be raked up, save that it ended in her death and the boy’s being sent, like a parcel nobody wanted, from one relation to another, until at last there remained no other relation but this fossil of Maggot’s Rents.

The boy stood in the shop-parlour, his hat in his hands, his breath making little clouds before his face, for the fire was small and grudging, and the old man sat opposite him in a great chair, wrapped in a dressing-gown that had once been crimson and was now the colour of a dried orange, and looked at him as a man looks at a pledge he suspects of being paste.

“So,” said Mr. Crumble, after a silence long enough to freeze the Thames, “you are Nathaniel.”

“Yes, grandfather.”

“Humph. And what do you do?”

“Do, sir?”

“Do. What are you good for? Can you read? Can you cipher? Can you keep your tongue behind your teeth and your fingers out of other men’s pockets? For I’ll tell you this, boy—there’s no room in this house for a fool, and less for a rogue.”

The boy, who had been bred in a hard school, answered stoutly enough that he could read, and cipher, and that he had never stolen so much as a pin, whereat the old man grunted, and said that pins were the very things that went first, and that he should keep his eye upon him.

Now, if you had asked any neighbour in Maggot’s Rents what manner of man Mr. Crumble was, you would have got for answer that he was a miser, a heathen, and a dried-up stick of an old fellow who had not a heart in him bigger than a sixpence. And indeed, to look at him, you would have said as much; for his face was all angles and his mouth was a trap, and his eyes, deep-set under tufted brows, had a way of going over a person like a pawnbroker’s hands over a watch, weighing, valuing, and finding it light. But there was that in the shop below—the shop that was never opened—which no neighbour knew, and which the boy was soon to discover.

For the first week he was set to sweep and dust and run errands, and was fed upon bread and dripping and a thin broth that had never known a fowl, and was watched at every turn by the old man, who would appear in doorways without warning, like a spider from a crack. But on the eighth day, being sent down into the shop to fetch a box of tallow candles, the boy found the shutter a little ajar, and the light of the street coming in a long dusty bar across the floor, and there, in that bar of light, he saw the pledges.

They were everywhere. They hung from the ceiling in clusters, they crowded the shelves in ranks, they were heaped in corners and stacked against the walls; watches by the hundred, rings by the thousand, silver spoons tied in bundles like asparagus, a fiddle, a child’s coral, a pair of buckled shoes, a wedding-ring upon a card, a set of false teeth grinning from a glass case, a ship in a bottle, a Bible with a broken clasp, and over all the dust lay thick and grey, like the ash of a dead fire.

“And what,” said a voice behind him, “do you mean by looking at what doesn’t concern you?”

The boy turned, and there stood the old man, and in his face was that which the boy had not seen before—a kind of hunger, and a kind of fear.

“I never saw so many things, sir,” said the boy.

“No,” said the old man, “nor never will again. They are pledges, boy. Pledges. Left with me by folk as wanted money, and never come back for. Thirty years, and never a one come back.” He took up a watch and turned it in his fingers. “That was a gentleman’s, that was. He said he’d return in a week. He’s been dead these five-and-twenty years, I make no doubt. And that ring—that was a woman’s, and she cried when she left it, and swore she’d have it back before the month was out. She never came. Nor him, nor her, nor any of ’em. They’re all dead, or gone, or forgot. And here they bide.”

“Why don’t you sell them, sir?”

“Sell them!” cried the old man, and his voice cracked like a dry twig. “Sell them! Am I a receiver of stolen goods? Am I a Jew? They are pledges, boy—pledges, and a pledge is a sacred thing. It is left in trust. And I have kept the trust. Thirty years, and never a one sold, never a one touched, never a one but what I’ve dusted with my own hands and set back in its place. Do you think they’d come back if I sold ’em? Do you think they’d come back at all?”

And here the old man stopped, and looked about him at the crowded shelves, and the boy saw that his eyes were not angry but wet.

“I thought,” said the old man, more softly, “that one of ’em would come back. I’ve waited. I’ve kept ’em. I’ve said to myself, ‘Abel Crumble, you keep ’em true, and one day there’ll be a knock at the door, and a face you know, and a voice saying, “I’ve come for my pledge.”’ But there’s been no knock. There’s never a knock. And I’m an old man, boy, and I’m tired of waiting.”

The boy stood a while, and then he said—and it was a strange thing for a boy of ten to say, but he had been bred in a hard school and knew what it was to wait for a knock that never came—“Grandfather, I’ll wait with you.”

The old man looked at him sharply, as if to find the lie in him; but the boy’s face was honest, and after a moment the old man’s mouth worked, and he turned away and blew his nose with a great trumpeting, and said that the boy was a fool, and that fools were costly, and that he should have no supper but bread and water for his impudence. But he had no sooner said it than he bade the girl who came in to char bring up a bit of the cold pie, and the boy saw that the pie was set before him, and that the old man watched him eat it with a kind of grudging satisfaction, as a man watches a plant he has watered in secret.

And so it went, week after week, and month after month. The boy learned the history of every pledge—for the old man, once the ice was broken, would talk of nothing else—and learned too the old man’s ways, his grunts and his growls, and the way he had of leaving a sixpence on the table where the boy would find it, and saying nothing. And the boy, for his part, never asked for the shop to be opened, nor for the pledges to be sold, but would sit with the old man of an evening and hear the tales of the folk who had left them, until the two of them, the old and the young, were as thick as thieves—which was the more remarkable, as the old man had never trusted a thief in his life.

And still, no knock came. And still the pledges hung in the dust and the dark, waiting for the folk who never returned. But the boy had come back—had come, indeed, where he had never been before—and the old man, who had trusted nobody, found that he had trusted one, and that the one was a boy of ten with a hole in his stocking and a heart as sound as a bell.
