The letter from Mr. Henry Ashcombe had arrived on a Tuesday, and by Thursday it had been read so many times that the folds threatened to part company altogether. Mrs. Ashcombe, who had never in her life been able to leave a piece of news to settle quietly, had carried it to the window, to the fire, and back again to the window, as though a change of light might alter its contents; and having at last satisfied herself that no reading could make it say anything but what it said, she laid it upon the table with the air of a woman disposing of a difficult question.

“It is a great deal of money,” she observed.

Her son, who stood at the window with his back to her, made no answer.

“Two hundred and forty a year,” continued Mrs. Ashcombe, “and the house found, and the garden, and the glebe, and Mr. Ellwood’s interest at your back into the bargain. There is not a clergyman in the county better provided for. And the living is in his gift, remember, absolutely in his gift, to bestow upon whom he pleases.”

“I am perfectly aware of it, ma’am.”

“And he has been so good as to think of you. It is a compliment, Edmund, that many a man would be proud of.”

“I am sensible of the compliment.”

Mrs. Ashcombe looked at her son’s shoulders, which were not the shoulders of a man who considered himself complimented, and she drew her work-basket towards her with a slight quickening of the breath which those who knew her best understood to mean that she was preparing for battle.

“Miss Ellwood,” she said, “is a very worthy young woman.”

“She is.”

“And her uncle’s affection for her is well known. He has no children of his own. She will not go portionless into the world.”

Edmund turned from the window at that, and there was in his countenance a mingling of amusement and impatience which his mother would have been glad to see less of. “You speak as though the whole affair were already settled, ma’am, and the banns to be published on Sunday next. I have not the smallest reason to suppose that Miss Ellwood has any inclination towards me whatsoever.”

“Inclination!” cried Mrs. Ashcombe. “My dear Edmund, when a man has a house and a garden and two hundred and forty pounds a year, and is besides a clergyman of the Establishment, he need not trouble himself greatly about inclination. The young woman will do her duty, and be thankful for the provision. It is not as though she were a beauty, to be choosing where she pleases. She has been three seasons in Bath, I understand, and nothing has come of it. She is not likely to refuse the first respectable offer that is made her.”

“You are very good to say so,” said Edmund drily. “But I have not yet made her an offer.”

“No — you have been too busy making eyes at Miss Danvers.”

The words were out before Mrs. Ashcombe could check them, and she had the grace to colour. Edmund coloured too, but he did not look away.

“I have known Miss Danvers since we were children,” he said quietly. “She is the daughter of a man who has done me more kindness than I can ever repay, and if I have shown her particular attention, it has been with the approval of her father and the full knowledge of my own intentions.”

“And what are your intentions, Edmund? To live upon air? To keep a wife and a family upon the forty pounds a year your curacy brings you, and the charity of the parish? I have nothing against Miss Danvers. She is a pretty, modest, well-behaved girl, and I am sure she would make any man a good wife. But she has not a penny in the world, and neither have you, and you cannot eat affection.”

“I have no wish to eat at all,” said Edmund, with a smile that was not entirely cheerful. “But I am not so great a fool as to suppose that a man may marry upon nothing. I have thought of it a great deal, ma’am, more than you know. I have thought of it until I am heartily sick of thinking, and I have come to no conclusion that satisfies me. If I take Mr. Ellwood’s living, I take it upon his terms. He has made no secret of them. He told me himself, in this very room, that he had a great regard for his niece, and that he could not bear to think of her going out of the county to marry a stranger, and that he should consider the man who married her and accepted the living as his own son. He was very plain with me. He said, ‘Ashcombe, I like you. I have watched you. You are a sensible fellow and a good preacher, and you will not beat my niece, I think, nor run through her fortune at the card-table. Marry her, and the living is yours. Marry elsewhere, and I wish you joy of your forty pounds a year.’ Those were his words, ma’am, and I have not yet forgotten the sound of them.”

Mrs. Ashcombe had listened to this speech with an expression of great satisfaction. “There!” she cried. “You see! He is a sensible man, and he speaks plainly, and I honour him for it. And as for Miss Ellwood, she is not such a fool as to expect to be courted like a belle of the assembly rooms. She knows her situation. She will be very well content with a husband who is civil to her, and a house of her own, and a position in the county. It is the way of the world, Edmund, and you may set your face against it as much as you please, but you will not alter it.”

Edmund was silent for a moment. Then he said, in a lower voice, “And what of Miss Danvers?”

“What of her?” returned his mother, with a little toss of her head. “She is young, and she is pretty, and she will find another husband. There is a young man at Stroud, a solicitor, I am told, who has been very attentive to her. She will do very well without you.”

“That is not what I asked, ma’am. I asked what I am to say to her. I have given her reason to believe — I have given her every reason to believe — that my affections are engaged. I cannot now go to her and say, ‘I am sorry, but I have had a better offer, and I must beg you to release me.’ I cannot do it. It would be a villainy.”

“It would be a prudence,” said Mrs. Ashcombe stoutly. “And as for villainy, Edmund, I would have you consider what sort of villainy it is to bring a woman into a life of poverty and dependence, to deny her the comforts she has been used to, to see her go without a carriage and without a servant and without the means to educate her children as they ought to be educated, all because you could not bear to disappoint her for a fortnight. That is the villainy, if you ask me. The other is nothing but a little unpleasantness at the outset, which will be forgot by everybody before the year is out.”

Edmund looked at his mother with an expression which she could not quite read. There was affection in it, and there was something else, too — a kind of wondering admiration, as though he were seeing her for the first time and was not entirely certain what he saw.

“You have a very decided way of putting things, ma’am,” he said.

“I have lived a great deal longer than you have, my dear, and I have seen a great deal more of the world, and I know that a man may fancy himself in love a dozen times before he is thirty, and be very miserable about it, and recover very well. But a living is not to be had every day. And Mr. Ellwood is not a young man. If you do not take it now, he may bestow it elsewhere, and then where will you be?”

“Where I am now,” said Edmund. “Only without the temptation.”

Mrs. Ashcombe rose from her chair and came to stand beside him at the window. The garden lay below them, very neat and quiet in the afternoon sun, and beyond it the church tower, and beyond that the fields sloping away towards the valley. It was a pleasant prospect, and it had been her home for twenty years, and she was not a woman to undervalue it.

“You will do as you think best,” she said at last, and her voice was gentler than it had been. “You are a man now, and you must decide for yourself. But I will say this, Edmund, and then I will say no more. Whatever you choose, choose it with your eyes open, and do not look back. If you take the living, take it heartily, and make Miss Ellwood a good husband, and never let her see that you are sorry for it. And if you refuse it — why, then, refuse it heartily too, and marry your Miss Danvers, and be as poor as church mice, and I daresay you will be very happy. But do not stand in the middle of the road, my dear, looking first one way and then the other, for that is the way to be run over.”

Edmund turned and took her hand. “You are a good mother,” he said.

“I am a practical one,” she replied, “which is very often the same thing.”

He smiled at that, and then he went out into the garden, and walked up and down the gravel path for a long time, with his hands behind his back and his head bent, and the rooks cawing in the elms above him. And whether he thought of Miss Ellwood’s plain face and two hundred and forty pounds a year, or of Miss Danvers’s pretty one and forty pounds a year, or of the strange, unaccountable manner in which a man’s whole life may turn upon a single Tuesday’s post, was known to nobody but himself.
