It is a truth very generally acknowledged that a young woman of respectable fortune, upon acquiring a sister-in-law of obscure origin and no evident income, will consider herself endowed with a special providence for the detection of mercenary motives. Clara Fortescue, though but two-and-twenty, and whose own fortune had been settled upon her with a liberality rather remarkable for a younger daughter, felt this providence strongly upon the occasion of her brother’s marriage to Miss Mary Whittaker. Mr. Fortescue was a man of sense, in the main, but his sister had long known that where the affections were warmly engaged, the intellect was but a feeble sentinel. He had met Miss Whittaker in Weymouth, where she was residing with an aunt of limited consequence and a wardrobe of rather more; and within a few weeks he was entirely captivated. Clara, upon receiving the intelligence at her father’s estate at Danfield Park in Kent, had not scrupled to pronounce the affair highly suspicious.

She was not a young woman given to open reproach, nor was she deficient in that sort of civility which passes for affection among near relatives; but she was exceedingly observant, and she had determined, from the very moment Miss Whittaker should enter her father’s house as a married woman, to watch her with a vigilance that should leave no artifice unexamined.

Mrs. Fortescue arrived on a Tuesday, in company with her husband, and was received with all the ceremony due to her new station. Clara, who had provided herself with a great number of silent observations to make, found her first opportunity at dinner. The table was handsomely ordered, as it always was at Danfield, and the plate was particularly fine—old family plate, which had descended through three generations and which Clara regarded with an affection second only to that which she bore her own writing-desk. She watched, therefore, with no small interest, to see how Mrs. Fortescue would comport herself when the servants withdrew and the dessert was placed upon the table.

Mary’s eye did indeed wander over the plate, but it was with the expression of one accustomed to survey whatever was set before her, and not with the greedy admiration Clara had expected. She spoke of the warmth of the journey, of the beauty of the lanes as they approached Danfield, and of her hope that the horses had not been over-tired. When the dessert was placed before her, she declined the pine with a gentle shake of the head, and took only a little fruit. Clara, who had eaten of the pine with a quiet satisfaction, considered this forbearance a very artful maneuver. A woman truly indifferent to luxury would have taken her share without remark; a woman excessively greedy would have taken too much; but a woman calculating her effect would do precisely what Mrs. Fortescue had done—decline the pine, and thereby draw attention to her own moderation, her self-command, and her elegant indifference to expense. It was, in Clara’s view, the most insidious form of vanity, because it wore the disguise of humility.

In the days that followed, Clara’s suspicions were rather increased than diminished by the uninterrupted propriety of her sister-in-law’s conduct. Mary rose early, breakfasted with the family, and expressed no dissatisfaction with the tea, which was of an inferior sort, Mr. Fortescue having lately contracted with a new merchant. She walked in the gardens with Clara’s mother, listened to that lady’s remarks upon the rose-trees with an attention that could not be called insincere, and offered, when asked, a number of observations upon the weather which were neither remarkably clever nor remarkably dull. She inquired after the neighbouring families with a modest curiosity, and when the names of the Misses Calloway were mentioned—a family of large fortune and little sense—she did not discover that eagerness for an introduction which Clara had looked for as a sure token of a fortune-hunting disposition.

This very absence of what Clara had expected was, to her reasoning, the strongest proof of design. That a young woman, raised in obscurity, and suddenly lifted into the society of Danfield Park, should feel no awkwardness, no fluttering impatience to be known, no exultation in her altered circumstances, was so contrary to nature as to be wholly incredible. The only possible explanation was that Mrs. Fortescue was acting a part, and acting it with a skill which argued long practice in the art of deception. Clara had read enough of human nature—or rather, she had read enough of certain novels, and had reflected upon them with a confidence her experience hardly warranted—to know that the most dangerous impostors were those who never betrayed themselves by a single unguarded word.

One morning, as they sat together in the drawing-room, Mrs. Fortescue took up a volume of sermons which lay upon the table, and read a passage with so much gravity and attention that Clara was momentarily confounded. She had supposed her sister-in-law incapable of serious reading; yet here she was, bent over a page of moral theology with the composure of a woman who understood what she read. But upon further reflection, Clara found this circumstance less surprising than alarming. A fortune-hunter who read sermons was a fortune-hunter of the deepest dye. It argued a mind not merely calculating but profoundly aware of the value of appearing religious. Clara determined to mention the circumstance to her particular friend, Miss Amelia Drayton, in her next letter, as affording a singular instance of designing conduct.

Mr. Fortescue, meanwhile, observed nothing but his wife’s perfections. He thought her gentle, elegant, and superior to the little foibles of women who had been raised in greater affluence; and he said so to Clara one evening, as they stood by the library fire, with a warmth which rather irritated that young lady, as it seemed to reflect upon the women who had been so raised.

“You must allow, Clara,” said he, “that Mary’s conduct is everything one could wish. She gives no trouble, makes no demands, and accommodates herself to everything with a cheerfulness I have rarely seen equalled.”

“Oh! I allow it fully,” replied Clara, with a smile which she endeavoured to make affectionate, but which was, in truth, exceedingly satirical. “I have never seen a young woman so perfectly mistress of herself.”

Mr. Fortescue, who was not quick at deciphering irony when it was wrapped in a compliment, received this speech with pleasure, and left the room satisfied that his sister was at last coming round.

Clara, left to her meditations, drew her chair nearer the fire and gave herself up to the contemplation of her brother’s delusion. She felt that she alone saw clearly. She alone perceived that this smooth surface of propriety concealed a depth of calculation which would, sooner or later, betray itself. She had only to wait. In the meantime, she would observe, and she would remember; and when the day of discovery came—as come it must—she would have the melancholy satisfaction of knowing that she had been right from the first.

That Mrs. Fortescue, in the quiet of her own apartment, was not wholly unconscious of the scrutiny to which she was subjected, is probable enough. Whether she was pained by it, or merely amused, cannot here be determined. She continued, however, to behave with a propriety so exact, a moderation so uniform, and a temper so unruffled, that Clara was driven, at last, to the reluctant conclusion that her sister-in-law was either the most artful or the most amiable woman she had ever known—and as she was wholly resolved it should be the former, she found in that resolution a satisfaction which almost compensated for the want of proof.
