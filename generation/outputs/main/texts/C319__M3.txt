The dress was laid out on the bed like a shed skin, pale and luminescent in the last of the October light. Clara touched the lace at the collar and felt the weight of what she had agreed to. It was a beautiful dress, chosen by Elias with a tenderness that had undone her reservations more effectively than any argument could have. He had held it up to her in the dressmaker's shop in Ross-on-Wye and smiled with such genuine hope that she had felt her own future rearrange itself around the gesture. She was twenty-two. Elias Blackmore was fifty-three. The difference was not so much in years as in worlds, but he had offered her a bridge between them, and she had taken it.

She left the room and walked the long gallery on the second floor of Trevethick Hall. The house had been built in the previous century by Elias's grandfather, a man whose portrait watched her now from the far end with the proprietary satisfaction of someone who understood that houses outlast people and that the right marriage could secure a legacy for generations. Clara had learned to read these portraits in the three months of her engagement. She had learned to read the house, too—the way the morning sun fell through the east windows and turned the oak panelling the colour of warm honey, the way the gardens sloped down to the River Wye in a series of terraces that must have cost a fortune and several men's lives to construct. She had taught herself to love it, or something close to love, the way one learns to love a language not native to one's tongue: deliberately, with practice, and with occasional moments of startling fluency that surprise even the speaker.

Elias had been kind to her from the first. She had come to Trevethick as companion to his sister, Mrs. Adele Holt, a woman whose grief over a late miscarriage had settled into a persistent melancholy that required gentle management. Clara had read to her, walked with her through the orchards, listened to her stories about Elias as a boy—how he had once nursed a kestrel back to health and refused to let it go even when its wing had mended, keeping it in the stable for months until it died of old age. Adele had meant this as an illustration of her brother's loyalty. Clara had heard in it something else: a man who held on to things past their natural season. But she had also seen the way he listened to his sister, the patience in his eyes, the way he adjusted his schedule to visit her every afternoon at four. He was a man who understood devotion as a daily practice rather than a grand gesture, and Clara, who had grown up in a household where her father's affection had been as unpredictable as spring weather, found this steadiness irresistible. Not exciting. Not thrilling. But irresistible in the way that solid ground is irresistible to someone who has been wading.

She reached the window at the end of the gallery and looked out over the western gardens. The light was thinning. Tomorrow she would stand in the church at Goodrich and say the words that would make her Mrs. Elias Blackmore, mistress of Trevethick Hall, guardian of its orchards and terraces and the long, shaded walk of limes that led to the river. She pressed her forehead against the glass and felt its coolness against her skin.

She heard the front door bell below, and then Pritchard's measured tread across the hall, and then voices—one familiar, one not. She caught fragments. "The coach from London—dreadful roads past Oxford—" and Elias's warm reply, welcoming, delighted. A guest for the wedding, then, arriving a day early. She turned from the window and made her way downstairs to perform her duties as hostess, a role she had been rehearsing with increasing confidence since her engagement.

He was standing in the hall when she reached the bottom of the stairs, shaking rain from his coat. He was tall, perhaps thirty, with dark hair that curled slightly at the collar and the kind of face that suggested someone had once told it it was handsome and it had never quite recovered from the knowledge. He looked up as she descended, and she felt something shift in her chest, a small tectonic movement, like a key turning in a lock she had not known existed.

"Clara," Elias said, coming forward to take her hand, "this is my nephew, Mr. Benedict Faraday. He has come from London for our wedding, despite the roads and the weather and what I can only assume were the protests of half the drawing rooms in Mayfair." Elias said this with pride. Benedict was his late brother's son, and Elias had spoken of him often—a young man who had distinguished himself in the Peninsula, who had come home and taken up a modest legal practice, who had refused every offer of financial assistance from his uncle with a quiet firmness that Elias alternately admired and lamented.

Benedict bowed. Clara curtsied. Their eyes met for a moment longer than was strictly necessary, and in that moment she saw that he knew. Not the specifics of her situation—those he would learn soon enough—but the general shape of it. He had seen young women marry older men before. He understood the arithmetic. And there was something in his expression, a kind of recognition that was not pity but something more dangerous: kinship.

Dinner was four courses. Elias sat at the head of the table, Benedict at his right, Clara at his left, and Adele across from her, picking at her food with the disinterest that had become habitual. The conversation turned on the war, the harvest, a recent debate in Parliament that Benedict had attended out of curiosity. He spoke well, with a slight hoarseness that Clara found she was attending to more closely than was wise. Elias was expansive, happy, pouring wine with the generosity of a man who felt that fortune had finally dealt him a hand worth celebrating. He told a story about Benedict as a boy—how he had once attempted to ride a young horse that had thrown him into a duck pond, and how he had emerged sputtering and laughing and insisting on trying again. Benedict bore this with good humour, but Clara saw him glance at her once, quickly, as if checking whether she was laughing at him or with him. She was with him. She was entirely with him.

After dinner, Adele retired with a headache, and Elias was called away to address a problem with one of the tenant farmers—a broken fence, sheep in the orchard, something that required his personal attention. He apologised profusely and left Benedict and Clara in the drawing room with the promise that he would return shortly.

They sat across from each other. The fire popped. Rain had begun again against the windows.

"So you are to be my aunt," Benedict said.

"So it seems."

"Uncle Elias speaks of you constantly. In his letters, he has described you in such terms that I confess I was prepared to be sceptical. He made you sound almost implausibly agreeable."

"And now?"

"Now I think he understated the case."

She looked at him. He held her gaze without presumption, without the calculated intensity she had learned to expect from men who wanted something from her. He was simply looking at her, and the simplicity of it was devastating.

"You could have been anything," she said, and then caught herself, surprised by the frankness of her own statement. But he understood. He did not pretend otherwise.

"I could have been," he agreed. "A year ago, I might have said I was waiting for something. But I have learned that waiting is its own kind of decision. One makes a life in the waiting, and then the thing one was waiting for arrives, and it is too late, because the life one has made is not the life that would have held it."

She felt the precision of this like a blade laid against her skin—not cutting, but present, demanding acknowledgment.

"Is it too late?" she asked.

"It is tomorrow."

They were silent. The fire settled. Somewhere in the house a door closed, and she heard Elias's voice in the hall, warm and cheerful, speaking to Pritchard about the rain.

Benedict stood. "I am glad my uncle has found you," he said. "I am glad someone has found you. That is not nothing, Clara. You will not think it nothing, in time."

She did not answer, because Elias was already in the doorway, shaking the last drops from his coat, his face open and bright with the simple happiness of a man returning to the people he loved, and she rose to meet him, and she took his arm, and she did not look back at Benedict Faraday, who stood by the fire watching her go with an expression that she would carry with her, she knew, for longer than was reasonable or wise or kind to any of them.

In the morning, the rain had stopped. She put on the dress. She said the words.
