Mr. Evelyn St. Clair had been three years in Australia, a land which, he had been informed, was rich in gold and singularly poor in everything else. He had found this to be true. The gold, indeed, he had discovered in considerable quantity, but it had done little to compensate him for the absence of those refinements he held most dear: the soft murmur of drawing-room conversation, the subtle fragrance of a well-chosen perfume, and the incomparable spectacle of a woman’s smile, artfully deployed. He had gone there a handsome boy of twenty-one with a fortune of modest proportions; he returned at twenty-four with a fortune of considerable size and a complexion the colour of a rather severe piece of burnt sienna. London, in the opalescent haze of a May afternoon, received him with its customary indifference, and he felt, with a little thrill of pleasure, that he was once more in a city where appearances were the only reality.

His first act was to repair to his club, to bathe his soul in the cool, hushed atmosphere of polished mahogany and discreet silence. His second was to send a note to the address of his fiancée, Miss Isobel Vance. His third, and most impulsive, was to call upon his dearest friend, the Honourable Crispin Falkland. It was at Falkland’s chambers in the Albany that the two great shocks of his return were administered to him, in that order, with the exquisite precision of a well-made play.

Falkland, a man whose aesthetic sensibilities were as refined as his waistcoats, greeted Evelyn with a cry of theatrical delight. “Evelyn! My dear fellow! Returned from the very jaws of the Antipodes! I was beginning to fear you had been devoured by some colonial monster, or, worse still, had developed a taste for colonial poetry.”

“One can endure anything for a sufficient sum, Crispin,” Evelyn replied, sinking into a chair upholstered in silk the colour of a faded rose. “Though I confess the poetry was a trial. But tell me of London. Tell me of Isobel. Has she pined away in my absence? Is she a lily drooping for want of her sun?”

Falkland paused. He adjusted a perfect gardenia in his buttonhole. “Isobel,” he said, with a curious delicacy, “is in the very best of health. She is, in fact, radiant. She is married.”

The words fell into the quiet room like a shard of glass. Evelyn felt no pain, only a profound sense of surprise, as if he had been told a rather clever but ultimately unbelievable joke. “Married?” he repeated. “To whom?”

“To me, old friend,” said Falkland, with a gentle, almost apologetic smile. “We were married last June.”

For a moment, the world seemed to tilt on its axis. Evelyn looked at his friend—at the handsome, sorrowful face, the perfectly cut clothes, the air of impeccable regret—and felt a sudden, sharp pang of something that was not jealousy, but a far more complex and interesting emotion. “You?” he said softly. “But I thought… I thought you were my friend.”

“I was. I am,” Falkland insisted. “But you were dead, Evelyn. At least, we believed you so. Your last letter was from some dreadful mining outpost, and then… silence. For over a year. The newspapers were full of shipwrecks, of men lost in the bush. Isobel was distraught. She wore black for you. I comforted her. It is a terrible thing, grief. It draws people together. One thing led to another. We were married in a moment of mutual despair.”

Evelyn listened to this recital with the detached air of a critic observing a scene of passing melodrama. He found it all rather well-constructed. “I see,” he said. “And where is Isobel now?”

“At our home in Belgrave Square. She will want to see you, Evelyn. She has never forgotten you. Indeed, she speaks of you constantly, with such tender sadness.”

“Then I shall call on her,” said Evelyn, rising. “It seems only polite.”

He found Isobel in a drawing-room that was a symphony of pale blues and silver, a room designed to flatter its occupant, and in this it succeeded magnificently. She rose to meet him, a vision in white silk, her hands outstretched, her eyes filled with a magnificent, shimmering sorrow.

“Evelyn! Oh, my dear, dear Evelyn! To see you alive! It is a miracle!”

“It is, rather,” he agreed, taking her cool, fragrant hands in his. “A miracle of resurrection, it seems, though I find my tomb has been rather promptly re-occupied.”

A faint blush coloured her cheeks. “You must not be cruel. We thought you were lost to us for ever. Crispin was so kind to me in my desolation. He is a good man.”

“He is an artist in everything, even in his friendships,” Evelyn murmured, his eyes scanning her face. He saw the subtle lines of contentment around her mouth, the healthy lustre in her hair, the lack of any real grief in her eyes, only the memory of a performance she had once given. “He tells me you pined for me.”

“Oh, I did! I did!” she cried, her voice trembling with a perfect, practiced emotion. “For a whole year, I was inconsolable. But time… time is a dreadful thing, Evelyn. It heals, but it also erases. And Crispin… he needed me. And I suppose I needed to be needed.”

Evelyn smiled. It was a slow, brilliant smile that did not reach his eyes. He saw it all now. The story of the shipwreck and the long grief was a charming fiction, a necessary stage-setting for the real drama. The truth, he suspected, was far simpler and far more interesting. She had grown bored. The long silence from a distant land had not been a source of torment, but of liberation. His memory, once a sacred flame, had become a rather tiresome duty. She had not been a lily drooping for the sun; she had been a hothouse orchid, requiring a more constant and immediate warmth. The thought was not bitter. It was, he realised with a surge of intellectual delight, profoundly flattering.

To be mourned so beautifully was a compliment to one’s memory. But to be forgotten so completely, to be so easily replaced—ah, that was a compliment to one’s reality. It meant he had been a tangible presence, a man whose absence was a genuine inconvenience, not a romantic abstraction. His death would have been a great sorrow; his survival was a mere complication.

“Do not trouble yourself to explain, Isobel,” he said, his voice smooth as polished marble. “I understand perfectly.”

“Do you?” she asked, her eyes searching his.

“Entirely,” he replied. “I have been away for three years. In that time, I have learned that there are only two great tragedies in life. One is not to get what one wants. The other is to get it. You, it seems, have avoided both.”

She looked puzzled. “I don’t understand.”

“It is of no consequence,” he said, releasing her hands. “The important thing is that we have all survived. You have a handsome husband and a beautiful home. I have a rather vulgar amount of money. And Crispin has a story he can tell for years. We are all, in our own way, enriched by the affair.”

He looked at the elegant woman before him, the delicate room, the life she had so deftly constructed in his absence. He felt a strange sense of gratitude towards her. She had saved him from the unbearable burden of a perfect, undying love. She had proved that life, unlike art, has a delightful and merciful capacity for revision. She had believed him dead, she said. It was a charming explanation. He had simply grown tiresome, he suspected. Both explanations, he decided, were equally flattering. One flattered his memory; the other, his manhood. And in London, in the 1890s, a man could ask for no greater compliment.
