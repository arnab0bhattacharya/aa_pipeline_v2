The intelligence reached Eleanor Ashworth not by design, but by the unguarded distress of her particular friend, Clara Fortescue. They were walking together in the shrubbery at Delaford Park, the seat of Clara's parents in Sussex, when the latter, after several vain attempts to speak with composure, yielded to the weight of her secret. Her brother, Mr. Frederick Fortescue, had lost a considerable sum at cards to a party of officers in Brighton, and was now compelled to seek his fortune in the West Indies before the week was out. Clara, in the midst of her tears, implored Eleanor to breathe not a syllable that might encourage him to linger in England, where his creditors grew daily more impatient. Eleanor gave the required promise, though her heart suggested a very different course of action; for she had privately esteemed Mr. Fortescue above all other young men of her acquaintance, and to be a passive instrument in his banishment was a refinement of suffering she had little expected to endure.

When the hour of the farewell dinner arrived, Eleanor descended to the drawing-room with a settled countenance, which she hoped might pass for tranquil. She found the family already assembled, and Mr. Fortescue among them, receiving the civilities of his relations with an air of easy cheerfulness that Eleanor knew not whether to admire or distrust. He spoke of his voyage as a matter of very little consequence, extolled the climate of the Indies, and lamented only that he must quit his sister's society; but Eleanor, who had studied his countenance with more attention than perhaps was prudent, thought she could perceive, beneath his assumed indifference, a degree of disquiet which his natural gaiety could not wholly conceal.

Mrs. Fortescue, a woman of more good sense than penetration, was chiefly occupied in superintending the arrangements of her table, and in assuring herself that the soup was sufficiently hot. She received Eleanor with her accustomed kindness, and expressed her sorrow at the occasion of their meeting, but with a philosophical resignation that rather surprised her guest. Mr. Fortescue, the elder, was a man of few words and fewer observations; he sat in his armchair, contemplating the fire with a steadiness that suggested either deep reflection or a very comfortable absence of thought.

At dinner, Eleanor was placed near Mr. Fortescue, a situation she had neither sought nor desired, and which she found peculiarly distressing. He addressed her with his usual pleasantry, remarked on the mildness of the season, and wondered whether the sea would be smooth at Portsmouth; but she could answer only in monosyllables, and was conscious that her voice faltered whenever she attempted more. Clara, seated on the other side of the table, watched them with an anxiety she could not entirely disguise, and Eleanor, meeting her friend's eye, felt all the force of the obligation that bound her to silence.

"I dare say, Miss Ashworth," said Mr. Fortescue, "you think me a very rash fellow to go so far upon so little encouragement. But a man of five-and-twenty must not be idle. My father's estate, though comfortable, is not so ample as to support two generations of younger sons, and I must shift for myself."

Eleanor, who had never heard him speak with such seriousness, could not but acknowledge the justice of his remark, though she thought she perceived in it something more than a consciousness of his secondary birth. She ventured to say that she hoped the climate would agree with him, and that he would find the employment he sought; but she added nothing further, and he, perhaps struck by her gravity, turned his attention to the lady on his other side, who was far better disposed to be amused.

When the ladies withdrew, Clara seized the first opportunity of speaking to Eleanor alone. She took her into a small alcove in the drawing-room, where a screen of India cloth partially concealed them from the rest of the party, and with much agitation begged to know whether her brother had said anything that might indicate a reluctance to leave England.

"He spoke of it as a matter of necessity," replied Eleanor, "but with a composure that appeared to me rather studied than natural."

"Studied!" cried Clara. "I am sure he is wretched. He has been wretched these three days, though he will not own it. But I know him, Eleanor. He is going because he thinks it right, and he would stay if anyone gave him the least encouragement; but I cannot, I must not, give it. You know why."

Eleanor did know why, and the knowledge sat heavy upon her conscience. She had no doubt that Mr. Fortescue, if he were aware of the esteem in which she held him, might be induced to remain; but she had promised Clara to say nothing, and Clara was her dearest friend. She contented herself, therefore, with observing that she hoped everything would turn out for the best, a sentiment so vague and so universally applicable that it could give offence to none, and comfort to nobody.

The gentlemen soon after entered the room, and Mr. Fortescue, advancing towards the tea-table with his customary ease, placed himself beside Eleanor, and entered into a desultory conversation upon the subject of books, a topic he knew to be agreeable to her. He spoke of a new novel he had been reading, and wondered whether she had seen it; but Eleanor, who could not command her thoughts sufficiently to remember the title, confessed she had not. He then remarked that he should leave behind him a small collection of volumes, and begged to know whether she would accept them, as a token of his regard.

Eleanor, though she could not refuse so civil an offer, accepted it with a hesitation that she could not wholly conceal, and which Mr. Fortescue, whose perceptions were quicker than his vanity was wont to acknowledge, did not fail to observe. He looked at her for a moment with an expression of surprise, and perhaps of something more, but immediately recovered himself, and turned the conversation to the general business of the evening.

The remainder of the visit passed in a manner which, to the rest of the company, appeared sufficiently agreeable. Mrs. Fortescue played at commerce with her nieces, Mr. Fortescue senior slept in his chair, and the younger Mr. Fortescue, after fulfilling the duties of his station with his usual address, withdrew early to prepare for his journey. Clara, in bidding him farewell, wept abundantly, and was comforted by his assurances of a speedy return; but Eleanor, who could not trust herself to speak, contented herself with a silent pressure of the hand, which he returned with a degree of warmth that she was left to interpret as she could.

When he was gone, and the house was quiet, Eleanor retired to her chamber, and sitting down by the window, gave way to those reflections which the business of the evening had compelled her to suppress. She thought of Mr. Fortescue, of his cheerfulness, his concealed distress, and his impending voyage; she thought of Clara, whose affection for her brother was mingled with a prudence that Eleanor could not but condemn, though she had promised to respect it; and she thought of herself, of the sacrifice she had made, and of the uncertainty of that future in which alone she could hope for any return. She was not, by nature, given to melancholy, and she had too much good sense to indulge it long; but she could not but feel that she had acted nobly, and that nobility, however satisfactory to one's self-esteem, is but a cold consolation when it is unobserved.

In the morning, she took leave of Clara, who thanked her with much warmth for her discretion, and assured her that she had acted as a true friend. Eleanor accepted the praise in silence, but she could not help wondering, as her carriage drove away from Delaford Park, whether true friendship did not sometimes require a courage which those who claim its name are not always willing to practise. The fields of Sussex, bright with the morning sun, presented a scene of tranquil beauty which she was little disposed to enjoy; and it was not until she had travelled some miles, and the image of Mr. Fortescue began to fade into the general confusion of her thoughts, that she recovered sufficiently to observe that the day was fine, and that the world, notwithstanding her private sorrow, was still going on very much as usual.
