It was upon a night of such dreary and unexampled tempest, that the vessel, which bore within its ribs the last remnant of a ruined people, arrived within sight of the harbour of Portworthy. The wind, which howled like a thing possessed of some malignant and eternal grief, drove the rain before it in vast, cold sheets, and the waves of the channel were thrown up in mountains of black water, that seemed to have no end nor beginning, but to be the very substance of the world’s despair. Upon the cliffs, where the great lighthouse cast its futile beam into the maelstrom, a small assembly of townsfolk had gathered, their faces pale and their hearts contracted with a fear that was older than reason, a fear of the unknown, of the invisible pestilence that had consumed the continent from which these fugitives came. They saw the ship—a great, dark shape that rose and fell with a sickening motion—and they felt not pity, but a cold and adamantine resolve to preserve themselves, to seal their shores against the misery that the sea had delivered to their door.

Among this assembly stood a young man, whose countenance, though marked by the same storm of weather, was yet more profoundly agitated by a tempest of the soul. His name was Julian Alistair, a physician newly come to practise in the town, and he had within him that ardour of youth which believes it can single-handedly turn back the tides of sorrow and injustice. He saw the ship not as a threat, but as a vessel of human suffering, and the faces that he could but dimly imagine upon its decks called to his own heart with a voice more powerful than the gale. “Friends,” he cried, his voice almost snatched away by the wind, “what is this fear that has turned you to stone? Are we a people of iron, that we should shut our gates upon the wretched? That ship carries not a plague, but our own humanity, cast upon the waters! To refuse them is to refuse the very image of ourselves in our hour of need; it is a crime against God and against the sanctity of our own hearts!” But his impassioned appeal was met with grim, averted faces. The Mayor, a stout man named Silas Croft, whose mind was a fortress of practicalities, laid a heavy hand upon his shoulder. “Dr. Alistair,” he said, his tone one of weary condescension, “your compassion does you credit, but it is a luxury we cannot afford. We must think of our own children, our own homes. One infected soul brought ashore could mean the death of us all.”

The matter, thus contested in the tempest, was brought to the council chamber, a room of dark, carved oak and solemn portraits, where the air was thick with the smoke of anxious pipes and the weight of an impossible decision. The storm rattled the leaded panes of the windows, as if some giant hand were demanding admittance, and the lamps flickered, casting long, dancing shadows that seemed to mock the gravity of the men who sat within. Through the long hours of the night, the debate raged, a lesser storm than that which beat upon the shore, but no less violent in its passions. There were men of commerce, who spoke of trade and the fear of a quarantine that would strangle their livelihood; there were men of property, who spoke of the sanctity of their families; and there was Julian Alistair, who stood before them, his face pale with a righteous and desperate energy, and pleaded for a morality that transcended their narrow self-interest.

He spoke of the physician’s oath, a sacred vow to preserve life, not to weigh it in the scales of convenience. He spoke of the terror and agony that must fill the hearts of those upon the water, who had seen their world consumed by fire and pestilence, and who now found the last shore of hope barred against them. “What are we,” he demanded, his voice rising in a crescendo of feeling, “if not our capacity for mercy? What is our safety worth, if it is purchased at the price of our souls? Let us not, in our terror, become the very monsters that we fear. Let us open our harbour, not as a surrender to a disease, but as a triumph of our humanity! We can build a hospital upon the point; we can tend to them with all the skill and science we possess. But to leave them to the sea, to the slow death of starvation and despair—that is a sickness of the spirit for which there is no cure, a plague that will consume this town more surely than any fever!” His words hung in the air, eloquent and terrible, and for a moment, he saw a flicker of doubt in the hardened eyes of his opponents. But it was a moment only. Fear, that most ancient and powerful of tyrants, reasserted its dominion. Croft shook his head, his face a mask of grim finality. “Your sentiment is a fire that would burn us all, Doctor. The council has decided. The harbour is closed.”

As the grey and mournful light of dawn began to break, struggling through the storm-wracked clouds, Julian Alistair found himself once more upon the cliffs. The council’s decision was a physical weight upon him, a cold stone in his breast. The ship still laboured in the swell, a dark testament to his failure. The townspeople had gone to their beds, their consciences perhaps troubled, but their lives preserved, and he was left alone with the ghosts of the dying and the unyielding roar of the sea. He looked upon the merciless water, and felt a desolation so profound that it seemed to match the very sublime and terrible grandeur of the scene before him. He, who had come to heal, was powerless to prevent this vast and slow-moving tragedy. His compassion, his reason, his very soul, had been weighed and found wanting by the fearful calculus of men. And in that moment of utter solitude and anguish, he knew that the true pestilence was not upon that ship, but had long ago taken root upon the shore, in the hardened chambers of the human heart. The storm would pass, the waves would calm, but the ruin wrought by this night’s fear would endure, a silent and invisible shipwreck from which they might never recover.
