Miss Eliza Ashworth had long been of the opinion that vanity was the most contemptible of all female failings, and she was pleased to observe in herself no such tendency. Her beauty, though universally acknowledged in the parish of Hartwell, was a subject upon which she never dwelt; she considered it a mere accident of nature, of no more real consequence than the colour of a hedge or the shape of a stone. Her mind, she flattered herself, was formed for more substantial pursuits—for the quiet observation of character, for the improvement of her understanding, and for the practice of those virtues which do not require an audience. It was with some surprise, therefore, and a feeling she would have termed only mild curiosity, that she first noticed the particular attentions of Mr. Fortescue.

This gentleman, a cousin of the squire at the neighbouring estate of Ashcombe, had arrived in Hampshire for the autumn, bringing with him an air of the metropolis that was at once dazzling and slightly absurd in the quiet surroundings of Hartwell. His conversation was polished, his dress impeccable, and his opinions on every subject, from the latest play in London to the proper management of a rose garden, were delivered with an easy confidence that commanded assent. When his eyes, of a very fine grey, began to rest upon Eliza with a frequency that the neighbourhood could not fail to remark, her mother, Mrs. Ashworth, was in raptures, while her younger sister, Sophia, declared it to be the most romantic thing imaginable. Eliza, for her part, maintained a placid composure. To be so distinguished by a man of Mr. Fortescue’s taste and discernment was, she admitted, a pleasing circumstance; it spoke well of her own qualities of mind that he, who had seen so much of the world, should find in her a companion worthy of his attention. She was careful to ascribe his preference to her conversation and good sense, never for a moment allowing that her face might have any share in the matter.

His courtship, if such it could be called, was conducted with an elegant persistence. He sought her opinion on books, praised the clarity of her arguments, and walked with her in all weathers, seemingly content with no other society. Eliza found herself looking forward to these encounters with an eagerness that she attributed to the intellectual stimulation they provided. Mr. Fortescue, she reasoned, was the first person in Hartwell who treated her not as a pretty girl to be flattered, but as a rational being to be engaged. She began to imagine a future of shared ideas and mutual respect, a partnership of minds that would be far superior to the common run of marriages. Her good sense, she believed, had guided her to a man who valued it above all else. It was a comfortable, a very comfortable, reflection.

The illusion was shattered on a Tuesday afternoon. Eliza had gone to the village lending library, a small, stuffy room above the baker’s shop, in search of a new volume of essays. As she was browsing, two young men, acquaintances of Mr. Fortescue from Ashcombe, entered the room, their conversation dying the instant they saw her. They bowed with a formality that struck her as strange. As she left, the door closing softly behind her, a snatch of their renewed conversation, carried on a draught from the open window, reached her ears. One voice, belonging to a Mr. Beauclerk, was unmistakable.

“It is a bet, then?”
“A handsome one,” came the reply, which she knew with a sickening certainty to be Mr. Fortescue’s. “Fifty guineas that I make the little country paragon declare herself in love with me before Michaelmas. She is so very proud of her indifference, you see. It will be a triumph to see it undone.”

Eliza did not run, nor did she faint. She walked home with a perfectly steady step, her mind a sudden, desolate blank. The words echoed in the silence of her thoughts, each one a small, sharp instrument of humiliation. *Little country paragon.* *A triumph.* *Undone.* The abstract virtues she had so cherished—her good sense, her rational superiority, her freedom from vanity—were suddenly revealed to be not virtues at all, but the very instruments of her own folly. She had not been admired for her mind; she had been made a fool of for it. Her supposed indifference had been the prize in a vulgar wager, and her careful self-regard the butt of a fashionable joke. The cost of her good sense, it seemed, was fifty guineas.

When Mr. Fortescue called the next day, he found her not in the garden, where he had expected, but in the parlour, seated with her needlework. She did not rise to greet him. Her mother and sister, sensing an atmosphere of unusual gravity, quickly found excuses to leave the room. Mr. Fortescue, his smile as confident as ever, began some light remark about the weather, but Eliza cut him short.

“Sir,” she said, her voice quiet but devoid of all warmth, “I believe you are in possession of fifty guineas that you did not have before Michaelmas. I hope you will find some use for it.” She did not look at him, but applied her needle to the linen with a precision that was its own form of contempt.

A deep, unbecoming flush spread over his handsome face. The polished urbanity fell away, leaving behind a young man, discomfited and exposed. “Miss Ashworth,” he stammered, “I can explain… it was a foolish jest, a thing of no meaning—”

“I am sure it was,” she interrupted, still not looking up. “To a man of your world, the feelings of a ‘little country paragon’ can be of no meaning at all. The jest, however, has been at my expense, and I must beg the favour of your absence in future.”

He had the grace not to argue. After a moment of thick silence, he bowed stiffly and quit the room, and the house, and, as it proved, the neighbourhood. Eliza remained seated, the needlework forgotten in her lap. She felt no triumph in her victory, only a profound and chastening emptiness. Her good sense had returned to her, but it felt like a cold and unwelcome companion. She had learned its worth, and its price, and found that she had paid for it with a part of her own heart she had not known was in the bargain. The world, she now saw with a clarity that was both painful and necessary, was a more complex and less rational place than she had ever supposed, and a woman’s good sense was best employed not in despising vanity, but in guarding against the flattery that so artfully mimicked esteem.
