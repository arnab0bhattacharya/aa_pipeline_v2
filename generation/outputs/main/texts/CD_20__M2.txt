The room in which young Arthur Pumblechoon had established his poetic headquarters was not, strictly speaking, a room, but rather a closet that had once been a pantry and was now a tomb of dust and despair. It occupied the very top floor of a leaning, groaning structure in the Borough, a place where the air was thick with the scent of boiled cabbage, stale beer, and the general melancholy of the human condition. Arthur sat upon a stool that wobbled with the erratic confidence of a drunkard, his face pale as a sheet of unbleached linen, his eyes wide with the fever of ambition, and his stomach hollow with the gnawing hunger of a man who had not eaten since the previous Tuesday. He was waiting for inspiration. It had not arrived. It had not even sent a forward scout. It was, in fact, behaving with the most insulting and deliberate indifference, much like a debtor who has vanished into the countryside with your last shilling.

"Arthur!" cried Mrs. Crump, the landlady, bursting through the door with the energy of a steam-engine and the grace of a falling anvil. She was a woman of formidable girth, dressed in a calico gown that seemed to be straining at its seams for the same reason that Arthur’s boots were straining at his toes. "Arthur, you idle, lazy, good-for-nothing creature! Do you think the rent is going to pay itself? Do you think the candles are going to light themselves? Do you think the mice are going to scrub the floor?"

"I am in the midst of a sublime conception, Mrs. Crump," Arthur replied, in a voice so thin and reedy that it might have been produced by a straw in a hurricane. "I am weaving the tapestry of the soul."

"The tapestry of the soul," repeated Mrs. Crump, with a sneer that could have peeled the varnish off the door. "And the tapestry of the rent? That is a tapestry I shall weave around your neck if you do not produce sixpence before the hour strikes eight. I have three other lodgers who pay their way in coin of the realm, not in rhyming couplets and philosophical sighs. Out with it, or out you go!"

She vanished, leaving behind a trail of scent that was indistinguishable from the smell of a chandler’s shop on a hot day. Arthur sighed, a long, shuddering exhalation that seemed to deflate his entire chest cavity. He was a poet. He was a genius. He was a star. Or so he believed. The fact that his star had not yet risen, and that his genius was currently being assessed by a woman who measured worth in shillings and pence, did not deter him. He was above such mundane considerations. He was destined for the immortality of the Parnassus, not the humility of the penny-plain.

However, his destiny was about to be interrupted by a more pressing calamity. The door, which Mrs. Crump had left ajar in her fury, creaked open to admit a figure of such terrifying dignity and aggressive presence that Arthur felt his knees buckle. It was Mr. Silas Vane, a creditor of the most ancient and implacable variety, who wore a coat of black wool so tight that it made him look as if he were perpetually being squeezed in a vice, and a wig so high that it seemed to be defying the laws of gravity.

"Mr. Pumblechoon," said Mr. Vane, his voice a low rumble like thunder in a distant valley. "I am informed that you are engaged in the noble art of verse. A noble art, indeed. But is it more noble than the payment of a debt? I have waited three days. I have waited three nights. I have waited with a patience that is a virtue, but even virtue has its limits, sir. I am here to collect the three pounds, four shillings, and sixpence that you owe me for the loan I extended to you when you were too proud to ask for help from your own family."

"I cannot pay you now, Mr. Vane," said Arthur, standing up with a dignity that was more aspirational than actual. "The Muse is silent. The spirit is in travail. I shall pay you when the work is done. When my name is known from here to the ends of the earth, I shall pay you with interest."

"Interest!" cried Mr. Vane, his face turning the color of a beetroot. "Interest! You will pay me in sonnets! I will accept no less than gold! You are a fool, Mr. Pumblechoon. A poet is a man who writes verses to please himself and to starve his creditors. You are no exception. I shall return tomorrow. And the day after. And the day after that. I am not a man who gives up. I am a man who persists. You will see me at this door until the day I am buried, and even then, I shall haunt you in my dreams until the last penny is paid."

With a bow that was stiff with menace, Mr. Vane withdrew, leaving Arthur to tremble in the aftermath of his visit. Arthur collapsed back onto his wobbly stool, his head in his hands. He was trapped. He was a prisoner of his own ambition, locked in a room with a landlord who wanted rent, a creditor who wanted gold, and a soul that wanted to fly but had no wings.

It was at this moment of supreme despair that a voice, clear and musical as a silver bell, floated up the stairs.

"Is anyone in there? I am looking for a new hatband. Or perhaps a pair of gloves. Or perhaps a little poetry? I am open to suggestions."

Arthur looked up. Standing in the doorway was a young woman of perhaps twenty years, dressed in a simple but neat gown of blue muslin. She had a face of such fresh and intelligent charm that it seemed to cast a light into the dusty room. It was Miss Elara Finch, the glove-seller from the shop across the street, who had a reputation for being as sensible as she was pretty, and as pretty as she was sensible.

"Miss Finch," said Arthur, recovering his composure with a swiftness that was almost unnatural. "I am... I am in a state of great distress."

"Distress is a common visitor in this house," said Miss Finch, stepping inside and closing the door behind her. She did not seem to mind the dust, or the smell, or the general state of disrepair. She looked at Arthur with eyes that were sharp and discerning. "You are the poet, are you not? The one who lives in the cloud?"

"In a manner of speaking," said Arthur, feeling a sudden warmth in his cheeks. "But the cloud is heavy. The rain is falling. And the thunder is close at hand."

"Mr. Vane," said Miss Finch, nodding towards the door. "I saw him. He looks like a storm cloud himself. But tell me, Mr. Pumblechoon, what is the matter with your poetry? Is it bad?"

"It is not bad," said Arthur, with a sudden flash of indignation. "It is ahead of its time. The world is not ready for it. The world is too materialistic. Too concerned with money and rent and gloves. It lacks the spiritual depth to appreciate the profound truths that I have to offer."

Miss Finch smiled, a small, polite smile that did not quite reach her eyes. "I sell gloves, Mr. Pumblechoon. I sell them to women who want to keep their hands warm and their fingers clean. It is a simple trade. It does not require spiritual depth. It requires only a steady hand and a clean conscience. And I find that I am more respected for my gloves than you are for your verses. My customers pay me. Your customers do not. My gloves fit. Your verses do not fit the times. Is that not a fact?"

Arthur opened his mouth to reply, but no words came out. He looked at Miss Finch, and for the first time in his life, he felt the weight of her judgment. It was not the judgment of a creditor, nor the judgment of a landlady. It was the judgment of a woman who saw the world clearly, and who saw him clearly. And what she saw was not a genius, but a man who was hiding behind his ambition.

"You are wrong," he said, but his voice was low. "You are too practical. You do not understand the beauty of the ideal."

"I understand the beauty of a full stomach and a paid debt," said Miss Finch, picking up a glove from her basket and examining it. "And I understand that if you do not find a way to make your poetry pay its way, you will end up in a debtor’s prison. And I do not think that is a very poetic ending. Do you?"

Arthur looked at the glove in her hand. It was a simple thing, made of soft leather, dyed a deep, rich brown. It was practical. It was useful. It was beautiful in its own way. And for the first time, Arthur wondered if he had been chasing a phantom, and if the real beauty was not in the clouds, but on the earth.

"I do not know," he said softly. "I do not know."

Miss Finch tucked the glove back into her basket. "Well, you have until eight o’clock to find out. And then you will have to pay Mrs. Crump. Good day, Mr. Pumblechoon."

She turned and left, her footsteps light on the creaking stairs. Arthur sat alone in the dusty room, the silence pressing in on him. He looked at the window, where the London sky was turning a dull, leaden gray. He looked at his hands, which were shaking. He looked at the floor, which was covered in dust. And for the first time, he did not wait for the Muse. He picked up his pen. He did not write a poem. He wrote a letter. It was to Mr. Vane. It was an apology. It was an admission. It was a beginning.
