The knocker fell once, twice, with the apologetic briskness of youth, and Mrs. Ashby, who had been sitting since three o’clock in the chair that had been Gerald’s, watching the light climb the wall from the escritoire to the portrait of the Italian poet—how many afternoons had she watched it climb, and the poet’s face emerge from shadow as though he had been waiting all day to speak, and then say nothing—did not rise at once. Let him knock again. Let him feel the weight of the brass, the cold of it, the importance of it; for a young man with a notebook in his pocket is a vessel of expectation, and expectation, she had long ago discovered, is the only thing that improves with keeping.

“Mrs. Ashby?” He was tall, and the hall was dark, and his hat was in his hand before the door was half open, so that he seemed to be offering it to her, a grey felt offering, and behind him the King’s Road ran its course of barrows and omnibuses and women with their hair in pins, all of it streaming past the fanlight in a brown and jolting light. “Mr. Pell. From the *Chronicle*. You received my letter.”

“I receive a great many letters,” said Mrs. Ashby, and gave him her hand, which was small and dry and ringed, and let him into the drawing-room where the fire had sunk to a red whisper and the smell of hyacinths was almost too much, almost a thing you could hold against him. “Sit there. No—there. The chair with the arms. It was Gerald’s, and he would have detested you, so you must be comfortable in it.”

The young man laughed, not knowing whether to, and sat, and drew out his notebook, and Mrs. Ashby, settling back into her own chair, felt the old pleasure rise in her like water in a lock—the pleasure of the stage before the curtain, of the audience come to be told, of the truth that was never quite the truth but something better, something with a shape to it.

“You want,” she said, “the circle. The Thursdays. The lamps and the talk and the great men with their collars undone.”

“If you would be so good.”

“There were no great men,” said Mrs. Ashby. “There was only Gerald, who was a great man, and the others, who came to see him. And there was the green lamp—that one, there, with the beads—and it was green because Gerald said the light of ordinary lamps was the light of offices and cemeteries, and he could not write under it. So we had the green lamp, and the room was never bright, and people said things in it they would not have said in the daylight. That is all a salon is, Mr. Pell. A room where the light is low enough for the truth to seem a kind of gossip.”

The young man’s pencil moved. She watched it move, and thought of the small quick fish in the aquarium at Brighton, and of Gerald’s hand on her wrist the first evening they met, and how he had said, *You will do very well; you have the gift of the oblique*—which she had never understood and never forgotten.

“Mr. Trelawny came every week,” she went on. “You will have read his *Sonnets of the Iron Age*.”

“Of course.”

“He was a little man, and he wore a sealskin coat in all weathers, and he had a way of standing with his back to the fire so that the seam of his trousers scorched, and he never noticed, and the smell of scorching was the smell of poetry to us. He was in love with me for eleven years. He wrote me a poem for every Thursday, and on the twelfth year he married a woman in Weymouth and was very happy, and sent me a ham at Christmas.”

“And you kept the poems?”

“I burned them,” said Mrs. Ashby, “in the grate, one by one, the winter Gerald died. They were not good poems. But I have sometimes thought the burning of them was the best thing I ever did for literature.”

She had not burned them. They were in the bureau, tied with a bootlace, and sometimes on sleepless nights she read them and laughed and was sorry. But the young man was writing now, his forehead pink with concentration, and she felt a tenderness for him, for his credulity, for the whole warm ignorant machinery of him. He would print it. Somewhere in the *Chronicle*, between the shipping news and the advertisements for corsets, there would be the sealskin coat and the ham at Christmas, and the world would believe it, and the world would be right to believe it, for what is history but the story that is best shaped?

“And the poet from Trieste,” she said. “You will want the poet from Trieste.”

“I have heard—”

“You have heard he was my lover. You have heard wrong, but you may put it in if you like. He came to us with a letter from a professor in Bologna, and he could not speak English, and Gerald could not speak Italian, so they sat all one evening and drank and said nothing, and by the end of it they were closer than any two men who have ever talked. The poet wept when he left. He kissed my hand and called me *signora*—which is what one calls a woman one has not understood—and he went back to Trieste and wrote a book about us all, and in the book I am a widow with a parrot, and Gerald is a cardinal. It sold eleven copies.”

“Did you mind?”

“I was a widow with a parrot,” said Mrs. Ashby. “For a week I was. It is astonishing how quickly one becomes what one has been described as being. That is the danger of your trade, Mr. Pell. You will describe me, and I shall become it, and the woman who sat in this chair and watched the light climb the wall will be gone, and no one will ever know she was here.”

The pencil stopped. He looked up, and she saw that he was not so young after all, that there was a small line between his brows, and that he was going to say something true, and she did not want him to say something true.

“What was he like?” the young man asked. “Gerald. Not the great man. The man who could not write under an ordinary lamp.”

The fire settled. Somewhere below, in the area, a pail was set down with a clang that came up through the floor and into the chairs and into their bones. Mrs. Ashby looked at the poet on the wall, who had emerged now entirely from the shadow, and who had been dead two hundred years and had never in his life been faithful to anyone.

“He was cruel,” she said, “on Tuesdays. He could not help it. On Tuesdays the words would not come, and he would walk up and down this room and say things that were not meant, and I would go and stand in the hall by the coats—there is a particular smell in a hall where many people have left their coats—and I would count the umbrellas, and by the time I had counted them he would be quiet. That is all. That is the whole of the great secret of the great man. He was cruel on Tuesdays, and I counted the umbrellas.”

The young man wrote it down. She saw him write it down, and she saw the words take their place in his mind, and she knew that by Friday they would be in print, and that the umbrellas would become a symbol, and the symbol would become a story, and the story would become the truth, and she would have done it herself, with her own hands, out of the old perverse love of the oblique.

“And now,” she said, “you must go, because at half past four the light leaves the poet’s face, and I do not like to be in the room when it goes. It is a small thing,” she added, rising, and giving him her hand again, “but one keeps one’s small things, Mr. Pell, when the great ones have gone.”

He thanked her at the door. The King’s Road was all blue now, and the lamps were coming on, one by one, down the long perspective, like a sentence being written. She watched him cross, and turn, and lift his hat—to her? to the house? to the story?—and then she shut the door, and stood a moment in the dark hall among the coats, and counted them, out of habit, out of love, out of the old need to make a shape against the coming of the night.
