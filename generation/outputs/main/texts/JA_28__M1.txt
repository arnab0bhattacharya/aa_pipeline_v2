Mr. Edmund Ashworth had long been of the opinion that the world was divided into two sorts of people: those who possessed a discerning intellect, and those who were destined to be the instruments of it. To the first, and smaller, category he assigned himself; to the second, a great many of his acquaintance. This conviction, fostered by a solitary education and a mind that preferred the company of books to that of men, gave him a serene confidence in his own judgement, and a particular susceptibility to the one form of flattery he could not detect: the flattery of having his own opinions returned to him with a delicate and admiring air.

It was this weakness that made him so agreeable a companion for Miss Georgiana Sinclair, who arrived at Ashworth Grange in the autumn of 1812 to stay with her cousins, the Fairfaxes. Miss Sinclair was possessed of a pleasing countenance, a gentle voice, and an expression of such intelligent deference that a man of Mr. Ashworth’s temperament was disposed to think her a prodigy of good sense within the first quarter of an hour. In conversation, she did not so much offer her own thoughts as anticipate his, her brow furrowing in concentration before she would say, in a tone of thoughtful discovery, “I believe you are right, Mr. Ashworth. I had not seen it in that light, but now that you have explained it, it appears to me the only rational conclusion.”

Such a compliment, so artfully constructed as to appear a mere by-product of her own reasoning, was irresistible. Edmund, who had hitherto considered society a tedious exercise in correcting the errors of others, found in Miss Sinclair a disciple of the most gratifying kind. He spoke on matters of philosophy, of politics, of the proper conduct of a gentleman’s estate, and found in her not an opponent to be vanquished, but a mind to be illuminated. He believed he was imparting wisdom; she knew she was confirming a prejudice.

His sister, Miss Ashworth, observed this burgeoning intimacy with a growing sense of alarm. Miss Ashworth was a young woman of few illusions, having made a thorough study of her brother’s character and found it sound in its principles, but lamentably insecure in its vanity. She saw Miss Sinclair’s conduct for what it was: a performance, as polished and as hollow as a stage bauble. The young lady’s agreement was never spontaneous; it was always preceded by a moment of considered thought, a slight tilt of the head that invited her interlocutor to believe he was witnessing the birth of a new conviction. Her praise was never for his character, but for his understanding, which is to say, for the opinions he already held.

One evening, as the party sat in the drawing-room, Mr. Fairfax raised the subject of a local magistrate, a Mr. Davies, whose methods were considered by some to be too lenient. Edmund, who had a high idea of his own judicial capacities, pronounced with great authority that leniency was a failing in a magistrate, that justice must be tempered not with mercy, but with an inflexible adherence to principle.

Miss Sinclair, who had been listening with her eyes fixed on the carpet as if in deep contemplation, now lifted them to his face. “It is a difficult distinction to make,” she said softly, “and one that lesser minds would surely confuse. They would speak of compassion, but you speak of the higher duty of order. It is a noble, if severe, position.”

Edmund coloured with pleasure. “You apprehend it precisely, Miss Sinclair. Mercy to the individual is often an injustice to the whole.”

Miss Ashworth, seeing her brother preen under this admiring gaze, felt the moment had come for a gentle, and perhaps futile, intervention. “And yet,” she said, turning to Miss Sinclair with a placid smile, “I wonder if you have found Mr. Davies’s methods to be as ineffective as my brother supposes? I believe the parish has been unusually quiet of late.”

It was a simple question, calculated to introduce a fact into a conversation governed by theory. Miss Sinclair’s composure was momentarily disturbed. A flicker of annoyance passed through her eyes before being replaced by her customary look of thoughtful deference. “I am sure you are better informed on such local matters than I can be, Miss Ashworth. My opinion is formed entirely from Mr. Ashworth’s superior reasoning on the subject.”

The masterstroke of the reply was not lost on Miss Ashworth. In a single sentence, Miss Sinclair had acknowledged her own ignorance, flattered Edmund’s intellect once more, and subtly dismissed the inconvenient fact as a trifle beneath their discussion. Edmund, who had been about to address the point of fact, was now diverted back to the point of principle.

“Precisely so,” he declared, looking at Miss Sinclair with renewed fondness. “The particularities of the case are of no consequence when the general principle is at stake. My sister, I am afraid, is apt to be distracted by small details.”

Miss Ashworth said no more. She saw that any attempt to expose the lady’s methods would be interpreted as a petty jealousy, an attack not on Miss Sinclair’s character, but on her brother’s judgement.
