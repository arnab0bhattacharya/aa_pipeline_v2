I had been seated in the private parlour which my friend Mr. Maxwell Grier was in the habit of using as a consulting-room, engaged in sorting a portfolio of botanical sketches, when the door was opened by Mrs. Cribb, the landlady of the establishment, to announce a visitor. It was already the third week of November, and a dismal rain was beating against the window-panes, casting a pallor over the afternoon which I found thoroughly disagreeable to my spirits.

The man who entered was a porter from the Great Northern Railway Hotel, a sturdy fellow in a drab uniform, bearing under his arm a leather portmanteau of considerable age. His face was flushed, whether from the weather or from his own agitation, and he stood for a moment wiping his boots upon the mat with a nervous deliberation which showed that he was uncertain how to broach his business.

"You are Mr. Grier, sir?" he asked, turning his cap in his hands.

Grier had been standing at the window, gazing out at the dreary procession of hansoms in the street below. He turned at the sound of the porter's voice, and I observed that alert, expectant expression which I had learned to recognize as the prelude to some interesting inquiry. He was a man of remarkable thinness, with a sharp, aquiline profile and grey eyes that possessed an extraordinary power of concentrating themselves upon the smallest object.

"I am Grier," he said. "What is your business, my man?"

"It's about a portmanteau, sir," the porter replied, setting the bag upon the table. "Left in Room 214 at our hotel, and never claimed. The gentleman what occupied the room cleared out in a rare hurry early this morning, and my manager sent me round with the luggage, having heard that you were the one to look into such matters."

Grier drew up a chair and sat down, resting his elbows upon his knees and his chin upon his clasped hands. "Let us hear the particulars," he said. "What was the gentleman's name, and how long had he been staying at the hotel?"

"He registered as Mr. Joseph Lethbridge, of 42 Mercer Street, Brighton," the porter answered. "He arrived on Tuesday evening, the eighteenth, and took Room 214, which is a single apartment on the second floor looking out upon the goods yard. He was a quiet sort of gentleman, tallish, with a dark beard going grey, dressed in a brown tweed suit. We didn't see much of him. He took his meals in the coffee-room regular enough, but spoke to nobody except to ask for matches on the Wednesday evening."

"And he left this morning?"

"Left at half-past six, sir. The night clerk saw him go. He did not settle his bill, nor send for a cab, but walked out into the street with nothing but a walking-stick and a small handbag. The chambermaid went up to do the room at eight and found this portmanteau under the bed, locked. She brought it down to the office. The manager tried the address in Brighton, but there is no such number as 42 upon Mercer Street, nor any person of the name of Lethbridge in the directory."

Grier nodded and drew the portmanteau towards him. It was a Gladstone bag of cowhide, much scuffed at the corners, with two brass clasps and a central lock. He examined the exterior for some minutes, turning it over in his long, delicate fingers and inspecting the straps and hinges with close attention. Then he produced from his waistcoat pocket a small bunch of skeleton keys, selected one, and inserted it into the lock. The clasp yielded at once, and the bag fell open.

"Empty, by Jove!" I exclaimed, leaning forward.

"Not entirely, Dr. Fallow," said Grier, with a faint smile. "You see, but you do not observe. There are three articles in this bag, and each of them is more eloquent than a dozen witnesses."

He laid the contents upon the table one by one. The first was a pair of ladies' kid gloves, white in colour, of a size to fit a small hand, slightly soiled at the fingertips. The second was a five-chambered Adams revolver, with a smell of recent discharge about it; and upon examining the cylinder, I perceived that one cartridge had been fired. The third was a Bradshaw railway timetable for the current month, folded open at a page and marked with a pencil line beneath a single entry.

Grier took up the gloves first. He turned them inside out, smelled them, and held them up to the light. "A lady's gloves, size six and a half, bought at a good shop, for the stitching is of the finest quality. They have been worn, but not recently, for the kid has dried stiff. More telling, however, is the mark upon the third finger of the left glove." He pointed to a faint indentation in the leather. "You will perceive a groove, such as is made by the continuous wearing of a wedding-ring. So we have a married woman, or a woman who has been married. The gloves are not new, yet they are not the gentleman's. Therefore they belonged to someone in whom he took a particular interest—someone whose gloves he carried away and preserved."

He laid the gloves aside and took up the revolver. He broke the action and examined the chamber with a magnifying glass. "Fired within the last forty-eight hours," said he. "The smell of the powder lingers, and there is a faint crust upon the nipple. One chamber only. I think we may exclude the notion of target practice. A single shot, fired in earnest, and the weapon then locked away in a portmanteau and abandoned."

"Abandoned or forgotten?" I asked.

"Forgotten, my dear Fallow, is a word I do not employ. This bag was placed under the bed with deliberate care. The gentleman intended to recover it. That he did not do so can only mean that his plans were altered by circumstances beyond his control, or that he found it necessary to separate himself from these articles with great speed."

He turned at last to the timetable. The pencil line was drawn beneath the 7:10 morning train from King's Cross to York, with a small cross beside the name of the town of Thirsk, which lies some twenty-three miles beyond York upon the North Eastern line.

"There is our destination," said Grier, tapping the page. "Thirsk. Our gentleman left the hotel at half-past six, which gave him precisely forty minutes to reach King's Cross and purchase a ticket. I think we may be tolerably certain that he caught that train. But why Thirsk? And why the false address in Brighton?"

He sat for a moment in silence, his brows drawn together. Then he turned to the porter. "You say he was tall, with a dark beard going grey, dressed in brown tweed. Did you notice his hands?"

"I didn't, sir. But the night clerk, Jenkins, said he had a queer sort of limp, as though one leg was shorter than the other."

Grier sprang to his feet. "A limp! That is the keystone of the whole matter. Listen, porter, and I will tell you what occurred. This man is not Mr. Lethbridge of Brighton, for no such person exists. He is someone who has been living under a false name for a purpose which I need not yet specify. On Tuesday evening he arrived in London carrying this portmanteau, which contained the evidence of a crime already committed or about to be committed. The lady's gloves belonged to his wife, or to a woman connected with him. The revolver has been fired. The timetable shows his intended line of flight. He registered under a false address because he dared not leave a real clue to his identity."

"But why leave the bag, sir?" asked the porter.

"Because he was warned. Someone came to the hotel during the night, or left a message for him, which convinced him that his room was watched and his movements known. In that moment of alarm, he abandoned everything and fled with only what he could carry in his hand. He dared not risk being stopped with a revolver and a woman's gloves upon his person. The bag was his sacrifice to necessity."

Grier took up his hat and coat. "I shall wire to the station-master at Thirsk," he said, "and I shall go myself by the afternoon express. Fallow, if your practice permits, I should be glad of your company. There is more in this affair than appears upon the surface, and I do not care to walk into it without a witness."

I closed my portfolio and rose. The rain had ceased, and a pale wintry light was breaking through the clouds over the rooftops. Grier paused at the door and turned to the porter, who stood staring with his mouth open.

"One thing more," said he. "The night clerk who saw him leave. Ask him whether the gentleman carried his stick in his right hand or his left. It may seem a trifle to you, but upon it may hang the direction of our entire inquiry."

The porter promised to make the inquiry, and withdrew. Grier turned to me with that quick, eager light in his eyes which I had learned to associate with the pursuit of a difficult problem.

"Come, Fallow," said he. "We have a train to catch, and a gentleman with a limp to overtake."
