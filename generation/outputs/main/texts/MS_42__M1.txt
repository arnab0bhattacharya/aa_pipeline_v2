I must speak, though the breath is a sharp instrument in my breast, and each word costs me a portion of the little life that remains. Do not turn away, Doctor; it is not the physic of the body I require of you now, but the medicine of the soul, and you are the only physician of any kind left to me. The room is close, is it not? Yet I feel a dreadful coldness creeping from my extremities, a coldness that has less to do with the failing of my blood than with the long winter of my conscience, which is at last drawing to its terrible, frozen close.

For seventy years I have lived in this town of Ashbourne, and they have called me virtuous. Mrs. Agnes Croft, the widow who kept her garden tidy and her opinions to herself, who sat in the same pew at St. Giles’s for half a century and never missed a Sunday save for the influenza of ’27. A life of quiet order, they say, of modest charity and uncomplaining solitude. What a mockery it is! A lie painted over a sepulchre. For I have carried within me a secret so black and foul that it has poisoned every hour of this long, quiet existence, and made of my virtue a mere mask, worn to hide the wretched criminal beneath.

It concerns my dearest friend, the only being I ever loved with the whole of my passionate, foolish heart. Her name was Eliza Vance. Oh, Eliza! Even now, after all these years, the sound of her name is a sweet agony. She was as I was not: radiant, full of a life that seemed to draw its very essence from the sun. Her laughter was a melody, her spirit a bright, unquenchable flame. We were as sisters, sharing every thought, every dream, until the autumn of 1813, when he came to Ashbourne.

His name was Mr. Julian Croft—a cousin of my late husband, though I did not know it then. He was a gentleman of fortune and a terrible, magnetic beauty, with eyes that seemed to hold all the storms and starlight of the universe. He spoke of far-off lands and grand philosophies, and his voice was a low music that could charm the birds from the trees or the sense from a woman’s head. Eliza, with her open and trusting nature, was captivated at once. And I—I was captivated too. Not by his grandeur, but by the singular, burning attention he paid to me. When he looked at me, I felt myself become the heroine of a tragic romance; my quiet nature, my melancholy, seemed to him a mark of profound sensibility. He would draw me aside and speak of the shallow cheerfulness of others, of the deep, shared understanding that only two such souls as ours could possess. I, who had always felt myself plain and overlooked, was suddenly exalted, made glorious in the light of his regard.

In my vanity and my terrible, secret love, I began to resent Eliza. Her brightness, which had once been my delight, now seemed a vulgar glare that obscured the subtle, intimate fire he had kindled for me alone. He confided in me his supposed difficulties with her—how her vivacity exhausted him, how he longed for the profound communion he found with me. And I, like the most wretched of fools, believed him. I became his accomplice in a slow, cruel betrayal. I would listen to Eliza’s joyful confessions of her love for him, and I would poison them with doubt. I would tell her, with a sigh of feigned concern, that I had seen him look upon her with weariness, that his heart was a dark and troubled sea upon which her little boat of happiness would surely founder. I whispered that he was not to be trusted, that his nature was too grand and terrible for provincial happiness. I sought to break her spirit, to make her doubt her own worth, all to keep him for myself.

The ruin was swift and absolute. He had his way with her, promising a marriage that he never intended, and then he abandoned her, leaving her not only with a broken heart but with a child she could not name and a shame that drove her from Ashbourne in the dead of night. I never saw her again. The last I heard, a year later, was a rumour that she had died in a workhouse in Manchester, alone and unforgiven. He, of course, was untouched. He moved on to some other town, some other conquest, leaving behind him the wreckage of the only true friend I ever had.

And I? I was left with the victory I had craved. I married his cousin, a good and dull man, and lived out my days in this quiet town, the very picture of rectitude. But every night, in the darkness of my room, I have seen Eliza’s face, not radiant with joy, but pale and accusing in the gloom of that final, dreadful evening when she came to me, her eyes wide with a terror I had helped to create. I have lived in a hell of my own making, a prison of respectable silence. This sickness is not an affliction; it is a release. It is the only judge I have ever faced whose sentence I can bear.

Therefore, I beg you, Doctor, as you are a man of honour and a healer of more than just the flesh, you must be my instrument of atonement. You travel to Manchester on your professional business. Find her descendants. She must have a child, or a grandchild, someone who carries her blood and perhaps her sorrow. Find them, and give them this—my gold locket, the only thing of value I have ever truly owned. And with it, tell them that Agnes Croft, who was once Agnes Albright, confesses her sin. Tell them I was a false friend, a jealous serpent, and that the ruin of their mother, their grandmother, was my doing. Ask them, in the name of a God I have long feared and never served, for their forgiveness. I do not expect it. I do not deserve it. But let them know that my long, quiet life was a lie, and that I died in the agony of the truth. Do this for me, and my soul, though it be damned, will know a moment’s peace before it descends into the eternal darkness it has so richly earned.
