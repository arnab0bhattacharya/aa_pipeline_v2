The rain had been falling since Tuesday, a warm, relentless rain that turned the dust of the plaza into a brown soup and sent the cockroaches climbing the walls of the customs house. In the back room of his chandlery, surrounded by coils of tarred rope and barrels of salt beef, Elias Vane listened to it drum on the tin roof and wished, not for the first time, that he had never agreed to the consul’s little favour.

The boxes were stacked in the corner behind the paint tins, four of them, made of good English oak and bound with iron straps that had already begun to weep orange tears in the salt air. They contained, according to the consul, the entire archives of the British consulate in Puerto Moscado: ledgers, correspondence, treaties, the accumulated paper of forty years of empire and trade. The consul himself, a pale, dyspeptic man named Haversham, had been recalled to London with the suddenness that attends the collapse of a government. He had come to Vane three days before his departure, his hands trembling, and begged him to hold the boxes until a replacement could be sent.

“It is a matter of some delicacy,” Haversham had said, mopping his forehead with a handkerchief. “There are documents in there that certain parties would very much like to get their hands on. The new government is not entirely friendly to our interests. You understand.”

Vane had understood. He had been in Puerto Moscado for fifteen years, long enough to know that a change of government meant a change of everything: the customs duties, the port fees, the colour of the flag over the garrison, and the identity of the men who came to collect their monthly envelopes. He had also understood that Haversham, in his panic, had chosen him precisely because he was a foreigner, a neutral party, a man with no stake in the local quarrels. What he had not understood, or had not allowed himself to understand, was that neutrality was a luxury that could be revoked at the point of a machete.

The knock came at the front of the shop, a heavy, impatient pounding that sent the parrots in their cages into a frenzy of squawking. Vane set down his ledger and went to the door. Through the rain-streaked glass he saw three men on horseback, their ponchos dark with water, and behind them a cart drawn by a pair of mules. The man in front was a colonel, or claimed to be. His name was Aurelio Sarmiento, and he had been, in the space of a single week, the commander of the garrison, the chief of police, and the self-appointed guardian of the revolution. He wore a gold-braided jacket that had belonged to a previous colonel, and his boots were caked in mud.

Vane opened the door. The rain blew in, spattering the floorboards.

“Señor Vane,” Sarmiento said, dismounting with a flourish that was somewhat undermined by the slickness of the cobbles. He was a small man with a thin moustache and eyes that moved constantly, cataloguing the shop, the street, the rooftops. “I am told you have some boxes that belong to the English.”

“I have some boxes that belong to the English consul,” Vane said. “Who has left the country.”

“Exactly so.” Sarmiento smiled, showing a gold tooth. “And since he has left, the boxes are now the property of the Republic. We are a sovereign nation, señor. We cannot allow foreign archives to sit in a chandler’s back room like so many sacks of flour.”

Vane thought of the ledgers inside the boxes. He had not read them, but he had glanced at the spines: *Correspondence, 1871–1875. Confidential.* He thought of the names that would be in them, the payments, the promises, the careful notations of who had been bought and for how much. He thought of what Sarmiento would do with such information, and what he would do to the man who had kept it from him.

“They are not mine to give,” Vane said.

Sarmiento’s smile did not waver, but something behind it shifted, like a rat moving under a floorboard. “You misunderstand me, señor. I am not asking. I am requisitioning. For the security of the state.”

He gestured, and the two men behind him dismounted. One of them carried a machete in a leather sheath. The other had a revolver tucked into his belt. They stood in the rain, waiting.

Vane was not a brave man. He had never been a brave man. He had come to South America to make money, and he had made a modest amount of it, and his chief ambition was to make a little more and then retire to a cottage in Devon where it rained in a proper, English way. He had no wife, no children, no cause. He had only his shop and his ledgers and his parrots and the four boxes in the back room.

And yet.

“Colonel,” he said, “I am a ship chandler. I sell rope and tar and salt beef. I am not a diplomat. I am not a soldier. I am a man of business. And in my business, when a man leaves goods in my keeping, I do not give them to the first person who asks. I wait for the man to come back, or I wait for his instructions. That is how trust works. If I gave you those boxes today, you would not trust me tomorrow. You would think, ‘This Vane, he is a man who gives up what is not his.’ And then you would not do business with me. And I would be ruined.”

Sarmiento’s eyes narrowed. “You are already ruined, señor, if you do not give me those boxes.”

“Perhaps,” Vane said. “But I am ruined either way. If I give you the boxes, I am a man without honour. If I do not, I am a man without a head. I would rather be a man without a head. It is more comfortable.”

He did not know where the words were coming from. They seemed to be spoken by someone else, someone braver and more foolish, someone who had not spent fifteen years bowing and scraping and keeping his mouth shut. He felt the rain on his face, and the fear in his stomach, and beneath the fear a strange, light sensation, almost like relief.

Sarmiento stared at him for a long moment. The rain dripped from the brim of his hat. Then he laughed, a short, sharp bark that startled the parrots.

“You are a madman,” he said. “An English madman. I have known many Englishmen, and they are all madmen, but you are the maddest of them all.” He turned to his men. “Mount up. We are wasting time.”

“Colonel?” the man with the machete said.

“The boxes are not worth it,” Sarmiento said. “Look at him. He would die for a box of paper. And then I would have a dead Englishman on my hands, and the English would send a gunboat, and I would have to explain to my superiors why I started a war over a chandler’s inventory. No. Let him keep his boxes. Let him rot with them.”

He swung into the saddle. The mules brayed. The rain continued to fall.

“But señor,” he said, looking down at Vane, “when the English do not send a new consul, and the boxes are still here in a year, and the rats have eaten the paper, and you have no one to give them to, you will remember this day. You will remember that you could have been a rich man, and you chose to be a fool.”

He spurred his horse and rode away, his men following, the cart rattling over the cobbles.

Vane stood in the doorway for a long time, watching the rain. Then he went back inside, closed the door, and sat down at his ledger. His hands were shaking. He poured himself a glass of aguardiente and drank it in one swallow. The parrots settled back into their cages, muttering to themselves.

In the corner, behind the paint tins, the four boxes sat undisturbed. They would sit there for another six months, until a new consul arrived, a thin, tired man named Pemberton who had been sent in a hurry and had no idea what he was supposed to do with forty years of paper. By then, Vane had read most of it. He knew who had been bought and for how much. He knew which of the local merchants had been smuggling and which had been informing. He knew a great deal that he had not known before.

He did not use any of it. He was a ship chandler, not a spy. But sometimes, on quiet evenings, when the rain was falling and the parrots were asleep, he would take out one of the ledgers and read a page or two, and smile at the foolishness of men who thought that paper could hold the world together.
