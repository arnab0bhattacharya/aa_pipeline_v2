Among the many chimneys of Chowbent—which town, if you would find it upon the map, lies somewhere in that smoky belt of England where the sky is of the colour of a well-used hearthstone, and the rain, when it falls, descends rather as a species of liquid soot than as the clean water of heaven—there stood, in a narrow street that went by the name of Tenterhook Lane, a shop of ironmongery, and behind the shop a parlour, and behind the parlour a small yard in which a single stunted shrub fought a losing battle with the cinders, and in that parlour dwelt the widow of the late Mr. Abel Quarrell, ironmonger, deceased.

Her name was Mrs. Dorcas Quarrell, and she was eight-and-twenty years of age, with a plain, honest, sensible face, a firm little mouth, and eyes that had in them a certain steady light—the light of a woman who has weighed the world in her own scales and found it wanting, yet has not therefore flung the scales away, but keeps them by her, ready for use. She had been married three years to a man of good heart and no head, who had left her the business, the debts, the shop, the stock, and the little yard aforesaid; and she had taken the whole concern in hand as a careful captain takes a leaky ship, and had stopped the leaks, and pumped out the water, and brought the craft to something like an even keel, by dint of rising early and lying down late and trusting nobody's arithmetic but her own.

Now it chanced that the fame of her good management—as fame will, being a gossiping jade—came to the ears of Mr. Titus Mawkin, cotton master, of Mawkin's Mill, a gentleman of fifty, of great substance, who had built himself a house with a portico and a lodge, and who was understood to be in want of a wife, not for any romantic reason (for he would have laughed that word to scorn, and indeed did laugh at it whenever he encountered it in the newspapers), but because a man in his position, he said, required a partner.

Mr. Mawkin was a large, square, red-faced man, with a loud voice and a habit of striking the palm of his left hand with the fingers of his right when he wished to enforce a statement, as though he were driving a nail into the argument. He spoke of everything in the language of commerce. He spoke of the weather as a speculation; of his digestion as a concern that required looking after; of his late wife (for he had buried one) as a loss that had been severely felt but was now written off. And when he first came a-courting Mrs. Quarrell, he came, as he himself expressed it, to lay his cards upon the table.

"You see before you, ma'am," said Mr. Mawkin, one Thursday afternoon, having planted his hat upon the counter as though it were a bale of goods, "a man who has made his way. I began with nothing—less than nothing, for my father left me a debt—and I have now four hundred hands in my employ, and a capital that would surprise you. I am not a man of fine phrases. I never was. I say what I mean, and I mean what I say. What I propose is this: a union of interests. You have a business—a tidy little business, ma'am, I don't deny it—and I have a business. You have a head for figures, and I have a head for figures. Let us put our heads together, and our figures too, and we shall do very well."

Mrs. Quarrell, who was polishing a brass candlestick with a piece of leather, and who had not ceased her polishing during this address, looked up at him with a calm eye.

"You speak of it, sir," said she, "as if it were a partnership."

"And what else is it?" returned Mr. Mawkin, bringing his hand down upon the counter with a smart slap. "What else is marriage, ma'am, but the most respectable partnership that a man and a woman can enter into? You bring your stock, I bring mine; we share the profits, and the goodwill, and the—the establishment. There is no nonsense about me, ma'am. I don't come here with flattery and fiddle-faddle. I come with an offer that any woman in Chowbent would jump at."

"That may be, sir," said Mrs. Quarrell, "and I am obliged to you for the compliment. But I have been in partnership before, and I know what it is to have a partner who was—if I may say it without disrespect to the dead—a partner who was not always at his post."

"Ah!" said Mr. Mawkin, shaking his head with the air of a man who perfectly understood the allusion. "You refer to poor Quarrell. A worthy man, ma'am, a worthy man, but not a man of business. I knew him. He would have been ruined in six months if you had not stepped in. I said so at the time. 'Quarrell,' said I, 'you have married a woman worth twenty of you.' And so you are, ma'am, and so you are. But I am not Quarrell. I am a man of method. I rise at five, I am at the mill at six, I dine at one, I take my tea at five, I go to bed at ten. I have no vices. I don't drink, I don't gamble, I don't go to the theatre. I am, if I may say so, a sound investment."

Mrs. Quarrell laid down the candlestick and the leather, and folded her hands upon her apron.

"Mr. Mawkin," said she, "you have been very explicit, and I will be explicit too. You are a man of substance, and I am a woman of small means, and there are those who would tell me that I ought to be thankful for your offer, and perhaps I ought. But I have a question to ask you, and I ask it in all seriousness. If I were to marry you, sir, what would become of me?"

Mr. Mawkin stared. "Become of you, ma'am? Why, you would become Mrs. Mawkin. You would have a carriage, and a house with a portico, and a servant in livery, and a pew in the church with a cushion to it. What would you have?"

"I would have," said Mrs. Quarrell, "a place of my own in the world, and a voice in my own affairs, and the right to say my own say. I have that now, sir, small as it is. I have worked for it, and I have kept it, and I have no mind to give it up for a cushion in a pew."

"Pooh, pooh, ma'am!" cried Mr. Mawkin, with a great laugh, which had in it more of the mill than of the marriage. "You talk like a child. A woman's place is to be guided by her husband. You would have your department, ma'am—the house, the servants, the linen, the china—and I should have mine. I should not interfere with you in the kitchen, and you would not interfere with me in the counting-house. That is the way to be comfortable. That is the way they do it in the best-regulated families."

"And if I had an opinion, sir," said Mrs. Quarrell, "upon the counting-house?"

Mr. Mawkin brought his hand down upon the counter again, and this time with a decided thump. "Then, ma'am," said he, "I should be obliged to you to keep it to yourself. I am not a man to be talked round. I have a clear head, and I know my own mind, and I have no opinion of a woman who sets up to know better than her husband. It is all very well in a shop, where there is nobody to contradict you; but in a house like mine, ma'am, there must be order. Order, ma'am, and method, and a proper subordination of parts. I am the head, and you would be the—the hands, if I may say so. The head directs, and the hands execute. That is the principle of a well-conducted mill, and it is the principle of a well-conducted marriage."

Mrs. Quarrell took up her candlestick again, and resumed her polishing.

"I thank you for your offer, Mr. Mawkin," said she, "and I am sure it is a very handsome one, and I hope you will find a wife who will do credit to it. But I am afraid I am not the woman for you. I am too fond of my own way, sir—it is a bad habit, I know, but I have had it a long time, and I don't think I could break myself of it now. And besides," she added, with a little smile, "I have a foolish fancy for a husband who would not look upon me as a part of the machinery."

Mr. Mawkin rose, and took up his hat, and put it on with a firm hand.

"Mrs. Quarrell," said he, "I am sorry to hear you speak so. I am sorry to see a woman of your sense—for you have sense, ma'am, I will say that for you—I am sorry to see you prefer a sentiment to a solid advantage. But I am not a man to be discouraged. I shall not press you now. I shall give you time to think it over. You will find, ma'am, when you have turned it over in your mind, that there is nothing in this world so valuable as a good connection, and nothing so cheap as a fancy. Good afternoon, ma'am. I shall call again."

He went out, and the little bell over the shop-door jingled after him with a shrill and saucy note, as though it laughed at him. Mrs. Quarrell stood for a moment looking at the door, and then she went to the window, and looked out into the smoky street, where the lamps were being lighted one by one, and the men were going home from the mills with their dinner-cans in their hands, and the children were playing in the gutter, and the whole world was going on in its old, dirty, cheerful, human way. And she thought of the house with the portico, and the carriage, and the cushion in the pew, and she thought of the counting-house and the department and the subordination of parts; and she turned away, and took up her candlestick again, and polished it with a will.

"I may be a fool," said Mrs. Quarrell to herself, "and perhaps I am; but I would rather be a fool with my own shop than a wise woman with another person's portico." And she polished the candlestick until it shone like a little mirror, and in it she saw her own face, and the face was cheerful.
