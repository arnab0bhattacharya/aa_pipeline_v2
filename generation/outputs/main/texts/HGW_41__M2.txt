The wind in the Outer Hebrides does not merely blow; it assails. It is a physical blow, a constant, grinding pressure against the stone of the lighthouse, and it had been blowing with such persistent malice for three days that I had begun to think the sea itself was trying to force its way into the lamp room. I was alone, as is usual in these parts, save for the rhythmic thud of the clockwork mechanism and the distant, moaning complaint of the gale. I am a man of few words, accustomed to the solitary vigil, and I confess that the incident which followed was reported to me by the keeper, Mr. Alistair Fane, with a brevity that I found initially lacking in the necessary scientific precision.

Fane was a large man, with hands like rinds and a face that had been weathered by salt and cold into a texture of leather. He stood in the watch-room, his eyes fixed on the dark, churning waters below the cliff, and when he spoke, his voice was low, as if he were afraid of waking the thing he described.

"It rose," he said. "Not like a fish. Like a house."

I was inclined to dismiss this as a hallucination brought on by the isolation and the strain of the lamp maintenance, for Fane had been known to drink too freely of the whisky provided by the mainland supply. But then I saw it. It was not a fish, nor a whale, nor any of the monstrous things the local fishermen tell in the pubs of Uig. It was a column of dark, wet muscle, rising vertically from the troughs of the waves. It had no distinct head, but a broad, flattened crown, and as it swayed in the wind, I could see the faint, pulsing glow of bioluminescence beneath its skin, a sickly green that cut through the moonlight. It did not swim; it stood. It watched the lighthouse.

The next morning, the sea was calmer, though the air was still sharp with frost. I wrote to the mainland, using the telegraph wire that ran down the cliff to the landing stage. I described the creature in terms that I hoped would sound sufficiently clinical to warrant a response, omitting the part where Fane had crossed himself. My reply came from Professor Arthur Halloway, a naturalist of some repute at the University of Edinburgh, who was known for his rather aggressive approach to the study of marine anomalies.

Halloway arrived three days later, accompanied by a porter carrying a heavy canvas case. He was a small man, precise in his movements, wearing a tweed suit that looked absurdly dry and clean against the grey, wet backdrop of the island. He did not look at the sea; he looked at the rocks, the tide lines, and finally at me with a critical eye.

"An octopus," he said, without preamble, as if the diagnosis were already settled. "A giant cephalopod, likely a new species. We must capture it for dissection. The brain structure alone will be worth the trouble."

I felt a cold knot form in my stomach. "It is not an octopus, sir," I said. "It has no tentacles. It is... solid."

Halloway waved a hand dismissively. "The sea is full of things that defy initial observation. I have brought a rifle and a camera. We shall observe it tonight. If it is dangerous, we shall put it down. If it is merely curious, we shall document it."

That night, the wind died down to a whisper, which was somehow more terrifying than the howling gale. The moon was full, casting a hard, white light on the water. We stood on the gallery, Halloway adjusting the focus of his camera, a large, brass-framed instrument that looked like a piece of church furniture, while I held the rifle. My hands were steady, but my mind was a turmoil. I felt a strange, irrational protective instinct toward the creature, a sense of kinship with the solitary, the misunderstood, the outcast.

It rose again.

This time, it came closer to the cliff base. The water around it bubbled and steamed, as if the creature were hot. It was taller than I had thought, perhaps twenty feet from base to crown. It moved with a slow, deliberate grace, swaying slightly as if testing the air. It did not look at us; it looked at the light of the lighthouse, its broad head tilting in a way that seemed almost inquisitive.

Halloway raised the camera. "Hold still," he whispered. "The exposure will take a minute."

I looked at the creature, and then at Halloway. The naturalist’s face was illuminated by the flash of his mind, eager, hungry, already dissecting the beast in his imagination. He saw a specimen. I saw a being.

The creature began to descend, sinking back into the depths. Halloway lowered the camera, looking disappointed. "It must have been a trick of the light, or a very large squid. We shall have to wait for it to return."

"I will go down," I said. The words came out before I could stop them.

Halloway turned to me, his eyes narrowing. "Down? To the water? In this cold?"

"I will take the boat," I said. "I will see if it is there. If it is, I will drive it away. It is too close to the foundation. It is a hazard."

Halloway looked at me for a long moment, then shrugged. "If you wish to risk your neck for the sake of a lighthouse, that is your affair. But do not expect me to follow you."

I took the rowboat and pushed off into the dark water. The rowing was slow and laborious. The moon was a bright disc in the sky, and the water was black and still. As I rowed toward the spot where the creature had risen, I felt a strange calm. The world above, with its telegraphs and professors and rifles, seemed to recede into insignificance.

I saw a shape in the water. It was not moving, just floating, its skin glistening. I rowed closer, my oars dipping silently. As I came within a few feet, the shape moved. It did not attack. It did not thrash. It simply turned its broad, faceless head toward me.

I stopped rowing. I sat in the boat, the oars resting on my knees, and I looked at it. It looked at me. There was no malice in it, no aggression. There was only a vast, indifferent curiosity. It was a creature of the deep, living by laws that had no relation to human law, and it had no reason to fear the small, warm-blooded thing in the wooden box.

I realized then that Halloway was wrong, and that I was wrong. It was not a monster to be killed, nor a hazard to be driven away. It was a neighbor. And I had no right to kill it, nor to drive it away. I was just a keeper, a man who kept the light burning.

I turned the boat around and rowed back to the lighthouse. When I returned, Halloway was waiting on the gallery, the rifle in his hands, his face set in a mask of determination.

"Well?" he asked. "Did you find it? Did you drive it off?"

I looked at him, and then at the dark sea. "No," I said. "It is gone. It will not come back."

Halloway frowned, disappointed. "A pity. We might have learned something."

"Yes," I said. "We might have."

I went inside and lit the lamp. The beam swept out across the water, cutting through the darkness, guiding the ships safely away from the rocks. I did not look at the water again. I knew it was there, deep down, in the cold, black places where the light does not reach. And I was content to let it stay there, where it belonged, far from the rifle and the camera, far from the hungry curiosity of men.
