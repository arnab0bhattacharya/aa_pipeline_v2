The bells of the great Senate House had but ceased their clangour, and the grey vapours of a November evening were descending upon the ruined spires and glassy domes of London, when Valerius found himself alone in the vast, echoing hall where the representatives of the English Republic had late assembled. He stood motionless, his hands clasped behind him, his dark eyes fixed upon the shifting shadows that played along the marble floor, while the wind, entering through the unglazed arches of the rotunda, swept round him with a mournful cadence, as if murmuring a dirge over the departed glories of that commonwealth which he had served with a devotion bordering upon idolatry. The council was dissolved; the senators had departed to their homes, to their comforts, and to their dreams; and Valerius remained, a solitary figure in the fading light, burdened with a knowledge that weighed upon his soul like lead, and from which there was no escape save in the utterance of a truth that he knew would be received with incredulity, with scorn, or with the base panic of a selfish terror.

The intelligence had arrived that morning, borne by a swift vessel from the southern colonies: a famine, unprecedented in its devastation, had fallen upon those distant provinces which supplied the mother country with the greater part of her sustenance. The blight had crept, month by month, across the fertile plains of Amara and the rich valleys of Severia, until the harvests of an entire continent lay withered and black beneath a sun that seemed to have turned traitor to the earth it ought to cherish. The reports were not vague, nor were they the exaggerations of terrified messengers; they were the careful, measured statements of governors and magistrates, men trained in the habit of observation and accustomed to report facts with the cold precision of those who have no interest in distorting them. Within a year—perhaps within ten months—the scarcity would reach the shores of England. The granaries would be exhausted; the means of transport, disrupted by the very calamity that necessitated them, would prove insufficient; and the populace of this mighty city, numbering many millions, would be confronted with the spectre of hunger.

This was the knowledge with which Valerius was armed, and which he was bound, by every obligation of conscience and of duty, to communicate to the people. Yet he knew, with a prescience born of long experience, the temper of his colleagues. He had seen them, that very afternoon, receive the tidings with a composed countenance, and then, by degrees, dismiss them. They had spoken of the uncertainty of remote events; of the possibility that the next vessel might bring more cheering intelligence; of the inexpediency of alarming a population already restless under the burdens of taxation and the restraints of a government which, though republican in name, had not yet wholly freed itself from the vices of older tyrannies. They had used, with a fluency that appalled him, the language of hope, as if hope were a substitute for bread, and as if the confidence of senators could turn aside the decree of nature. One among them, a man of considerable eloquence and shallow understanding, had dilated upon the resilience of the English character, upon the resources of science, upon the power of human ingenuity to triumph over calamity; and had concluded with an appeal to the patriotism of the assembly, urging that they should not, by a premature disclosure, undermine the public confidence which was, he affirmed, the true foundation of national prosperity. Valerius had listened in silence, his heart swelling with a grief that was almost indignation; for he perceived that what these men called patriotism was, in truth, a cowardly reluctance to face a peril which demanded an immediate and painful exertion. They preferred to hope that the famine would not arrive, because to acknowledge its approach would compel them to labour, to sacrifice, and to endure the displeasure of a populace taught by long indulgence to expect nothing but prosperity from its rulers.

He left the Senate House and walked through the broad avenues of the city, now illumined by the pale, flickering lights that lined their edges. The rain had begun to fall, a cold, persistent rain, driven by a wind that came from the east, laden with the chill of desolate seasons. He passed the dwellings of the poor, where the sounds of domestic life reached him through the narrow casements: the cries of children, the murmur of voices, the occasional strain of some plaintive melody; and his heart bled within him as he thought of the misery that awaited these unconscious beings, who looked to their rulers for protection, and who would find, when the hour of trial came, that they had been betrayed by a timid and self-interested silence. He thought of his own position: a young man, scarce thirty years of age, raised to the senatorial dignity by the suffrages of a constituency that had trusted him because he had shown himself, in lesser stations, to be a man of unflinching integrity and of a warm, though regulated, sensibility. He had dedicated his life to the public good. He had accepted, as the guiding principle of his existence, the belief that the happiness of the many ought to be the aim of all political action; and he had pursued this aim with a fervour that had sometimes exposed him to the ridicule of the cynical and the mistrust of the ambitious. Yet he had never faltered. He had endured the loneliness that is the portion of every man who refuses to conform his conscience to the convenience of his associates; and he had found, in the consciousness of rectitude, a reward that all the applause of the worldly could not bestow.

But now, as he walked through the rain-swept streets, the sense of his isolation pressed upon him with a force that was well-nigh overwhelming. He was alone. He had no companion in whose sympathy he might seek refuge; no friend to whom he could unbosom the anguish of his mind. He had loved, once, a woman whose gentle nature and elevated intellect had seemed to him the crown of all earthly blessings; but she had been taken from him by a lingering illness, and the grave had closed over her, leaving him to wander through the remainder of his days with a void in his heart that no public honour could fill. From that hour, his devotion to the public cause had acquired the intensity of a religion; he had thrown himself into the business of the state with a fervour that was partly the energy of a generous mind, and partly the desperation of a heart that sought, in labour, oblivion of its grief. And now, in this supreme moment, when the welfare of millions depended upon his courage, he felt the full weight of his solitude. There was no one to sustain him; no voice to whisper that he was right; no hand to clasp his in the silence of a shared resolve.

He reached his lodging, a modest chamber in a quarter of the city but little frequented by the great, and seating himself by the dying embers of his fire, gave himself up to reflection. The decision was his alone. He might obey the majority of his colleagues, remain silent, and trust to the chances of the future; or he might, by a single bold and decisive act, apprise the people of their danger, and summon them to the exertions and the sacrifices which alone could avert the catastrophe. He knew what the consequences of such an act would be. He would be denounced as an alarmist, as a demagogue, as one who sought to undermine the authority of the republic by spreading disquiet among its citizens. His colleagues would turn against him; his political career, which had been the work of years of patient and laborious effort, would be shattered; and he would be left, a marked and isolated man, to endure the obloquy of those very persons whose interests he had sought to preserve. Yet, if he remained silent, and the famine came, as come it must, how would he answer to his own conscience? How would he bear the memory of that suppressed truth, when the cries of the starving rose from every quarter of the city, and when the blood of those who perished through his timidity would cry out against him from the ground?

He rose from his seat and paced the narrow room, his frame agitated by the conflict of his emotions. The wind without had increased to a storm; it shook the casement, and whistled through the crevices of the ancient walls, as if the elements themselves were in sympathy with the tumult of his mind. He thought of the sublimity of duty, of that stern and imperative voice which bids a man forsake all things—friends, reputation, ease, life itself—for the sake of a principle that he knows to be just. He thought of the heroes of old, of those who had stood alone against the multitudes, and had preferred death to the betrayal of a trust; and he felt, kindling within his breast, a flame that was not of this earth, a flame that burned the brighter for the darkness that surrounded it, and that illuminated, with a clear and steady radiance, the path which he was bound to tread.

He would speak. On the morrow, when the Senate reassembled, he would rise in his place, and, regardless of the displeasure of his colleagues, and of the storms that his words would awaken, he would tell the people, through their representatives, the truth. He would lay before them the full extent of the calamity that impended; he would call upon them to exert every resource of the state, to summon every faculty of invention and of industry, to meet the crisis with a resolution worthy of a free and enlightened nation. He knew that he would be opposed, ridiculed, perhaps silenced; but he knew also that the truth, once spoken, could not wholly perish, and that even if it were stifled for a season, it would rise again, in some hour of sharper necessity, with a power that no human artifice could withstand. He sat down, and, taking pen and paper, began to compose the speech which he intended to deliver—a speech that should be, not the utterance of a politician, but the confession of a soul that had wrestled with anguish, and had emerged, through suffering, into the clear light of a solemn and unalterable resolve.
