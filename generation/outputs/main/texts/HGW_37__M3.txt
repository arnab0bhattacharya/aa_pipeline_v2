I must confess that when I first emerged from the Chronometric Apparatus into the brilliant, sunlit plaza of Voon, I was expecting something rather different. I had anticipated the ruins of my own century, or at best a smattering of superstitious savages gawping at my brass buckles. Instead, I found a city of white, curving metal and tall glass spires, populated entirely by the most vigorous and handsome young people I had ever beheld.

I stood there for some moments, blinking in the harsh light, my heavy travelling-cloak gathered about me. A conveyance had just deposited a group of citizens upon the wide pavement, and they were moving past me with that brisk, purposeful air one associates with city folk the world over. They were, I noticed, all seemingly of an age—about thirty, or perhaps a trifle less. Their forms were upright, their eyes bright, their hair uniformly black, brown, or a most striking auburn. There was not a grey hair among them, nor a lined face, nor a stooping shoulder. They were magnificent specimens, I must admit—the very flower of an apparently perfect humanity.

It was one of these young men who first noticed me. He stopped short, stared, and then, with a sharp exclamation, seized the arm of his companion. "Look!" he said. "A revenant!"

They gathered round me rapidly, a dozen or more, their faces expressing not anger but a sort of appalled curiosity. I observed, too, that several of them held their noses. I cannot say I blamed them; the Chronometric Apparatus is not a pleasant mode of transit, and I had not bathed for some twelve hours.

"Take care!" cried one young woman, pressing back against her companion. "It is a spirit from the old world. See its hair—it is the colour of ashes!"

I was, I confess, a little nettled by this. I am not a vain man, but there is a certain dignity in grey hair, or so I had always supposed. "I am not a spirit," I said, as firmly as I could. "I am a traveller. I come from a time long past, and I mean you no harm."

They stared at me as if I had spoken in a foreign tongue—which, indeed, I practically had, for my English was a good deal more guttural than their own. But they understood me, after a fashion, for their language was plainly a descendant of our own.

"He speaks!" muttered a young man with a particularly fine head of auburn curls. "The dead speak!"

"I am not dead," I said, with what I felt was admirable patience. "I am merely old."

This produced a most extraordinary effect. They fell back several paces, and a murmur went up from the crowd. "Old!" The word was repeated in tones of the utmost horror, as if I had said I was a leper.

A young woman, bolder than the rest, stepped forward. She was very handsome, with a clear, bright eye and a firm, decided mouth. "What do you mean by 'old'?" she demanded. "We have no such word—no, that is not true, we have it in the old records, but it is a word of disease. You are diseased, then?"

"I am not diseased," I said. "I have lived a long time. It is natural, in the world I come from, for people to grow old. Their hair turns grey, their faces acquire lines, their bodies grow less supple. It is the way of things."

The young woman stared at me. "But why?" she said. "We have conquered all that. The process was found to be a mere chemical decay. We arrest it at the optimum point. No one decays. Decay is death."

"Decay is life," I said, with more force, perhaps, than elegance. "It is the slow accumulation of experience, of memory, of wisdom. A man who has lived sixty years has seen more, felt more, understood more, than a man who has lived thirty. His body may be less vigorous, but his mind is richer."

"But his body!" she cried, with something like a shudder. "Look at your body! It is failing. It is crumbling. How can a mind be rich in a crumbling body?"

"Very easily," I said. "The body is a vehicle. It carries the mind. When the vehicle grows old, it does not mean the passenger is dead."

This argument, I could see, was lost on them. They were a people who had never seen decay, and they regarded it as I might regard a broken leg—with sympathy, perhaps, but with no understanding of its place in the natural order.

A young man, who had been examining me with great intensity, now spoke up. "But if you are not a spirit," he said, "why do you wear those strange coverings? And why is your face so—so folded?"

I could not help smiling at this. "My face is folded because I have smiled and frowned and laughed a great many times," I said. "Each line is a memory. These coverings, as you call them, are the garments of my time. I wear them because I am a man of my time, as you are of yours."

The young woman, whose name I later learned was Vael, looked at me with a mixture of pity and fascination. "We must take him to the Curators," she said. "They will know what to do with him. Perhaps he can be repaired."

"I do not wish to be repaired," I said firmly. "I wish to be understood."

But my wishes, it appeared, were of little account. I was escorted—very gently, I must say, and with no unkindness—through the shining streets of Voon to a great white building that stood upon a hill. The people we passed stared at me as I might stare at a mastodon, and small children pointed and laughed. I felt very much like a specimen in a glass case.

Within the building, I was brought before a council of three persons, who sat upon a raised dais and regarded me with grave, intelligent eyes. They were, like all the people of Voon, young and handsome. Their leader, a man with a broad, thoughtful brow, addressed me in a tone of great courtesy.

"We are told," he said, "that you claim to be from the past, and that you are—what was the word?—old. Is this true?"

"It is true," I said.

"And you say that in your time, all people grew—old?"

"All people," I said. "It was the natural course of life. A man was born, he grew to manhood, he lived, he grew old, and he died. It was the way of things."

The Curator was silent for a moment. Then he said: "And you see nothing wrong in this?"

"I see everything right in it," I said. "It is the way of nature. It gives life its shape, its meaning. A life without end is a life without urgency, without purpose. It is the knowledge that we must die that makes us value the time we have."

The Curator looked at his colleagues. "But we do not die," he said. "Not in the way you mean. We continue. We learn. We grow—in knowledge, if not in years. Is that not better?"

"It may be better," I said. "But it is not life. It is something else. Something I do not understand."

The Curator nodded slowly. "Then perhaps," he said, "we have something to learn from each other. You will stay with us, I hope, and tell us of your world, and we will tell you of ours. And perhaps, in time, we will understand each other better."

I agreed, for I had little choice, and besides, I was curious. And so I came to dwell among the people of Voon, a grey-haired ghost in a city of eternal youth, preaching the dignity of decay to a people who had forgotten what decay meant.

They were kind to me, I will say that. They fed me, housed me, listened to my stories with great attention. But I could see, always, the pity in their eyes, the unspoken conviction that I was a relic, a curiosity, a thing to be studied and perhaps, one day, repaired.

And I, for my part, looked upon their smooth, unlined faces and their bright, untroubled eyes, and I felt a pity of my own. For they had conquered death, it was true, but they had also conquered something else—something they did not even know they had lost. They had conquered the need to strive, the need to hurry, the need to love fiercely and lose bravely and grow old gracefully. They had conquered, in short, the very things that made life worth living.

And I thought, as I sat in my white room in the great white building on the hill, that there are worse things than growing old. There is, for instance, never growing old at all.
