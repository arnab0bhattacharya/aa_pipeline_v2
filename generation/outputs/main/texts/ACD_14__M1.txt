The rain had been falling since noon, a persistent, chilling drizzle that turned the gutters of the Commercial Road into miniature canals of black water. It was a night fit for neither man nor beast, yet the bell above my door announced a visitor at half-past nine. I looked up from my notes to see a stout man in a voluminous oilskin coat, dripping water onto the worn carpet of my chambers. It was Mr. Reuben Isaacs, the pawnbroker from the corner of Goulston Street, a man known for his discretion and his sharp eye for a bargain.

“Forgive the intrusion, Mr. Vance,” he said, shaking the rain from his hat. “But a matter has arisen which I felt should not wait for the morning.”

I rose and gestured him to a chair. “You are welcome, Isaacs. The weather drives sensible men indoors, and you are nothing if not sensible.”

He sat heavily, producing a handkerchief to wipe his face. “Sensible, perhaps, but troubled.” He reached into his coat and produced a small object wrapped in oiled silk. He unwrapped it with the care of a man handling a holy relic. It was a silver hunter-case watch, heavy and tarnished, attached to a broken length of black silk cord.

“Pledged this evening,” Isaacs said, his voice dropping to a confidential murmur. “For two shillings. A mere pittance, but I took it because of the crest.”

He handed it to me. I pressed the catch, and the case sprang open. The dial was cracked near the seven, and the hands had ceased their motion at ten minutes past four. But it was the engraving on the inner case that held my attention. It was a fine piece of work: a rampant lion beneath a coronet, with the motto *Semper Vigilo*.

“The crest of the Harrowgate family,” I remarked. “Baronets of Hertfordshire. I recall reading of the tragedy—the heir, young Edmund Harrowgate, drowned off the coast of Sardinia two years ago while yachting.”

“The very same,” Isaacs affirmed. “His father died of grief shortly after. The estate is in Chancery. What is a watch bearing the family arms doing in the hands of a gutter-snipe?”

“And the boy?”

“A wretched creature of eleven or twelve. Name of Pip. He sleeps in the stables behind the ‘Dog and Duck.’ He claims a gentleman gave it to him.”

I examined the watch more closely. The silver was scratched, but the mechanism seemed sound. The stem was bent slightly, as if it had been wrenched from the chain by force. “Did he describe the gentleman?”

“He did. A tall man in a grey Inverness cape and a deerstalker hat. The boy says he was paid a shilling to carry a message to a house in Stepney.”

“Did he deliver it?”

“He could not read the address. He lost the paper in the fog. He is terrified, Vance. He thinks the police will hang him for theft.”

I closed the watch and slipped it into my pocket. “Bring the boy to me, Isaacs. I wish to hear this tale from his own lips. I shall walk back with you; the exercise will clear my head.”

The walk to Whitechapel was a study in contrasts. We left the relative civility of my offices and plunged into the labyrinth of narrow alleys where the gaslight struggled against the gloom. The air smelled of wet wool, stale gin, and the river. Isaacs led me to the rear of a public house, where a lantern cast a sickly yellow glow over a yard of cobbles.

“Pip!” Isaacs called out. “Come here, boy.”

A shape detached itself from the shadows of a coal scuttle. He was a small, ragged figure, barefoot despite the cold, with a face pinched by hunger and fear. His eyes, large and dark, darted between us like a cornered rat’s.

“I ain’t done nothin’,” he stammered. “I swear it, Mr. Isaacs.”

“Quiet, lad,” I said, keeping my voice firm but not unkind. “No one is accusing you. But you must tell me the truth. Where did you get the watch?”

“I told the guv’nor,” the boy said, his teeth chattering. “The toff give it me. Down by the bridge. He said he’d give me a bob to run an errand.”

“Describe him again,” I ordered.

“Tall he was. Couldn’t see his face proper—scarf pulled up high. But he had a ring. A big red stone on his little finger. And his gloves... they was kid, but they was all wet and stained.”

“Stained with what?”

“Dunno. Looked like rust.”

I looked at Isaacs. Rust, or dried blood. “And the message?”

“He writ it down on a bit of paper. Fancy paper, it was. He said to take it to a house in Stepney. Number 4, Grafton Court.”

“You cannot read,” I said. “How do you know the address?”

“He told me. He said I was to knock three times and ask for Mr. Smith. But when I got to the bridge, the wind took the paper. It went right into the water. I run off, I did. I was scared he’d be angry.”

“He gave you the watch before you left?”

“Yes, sir. Said he’d pay me the shilling when I came back with the answer. He said the watch was a... a guarantee.”

I knelt down to be on the boy’s level. The stench of the stable was overpowering, but I ignored it. “Pip, look at me. When did this happen?”

“Yesterday. At dusk.”

“And the man’s boots?”

The boy blinked. “Boots? They was square-toed. And they squeaked. I remember thinking it was funny, a toff with squeaky boots.”

I stood up and adjusted my collar. The picture was forming with remarkable clarity. A tall man, florid of face perhaps—though the scarf hid it—wearing a ring and kid gloves stained with blood. He was in a hurry, anxious enough to part with a valuable heirloom to secure a messenger, yet careless enough to let the address be lost. The watch was the key. It was not a pledge; it was a lure. Or perhaps a token.

“We shall go to Grafton Court,” I said.

“Now?” Isaacs asked, his eyes widening.

“Now. If my theory is correct, the owner of that watch is not dead. Or if he is, he has returned from the grave to settle a score.”

We left the boy in the care of the publican’s wife with instructions to feed him and keep him indoors. The cab ride to Stepney was silent, save for the clatter of the wheels on the wet stones. The rain had ceased, but a thick fog was rolling in from the Thames, muffling the sounds of the city.

Grafton Court was a respectable terrace of brick, the kind of place where clerks and minor functionaries lived in quiet desperation. Number 4 was in darkness. I tried the door; it was unlocked.

“Stay here,” I whispered to Isaacs.

I stepped into the narrow hallway. The air was cold, carrying the scent of damp plaster and... something else. A faint, metallic odor. I struck a match. The floorboards were bare. On the hall table lay a single item: a kid glove, stained brown.

I moved into the parlor. The room was furnished, but sparsely. A fire had burned low in the grate. On the mantelpiece stood a silver candlestick, tarnished and bent.

Then I saw the writing.

On the wall, scrawled in a jagged, frantic hand, was a single word: *VINDICO*.

Latin. *I avenge.*

I heard a creak behind me. I spun around, the match flaring in my hand. A figure stood in the doorway of the kitchen. It was a man, tall and gaunt, his face hidden in shadow. He wore a grey Inverness cape.

“You are late, messenger,” the man said. His voice was a dry rasp, like dead leaves skittering over pavement.

“I am not the messenger,” I replied, stepping back toward the center of the room. “I am an agent of inquiry. And you, I think, are not dead, Mr. Harrowgate.”

The man stepped forward into the light. His face was pale, the cheeks sunken, but the eyes burned with a feverish intensity. The ring on his finger—the red stone Pip had described—glinted dully.

“Edmund Harrowgate died in Sardinia,” the man whispered. “He drowned.”

“An empty coffin, no doubt,” I said. “Or a substituted body. It is an old trick. But why the charade? Why the watch?”

The man laughed, a sound devoid of humor. “The watch was my father’s. I sent it as a warning to the man who ruined me. The man who let me drown to steal my inheritance. He lives in this house. He thinks he is safe.”

“And the boy?”

“A fool. But he served his purpose. He brought the warning. Though he lost the address, I knew my quarry would come here eventually. He is a creature of habit.”

“He is not here,” I said. “The house is empty.”

“He is here,” Harrowgate hissed. He reached into his coat and produced a heavy revolver. “He is in the cellar. I have been waiting for him. And now I have found you instead.”

The situation was delicate. The man was clearly deranged, his mind unhinged by two years of exile and hatred. To rush him would be suicide.

“You have been waiting a long time,” I said, keeping my tone conversational. “The fire is nearly out. You must be cold.”

“I am burning,” he said. “Burning for justice.”

“The police will not see it as justice,” I said. “They will see murder.”

“The police are fools!”

As he spoke, he took a step forward. His boot squeaked on the floorboards. It was the sound Pip had heard. In that instant, the door behind me burst open. Isaacs, unable to contain his curiosity, had entered.

Harrowgate spun toward the noise. In that moment of distraction, I lunged. Not at the man, but at the heavy silver candlestick on the mantel. I seized it and hurled it with all my strength.

It struck his wrist with a sickening crunch. The revolver clattered to the floor. Harrowgate cried out, clutching his arm, and fell back against the wall.

Isaacs stood in the doorway, his mouth agape. “Good heavens!”

I kicked the revolver away and pinned the man against the wall. He offered no further resistance, his energy seemingly spent with the shock of the blow. He slumped down, burying his face in his hands.

“The cellar,” he moaned. “I thought he was there. I locked him in.”

I looked at Isaacs. “Go to the street. Find a constable. Quickly.”

As the pawnbroker hurried away, I turned back to the wretched figure on the floor. The rage had gone out of him, leaving only a hollow shell.

“He stole my life,” Harrowgate whispered. “He wrote to my father that I was dead. He forged the will.”

“The law will correct these wrongs,” I said, though I knew how slow the wheels of justice ground. “But not through blood.”
