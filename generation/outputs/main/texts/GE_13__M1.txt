It is a truth that the world of a provincial town, when viewed from the windows of a modest rectory, can present a moral geography as complex and treacherous as any mountain range; for the peaks of social ambition are sharp and the valleys of disappointment are deep, and the path between them is often obscured by the fog of one’s own hopes. Mrs. Priscilla Harding, wife to the Reverend Arthur Harding, curate of St. Jude’s in the town of Wyverstone, found herself navigating this terrain daily. Her mind, furnished with the philosophy of the German idealists and a reading of the classics far beyond the usual female accomplishment, felt itself an unwieldy instrument for the delicate task of parish work. She could discourse with some authority on the pre-Socratic conceptions of *logos*, yet she found it impossible to persuade the aged Mrs. Gable that a draught of gin was not, in fact, a sovereign remedy for rheumatism.

Her education, which had been the pride of her schoolmaster father in a more genteel but less prosperous parish, now marked her as an alien. The ladies of the Wyverstone sewing circle, who considered a subscription to the *Ladies’ Repository* the height of literary engagement, regarded Priscilla with a mixture of admiration and suspicion. They would listen to her remarks on the proper translation of a Latin hymn with polite, glazed eyes, and then speak amongst themselves of the price of mutton and the failings of the vicar’s cook, a conversation which Priscilla found trivial, but which was, she was forced to admit, the true lingua franca of their shared existence. To the poor, her fluency was a greater barrier still. When she visited the damp cottages along the canal, her carefully reasoned arguments for cleanliness and temperance were met with a sullen, unintelligible silence. Her husband’s simple, heartfelt platitudes, on the other hand, were received with a ready, if fleeting, trust. They understood his kindness; they could not comprehend her logic.

Her ambition, which she told herself was for Arthur’s sake and for the good of the Church, was a slow, insidious thing, like the ivy that clings to a wall, believing itself to be an ornament while it works to loosen the stones. She had married Arthur Harding for his sterling character, his gentle piety, and his handsome face, but in the quiet of her own mind, she had also married him for the man she intended to make of him. She saw in him the raw material for a great parish priest, a thinker who could elevate the spiritual life of the county. She encouraged his reading, polished his sermons, and subtly steered him away from the company of the hunting squires and towards the society of the learned magistrate, Mr. Finch, and the wealthy mill-owner, Mr. Crompton. She believed she was cultivating a garden, when in truth she was perhaps merely planting a single, exotic flower in soil that would not sustain it.

The living of Thornton St. Mary, a pleasant benefice with a good house and an income that would allow them to escape the genteel penury of their curacy, fell vacant. It was an open secret that the vicar, Mr. Finch, was Arthur’s patron, and for a year the entire parish of Wyverstone had treated the Hardings with the deference due to future incumbents. Priscilla allowed herself to dream of a library with proper shelves, of a garden where she could cultivate roses without fear of the landlord’s displeasure. She saw Arthur, in her mind’s eye, standing before a better-educated flock, his sermons discussed and admired.

The news came, as such news often does, not with a thunderclap but with a quiet, crushing weight. The living had been given to Mr. Thorne, a man whose most notable qualification was his connection to the Earl of Wexbury. Mr. Thorne was a decent, dull man, whose sermons were a soporific remedy for Sunday afternoon indigestion. He had a wife who spoke of nothing but her children and her ailments, and a talent for saying nothing with great conviction. He was, in short, everything Arthur was not, save for the one thing that mattered: he had connections.

Priscilla felt the blow not as a disappointment, but as a judgment. A cold, clarifying horror crept over her as she sat in their small drawing-room, the faded chintz of the sofa suddenly seeming to mock her. She looked at Arthur, who was reading a letter from Mr. Finch with a face of pained bewilderment, and she saw the full consequence of her own labours. Had her cultivation of Mr. Finch, her intellectual posturing, her subtle attempts to present Arthur as a man of consequence, only served to make their patron feel that the Hardings were too grand, too ambitious, too *expectant*? Had she, in her eagerness to raise her husband, inadvertently made him appear a rival to be managed, rather than a protégé to be advanced? It is a peculiar torment of the human heart that the very qualities we cherish in ourselves can become the instruments of our own undoing. Her ambition, which she had framed as a form of love, had revealed itself as a form of vanity.

She rose and went to the window, looking out at the familiar, unchanging prospect of the church spire and the grimy rooftops. The world, which had seemed a ladder to be climbed, now appeared a closed system, governed by laws of birth and connection to which she and Arthur were strangers. Her intellect, her one source of pride, had been a fool’s errand. The parish ladies would have their quiet satisfaction, the poor would go on mistrusting her, and Arthur would remain a curate, his potential slowly curdling into a gentle, uncomplaining resignation. She had not harmed him with a single act of malice, but with a thousand acts of misguided love, a slow poison administered with the best of intentions. Turning back into the room, she saw her husband looking at her, his expression full of a dawning, fearful comprehension, and she knew that the hardest part of their new life would not be the lack of a good income, but the silence she would have to learn to keep.
