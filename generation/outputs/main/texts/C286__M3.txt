The morning fog off the canal smelled of wet stone and rotting hemp. Clara wiped the dampness from the windowsill, her English wool dress too heavy for the climate, her hands raw from the lye. Across the narrow street, the spire of Saint Aelred’s pushed through the mist, tolling the hour. She counted the strokes—seven—and turned back to the kitchen. The house was tall and narrow, with floors of dark wood that groaned underfoot and walls of plaster that sweated in winter. The doctor kept his clinic in the front parlor, and the poor of the Waeterschoot district queued before dawn on the cobblestones outside, their coughs seeping through the door.

She had been in Viersel six weeks. The doctor had advertised in an English periodical for a housekeeper, and she had answered with a letter that was careful to mention competence and discretion but silent on everything else. Dr. Maarten Vos had replied in English so formal it read like a translation, which it was. He was a widower, he wrote, with a sister who kept his house inadequately. He required someone experienced with medicines and accounts. He did not ask why a woman of evident education sought a post so far from home.

The sister, Mevrouw Greet, had asked. She had asked in the first hour of Clara’s arrival, standing in the hallway with her arms folded across a black apron, her French rapid and clipped. Clara had answered in her own careful French that she was a widow seeking honest employment. Greet had looked at her hands, which were clean and pale, and at her collar, which was linen and properly stitched, and had said nothing more. But the silence had a texture to it, dense with suspicion.

Clara lifted the pot of bone broth from the stove and carried it to the front room, where the doctor had already begun seeing patients. He was a lean man, forty perhaps, with deep-set eyes and a beard trimmed close. He wore the same brown coat every day, patched at the elbows, and he spoke to the poor of the Waeterschoot in their own dialect, which Clara could not yet follow. He looked up as she set the broth on the side table and nodded. He was always grateful, always abstracted, always turning back to the next patient before she could quite finish a curtsy.

The morning’s patients were the usual procession: a dockworker’s wife with a child burning with fever, an old man with a cough that rattled like stones in a box, a girl of perhaps twelve with a hand swollen from a wound gone bad. Clara stood at the back of the room and watched the doctor clean the girl’s wound with spirits and bind it with linen she had boiled the night before. She had prepared the dressings exactly as he had shown her, rolled tight and kept in a covered dish. He reached for them without looking, and his hand found them, and he did not need to ask. This was what she could offer. This was the currency of usefulness.

Greet appeared in the doorway. She wore her perpetual expression of controlled disapproval, her mouth a pressed line beneath her bonnet. She watched Clara for a moment, then crossed to the side table and tasted the broth with a spoon. She set the spoon down with a click.

“You have put salt in this,” Greet said.

“Yes,” Clara said.

“It does not take salt. The doctor takes no salt in his broth. I have told you this.”

Clara had not been told. She had been told many things, but not this. She looked at Greet and saw no malice, exactly, but something harder—the vigilance of a woman who had buried a sister-in-law and a child and who guarded her remaining brother with the ferocity of a small, besieged army.

“I will remember,” Clara said.

Greet picked up the pot and carried it back to the kitchen without another word. Clara heard her pouring it out. She closed her eyes for a moment, then opened them and returned to her station.

In the afternoon, when the clinic was closed and the doctor had gone to visit a patient in the tanneries, Clara sat in the kitchen mending sheets. Greet was kneading bread across from her. The room was warm and close, and the only sound was the thud of dough against the board.

“You are Protestant,” Greet said. It was not a question.

“Yes.”

Greet’s hands continued their work, pressing and folding. “My brother does not care about such things. He says God does not see the difference. But I see the difference.”

Clara threaded her needle. “I would not presume to argue religion with you.”

“You do not go to Mass. The neighbors have noticed. They say the Englishwoman in the doctor’s house is a heretic who refuses the sacraments. They say it brings ill fortune on the quarter.”

Clara pulled the thread through the linen. Her stitches were small and even. She had learned to sew in a school run by clergymen’s daughters, and the memory of that place was so vivid sometimes that the smell of beeswax and chalk seemed to rise from the fabric in her hands. “I do not refuse anything,” she said. “I simply do not attend. I would not wish to be dishonest in a church.”

Greet stopped kneading. She looked at Clara with something that might have been the beginning of grudging respect, or might have been nothing at all. “Dishonest,” she repeated. “You think honesty is worth more than belonging.”

Clara thought of England. She thought of the rectory in Hallowfield and the vicar’s study with its green lamp and its locked drawer. She thought of the letters she had found, and the quiet, annihilating conversation that had followed, in which the vicar had explained with perfect gentleness that she must leave, that the matter could not be spoken of, that her late husband’s name must be protected, that the parish must not be disturbed. She thought of how she had packed her trunk in the dark and walked to the coaching inn with money she had sewn into the hem of her petticoat. She thought of how clean the air had tasted on the packet boat, crossing the channel, as though she had been breathing through a cloth for years and someone had finally pulled it away.

“I think honesty is all I have left,” she said.

Greet resumed kneading. The rhythm was slower now, almost thoughtful. “The Janssens’ boy is sick again. The one with the chest. Maarten will need someone to sit with him tonight while he sees the Vermeulen woman. She is in labor and it is going badly. You will sit with the boy. You know the fever draught?”

“Yes.”

“Then prepare it. And do not put salt in the broth you take to the Janssens. They are poor enough without insult.”

Clara looked up. Greet was not looking at her. She was pressing the dough into a round with the heels of her hands, her face flushed from the heat, her bonnet strings damp at the throat. But she had said *you will sit with the boy*, and she had said *you know the fever draught*, and in those words Clara heard the first, faint sound of a door being unlocked, just slightly, just enough to let a woman through who had nowhere else to go.

That night, Clara sat beside the Janssens’ boy in a room that smelled of damp straw and tallow. The boy was five, perhaps six, his breathing shallow and labored. She bathed his forehead with a cloth dipped in vinegar water and gave him the draught in spoonfuls, waiting between each for him to swallow. His mother sat in the corner, watching. She had not spoken to Clara beyond a nod when she entered. The Waeterschoot did not trust strangers, and it did not trust the English, and it did not trust Protestants. But the doctor had sent her, and so they let her stay.

Clara sat and watched the boy breathe. She counted the rise and fall of his chest, and she thought of nothing at all. The candle guttered. Outside, the canal lapped against the quai wall, and somewhere in the tanneries a dog barked, and the bells of Saint Aelred’s struck midnight, and she was still sitting there when the boy’s breathing eased and his mother began, very quietly, to weep with relief. Clara folded the cloth, laid it in the basin, and did not move from the chair. She would sit there until the doctor came, and then she would walk back through the fog to the narrow house on the canal, where Greet would have left bread and cheese on the kitchen table, and where the door would be unbolted.
