Lord Arthur Blenkinsop had been called many things in his thirty years, but never, by anyone’s intention, a wit. The most generous of his acquaintances described him as ‘thoroughly reliable’, a phrase which, in the drawing-rooms of Mayfair, is the most exquisite form of condemnation. The Duke of Rutland had once remarked, with a sigh that suggested the weight of historical truth, that conversing with young Blenkinsop was like reading a directory: useful, perhaps, but possessing none of the charms of fiction. He was a man of impeccable breeding and an intellect so profoundly untroubled by thought that he was regarded by the hostesses of London as a kind of living, breathing piece of furniture—pleasant to have around, but never to be mistaken for the main attraction.

It was this very quality of amiable vacuity that made him the perfect guest at The Athenaeum Club on a damp Tuesday in November. He was seated in a deep leather armchair, contemplating the fire with the earnest concentration he usually reserved for a well-turned-out horse, when he was approached by a man of extraordinary aspect. The stranger was small, dapper, and possessed a moustache so waxed and pointed it seemed a deliberate act of aggression upon the air. He bowed with a flourish that sent a faint scent of cologne and Gauloises across the room.

“Monsieur le Comte de Valois?” the man enquired, his English a glorious, lilting ruin.

Lord Blenkinsop, startled from his reverie, looked up. “I am afraid not. I am Blenkinsop.”

The stranger’s eyes, dark and glittering as jet buttons, widened in theatrical delight. “Ah, but of course! The nom de guerre! It is an honour, Monsieur. I am Jean-Pierre Dubois, of *Le Figaro*. I have crossed the Channel for one purpose only: to hear the master of paradox speak. To capture the lightning of your wit upon the page!”

Before Lord Blenkinsop could explain that he had no interest in meteorology and had never been called master of anything, Dubois had snapped open a small notebook. The journalist’s admiration was so palpable, so utterly sincere, that to deny it would have felt like a discourtesy. Blenkinsop, a man who had spent his life avoiding discourtesy, simply smiled his vague, pleasant smile.

“It is a pity about the weather,” Blenkinsop offered, as a butler placed a glass of port by his elbow.

Dubois’s pencil flew across the page. He looked up, his face alight with fervour. “‘A pity about the weather’! Magnificent! The banality of the complaint, transformed by the context into a devastating critique of the English soul! The weather as metaphor for a nation’s emotional repression! Genius, monsieur, pure genius!”

Lord Blenkinsop blinked. “Well, it does make the fields rather muddy.”

“The fields!” Dubois whispered, scribbling furiously. “He speaks of the fields! The pastoral ideal, crushed under the heel of a relentless, grey reality! Oh, this is better than I dared to hope.”

By the time Dubois had departed, promising to return with a full interview, Lord Blenkinsop felt a curious warmth spreading through his chest. It was not the port. It was the novel sensation of being found interesting. When his friend, the impossibly handsome and clever Lord Giles Finch-Hatton, sauntered over, Blenkinsop felt a confidence he had never known before.

“Who was the little Frenchman?” Giles asked, settling into the opposite chair. “He looked as though he’d found a new religion.”

“A journalist,” said Blenkinsop, with a casual air he did not feel. “He seemed to think I had something to say.”

Giles raised a perfectly sculpted eyebrow. “How novel. What did you tell him? The correct way to tie a cravat?”

“I made a small observation about the weather,” Blenkinsop said, swirling his port. “He found it quite profound.”

Giles laughed, a sound like shattering crystal. “My dear Arthur, the French find profundity in a bad omelette. I shouldn’t let it go to your head.”

But it was too late. The seed had been planted in the barren soil of Blenkinsop’s self-regard, and it began to sprout with astonishing speed. The next evening at Lady Althea’s soirée, he found himself the centre of a small but captivated audience. Giles was there, along with the witty and cynical Mrs. Beatrice Croft.

“I am told,” Mrs. Croft drawled, examining her fan, “that you have become a philosopher, Lord Blenkinsop.”

Blenkinsop, emboldened by Dubois’s praise, decided to deploy what he considered a paradox. “Only in the sense that I have come to believe that the most profound truths are often the simplest ones.”

There was a moment of silence. Giles looked as if he had swallowed a wasp. But then, a young debutante, Lady Annabel, giggled. And then another. Soon, the whole group was laughing, not with him, he felt, but at him. Yet, in his newfound state, the distinction was lost. He heard only the applause. He was a wit! He was a conversationalist!

“A masterpiece!” declared Sir Reginald, a portly baronet who always laughed loudest at the jokes of the powerful. “The juxtaposition of profound truth with simple banality! It’s like a verbal Duchamp! The urinal of conversation!”

Blenkinsop beamed. He had no idea who Duchamp was, but he felt a kinship with the man. He launched into another. “And as for society,” he declared, “I find it is much like a teapot. It is often empty, and one must be careful not to get scalded.”

The laughter was louder this time, more frantic. Giles Finch-Hatton pulled him aside, his face a mask of concern.

“Arthur, for God’s sake, what are you doing?”

“I am conversing,” Blenkinsop said, with an air of immense satisfaction.

“You are making a spectacle of yourself! They’re not laughing at your wit, they’re laughing at you. It’s cruel.”

Blenkinsop smiled a serene, superior smile. “My dear Giles, you are simply jealous. The herd can never understand the lion.”

Giles stared at him, horrified. The transformation was complete. The dull, decent, utterly reliable Blenkinsop was gone, replaced by a preening peacock, convinced of his own brilliance. Over the next week, his ‘witticisms’ became the talk of the town. He was invited everywhere, a novelty act, a fool whose pronouncements were eagerly transcribed and repeated as examples of the ultimate absurdity. He would stand by the mantelpiece, a glass of sherry in hand, and dispense his pearls. “A good friend,” he announced one evening, “is like a warm coat. You only truly appreciate him when you are cold.” The ensuing gale of mirth nearly took the roof off the house.

The breaking point came at the Duchess of Pembroke’s ball. The room was a sea of jewels and silks, and at its centre was Lord Blenkinsop, holding court. Even the Prince of Wales, who had heard the rumours, was observing with an amused, quizzical expression.

“I have been thinking,” Blenkinsop announced, his voice ringing with newfound authority, “about the nature of art.”

Giles Finch-Hatton, who had been watching in despair, saw his chance. He knew it was time for a calculated cruelty, a surgical strike to lance the boil of his friend’s delusion.

“Oh, do tell us, Arthur,” Giles said, his voice carrying across the hushed circle. “We are all agog.”

Blenkinsop puffed out his chest. “I have concluded that true art, like true character, is a matter of what one leaves out.”

The crowd waited for the laugh, but it did not come. The sentence hung in the air, awkward and naked without its usual context of absurdity. It was, in fact, almost sensible. The joke had fallen flat.

Sir Reginald coughed. Lady Annabel looked at her shoes. The Prince of Wales turned away to speak to a lady-in-waiting.

But Giles was not finished. “An interesting theory, Arthur,” he said, his tone one of mild, academic inquiry. “But would you not say that it is precisely the *addition* of an unexpected element that creates genius? The single, discordant note in a perfect chord? The one absurd truth in a sea of elegant lies?”

It was a question. A real, genuine, thought-provoking question. And Lord Arthur Blenkinsop, the man who had been hailed as the wittiest soul in London, the master of paradox, the lion of conversation, stared at his friend with a look of pure, uncomprehending panic. His mind, so recently a whirlwind of celebrated nonsense, was suddenly, terrifyingly empty. He opened his mouth, then closed it. He had nothing to say.

The silence was absolute. The laughter was gone. In its place was a far more damning sound: the quiet, collective sigh of an audience that has just realized the show is over. Giles Finch-Hatton looked at his friend’s stricken face and felt a pang of pity. He had saved Arthur from himself, but he had also, in a way, killed the most interesting man in London. The rest of the evening, no one asked Lord Blenkinsop for his opinion on anything. And he was, once again, perfectly and exquisitely dull.
