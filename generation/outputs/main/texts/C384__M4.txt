The rain had been falling for three days, a soft, persistent grey drizzle that turned the Cumberland fells into a single smudge of muted green and brown. It was the kind of rain that seeped into a man’s bones, and Silas Ward, the last of the Wards of Thornhallow Manor, felt it in every joint. He sat in the library, the one room he still bothered to heat, watching a spider mend a broken strand of its web in the corner of a tall window. The house groaned around him, a great, dying animal settling into the earth. He was its sole remaining heartbeat.

The knock at the door was so faint he almost missed it, a timid tap-tap lost in the drumming of the rain. He rose slowly, his bones creaking a protest, and made his way through the cavernous, dust-sheeted hall. He expected no one. He *was* no one, to the world. When he pulled the heavy oak door ajar, a gust of damp air and the scent of wet wool and heather swept in. Standing there, clutching a basket to her chest, was a girl. She was perhaps twelve, with hair the colour of damp straw and eyes that held the clear, direct gaze of a robin. He knew her; she was the daughter of the tenant farmer, Mr. Cringle, who worked the eastern fields. Elara, he thought her name was.

“Mr. Ward, sir,” she said, her voice barely above a whisper. “Mother sent these. She said you might be wanting for something fresh.” She held up the basket, which contained a few precious eggs and a small, misshapen loaf of bread.

He stared at her, then at the basket. He had not spoken to a soul in a fortnight. His voice, when he found it, was a rusty croak. “Thank you. You’d best come in, out of this.”

She stepped hesitantly into the hall, her eyes wide as she took in the grand, decaying space: the sweeping staircase with its chipped banister, the portraits of his father and grandfather staring down with painted, imperious disdain. He led her to the library, where the fire offered the only welcome in the house. She stood awkwardly by the door, dripping onto the worn Persian rug.

“Sit,” he commanded, gesturing to a chair near the hearth. She obeyed, perching on the edge as if ready to flee. He took his own chair opposite her, and for a long moment, there was only the sound of the fire and the rain. He felt an odd compulsion, a strange and unfamiliar urge to fill the silence. He had been silent for so long. The stories of his life had curdled inside him, unheard and unshared. His wife, his Eleanor, had known the broad strokes, but not the fine, painful details. His sons had been too busy living their own brief lives to ask. And his friends… they were all ghosts now, their voices faded to echoes in his memory.

“You’ll be wondering,” he began, his voice still rough, “why an old man rattles around in a place like this.” He didn't wait for her to answer. “It’s a foolish thing, keeping a house this size for one. But it’s the only thing left that remembers me.”

He looked into the fire, the flames dancing in his tired eyes. “I was not always the last. Once, this house was full of noise. My father, God rest him, was a man of thunder. You could hear him bellowing from the far pasture. He loved this land more than he loved any of us, I think. He taught me that a man’s worth was measured in stone walls and sheep. My mother, she was the quiet one. She brought the grace to this house. She would play the pianoforte in the evenings, and the notes would drift up the stairs and fill all the empty corners with something that felt like peace.”

Elara watched him, her small face solemn. She did not interrupt.

“There was a girl,” he continued, his voice softening. “Before Eleanor. Her name was Beatrice. Beatrice Finch. She was the daughter of the village apothecary. She had hair the colour of a fox’s pelt and a laugh that could make the birds jealous. We were young. I was to go to London to study law, but I would have thrown it all away for her. We met in the old mill by the river, the one that’s just a ruin now. We planned a life. A small house, a garden, a boat on the mere.” He paused, the memory as sharp and painful as a shard of glass. “But my father found out. He called her a ‘shopkeeper’s chit’ and forbade me to see her again. He said I had a duty to the name of Ward. I was a coward. I listened. A month later, Beatrice was married to a farmer from the next valley. I never saw her again. I heard she died in childbed some ten years later.”

He shook his head slowly. “I went to London. I learned the law. I became a man of property and consequence. And I was utterly, profoundly miserable. I returned to Thornhallow when my father died, a stranger in my own home. It was then I met Eleanor. She was not like Beatrice. She was calm, steady, like the deep water of the tarn on a still day. She didn’t make me laugh, but she made me feel… anchored. We married. It was a good match, everyone said. And it was. It was a quiet, respectable sort of love. It grew in the soil of duty and companionship. She gave me two sons, my Robert and my John.”

His gaze drifted to a portrait over the mantelpiece, a young man with his own stubborn jaw. “Robert, my eldest. He was all fire and ambition. He hated this place. He saw only the rot and the damp and the endless, muddy fields. He went to sea, to make his fortune in the East. He wrote for a while. Letters full of grand tales and strange spices. Then the letters stopped. We got word from a shipping company. Fever, they said. Died in a port in Bengal. He was twenty-four.”

The old man’s voice was a dry rasp. “John was different. He was like his mother. Gentle. He loved the land. He would have made a fine master of Thornhallow. But the consumption took him. He wasted away in the room upstairs, the one with the south-facing window. Eleanor nursed him to the end. And when he died, a part of her died with him. She followed him a year later. She just… faded. Her heart gave out, the doctor said. But I knew better. It was broken.”

He fell silent, the weight of the telling heavy in the air. The girl, Elara, had not moved. Her eyes were fixed on him, filled not with pity, but with a grave and serious attention. He had never told this story, not in its entirety, not with the shame of Beatrice and the bitter ache of his sons. He had never allowed himself to feel the full, crushing weight of it.

“And now,” he said, his voice a mere whisper, “there is only me. The last leaf on a dead tree. Waiting for the wind.”

Elara was quiet for a long time. Then, she carefully set her basket on the floor. She stood up and walked not to the door, but to the bookshelf. She ran a small finger along the spine of a leather-bound volume. “My father says you have more books than the parson,” she said, her voice clear and steady in the quiet room. “He says you must have read them all.”

Silas Ward looked at her, this child of the damp fields, and for the first time in years, he felt something other than the dull, familiar ache of loss. It was a flicker of something else. Connection. He had given her his ghosts, and she had not run from them.

“Not all of them,” he said, his voice a little stronger. “Not yet.”
