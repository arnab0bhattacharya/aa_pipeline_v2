The fog lay heavy over London that November evening, a thick, sulphurous blanket that clung to the gas-lamps and obscured the sharp angles of the city. I stood in my consulting room in Harley Street, holding a letter that had arrived that morning, its envelope stained with a brownish dirt that smelled faintly of dust and dried blood. The paper was of the heavy, rough sort used in the Indian Army, and the handwriting was cramped, hurried, and written in ink that had long since faded to a pale rust.

"You look as though you have seen a ghost, Colonel," said the voice from the settee. It was Mr. Silas Thorne, a man of no particular rank in the service of the law, but possessed of a mind that was as sharp and untidy as a fox terrier. He was a small, wiry figure, dressed in a tweed suit that had seen better days, his eyes bright and alert beneath a brimless cap.

"A ghost, Thorne?" I replied, my voice sounding harsh in the quiet room. "I should prefer to call it a riddle. Or perhaps a trap."

I handed him the letter. Thorne took it, sniffing it delicately before reading. He was a man who treated every object as a potential witness. "Let me see," he murmured, adjusting his spectacles. "No postmark. No return address. Just a signature: *J. K. Vane.* And the date, 1881. That is six years ago. The content is simple. He claims to have been a patient of yours at the Fort of Jalalabad. He states that you performed an operation on his leg, and that in the confusion of the siege, you buried a small iron box near the eastern rampart. He asks for your assistance in recovering it, offering a substantial sum for your time."

"I have no recollection of any such patient," I said, sinking into my chair. "I treated hundreds of men in that campaign. I recall the heat, the flies, the smell of carbolic acid, and the endless stream of dysentery and shrapnel wounds. But I have no memory of a Vane. Nor do I recall burying any iron box. That is against every regulation of military hygiene and sanity."

Thorne tapped the letter against his knee. "Regulations are for men who have not lived through a siege, Colonel. In Jalalabad, the ground was soft, and the dead were buried in haste. It is possible. But why would a man write to you now, after six years of silence? And why would he choose to do so through the post, rather than appearing at your door?"

"I do not know," I admitted. "But I have a feeling that if I go to this man, I will be drawn into a web from which I cannot escape. He mentions a 'dispute of legacy.' That sounds like money, Thorne. And money, in my experience, brings with it murder."

Thorne smiled, a thin, humorless expression. "Then we must assume the worst. Who is this Vane? Have you made any inquiries?"

"I sent a note to the War Office, but they have no record of a Vane in my battalion. I also inquired at the old hospital in Peshawar. The old surgeon there, a man named Harcourt, said he had no knowledge of the name. But he did say something curious. He mentioned that a man by that name had been treated for a minor wound, but that he left the hospital under suspicious circumstances, taking his papers with him. He described the man as having a habit of speaking in riddles and a way of moving that suggested he was always listening."

"Listening," Thorne repeated. "To what?"

"To the wind, perhaps. Or to the footsteps of those who might follow him."

I rose and walked to the window. The fog outside seemed to press against the glass. "I have an appointment with this Vane. He has left a card with the porter. He is to meet me at the hotel in Waterloo Place at seven o'clock. I am going, Thorne. I cannot let the matter rest. If this man is a fraud, I will expose him. If he is genuine, I must know what I buried."

"You will need a second," Thorne said, standing up and buttoning his coat. "A man of your temperament, Colonel, is prone to impulsive actions. I shall accompany you. We shall observe, and we shall be ready."

The hotel was a respectable establishment, its marble lobby polished to a mirror shine. We entered at six-forty, our breath visible in the cold air. The lobby was nearly empty, save for a few travelers waiting for their cabs. In the corner, seated in a winged chair, sat a man who matched the description I had received. He was tall, lean, and dressed in a dark, high-collared coat. His face was pale, with high cheekbones and a mouth that seemed to be set in a permanent, faint smile. His eyes, as he turned to look at us, were a startlingly light blue, almost colorless, and they fixed upon me with an intensity that made me feel as though I were under a microscope.

"Colonel Ashworth," he said. His voice was soft, cultured, and devoid of any regional accent. "And you must be Mr. Thorne. I was told you were a man of considerable insight."

"You are Mr. Vane," I said, keeping my voice level. "You have called me to discuss a matter of some antiquity."

"I have," Vane replied. He did not rise from his chair. "I have waited six years for this moment, Colonel. I have waited for the moment when you would be ready to listen. I am not here to insult you, nor to rob you. I am here because the box I spoke of contains a document. A map. A map of a mine shaft that was discovered near the fort. It is valuable. Very valuable. And it is in danger."

"In danger from whom?" Thorne asked, stepping forward.

"From those who know of its existence. From the men who died to keep it secret. And from the men who will kill to possess it." Vane’s eyes moved to Thorne, then back to me. "You buried it because you were afraid. You were afraid of what it represented. But fear is a poor guardian, Colonel. I have come to offer you a partnership. Help me recover the box, and we will share the proceeds. Refuse, and you will find that your silence will not protect you. They are already looking for you."

A chill ran down my spine. The man’s words were calm, but the threat beneath them was palpable. I looked at Thorne, who was studying Vane with a critical eye.

"Who is 'they'?" I asked.

Vane smiled, and for the first time, it looked genuine. "That, Colonel, is a question for the next step. But I can tell you this: time is short. The fog is clearing, and with it, the shadows are growing longer. Will you come with me to the warehouse in East London where I have arranged for the digging? Or will you remain here, safe and warm, while your fate is decided by others?"

I looked at the man, at his pale face and his strange, light eyes. I thought of the heat of Jalalabad, the smell of the earth, and the faces of the men I had saved and lost. I did not know if this was a trap or a truth, but I knew that I could not turn away.

"Show me," I said.

Vane rose, his movements fluid and silent. "Very well. Let us go. The carriage is waiting." As we moved toward the door, he paused and looked back at Thorne. "And you, Mr. Thorne. I hope you are not too attached to your comfort. The road ahead is rough."

Thorne shrugged, a gesture of mild indifference. "I have walked rougher roads, sir. Let us proceed."

We stepped out into the fog, the city of London stretching out before us in a haze of grey and gold, and I felt, for the first time in years, that I was once again in the field, with the enemy unknown and the ground uncertain.
