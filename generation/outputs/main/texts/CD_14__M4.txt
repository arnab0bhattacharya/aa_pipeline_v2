Upon a certain trim and crescentshaped terrace in the genteel suburb of Islington—a terrace of houses so precisely alike in their stuccoed countenances, their little green railings, and their prim little porches, that they might have been born at a single birth, like a litter of architectural puppies—there resided, in the year eighteen hundred and forty-something, a young lady by the name of Miss Clementina Peabody. She was a young lady of a resolute spirit and a tender heart, two possessions which, when held in company, are apt to produce a most fatiguing internal warfare; and it was her particular misfortune to have been engaged for the space of seven years to a gentleman whose entire stock-in-trade, both of hope and of substance, consisted in the expectation of an inheritance.

This gentleman was Mr. Augustus Wobbleton, a person of a sanguine complexion and a most capacious appetite for the future. His prospects, he was wont to observe, were of the brightest; they were, indeed, perpetually brightening, like a sun that is for ever rising and never, by any chance, arrives at noon. His uncle, a Mr. Bartholomew Grudge, a retired tallow-chandler of vast possessions and a temper soured by the perpetual contemplation of other people’s happiness, was the sole obstacle between Mr. Wobbleton and the realisation of his fondest dreams. The uncle, it should be understood, was a gentleman of a singularly robust constitution, who took a grim and daily pleasure in his own good health, and who regarded his nephew’s expectations with the same sort of feeling that a cat might regard a mouse that had grown too old and cunning to be caught.

The interview which was to decide the fate of Miss Peabody took place in the front parlour of the villa, a room of such oppressive respectability that the very antimacassars seemed to sit in judgment upon the visitor. Mr. Wobbleton had been summoned by a note of a most decisive character, and he entered with the air of a man who has been called to account for a debt he has no intention of paying.

‘Clementina, my love,’ said he, spreading his hands as if to embrace the entire situation, ‘you are agitated. You are flushed. You have been over-exerting yourself with the—the needle, or the—the pianoforte, perhaps?’

‘Augustus,’ returned the young lady, and there was a tremor in her voice that spoke of seven years of suppressed indignation, ‘I have been over-exerting myself with nothing but patience. I have been patient for seven years. I am now going to be impatient.’

‘Impatient, my dear?’ said Mr. Wobbleton, with the smile of a man who believes he is about to be offered a cup of tea. ‘Impatient for what?’

‘For you, Augustus! For our wedding-day! For a home of our own, be it ever so humble, instead of this perpetual waiting upon the convenience of an uncle who, I am sorry to say, appears to have no intention of being convenient at all!’

Mr. Wobbleton’s smile congealed. He looked at the ceiling, he looked at the carpet, he looked at his hat, which he turned round and round in his hands as though it were a wheel that might, if properly spun, carry him away from the conversation. ‘My dear Clementina,’ he said at last, in the tone of a man who is about to explain the laws of gravitation to a child, ‘my uncle is not a young man. His habits are not—not regular. He is, I may say, on the brink. The least shock, the least imprudence—’

‘The least imprudence!’ cried Miss Peabody, and here she rose from her chair with a fine flash of the eye. ‘Augustus Wobbleton, I have heard of your uncle’s brink for seven years! He has been on the brink so long that I believe he has built a house there, and taken up his permanent residence! He is a hale, hearty, obstinate old gentleman, who eats a mutton-chop every day of his life, and enjoys it, I’ll be bound, with the malicious satisfaction of knowing that every chop is a nail in the coffin of your hopes!’

At this point, the door of the parlour—which had been left ajar, a circumstance that will presently be seen to be of the first importance—was pushed open a little further, and there appeared in the aperture the head of an elderly gentleman, who had been listening with an interest that can only be described as voracious. This was Mr. Bartholomew Grudge himself, who, having called upon his nephew upon a matter of business, had been shown into the parlour by a maid-servant of a romantic turn of mind, and had been concealed behind a screen of japanned leather ever since.

‘Ahem!’ said Mr. Grudge.

Mr. Wobbleton started as if he had been shot. ‘Uncle! You here! I—I was not aware—’

‘No, sir,’ said Mr. Grudge, advancing into the room with the slow and deliberate step of a man who is enjoying himself immensely, ‘you were not aware. You were not aware that I had a pair of ears, sir, or that I had the use of them. I have heard your prospects, sir. I have heard your brink, sir. And I have heard this young lady’s very just and spirited observations upon my mutton-chops!’

Miss Peabody, who had turned a little pale, now turned a little red, and then, being a young lady of spirit, turned to face the old gentleman with a courage that did her honour. ‘I am sorry, sir, if I have given offence. But I have spoken the truth, and I am not ashamed of it.’

‘Offence?’ cried Mr. Grudge, and here he laughed a dry, creaking laugh, like the opening of a rusty gate. ‘Offence, my dear? You have done me a service! You have opened my eyes! For seven years, sir,’ and here he turned upon his nephew with a dreadful glee, ‘for seven years you have been waiting for my death! You have been measuring my coffin, sir, and calculating the price of the nails! And you, my dear,’ he added, turning to Clementina with a horrible gallantry, ‘you have been waiting for my nephew. Well, sir, I have a word to say to you both. You shall have your wish, sir—in part. You shall be married, sir, when I am dead. And I, sir, have just discovered a reason for living for ever!’

With this, Mr. Grudge drew himself up to his full height—which was not considerable, but which he made the most of—and looked upon the young couple with the air of a man who has just received a new lease of life from the very hands that sought to terminate it.

‘You will find, sir,’ he continued, ‘that I am a man of my word. I have never been ill in my life, sir, and I do not intend to begin now. I shall take a house at Brighton, sir, for the sea-air. I shall take a course of the pump-room, sir, for the digestion. I shall consult a physician of the highest eminence, sir, and I shall follow his advice to the letter—in reverse! I shall live, sir, to be a hundred! I shall live, sir, to dance at the wedding of your grandchildren! And when, at last, sir, I do condescend to die—if I ever do—it will be out of pure spite, sir, and I shall leave my money to a hospital for decayed tallow-chandlers!’

Having delivered himself of this extraordinary speech, Mr. Grudge clapped his hat upon his head, and marched out of the room, and out of the house, and out of the lives of Mr. Wobbleton and Miss Peabody, leaving behind him a silence that was more eloquent than any words.

Mr. Wobbleton sat down upon the sofa, and looked at his boots. Miss Peabody stood by the window, and looked at the little green railings. At length, she spoke.

‘Augustus,’ she said, ‘I think we have both been very foolish.’

‘I think we have,’ said Mr. Wobbleton, in a small voice.

‘And I think,’ she continued, ‘that we have been waiting for a dead man’s shoes, when we might have been walking in our own.’

‘I think we have,’ said Mr. Wobbleton again.

There was another pause. Then Miss Peabody turned from the window, and there was a smile upon her face—a smile that was not without a certain tender archness.

‘Augustus,’ she said, ‘can you—can you keep a shop?’

Mr. Wobbleton looked up. ‘A shop, my dear?’

‘A little shop,’ said Clementina, ‘in a little street, with a little bell that goes tinkle-tinkle whenever the door opens, and a little parlour behind it, and a little garden with a little bench, and—and a little wife to keep the accounts?’

Mr. Wobbleton rose from the sofa. He looked at his boots no longer; he looked at Clementina. And in that look there was a dawning of something that had not been there for seven years—a something that was very like a man.

‘Clementina,’ he said, ‘I have been a very great fool. I have been waiting for my uncle to die, when I might have been living. I have been expecting a fortune, when I had a treasure by my side. I will keep a shop, my dear, if you will keep me.’

And so it was that, in a little street in Islington, not a quarter of a mile from the trim and crescentshaped terrace, there might have been seen, some months later, a small establishment with the name of WOBBLETON upon the door, and within it a young man of a sanguine complexion, who served the customers with an alacrity that was quite remarkable in one who had so lately been a gentleman of expectations, and a young woman who kept the accounts, and who was heard to observe, upon more than one occasion, that a little shop in the hand was worth a great inheritance in the bush.

As for Mr. Bartholomew Grudge, he removed to Brighton, as he had threatened, and there took such excellent care of himself, and was so perpetually and maliciously cheerful, that he outlived his nephew by a full ten years, and left his entire fortune to a home for indigent cats, in which establishment he was himself, I am happy to say, a most contented and long-lived inmate.
