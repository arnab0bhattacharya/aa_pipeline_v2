The ironmonger’s key was a cold, heavy weight in Elspeth’s hand. Forty years. It felt less like a possession and more like a fossil, the last remnant of a life that had once teemed and roared. She stood on the top step of number 14, her hand on the worn brass of the door-knocker, and looked down the length of Wren Street. The plane trees, mere saplings when she and Arthur had first arrived, now formed a high, whispering canopy, their leaves the colour of old pennies in the thin October light. Tomorrow, the house would no longer be hers. The sale was complete. All that remained was the walking away.

She began at the bottom of the street, near the corner with the chandler’s. Number 1, the grandest house, with its white stone steps and black iron railings. The Ashworths had lived there. Mr. Ashworth, a man built of starch and disapproval, who had tipped his hat to her but never truly seen her. Mrs. Ashworth, a wisp of a woman who had died of the influenza in ’18, her absence leaving the house silent as a tomb. Elspeth remembered the day their son, Julian, had come home from the Somme. He hadn’t been the bright-eyed boy who’d played conkers in the gutter. He was a ghost in a uniform, his eyes holding a landscape she couldn’t comprehend. The Ashworths were gone now, too. The house was a solicitor’s, its windows curtained and blank.

Further along, at number 5, lived the Flynns. A boisterous, Catholic family from Cork, their presence a constant, colourful disruption. Mrs. Flynn’s laughter, a sound like shattering crystal, would ring out from her kitchen window. The smell of baking soda bread and boiled cabbage, a scent Elspeth had first found foreign and later found comforting, always hung in the air around their door. She had been a new bride then, twenty-two years old and still finding her feet in the vast, grey city. Mrs. Flynn had been the one to show her how to get the coal-dust off the doorstep and where to find the best butcher. Elspeth had been a girl who looked over her shoulder, waiting for the world to notice her. Now, at sixty-two, she was a woman who walked with the certainty of a map, her own steps the only guide she needed. The Flynns had all emigrated to Boston, a letter from Mrs. Flynn arriving for years before dwindling into silence.

She paused before number 8, the house with the blue door. The Lamonts. Professor Lamont, a philosopher with a magnificent beard and a mind that seemed to live in ancient Athens, and his wife, Beatrice, who painted wild, impressionistic watercolours of the squares. Beatrice had been Elspeth’s first real friend in London. They would take tea in Beatrice’s cluttered studio, the air thick with the smell of turpentine and Earl Grey, and talk for hours. Beatrice had encouraged Elspeth’s own small, secret ambition: to write. Elspeth had filled notebooks with poems and short stories, her handwriting neat and hopeful. She was the woman of possibility then, a vessel waiting to be filled. The Lamonts had moved to Cambridge for the professor’s work. Beatrice sent a Christmas card every year, her signature now a frail, spidery thing.

Across the street, at number 11, was the Hartley house. Arthur had worked with Mr. Hartley at the shipping office. The Hartleys were solid, respectable, and utterly predictable. They had two children, a boy and a girl, who grew up in a neat, orderly progression of scraped knees and school uniforms. Elspeth and Arthur had wanted children, too. For a few years, it was the great, aching silence between them. Then, little Thomas had come along. He had Arthur’s gentle eyes and Elspeth’s stubborn chin. She remembered pushing his pram along this very pavement, the world narrowing to the rhythm of the wheels and the soft weight of her son. She was a mother then, her days measured in feeds and naps, her heart a fierce, protective thing. The Hartleys had moved to the suburbs years ago, chasing a garden and a quieter life.

Her own house, number 14, stood solid and familiar. The worn step where Thomas had sat to tie his laces. The window box where Arthur had planted geraniums every spring. She could see the faint outline of the chalk games Thomas and his friends had drawn on the pavement below, long since washed away by a thousand rains. The woman who had lived in this house had been a wife, a mother, a homemaker. She had been the heart of a small, contained world. Now, the heart was gone. Arthur, lost to the cancer five years ago. Thomas, a grown man with a family of his own in Manchester, a kind stranger who visited out of duty. The house was just a shell, its memories echoing in empty rooms.

She kept walking. At number 20 lived the doctor, a stern but kind man who had delivered Thomas into the world and, twenty years later, had sat with her as Arthur slipped away. At number 23, a young poet had lived for a single, brilliant summer before disappearing to Paris, leaving behind a stack of unpaid bills and a lingering scent of clove cigarettes. Each doorway held a face, a voice, a fragment of a life. The grumpy bookbinder who had repaired her copy of *Middlemarch*. The cheerful widow who had taught her to make jam. The young clerk who had been jilted at the altar and had stood weeping in the rain one Tuesday afternoon. They were all gone, scattered by time and circumstance. The street was no longer a collection of people she knew; it was a collection of ghosts she remembered.

At the very end of Wren Street, where it met the bustle of the main road, was the old smithy, long since converted into a garage. The air no longer smelled of hot iron and coal, but of petrol and oil. A young man in overalls was polishing the bonnet of a motorcar, his movements quick and efficient. He looked up as she passed, gave a brief, indifferent nod, and returned to his work. He didn’t know her. She was just an old woman out for a walk.

Elspeth turned and began to walk back. The light was failing now, and the gas lamps were beginning to hiss and glow, casting long, dancing shadows. The street looked different in the twilight, softer and more mysterious. It was no longer her street. It was just a street. She was not the young bride, nor the hopeful friend, nor the doting mother. She was the sum of them all, and none of them. She was simply Elspeth, walking away.

She reached her own door one last time. She did not go in. Instead, she stood for a moment, her hand resting on the cold, black iron of the railing. She closed her eyes and listened. She could almost hear it all: the phantom echoes of laughter, of children’s shouts, of slammed doors and whispered conversations. A symphony of forty years, playing its final, fading note. Then, she turned the key in the lock for the last time and walked on, her footsteps steady on the pavement, heading towards the noise and the light of the city that waited at the end of the road.
