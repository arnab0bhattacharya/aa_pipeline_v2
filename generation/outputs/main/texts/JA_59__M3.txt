The arrival of Master Charles Fanshawe at his family’s residence in Kent, for the Easter holidays, was an event his elder sister Catherine had anticipated with more pleasure than wisdom. He was lately sent away to school, and being only fifteen, and full of the restless importance of a boy who has lately learned to construe Latin without weeping, he was deemed by himself, and by his fond mama, to be quite a man of the world. Catherine, who possessed more discernment than her mother, but not quite enough to curb her own indulgent humour, looked at his spotted neckcloth and his air of frigid consequence, and was very much amused.

The Fanshawe family, residing at Willowbrook, had but one neighbour of any consequence—a family of the name of Arundell, residing at the Grange, a house sufficiently near to be visited every day, and sufficiently distant to require a carriage for the journey. Mr. Arundell was a man of large fortune and small intellect; his lady was a woman of small fortune and large pretension; and their establishment was completed by a niece and a governess. The niece was an heiress of sixteen, and the governess was a Miss Blake; and it was upon the shoulders of this latter lady that the storm was about to descend.

Miss Blake was twenty-two, rather pretty, extremely well-mannered, and possessed of that quiet good sense which is more dangerous to the peace of a household than any amount of open coquetry. Catherine, who had frequent occasion to call at the Grange, had found in her a companion whose conversation was a relief from the elaborate insipidity of Mrs. Arundell, and the somewhat dictatorial kindness of Miss Arundell herself. She liked Miss Blake, and Miss Blake liked her; and they were both sensible enough to know that the world at large would not care a fig for their mutual regard.

Charles was taken to the Grange within three days of his arrival, and the visit produced an effect that Catherine had not foreseen. He came home silent, thoughtful, and decidedly absent at dinner. He spilled his wine, neglected his mutton, and was finally observed to be gazing at the chimney-piece with the fixed intensity of one who beholds a vision. Mrs. Fanshawe attributed it to the fatigue of his journey; Catherine, who had seen him eat three plates of muffin an hour before, was inclined to suspect another cause.

That cause was made sufficiently plain the following morning, when Charles sought his sister in the breakfast-parlour, and, after some awkward preliminary discourse on the weather and the state of the stables, suddenly demanded whether she had ever observed Miss Blake’s eyes.

"I have observed them," said Catherine, "on every occasion when Miss Blake has been so good as to look at me. They are eyes, Charles. I believe they serve her admirably."

"You do not understand me," said he, with the grave impatience of a boy who has lately discovered that the entire sex is incomprehensible. "I mean her expression. There is a depth—a melancholy—a something in them which speaks of feelings she has been forced to suppress. I should like," he added, warming to his subject, and thrusting his hands into his pockets with an air of manly resolution, "to be the one to restore to her the happiness she has lost."

Catherine, who had not suspected Miss Blake of having lost any happiness beyond the general inconvenience of being a governess, was a good deal surprised; but she kept her countenance, and only said, "And how do you propose to set about it?"

"I have written some verses," said Charles, colouring deeply.

"Good heavens!" said Catherine, to herself; but aloud she only said, "Indeed?"

"They are in my room. I shall copy them out fairly, and send them by William. He can be trusted. I have spoken to him already."

"Charles," said his sister, with sudden gravity, "you will do nothing of the kind."

He looked at her with an astonishment which was not unmixed with pity. "You do not know her," said he. "You have not penetrated her character as I have. In one half-hour of conversation with her yesterday, while you were all occupied with Miss Arundell's music, I learned more of her true mind than you could discover in a twelvemonth."

"I dare say," said Catherine, who remembered that the half-hour in question had been spent by Miss Blake in teaching Charles the proper pronunciation of the word *antipodes*, which he had got scandalously wrong. "But you must consider, my dear Charles, what the world would say."

"The world!" repeated Charles, with an indignation which would have done honour to a man of five-and-twenty. "I do not care a fig for the world."

"No," said Catherine, drily, "I perceive you do not. But Mama does; and so, I suspect, does Mrs. Arundell; and so, indeed, does Miss Blake herself, who, whatever her private feelings may be, is not likely to receive a poetical effusion from a boy of fifteen with anything but alarm."

Charles winced at the word *boy*. It was a shaft that told. He drew himself up, said that he was nearly sixteen, and that Catherine was unkind to speak so; and then, with the inconsistency natural to his age, sat down and begged her to tell him what he ought to do.

This was a more difficult question than Catherine was prepared to answer. She liked Miss Blake, and she was sincerely unwilling to involve her in a situation which must be equally painful and ridiculous; but she was also aware that any interference on her part, however well-meant, would be construed by Charles as a want of feeling, and by Mrs. Fanshawe, if that lady should hear of it, as a want of proper regard for the family dignity. She therefore contented herself with advising him to wait, to say nothing to his mother, and to abstain, at least for the present, from the transmission of any verses through the medium of William.

Charles, who was in that stage of attachment where counsel is received with reverence and disregarded with impunity, promised everything; and Catherine, who knew her brother, was not deceived. She determined, however, to take her own measures. She would call at the Grange the very next morning, and, by some means or other, put Miss Blake upon her guard.

She found that lady alone in the schoolroom, hemming a handkerchief with that placid dexterity which is the peculiar gift of women who have been taught to sew at a charity school and have risen in the world. Miss Blake received her with pleasure, and they talked for some minutes of the weather, of Miss Arundell's new pelisse, and of the merits of the latest novel from the circulating library, before Catherine, summoning her resolution, contrived to introduce the subject of Charles.

"He is very much grown," said she, "and thinks himself, I believe, quite a man; but I am afraid he is not yet quite proof against the attentions of a handsome face."

Miss Blake’s needle paused for an instant, and then resumed its course. "He is a very pleasing boy," said she, "and seems to have a good deal of sensibility for his years."

"He has a great deal too much," said Catherine, "and I am half afraid he has been exercising it in your direction."

The colour rose in Miss Blake’s cheek, but she answered with composure. "I am sure," said she, "I am very much obliged to Master Fanshawe for his good opinion; but I cannot think that a young gentleman of his age should be seriously—"

"I do not think him serious," interrupted Catherine, eagerly; "that is to say, I think him serious in his feelings, but not in his intentions—or rather, his intentions are serious enough, but they are founded in nonsense. He has written verses, Miss Blake."

"Verses!"

"Upon your eyes, I understand; though I have not seen them. He was to have sent them by our footman. I have forbidden it, but I cannot answer for his discretion. I thought it best you should be prepared."

Miss Blake laid down her work, and looked at Catherine with an expression which was half gratitude and half amusement. "You are very kind," said she. "I am sure I do not know what I have done to inspire such a regard; but I hope I know what is due to myself, and to his family, too well to encourage it for an instant. If he should write—or send any message—I shall return it unopened, and beg him to think no more of the matter."

"Do," said Catherine; "and tell him, if you can, that his verses are bad. That will wound him more than any refusal, and may cure him entirely."

Miss Blake smiled, and shook her head; and they parted with an increase of mutual regard, which, under the circumstances, was perhaps the most dangerous consequence of the whole affair.
