It was upon a raw November night in the year 1894 that I found myself upon the narrow platform of Charnley Junction, some thirty miles south of London. The wind swept in bitter gusts across the bleak stretch of marshland, carrying with it a fine, driving rain that found its way beneath the collar of my greatcoat. I had been despatched from the chief superintendent's office upon a matter which, though trivial in its surface details, had caused no small degree of consternation in the operating department of the Grand Southern Railway.

The facts were briefly these. Three nights previously, a goods train conveying a quantity of valuable securities in the charge of a trusted clerk named Horner had passed through Charnley Junction shortly after midnight. Upon its arrival at the marshalling yards of Deptford, the sealed van had been found intact, but the iron deed-box containing some seven thousand pounds in negotiable bonds had been prised open and emptied. The clerk, Horner, was a man of fifteen years' unblemished service, steady, sober, and entirely above suspicion. He swore that he had never left his van between Croydon and Deptford. The singular feature of the affair, however, lay in the testimony of the night signalman at Charnley, a certain Josiah Bream, a man of dour and methodical habits. Bream declared that as the goods train had slowed to take the junction points, he had observed a lamp signalling from the window of an empty carriage attached midway in the train. He had seen the light move to and fro three times, as though conveying a message to someone upon the embankment. His report had been treated by the company's officials with undisguised contempt, for Bream had been found that same night in some disorientation, stumbling by his levers and reeking of spirits. He had been suspended from duty. Yet the bonds were gone, and the matter rested there.

I had been engaged upon the advice of Mr Collings, the line inspector, who retained a stubborn faith in Bream's honesty. It was at Collings's invitation that I now stood upon the streaming platform, awaiting the midnight goods train from Croydon. Collings himself was a tall, gaunt man in a dripping oilskin coat, who paced beside me with his hands clasped behind his back.

"You will understand, sir," said he, shouting above the wind, "that Bream has held this junction for nineteen years. He is not the man to see phantoms where none exist."

"Yet he was drunk," I observed.

"Aye, that is the puzzle of it. I will not deny that he was in a state which the station-master had every right to condemn. But Bream has not touched strong liquor since the death of his wife six years ago. I would take my oath upon it."

"And you believe that someone drugged his cocoa?"

"It is the only construction I can put upon it. The flask he carries was half empty and bitter to the taste. But who would drug a signalman, and to what end?"

The answer, I thought, was plain enough. Someone had required the signalman's senses to be clouded at the precise moment when the goods train passed through. Whether that same person had also been upon the train was the question I had come to determine.

At twenty minutes past midnight the distant rumble of the approaching train made itself heard above the wind. The signal lamps swung in their frames, and the great steel points clanged beneath our feet. Presently the engine loomed out of the darkness, a hissing, grinding mass of iron, dragging behind it a long tail of darkened wagons and closed vans. I had arranged with Collings that the train should be halted at the junction for the inspection of the couplings, and as the hissing brakes brought the long line to a standstill, I stepped forward with my lantern.

It was the sixth vehicle from the engine, a covered carriage van, empty save for a few packing cases, which Bream had named as the source of the light. I climbed the iron step and threw open the sliding door. The interior was pitch black and smelled of damp straw. I held my lantern within. The floor was of bare planking, and the straw was trampled flat. There was nothing.

I stepped down and walked the length of the van, examining the exterior. It was upon the far side, away from the platform, that I found what I had half expected to find. The wooden frame of the door had been freshly chiselled away at the level of the lock, and a thin brass plate, no larger than a shilling, had been let into the wood so as to cover a neat aperture. It was the work of a skilled mechanic. A slender rod, I judged, might be inserted through this hole, and by its means the interior bolt could be drawn back from without. I called Collings to my side and pointed to the tampering. He examined it in silence, his lantern close to the woodwork.

"By heavens!" he muttered. "That is fresh work. The paint is scarcely broken."

"Which tells us," said I, "that the thief did not enter the van at all. He reached in, drew the bolt, and had access to the interior while the train was in motion. But how the bonds were removed from the sealed van at the rear remains the difficulty."

Collings shook his head. "The sealed van was locked at Croydon and not opened until Deptford. I have seen the seals myself."

"Then the bonds were not in the sealed van when it left Croydon," said I.

We stood in the rain, the train panting beside us. I turned the matter over in my mind. A clerk named Horner had charge of the bonds. A van had been tampered with. A signalman had been drugged. A light had been seen. I walked back along the train to the sealed van and raised my lantern to the great padlock. The seal was unbroken. I rapped upon the wooden panels and listened. The sound was solid enough. Then I knelt and examined the iron framework of the underframe. It was upon the coupling housing that I observed a bundle, wrapped in oilskin and bound with twine, wedged firmly into a cavity. I drew it forth. It was heavy. I cut the twine with my penknife. The package contained a quantity of papers, and at the top, held by an elastic band, were the missing bonds.

"You see," said I, turning to Collings, who had watched my movements with astonishment, "the thief never entered the sealed van at all. He removed the bonds at Croydon before the train departed, hid them in this prepared cache beneath the van, and then, during the journey, signalled to a confederate upon the embankment here at Charnley, where the train slows for the junction. The confederate was to extract the bundle during the halt. Bream's presence, alert and unclouded, would have detected the man upon the line. Therefore Bream was drugged."

"But the signalling from the carriage van?" said Collings.

"The thief himself rode in the van. He had tampered with the lock, as you see, so that he could enter and leave it at will. He signalled to his confederate and then, the work being done, slipped from the train while it was halted. That is why the van was found empty at Deptford."

Collings looked at me with something approaching awe. "And Horner?" he asked.

"Horner," said I, "is at this moment in the waiting-room of the station, having been good enough to accompany us upon the pretence of identifying the seals. I observed his hands when he handed me his ticket at Croydon. The forefinger and thumb of his right hand bore ink-stains of a peculiar brown hue, the mark of a chandler's sealing-wax. He had broken the seal at Croydon, abstracted the bonds, and resealed the van with a forged stamp. The tampering of the carriage van was done with a chisel taken from his own tool-bag."

At that moment the door of the waiting-room opened, and a short, thickset man in a black ulster stepped out upon the platform. Collings laid his hand upon my arm.

"That is Horner," he whispered.

"Then we have him," said I. "You will oblige me by summoning the two constables whom I see stationed at the end of the platform. I will engage him in conversation."

I walked forward, the oilskin bundle beneath my arm, and met the clerk as he advanced. His face was pale in the lamplight, and his eyes, which were small and shifty, fell upon the bundle and remained there. I observed the brown stains upon his fingers. He licked his lips.

"The inspection is satisfactory, I trust?" said he, in a voice which he strove to make steady.

"I have found the bonds," I answered.

He took a step backward. "The bonds?" he repeated.

"Here, beneath the sealed van, where you placed them. The game is up, Horner. Your confederate upon the embankment did not arrive, owing to the fact that the signalman, though drugged, retained sufficient consciousness to send a telegram to the next box. The line was watched."

It was a falsehood, but it served its purpose. The man's jaw dropped, and he cast one furtive glance towards the dark fields beyond the platform. Then the two constables were upon him, and he submitted without a struggle.

I walked back to the waiting-room, where a fire of coke and slack cast a smoky warmth into the chill air. Bream was sitting there, haggard and grey, his hands clasped between his knees. He looked up as I entered.

"Well?" he asked.

"You may go back to your levers on Monday," said I. "Your character is restored."

He put his face in his hands and said nothing. Outside, the engine gave a long whistle, and the goods train moved forward into the darkness, its tail lamps swinging red as it rounded the curve of the junction.
