I had accompanied Mr. Penshurst to the Bellinger Club upon more than one occasion, but never to a meeting so charged with quiet unease. The club occupied a narrow building off a quiet court near the Strand, its interior all dark wood and leather, smelling of pipe smoke and old paper. It was upon Penshurst’s invitation that my companion, the investigator Aldous Croft, had agreed to attend the Saturday tournament without formal introduction. Croft had a gift for fading into the upholstery when it suited him, and it suited him now.

The matter was this. A member, Mr. Silas Vane, had over the span of four weeks defeated every opponent in the club’s regular tournament. He had done so not by sound and predictable play, but by a sequence of moves so unorthodox that none of his fellows could recall the like. A retired civil servant of modest habits and middling reputation, Vane had never before distinguished himself at the board. His sudden mastery was remarkable enough. What had brought the committee to Croft’s door was graver still. The previous Saturday, a Colonel Haythorne had collapsed in his chair moments after drinking from the cup of tea that stood at his elbow. He had been carried out, blue about the lips, and the physician summoned from the street had pronounced it a seizure of the heart. No one had thought to preserve the dregs.

Croft sat in a deep armchair at the far side of the room, a copy of *The Standard* spread before him. I took a chair nearby, within view of the tournament table. There were eight players that evening. Vane sat at the far end, a thin man of perhaps fifty, his dark coat buttoned high, his hands very white and still upon his knees. His opponent was a young barrister named Mercer, who had the look of a man who had staked more upon the game than he could afford.

The play began at half-past seven. A steward moved among the tables with a tray, setting down cups of tea. I observed that the cups were of plain white china and that each was filled from the same pot. Croft had already told me that the tea was prepared in a small kitchen behind the hall by a single steward, a man named Polgreen, and that no member was permitted behind the counter.

For twenty minutes the only sounds were the click of pieces and the low murmur of kibitzers. Then Vane made his move. I saw Mercer stiffen. He leaned forward, examined the board, and sat back with a sharp exhalation. The move, I was told afterward by Penshurst, was a knight sacrifice that no sane player would have attempted, and yet it had opened a line which Mercer could not now close.

I glanced at Croft. He had lowered his paper a half-inch and was watching Vane’s hands.

It was the hands that told the story. As Vane reached for his tea, his fingers brushed the underside of the saucer. The gesture was so natural that I should not have marked it, had I not been watching for something. He lifted the cup, drank, and set it down again. The movement was smooth, unhurried.

Mercer, meanwhile, had drunk from his own cup. Within five minutes his play grew sluggish. He rubbed his forehead. His bishop hung en prise, and he did not notice. Vane pressed his advantage with cold precision, and within a quarter of an hour Mercer resigned. He sat motionless in his chair, grey about the face, until Penshurst guided him to a bench by the wall.

Croft rose and crossed the room. He did not address Vane. Instead he spoke quietly to the steward, Polgreen, and asked whether he might examine the kitchen. Polgreen, a stout man with a florid complexion, looked puzzled but assented. Croft disappeared through the door and was gone for some minutes. When he returned, his expression was unreadable.

He sat down beside me.

“Did you observe Vane’s right hand when he lifted his cup?” he asked, in a low voice.

“He touched the saucer.”

“Precisely. And what did Mercer do when he drank?”

“Nothing. He simply drank.”

“And he is now half-dazed upon that bench. You saw the same thing with Haythorne, I collect. The question is not what is in the tea, but what is on the cup.”

I confess I did not follow him.

“The tea is harmless,” said Croft. “I tasted it myself from the pot. But the underside of the rim, on the side from which the opponent drinks, has been treated. A thin film of something, applied with a fine brush, no doubt dissolved from the heat of the water. Vane drinks from the other side of his own cup, or wipes the rim first with his handkerchief, which he has just done. You observed it?”

I had not. I said so.

“Then observe him now,” said Croft.

Vane had risen and was making his way toward the door. Croft intercepted him with a polite word.

“Mr. Vane, I believe? My name is Croft. I have been asked by the committee to look into the circumstances of Colonel Haythorne’s death.”

Vane’s face went pale, but his manner was controlled. “I am not aware that there were any circumstances to look into. The man had a seizure.”

“Perhaps. But you will permit me to observe, sir, that your record at this board is a singular one. A month ago you were an indifferent player. Today you play like a master. And every man who sits opposite you falls ill within the hour.”

Vane said nothing. His hands, which had been at his sides, closed slowly.

“I have just come from the kitchen,” Croft continued. “The cups are set out upon a shelf, each in its saucer. There is a small tin upon that shelf, unlabeled, which contains a paste. I have taken the liberty of removing a sample. I am no chemist, but I have seen the effects of digitalis before, and I think the analyst will confirm it.”

The word hung in the air. Vane’s jaw tightened.

“You are mistaken,” he said.

“Then you will not object to the analysis. But I must ask you to remain.”

Vane did not remain. He turned and walked quickly toward the street door. Penshurst stepped into his path, and there was a brief struggle. The white china cup, which Vane had carried with him from the table, fell to the floor and shattered. He was brought back into the hall and held there until a constable could be summoned.

It was past midnight when Croft and I walked back toward the Strand. The fog had thickened, and the lamps were dim haloes in the murk.

“How did you know to look at the cup?” I asked.

“I did not, at first,” he answered. “But the facts were plain enough. Vane won by moves no one could predict. That is not genius, Watson—it is chemistry. A man who is half-poisoned does not play his best game. The moves were not brilliant. They were merely the moves of a competent player against a dazed one. When I learned that Haythorne had drunk his tea and collapsed, the direction was clear. The only question was the means.”

“And the tin in the kitchen?”

“Vane had access. He arrived early each Saturday, before the other members. Polgreen is a man who trusts his club’s gentlemen. He would not question a member who lingered near the shelf. It was a simple matter to treat the cups as they stood, on the side opposite to Vane’s own seat.”

“But why? What had these men done to him?”

Croft was silent for a moment. “That is the question I have not yet answered,” he said. “But I think we shall find, when we look into Colonel Haythorne’s regimental years, and young Mercer’s recent brief, that the thread runs back to a matter in India some fifteen years gone. Vane was a clerk in the Bengal office. He had a grievance, and he had patience. That is a combination which has undone better men than Silas Vane.”

He turned up his collar against the fog and said no more.
