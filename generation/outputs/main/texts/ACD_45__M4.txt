I had taken modest lodgings in a quiet house in the Polygon, at Southampton, for the few weeks that must elapse before my ship should be paid off and I should find myself once more at leisure; and it was there, upon a blustering evening in the November of '94, that my whole adventure may be said to have begun. I am a chief officer in the merchant service, named Allan Strachan, and I had come home from the River Plate with a clean bill of lading and a mind full of nothing more troublesome than the price of a new sea-boot.

The knock at my sitting-room door was therefore unwelcome, and the more so when it proved to be my landlady with a card upon a salver. "A gentleman to see you, sir," said she. "He would not give his business, but he is very pressing."

The gentleman was shown up, and I saw a tall, stooping man of sixty or thereabouts, dressed in decent black, with a face the colour of tallow and hands that would not be still. He gave the name of Mr. Josiah Crane, of a firm of solicitors in Fenchurch Street, and he came, he said, upon a matter of the gravest confidence.

"You were lately in the *Rosalind*, from Buenos Ayres?" he asked.

"I was," said I, "and glad to be out of her, for she was a wet ship and a slow one."

"Did you bring home a passenger named Voss? A Mr. Henry Voss?"

I considered. "We brought home a dying man," said I, "by that name, or near it. He came aboard at Monte Video with a doctor's certificate and a face like a candle. He died in the Channel, two days out of the Needles, and we buried him at sea by the usual form."

Mr. Crane leaned forward and laid his hat upon his knee with a care that struck me as disproportionate. "Before he died," said he, "did he give you anything? A letter, sir—a sealed letter?"

"He did," I answered, "and he made me swear a great oath upon it, for he was too far gone to write fair. He had it from his own hand, sealed with a wafer, and he charged me to deliver it into no hands but those of his wife's family—the Deverells, of a house called Long Meadow, near Lyndhurst. He would have me promise that it should not go by post, nor by any servant, but from my own hand to theirs. I gave the promise, and I kept it. I rode over to Long Meadow the very day after I was paid off."

"And you delivered it?"

"I delivered it into the hand of a lady who named herself Mrs. Deverell, and I told her the man was dead and that his last word was for her. She took it, sir, and she looked at it as a woman might look at a snake. Then there was a step in the passage, and a grey-haired gentleman came in—her husband, I took it—and he asked what it was. She told him. He took the letter out of her hand, and before I could say a word he had thrust it into the fire and held it down with the poker till it was ash."

Mr. Crane's yellow face grew yellower. "He burned it unread?"

"Unread, and he begged my pardon for the warmth of his manner, and offered me a glass of sherry, and I drank it and came away. I thought it a family matter and no concern of mine—until last night."

"Last night?"

"Last night," said I, "my rooms were entered while I was at my supper below, and my sea-chest was broken open, and every paper in it turned over. Nothing was taken that I can find—not a sovereign, not a shirt-stud—but the lining of my old coat was slit as with a knife, and my ditty-box emptied on the floor. They were looking for something, Mr. Crane, and I will be hanged if I can think what, unless it was that letter which I no longer have."

The solicitor sat a long while silent, turning his hat. At last he said: "Mr. Strachan, I am not at liberty to tell you all I know, but I will tell you this much. Henry Voss was my client, and he was a man who had made enemies in a hard country. He believed that if a certain paper of his ever came into the hands of the Deverells it would cost him his life; and he believed, on the other hand, that if it were lost or destroyed, the same men who hunted him would hunt his widow. He was a frightened man, sir, and frightened men make strange dispositions. I do not know what was in that letter. I am not sure that I wish to know. But I am very sure of this—that the men who broke your chest are not the Deverells, and that they will not stop at a chest."

He gave me an address in Baker Street, and I went up to town by the morning train.

The rooms in Baker Street were as I had pictured them from the accounts in the public prints, and the gentleman who received me was as lean and keen as his reputation. He heard my tale without once interrupting, his long fingers pressed together, his eyes shut as though he slept; and when I had done he opened them and asked me a single question.

"You said the lady looked at the letter as at a snake. Was it fear, or was it loathing?"

"Loathing, sir, if I know the human face."

"Then the lady knew the hand, and knew it for a hand she hated. And the husband burned it before she could read it—not to protect himself, but to protect her from what it said. That is a very different matter from the common run of such affairs." He rose and took down a great index-book from the shelf. "Voss—Voss. I have a note of a Henry Voss, of Rosario, who was concerned in the affair of the *Aguila Blanca* in '89, and who gave evidence at the trial of a man named Serrano. Serrano was acquitted, and three of the witnesses against him died within the year, and Voss fled to Europe. It is all of a piece. The letter was Voss's last weapon, and the men who want it are the friends of Serrano."

"But the letter is ash," said I.

"The letter is ash," said he, "and that is precisely why you were robbed. They do not know it is ash. They believe you carried it home and that you have it still, or that you have sold it, or that you can be made to say where it lies. A man who has burned a paper is a man who can be watched with profit; and depend upon it, Mr. Strachan, you have been watched from the hour you left Lyndhurst."

He stepped to the window and looked down into the street, and a slow smile came over his face. "There is a man at the corner now," said he, "in a brown ulster, who has stood there these twenty minutes, and who has the look of a foreigner trying very hard to be an Englishman. I think, Watson—I beg your pardon, I should say, I think, Mr. Strachan—that we shall do well to give him something to follow."

What followed I shall set down briefly, for it was over in a night. Holmes—for it was he—had me write, in a hand not my own, a letter to Mr. Crane, which was to be left at the solicitor's office and which purported to say that I had grown frightened and would give up the paper for a price, and that I would bring it to the yard of the old George Inn at Lyndhurst at nine of the clock on the following evening. The letter was sealed, and the brown ulster followed the messenger who carried it; and the messenger was a constable in plain clothes.

We were at the inn before eight, Holmes and I and two officers of the Hampshire constabulary, and we had not long to wait. There came into the yard, at a quarter before nine, not one man but three, and the foremost of them was the grey-haired gentleman whom I had last seen thrusting a letter into a fire. He was no more a Deverell, Holmes told me afterwards, than I was; he was a Spanish agent named Rojas, who had married the widow's sister to get at the family, and who had been waiting all these months for Voss's paper to come home. He had burned it in my presence because he dared not let it be read, and he had ransacked my chest because he could not believe his own good fortune. When the officers closed upon him he made no resistance, but he looked at me with a kind of weary hatred and said in good English: "You carried it across the sea, and you never knew what you carried. It would have hanged me. I have burned it, and I am still hanged. That is a joke of God's, is it not?"

Holmes was silent until they had taken him away. Then he said, "There is one point still in the dark, and I confess it troubles me. The lady at Long Meadow knew the hand, and loathed it. What had Henry Voss to do with her?"

It was Mr. Crane who answered, when we told him the tale next day. "Voss," said he, "was her first husband, and she believed him dead these ten years. He was not a good man, Mr. Strachan, but he was a clever one, and his last cleverness was to send his confession home by the hand of an honest sailor who could not read it, and to trust that the honest sailor's oath would carry it past the very men who were hunting him. He was right in the sailor, and wrong in the wife; for she would have read it, and forgiven him, and the paper would have gone to the police. It was the husband who burned it—the second husband, who had his own reasons. There are more wickednesses in a quiet English house, sir, than ever came out of South America."

I went back to Southampton the next day, and I have never since carried a sealed letter for any dying man; but I have often thought of the thin yellow face in the bunk, and of the oath he made me swear, and of how near the truth I came to carrying home in my ditty-box without ever knowing it.
