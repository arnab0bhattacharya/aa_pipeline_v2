The sky did not fall; it leaked. It began as a faint, sizzling hiss above the moors, a sound like a tea-kettle boiling dry, and then the air turned the colour of bruised plum. The stones came down not as rocks, but as coals, trailing ribbons of blue and violet smoke that twisted in the still evening air. By the time the first impact struck the heather, turning the green and brown expanse into a sea of screaming orange, the village of Harewood was in a state of such profound, stupid paralysis that the screaming of the cattle in the yards was the only thing that seemed real.

Thomas Armitage, the owner of the Harewood Textile Works, stood on the veranda of his house, his morning coat open, his pocket-watch trembling in his hand. He was a man of precise habits and moderate appetites, a creature who had spent forty years smoothing the rough edges of the world into neat, profitable lines. But the line of fire was moving fast, a jagged scar cutting across the high ground toward the town, and the smoke was already a thick, acrid wall that smelled of burning resin and ozone. The wind was wrong. It was blowing the fire down, into the valley, into the very heart of his livelihood and his life.

Below, in the street, the mill hands were emerging from their cottages. They were a rough, soot-stained crowd, men who had spent their days hunched over looms and their evenings in the pub, men who owed Armitage their wages and who owed the union their blood. They looked up at the sky with a mixture of terror and a grim, fatalistic curiosity. It was not a storm they could curse; it was an act of God, or nature, or perhaps the Devil, and it did not care about rent or rates or the price of cotton.

The fire reached the edge of the scrub land near the reservoir. The heat was a physical weight, pressing against the skin. A column of smoke rose, thick and black, blotting out the dying sun. The town crier’s voice was lost in the roar of the wind. Armitage saw a figure moving against the backdrop of the flames. It was not a policeman, nor a fireman, for there were none. It was a young man, lean and sharp-featured, with a coat that had seen better days. It was Silas Vane.

Vane was a striker. He had been blacklisted for six months, turned out of every mill in the valley for the crime of asking for a penny more on the pound. He was a man of little money and much opinion, a creature of the new world that Armitage distrusted. Yet here he was, standing in the middle of the road, his face illuminated by the hellish glow behind him. He was shouting, but the wind tore the words away. Armitage saw him point toward the water tower, then toward the hose reels that stood chained in the yard of the abandoned wool store.

The crowd moved. It was not a mob, not exactly. It was a body of men responding to a sudden, terrible necessity. They rushed the wool store. The chain broke with a snap that was lost in the roar of the fire. Vane was there, cutting the links with a crowbar, his movements economical and fierce. He did not look at Armitage. He did not look at the men who had once thrown stones at him. He looked only at the fire.

Armitage felt a strange, cold clarity in his mind. The usual machinery of his life—the ledgers, the contracts, the social obligations—had ceased to function. There was only the fire, and the men, and the water. He looked at his hands. They were soft, unaccustomed to labour. But the fire was eating the roof of the engine house, the place where his new steam boilers stood, the place that represented the future of the mill. If that roof fell, the boilers would crack, and the investment would be lost. More than that, the heat would reach the cotton stores. The town would burn.

He went down the steps. His boots crunched on the gravel. He walked through the crowd. The men parted for him, not out of respect, but out of a confused surprise. Here was the Master, stepping into the mud and the ash. Vane saw him. The young striker stopped for a moment, his crowbar raised. There was no hatred in his eyes, only a weary assessment. He nodded, a single, sharp jerk, and turned back to the hose.

Armitage did not speak. He walked to the reel. His hands, shaking slightly, gripped the nozzle. It was heavy, cold iron. He felt the water surge through the pipe, a cold, clean shock against his palms. He aimed the stream at the base of the flames, where the heather was consuming the dry grass. The water hissed and steamed, a white ghost rising into the red sky.

It was not enough. One hose was a drop in the ocean. But it was a start. The men saw the Master working. Something shifted in the air. The blacklisted striker and the mill owner were no longer two points on a graph, a low and a high, a debtor and a creditor. They were two men holding a line against a tide.

Vane grabbed the other end of the hose. His arms were strong, corded with the muscle of a man who worked with his hands. He and Armitage stood shoulder to shoulder, the water arcing between them. The fire roared, a living thing, but it was being pushed back. The steam rose, thick and blinding, smelling of wet earth and iron.

For an hour, they fought. The sun went down, and the fire became the only light. The men of Harewood worked with a desperate, silent energy. They passed buckets, they hauled ropes, they broke down doors to get water from the private cisterns. There was no shouting, no union songs, no political speeches. There was only the work. The water, the sweat, the heat.

Armitage’s hands blistered. His coat was soaked through. He felt a pain in his shoulder, a dull ache that was new and strange. But he did not stop. He looked at Vane. The young man was pale, his lips cracked, but his eyes were clear. They met across the spray of the water. There was no triumph in Vane’s face, no relief. There was only the shared knowledge of a job done, a small, insignificant victory in the face of a vast, indifferent universe.

The fire did not die that night. It burned on, a slow, smoldering scar across the moors. But the town was safe. The mill was saved. The boilers stood intact.

As the smoke began to thin, and the first stars appeared in the cleared sky, the men began to drift apart. They wiped their faces, checked their hands, and went back to their cottages. Vane looked at Armitage one last time. He did not bow, nor did he smile. He simply turned and walked away, his stride steady, his shadow long and dark on the wet cobbles.

Armitage stood alone in the yard. The water was still running, a thin trickle now. He looked at his hands. They were red, raw, and trembling. He thought of the blacklists, the fines, the petty wars of the last decade. It seemed absurd, now. A farce played by children while the house was on fire. He picked up his pocket-watch. The hands had stopped. He set them to the time, a mechanical act, a small reassertion of order.

He went inside. The house was dark. The fire had died, leaving only the smell of ash and the memory of heat. He sat down at his desk. The papers were still there, the ledgers, the contracts. They looked flat and dead. He picked up his pen. The ink was dry. He had to wet the nib. He dipped it, and for a long time, he stared at the blank page, waiting for the words to come, wondering what kind of man he was now, and what kind of world he was to live in. The stars circled on, cold and distant, indifferent to the small, warm, trembling life below.
