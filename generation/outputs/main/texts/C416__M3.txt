The house on Tredgar Street stood with its back to the James River, a tall, narrow-eyed brick creature that seemed to hunch against the Virginia damp. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of coal smoke and camphor. Elias Vance sat in his leather chair, slumped to the left, his right hand curled into a useless claw upon the writing desk. His mouth hung agape, the muscles having abandoned their duty. His eyes, however, were terribly alive—swimming in a silent, frantic agony that made his wife, Clara, step back from the bed they had moved into the study.

"He was fine at breakfast," Clara said, her voice barely a whisper. She was a woman made of iron and fine china, unyielding yet fragile in the gaslight. "He spent the morning here, as he always does. When I brought his tea at noon, I found him like this. The doctor says apoplexy."

The investigator, a quiet man named Silas Reed, stood by the desk. He did not look at the broken gentleman in the chair, but rather at the objects that shared his paralysis. Reed was a man who believed that a catastrophe rarely arrived without leaving a paper trail. On the blotter lay two items: a folded letter, its paper cheap and yellowed, and a charcoal sketch of a building.

Reed picked up the sketch first. It depicted a church, but not the Anglican sort found in Richmond. It was a heavy, imposing structure with twin bell towers that curved at the tops like the horns of a ram. The lines were jagged, drawn with a frantic, shaking pressure. At the base of the left tower, the artist had scribbled a dense, dark shadow, as if trying to black out the stone.

"He drew this?" Reed asked.

"He must have. It was not here yesterday. The charcoal stick is there, by the edge of the desk. His fingers are stained with it," Clara replied. "He returned from Mexico three months ago. He was a mining engineer, you know. He spent two years in the mountains of Guerrero. He came back changed. Quieter. He would not speak of his time there, only that the venture had failed and the claim was worthless."

Reed nodded and turned his attention to the letter. It was written in Spanish, a hasty, uneducated hand. Reed’s own Spanish was functional, born of years tracing missing shipments along the Gulf coast. He scanned the lines, his brow furrowing.

*Amigo Elias,* it began. *The bells rang again on the eve of the Feast of the Dead. The villagers say the blood of the foreigner keeps the stone wet. I tell them it is only the rain. But the graves are empty, as you left them. The priest, Father Tomas, he knows we moved them. He demands confession. He says the curse will follow the gold. I beg you, send money so we can rebuild the wall. The earth is soft, and the things beneath the church are restless.*

Reed read the passage twice. He looked at Elias. The engineer’s eyes were fixed on Reed, wide and pleading. A thin line of saliva tracked down his chin.

"Mrs. Vance," Reed said slowly, "did your husband ever mention a place called San Cristóbal de las Sombras? Or a church there?"

Clara’s face paled. "No. Only the mine. He called it the Del Rey shaft."

Reed placed the letter back on the desk. He knelt beside Elias, ignoring the faint smell of urine that hung in the air. "Mr. Vance," Reed said, his voice low and steady. "I am going to find out what this means. Blink once if you understand."

Elias Vance blinked once, hard. His left hand, the only one he could still control, trembled violently against his thigh.

Reed stood and turned to Clara. "I will need a map. And I will need to send a telegram. Has your husband received any packages from Mexico recently? A crate, perhaps, or a heavy trunk?"

Clara hesitated, her hand going to her throat. "A trunk. It arrived two weeks after he did. He kept it locked in the cellar. He told me never to touch it, that it contained only mining equipment that needed cleaning."

Reed descended into the cellar. The house’s foundation smelled of old river mud. He found the trunk beneath the stairs, a heavy, iron-bound box. It was not locked; the padlock hung open, as if someone had recently inspected it. Reed lifted the lid. Inside, beneath a layer of greasy canvas, were pickaxes and lanterns, the standard tools of a mining engineer. But at the very bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, was a small, dense bag.

Reed carried the bag upstairs. Elias’s eyes tracked it like a man watching his own hanging. Reed loosened the drawstring and tipped the contents onto the desk.

It was gold. Not dust or nuggets, but refined, heavy ingots, stamped with a crude, unfamiliar mark. Beside the ingots, a small, dark stone tablet fell out. It was basalt, carved with intricate, spiraling glyphs that predated the Spanish.

Reed looked from the gold to the sketch of the church. "He did not fail, Mrs. Vance. He found something. And whatever he did to get it out of Mexico has followed him here."

Clara stared at the gold, her breath catching. "How much is there?"

"Enough to ruin a man, if his mind is already broken," Reed said. He held up the basalt tablet. "This is pre-Columbian. It was likely hidden beneath a colonial church. The Spanish built their churches on top of the old gods’ temples, you see. To conquer them."

Reed picked up the Spanish letter again and read the crucial line aloud: *"The graves are empty, as you left them."*

He looked at Elias. "You didn't just mine gold, Mr. Vance. You found something beneath the church, and you dug up the dead to hide it. Or to hide what you found. You bribed your way out, but the locals, or the priest, they know. And they want their due."

Elias Vance let out a long, low moan. His left hand moved to his chest, clutching at his shirt. His eyes rolled back, and his body seized. Clara screamed, rushing to him, but Reed stepped back. He had seen death before, and he recognized the finality in the man's violent shuddering. The apoplexy, long threatened by the weight of his secret, had claimed its final pound of flesh.

The doctor arrived an hour later, too late to do anything but declare the obvious. The house on Tredgar Street fell into a hush, the kind of silence that settles over a place where a soul has been violently squeezed from its body. Clara sat in the hallway, staring at the wall, her hands folded in her lap. She had not cried.

Reed remained in the study, standing before the desk. He looked at the sketch of the church with the ram-horned towers, the frantic charcoal lines, the dark, scribbled shadow at its base. He picked up the basalt tablet again, tracing the spiraling glyphs with a gloved finger. The stone was cold, colder than the damp Virginia air warranted, as if it retained the chill of a subterranean vault.

He wrapped the tablet and the gold back into the oilcloth and placed them in his satchel. He took the Spanish letter and the sketch as well. Clara did not ask where he was taking them. She had retreated into a numb stillness, her iron will bent under the sudden weight of a fortune bought with blood and a silence that had finally killed her husband.

As Reed stepped out into the Richmond evening, the fog was rolling in from the river, muffling the gas lamps and the distant clatter of streetcars. He walked toward the telegraph office, his satchel heavy at his side. He would wire a contact in Veracruz, a man who knew the mountains of Guerrero and the secrets buried beneath their stones. He would find out what had happened at San Cristóbal de las Sombras, not for Clara Vance, and not for the dead man who could no longer speak, but for himself. Because Reed knew that some secrets were not meant to stay buried, and the gold in his bag seemed to pulse with a quiet, insistent heat, demanding to be heard.
