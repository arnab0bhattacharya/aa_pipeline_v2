The pestilence had passed over England like a dark tide, leaving in its receding wake the shattered remnants of our nation, much as a storm leaves the once peaceful shore strewn with the wrecks of vessels and the bodies of the drowned. I had taken refuge in a solitary cottage upon the outskirts of the village of Avesbury, in that county of Berkshire which, in former days of prosperity, had smiled with verdant meadows and abundant harvests. Now, the fields lay fallow, and the homes of men were become the sepulchres of their hopes; yet nature, unheeding of human woe, had begun to clothe the earth anew in a garment of dreadful and unfeeling beauty, as though to mock the desolation of the survivors.

It was in this state of bitter isolation that I dwelt, a widow bereft of all that rendered existence precious. My two children, my sweet Clara and my little Edmund, had been snatched from my embrace by the remorseless malady. I had watched the light fade from their eyes; I had felt the coldness of their hands grow stony in my own; and when at length the grave had closed over them, my heart, too, was buried in that narrow house of clay. I conversed with no one, save when the necessities of this ruined life compelled me to exchange brief and mournful words with the few villagers who, like myself, dragged out a joyless existence among the ruins of our former happiness. They regarded me with pity, but I heeded not their compassion; I desired only to be left alone with my grief, which was the sole remaining treasure of my desolate soul.

It was in the autumn of that year, when the winds began to howl through the leafless branches and the rain beat against my casement with a melancholy cadence, that the woman who styled herself the keeper of the parish poor approached my door. She was a creature of rugged frame and weathered visage, upon whose countenance the sufferings of the times had engraven lines of severe and unyielding resolve. She came with a request—a request that tore open my bleeding heart and forced me to confront the very image of my own despair.

"There is a child," she said, in a voice that strove to be gentle but which grated harshly upon my wounded spirit. "A boy, left without father or mother in the late visitation. He is but six years of age, and he has none to give him shelter. I beseech you, Mrs. Marchand, to take him in. He is silent and gentle, and will be no burden to you."

At these words a shudder convulsed my frame. "Take in a child!" I exclaimed, and the vehemence of my emotion surprised even myself. "Do you ask me to take in a child, when my own lie mouldering in the churchyard beneath the yew? Do you bid me open my heart to a stranger, when the wounds of my bereavement are yet fresh and bleeding? No! I will not. I cannot. The sight of a child's face would drive me to madness. I am utterly forsaken, and I desire to remain so. Go, and trouble me no more."

The woman looked at me with an expression of sorrowful reproach, but she did not press her suit. She departed, and I was left to the solitude I had so fiercely claimed. Yet, even as I stood at the window watching her retreating figure, I observed that she was not alone. Beside her, a little form was walking—a boy, clad in garments of coarse and sombre hue. His face was pale, and his dark eyes, large and luminous, were fixed upon my dwelling with a gaze of such profound and silent sorrow that, despite myself, I felt a pang of something akin to compassion. But I crushed the feeling as it arose. I had sworn never again to expose my heart to the agonizing tenderness of a parent's love, and I turned away, resolving to dismiss the image from my mind.

But the image would not be dismissed. On the following day, as I sat in my lonely parlour engaged in some mechanical task, I heard a faint sound at my door. I arose and opened it, and there, upon the threshold, stood the boy. He was alone. He looked up at me with those same dark, sorrowful eyes, but he spoke no word. His silence was more eloquent than any speech; it was the silence of a creature who has known suffering too deep for utterance, who has been abandoned by all the world and has none to whom he may appeal for succour.

"Go away," I said, in a voice that trembled despite my efforts to render it stern. "I cannot receive you. You must not come here again."

The boy did not move. He continued to gaze at me with that look of unutterable melancholy, and I saw that his small frame was trembling with the cold, for the autumn wind was piercing, and his clothing was scant and insufficient. For a moment I hesitated. A voice within me whispered that here was a being more desolate than myself—a child, orphaned and friendless, cast upon a world that had no pity for the helpless. But the memory of my own lost ones rose up before me with overwhelming force. I saw Clara's dying face; I heard Edmund's feeble voice calling upon me in his last hour; and the agony of those recollections hardened my heart against the suppliant at my door.

"Go!" I repeated, more fiercely. "I have said I will not receive you. Why do you torment me?"

The boy slowly turned and descended the steps. He walked away with a gait that was feeble and uncertain, and I closed the door with a violence that shook the frail tenement. Then, throwing myself upon my chair, I gave way to a paroxysm of grief and self-reproach such as I had not experienced since the hour of my children's death. I wept until my tears were exhausted, and when at length the storm of my passion had subsided, I was left in a state of torpor and despondency more profound than any I had before endured.

But the boy returned. Day after day, he presented himself at my door. Sometimes he came in the morning, when the mist hung heavy over the fields and the air was raw with the chill of approaching winter; sometimes in the evening, when the sun set in a sky of lurid and portentous crimson, casting a blood-red light upon the ruins of the village. He never spoke. He merely stood upon the threshold and looked at me with those great, appealing eyes, and as often as I bade him depart, so often did he return. The villagers, it appeared, had wearied of his importunity, or perhaps they were themselves too burdened with their own miseries to afford him a refuge. He had fixed upon me as his last hope, and with a constancy and patience that were almost superhuman, he persisted in his silent appeal.

I know not how it came to pass that, by degrees, the ice around my heart began to yield. Perhaps it was that the very persistence of the child's devotion awakened in me a sense of my own cruelty; perhaps it was that his silence and his sorrow spoke a language which my soul, steeped in grief, could not fail to comprehend. I began to look for his coming, and when he appeared, I no longer bade him go with harsh and angry words, but stood gazing at him in silence, while a conflict of emotions raged within my breast. Pity, tenderness, remorse, and fear contended for the mastery; and I was torn between the desire to clasp the child to my bosom and the dread of reopening those wounds which time had begun, however imperfectly, to heal.

One evening, as the tempest raged with unwonted fury and the rain descended in torrents, I heard the familiar sound at my door. I opened it, and there stood the boy, drenched and shivering, his pale face upturned to mine, his dark eyes filled with a mute and piteous entreaty that pierced the very depths of my being. For a long moment I stood motionless, gazing upon that frail and trembling form, while the storm howled around us and the wind swept the rain into my face. Then, with a cry that was half a sob and half a prayer, I fell upon my knees and drew the child into my arms.

"Come in," I murmured, as I pressed him to my breast and felt the coldness of his little frame against my own. "Come in, my poor, poor child. I will not send you away again."

He made no answer, but his arms crept about my neck, and I felt his tears upon my cheek—tears which, mingling with my own, seemed to wash away some portion of the bitterness and despair that had so long possessed my soul. In that hour of darkness and of storm, as I clasped to my heart the orphan who had sought so persistently for shelter at my door, I seemed to hear, amid the tumult of the elements, a voice whispering that the Almighty had not utterly forsaken me—that even in the depths of my affliction, there remained a duty, a labour of love, which, if I embraced it, might yet rescue me from the abyss of despair and restore to my wounded spirit some faint semblance of peace.
