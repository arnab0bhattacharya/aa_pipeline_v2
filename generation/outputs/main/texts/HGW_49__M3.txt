It is a curious thing how the approach of matrimony, which society declares to be the crowning achievement of a woman’s life, can so closely resemble the atmosphere of a beleaguered city expecting an imminent siege. I had been engaged for three months to Mr. Henry Plender, a man of considerable means and a partner in the firm of Plender, Cootes, and Plender, situated in Mincing Lane. He was a widower of forty-seven, very kindly, very well-groomed, and possessed of a comfortable, red-brick villa at Ealing. He was, in every material respect, an excellent match. I was thirty-one, and for ten of those years I had earned my own living as a typist at the Imperial Chemical Company’s offices in Leadenhall Street. 

It was on a Thursday evening, the eve of our wedding, that I found myself making my way back to the City. The wedding was to be a quiet affair at St. Botolph’s in the morning. My trousseau was packed, my lodgings in Bloomsbury were already dismantled, and I was to spend the night with my cousin Edith in Islington. Yet, instead of going directly to Islington, I had taken the omnibus to the corner of Leadenhall Street. I told myself I had left a brass-handled paper-knife in my desk, but in truth, I was drawn back by an irresistible, almost morbid curiosity to see the place one last time.

The City was wonderfully empty. The great tide of clerks and typists and stockbrokers had ebbed hours ago, leaving behind only the echoes of their footsteps and a few scattered clouds of dust in the summer twilight. I walked past the Royal Exchange, where the shutters were down and the pavement was littered with the torn fragments of the afternoon’s financial papers, and turned into the narrow canyon of our street. The Imperial Chemical Company’s office was a tall, narrow building of yellow brick, indistinguishable from its neighbours save for a heavy brass plate beside the door. I let myself in with my latchkey. The lower stairs were unlit, and I had to feel my way up to the first floor, where our typing-room looked out over the rooftops.

I unlocked the door of the typing-room and went in. The air was close and still, heavy with the mingled odours of typewriter oil, ozone from the electric lamps, and stale paper. The long, bare deal tables were clear of work. Against the window stood my machine—a robust, somewhat battered Yost. I went over to it and sat down on the high stool, laying my fingers on the cold metal of the keys. It is a strange thing, perhaps, to feel affection for a mechanical contrivance, but I confess I felt a sudden, sharp pang of tenderness for that instrument. For ten years it had been my comrade. It had given me my fifty shillings a week, and with those fifty shillings I had maintained my lodgings, bought my clothes, and preserved that absolute, untrammelled independence which is, I suppose, the most exquisite of modern luxuries.

Mr. Plender did not understand this. He was all kindness, but he viewed my employment as a temporary misfortune, a sort of social disease from which marriage was the proper cure. “My dear Millicent,” he had said to me only the week before, with that paternal smile which he doubtless considered very winning, “you must not trouble yourself with ledgers and shorthand any longer. I have quite enough for two, and I cannot have my wife associated with a commercial office. People would think I could not provide for you.” He had said it with an air of immense finality, as though he were announcing a new law of nature. And I, in the flurry of preparations and the pressure of convention, had simply nodded and smiled back.

But now, sitting in the quiet twilight of the empty office, I began to feel a very genuine alarm. I thought of the villa at Ealing, of the spacious drawing-room with its heavy plush curtains and its inevitable aspidistra, of the servants who would answer my bell, and of the long, empty afternoons stretching out before me into an interminable future. I thought of Mr. Plender’s slippers warming by the grate, and his gentle, unquestioning assumption that I would be content. It seemed to me, in that moment, a very suffocating prospect. I had been a free woman, earning a definite wage, and now I was to be a dependent, a ornamental appendage to a man’s establishment.

I stood up abruptly and went to the window. The City lay spread out beneath me in the dusk, a forest of chimney-pots and slanted roofs, with the great dome of St. Paul’s looming darkly against a pale green sky. Far below, a newspaper boy was calling the evening editions, his voice rising thin and clear in the still air. I thought of the vast, restless machine of commerce that had been pulsing here all day, and of my own small, definite part in it. I had transcribed letters, typed out invoices, and sent telegrams that had helped, in some microscopic way, to turn the wheels of that great machine. And tomorrow, I was to step out of it all, to become merely Mrs. Henry Plender, of Ealing.

I turned back to my desk and began to open the drawers, pretending to search for the paper-knife. It was not there, but I found a half-used reel of typewriter ribbon, a few loose stamps, and a small, battered notebook in which I had once kept a record of my shorthand speeds. I took the notebook and the reel of ribbon and slipped them into my jacket pocket. They were useless things, but I wanted some tangible relic of my working life.

As I did so, I heard a sound in the corridor outside. It was a heavy, deliberate footstep. I confess my heart gave a jump; the office was supposed to be entirely deserted. The door opened, and Mr. Cootes, the senior partner, appeared in the doorway. He was a tall, elderly man with a grey, stubbly beard and a perpetual air of mild distraction. He was carrying a dispatch-case and looked thoroughly surprised to see me.

“Miss Vane!” he exclaimed. “I thought you had left us for good. What are you doing here at this hour?”

“I came to fetch a paper-knife, Mr. Cootes,” I said, feeling suddenly very foolish. “I—I forgot it.”

“Paper-knife?” he repeated, staring at me. “My dear girl, you don’t need a paper-knife where you’re going. I came back to fetch a contract I had mislaid. But never mind that. So tomorrow is the great day, eh? We shall all miss you very much. You have been an excellent typist—quick, accurate, and very intelligent. I don’t mind telling you that it will be extremely difficult to replace you.”

He said it in his vague, abstracted way, but the words gave me an extraordinary pleasure. I had not realised how much I had wanted to hear them. I thanked him, and he stood for a moment, looking at me with a kind of puzzled sympathy.

“And so you are giving it all up,” he went on. “Marriage. The villa. The whole establishment. Well, well. I suppose it is the natural thing. But I must say, Miss Vane, I have always thought you had a remarkably fine business head. It seems a pity, in a way, to bury it in Ealing. However, I dare say you know your own mind.”

He wished me good night, gathered up his dispatch-case, and departed, leaving me alone once more. But the room seemed different now. The quietness was no longer oppressive; it was charged with a sudden, startling significance. I looked at my typewriter, and then I looked out at the City, and a very remarkable idea took hold of my brain.

I sat down again on the high stool and faced the machine. The keys gleamed dully in the half-light. I thought of Mr. Plender’s kindly, handsome face, of his comfortable income, of his red-brick villa, and of his absolute, unshakeable conviction that a woman’s place was in the home. And then I thought of my fifty shillings a week, of my small, bright lodgings in Bloomsbury, of the omnibus rides in the morning, of the bustle and rush of the office, and of the immense, solid satisfaction of being a free agent in a world of workers.

It was, I suppose, a moment of what the philosophers call clear vision. The scales fell from my eyes with a literal, physical abruptness. I saw my future stretching out before me, not as a bed of roses, but as a long, slow suffocation. I saw myself becoming a piece of expensive furniture, a decorative appendage to a man’s establishment, and I knew, with a sudden, overwhelming certainty, that I could not bear it.

I stood up, took my hat from the peg, and went out, locking the door behind me. I went down the stairs and out into the street. The air was cool and fresh. I walked rapidly, my mind made up. I would not go to Islington. I would go back to my lodgings, which were not entirely dismantled, and I would write a note to Mr. Plender. I would tell him, as kindly and as firmly as I could, that I had decided not to marry him. I would tell him that I preferred my typewriter to his drawing-room, and my fifty shillings to his thousands. I would tell him that I was a worker, and that I intended to remain a worker.

I reached the corner of the street and hailed a passing hansom. 

“Bloomsbury!” I called out to the driver, and climbed in. 

As the cab rattled away over the cobblestones, I put my hand in my pocket and felt the reel of typewriter ribbon. It seemed to me the most precious thing in the world.
