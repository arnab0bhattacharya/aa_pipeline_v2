Eleanor Ashworth had always believed herself possessed of a singular clarity in the reading of human character, and chief among her fixed conclusions was the conviction that her elder sister, Helena, was entirely devoid of strong feeling. Helena’s temper was so uniformly composed, her address so flawlessly mild, that she navigated the drawing rooms of their Devonshire neighbourhood with a placidity that Eleanor found not merely tiresome, but almost suspicious. Where Eleanor was quick to feel, and quicker to speak, Helena met every occurrence—the utmost praise or the sharpest slight—with the same serene smile. She had refused two very eligible offers of marriage in the course of three years with so little visible agitation that Eleanor had almost fancied her made of marble.

The family resided at Thornwood, a respectable, if somewhat weather-beaten, estate nestled in a valley three miles from Exeter. It was an arrangement that threw the sisters much together; and as Helena had lately assumed the entire management of the household, following their mother’s lingering indisposition, her time was chiefly divided between the still-room and the accounts. It was in the pursuit of the latter occupation that Eleanor found herself one rainy morning in Helena’s private sitting-room, seeking a particular receipt book that her sister had mislaid.

Helena was gone to the village. The room was quiet, and the fire burnt low, casting a dull light over the orderly desk. Eleanor, after searching the obvious places with an impatience natural to her disposition, was drawn at last to open a small mahogany writing-desk that stood in the corner of the room. She was not, by nature, given to prying; but the receipt book might, she reasoned, have been slipped beneath the desk’s fitted lid, and the lock, which was always precarious, yielded at once to her touch. The book was not there. What she found instead was a packet of letters, tied firmly with a ribbon of deep blue, and bearing, as she could not help but observe, the direction of a gentleman’s hand.

Eleanor’s conscience was not so entirely dead as to permit her to untie the ribbon without a struggle. She stood for a moment, the packet in her hand, feeling all the impropriety of her situation. But curiosity, that most powerful of human impulses, had always held a stronger sway over her than principle; and the sight of her sister’s name written with such evident tenderness upon the outer fold was enough to overcome her remaining scruples. She untied the ribbon, and spread the letters before her.

They were from Mr. Arthur Penrose. Mr. Penrose was the younger son of a respectable family in the neighbourhood, a man of pleasing address and moderate fortune, who had, the previous autumn, spent six weeks in the vicinity on a shooting engagement. He had been a frequent visitor at Thornwood, and Eleanor had thought him agreeable enough, though she had marked his attention to Helena as nothing more than the common civility of a gentleman towards the mistress of the house. That he had written to her sister, and written repeatedly, was astonishing; but that Helena had preserved the letters, and preserved them in a place of such careful concealment, was more astonishing still.

The contents of the letters were such as to leave no room for doubt as to their nature. Mr. Penrose wrote with an ardour that was, perhaps, more remarkable for its sincerity than its elegance. He spoke of his admiration in terms that Eleanor, who had read a good many novels, found both moving and slightly overheated. He lamented the distance that circumstance and prudence had placed between them; he spoke of his hopes, his fears, and his unshakeable devotion; and he begged, in terms of the utmost respect, that Helena would not condemn him for the freedom of his expressions. The latest letter was dated only a fortnight before, and contained a passage which Eleanor read twice over, so entirely did it overturn every notion she had formed:

*“I know too well,”* Mr. Penrose had written, *“that you have been urged by those around you to look higher, and that your own judgment has, with that steadiness which I admire even while it wounds me, inclined you to dismiss my suit. I will not distress you by repeating my arguments. I ask only that you will believe me when I say that my happiness is so entirely bound up in yours, that I could not, without a struggle, resign every hope of calling you my own. If, however, your decision is fixed, I shall endeavour to submit to it with such grace as a man may summon who has lost the only object that ever gave his life meaning. Command my absence, and I shall obey; but know that my heart follows you into every room of Thornwood, and will follow you, my dearest Helena, to the end.”*

Eleanor let the letter fall upon the desk. She was overpowered by the discovery, not so much by the fact of Mr. Penrose’s attachment—though that was surprising enough—but by the revelation it contained of her sister’s hidden life. That Helena, who had seemed so indifferent, so composedly superior to the common weaknesses of her sex, should have received such a letter, and kept it, and concealed it, argued a depth of feeling that Eleanor had never once suspected. She thought again of Helena’s refusal of those two eligible offers, which the family had attributed to a prudent coldness of temper. Could it be, then, that Helena had refused them because her heart was already engaged? And if so, what was Eleanor to make of her sister’s present conduct—the continued correspondence, the secrecy, the careful composure that had so often been mistaken for insensibility?

She was still sitting in this attitude of bewildered reflection when she heard the sound of carriage wheels upon the gravel. Helena had returned. Eleanor, with a sudden consciousness of guilt, gathered up the letters, crammed them back into the desk, and forced the lid shut. She had barely regained her seat by the window when the door opened, and her sister entered, bringing with her the fresh air of the rainy afternoon and an expression of tranquil composure that now struck Eleanor as nothing short of extraordinary.

“Well, Eleanor,” said Helena, untying her bonnet with her usual unruffled air, “I have brought home the silk for Mama’s new gown. Mrs. Fowler assures me it is the precise shade she desired, though I confess I think it a trifle dark. And I met Mr. Penrose on the road. He enquired very particularly after you.”

Eleanor coloured deeply, and stammered out a reply she could not afterwards recall. She watched her sister move about the room, putting away her things, stirring the fire, settling into her chair with the quiet regularity that had always characterized her movements. It was the same Helena she had always known; and yet she was not the same. Eleanor now beheld her as a woman concealing a profound attachment, maintaining, under the very eye of her family, a secret correspondence, and wearing, with perfect success, the mask of indifference.

The question now pressing upon Eleanor with all the weight of its urgency was whether she should confess what she had seen. To say nothing would be to continue in ignorance of a matter that evidently concerned her sister’s happiness most nearly; to speak would be to acknowledge an act of intrusion for which she could offer no adequate defence. She might, she thought, approach the subject indirectly—might lead Helena to speak of Mr. Penrose, and so discover, without betrayal, what her sister’s true sentiments were. But Eleanor knew her own address too well to trust it in so delicate a negotiation. She was apt to speak hastily, to press a point beyond the bounds of tact, and to betray, by the very eagerness of her manner, the interest she wished to conceal.

Helena, meanwhile, had taken up her book, and was reading with that appearance of undisturbed tranquillity which Eleanor now found almost insupportable. How, she wondered, could a woman who had received such a letter as she had just read sit down, an hour later, to a novel, with all the composure of a person who had never known an anxious thought? The contrast was bewildering. It was as if she had discovered, beneath the surface of a calm and placid lake, some hidden current of which she had never suspected the existence.

Eleanor resolved, at length, upon a middle course. She would say nothing of the letters—nothing, at least, until she had had time to consider the matter more fully. But she would observe her sister with a new and careful attention, and she would not rest until she had discovered, by whatever means her conscience would permit, whether Helena’s heart was indeed engaged, and whether Mr. Penrose’s suit had been dismissed, or merely deferred. She could not be easy until she knew the whole; and yet she felt, with a pang of self-reproach, that her chief difficulty would lie not in the discovery of the truth, but in the concealment of her own folly.
