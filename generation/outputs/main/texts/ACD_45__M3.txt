I had been resting in my armchair upon the evening of the fourteenth of November, nursing a pipe and watching the rain streak the glass of my Baker Street windows, when my friend Mr. Penrith entered unannounced. He was a young man, steady in his bearing, who served as first officer aboard the merchant vessel *Cormorant*, and I had done him a professional service two years before in a matter of forged shipping manifests. He shook the wet from his coat and stood with his hands clasped behind his back, regarding me with the uneasy look of a man who has walked into trouble through no fault of his own.

"You are in some anxiety," said I, motioning him to the chair opposite.

"I am, Mr. Bellamy, and I should be grateful for your advice. I have stumbled into a business I do not understand, and it has the look of something ugly."

He drew a folded telegram from his pocket and handed it to me. It was addressed to him at his lodgings in Southampton and read: FAMILY BURNED LETTER UNREAD. YOUR ROOMS SEARCHED TONIGHT. COME TO LONDON DIRECTLY. STOP. It was unsigned.

"I will tell you the whole of it from the beginning," said he, "for I confess I am at a loss." He settled himself and spoke in the quick, precise manner of a man accustomed to giving reports. "We put in at Buenos Aires on the outbound leg in September. A passenger came aboard, a Mr. Edgar Vane, an Englishman by his speech, though he had the yellow look of a man whose lungs are gone. He was thin to the point of emaciation, and he coughed without ceasing, yet he bore himself with a kind of desperate cheerfulness. He had booked passage to Southampton and no farther. He ate alone and spoke to no one on the ship save myself, and that chiefly to ask questions about the port—tides, pilot arrangements, the hour of docking.

"During the crossing he grew worse. By the time we were abreast of the Azores he could not come on deck. I visited him in his cabin, for he had no servant and seemed to have no friends aboard. He was sitting up in his bunk with a writing-case on his knees, sealing a letter. He looked at me for a long moment, and then he said: 'Mr. Penrith, I am a dying man. I shall not see Southampton, I think. I have a cousin in England, a Mrs. Calloway, who lives at a house called Foxglove Lodge, on the Old Shoreham Road. Will you carry this letter to her when you dock? It is a private matter, a family matter, and I would ask you to place it in her hands and no other's.'

"I told him I would do so. He pressed the letter upon me and made me promise I would deliver it the same day I came ashore. He seemed easier in his mind after that. He died three nights later, four hundred miles south of the Lizard. We buried him at sea."

Penrith paused and turned his hat in his hands. "I came ashore at Southampton on the morning of the twelfth. I made enquiries and found Foxgrove Lodge without difficulty. It is a substantial house, standing in its own grounds, with a wall about it. I presented myself at the door and asked for Mrs. Calloway. A woman answered—tall, dark, perhaps forty, with the bearing of a woman who is used to managing a household. I gave my name and said I had a letter for her from her cousin, delivered as a dying request.

"She did not ask me in. She stood in the doorway and looked at the sealed envelope for a long moment. Then she said, in a voice I can only describe as controlled: 'My cousin is dead?' I said he was. She said: 'How did he seem at the last?' I told her he was resigned and that his mind was clear. She took the letter from me, held it by the corner as though it were something unclean, and then, without breaking the seal, she turned and threw it upon the fire that was burning in the hall grate. I saw it blacken and curl and the wax run. She watched it until nothing remained but ash. Then she turned back to me and said: 'You have done your duty, Mr. Penrith. I am obliged to you. Good day.' And she closed the door."

"And that was all?" said I.

"That was all. I thought it strange, but a man's grief takes odd forms, and I had no reason to linger. I went back to my lodgings in Above Bar Street and thought no more of it. Yesterday morning I returned to my rooms after breakfast and found the place in disorder. My chest was open, my papers scattered, the mattress cut, and the boards of the floor pried up in two places. Nothing was taken, for I had nothing worth taking. But whoever had been there had searched with a thoroughness that spoke of desperation. My landlady had heard nothing. The police could make nothing of it. Then, this morning, the telegram arrived."

I sat for some moments considering the matter. "Tell me," said I, "did you observe the seal upon the letter?"

"It was plain red wax, stamped with a device I did not recognise. A bird, I think. A heron or a crane."

"And the envelope? What colour was it?"

"White. Good quality. The address was written in a fine hand, but shaking—the hand of a sick man."

"And Mrs. Calloway did not open it?"

"She did not."

"Then she knew what was in it, or believed she did, and she did not wish it read. That is significant. A woman who burns a letter unread is not destroying a message. She is destroying a thing. The thing itself—the paper, the writing, the proof of its existence—is what she fears. You have carried something home in your coat, Mr. Penrith, or rather the knowledge that such a thing once existed and was sent to her. Someone else knows of it, and that someone did not know it had been destroyed. They searched your rooms expecting to find it."

"But it is gone," said he. "It is ash."

"Which leaves us with the question of who else knew that Vane had written it, and who had an interest in possessing it. You said he ate alone and spoke to no one save yourself."

"That is so."

"Then we must look at what Vane left behind him. Did he travel with luggage?"

"A single trunk. It was taken in charge by the shipping agent at Southampton, for he had no instructions regarding it."

"There is our thread," said I, rising. "If Vane carried something besides the letter—a document, a map, an object of value—then it may still be in his trunk. The person who searched your rooms was looking for the letter, but the trunk may contain what they truly want. You say the shipping agent holds it?"

"Mr. Grieves, of Canute Road."

"We shall go down to Southampton on the morning train," said I. "But first, tell me one thing more. The seal—a heron or a crane. Are you certain?"

He considered. "A heron. Yes. I am nearly certain."

I drew my notebook towards me. "There is a family of that name connected with a rubber concern in the Argentine, the Vane-Merrick company, which was the subject of a Chancery suit three years ago. The matter concerned the disposition of shares held in trust. If your Mr. Edgar Vane was the younger brother of Henry Vane, who died in Buenos Aires in eighty-eight, then that letter was almost certainly a codicil or a confession relating to the trust. Mrs. Calloway is the widow of the third brother, who died at sea in eighty-seven. She burns the letter because it threatens her interest. And someone who knew of its existence—perhaps the agent who watched Vane write it, perhaps another passenger under another name—has followed you to England."

Penrith looked grave. "Then I am in danger."

"You have been in danger since you took the letter aboard. But the danger is passing. The letter is gone, and once that is known, you are merely a man who carried an envelope. The real danger lies elsewhere now, and it is for us to find it before it finds us. Be at Waterloo at eight o'clock. We shall see what Mr. Grieves has in his warehouse, and what the trunk of the dead man can tell us that the living will not."

He nodded and left me. I sat for a time with my pipe going out and the rain still falling, turning the matter in my mind. A heron in red wax, a woman who burns what she will not read, and a search conducted in silence—these were the pieces. I had no doubt that by morning I should see the shape they made.
