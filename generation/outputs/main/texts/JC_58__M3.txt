The yellow fog of the river had crept up that evening into the quiet by-street, and it seemed to Ivan Kyrilovich as though the very dampness of the brickwork were a subtle, penetrating thing, creeping into the marrow of his bones, into the hidden recesses of his conscience. The gaslight in the narrow room flickered, casting upon the stained wallpaper shadows that wavered with a sort of treacherous uncertainty. He stood by the sash window, looking out upon nothing but a veil of murky obscurity, and he heard behind him the faint, rhythmical sound of her breathing. She sat in the armchair, her hands lying idle in her lap, possessing the profound stillness of an Englishwoman who waits for a confession she does not know how to demand.

He had received the message that morning. It had come in the form of a torn scrap of paper, thrust beneath the door by an unknown hand, bearing only two words in Cyrillic script, an imperative of blood and iron. To-morrow he would be crossing the water, returning to the vast, silent land of snow and absolutism, not as a returning exile, but as an instrument of an inexorable destiny. The shadow of a great moral darkness had fallen upon his soul, and he felt the weight of it in the silence of the room.

“Why do you stand so far away?” she asked, her voice floating across the dim apartment, faint and clear, with a vibration that was the first tremor of an awakening dread. “You are not here, Ivan. You are very far.”

He turned slowly, and the gaslight touched the hard line of his jaw, the deep-set, brooding eyes that looked at her as if from the depths of an abyssal sea. He struggled against the spell of his own duplicity, a duplicity that was not of his making, but of the vast, shadowy committee that had shaped his life. He had married her in a moment of impulse, seeking an anchor in the tranquillity of her nature, a refuge from the harsh, ringing fanaticism of his youth. But the past had reached out and claimed him, with a grip of iron beneath a velvet glove.

“I am thinking,” he said, haltingly, the words falling from his lips with a leaden weight. “It is nothing. The fog, perhaps. It makes a man see things.”

She rose and came toward him, her grey dress trailing softly on the worn carpet, and she laid her cool, firm hand upon his arm. He felt the warmth of her presence, the untroubled innocence of her belief in him, and a profound revulsion shook his heart—a revulsion not against her, but against the merciless logic of his own existence. He was a man marked for a deed of darkness, and he was standing in the sanctuary of her trust, a traitor to the very light of her eyes.

“You are going away,” she whispered, the words breaking from her with a sudden, piercing intuition that made him wince. “I have known it for days. You have the look of a man who is taking leave of the world.”

He did not deny it. To deny it would be to add a cowardly falsehood to the sin of concealment. He remained silent, his gaze fixed upon the pale oval of her face, upon the fair hair that framed it in the flickering light. She seemed to him, at that moment, the embodiment of all that was remote and unattainable, the symbol of a peace that he had forfeited by the rashness of his convictions. The committee had spoken, and he was no longer Ivan, the husband, the quiet lodger in a London by-street; he was a force, a projected weapon, a man already half-draped in the shroud of his own doom.

“Who has called you back?” she asked, her voice trembling now with a vague terror, as if the shadows in the corners of the room had suddenly become alive with menacing shapes. “What is it that you must do?”

He made a gesture of helplessness, a slow, weary movement of his hand that seemed to encompass the whole tragic futility of his position. “There are things,” he muttered, “that a man cannot explain. They are not of his choosing. They are a part of him, as the roots of a tree are a part of the earth.”

She did not understand. She could not understand. Her mind was a clear, English stream, flowing over a bed of rational certainties, incapable of fathoming the dark, turbulent depths of the Slavic soul. She believed him to be a political exile, a man of letters, a victim of censorship. She did not know that the quiet hands, which had caressed her hair, were destined within a few days to be steeped in the violent reality of a political execution.

“I have trusted you, Ivan,” she said, and there was a note of reproach in her tone, a gentle, heart-breaking reproach that smote him more fiercely than any accusation of treachery. “I have asked for nothing. But now, when you are going, you must tell me. I am your wife.”

The word fell upon his ears with a cruel irony. Wife. What was a wife to the great, impersonal cause that demanded his allegiance? A mere incident, a breath of tenderness in a life dedicated to destruction. And yet, looking into her eyes, he felt the foundations of his fanaticism tremble. He was a man standing upon the edge of a precipice, drawn forward by an invisible current, and clinging to the frail reed of human affection.

“You will know soon enough,” he said, and his voice was thick and uneven, as if the words were forced through a stricture of pain. “It is a journey. A long journey. I may not return.”

She clung to him then, with a sudden, fierce abandon, and he felt her tears upon his cheek, hot and scalding, like the very tears of a remorse that was yet to come. He held her in his arms, and in the exulting consciousness of his own weakness, he felt the overwhelming desire to cast aside the burden of his mission, to remain in the quiet of this room, to be only a man, a husband, a creature of simple, human affections. But the spell of the committee was upon him, stronger than the spell of her love. It was a spell woven in the dim twilight of revolutionary cellars, in the murk of Siberian prisons, a spell that had the sanctity of a religion and the inevitability of death.

He disengaged himself gently, and looked down at her, seeing the fascination of her person in the yellow fog of the London night as if in the blaze of noonday. He had already paid for his convictions with the sacrifice of his own identity. He was no longer a man, but a symbol, a shadow moving toward a preordained act.

“I must go,” he said, and the words were spoken with a finality that closed the door of his heart against her. “Do not ask me why. It is a thing that stands between me and the freedom of the world.”

She drew back, her face white and drawn, her eyes wide with a terror that was born of the unknown. She had lost the notion of all impossibility. Anything could happen on this night of wonder. He could see the dimples on her cheeks, the trembling of her lips, the faint, pathetic droop of her shoulders. She became quiet and wily, with the instinctive wile of a woman who seeks to hold a man by the sheer force of her weakness.

“Will you write to me?” she asked, coaxingly, her voice faint as if from the depths of a dream. “Will you let me know that you are alive?”

He made no movement. He was listening to the distant rumble of a cart in the street, a sound that seemed to him like the rolling of a drum, a summons to a battle from which there was no return. He wrestled with the spectre of his own destiny, and the spectre arose, standing by her side, pitiless and secret, with a finger on its pale lips.

“I will write,” he said, knowing that he lied, knowing that he would never write again, that the silence of the grave would be his only message.

He stooped low, pressed the hem of her skirt to his lips, with a muttered command—“Tell them I would not stay”—and was gone suddenly from her, silent, without as much as a footfall in the foggy night.

The next evening the mail-boat was leaving the Channel port, and Ivan Kyrilovich stood upon the deck, looking back at the receding lights of England. The sea was a heaving expanse of blackness, and the wind blew cold and keen, carrying with it the salt spray that stung his face. He had left behind him the quiet by-street, the yellow fog, the woman who had trusted him. He was returning to the land of his birth, a land of snow and absolutism, where the very air was heavy with the scent of impending doom.

He thought of her, sitting in the armchair in the dim room, her hands lying idle in her lap, waiting for a letter that would never come. He thought of the man he was to kill, a man he had never seen, a man who represented to him only a system, an abstraction, a thing of darkness and oppression. He felt no hatred, no personal animosity. He was merely an instrument, a tool in the hands of the invisible committee, a man who had surrendered his will to the collective will of the revolution.

The Channel was rough, and the ship pitched and rolled in the darkness. Ivan stood at the rail, his eyes fixed upon the eastern horizon, where a faint, grey light was beginning to spread across the sky. He was alone with his thoughts, alone with the vast, impersonal forces that had shaped his life. The moral darkness that had fallen upon him was like a shroud, and he felt the chill of it in his heart.

He thought of the words he had spoken to her: “It is a thing that stands between me and the freedom of the world.” And he knew, with a sudden, piercing clarity, that the thing which stood between him and freedom was not the cause, not the committee, not the act of violence he was about to commit. It was the silence itself, the profound, abysmal silence of a man who had chosen to live in the shadow of a lie, and who was about to pay for that choice with the soul of a woman and the life of a man.

The lights of England had vanished behind him, swallowed up in the impenetrable darkness of the night. He turned his face toward the east, toward the vast, silent land that awaited him, and he felt the spell of the committee descend upon him once more, with its finger on its pale lips. The ship ploughed on through the black water, and the wind blew cold, and the night closed in around him like a prison.
