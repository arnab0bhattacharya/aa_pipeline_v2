The morning mist still clung to the willows bordering the slow reaches of the Arrow, turning the familiar landscape into a pale version of its summer self, when Edward Leatherhead walked out to take a final survey of his inheritance. It was an inheritance that presented itself to his mind in somewhat the same ghostly and indeterminate fashion as that morning mist: there was the fair outward show of the land—the broad acres, the thriving homestead, the tenantry who touched their hats with that comfortable deference which belongs to an old-established name—but within, where the solid substance of pecuniary value should have lain, there was only a nebulous deficit, a vacuity of debt that had descended upon him with the suddenness of an autumn fog. His father, good easy man, had been one of those persons whose benevolence is exercised chiefly at the expense of their creditors; a man who could not pass a broken-winded horse in a fair without purchasing it at a price that implied a compliment to the seller’s judgment, and who lent money to needy neighbours with a punctuality that would have been highly commendable had it not been for the unfortunate circumstance that the money was not his own to lend. The elder Leatherhead had died with the character of an open-handed gentleman, and had left his son the melancholy privilege of discovering that open-handedness, when it takes the form of signing promissory notes without reckoning the interest, is a virtue that may be classed among the expensive luxuries of life.

Edward stood by the gate of the home meadow, whistling softly to himself—a habit he had when his mind was working at a problem that required a steady and deliberate resolution. He was a young man of five-and-twenty, with a face that bore the stamp of that mixed constitution which is common enough in our Midland counties: a certain squareness of jaw that argued a capacity for obstinate endurance, tempered by eyes of a hazel colour, in which a quicker sensibility might be read. He had been bred to no profession, his father having considered that a Leatherhead of Chilverton must necessarily be above the necessity of trade; and the consequence was that he found himself at an age when most men have already entered upon some calling, equipped with no weapon against adversity but a tolerably good education, a sound constitution, and a not inconsiderable share of that self-reliance which is the slow product of an English country upbringing.

He was turning over in his mind the two courses that lay open to him, and weighing them with that careful balancing of consequences which is perhaps less the result of philosophic training than of sheer necessity, when a voice broke upon his meditations from the other side of the hedge.

"Nay, Mester Edward, yo' munna go for to fret yo'rself to t' bone about they debts. I mind the day when yo'r feyther come home wi' that there gray mare—feyther to the one as foaled in the lower paddock—and paid seven-an'-twenty pound for her, an' she no more worth than fifteen, if she were that. But he were a kind maister, God rest him, an' I dunno as we'd wish it otherwise."

It was old Reuben Mott, the head ploughman, who had been with the Leatherheads for better than forty years, and who had a habit of appearing at gates and hedgerows with the punctuality of a homing bird whenever there was any matter of family concern abroad. He was a short, squarely built man, with a face of that weather-beaten complexion which seems to be the natural product of a Warwickshire soil, and his speech had the broad, deliberate movement of a man whose thoughts are slow to form but sure when they come.

"I'm not fretting, Reuben," said Edward, with a smile that was perhaps a trifle forced. "But a man must look his difficulties in the face, and mine are just now of a sort that won't be stared down. I've had the bailiff's letter, and there's the interest due at Michaelmas besides."

"Ay, ay, the bailiff," said Reuben, shaking his head slowly, as if the word itself carried a weight that required to be shifted from one side of his mind to the other before it could be properly comprehended. "But there's Mester Bartholomew, now—yo'r uncle. He's got brass enough, an' to spare. He could pay it all off wi' what he earns in a month, I'll be bound."

"There's Mester Bartholomew, certainly," said Edward, and there was a slight contraction of his brows as he spoke the name, which showed that this particular remedy was not without its own peculiar bitterness.

Bartholomew Leatherhead was his father's elder brother, a man who had made a considerable fortune in the Birmingham hardware trade, and who had retired some fifteen years since to a property in the neighbourhood of Leamington, where he maintained an establishment of a solidity and respectability that was meant to convey, without the vulgarity of explicit statement, that its owner was a person who had never permitted sentiment to interfere with the main chance. He was a man of sixty, with a hard, shrewd face, in which the small grey eyes had a trick of fixing themselves upon an object with a steadiness that was not so much penetrating as appraising, as if he were habitually estimating the value of whatever came under his notice, whether it were a piece of ironmongery or a human character. He had never forgiven his younger brother for the combination of improvidence and amiability which had led to the gradual alienation of a portion of the family property; and the fact that the elder Leatherhead had preserved, through all his embarrassments, a cheerful and confiding disposition, had only served to deepen that resentment in Bartholomew's mind, for there is nothing which a practical man finds harder to forgive than the spectacle of happiness built upon foundations which he knows to be unsound. It is not the folly itself that offends him, so much as the impunity with which it appears to be attended; and the consciousness that he, who has denied himself a hundred pleasures and curbed a score of generous impulses in the service of prudence, is not visibly happier than the man who has denied himself nothing, breeds in him a species of moral irritation which he calls by the respectable name of disapprobation.

To go to such a man and ask for money was to invite a lecture upon the consequences of extravagance, delivered with that weighty emphasis which belongs to a person who feels that his own conduct furnishes the text. Edward knew this well enough; he had seen his father wince under that emphasis more than once, and the memory of those occasions was fresh enough to make the prospect of a personal application exceedingly distasteful. But there was the alternative—the sale of the land. And here, too, there were considerations which pressed upon him with a force that was not merely sentimental. The Chilverton estate had been in the family for four generations; it was bound up with the name in a way that made the idea of alienating it seem something more than a mere commercial transaction—it was a kind of moral divorce, a separation from the very roots of his identity. And the tenantry, too—men like Reuben Mott, whose whole lives had been passed under the Leatherhead roof-tree—what would become of them under a new landlord, perhaps some stranger from Birmingham or London, who would look upon them and their holdings merely as so many items in a balance-sheet?

He walked on slowly towards the house, turning the matter over and over in his mind with that patient deliberation which is perhaps the surest sign of a character that has not yet been hardened into its final shape. For it is one of the chief advantages of youth that the will is still plastic, capable of being moulded by circumstances, and not yet fixed into that rigid form which makes the mature man's decisions either wholly right or wholly wrong, with very little margin for that gradual process of modification by which the wisest of our judgments are arrived at. Edward was not conscious of any heroic resolve; he was simply a young man in a difficulty, walking home to his breakfast, and trying to see his way clearly through a tangle of considerations in which pride and duty and affection were so inextricably interwoven that it was impossible to give precedence to any one of them without doing some violence to the rest.

As he entered the lane that led to Chilverton House, he met the post-boy bringing up the bag from Warwick, and among the letters was one bearing the Leamington postmark and the precise, angular handwriting of his uncle. Edward broke the seal and read as he walked. It was brief and to the point. Bartholomew had heard, through the customary channels of country gossip, that his nephew's affairs were not in the most satisfactory condition, and he offered, with a plainness that was meant to be kind but which carried with it a certain blunt authority, to advance such sums as might be necessary to clear the encumbrances, upon the understanding that the estate should be conveyed to him as security, and that the management of it should in future be conducted under his supervision. "I have no desire," the letter concluded, "to see the name of Leatherhead brought into the market for the benefit of every hungry creditor in the county; and if you will come to me on Thursday next, we can discuss the matter at length. I am not a man to make conditions that are unreasonable, but I must have security, and I must have control. Your father was an excellent man in his way, but his way was not a way that I could commend."

Edward folded the letter and put it in his breast-pocket, and stood still for a moment in the lane, looking across the fields to where the roof of Chilverton House showed grey above the elms. The mist was lifting now, and the sun was beginning to touch the higher grounds with a warmth that promised a fair day. There was something in the mere physical aspect of the landscape—the familiar hedgerows, the ploughed fields with their dark brown furrows, the distant spire of Chilverton church—that seemed to reinforce the arguments which his feelings had already been putting forward with a force that was not to be resisted. He would go to his uncle on Thursday, and he would accept the conditions, hard as they might be. It was better, after all, to bear the humiliation of dependence than to part with the land that had been the home of his race; better to submit to the practical wisdom of a man who had never made a mistake, than to preserve an independence that would be purchased at the cost of everything that made independence valuable. And who could tell? The control might not be so very intolerable; and perhaps, in the slow course of years, even Bartholomew's hardness might soften a little, under the influence of that closer relationship which common interests and common cares are sometimes able to establish between men who have little else in common. It was a faint hope, perhaps; but the young are licensed in such expectations, and the licence is not the least valuable of the privileges which youth enjoys, since without it the courage to act would sometimes fail us altogether, and we should stand forever hesitating at the cross-roads, unable to take the first step upon a journey that must needs be made.
