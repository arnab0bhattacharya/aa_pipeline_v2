If there lived within the scaly, damp, and weeping bosom of the Thames estuary a mortal who desired nothing beyond a quiet life, that man was Mr. Solomon Pudge. Solomon kept the Ferry at Muckford Creek, a squat, amphibious little hamlet clinging to the skirts of Gravesend, and he lived in a state of perpetual, tremulous anxiety, bobbing upon the muddy waters of his existence like a cork in a tempest, forever expecting the next wave to swamp him entirely. His dwelling, a crazy, leaky tenement of timber and tar, leaned so affectionately towards the river, as if whispering sweet nothings to the tide, that at high water one might have supposed it was making a desperate attempt to plunge itself beneath the waves and end its earthly sufferings. But Mr. Pudge did not wish to end his sufferings; he merely wished them to be quiet. He was a man of a lean and hollow aspect, with a countenance the exact colour and consistency of a dried salt-fish, and he moved about his ferry-boat with an air of profound apology, as though he were exceedingly sorry to charge anyone twopence for the inconvenience of being conveyed across the water.

Mr. Pudge had a daughter, Miss Tilly Pudge, a bouncing, rosy-cheeked, bright-eyed girl of nineteen, who possessed a spirit entirely incompatible with her father’s desire for a tranquil subsistence, and who was, in fact, the very apple of discord in the quiet life he so desperately sought. Tilly was courted—she was courted fiercely, relentlessly, and with a degree of theatrical obstinacy that would have done credit to the London stage—by two men, as opposite in their natures, pursuits, and head-gear as it is possible for two human beings to be.

The first of these was Mr. Ezekiel Flint, a preventive officer of His Majesty’s Customs. Mr. Mr. Flint was a man of a rigid, unbending, and uncompromising character, who carried his chin in the air and his hands in the pockets of his official coat, as if he were defying the entire smugglers' navy to knock a single grain of honesty out of him. He spoke in a loud, dictatorial voice, and had a habit of explaining, to anyone who would listen—and indeed, to many who would not—that the Law was a sacred institution, and that he, personally, was its chief earthly representative upon the marshes. He courted Tilly Pudge with a heavy, serious, and statistical eloquence, offering her, in lieu of poetry, the comforting reflection that she would be the wife of a man who had a pension to look forward to, and who could boast of having seized, in his time, no less than four hundred and seventeen tubs of contraband spirits.

His rival was Mr. Jasper Roke, a personage of a very different kidney. Mr. Roke was a smuggler—a bold, dashing, romantic smuggler, with a wet tarpaulin coat, a reckless eye, and a laugh that echoed across the desolate marshes like the caw of a joyous rook. He courted Tilly with gold coins, foreign sweetmeats, and fragments of lace, which he presented to her with an air of easy, magnificent, and illegal generosity. He had a peculiar verbal tic, a habit of appending the word 'precisely' to every sentence he uttered, which gave his conversations an air of definitive, philosophical certainty, even when he was saying nothing at all. 'A fine night for a sail, Miss Tilly—precisely.' 'The fog is thick enough to cut with a knife—precisely.' 'Your father is a man of singular caution—precisely.' When Mr. Ezekiel Flint beheld Mr. Jasper Roke in the vicinity of Muckford Creek, his face turned a deep magenta, and he swelled with official indignation, like a turkey-cock surveying a trespasser. When Mr. Roke beheld Mr. Flint, he merely smiled with a dangerous, good-natured contempt, and inquired if the officer had caught any colds lately, or any other commodities worth the having.

Now, it happened upon a certain evening in the autumn of the year eighteen hundred and thirty-something, that the Thames estuary wrapped itself in a fog so thick, so yellow, so utterly impenetrable, that it seemed as if the whole river had been boiled in a great kettle, and the steam thereof had settled down upon the face of the waters to choke and suffurate all living things. It was the sort of fog that crept in at the keyholes, and made the candles burn blue, and caused the very frogs in the marshes to croak with a muffled and asthmatic distress. Mr. Pudge, seeing the fog rolling in, rubbed his hands with a dismal satisfaction, and retired to his fireside, fully anticipating a night of undisturbed, unbroken, and profound quiet.

But Providence, or the Customs, or the smugglers—or perhaps all three in conjunction—had decreed otherwise.

At about the hour of nine, a violent and official knocking came to the door. It was Mr. Ezekiel Flint. He was wrapped in a great-coat, and his hat was encrusted with a thick layer of moisture, which gave him the appearance of having been recently dipped in a tank of water. He demanded, in a voice that shook the crazy rafters, the immediate services of Mr. Pudge and his ferry-boat.

'I am in pursuit, Mr. Pudge,' said he, striking his stick upon the floor, 'of a certain vessel—a suspicious lugger—which I have reason to believe is lying in Muckford Creek, or thereabouts. I must have my boat, Mr. Pudge! The Majesty of the Law demands it!'

Mr. Pudge, trembling like a jelly, protested that the fog was too thick, that the tide was running out, that the creek was dangerous, and that he wanted nothing so much as to be left alone in his quiet life. But Mr. Flint would hear no excuses. The Majesty of the Law was deaf to the murmurs of ferryman.

And while Mr. Flint was still thundering his demands, another knocking came to the door—a gentle, but insinuating, and remarkably rapid knocking—and Mr. Jasper Roke walked in. He was wet to the skin, but he carried himself with an air of jaunty unconcern, and bowed to the company with a grace that would have done honour to a marquis. He looked at Mr. Flint, and he smiled. Mr. Flint looked at him, and swelled.

'A fine evening, Mr. Flint—precisely,' said Mr. Roke. 'You are out upon the water tonight, I perceive. The fog is rather favourable to your profession—precisely.'

'You are a rogue, sir!' cried Mr. Flint. 'A known and convicted rogue! And I shall lay you by the heels before the night is over!'

'You are very kind, Mr. Flint—precisely,' returned the smuggler, with a bow. 'But I must beg leave to inform you, Mr. Pudge, that I, too, require your ferry tonight. My boat has gone aground on the mud-flats, and I must be conveyed to the other side. I will pay you double—triple—quadruple your usual fare!'

Poor Mr. Pudge looked from one to the other, and wrung his hands in an agony of indecision. To take the one was to offend the other; to take the other was to incur the wrath of the first; to take both was to invite a naval engagement in the middle of Muckford Creek, and to blow his quiet life into a thousand fragments. Tilly, who was standing by the fire, drying her eyes with the corner of her apron—for she had been moved by the distressing scene—looked at Mr. Roke, and then at Mr. Flint, and then at her father, and her heart was torn between them, as a piece of parchment is torn between two dogs.

'Gentlemen,' said Mr. Pudge, at last, with a feeble, watery, and deprecating smile, 'I am sure I do not wish to be uncivil to anyone. I am a quiet man, gentlemen—a very quiet man. If you will both step into the boat, I will convey you both. There is room enough for two, and the creek is wide enough for both of you, and I am sure I hope you will not quarrel in my ferry-boat, for it is a very old boat, and not at all calculated to withstand the shock of any violent altercation.'

Mr. Flint consented with a growl. Mr. Roke consented with a smile. Tilly wrapped her shawl about her, and followed them down to the water's edge, to see them off.

The ferry-boat was a crazy, leaky, and disreputable old tub, which wallowed in the water like a sick pig in a trough. Mr. Pudge took the oars, and the two rivals seated themselves at opposite ends of the boat, eyeing each other with glances of concentrated animosity. The fog closed around them, yellow, thick, and muffling, like a great woolen blanket. The only sounds were the drip, drip, drip of the moisture from the oars, the gurgling of the water against the rotting planks, and the hard breathing of Mr. Ezekiel Flint.

They had not proceeded far, when Mr. Flint, leaning forward, and addressing Mr. Roke in a voice of terrible and official severity, said:

'I know you, Roke! I know your game! You are going to meet that lugger. You are going to run your cargo. But I have you now! I am with you in the boat, and I shall not lose sight of you until I have seen you safely lodged in the Custom-House!'

Mr. Roke laughed—a low, soft, melodious, and provoking laugh. 'You are very zealous, Mr. Flint—precisely,' said he. 'But you are also very much mistaken—precisely. I am not going to meet any lugger. I am going to see a young lady—a young lady of my acquaintance—on the other side of the creek—precisely.'

'A young lady!' cried Mr. Flint, turning purple. 'You are a hypocrite, sir! A deceiving, false, and treacherous hypocrite!'

Mr. Roke smiled again, and said nothing.

They rowed on in silence for some minutes, the fog growing thicker and thicker, until they could not see each other's faces. Suddenly, out of the darkness, there loomed a great black shape, like the hull of a vessel. Mr. Flint started to his feet, with a cry of triumph. 'The lugger!' he shouted. 'I have her! I have her!'

But at that moment, the ferry-boat, which had been wandering blindly through the fog, struck against a submerged post, or a mud-bank, or some other obstruction, with a violent and tremendous shock. Mr. Flint, who was standing, was precipitated headlong into the water. Mr. Roke, who was sitting, was thrown backwards into the bottom of the boat. Mr. Pudge, who was rowing, was jerked from his seat, and fell sprawling across the oars. The boat filled with water, and began to sink.
