If any bustling searcher in this great wilderness of bricks were to inquire of the stars, or the parish beadle, or even the very omnibus horses, what sudden turn of Fortune’s wheel could plunge a gentle young woman from the warmth of a feather-bed into the chilling draughts of independence, he would be told, no doubt, that it was the Devil, the Locomotive, and the Share-Market. For it is a solemn and undeniable truth, that when the iron horse begins to sniff up the vapours of public money, he usually leaves nothing behind him but a scorching trail of ruined ledgers and broken-hearted nieces.

Mr. Gideon Mudge, of Mudge Villa, Clapham, had been a man of substance. He was a man of weight. He was a man who dined upon four joints a week, drank his port with a solemnity befitting its age, and slept the deep sleep of the unimpeachable capitalist. But Mr. Mudge had caught the fever of the railway. He dreamt of dividends; he fancied himself a great engineer of human wealth; he bought shares in the Grand Euphoric, the Little Potherington, and the Tumbledown Junction, until, one fatal Tuesday, the whole magnificent edifice of iron and paper collapsed, and Mr. Mudge collapsed with it.

His niece, Miss Penelope Trent, was sitting in the morning-room, arranging a nosegay of greenhouse flowers, when the intelligence was conveyed to her by the housemaid, who had heard it from the butcher, who had received it from the cook, who had it from Mr. Mudge himself, in a voice so broken and small that it seemed to have shrunk into his very boots. Penelope was a tender-hearted girl, with eyes of a soft blue, a complexion like a newly-washed peach, and a disposition so gentle that she would not have stepped upon a beetle. She had been brought up in a cloud of comforts. She had been taught the piano, the French tongue, the art of drawing in pencil, and the still finer art of pretending to understand Italian; but of the hard, unyielding world, she knew absolutely nothing.

She knew it now, however, for the furniture was to be seized, the servants dismissed, and the greenhouse sold to pay the baker. The gentle Penelope was left to gaze upon the ruins of her uncle’s fortune, and to wonder, with a swelling heart, how her delicate white hands were to earn her bread.

It was in this dark hour of her extremity that a letter arrived. The envelope was thin, the wax was cracked, and the handwriting possessed that angular, aggressive sharpness which seems to threaten the reader with bodily harm. It was from Mrs. Priscilla Snorkel, a lady who had once governed Penelope’s education, and who now governed a young ladies’ seminary in the lower reaches of Brixton.

Mrs. Snorkel wrote to offer a situation. The situation was that of an assistant teacher. The salary was to be seven pounds per annum. Seven pounds! A sum so diminutive, so utterly contemptible, that it might have been the weekly pocket-money of a wealthy spaniel; yet, to Penelope, standing amid the shadows of Mudge Villa, it seemed a fortune. She accepted it with tears, packed her small trunk, and set off, like a brave little pilgrim, into the wilderness.

Mrs. Snorkel’s establishment was called “The Elms,” though there was not an elm within three miles, and the only vegetation visible was a single, stunted shrub in a front yard that seemed to be slowly dying of despair. The house itself was a tall, thin, melancholy villa, with a green door that looked as if it were suffering from a bilious attack, and windows so shrouded in dust that they appeared to be winking, knowingly, at the poverty within.

Penelope knocked, and the door was opened by a small girl, whose face was so thoroughly smudged with ink that it seemed to have been dipped, head foremost, into a writing-standish. The small girl said nothing, but led the way down a dark passage, smelling of damp plaster and boiled mutton, into a room which Mrs. Snorkel was pleased to call her study.

Here sat the mistress of the house. Mrs. Snorkel was a woman of great bones, sharp elbows, and an expression of face that seemed to have been expressly designed to discourage any foolish tendencies towards mirth. She wore a cap that resembled a collapsed dumpling, and a pair of spectacles which she continually took off and put on again, as if she were perpetually hoping that the world might look better through them, and perpetually discovering that it did not.

“Ah!” said Mrs. Snorkel, in a voice like the grating of a rusty hinge. “So you have come. Hem! Well. I dare say you think seven pounds a very low sum. I dare say you do. But you must remember, Miss Trent, that I am not a money-making machine. I am not a railway. I am a woman of intellect, and I educate the mind. The mind, Miss Trent! The mind!” Here she struck the table a severe blow with a ruler, as if the mind were a naughty child that required immediate correction.

Penelope murmured that she was very grateful, and Mrs. Snorkel, having satisfied herself that her new assistant was sufficiently subdued, proceeded to conduct her over the premises.

The schoolroom was a long, low chamber, at the end of which was a sort of dais, upon which stood Mrs. Snorkel’s desk, like a judgment-seat. Here sat, or rather wriggled, some fifteen young ladies, of ages varying from six to fourteen. They were a singular collection of humanity. There were long girls, short girls, girls with freckles, girls with spectacles, and one small, round girl, who appeared to be doing nothing but staring at the ceiling with an expression of profound idiocy. The air was thick with the smell of slate-pencil, damp wool, and that mysterious, indescribable odour which belongs exclusively to the morning room of a cheap school.

“This,” said Mrs. Snorkel, waving her hand with a sort of grim pride, “is where the mind is fed. You will take the junior class in reading, the middle class in spelling, and the senior class in the use of the globes. You will also hear the infant class repeat the Catechism, and you will correct their copies. Your hours will be from six in the morning until nine at night, with half an hour for tea. I think you will find that enough. Hem! I have no doubt you will find it quite enough.”

Penelope thought she would. But she said nothing, and Mrs. Snorkel, having pointed out a small, dark closet, in which were a few ragged books, a broken globe, and a human skull with a piece of the top knocked off, left her to begin her labours.

The day passed in a wild confusion of slates, tears, ink, and the Catechism. The infant class would not repeat it. They said it was dull, and they preferred to suck their thumbs. The junior class could not read. They stumbled over every word, as if the letters were so many stones in their path. The middle class spelt everything wrong, and the senior class, when called upon to point out the Equator upon the globe, pointed to the North Pole, and seemed to think they had done a very clever thing.

Penelope, tired and faint, sat down at last in the small, dark closet, and wondered if this was indeed the end of all her fine dreams. She had thought, in the days of Mudge Villa, that life was a smooth and pleasant road. She now found it to be a rough, steep, and stony lane, full of slates and globes, and the Catechism.

But Penelope was a brave girl. She dried her eyes, she straightened her back, and she resolved that she would not be beaten. She would teach the infants; she would hear the juniors; she would correct the spelling of the middle class; and she would show the seniors where the Equator was, if she had to hold their hands upon the globe. Seven pounds a year was not much, but it was independence, and independence, even in a damp schoolroom in Brixton, was better than sitting down to weep over a ruined railway. So, with a heart full of trembling courage, she took up her pen, dipped it in the ink, and began her first day’s work, a humble, solitary little heroine in the great, noisy, indifferent world.
