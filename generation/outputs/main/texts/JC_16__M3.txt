The wire had lain upon the desk for upwards of two hours, a slight, innocuous strip of paper, yet heavy with a weight which I found singularly oppressive. It had been brought up from the office of the Governor by a peon, a stealthy, bare-footed man who had deposited it with that profound, unsmiling bow of profound servility which always leaves one feeling as though an insult had been offered and accepted. It was from the Foreign Office, transmitted via Kingston, and it announced, with that abrupt, detached callousness peculiar to the utterances of distant authorities, the impending arrival of His Majesty’s ship *Orion*.

The purpose of the visit was "the protection of British interests." The words, in their stark, official phrasing, seemed to stand up from the paper with a kind of menacing rigidity. I read them again, and for the first time in twenty years, I found myself brought up sharply by a profound and paralyzing ignorance. I asked myself—what interests? I stood up and walked to the shuttered window. The glass was thick with the grime of the dry season, and through the warped slats of the *persianas* the heat of the afternoon entered like a heavy, palpable breath. The vast, unmoving expanse of the harbor lay below, a sheet of molten brass under the vertical sun. On the opposite shore, the low, fever-stricken line of the mangrove swamps melted into a quivering haze, while to the right, the wooden jetties of the town jutted out, black and rotten, like the decaying teeth in the jaw of some colossal, forgotten monster.

For twenty years I had been the guardian of this desolation. My report, if I may say so, had been a chronicle of nothing—of revolutions that began and ended in the drinking shops, of consular fees collected with a weary regularity, of British subjects, mostly degenerate half-castes or ruined men, marrying the daughters of the country and sinking without a trace into the vast, indigenous twilight. I had written of the export of hides, of the intermittent workings of a British-owned mine in the interior, of the shifting politics of this petty, bloodstained republic. I had written it all with a conscientiousness that had become, over the years, a kind of mechanical penance. And now, they were sending a warship. For what? To protect what? The *Orion* would come steaming into the bay, her grey sides flanked with the might of the Empire, her officers clean-shaven and intolerant, looking upon my domain with the cold, contemptuous eyes of a world that had forgotten my existence. They would ask me what was to be done, and I, who had lived so long in the shadow of this monstrous, absurd country, would have no answer to give them.

I turned from the window and looked about the room. It was a room of shadows and dust. The heavy mahogany furniture, imported from England in a time of forgotten optimism, had swollen and cracked with the damp of the tropics. The files were stacked in precarious towers, a catafalque of forgotten lives and meaningless events. On the wall, a faded photograph of the Queen stared down with a fixed, imperial melancholy. I looked at her face, and I wondered if she, in her distant palace, had any conception of the nature of the interests she was protecting here, at the edge of the world. Did she know of the stagnant waters, the yellow fever, the squalid politics, the slow, inevitable decay of everything European under this remorseless sun? I thought not. The Empire was a vast, blind organism, moving by its own momentum, sending its ships and its consuls to the ends of the earth without a clear understanding of why. And I, who had been its eyes and ears here for two decades, had become as blind and deaf as the organism itself.

I sat down again and took up a pen. I must answer the telegram. I must acknowledge the receipt of the communication and express my satisfaction at the forthcoming cooperation of the naval authorities. But the pen hovered over the paper. I found myself trying to conjure up a picture of these "British interests." There was the mine, of course. The *Compania Minera del Norte*, a British-owned venture, had been struggling for years against the corruption of the local officials and the apathy of its own directors in London. Its manager, a man named Hallowes, had died of fever a month ago, and the mine was now in the hands of a single engineer, a dispirited Welshman who spent his days writing letters of complaint that nobody read. Was this the interest? A hole in the ground, manned by a dying man, producing copper of an inferior quality at a loss?

Then there was the railway concession, a tangle of legal obligations and bribes that had never produced a single mile of track. And there was the debt, the eternal, crushing debt owed to British bondholders by a government that changed every six months. Were these the interests? They seemed to me, at that moment, so many shadows, so many insubstantial phantoms, conjured up by the imagination of men who had never set foot on this shore. The reality was the heat, the silence, the slow, creeping corruption of the land itself. And I was a part of it. I had become a part of it. My very soul had taken on the color of the place—the color of decay, of resignation, of a moral twilight in which the distinctions between right and wrong, between loyalty and betrayal, had become as blurred as the outlines of the shore in the afternoon haze.

The door opened, and my clerk, a man of mixed blood, entered with a rustle of papers. He was a thin, sallow individual, with the obsequious manner of his kind. He looked at me with his dark, inscrutable eyes, and I saw in them a reflection of my own futility.

"The Governor has sent a messenger," he said, in his soft, deferential voice. "He wishes to know if the Señor Consul has any news of importance."

The Governor—a man with a face like a yellow skull, who had attained power by the simple expedient of murdering his predecessor—wished to know. I looked at my clerk, and a sudden, bitter irony possessed me.

"Tell the Governor," I said, "that I have no news of importance. Tell him that I am writing a report."

The clerk bowed and withdrew. I heard his bare feet shuffling on the cracked tiles of the corridor, and then silence fell again, the vast, brooding silence of the tropics, in which a man can hear the beating of his own heart. I took up the pen again, and this time I wrote. I wrote of the mine, of the railway, of the debt. I wrote of the political situation, of the threat of another revolution, of the need for a show of force. I wrote it all down, neatly, competently, with the practiced ease of a man who has learned to say nothing in a great many words. But as I wrote, I knew that I was lying. I was lying not in the facts, but in the implication. I was implying that these things mattered, that they were worth the powder and shot, worth the dispatch of a warship, worth the attention of the Empire. And I knew, with a conviction that went deeper than words, that they did not matter. They did not matter at all.

The truth was, I had been here too long. I had put down roots, not in the soil, but in the shadows. My interests were no longer the interests of the Foreign Office. They were the interests of the heat, the silence, the slow, measured passage of days. I had become a native of this desolation. And the warship, when it came, would be an intruder, a reminder of a world that I had ceased to belong to—a world of purpose, of action, of moral certainties, which had become to me as remote and as meaningless as the stars.

I finished the report and sealed it. I rang the bell for the clerk.

"Send this to the telegraph office," I said. "And tell the boy to bring me a bottle of the Governor’s aguardiente."

The clerk looked at me with a flicker of surprise, but he said nothing. He took the envelope and disappeared. I sat in the gathering dusk, listening to the distant sound of the surf on the bar, and I waited for the coming of the ship. The shadows lengthened in the room, swallowing up the furniture, the files, the photograph of the Queen. And in the darkness, I seemed to see the grey shape of the *Orion*, steaming swiftly towards me through the night, bringing with her the might and the mockery of the Empire. I wondered, with a sort of detached curiosity, what they would make of me, this old man of the tropics, sitting in his ruined consulate, guarding a treasury of shadows. And I wondered, too, if they would understand that I had long ago ceased to care.
