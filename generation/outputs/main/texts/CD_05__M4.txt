The mail-packet from Dover to Calais, in the autumn of the year seventeen hundred and eighty-nine, was a vessel of so obstinate a disposition, and so crowded with passengers of every degree of discomfort, that Mr. Tobias Vellum, junior clerk in the house of Gribble, Gammon, and Vellum, of Lombard Street, had ample leisure, during the passage, to reflect upon the singular nature of his errand, and to wish himself, with a fervour amounting to passion, safely back among the ledgers. He was a young man of a neat, spare figure, with a high forehead, spectacles of a modest rim, and that air of careful precision which is the natural product of a desk, a stool, and a father who has risen by the same. He carried, stitched into the lining of his waistcoat, a letter of credit, and in his breast-pocket a bundle of papers relating to the deposits of the noble house of Vaudrecourt—monies placed, in more confident days, with the London bankers, and now, by the flight of the family and the confusion of France, become a matter of the most delicate and uncomfortable solicitation.

The packet, having pitched and rolled through a night of considerable misery, came at last to Calais; and Mr. Vellum, pale and disarranged, was jolted onward through a country that seemed to him to have gone mad in its Sunday clothes. For the harvest was in, and the roads were full; but the faces of the harvesters were not the faces of men who sing at their work. They stood in knots at the corners of the fields, watching the carriage with a stony and speculative eye; and once, at a village where the horses were changed, a woman with a lean child at her breast cried out something after him in a shrill voice, at which the postilion laughed and the other passengers looked suddenly grave. Mr. Vellum, who had never before been out of England, and who regarded the French tongue as a sort of elegant disorder, could make nothing of it; but he observed that the chateau of Vaudrecourt, when at length it rose before him at the end of a long avenue of mutilated limes, wore an aspect of sullen grandeur that did not at all accord with the accounts of it he had received in Lombard Street.

He was admitted by a servant whose livery was good but whose manner was better—that is to say, it was the manner of a man who had learned to be served rather than to serve. He was conducted through a hall of cold marble and faded gilding, where the portraits of the Vaudrecourt family looked down upon him with the peculiar haughtiness of ancestors who have been recently insulted, into a small apartment that had been fitted up as a counting-house, and there, at a desk of mahogany, sat the steward.

Mr. Hyacinthe Dufour was a man of perhaps fifty years, broad and ruddy, with a bald head polished like a billiard-ball, and small bright eyes that moved with the quick, comprehensive glance of a man who is accustomed to add up a column of figures and to estimate a visitor in the same instant. He wore a coat of good brown cloth, a waistcoat of striped silk, and shoes with silver buckles; and about him, in the room, were ranged the evidences of a comfortable and even luxurious administration—a bottle of wine, a plate of cold fowl, a dish of figs, and a great ledger open at a page of figures that would have gladdened the heart of Mr. Gribble himself.

“Monsieur Vellum?” said the steward, rising with a bow that was not without a certain easy patronage. “I had the honour of your letter. You are welcome to Vaudrecourt. You will find us, I fear, a little disordered—the times are not gentle with us—but we do what we can.”

Mr. Vellum, who had prepared a speech of elaborate courtesy, found himself, as he afterwards expressed it, “taken at a disadvantage”; for the steward spoke English of a fluency that was almost insolent, and the room, with its wine and its figs and its blazing fire, was not the room of a man who had been ruined by a revolution. He stated his business, however, with the clearness of a clerk: the deposits were intact, the house of Gribble, Gammon, and Vellum desired only to know to whom the monies should be paid, and upon what authority.

“To whom?” repeated the steward, with a smile that was bland and yet not quite pleasant. “Why, to the family, monsieur—to the family, through me. The Marquis is—ah—absent. The Marquis is, as you would say, upon his travels. The Marchioness is an invalid. The young ladies are in the convent at Amiens. I am the sole representative of the house of Vaudrecourt at this moment; and I assure you, monsieur, that no one is more anxious than I am to preserve its interests.”

He poured out a glass of wine as he spoke, and pushed it toward the clerk with a gesture that was hospitable and yet, somehow, final. Mr. Vellum, who did not drink wine at that hour, and who was, besides, a young man of a cautious habit, declined it with a bow; and it was then that he observed, through the window, a little group of villagers standing at the gate of the courtyard, silent and watchful, staring at the chateau with the same stony and speculative eye that he had seen in the fields.

“The people are troublesome?” he asked.

“The people,” said the steward, with a shrug, “are hungry. They have been hungry for a great many years, monsieur, and they have lately discovered that they are hungry. It is a discovery that makes men unreasonable. But they are quiet enough. They know that the chateau has a friend in me.”

There was something in the way he said it—a certain dwelling upon the pronoun, a certain expansion of the broad chest—that made Mr. Vellum look at him more narrowly. And as he looked, he became aware of a thing that had been at first invisible to him: that the room, the wine, the figs, the silver buckles, the very polish of the mahogany desk, were all of them the appurtenances of a master, and not of a servant. The steward sat in the chateau as a man sits in his own house; and the portrait of the old Marquis, which hung above the chimney-piece, had been turned, Mr. Vellum now noticed, with its face to the wall.

He was shown to his chamber by a maid whose curtsey was a thing of the past, and who answered his questions with a brevity that was almost contempt. At supper, in a great dining-room where the candles were few and the plate was many, the steward talked of the estate, of the rents, of the harvest, of the price of corn, with the easy mastery of a man who has the figures at his fingers’ ends; and once, when Mr. Vellum ventured to inquire after the health of the Marchioness, he replied, with a wave of his hand, that she was “upstairs—quite upstairs,” and passed immediately to the subject of the salt-tax.

It was late that night, when the clerk, unable to sleep for the unwonted noises of the old house and the unwonted thoughts within him, went to the window, that he saw the thing which decided his errand. In the courtyard below, by the light of a lanthorn, the steward stood talking with two men in the dress of peasants; and as Mr. Vellum watched, he saw him take from his pocket a handful of silver and distribute it among them, with the air of a man who pays wages, or buys silence. The men went away; and the steward, turning, looked up at the chateau, at the dark windows, at the very window where the clerk stood—and though he could not have seen him, Mr. Vellum drew back as if he had been detected in a crime.

In the morning, he rose early, and went out into the village. The people were stirring, but they were not merry; and when he asked for the inn, he was directed to a house where a man sat mending a net, and who, upon hearing that he was an Englishman and a banker’s clerk, laid down his work and looked at him a long time in silence.

“You come for the money of the Vaudrecourt?” he said at last, in a rough patois that Mr. Vellum could barely follow. “You will give it to Dufour?”

“I shall give it,” said Mr. Vellum, with a clerk’s precision, “to the proper authority.”

The man laughed—a short, ugly laugh. “The proper authority,” he repeated, “is the man with the silver buckles. He has been the proper authority for two years. He has sold the woods, monsieur; he has sold the salt; he has sold the very game in the park. And the Marquis—the Marquis is in England, they say, and the Marchioness is in her chamber, and the young ladies are in the convent. And we—we are hungry. You will give the money to Dufour, and Dufour will give it to—who knows? Perhaps to the Marquis, perhaps to himself. But not to us.”

Mr. Vellum walked back to the chateau through the mutilated limes, and he was aware, as he walked, of a great and uncomfortable conflict within him. He was a clerk, and his business was with the letter of his instructions; and the letter of his instructions was to pay the monies of the house of Vaudrecourt to the house of Vaudrecourt, or to its accredited representative. And the accredited representative, in the absence of the family, was undoubtedly the steward. And yet—and yet—the face of the lean woman in the field, the stony eyes of the villagers at the gate, the silver buckles, the portrait turned to the wall, the little group in the courtyard by the lanthorn—these things made a case that was not in the ledger.

He found the steward in the counting-house, with the bottle of wine before him and the great ledger open at the page of figures. And Mr. Vellum, who had never in his life done a bold thing, and who was conscious of a perspiration upon his forehead and a trembling in his knees, sat down opposite to him, and took out his papers, and said—

“Monsieur Dufour, I have considered the matter, and I must request that the monies be paid into the hands of the family, or of some person duly authorized by them, and not into the hands of any steward, however—however respectable.”

The steward put down his glass, and looked at him with a smile that was very still. “And if the family cannot be found?” he said.

“Then,” said Mr. Vellum, “I shall carry the monies back to London, and await their pleasure.”
