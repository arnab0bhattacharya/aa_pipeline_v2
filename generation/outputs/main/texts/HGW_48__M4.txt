It was on the eleventh of May, I remember, that the thing first became plain to me, though the almanacs will tell you the winter of that year began in the ordinary way and that the frosts were seasonable. I am a farmer, and my name is Joseph Harden, of Clapper’s Farm, which lies in a hollow of the Weald about two miles from the village of Marshden, and I am not a man given to fancies. I keep accounts, I dose my cattle when they are sick, and I have never in my life seen a ghost nor expected to. But on that morning I stood in my north meadow with the dew thick on the grass and the rooks making a great clatter in the elms, and I said to myself, “The sun is not right.”

It was not a cloud. The sky was a clear, pale, watery blue from end to end, and yet the light that fell upon the fields was thin and cold, like the light of an afternoon in late October. There was no warmth in it. I put my hand on the top rail of the gate, where the sun had been falling full for an hour, and it was no warmer than the underside. I looked at my shadow, and it seemed to me fainter than it should have been, and the edge of it less sharp. I am not an educated man in the way of the schools, but I have read a good deal in the Farmer’s Almanac and in the popular papers, and I knew well enough that the sun is the great engine of the world, and that if the engine falters, everything upon the earth falters with it.

I said nothing at first. A man does not care to be thought a fool in his own parish. But when the frost came again in the last week of May, and the young wheat that had sprouted so bravely in April turned black and withered in the ear, and when June brought no warmth but only a grey, still coldness that kept the swallows low and the bees in their hives, I began to talk to my neighbours.

The first man I spoke to was Thomas Reade, who farms the long field on the other side of the lane, a stout, red-faced fellow who laughs at everything and believes in nothing but the price of hops. “It is a late season, Joseph,” said he, “that is all. My father used to tell of a June in ‘thirty-six when they lit fires in the orchards to save the blossom. It will come right.”

“It has not come right,” said I. “And it is not the season that is late. It is the sun that is weak. Look at it now.” And indeed the sun hung in the sky that afternoon like a shilling seen through a bottle of pale ale, round and bright enough to look at without blinking, which I had never in my life been able to do before.

Reade laughed, but I saw him look at it, and I saw him stop laughing. “Well,” said he at last, “what would you have us do?”

“Grow under glass,” said I. “If the sun will not do its work in the open, we must make the most of what it gives us and hold the warmth in. I have been reading of it in the Farmer’s Almanac. A glasshouse will keep a temperature of ten degrees above the open air, and if the light is weak, then we must have more of it, and spread our crops where every ray can reach them.”

He thought me mad, of course. But I had already begun. I had a long frame of glass, which I had used for cucumbers in the old days, and I set it over a bed of early potatoes and another of beans, and I watched them, and I tell you the beans in the frame were a full three inches above the beans in the open by the middle of July, and green and healthy, while the others were stunted and yellow. I showed this to Reade, and to old Mutter, who keeps the mill, and to the two Pratts, who are brothers and farm together at the top of the hill, and they began to see reason.

It was the Pratts who first put the thing on a proper footing. “If one frame will do that,” said Herbert Pratt, who is the cleverer of the two, “then twenty frames will do twenty times as much, and a house of glass as big as a barn will feed the parish.” And so we formed what we called the Marshden Glasshouse Society, and we put in what money we had, and we bought glass and timber and nails, and we built, in the field behind my barn, a long, low structure of glass and whitewashed wood, with a stove at one end for the cold nights, and we planted it with potatoes and carrots and cabbages and a little wheat in boxes, and we tended it as a man tends his own children.

The government in London, meanwhile, was telling us that the whole affair was temporary. I have the newspaper by me now, and I have read the words a hundred times. “The present deficiency of solar radiation,” it said, “is due to causes at present under investigation, and there is every reason to believe that it will prove of a temporary character. The public is advised to pursue its ordinary avocations and to place no reliance upon the rumours which have been circulated by interested persons.” That is the way they write. I have never been in London, but I have a great respect for government, and I wanted to believe it. But when I wrote to the Board of Agriculture to ask whether there was any grant for glasshouses, and whether they might advise us as to the best methods of forcing crops under the present conditions, I got a letter back which said that my communication had been received and would be laid before the proper authorities, and that in the meantime I was requested to observe that the ordinary processes of agriculture were not affected by the phenomena in question.

“The ordinary processes of agriculture,” said I to Reade, “are affected to the tune of half my wheat crop and all my hops. If the government will not help us, we must help ourselves.”

And so we did. By the autumn we had three glasshouses, and the Pratts had built a fourth, and we had dug up the flower garden in front of the mill and put it under glass, and the whole village was eating beans and cabbages that had never seen the open sky. It was a strange sight, I can tell you, to stand in my field of a December morning and see the frost thick on the fallow, and the cattle huddled in the straw, and the rooks silent in the elms, and then to step into the glasshouse and find the air warm and moist and smelling of green things, and the beans climbing their sticks as if it were June. It was like a bit of the old world kept alive in a box.

But the cold went on. The winter did not end. January came, and February, and the sun was no stronger, and the frosts were harder, and we burned more coal in the stove than I care to think of. The glasshouses were a great success, but they were not enough. We could not grow wheat enough to feed the parish in a few frames of glass, and the price of coal was going up, and the government was still saying that the thing was temporary. I wrote again, and this time I wrote to my member, a Mr. Pellow, who is a barrister and a very polite man, and I asked him plainly whether the government had any plan for feeding the country if the sun did not recover itself. He wrote back a very long letter, in which he said that the government was in communication with the Royal Society, and that the matter was receiving the most careful attention, and that I might rest assured that everything that could be done would be done. But he did not tell me what.

I am not a politician, and I do not pretend to understand the ways of London. But I am a farmer, and I know that a man who waits for the government to tell him when to sow his seed will starve in a good year. And so I say to my neighbours, and I say it to anyone who will listen: if the sun is weak, then we must be strong, and if the winter does not end, then we must make our own summer under glass. It is not a great thing that we have done at Marshden, but it is something. We have kept the beans growing and the children fed, and we have proved that a man with a frame of glass and a stove and a little common sense can beat the government and the weather both. And if the sun never comes back, why, then, I suppose we shall have to build a bigger glasshouse.
