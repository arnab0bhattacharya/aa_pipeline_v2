The heat in the Valley of the Kings was a physical presence, a weight that pressed down on the shoulders and shimmered up from the bleached stones. Margaret sat beneath the awning of their tent, fanning herself with a copy of *The Egyptian Gazette*, the newsprint smudged with her perspiration. Her father, Mr. Horace Whitfield, was deep in conversation with their dragoman, a man named Farid, about the logistics of their return journey to Luxor. The words “earliest possible convenience” drifted over to her on the dry wind, and she felt a familiar, suffocating tightness in her chest.

They had been in Egypt for three weeks. It was meant to be a tour of the antiquities, a diversion her father had prescribed for her after a season of what he called “nerves.” She suspected it was more to do with his desire to see her settled with their cousin, Arthur, a man with the personality of a damp ledger and a profitable woolen mill in Leeds. Egypt, her father had declared, would provide a suitable backdrop for her to clear her head and see reason.

But Egypt had done the opposite. It had filled her head with a wild, sun-drenched madness. The scale of the monuments, the sheer age of everything, the relentless, beautiful indifference of the Nile—it all served to make the parlours of Leeds seem like a stuffy, airless tomb.

Her salvation had come in the form of a trench. Two days ago, while their guide had been lecturing on the cartouches of a minor pharaoh, Margaret had wandered off, drawn by the sound of rhythmic scraping and the low murmur of workmen. She had found a site tucked into a cleft of the hillside, a neat, rectangular excavation revealing the top of a sealed doorway. And there, directing the work with a quiet authority that belied his youth, was a man.

He was not dressed like the other Europeans she had met, in stiff collars and sun helmets. He wore a simple linen shirt, sleeves rolled to the elbow, and dusty trousers. His face was tanned a deep bronze, and a pair of wire-rimmed spectacles was perched on his nose, giving him an owlishly serious look that she found utterly captivating. He was examining a fragment of pottery with the focused reverence of a priest.

She had cleared her throat, and he had looked up, startled. “I’m sorry,” she’d said, her voice sounding small and foolish. “I didn’t mean to intrude. I heard the digging.”

He had smiled then, a quick, unguarded smile that crinkled the corners of his eyes. “Not at all. It’s a public valley, more or less. You’re welcome to look, so long as you don’t step on the trench edges.” His name was Mr. Thomas Ashworth, and he was a junior archaeologist with the expedition sponsored by the Pelham Museum. The tomb, he explained, belonged to a lesser noble of the 19th Dynasty, a man named Khaemwaset. “His name means ‘He Appears in Thebes,’” he’d said, his eyes alight with enthusiasm. “Fitting, don’t you think? He’s been waiting a long time to appear again.”

For the next two days, Margaret had returned to the trench, ostensibly to sketch the friezes, but really to listen to Thomas talk. He spoke of the delicate work of clearing debris, the thrill of a seal being found intact, the story a single shard of pottery could tell about a life lived three thousand years ago. He was a man of quiet passion, his world one of patient discovery, not of profit margins and social obligations. He made her feel that a life could be more than a series of prescribed movements.

Now, her father’s voice cut through her reverie. “Margaret, are you listening? We shall leave on the morning steamer. I’ve had enough of this dust and these flies. It is time we returned to civilisation.”

“So soon?” she asked, her voice carefully neutral. “I thought we were to stay another week.”

“Circumstances have changed,” he said, his gaze flicking towards the distant hillside where he knew she had been spending her time. “Your mother’s letters grow more insistent. And Arthur has written again. He is eager for our return.”

Arthur. The name landed like a stone in her stomach. She saw it all with a sudden, sickening clarity. Her father had noticed her interest in Thomas, had seen the light in her eyes, and had decided to extinguish it before it could catch. The journey was to be cut short not for her mother’s sake, but to snuff out this inconvenient spark of independence.

That evening, as the sun bled the sky orange and purple over the western cliffs, she slipped away from their camp. She found Thomas at his trench, packing his tools into a wooden crate. The workmen had gone, and the valley was silent save for the whisper of the wind.

“Mr. Ashworth,” she said, and he turned, his face lighting up in the dusk.

“Miss Whitfield. You’ve come to say goodbye, then. Your father’s man told our director you’re leaving tomorrow.” His voice was even, but she detected a note of disappointment.

“Yes. My father has decided our tour must end.” She paused, twisting her hands in her skirt. “I wanted to thank you. For showing me your work. It has been… the most extraordinary part of my journey.”

He took off his spectacles and wiped them on his shirt. “It has been a pleasure to have an audience who is genuinely interested. Most people just want to know if there’s gold.”

“Is there?” she asked, managing a small smile.

“Not in this tomb, I’m afraid. Only the quiet ghosts of a forgotten family. The real treasure is the knowledge.” He gestured to the dark doorway. “Tomorrow, we break the seal. I expect to find little more than rubble and a few broken pots. But it’s the story, you see. The story of Khaemwaset and his world. That’s the gold.”

She stood there, on the edge of his trench, and felt a chasm opening between them that had nothing to do with the physical distance from Luxor to Leeds. He was a man who dealt in the deep, slow time of centuries. She was a woman trapped in the frantic, shallow time of a single social season.

“I think your work is the most wonderful thing I have ever seen,” she said, the words tumbling out with a sincerity that surprised her. “To be able to touch the past, to bring it back to life… it is a kind of magic.”

He looked at her for a long moment, the twilight deepening the blue of his eyes. “It is mostly dust and backache,” he said softly. “But sometimes… sometimes it feels like magic.”

He took a step closer. “Miss Whitfield… Margaret. I know it is not my place, but… your father’s world and my world, they don’t often mix. But if you were ever to find yourself in London, the Pelham Museum has a rather fine collection of Egyptian artefacts. I could show you.”

It was a lifeline, a slender thread thrown across the chasm. It was also an impossibility, and they both knew it. She was a daughter of means, expected to marry within her circle. He was a scholar, a man of modest prospects and a life dedicated to the dead. Her father would never approve.

“I would like that,” she whispered, her voice thick with unshed tears. She knew it was a lie, a beautiful, hopeless lie.

A call from the camp broke the spell. Her father’s voice, sharp with impatience, echoed through the gathering gloom. “Margaret! Where are you?”

She flinched. Thomas’s expression hardened slightly. “You should go,” he said, his voice gentle but firm. “Don’t keep him waiting.”

She nodded, unable to speak. She turned and walked away, her feet clumsy on the stony path. She did not look back, because she knew that if she did, the fragile resolve her father had instilled in her would shatter entirely. She walked towards the tents, towards the steamer, towards Arthur and Leeds and a life of quiet, suffocating order.

Behind her, the Valley of the Kings settled into darkness. Thomas Ashworth stood alone by his trench, the tomb of Khaemwaset a black rectangle in the hillside. He looked at the spot where she had stood, then turned back to his work, the story of a forgotten nobleman waiting for him in the dust. The next morning, he would break the seal. And on the river, a steamer would push its way north, carrying a young woman away from the only magic she had ever known.
