It was a truth universally acknowledged in the town of Marlow—though perhaps acknowledged with more of a sigh than a smile—that the Reverend Mr. Theophilus Bell, Vicar of St. Cuthbert’s, was a man whose goodness of heart was equalled only by his deficiency in the practical arithmetic of daily life. He was a scholar of no mean repute, having published a slender but elegant volume of sermons on the Book of Job, and could discourse with moving erudition upon the Hebrew text; but the simple ledger of the parish charities, the weekly collection, the disbursements for coal and blankets and broth, these were mysteries he regarded with a vague and holy dread, as a man might regard the internal workings of a clock—marvellous, necessary, and best left to a more mechanically-minded providence.

That providence had, for some seven years, worn the countenance of the Reverend Mr. Josiah Trumbull, his curate. Mr. Trumbull was a man of five-and-thirty, with a smooth, pale face, a manner of unctuous deference, and a way of speaking of the poor as “our deserving objects” that had at first commended him warmly to Mr. Bell’s simple heart. He it was who kept the accounts of the Dorcas Society and the Parish Relief Fund; he it was who interviewed the applicants for aid, who weighed the claims of the widow against those of the aged pauper, and who, in short, stood as a firm and efficient barrier between the Reverend Mr. Bell’s charitable impulses and the clamorous realities of need. The vicar, when he thought of the matter at all, which was seldom, congratulated himself on having found so able a coadjutor.

It was Miss Henrietta Marsh who discovered the truth. She was the daughter of a retired surgeon, a young woman of five-and-twenty, with a clear grey eye, a resolute chin, and that uncomfortable habit of mind which insists upon seeing things as they are, rather than as they are piously hoped to be. She had taken upon herself the visiting of the aged poor in the hamlet of Cotesford, a cluster of cottages a mile from the town, and it was her custom to carry small sums from the Parish Relief Fund to certain of the more infirm among them. It was thus that she came to notice a discrepancy—at first so slight as to seem a mere slip of the memory—between the sum recorded in Mr. Trumbull’s neat hand as having been disbursed to old Mrs. Nuttall and the sum which that aged and garrulous person declared she had actually received. The difference was but a shilling; but Miss Marsh, having a mind of that uncomfortable sort, did not dismiss it.

She began, quietly and without fuss, to keep her own small account. She noted the sums recorded for the Widow Pemberton, for the crippled boy at the mill, for the family of the drunken weaver whose wife was, as Mr. Trumbull observed, “a most importunate and undeserving case.” The discrepancies grew. They were never large—a shilling here, half a crown there, a pound on one occasion when a subscription for a new stove for the schoolroom had been gathered—but they were persistent, and they pointed, with the relentless logic of figures, to one conclusion. Mr. Trumbull, the smooth, the deferential, the unctuous Mr. Trumbull, was a thief.

The discovery affected Miss Marsh with a peculiar horror. It was not the magnitude of the sum—she calculated the total defalcation at perhaps forty pounds over seven years—but the cold, systematic, respectable nature of the fraud that appalled her. The man had stolen not from the rich, who might have borne it, but from the poorest, the most helpless, the very “deserving objects” he had professed to cherish; and he had done it under the cloak of a charity that should have been sacred. She thought of the old women in their cold cottages, counting their pitiful shillings; she thought of the boy at the mill, whose crutch had been mended with a cheaper wood than the subscription had warranted; and her clear grey eye grew hard.

But what was she to do? To expose Mr. Trumbull was, she knew, to break the heart of Mr. Bell. She had seen the vicar in his study, surrounded by his books, his white hair falling over his brow, his mild blue eyes suffused with a gentle, bewildered kindness as he spoke of his curate. “A good man, my dear Miss Marsh,” he had said, “a very good man. I am not fit for the world, I fear; I am a dreamer, a scholar; but Trumbull—Trumbull is my right hand. He understands the poor. He has a head for figures. I thank God for him daily.” To tell him the truth would be to shatter that simple gratitude, to poison the memory of seven years, to make him feel that his own unworldly trust had been not a virtue but a folly. It would be, she thought, like telling a child that the father he loved had been a villain.

She wrestled with the question for a fortnight. She walked in the frosty lanes and turned it over in her mind; she lay awake at night and considered it from every side. She was not a woman given to self-deception, and she did not disguise from herself that her own feelings were involved. She was fond of Mr. Bell—fond of him with a daughterly tenderness that had grown up in her during the years of her visits to the vicarage; and she knew that to strike him this blow would be to wound herself as well. But she knew also that to be silent was to be an accomplice after the fact; that the fraud would continue; that other old women would be cheated of their shillings; and that the longer the deception lasted, the more terrible would be the awakening.

At last she resolved to act. She chose a Tuesday afternoon, when she knew Mr. Trumbull was gone to a meeting of the Diocesan Society in the neighbouring town, and she walked up the gravel path to the vicarage with a heart that beat more quickly than she would have cared to confess. She found Mr. Bell in his study, as she had expected, seated at his table with a folio of the Fathers open before him and a cup of cold tea at his elbow. He looked up with his mild, abstracted smile.

“Miss Marsh! This is a pleasure. You find me—ah—engaged in a little patristic recreation. Chrysostom, you know. A wonderful man. A wonderful man. But I fear I am not—ah—very useful in the parish. Trumbull does it all. A good man, Miss Marsh. A very good man.”

Henrietta seated herself in the chair he indicated, and for a moment her resolution faltered. The afternoon light fell softly through the window upon his white hair and his gentle, dreaming face, and she thought, with a pang, of the peace she was about to destroy. But she had come too far to retreat. She drew a small paper from her pocket—the paper on which she had set down her careful figures—and laid it on the table before him.

“Mr. Bell,” she said, and her voice was steady, though her hands were not, “I must ask you to look at these figures. They are the sums recorded in Mr. Trumbull’s accounts for the relief of the poor at Cotesford, and the sums which I myself have carried to the poor. There is a difference. It is not a large difference in any one case, but it is a difference that has gone on for seven years.”

The vicar looked at the paper, then at her, with a puzzled, almost childlike bewilderment. He put on his spectacles, took them off again, and rubbed them with his handkerchief. “I—I don’t quite understand, my dear. Trumbull—Trumbull is so careful. There must be some mistake. A clerical error—a—a—“

“It is no mistake, Mr. Bell,” she said, gently but firmly. “I have checked it a dozen times. The money has not reached the poor. It has gone—elsewhere.”

He was silent for a long moment, and she watched the slow dawn of comprehension in his face—the first shock of incredulity, the struggle of his mind against the hateful fact, and then the deep, irremediable sadness that settled upon his features like a shadow. He did not speak; he only sat and looked at the paper, and his hands trembled so that he had to lay them flat upon the table to steady them.

“Seven years,” he said at last, in a voice that was scarcely audible. “Seven years. And I—I thanked God for him daily.”

Henrietta rose and stood beside him, and laid her hand upon his shoulder. “You must not reproach yourself,” she said. “Your trust was a good thing. It is he who has betrayed it.”

But the old man shook his head slowly, and the tears stood in his mild blue eyes. “I should have known,” he said. “I am a dreamer. I am not fit for the world. But I should have known.”
