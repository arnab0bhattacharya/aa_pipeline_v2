In the imperial gardens of Marv, where the peacocks walked with the solemnity of ambassadors and the rose-trees bore blossoms of a crimson so profound that they seemed to bleed rather than bloom, there was a pool of black marble. It was a pool of infinite elegance, dedicated entirely to the aesthetic contemplation of stillness. Around its alabaster edges were carved the deeds of forgotten kings, though the kings themselves were considerably less forgotten than their deeds, having been merely tedious. In this pool, resting upon the water like a large and solitary opal, lived a White Water Lily.

She was a flower of remarkable beauty. Her petals were of a pallid, luminous white, and her heart was a subtle gold. She had been placed there by the Master of the Imperial Pleasaunces, a gentleman who held the entirely correct opinion that nature is merely a raw material, entirely lacking in finish until a gardener of genius has edited it. The Lily, consequently, was very proud. She was aware that she was the sole reason the pool existed, and she looked upon the other inhabitants of the water with the detached amusement of a society hostess compelled to tolerate her own relations.

The most vocal of these inhabitants were the Frogs. They were fat, green, and remarkably self-satisfied. They spent their days basking on the warm stones at the pool’s margin, and their evenings in a strenuous pursuit of banality which they termed music. They were, in short, the absolute epitome of the English middle classes, transplanted into a garden in Marv.

“You are a great fool,” croaked the eldest Frog one evening, as the twilight began to drape its violet silks over the garden. “You sit there all day, doing absolutely nothing, and at night you merely open your petals. It is a profoundly unpractical existence. Where is your industry? Where is your utility? I have just eaten three hundred flies, and I feel a vast and solid satisfaction. You have eaten nothing. You are merely decorative. It is a very dangerous thing to be merely decorative. The moment people find you useless, they pluck you.”

“To be useful is to be vulgar,” replied the Lily, with a gentle, swaying disdain. “The only excuse for making a thing is that it is beautiful. The only excuse for eating three hundred flies is that one is a Frog. You have committed both errors, and I beg you will not attempt to converse with me until you have acquired a modicum of aesthetic taste.”

The Frog, who had no taste whatsoever, save a decided preference for damp mud, merely blinked his prominent eyes and sank into the water, leaving a circle of ripples that were entirely devoid of philosophical significance.

The Goldfish, who were much more refined than the Frogs, though infinitely more shallow, swam up to the surface to offer their counsel. They were magnificent creatures, scaled in burnished orange and silver, and they moved with the languid arrogance of those who are perpetually admired. They lived entirely on adulation, and when no one was looking at them, they suffered from a profound melancholia.

“My dear Lily,” said the largest Goldfish, whose scales gleamed like a newly minted sovereign, “you are wasting your youth. Here we are, in a palace pool, the very apex of aquatic society. Yet you persist in looking upwards. It is terribly bad form to look upwards. One should only look at one’s peers, or at one’s reflection in the water. Reflections are always so much more agreeable than realities. But you insist on staring at the sky.”

“I am not staring at the sky,” said the Lily. “The sky is merely a tedious expanse of blue or black, entirely devoid of character. I am looking at the Moon.”

“The Moon!” exclaimed the Goldfish, with a little shiver of his gilded tail. “How utterly pointless! The Moon is a cold, distant, and entirely unprofitable body. It has no society. It is uninhabited. To admire the Moon is to confess a flaw in one’s character. It is a form of romanticism, and romanticism, as everyone knows, is merely a polite term for bad breeding.”

“You are very wise,” murmured the Lily, “for a creature that has a memory of exactly three seconds. The Moon is distant, it is true. But distance is the very essence of beauty. One can only love that which one cannot possess. To possess a thing is to understand it, and to understand a thing is to find it entirely devoid of charm. I love the Moon because I know nothing of it, and I hope to remain in that state of exquisite ignorance forever.”

The Goldfish yawned, a feat of aquatic engineering which he performed with great elegance, and swam away to examine his reflection in the side of a submerged bronze vase. He felt that the Lily was decidedly eccentric, and eccentricity, in a palace pool, was the only sin that could not be forgiven.

As the darkness deepened, the garden of Marv underwent a transformation. The heavy perfumes of the day, the blatant and obvious scents of the rose and the jasmine, withdrew, and in their place came the subtle, silver fragrance of the night. The Moon rose. It climbed above the dark cypresses, shedding a pale, cold light over the marble pool. It was a Moon of perfect beauty, a luminous pearl set in the velvet casket of the night.

The Lily felt a tremor of pure delight. Slowly, majestically, she opened her petals. It was an act of supreme homage. She opened them wide, until she was a perfect star of white upon the black water, pouring out her soul in a silent symphony of colour and form. Every curve of her petals was a caress, every line a declaration of devotion. She was no longer a flower; she was a prayer.

The Moon, high above, poured its light down upon her. It touched her heart of gold, and turned her petals to a translucent silver. The Lily was perfectly happy. She had no thought for the morrow, no care for the Frogs, no concern for the Goldfish. She existed only in the pure, intellectual contemplation of the beautiful.

“How exquisite she is tonight,” said a Nightingale to a Rose, on the terrace above.

“She is merely showing off,” replied the Rose, who was intensely jealous of the Lily’s pallor, having spent the entire day trying to achieve it, and succeeding only in turning a rather unbecoming shade of magenta. “And besides, she is in love with the Moon. It is a very low attachment. The Moon has no scent.”

“Love has nothing whatsoever to do with scent,” said the Nightingale. “Love is a form of madness, generally cured by marriage, but in flowers, who are spared that institution, it remains a purely poetic affliction.”

But the Lily did not hear them. She was engaged in a silent, spiritual communion with her celestial lover. She felt that the Moon understood her, that the pale, cold rays were a reciprocal affection, a subtle acknowledgment of her beauty. In the world of art, she thought, there is no need for the gross materiality of touch. To be looked at by the Moon was a more perfect union than any embrace. It was a marriage of light and form, a flawless, passionless passion that satisfied the intellect and left the senses untouched.

The hours passed in a silver dream. But the night, which is always treacherous to those who love it, began to wane. A faint, grey smear appeared in the east. The Moon, pale and weary, began to descend towards the horizon. The Lily felt a sharp, sudden sorrow. It was the sorrow of the artist who sees the vision fade, the sorrow of the lover who watches the beloved depart.

Slowly, reluctantly, she began to close her petals. She drew them in, one by one, shutting out the harsh, advancing light. She became a tight, green bud once more, a sealed envelope of unspoken grief. The water around her grew black and cold. The Frogs began to stir in the mud, preparing for another day of fatuous industry. The Goldfish swam up to the surface, eager to begin their ceaseless pursuit of admiration.

“Good morning,” said the eldest Frog, bobbing his head above the water. “I trust you had a useless night. I have already eaten a beetle. It was a very nourishing experience. I feel quite virtuous.”

“Virtue is the last refuge of the unimaginative,” said the Lily, her voice muffled by her closed petals. “I have spent the night in the contemplation of absolute beauty, and I find that it has left me entirely empty. It is the perfect state. To be full is to be heavy. To be empty is to be light. I am lighter than the air, and considerably more profound than the mud in which you are sitting.”

The Frog did not understand a word she said, which was perhaps the highest compliment he could have paid her. He sank back into the water, entirely satisfied with his beetle.

And so the days and nights passed in the palace pool of Marv. Every evening, as the Moon rose, the Lily would open her heart in a passion of white and gold. Every morning, as the dawn broke, she would close it again in an exquisite, silent sorrow. The Frogs continued to eat, and the Goldfish continued to admire themselves. They told the Lily she was foolish. They told her that the Moon was cold, and distant, and entirely indifferent to her devotion.

Perhaps they were right. The Moon, in all probability, cared nothing for the White Water Lily. It is the nature of beautiful things to be entirely indifferent to the things that love them. A woman does not love the man who worships her; she merely finds him useful. The Moon, being the supreme achievement of nature, was probably entirely heartless. But the Lily did not care. She had discovered the great secret that the only love worth having is the love that is never returned. It is a love that asks nothing, expects nothing, and therefore suffers nothing, save the purely aesthetic melancholy of seeing perfection depart. It is a love that is entirely selfish, because it is concerned only with the emotion of the lover, and entirely unselfish, because it demands nothing of the beloved. It is the only true form of art. And so, night after night, she opened her petals, and was perfectly, tragically, beautifully happy.
