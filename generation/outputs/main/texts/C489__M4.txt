The fog had come up the Thames that afternoon and by dusk it had swallowed the river entirely, so that the cab horses crossing London Bridge seemed to step out upon a white nothingness and vanish. Reverend Thomas Alder stood at the window of his lodgings in Camberwell and watched the lamplighter make his rounds, a small bobbing star in the gathering dark. He had been home three weeks and still could not accustom himself to the quiet. In Shandong the nights had been full of sound—dogs, wind, the lowing of cattle, the distant chant of a funeral or a wedding. Here the silence was so complete it seemed to press against the glass.

His wife, Margaret, was downstairs seeing to the supper. She had been anxious since the day he arrived, though she tried to hide it. She had never liked the China mission. She had liked it even less when he had come home thin and grey-faced, with a cough that lingered, and a way of looking over his shoulder when a door opened suddenly.

It was the luggage that had unsettled her most. The trunks had come up from the ship three days after Thomas himself, and when she had opened them to air the clothes she had found everything—every shirt, every book, every packet of letters—arranged with a precision that was not her husband's. Thomas was a careless packer at the best of times. But here were his collars folded as though by a valet, his sermon notes squared and tied with string he had not used, his Bible placed at the very bottom of the trunk beneath a layer of underlinen, when she knew he always kept it at the top. She had said nothing to him at first. She had simply stood in the spare room with her hands pressed together and felt the cold come up through the floorboards.

The man had begun following Thomas on the second day after his arrival. Thomas had noticed him first on the omnibus to the Missionary Society offices in Cheapside—a tall fellow in a brown coat, clean-shaven, with the flat, patient face of a man who has learned to wait. Thomas had thought nothing of it. London was full of faces. But then he had seen him again outside the Society's doors, standing beneath the awning of a tobacconist's, and again that evening at the corner of the street where the Alder lodgings stood, though when Thomas had turned to look the man had been studying a newspaper with great absorption.

He had not told Margaret. She had enough to fret over. But he had begun to take a different route home each evening, and each evening, sooner or later, the brown coat would appear at the edge of things, never close enough to speak to, never far enough to forget.

On the fourth day he had gone to see Mr. Edwin Voss.

Voss was a private investigator of the quieter sort, with offices above a stationer's in Holborn. He was a small, neat man with spectacles and a habit of polishing them while he listened, which gave him an air of gentle distraction that was entirely false. Margaret had insisted on the visit. She had heard of Voss from her sister, whose husband had once employed him in a matter of a missing deed-box, and she had written him a letter without telling Thomas, and then confessed it in tears over breakfast.

" You think I am foolish," she had said.

" I think you are frightened," Thomas had replied. " That is not the same thing."

Voss had listened to the whole account without interruption—the luggage, the man in the brown coat, the cough, the letters from China that Thomas had burned in the grate the week before, though he could not say now why he had burned them. When Voss had finished polishing his spectacles he had put them on and looked at Thomas for a long moment.

" You were at Chining-fu," he said. " During the troubles."

" I was."

" There were others."

" There were four of us. Two are dead. One is in Edinburgh, I believe, and does not answer letters."

" And the fourth?"

Thomas had been silent. The clock on Voss's mantel had ticked. " The fourth," he had said at last, " is not a matter I care to discuss."

Voss had nodded slowly, as though this were the answer he had expected. " The man who follows you," he had said. " Would you know him again?"

" I would."

" Then I shall find out who he is. In the meantime, Reverend, I would ask you not to burn anything else."

That had been Tuesday. Now it was Friday, and the fog had come up, and Thomas stood at the window watching the lamplighter turn the corner into the next street. Margaret called him down to supper. He ate without tasting. The cough came on him twice, and each time she looked at him across the table with that expression he had come to dread—the expression of a woman who is calculating how much her husband is not telling her.

After supper he went to the front door to take the air. The fog had thickened. He stood on the step with his collar turned up and listened to the drip of water from the eaves. And then, across the street, beneath the lamp that the lamplighter had just lit, he saw the brown coat.

The man was standing quite still, his hands in his pockets, his face turned toward the house. He made no attempt to hide himself. For a long moment the two of them looked at one another through the fog, and Thomas felt the old coldness come into his chest, the coldness that had been with him since Chining-fu, since the night the mission compound had burned and he had run—he, who had never run from anything—and had heard behind him the voice of the fourth man, the man who had not run, calling his name in the dark.

He stepped back inside and shut the door. He stood in the hall with his hand on the latch and his heart going like a hammer.

In the morning Voss came to Camberwell. He came early, before the fog had lifted, and he sat in the parlour with his hat on his knees and told them what he had learned. The man in the brown coat was named Craddock. He was not a policeman. He was not a criminal. He was a clerk in the employ of a shipping firm with interests in the China trade, and he had been engaged—by whom Voss could not yet say—to watch the Reverend Thomas Alder and to report upon his movements, his visitors, and his correspondence.

" And the luggage?" Margaret asked. Her voice was steady, but her hands were not.

" The luggage was searched before it left the ship," Voss said. " By someone who knew what he was looking for and did not find it. He repacked it carefully so that you would know it had been done. It was a message, Mrs. Alder. Not a theft."

Thomas sat very still. He was thinking of the letters he had burned, and of the fourth man, and of the thing he had carried out of Chining-fu in his coat pocket and had never spoken of to anyone, not even to his wife. He was thinking that the past did not stay in the past, no matter how far you sailed from it, no matter how many letters you burned.

" What do they want?" he said.

Voss polished his spectacles. " I think," he said, " they want to know what you brought home."
