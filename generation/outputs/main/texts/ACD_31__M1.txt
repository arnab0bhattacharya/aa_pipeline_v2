It was in the wet spring of ’93 that a small, grey envelope, bearing the Hampshire postmark of Wychford, found its way to my chambers. The paper was cheap and the hand, though educated, was that of a woman in a state of some agitation. It spoke of damp letters and a vanished gamekeeper, and concluded with the hope that this missive, sent under cover to a cousin in London, might escape the notice of those it concerned. My friend, Sherlock Holmes, read it through with the quick, darting attention he reserved for those problems which came to him in an unorthodox fashion.

“A curious trifle, Watson,” said he, tossing the letter into the hearth. “The writer, a Mrs. Sarah Prewett, is a woman of observation. She notes that the squire’s correspondence is always ‘slightly tacky to the touch, as though a hot iron had been drawn across the seal’. A fascinating detail. It speaks not of clumsiness, but of a method. And the disappearance of one Mr. Silas Croft, the gamekeeper, three days since? The two facts must be connected. The county police, I take it, are content to believe the man has deserted his post for the public house?”

“So the letter implies,” I replied.

“Then we shall disabuse them of the notion. We go to Wychford.”

Our arrival in the village was marked by a persistent, clinging drizzle that seemed to seep into the very stones of the cottages. The place was somnolent, its single street deserted but for a few chickens scratching in the mud. Mrs. Prewett’s post office was a cramped establishment, smelling of sealing wax and stale newsprint. She was a woman of fifty, with a keen, anxious face and hands that were never still.

“Dr. Watson! And Mr. Holmes!” she whispered, her eyes wide with alarm and relief. “Thank heaven you have come. I feared my letter might have been… misdirected.”

“Your letter was admirably clear, madam,” said Holmes, his gaze sweeping the small room, taking in the ledgers, the scales, the rows of stamps. “You say the squire, Mr. Alistair Finch, receives his letters in this state?”

“Always, sir. For the past month, at least. I handle the mail myself. I cannot be mistaken. It is a small thing, I know, but it troubled me.”

“A small thing is often the thread which unravels the whole tapestry. And this gamekeeper, Croft? A good man?”

“The best, sir. Quiet. A soldier once, I believe. He was devoted to the squire’s daughter, Miss Eleanor. It was common knowledge. But lately… he seemed troubled. He came to me for some stamps the day before he vanished. He was pale, and his hands shook. He said he had ‘seen something he oughtn’t to have’.”

Holmes’s eyes gleamed. “Precisely. The pivotal moment. You have been of the greatest assistance, Mrs. Prewett. We will trouble you no further for the present.”

We took rooms at the village inn, and Holmes spent the afternoon in what he called ‘a perambulation of the facts’. He spoke to the ostler, to a ploughman, and to a postman on his round, always with the same quiet, purposeful air. By evening, he had pieced together a picture.

“Alistair Finch,” he announced as we sat before a modest fire, “is a man of property, but not of liquid means. The estate is entailed. His chief asset is his daughter, Eleanor, whose beauty is matched only by her apparent docility. The intended suitor is Sir Giles Rotherham, a man of considerable fortune and, if rumour is to be believed, considerable cruelty. The marriage would solve Finch’s debts. Croft, the loyal gamekeeper, was an obstacle to this neat arrangement.”

“And the damp letters?” I asked.

“That is the masterstroke, Watson. The tool of a subtle and patient mind. Sir Giles, I fancy, is not merely a brute. He is a strategist. He wishes to know the contents of Finch’s correspondence—perhaps regarding the marriage settlements, or perhaps some other secret. A common blackmailer’s trick, but executed with uncommon finesse.”

The following morning, we presented ourselves at Wychford Grange. The house was a fine Jacobean manor, but it had a melancholy air. Squire Finch was a portly, florid man with the bluster of a bully who fears he is losing his grip. He received us with ill-concealed annoyance.

“Investigators? From London? I have sent for no one! This is an impertinence!”

“It was sent for by a loyal member of your household, sir,” said Holmes, his voice cool and level. “One who is concerned by the disappearance of your gamekeeper, Silas Croft.”

At that moment, a young woman entered the room. She was pale and slender, with a mass of auburn hair and eyes that held a deep, silent terror. “Father,” she said, her voice barely a whisper. “Please.”

“Eleanor, be silent!” Finch snapped. “This does not concern you.”

“On the contrary,” said Holmes, his gaze fixed upon the girl, “it concerns her more than anyone. Miss Finch, did Silas Croft speak to you before he vanished?”

She flinched, her eyes darting towards a large, ornate bureau in the corner of the room. It was a fleeting gesture, but Holmes saw it.

“A handsome piece,” he remarked, strolling towards it. “Flemish, is it not? The lock is a particularly ingenious one. A Bramah, I fancy. But then, a man who uses a hot letter opener to steam a seal would have little trouble with a mere lock.”

Finch’s face went purple. “What are you suggesting?”

“I am suggesting,” said Holmes, his hand resting on the polished wood, “that there is a secret in this room which Silas Croft discovered. A secret worth killing for.” He turned suddenly. “Watson, the poker, if you please.”

Before I could move, the door opened and a tall, well-dressed man with a severe face and cold grey eyes entered the room. “What is the meaning of this uproar, Finch?” he demanded. It was Sir Giles Rotherham.

“The meaning, Sir Giles,” said Holmes, “is that a conspiracy of silence is at an end. You have been intercepting the squire’s letters to ascertain his financial state and to ensure the marriage contract was to your advantage. You used a heated blade to lift the wax, read the contents, and resealed the envelope. A neat trick, but one that left a tell-tale dampness.”

Sir Giles smiled, a cold, reptilian expression. “A fanciful theory. You have no proof.”

“Do I not?” Holmes turned to the bureau. “This desk is kept locked. Yet there is a fine scratch upon the brasswork, just here, of a recent date. It is the mark of a service revolver being drawn in haste and striking the wood. A revolver, I would wager, that was used to compel Mr. Croft to accompany you to some quiet spot on the estate.”

Eleanor Finch gave a small, choked sob. “It is true,” she whispered. “Silas told me. He saw Sir Giles in the library, with the letters. He heard him threaten my father. He was going to tell him he would expose the whole affair. Sir Giles followed him out… and he never came back.”

Sir Giles’s composure cracked. His hand flew to his waistcoat, but Holmes was faster. With a sudden, fluid motion, he struck the man’s wrist. A small, nickel-plated revolver clattered to the floor.

“The game is up, Rotherham,” said Holmes, his voice like ice. “You will find the body of Silas Croft in the old ice-house, I believe. The servants say it has not been used this season.”

The subsequent confession was a sordid affair of greed and brutality. Sir Giles, faced with the certainty of the gallows, admitted to luring Croft to the ice-house on the pretext of a clandestine meeting and there shooting him. The letters had been his one weakness, the one piece of evidence he could not afford to have exposed.

The case was concluded with remarkable speed. Sir Giles Rotherham was tried for murder at the Winchester Assizes and hanged. The marriage between Eleanor Finch and the man who had so nearly ruined her life was, of course, called off. I learned later that she married a respectable young clergyman from a neighbouring parish, and that the squire, chastened by the affair, learned to manage his estate with greater prudence.

As for Mrs. Prewett, the postmistress, she received a handsome annuity from the Finch family, and her letters to London were never again troubled by the damp. Holmes, of course, dismissed the whole affair as a commonplace murder, and was more interested in a new monograph he was writing on the distinguishing characteristics of one hundred and forty varieties of cigar ash. But for me, the memory of that quiet Hampshire village and the courage of a simple woman who dared to post a single, fateful letter, remains one of the most singular episodes in my collection of his adventures.
