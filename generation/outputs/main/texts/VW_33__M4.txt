The fork made a small sound against the plate—a single clear note, like a glass struck lightly at the edge of the table—and Clara set it down again, and looked at the hand that had done it, her own hand, pale against the white cloth, and thought, I have done it, then; it is done; and the words seemed to have no meaning at all, merely a shape, a row of pebbles laid on the shore, and the tide of the dinner going on and on around her, the tide of the aunts.

“—for my part,” Aunt Beatrice was saying, her voice rising and falling like something soft and heavy dropped again and again onto a cushion, “I have never held with the modern notion that a girl must be forever gadding. There is a time for everything, I say, and the time for settling is when one is young, and the settling itself—why, it is the most natural thing in the world, and the more natural a thing is, the less fuss should be made of it. That is my opinion.”

“Oh, quite, quite,” said Aunt Millicent, who agreed with everyone and thereby agreed with no one, and she smiled her soft, vague smile at the candles, at the silver, at the flushed face of Clara’s mother, who sat at the foot of the table with her lips pressed together as though she were holding something between them that must not be allowed to escape.

Clara looked at her mother. Her mother looked at the salt cellar. The salt cellar was small and of heavy cut glass, and it caught the light of the candles and threw it back in little fractured stars, and Clara thought: she knows. She has known since before dinner, since I came down the stairs, since I stood in the hall and could not make my hands be still; and she has said nothing, and will say nothing, and the nothing is a great wall between us, bricked up out of all the things we do not say, and behind the wall she is waiting, as I am waiting, for the evening to be over.

“And how is your dear Edmund?” asked Aunt Beatrice, turning her head, and the whole table turned with her, like a field of corn under a wind, all faces inclining toward the empty chair at Clara’s right hand. “He is late this evening, is he not? It is not like him to be late.”

“He telephoned,” said Clara’s father from the head of the table, and he cleared his throat, and folded his napkin, and unfolded it again, a man who had spent his life among figures and found them obedient, and now found himself among women and words and could not make them come right. “He telephoned to say he would be detained at the office. He will be here presently.”

“Ah, the office,” said Aunt Beatrice, with satisfaction, as though the office were a well-known and estimable person. “There is a young man who knows his duty. Clara, my dear, you are very silent this evening. You are pale, too. Are you quite well?”

“Quite well, Aunt,” said Clara, and her voice came out small and smooth and entirely unlike her own, a voice she had borrowed from somewhere, from a drawer in a spare room, and she thought: if I speak again I shall not be able to keep it up, I shall say something, I shall break the wall, and they will all look at me, and the looking will be worse than the saying; and so she took up her glass and drank water and set it down and looked at the water in the glass, which trembled, and was still.

The dinner went on. The soup was removed, and the fish was brought, and the fish was sole, and the sole lay upon its dish in a pale and placid manner, and Aunt Millicent remarked that she had always preferred a sole to a turbot, she could not say why, and Aunt Beatrice said that a turbot was a more substantial fish, and there was a moment of quiet in which the only sound was the sound of knives and forks, and Clara thought: he will come in at that door, and he will look at me, and he will see nothing, because he is kind and dull and he sees only what he has always seen, and he will sit down in that chair and ask me if I am well, and I shall say quite well, and then—then I must tell him. But not here. Not with the aunts. Not with the candles and the sole. Afterwards, in the hall, in the little room where the coats are hung, among the umbrellas and the galoshes, I shall say: Edmund, I cannot. I cannot. And he will look at me with those kind, bewildered eyes, and he will say—what will he say?—that I am tired, that I am overwrought, that I do not mean it, that in the morning I shall think differently. And I shall say: no. No. And the word will be like a stone dropped into a well, and the well will be his kindness, and I shall hear it fall and fall and never hear it strike the bottom.

“You are not eating, Clara,” said her mother, and her voice was sharp, and it cut across the table, and Aunt Beatrice looked up, and Aunt Millicent looked up, and Clara’s father looked at his wife and then at his daughter, and Clara thought: now. Now it will come out. She will say it. She will say, Clara has something to tell us, I see it in her face, she has been strange all day, she has been strange for a week, and the aunts will put down their forks, and the whole thing will be said here, over the sole, and it will be worse than anything, it will be unbearable, and yet—and yet it would be over, it would be done, and there would be nothing left to fear.

But her mother said nothing more. Her mother looked at the salt cellar, and her lips pressed together, and Clara understood, with a sudden sinking of the heart, that her mother would not speak; that her mother was waiting, as she herself was waiting, for Edmund; that her mother believed, in some deep and wordless place, that when Edmund came, when Edmund sat down in that chair and smiled his kind, dull smile, the thing would be mended, the wall would come down, the evening would be like other evenings, and Clara would be good, would be sensible, would be married in the spring as had been arranged.

And the door opened.

He came in with the air of a man who has been detained at the office and is sorry for it, and he was tall and fair and a little heavy about the shoulders, and he bowed to the aunts and shook hands with Clara’s father and said a word to Clara’s mother, and then he came round the table and took his place at Clara’s right hand, and the whole room seemed to settle, to breathe out, to become itself again, and Aunt Beatrice said, “Ah, Edmund, we had quite given you up,” and Edmund said, “I am very sorry, I was detained,” and he turned to Clara and said, “You are pale, my dear. Are you quite well?”

“Quite well,” said Clara, and she looked at him, at his kind, dull, patient face, at the face that had been beside her for a year, at the hands that had held hers, at the eyes that had looked at her and seen—what? A wife, perhaps. A house in the country. Children. A life arranged, and orderly, and without surprises, and she thought: he does not know. He will never know until I tell him. And I must tell him. And I cannot tell him here. And the evening is going on, and the aunts are talking, and the candles are burning down, and the sole is gone, and the roast is coming, and the whole world is going on and on and will not stop for me, will not stop for a single moment, and I must sit here and smile and eat and say quite well, and all the time—all the time—the words are in my mouth, the words that will break his heart and set me free, and I cannot say them, not yet, not here, not with the aunts.

“I am glad,” said Edmund, and he smiled, and he took up his fork, and he began to eat, and the conversation flowed around them, and Clara sat very still, and the candle flames stood up straight and steady in the quiet air, and the silver shone, and the salt cellar threw its little fractured stars upon the cloth, and the evening went on, and on, and on.
