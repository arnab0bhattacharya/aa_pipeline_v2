It is a common error, and one which those who have never felt the pressure of narrow circumstances are most ready to commit, to suppose that the great moral crises of a life arrive with some clear signal of their own importance. They are more often discerned only in the looking back, when the whole landscape of a man’s existence has been altered, and he can see the single, unremarkable turning where the path forked. For Thomas Heywood, in the autumn of 1847, the crisis arrived not as a thunderclap but as a letter on a Tuesday morning, the paper thin and foreign, smelling of salt and distance. The town of Netherfield, with its weekly market and its slow, tidal flow of agricultural life, had its own rhythms, and the letter was a stone thrown into the still water of his routine.

The promise he had made to his mother had been a thing of quiet, unadorned gravity. In her last weeks, when the consumption had reduced her to a flutter of breath and bone, her mind had been preternaturally clear on one point alone: the future of her younger son, Joe. “He’s not simple, Tom, not in his heart,” she had whispered, her hand, so thin he feared to hold it, gripping his with a final, desperate strength. “He’s like a child that’s grown tall. He sees the world through a mist. You must be his eyes when the mist thickens. You must not let the world be harsh with him.” Thomas, then a man of twenty, had given his word, and it had settled in him not as a burden, but as a piece of his own moral skeleton, a fact of his being as certain as the bones of his hands.

And he had been faithful to it. For eight years, he had built his life around Joe. He had taken the apprenticeship with Mr. Clegg, the wheelwright, not because it was the work he would have chosen, but because it was steady and kept him in Netherfield. He had managed their small inheritance with a parsimony that the neighbours mistook for avarice, seeing only the mended coat and the meagre fire, and not the shillings laid by against the day when Joe might need a doctor or a roof of his own. Joe, in his turn, loved his brother with the whole, uncomplicated force of his nature. He would sit for hours in the workshop, the scent of sawdust and tar thick in the air, watching Thomas’s strong hands shape a spoke or set a felloe, his own mind, a placid and shallow pool, reflecting only the simple satisfaction of the scene. He was a large, gentle creature, his face unlined by the anxieties that had etched themselves so early around his brother’s eyes. To the town, he was ‘simple Joe Heywood’, a figure of mild pity or coarse amusement; to Thomas, he was the living soul of his promise.

The letter was from his mother’s brother, his uncle Silas, who had emigrated to Upper Canada a dozen years before and of whom they had heard little. Silas was a man of energy and imagination, and his letter was a map of a new world. He wrote of land so rich it seemed to breathe fertility, of timber as straight and tall as church spires, of a burgeoning trade in which a man with a strong back and a willing heart could make a fortune in a decade. He had a contract for clearing a section of forest near a new settlement on Lake Huron, and he needed a partner, a man he could trust. He had thought of Thomas. The opportunity was of a kind that does not knock twice; it was a door thrown open to a sunlit country, away from the damp, encroaching shadows of a Nottinghamshire winter, away from the grinding, slow-motion struggle of Netherfield.

Thomas read the letter three times, and with each reading, the world of the wheelwright’s shop seemed to shrink and grow greyer. He felt a fierce, unfamiliar stirring in his blood—the call of a future that was his own, a future not defined by duty but by desire. He could see it: the towering pines falling before his axe, the broad acres of black soil, the house he would build, the life he would command. It was a vision of strength and self-reliance, a manhood unbound by the gentle, heavy chains of the past. For an hour, he walked by the river, the letter clutched in his pocket, and felt the intoxicating lightness of a man on the verge of escape.

But when he returned to the cottage, the vision collided with reality. Joe was sitting on the hearth, patiently trying to mend a broken whistle, his brow furrowed in an earnest, hopeless concentration. He looked up as Thomas entered, his face breaking into a wide, guileless smile. “I can’t make it toot, Tom,” he said, holding it out. In that moment, the question, which had seemed a mere practical difficulty—how to manage Joe’s passage and care for him in a wild land—resolved itself into its true, terrible form. Joe could not go. The thought of the long sea voyage, the crowded emigrant ship, the strangeness and hardship of the clearing, was an impossibility. It would be like taking a hothouse plant and planting it on a frozen fell. The promise to his mother surged back, not as a memory, but as a living presence. *You must not let the world be harsh with him.*

The temptation was to rationalise, to find a way to honour both his dead mother and his own living self. He could leave Joe with their Aunt Sarah, a decent woman but with a house full of her own children and little to spare. He could send money back, more than Joe would ever need. He would be providing for him, would he not? He would be fulfilling the material part of the promise, and in a fashion far more splendid than he could manage by staying. But Thomas was not a man who could be satisfied with the material part of a spiritual vow. He knew that the promise was not merely for Joe’s bread and board; it was for the comfort of his presence, the steadying hand on his shoulder, the familiar voice in the dark. To leave Joe would be to abandon him to a world that would misunderstand and misuse him, to a loneliness more profound than any poverty. It would be to break the promise in its essence, while keeping its letter. And a promise so broken, he felt with a certainty that had the force of a physical law, would poison the very fortune he went to seek. The wealth of the new world would be as ashes in his mouth, for it would be bought with the currency of his own integrity.

He sat up late that night, the letter on the table before him, the candle casting long, wavering shadows. He thought of his mother’s face in the firelight, of the trust in her eyes. He thought of the vast, green promise of Canada, of the man he might have been there. And he thought of Joe, asleep in the next room, his breathing soft and even, a child in a man’s body, utterly dependent on the word of the dead. It was not a simple choice between love and ambition. It was a question of what a man’s word is worth, and whether the claims of the past can ever be truly severed from the duties of the present. A promise to the dead, he saw at last, does not bind the living by some supernatural force; it binds them because it has become part of their own soul, and to break it would be a form of self-destruction. The future he would build would have to be built here, in Netherfield, with wood and iron, and the slow, patient love of a brother. He picked up the letter, and held it to the candle flame, watching the curling black script vanish into light and heat. In the morning, he would tell Joe that they were not going away, and would see the simple, unquestioning joy dawn on his brother’s face, and know that he was, after all, a rich man.
