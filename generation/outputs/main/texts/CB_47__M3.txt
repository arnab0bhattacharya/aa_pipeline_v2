The wind from the moors came down upon Stubbs Cross like a chastisement. It shook the lattice of the grey stone vicarage where I lodged, and swept through the valley with a voice of fierce, unutterable reproach. I had been married two years to Mr. Thorne, the master of the great worsted mill that crouched, a dark and breathing monster, by the river; yet in all that time, I had not learned to blunt the edge of that solitude which was my daily bread. My husband was a man of iron will and sordid calculations; he looked upon me as a piece of household furniture, useful to preside over his table, but utterly unworthy of his confidence. His men and women—the pale, toil-worn denizens of the village—regarded me with a settled, sullen aversion. I was the master’s wife; therefore, I was of the race of oppressors. The bread of affliction is heavy, but the waters of bitterness, drunk alone in the midst of a crowd that despises you, are a more agonizing draught.

It was in the deepening of an autumn twilight that I first went down into the village, moved by a spirit I could not wholly tame, to seek out the sick. The fever was in the cottages. It bred in the close, noisome air, fed by the damp of the river and the unmitigated filth of the alleys. My husband laughed—a hard, dry, grating sound—when I spoke of carrying broth and medicine. "Let them die and be damned," he said, turning to his ledgers; "the parish must bury them, and I must have fresh hands. You will only breed ingratitude, Esther." But the word of the Lord, which burns like fire in the bones, was upon me. I could not sit by my own clear hearth and know that, scarce a furlong off, a fellow-creature was gasping out his breath in darkness.

I took my basket and went. The first cottages I entered yielded only terror and a reluctant, sullen tolerance. The women curtseyed stiffly, their faces pale and drawn, their eyes casting furtive, suspicious glances at my plain stuff gown and clean cloak, as if they suspected some snare. They took the broth I offered, but they did not thank me. They watched me as a caged bird watches the hand of its captor—with a dumb, prescient dread. I spoke to them of comfort, of the mercy of God; they answered me in monosyllables, turning their faces to the wall. The atmosphere of those low rooms was heavy with the smell of fever, damp wool, and unwholesome humanity. I felt a strange, secret fire of indignation kindle within me—not against the poor, but against the iron fate that had cast my lot among the masters.

At the end of a miry lane, hemmed in by the black, smoking walls of the dye-works, stood a hovel smaller and more ruinous than the rest. Its roof sagged beneath the weight of accumulated soot; its single window was a square of patched glass, through which a dim, yellowish light struggled. I lifted the latch and entered. On a pallet of dirty straw lay a girl, her cheeks flushed with the scarlet hue of consumption, her great dark eyes burning with unnatural fire. Beside her sat an older woman—her mother, I deemed her—gaunt, grim, and unyielding as the Yorkshire rock. She did not rise; she did not speak. She fixed upon me a look so black, so full of concentrated scorn, that I paused upon the threshold.

"You are the master's wife," she said. It was not a question, but a sentence of judgment.

"I am Mrs. Thorne," I answered, striving to keep my voice low and steady, though my heart beat thick. "I heard your daughter was ill. I have brought some wine and a little arrowroot."

"We want nothing at your hands," replied the woman. Her voice was like the grinding of a millstone. "Take your fine things and go back to the Hall. You can do nothing here. The Almighty does not need the devil's agents to do His work."

The words struck me with a sudden, stinging force. I felt the blood mount to my forehead. "You are cruel and unjust," I said, my restraint breaking for a moment. "I am not the devil, nor is my coming here a mockery. I seek only to serve."

"Serve!" echoed the woman, with a bitter, grating laugh. "Aye, you and your sort serve us as the cat serves the mouse. Did your husband not turn my son out of the mill when the cough was upon him, lest he should infect the others? And where is he now? In the churchyard, under six feet of cold clay. And you come here with wine? Keep it. It is bought with the price of our blood."

She pointed a thin, trembling finger at the door. I looked at the girl upon the pallet; her eyes met mine with a strange, sorrowful intelligence. I was dismissed. I turned and left the hovel, the wind rushing to meet me as I stepped into the lane, tearing at my bonnet and whirling the ashes of the mill into my face.

But I did not retreat to the Hall. I walked onward, up the bleak ascent towards the moor, until the roar of the mill was subdued to a murmur. The night was black and starless; the clouds drove across the sky like shattered battalions. In my soul there was a tempest to match it. Why had she refused me? What was this deep, rankling wound that bled beneath the surface of their poverty? I had been proud, perhaps; I had been ignorant. I had thought that alms could bridge the gulf. I saw now, with a terrible clearness, that it was a gulf of blood.

From that hour, my purpose was fixed. I would not be driven from that hovel by the thunders of a grief-stricken mother. If my husband was their tyrant, I would be their servant. I would learn the history of that outcast son; I would sift the ashes of their wrongs. It was a stern, a perilous vow; it drew me away from the smooth paths of domestic quiet into a labyrinth of suffering and sin. But the die was cast. I felt within me a vocation stronger than my woman's weakness, mightier than my wife's submission. The fire of a righteous indignation was kindled; it should not be quenched.
