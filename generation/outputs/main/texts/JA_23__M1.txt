Miss Frances Harcourt had been at some pains to cultivate a reputation for frankness, a quality she esteemed in herself above all others, and which she was persuaded her neighbours must admire, if only they had the sense to perceive its value. It was a reputation, however, that stood her in poor stead in the society of a small country town like Ashford, where opinions, if they must be had, were best kept as private as one’s morning chocolate. Frances, by contrast, held that a sentiment honestly felt was a sentiment worth expressing, and she expressed herself on the subject of the newly married Mr and Mrs Ashton with a candour that left her audience aghast.

“Indeed,” she declared to Mrs Gable and her two daughters, over a card table that evening, “I cannot think what possessed Mr Ashton. A man of his standing, so estimable in every other particular, to be thus caught! Mrs Ashton is pretty, I grant you; but prettiness is a fragile commodity. She has no conversation, no understanding beyond the pattern of a new bonnet. It is a marriage of such profound inequality of mind as to be quite a tragedy of its own small kind. He will be bored within a twelvemonth.”

The silence that followed was of a quality peculiarly damning. Mrs Gable, a woman whose chief talent was the maintenance of a smooth social surface, fumbled with her fan. The Miss Gables exchanged a look of such horrified delight that Frances mistook it for agreement.

“I am glad you see it as I do,” she pressed on, emboldened. “One ought to speak the truth of these matters. It is a disservice to Mr Ashton to pretend otherwise. A wife should be a companion, not an ornament.”

It was only the next morning, when the truth reached her via the most indirect and mortifying of channels, that Frances understood the true nature of the silence at the card table. Mr Ashton’s new bride, the former Miss Beatrice Croft, was the younger sister of Mr Thomas Croft, the very gentleman whose good opinion Frances had been labouring to secure for the past three months.

Mr Croft had come into the neighbourhood in the spring, taking the lease of Bexley Hall. He was possessed of a fortune, a quiet manner, and an understanding that seemed to Frances perfectly to match her own. They had discussed poetry and the follies of the French; he had listened to her opinions with an attentiveness she had found both novel and intoxicating. She had dared to believe that he saw in her not a flighty girl, but a woman of sense and principle. Now, that principle had led her to publicly disparage his sister as a fool.

The discovery was made by her aunt, Lady Ashcombe, a woman whose social perception was as sharp as her tongue. “Frances,” she said, not looking up from her embroidery, “I have just had a note from Mrs Gable. She is in a great deal of pain on your account, and wishes to know if you are quite well. It seems your frankness has taken an unfortunate turn.”

Frances felt the blood drain from her face. “I do not understand you, Aunt.”

“Do you not? Then you have lost your wits entirely. The bride you so thoroughly dissected last evening is Mr Croft’s sister. The man you have been pursuing with all the subtlety of a fox-hunt is her brother.”

The world tilted on its axis. Frances’s pride, that sturdy structure upon which she had built her entire sense of self, crumbled into dust. Her frankness was not a virtue; it was a vulgarity. Her honesty was not a principle; it was a weapon, wielded with a clumsiness that had wounded the one person she most wished to impress. She saw, in a sudden, sickening flash, the whole affair from Mr Croft’s perspective: a woman who prided herself on judgment, passing a cruel and public sentence upon his family. What could he think of her, but that she was envious, malicious, and utterly devoid of the delicacy of feeling he must value in a woman?

For several days, she was a prisoner of her own mortification, refusing all company. To see Mr Croft would be insupportable; to see Mrs Gable would be to relive the scene of her disgrace. She had built her house of character upon the rock of her own opinion, and had found it to be built upon sand.

At last, an invitation she could not refuse arrived: the Ashtons were to give a small dinner, and Lady Ashcombe informed her niece that they would attend. “You will face the consequences of your own tongue, Frances,” her aunt said, with a grim sort of satisfaction. “It is high time you learned that the world is not a debating club.”

The evening was an agony. Frances felt every eye upon her, every polite greeting a veiled accusation. Mr Ashton was civil, but his civility was a wall. Mrs Ashton, the object of her scorn, was quiet and composed, her prettiness now seeming to Frances a kind of gentle dignity she had been too blind to see. And then, she saw him. Mr Croft stood by the mantelpiece, speaking to his sister. He looked up, and his eyes met Frances’s. There was no anger in his expression, no contempt; only a cool, unreadable politeness that was infinitely worse.

It was Mrs Ashton who approached her. “Miss Harcourt,” she said, her voice soft. “I am so glad you could come.”

Frances could barely form a reply. “Mrs Ashton. You are very kind.”

“My brother tells me you are a great reader,” Mrs Ashton continued, her gaze direct and without malice. “He says you have a most decided opinion on everything.”

The words were a gentle reproach, but they stung more than any accusation. Here was the sister, the supposed simpleton, demonstrating a social grace Frances herself had utterly lacked. In that moment, she saw the truth of the matter. Her pride in her own judgment had made her blind to the virtues of others. She had not been frank; she had been cruel.

Her opportunity came later in the evening. Finding herself alone with Mr Croft near the window, she knew she must speak. Her heart hammered against her ribs, but the old habit of directness, now chastened by shame, served her one last time.

“Mr Croft,” she began, her voice unsteady. “I must speak to you. I have behaved in a way that has caused me the deepest regret. What I said of your sister… it was unforgivable.”

He looked at her, his expression still guarded. “It was an opinion freely given, Miss Harcourt.”

“It was an opinion born of ignorance and arrogance,” she corrected him, her gaze unwavering. “I mistook my own conceit for honesty. I have wounded you and your family, and in doing so, I have shown myself to be a person of far less character than I had imagined. I can only ask for your pardon, though I know I do not deserve it.”

Mr Croft was silent for a long moment. The noise of the party seemed to fade away. He then gave a small, slow nod. “My sister is very forgiving,” he said quietly. “And I believe that a person who can see their own folly so clearly is not without merit.”

It was not a declaration, nor was it absolution. It was, perhaps, a beginning. Frances felt a weight lift from her heart, not of triumph, but of relief. She had lost the battle of her own pride, but in its defeat, she had found a truer, more valuable quality: humility. And as she looked at Mr Croft, she understood that a good opinion, honestly earned, was worth infinitely more than a frank opinion, hastily given.
