The heat of Florence in June was a solid, physical thing, a weight that pressed upon the shoulders and made the very stones of the Lungarno seem to shimmer. For Emmeline, fresh from the grey, damp breath of an English spring, it was both oppressive and intoxicating. Her sister, Beatrice, now Mrs. Arthur Pendelton, had met her at the station with a flurry of kisses and a wave of lavender water, and had installed her in a cool, high-ceilinged room in their villa in the hills of Bellosguardo, from which the whole city lay spread out below like a magnificent, terracotta-coloured toy.

“You must meet everyone,” Beatrice declared on the third day, fanning herself with a lace fan as they took tea on the terrace. “The colony here is so stimulating. So… alive. There is Mr. Alistair Finch, the poet—quite scandalous, my dear, he lives with a Contessa—and Mrs. Ida Vance, who paints the most extraordinary, morbid things. And then there is Mr. Julian Ashworth, the critic. He is the sun around which they all orbit. You will be dazzled.”

Emmeline, who had spent her life in the quiet of a Shropshire parsonage, was not at all sure she wished to be dazzled. But Beatrice was insistent, and so, a week later, Emmeline found herself seated in the long, shadowed drawing-room of the Villa Vespertina, the home of Mrs. Vance.

It was a room designed for performance. The light was carefully subdued, the furniture was heavy and dark, and the air was thick with the scent of lilies and Turkish tobacco. The guests were arranged in attitudes of languid brilliance. Alistair Finch, a man with the face of a dissipated angel, was declaiming in a low, musical voice about the necessity of suffering for art, while a woman with kohl-rimmed eyes—the Contessa, presumably—hung on his every word. Mrs. Vance, a tall, gaunt woman in a gown of severe black, watched the room with the predatory stillness of a hawk.

And at the centre of it all was Julian Ashworth. He was not a large man, but he possessed a peculiar intensity that made him seem to fill the space around him. He spoke with a clipped, precise authority, his words falling like chips of ice into the warm, perfumed air. He was dissecting the work of a young painter who had recently left Florence.

“Poor dear,” Ashworth said, a thin smile playing on his lips. “He had a certain… vigour. But no intellect. He painted the Duomo as if it were a wedding cake. He saw the surface, you understand, and thought it was the soul. It is the tragedy of the English in Italy. We come for the beauty and mistake it for profundity.”

There was a ripple of appreciative, cruel laughter. Emmeline felt a flush of shame, though she was not sure for whom. She felt utterly out of her depth, a plain brown sparrow among birds of paradise who were all too ready to peck one another to pieces. Beatrice, she saw, was laughing too, her hand resting lightly on the arm of a portly man who was not her husband.

It was then that Emmeline noticed the man standing by the tall windows, half-hidden in the shadow of a heavy velvet curtain. He was not speaking, nor was he laughing. He was simply watching, a glass of wine untouched in his hand. He was of middle height, with a quiet, unremarkable face and kind, intelligent eyes that seemed to miss nothing. While the others performed, he observed. He was the only person in the room who seemed to be breathing at a normal rate.

During a lull, as Mrs. Vance was called away to attend to a servant, Emmeline found herself standing near him. He turned to her, and his smile was gentle, without a trace of the condescension that flavoured the air.

“You are Mrs. Pendelton’s sister,” he said. It was a statement, not a question. “Miss Fairbrother. I am Henry Latimer. I am afraid you have been thrown into the lion’s den.”

“They are very… clever,” Emmeline managed, her voice sounding small to her own ears.

“They are,” he agreed. “They are also very frightened. It is why they must constantly reassure one another of their own brilliance. It is a house built on sand, you see, and they can feel the tide coming in.”

He spoke without malice, merely with a quiet, sad certainty. He was a painter, he explained, though he did not exhibit with the others. He painted landscapes, mostly. “I find the hills around Fiesole far more honest company than most people,” he said.

Their conversation was a small, quiet oasis in the midst of the glittering, arid desert of the party. He asked her about Shropshire, and listened with genuine interest as she described the quiet lanes and the scent of wet leaves in autumn. He did not try to impress her, nor did he mock the things she loved. He simply talked, and in doing so, made her feel less like a specimen under glass and more like a person.

When Julian Ashworth’s voice cut across the room, it felt like a physical blow. “Ah, Latimer. Still hiding in the corners, I see? Still painting your pretty little views for the tourists to buy? Tell me, do you ever feel the urge to create something that isn’t merely… decorative?”

The room fell silent. All eyes turned to them. Emmeline felt a surge of hot anger. She saw the casual cruelty in Ashworth’s smile, the way he wielded his wit like a rapier, not to illuminate, but to wound. She saw the flicker of pain in Latimer’s eyes before he masked it with a calm that must have cost him a great deal.

“I find decoration has its place, Julian,” Latimer replied, his voice even. “It offers a little comfort. Something the world is often in short supply of.”

Ashworth gave a dismissive sniff and turned away, seeking a more receptive audience for his next sally. The moment passed, but the damage was done. Emmeline saw the fragile ecosystem of the room for what it was: a brutal hierarchy of wit and power, and Henry Latimer, in his quiet decency, was its victim.

A week later, she contrived to visit his small studio near the Porta Romana. It was a world away from the stifling grandeur of the Villa Vespertina. The room was filled with light and the smell of turpentine and oil. Canvases were stacked against the walls, and on an easel was a painting of the Arno valley, not a grand, dramatic vista, but a quiet, intimate view of a bend in the river, with a single cypress tree standing guard. It was full of a profound and gentle peace.

He was embarrassed by her visit, but pleased. They drank tea from chipped cups, and he showed her his work. He spoke of light, and colour, and the difficulty of capturing a feeling that was true. He was not brilliant in the way the others were. He did not sparkle or dazzle. But he was real. He was the solid ground beneath the shifting, treacherous sands of the English colony.

As she was leaving, he pressed a small, unfinished sketch into her hand. It was of her, seen in profile as she looked out of his studio window, her face soft with a quiet attention.

“It is not very good,” he said, flushing. “But it is honest.”

Emmeline looked from the sketch to his kind, anxious face. The brilliance and cruelty of the drawing-rooms of Florence suddenly seemed very far away, a noisy, empty spectacle. She had come to Italy to be dazzled, but she had found something far more precious in the quiet honesty of a man who was neither. She held the sketch to her breast, and for the first time since her arrival, she felt the heat of the city not as a weight, but as a warmth.
