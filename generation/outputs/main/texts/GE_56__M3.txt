Mr. Lionel Transome had returned to England with no more than seven hundred pounds in his pocket and a trunk full of Moorish embroideries, having spent the larger part of his fortune in a grand, restless circumnavigation of the globe that had yielded him every pleasure except contentment. He was a man of forty, with a constitution already showing the subtle ravages of sun and fever, and a countenance that had once been cast in a mold of striking romantic elegance, but was now marked by that peculiar air of exhausted expectation which belongs to those who have sought the world’s wonders too avidly and found them wanting. For ten years he had not set foot on British soil, and in that interval the slow, sure march of provincial and metropolitan life had wrought changes of which he was wholly ignorant—changes not merely in the stucco frontages of streets, but in the moral and intellectual substance of persons he had once known intimately, or rather, had known with that superficiality which passes for intimacy among the self-absorbed.

Among these persons was his cousin, Miss Hester Winwood. He remembered her as a thin, pale young woman of two-and-twenty, given to sitting silently in corners at family gatherings in the bleak house at Dinton, her eyes following the lively discourse of others with a timid, almost apologetic attention, as though she were conscious that her own existence was an intrusion upon the more vivid lives around her. Lionel had dismissed her then, as men dismiss women who possess neither beauty nor wealth nor that provocative helplessness which flatters the masculine instinct for protection. He had bestowed upon her precisely the sort of pitying notice that a prosperous young man of fashion gives to a poor relation—speaking to her occasionally with a careless kindness that was harder to bear than indifference, and then forgetting her existence entirely before he had crossed the threshold.

But the world had not forgotten Hester Winwood, nor had she forgotten herself into oblivion. There had grown up in the quiet, uneventful years of Lionel’s absence a small but significant body of literature dealing with the condition of the laboring poor, the education of women, and the moral economy of industrial life; and in this literature the name of Hester Winwood had come to occupy a place of recognized authority. Her book, “The Foundations of Social Order,” published three years before Lionel’s return, had passed through four editions and had been reviewed with grave respect in the quarterly journals. It was not a brilliant book, nor one that stirred the passions; it was a patient, weighty, carefully reasoned work, drawing its facts from personal observation of workhouses and factories, and its conclusions from a mind trained in the severe school of practical experience. She had not written it in the fervor of youthful enthusiasm, but in the mature, reflective period of her thirty-fifth year, when the slow accumulation of thought and feeling had at last found its natural outlet.

Lionel, upon learning of this transformation, experienced a complex disturbance of spirit which he could not immediately analyze. He was seated in his lodgings in Bloomsbury, a faded apartment that suited his diminished circumstances, holding in his hand a copy of her book which a mutual acquaintance had pressed upon him. The afternoon light fell upon the page, and he read with growing astonishment sentences that showed not merely intelligence, but a moral earnestness, a breadth of sympathetic insight, that seemed to belong to a different order of being from the shrinking girl he remembered. He was conscious of a sting—a sharp, humiliating sting—which he tried to attribute to surprise, but which he knew in his secret heart was the pain of discovering that he had misjudged, and that the person he had most contemptuously undervalued had outgrown him in all the things that give dignity to life.

It was in this mood of uncertain humiliation that he determined to seek her out. She was living, he learned, in a modest house in a quiet square near the British Museum, maintaining herself on the proceeds of her writing and a small legacy left to her by an aunt. He wrote to her, asking permission to call, and received a brief, civil reply in a hand that was firm and clear, without pretension, giving him an hour on the following afternoon.

As he walked through the streets toward her house, he found himself trying to frame the attitude he should assume. Should he meet her as the repentant prodigal, acknowledging his former blindness and soliciting her forgiveness? Or should he present himself as the man of the world, returned from distant lands, bringing to her quiet study the freshness of wider experience? The truth was—and truth has a way of asserting itself in these preparatory moments when the soul rehearses its part—that he did not know what he wanted from her. He was drawn to her by a motive that was partly curiosity, partly a dim, unacknowledged need for the respect of a person whose judgment he had formerly disregarded, and partly by that deeper yearning which comes to men who have exhausted pleasure and find themselves, at last, hungry for the bread of serious human fellowship. He wanted her pity; he wanted her respect; and he was not sure that he could endure either.

She received him in a small study lined with books, the plain mahogany table bearing no ornaments except a reading-lamp and a pile of manuscript. She was taller than he remembered, or perhaps the dignity of her bearing made her seem so. Her face was still pale, still thin, but the features had acquired a clearness and decision that belonged to the settled purpose within. Her eyes, which had once been downcast and uncertain, met his now with a directness that was not aggressive, but simply the natural expression of a mind that had long since ceased to fear the judgment of others. She was dressed in dark gray stuff, plainly made, and there was about her whole person that unmistakable sign of a life consecrated to work—the absence of all those small concessions to vanity which, in the eyes of a man like Lionel, had once been the measure of a woman’s claim to notice.

“You find me much changed, I suppose,” she said, after the first greetings had been exchanged. There was no bitterness in her tone, no triumphant consciousness of the reversal their positions had undergone. She spoke with the quiet simplicity of one who had long since passed beyond the need for either vindication or apology.

“Greatly changed,” he answered, and then, with an impulse that surprised himself, he added: “I have been reading your book.”

“And what do you think of it?” she asked, not with the eagerness of an author soliciting praise, but with the calm interest of a worker inquiring into the reception of a product.

He paused. He had not read it carefully enough to offer an honest judgment, and he was not base enough to offer a dishonest one. “I think,” he said slowly, “that I am not competent to judge it. I have spent my life in seeing things, and you have spent yours in understanding them.”

It was a confession rather than a compliment, and she recognized it as such. There was a moment’s silence between them—a silence in which two lives, so widely separated in their development, seemed to touch for an instant at a single point of mutual recognition. Then she spoke again, not of her book or his travels, but of the common things that belong to the surface of life; and he was grateful to her for it, as a man is grateful for mercy when he had expected judgment.

But as he walked home through the gathering dusk, past the grimy squares and streets where the lamplighters were beginning their round, he was aware that the interview had left in him a deep, unresolved trouble. He had not obtained what he sought, because he had not known what he sought; and the clear, quiet eyes of Hester Winwood had shown him, more plainly than any words could have done, the full measure of that spiritual poverty which is the inevitable wages of a life spent in the pursuit of sensations rather than meanings. He felt himself, for the first time, truly poor—not in the reckoning of pounds, but in that harder coin of which the world takes no account until the hour of inward settlement comes.
