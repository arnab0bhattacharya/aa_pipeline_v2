He stood before me in the fading light of the gas-lamp, a ghost out of a past I had thought buried beneath the accumulating dust of my present respectability. It was in the narrow street outside, where the river-fog crept up from the Thames to mingle with the soot, clinging to the iron railings, softening the harsh angles of the great offices of state into something spectral and insubstantial. He had been a boy at Aldenham together—a fellow called Vyse, Gerald Vyse—and I had not set eyes on him for twelve years. But there was no mistaking that lean, dark head, tilted back with a sort of ironic defiance, or those eyes which had always seemed to look at one through a pane of smoked glass, as if the world were a spectacle he found at once ridiculous and offensive. He wore a coat that had seen better days, and his boots were thick with the yellow London mud, and he carried under his arm a bundle of papers tied with a piece of twine. He looked, I thought, like a man who has been walking for a long time in a hostile country, and has learned to carry his whole fortune upon his back.

"You are not surprised," he said, in that low, even voice I remembered, which had a way of dropping the last syllables of a sentence into a kind of abyss, as though the words themselves were ashamed of the silence that must follow them.

I was surprised—profoundly, utterly surprised—but I said I was not. It was a lie, of course, and I knew it for a lie as it left my lips, but there are moments when the truth is an impossibility, when the whole fabric of one's existence seems to hang upon the acceptance of a fiction, and one speaks not to communicate but to preserve the illusion that one is still the master of one's own destiny. I had become, in the years since Aldenham, a man of system, a man of parts in the great machine of government—private secretary to Sir Horace Plendergast, who was at that time the Minister of Works, and who sat for a constituency in the north that would have returned a dung-hill to Parliament so long as it wore the right rosette. I had my office, my routines, my careful correspondence, my orderly progress through the labyrinth of Whitehall. And into this ordered world, as if flung by some malevolent hand from the outer darkness, stepped Gerald Vyse, with his bundle of papers and his air of carrying about with him the knowledge of some tremendous and terrible secret.

We went into a public house that stood at the corner, a low, dark place where the gas-jets flickered over polished brass and the air was thick with the fumes of tobacco and spilled beer. He would not sit down. He stood at the bar and drank half a glass of bitter ale in one motion, as a man takes medicine, and then turned to me with that disquieting smile which had always seemed to me to be less an expression of pleasure than a kind of rictus—the grimace of one who has seen through the joke of life and finds it not amusing but merely obscene.

"I have come," he said, "because I want to see him."

I did not need to ask whom he meant. There was only one *him* in my world now—Sir Horace Plendergast, who had the heavy face of a man perpetually digesting a difficult meal, and whose mind, if mind it could be called, moved with the slow, majestic certainty of a glacier across the landscape of public affairs. He was not a clever man. He was not even, in any profound sense, an honest man. But he was powerful, in that blunt, animal way that passes for power in a democracy which has not yet learned to distinguish between force and reason. And I—his private secretary, his keeper of papers, his composer of phrases—I was the mechanism by which that power was made to appear, if not intelligent, then at least coherent.

"Gerald," I said, and I was aware even as I spoke of the patronising note in my voice, the note of a man who has arrived and wishes to be generous to one who has not, "I cannot imagine what you would want with Sir Horace."

"Cannot you?" he said, and his eyes held mine with a steadiness that made me uncomfortable. "I should have thought it was simple enough. I want to talk to him. I want to put a proposition to him. A certain—rearrangement of the social order."

I stared at him. I had heard rumours, of course—vague, disturbing rumours that Vyse had gone abroad, had involved himself with the revolutionary groups on the Continent, with the wild men of Geneva and Lyons who printed their seditious pamphlets in cellars and spoke of the destruction of governments as casually as one speaks of the weather. But I had not believed it—or rather, I had believed it in the way one believes in the existence of distant volcanoes: as a fact, certainly, but a fact without immediate personal relevance. Now, standing in the reeking bar with this man who had once been my friend, I felt the ground shift beneath me, as if the whole solid edifice of my London life were no more than a crust laid over a furnace.

"You are mad," I said, but without conviction.

"Possibly," he replied. "But I want to see him. You can arrange it. I know you can. You have the entrée. You are the doorkeeper, are you not? The man who decides who shall pass and who shall be turned away."

There was something in his voice—a bitterness, a contempt—that I should have heeded. But I did not. I heard only what I wished to hear: the appeal of an old friend, the opportunity to be magnanimous, to show that I had not forgotten the bonds of youth in the rush of my advancement. I saw myself, in that moment, as a figure of reconciliation—a bridge between the world of authority and the world of dissent, a man who could bring enemies together across the table of reason. It was a fine, flattering picture, and I embraced it with the eagerness of a man who, having sold his soul, is anxious to believe that the transaction has not been entirely without moral worth.

"I will arrange it," I said. "But Gerald—you must understand—he is not the man you imagine. He is not a thinker. He is not a philosopher of government. He is a practical man."

Vyse smiled again—that terrible, thin-lipped smile. "They are all practical men," he said softly. "That is why the world is as it is."

I arranged the meeting for the following Thursday, in Sir Horace's private room at the Office. I told him only that an old school-fellow of mine, now a journalist of some consequence on the Continent, wished to discuss certain matters of public interest. Sir Horace, who was not above vanity, and who enjoyed the company of men who could be useful to him, agreed with that ponderous affability which was his way of disguising the essential vacancy of his mind. "Bring him in, Frobisher, bring him in," he said, waving a thick hand as if dispersing a cloud of flies. "Always glad to meet a man of ideas. Though ideas, you know—ideas are all very well in their place. What the country wants is administration."

I did not tell Vyse what Sir Horace had said. I did not, in truth, repeat it to myself with any clear sense of its significance. I was living in a kind of dream—the dream of a man who believes that by an act of personal will he can reconcile the irreconcilable, that the gap between the world as it is and the world as one would wish it to be can be bridged by the simple exercise of good intentions. I was a fool. I see that now, with the terrible clarity that comes after the event, when all the circumstances arrange themselves in retrospect into a pattern whose meaning was visible all along, if one had but had the courage to look.

Vyse arrived at the appointed hour, and I ushered him into Sir Horace's room, which looked out over the river. It was a large, sombre apartment, furnished in the heavy style of the sixties, with dark panelling and oil portraits of former ministers whose names were already half-forgotten. Sir Horace sat behind his desk, a massive oak construction that seemed to have been designed not so much for the transaction of business as for the intimidation of visitors. He rose as we entered, and I saw Vyse's eyes travel over him with a slow, appraising gaze—the gaze of a man examining a specimen.

"Sir Horace," I said, "may I present Mr Vyse."

They shook hands. Sir Horace said something about being delighted, and Vyse said something about being honoured, and for a moment the two men stood regarding each other across the desk with that careful politeness which in England serves as a mask for every species of hostility and distrust. Then Sir Horace sat down, and Vyse sat down, and I, standing by the door, had the curious sensation of watching two figures on a stage, performing a play whose plot I did not know.

Vyse began to speak. His voice was low, measured, precise, with a foreign cadence that I had not noticed before—as if the years abroad had altered not merely his opinions but the very rhythm of his speech. He spoke of the condition of the working classes, of the iniquity of a system that concentrated wealth in the hands of the few, of the necessity for a fundamental reordering of society. He spoke without heat, without passion, without any of the gestures of the orator or the agitator. It was the voice of a man reciting facts—facts which, by their very nature, carried their own condemnation.

Sir Horace listened. He sat with his thick hands folded upon the desk, his heavy face impassive, and I could not tell whether he was moved or merely bored. When Vyse had finished, there was a silence—a silence that seemed to me to have a physical weight, as if the very air in the room had thickened and become something one could touch.

"My dear sir," Sir Horace said at last, in that bluff, hearty tone which he reserved for occasions when he wished to convey the impression that he had given a matter his most serious consideration, "you put your case with great force. Great force. But you must allow me to say that these are matters which require the most careful deliberation. The government of a great empire is not a thing to be undertaken lightly. There are interests—vast interests—which must be considered. The stability of the state—"

"I know," said Vyse, and his voice was so quiet that I leaned forward involuntarily to catch the words. "I know all about the interests. I know about the stability. That is why I have come."

He untied the twine around his bundle of papers and spread them out upon the desk. I saw Sir Horace's eyes widen—a flicker of something that was not quite alarm but was not far from it—and I saw Vyse's hand move with a deliberate, unhurried motion to the inside of his coat. What he drew out was not a weapon. It was a letter—a single sheet of paper, folded once, bearing a crest I recognised.

"I think," said Vyse, "that you will find this interesting."

He held it out. Sir Horace took it, unfolded it, and as he read, the colour drained from his face with a slow, sickly progress, as if the blood were being drawn from him by some invisible pump.
