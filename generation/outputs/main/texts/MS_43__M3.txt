My heart bled within me as I regarded his still, silken form. I was not a monster; I did not revel in the destruction of the beautiful and the innocent. Yet, in the name of science, I had steeled my soul, committing an act which, in my darker hours, threatened to drive me to the very brink of despair. The apparatus was my own design, the culmination of a thousand failures, a thousand sleepless nights spent in the noxious confines of my laboratory.

The vital fluid, if it existed at all, refused to answer my summons.

The silence was broken by a low, contemptuous laugh from the front row. Lord Valerius stepped forward, his voice dripping with a manufactured sorrow that masked his evident triumph. "My dear Doctor," he said, his tone carrying the weight of absolute authority, "we are all profoundly grateful for your exertions this evening. You have demonstrated, with remarkable clarity, the utter fallacy of your own hypotheses. The animal is no more alive now than before your intervention. You have proven that electricity, however powerful, is not the vital fluid you seek. Perhaps it is time to accept that life is a mystery that your mechanical apparatus can never unriddle."

A wave of humiliation washed over me, a cold, crushing tide that threatened to extinguish my reason. The audience began to stir, their murmurs of disappointment quickly curdling into open mockery. I stood upon the dais, a solitary, condemned figure, surrounded by the instruments of my failure. The eyes of the assembled philosophers were upon me, their gazes sharp as poniards, each one piercing the fragile shield of my self-respect. I felt as if the very floor beneath my feet was giving way, plunging me into an abyss of ignominy from which there could be no return.

Yet, even in that nadir of despair, a stubborn, unquenchable flame flickered within my breast. I would not surrender. I could not. The truth of my theories was not invalidated by a single failure. The vital fluid existed; I was certain of it, as certain as I was of my own existence. The fault lay not in the principle, but in the execution, in the imperfect nature of my apparatus, in the unpredictable variables that govern the material world. I would retire to my laboratory, I would redouble my efforts, and I would return, not to seek the approbation of these men, but to vindicate the truth that burned within me.

I raised my head, meeting the gaze of Lord Valerius with a steadiness that surprised even myself. "My lord," I replied, my voice barely audible, yet carrying a weight of emotion that silenced the murmuring crowd, "you mistake a temporary defeat for an absolute refutation. The mystery of life is not so easily dismissed. I have failed today, but the pursuit of truth is a path strewn with the ruins of failed experiments. I shall not abandon it. The vital fluid exists, and one day, I shall prove it, even if it costs me my life."

With that, I turned from the dais, my heart heavy with sorrow. The assembly parted before me, their faces averted, their whispers following me like a swarm of venomous insects. I walked through the cold, echoing corridors of the Institution, my footsteps ringing upon the marble flags. The night outside was bitter, the stars cold and distant.
