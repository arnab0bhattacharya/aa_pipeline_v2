The rain had not ceased for three days, and the drawing room at Thornebeck Hall smelled of damp wool and tallow. Eleanor Ashford sat by the fire, turning the page of a review she had already read twice, and listened to the housekeeper’s footsteps retreat across the flagged hall. Her father was in York on business that would keep him a fortnight. Her brother, Hugh, was in London, where he spent most of his months now, and wrote letters that mentioned tailors and racehorses and never once asked after her. She was twenty-three, possessed of nine thousand pounds a year and the manor itself in entail, and she had refused four suitors since Michaelmas without rising from her chair to do it.

She had refused Lord Pellew because he had told her that her eyes were like the Mediterranean, which she had never seen and neither had he. She had refused Mr. Barton because he had agreed with everything she said for the space of an entire evening, including a remark she had made deliberately wrong about the corn laws to try his temper. She had refused Sir Charles Wren because he had spoken to her father’s steward about the timber in the east copse before he had spoken to her. The fourth had wept, and she had found that she could not respect a man who wept before he was even refused.

The housekeeper returned and stood in the doorway with an expression that meant she disapproved of something but considered it beneath her dignity to say so. “A gentleman to see you, miss. A Mr. Marlow. He says he was master to Mr. Hugh.”

Eleanor set down the review. She remembered the name. Hugh had been fifteen and impossible, sent down from Harrow for some mischief involving the master’s horse, and her father had engaged a private tutor to prepare him for Cambridge—a young man, poor, recently down from Oxford, who had walked from Leeds to Thornebeck in November weather with all his books in a knapsack. Hugh had written to her at the time, in one of his few communicative moods, that Marlow had boxed his ears the first day and made him read Thucydides until he was sick of it, and that he was the only man alive who did not flinch when their father spoke.

“Show him in,” she said, and then, because the housekeeper still stood there with her disapproval showing like a bone beneath thin skin, “and tea, Mrs. Hett. He will be cold.”

She had expected to find him changed. She had not expected to find him so much changed. He came in and stood before the fire with his hat in his hands, and she saw that the knapsack had become a shabby greatcoat, and that the young man’s earnestness had been worn down to something quieter and more stubborn, the way a stone is worn by water. His hair was thinner. His boots had been mended, and mended again, and the cuffs of his coat were frayed. He was thirty now, or thirty-one. He looked at her without smiling, without bowing more than was necessary, and said, “Miss Ashworth. I beg your pardon for coming without warning. I keep the grammar school at Kipping now, and I had business in Halifax, and I found I could not pass this road without—” He stopped. “Without making myself foolish, I suppose. But I have made myself foolish before, and survived it.”

She had risen when he entered, and now she sat again and gestured to the chair opposite. He sat, and she saw that he did not look about the room, did not take stock of the silver or the paneling or the Turkey carpet, as every suitor had done, measuring what he might gain. He looked at her, and at the fire, and at his own hands, which were rough and ink-stained.

“Mr. Marlow,” she said. “I remember you. Hugh said you were the only tutor he did not drive away.”

“I was too poor to be driven away,” he said. “And too stubborn. Your brother was a trial, but he was not stupid, and I have found that stupidity is the only thing I cannot teach.”

She almost smiled. She could not remember the last time she had almost smiled at something a man had said to her in this room.

“You keep a school now,” she said.

“At Kipping. Forty boys, most of them the sons of weavers. The parish pays me forty pounds a year, and the parents send what they can, which is sometimes nothing and sometimes a sack of flour. I teach them Latin and Greek and enough arithmetic to keep them from being cheated, and I have written a grammar that no one will publish, because no one publishes anything for the sons of weavers.”

“You wrote to my brother about it, I think. He mentioned it.”

“He wrote back to say that he had shown it to a publisher he knew, and the publisher had said there was no market for it. He was kind about it. kinder than I deserved. I was not writing to ask for his help, only to tell him what I was doing. But he offered it, which is more than most men would do.”

The tea came, and Mrs. Hett set it out with the good china, which was an act of defiance, since Mrs. Hett believed that visitors who came without carriages ought to be given the everyday cups. Eleanor poured, and Marlow took his cup and drank without the little ceremony of complimenting the tea, which she found she was grateful for.

They talked. She did not know afterward how it had begun, but they talked about the school, and about the boys, and about what could be done for a child who was clever and hungry, and then about the corn laws, and he disagreed with her, not rudely but flatly, without softening it, without retreating, and she found herself arguing harder than she had argued with anyone in years. He said that she was wrong, and told her why, and did not pause to wonder whether he ought to have said it. She was aware, as she had been aware with no other man, that he was not performing. He did not raise his voice or lower it for effect. He did not watch her face to see whether she was pleased. He spoke as if the truth were a thing that existed whether she liked it or not, and as if she were a person who could bear to hear it.

The light failed. The rain thickened against the windows. She rang for candles, and when they came, the room seemed smaller, and she was conscious of the distance between their chairs, which was not very great, and of the fact that he had not moved closer to her, and had not moved farther away.

“Miss Ashworth,” he said, and set down his cup. “I must go. I did not come here to—to impose on your kindness. I came because I wanted to see you. I have wanted to see you for a very long time. I knew that it was wrong, and I came anyway, and now I have stayed too long, and I must go before I say something that I have no right to say.”

She looked at him. The fire had burned down, and the candlelight was not kind to his coat or his boots, but it was kind to his face, which had the look of a man who had been tired for a long time and had stopped minding it. She thought of the four suitors, and of the Mediterranean, and of the weeping, and of the timber in the east copse, and she thought of the nine thousand pounds and the manor and the entail, and of his forty pounds a year and his school at Kipping and his grammar that no one would publish.

“You may say it,” she said.

“I will not,” he said. “I know what I am, and I know what you are. I have forty pounds a year and a room above the schoolroom that smells of damp chalk. I will not say it, because if I said it, you might think that I was the kind of man who could say it, and I am not. I am not the kind of man who asks a woman to give up what you have, and I am not the kind of man who can pretend that what you have does not matter. It matters. It would matter between us even if the world did not know it, because we would both know it, and I would know it every day, and it would make me less than I am, and I have very little already, and I cannot afford to be less.”

He stood. She stood. They faced each other across the tea things and the good china and the space that was not very great.

“You are the only man,” she said, and her voice was not steady, and she did not trouble to make it so, “who has ever spoken to me as if I could bear the truth. You are the only man who has not tried to sell me a version of myself that I do not recognize. And now you tell me that you will not say the thing that I—” She stopped. She had not meant to say that. She had not known, until she said it, that it was true.

He looked at her, and she saw that he was afraid, and that he was angry at being afraid, and that the anger and the fear were wrestling with something else that was stronger than both.

“Miss Ashworth,” he said. “Eleanor. If I were a different man—”

“You are the man you are,” she said. “I have refused four men who were different, and I did not refuse them because they were not rich enough. I refused them because they were not honest enough. And now the one man who is honest enough tells me that his honesty will not let him speak.”

He put on his greatcoat. He took up his hat. He stood in the doorway, and the rain was loud behind him, and the hall was dark and cold and smelled of stone.

“I will write to you,” he said. “If you will let me. I will write to you about the school, and the boys, and the grammar, and I will not write anything that I have no right to write, and you will know, because you are not stupid, what I am not writing, and I will know that you know, and that will have to be enough, because it is all I have.”

She nodded. She could not speak.

He went out into the rain. She stood in the doorway and listened to his footsteps on the gravel until she could not hear them anymore, and then she went back to the fire and sat down and took up the review and read the same page for the third time, and did not see a single word of it.
