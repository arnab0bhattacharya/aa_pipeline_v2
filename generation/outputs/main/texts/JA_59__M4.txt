The affair at Hartwell Priors had, from the first, been of a nature to excite the liveliest apprehensions in the bosom of every member of the Ferrers family; and though it was generally agreed that nothing could be more improper, nothing more entirely to be deprecated, there was yet a considerable diversity of opinion as to the precise degree of blame to be attached to the young lady chiefly concerned, and a still greater uncertainty as to the most effectual means of putting an end to it.

Mr. Edward Ferrers, the younger son, had returned from Winchester at the age of seventeen, with a complexion much improved by air and exercise, a manner considerably emboldened by the society of boys older than himself, and a heart entirely at leisure. Within a fortnight of his arrival he had fallen in love; within three weeks he had declared himself; and by the end of the month it was understood, through the medium of his sister’s observation rather than any direct avowal, that Miss Ashby, the governess at Hartwell Priors, had received his addresses with a composure which did her credit, and a rejection which did her still more.

“I cannot conceive,” said Mrs. Ferrers, for the fourth time that morning, “what Lady Hartwell can be thinking of, to keep a young woman of such an appearance in the house. It is not as if the children were infants. The eldest Miss Hartwell is fifteen if she is a day. A governess of two-and-twenty, with that complexion, and those eyes, and no fortune whatsoever, is a very dangerous inmate in a family where there are sons.”

“But there are no sons at Hartwell Priors,” observed Miss Ferrers, without looking up from her work.

“There is Edward,” returned her mother, with dignity.

“Edward is not at Hartwell Priors. He is at home. That is the misfortune.”

“It is no misfortune, Maria, that your brother should be under his father’s roof. It is a great blessing, and one which I trust he knows how to value. But I confess I should be glad to know what Mr. Ferrers means to do. He has not spoken to Edward, I am persuaded. He never speaks when he ought.”

Miss Maria Ferrers, who had long since discovered that her mother’s opinions, when delivered with sufficient energy, required no answer, continued to ply her needle in silence. She was a young woman of three-and-twenty, with a clear understanding and a disposition to be amused by that which others found merely distressing; and the present situation, though it occasioned her some uneasiness on her brother’s account, afforded her, she was obliged to confess, a very considerable degree of entertainment.

She had known Miss Ashby for above a year, and had liked her from the beginning—liked her for her good sense, her unassuming manners, and a certain quiet archness of observation which Miss Ferrers recognised as the natural product of a mind obliged to make its own way in the world. That a boy of seventeen should fancy himself in love with her was no more than might have been predicted; that Miss Ashby should have the good sense to refuse him was equally to be expected; and that Edward should be miserable in consequence was, in Miss Ferrers’s opinion, a state of affairs so exactly suited to his years and his situation that she could not find it in her heart to wish it otherwise.

The difficulty was to know how to act. To laugh at him would be cruelty; to sympathise with him would be folly; and to ignore the whole affair, as her father seemed inclined to do, was to run the risk of its becoming serious.

Edward himself appeared at breakfast the next morning with a countenance of such settled woe that his sister could scarcely preserve her gravity.

“You are not eating, Edward,” she said. “The ham is excellent.”

“I have no appetite.”

“That is a bad sign at seventeen. At seven-and-twenty it would be a worse. You must take care, or you will grow thin, and then the ladies will be sorry for you, and that will never do.”

“I do not desire any lady’s pity,” said Edward, with a look of injured dignity, “except one.”

“And she, I collect, is not to be pitied into compliance. Miss Ashby has a great deal of sense, Edward. You must allow her that.”

“I allow her everything. She is an angel.”

“Angels are not in the habit of marrying schoolboys.”

“I am not a schoolboy. I am seventeen.”

“Exactly. And she is two-and-twenty. In five years you will be two-and-twenty, and she will be seven-and-twenty, and you will wonder what you ever saw in her.”

“I shall never wonder. I shall never marry anyone else. I shall go into the army and be killed.”

“That would certainly be one way of settling the matter,” said Miss Ferrers, “but a very uncomfortable one for the rest of us. Suppose you go into the army and are not killed? You would then be a lieutenant of five-and-twenty, with a wife of thirty, and no money to keep her on. That would be worse than the present state of affairs, I think.”

Edward looked at her with the air of one who suspects that he is being laughed at, but is not quite certain.

“You do not understand,” he said. “You cannot. You have never loved.”

“I have loved several people,” replied his sister, “but I have never been so obliging as to let them know it. It is a great saving of trouble.”

“You are mocking me.”

“I am advising you. There is a difference. But I see you are not in a humour to be advised, so I will only say this: if you must be in love with Miss Ashby, be in love with her like a gentleman. Do not write her any letters that she will be obliged to burn. Do not waylay her in the shrubbery. Do not make her situation at Hartwell Priors so unpleasant that she is forced to leave it. She has no fortune, and a governess’s place is not easy to come by. If you care for her at all, you will not do her a mischief.”

Edward was silent for a moment. Then he said, in a lower voice, “She will not even see me.”

“That is very proper of her.”

“She sent me a message by the housemaid—by the housemaid, Maria!—to say that she hoped I was well, and that she should be obliged to me if I would not come into the garden again without a proper invitation.”

“Excellent woman!” cried Miss Ferrers. “I like her better every day.”

“You are not in my situation. You cannot feel what I feel.”

“No, indeed. I have never sent a message by a housemaid in my life. It is an expedient I should never have thought of.”

Edward rose from the table with an expression of deep reproach, and quitted the room. His sister looked after him with a smile which was not altogether without tenderness, and then, putting on her bonnet, walked over to Hartwell Priors to pay a morning visit.

She found Miss Ashby in the schoolroom, engaged in hearing the youngest Miss Hartwell repeat her French verbs. The children were dismissed with a nod, and the two young women were left together.

“I am come,” said Miss Ferrers, “to thank you.”

“To thank me?” said Miss Ashby, colouring slightly. “For what?”

“For your behaviour to my brother. It was everything that was right and kind and sensible. I am afraid he has been very troublesome.”

“He has been very young,” said Miss Ashby, with a smile, “and I have been young myself. I know what it is to fancy oneself in love at seventeen. It is not a thing to be angry with.”

“You are very good. But I fear he will not be easily persuaded to give up.”

“He will be persuaded when he has something else to think of. Boys are like that. In a month he will have forgotten me, or he will fancy himself in love with someone else, which comes to the same thing.”

“And you? Will you forget him?”

Miss Ashby looked up with a little surprise. “I shall remember him with gratitude,” she said. “He was very much in earnest, and that is always flattering. But I am a governess, Miss Ferrers. I have no time for romance, and no fortune to make it agreeable. If I were to marry every boy who fancied himself in love with me, I should have been married six times over, and be a widow by now, with nothing to live on but my memories.”

Miss Ferrers laughed. “You are the most sensible woman I know.”

“I am the most practical. It is not quite the same thing. But I am glad you do not think me unkind.”

“I think you everything that is good. And I am ashamed to say that I am also a little amused.”

“So am I,” said Miss Ashby, with a twinkle. “But we must not let him see it. He would never forgive us.”

“No, indeed. He is very serious. He has threatened to go into the army and be killed.”

“That is a very proper ambition for a young man. I hope he will do neither.”

“I shall do my best to prevent it. But I cannot answer for my mother. She is convinced that you are a designing woman, and that the whole affair is a plot against her peace of mind.”

“I am sorry for it. I have no designs upon anyone’s peace of mind, least of all my own.”

“You are not happy here?”

“I am as happy as a governess can expect to be. The children are good, and Lady Hartwell is kind, and I have a room of my own. It is not a brilliant prospect, but it is a respectable one. I should be sorry to lose it.”

“You shall not lose it through Edward. I will answer for that. He will go back to school at Michaelmas, and by Christmas he will have forgotten all about it.”

“I hope so. And I hope he will be happy.”

“He will be. He is seventeen. Happiness is a habit with him.”

Miss Ferrers took her leave, and walked home through the autumn sunshine, thinking that she had never liked Miss Ashby so well, and never been so much inclined to laugh at her brother. It was a difficult thing, she reflected, to be loyal to one’s family and amused by them at the same time; but it was not impossible, and she was resolved to attempt it. She would not laugh at Edward to his face; she would not encourage him in his folly; but she would not pretend to be more distressed than she was. He would get over it, as boys do; and Miss Ashby would stay at Hartwell Priors, and be a governess, and marry nobody, perhaps, or perhaps a clergyman of forty with a good living. It was all very sad, and very ridiculous, and very much what one expected. She went into the house, and found her mother in the drawing-room, still talking of the impropriety of Lady Hartwell’s establishment.

“I have been to Hartwell Priors,” said Miss Ferrers.

“You have? And did you see her?”

“I saw Miss Ashby. She is very well, and very sensible, and entirely resolved to have nothing to do with Edward.”

“I am glad to hear it. But I do not like her situation. A young woman of that age, with no fortune, and such an air—it is not respectable.”
