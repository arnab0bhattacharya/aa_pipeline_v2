The afternoon light in the drawing-room of Lord Ashworth’s Mayfair townhouse fell in slanting bars of amber dust, illuminating the stillness of a life that had long since ceased to be lived and had merely been preserved, much like a butterfly in a jar. Lord Ashworth sat in a chair of dark mahogany, his legs crossed with the languid precision of a cat that has already decided it is too old to hunt, yet too proud to beg for food. He was a man who had spent forty years at the Foreign Office, a tenure which had stripped the meat from his bones and left only the polished, cynical architecture of a mind that had seen every treaty signed in blood and every alliance broken by a glance.

Before him stood Julian, his great-nephew, a young man of such startling and fragile beauty that he seemed less a human being and more an accidental arrangement of cream-colored silk and rose-petal cheeks. Julian was trembling, not with cold, but with the specific, electric anxiety of a debutante on the eve of a ball, or a saint on the eve of a martyrdom. He held a hat in his hands that did not fit, and his eyes were wide with a terror that Lord Ashworth found, in a distant, detached way, rather agreeable.

"You are worried," Lord Ashworth said, his voice like dry leaves rustling over a grave. "You are worried because you believe that to succeed in society, one must be sincere. This is the first error. Sincerity is the refuge of those who have nothing to hide and nothing to gain. It is the virtue of the mediocre. To be truly successful, Julian, you must be absolutely insincere. You must say the opposite of what you mean, and mean the opposite of what you say. It is a game of mirrors, my boy. If you smile, you must be thinking of murder. If you weep, you must be calculating the price of the tears."

Julian stared, his lips parting slightly. "But Uncle, if I say the opposite, they will know I am lying. Lying is... improper."

"Lying is the only proper thing in an improper world," Lord Ashworth replied, taking a sip of brandy that he did not taste. "Truth is a blunt instrument. It breaks things. It bruises. One should never use a blunt instrument when a scalpel will do. But the scalpel must be hidden in a sheath of velvet. When you meet a woman, do not tell her you find her attractive. Tell her you find her tiresome. She will hate you. And then, when you leave, she will think of you all night. Hate is a form of memory. Indifference is the only true death. So, be hated. Be fascinatingly, intensely hated. That is the highest form of popularity."

Julian looked as if he were trying to swallow a stone. "I cannot... I do not know how to be hated, Uncle. I am too nice. People like me. It makes me uncomfortable."

"Uncomfortable? Good. Comfort is the anesthetic of the soul. It numbs you to the pain of existence. You must seek discomfort. You must be the thorn in the side of the comfortable class. When a man offers you a compliment, reject it with such vigorous, theatrical disgust that he will question his own perception of reality. When a woman offers you a sympathy, offer her a logic so cold and precise that she will feel she has been dissected. Do not let them feel anything. Make them feel *something*. Make them feel the chill of your indifference. That is power."

Lord Ashworth leaned forward, his eyes narrowing into slits of pale, intelligent fire. "Remember, Julian, the secret of life is to have no morals. My morals are to have a moral view of everything. But for a young man, it is better to have no view at all. Have a view, and you become a painter. Have no view, and you become a prince. Do not let anyone see you think. Thinking is dangerous. It reveals the mechanism. Be a mask. A beautiful, empty, perfect mask. And never, under any circumstances, explain yourself. Explanation is the confession of a criminal. The innocent need not explain; they merely are. The guilty explain, and in explaining, they condemn themselves."

Julian stood there, pale as a lily that has been left in the rain, his mind whirling with the impossible, terrifying logic of his uncle’s counsel. He bowed, stiffly, and retreated into the hallway, the sound of his footsteps echoing like the ticking of a clock that had lost its chime.

Lord Ashworth watched him go, a faint, sad smile playing on his thin lips. "He will ruin himself," he murmured to the empty room. "They always do. They take the joke too seriously, or not seriously enough. It is the tragedy of the young: they think we are teaching them a game, when we are only describing a prison."

Three weeks later, the letter arrived at the townhouse. It was from Julian, written on paper so thick it could have stopped a bullet, the ink a deep, bruised purple.

*Dear Uncle,*

*I have followed your instructions to the letter. I attended the dinner at Lady Pemberton’s. When she asked me what I thought of her new conservatory, I told her it was a monument to bad taste and poor structural engineering. She turned the color of a ripe tomato. I then complimented the hostess on the coldness of the soup, stating that it was the only element of the evening that seemed to have any consistency. The hostess, a woman of formidable reputation, dropped her fork and left the room in a state of what I can only describe as dignified fury. I followed her to the drawing-room and told her that her anger was the most honest emotion I had seen in years, and that I was envious of its purity. She wept. I told her that tears were merely the body’s way of diluting its own sorrow, and that she should stop, as it was wasting a resource. She stopped. She looked at me as if I were a ghost, or a demon, or perhaps a very clever monkey.*

*But then, the impossible happened. The next day, Lady Pemberton sent for me. She said that I was the only man in London who had the courage to tell her the truth. She said that I was 'a diamond in the rough' and that she wished me to be her permanent companion in all social functions. I am now the darling of the ton. They call me 'The Mirror.' They say that to be seen with me is to be seen clearly. I have never been more popular. I have never been more miserable. I say the opposite of what I mean, and they believe it. I mean the opposite of what I say, and they admire it. I am a mask, Uncle, and the face behind the mask is screaming, but no one can hear it because I am too busy explaining why the silence is so profound.*

*Please, tell me what to do next. I am successful. I am hated, admired, and desired. I am everything you said I could be. And I am dying.*

Lord Ashworth read the letter twice. He set it down on the mahogany table, the sound of the paper against the wood a sharp, final crack. He looked out the window at the London fog, which was beginning to roll in over the rooftops, swallowing the gas lamps one by one, turning the world into a soft, gray blur. He picked up his brandy glass, which was still warm from his earlier sip, and raised it to his lips.

"He is right," Lord Ashworth said softly, to the fog, to the dust, to the empty air. "To be successful is to be a ghost. To be loved is to be a mistake. I told him to be a mask, and he became one. I told him to be empty, and he was. It is a perfect tragedy. It is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen."

He drank the brandy, and for the first time in years, he tasted it. It was bitter, and sweet, and utterly alone.
