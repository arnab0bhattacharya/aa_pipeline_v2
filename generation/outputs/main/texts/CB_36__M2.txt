The wind off the moor did not howl that night; it screamed, a thin, high-pitched keen that rattled the panes of my window and seemed to tear at the very fabric of the sky. I stood with my face pressed against the cold glass, watching the sleet lash against the iron railings of the garden below. It was a ugly night, fit for burial or despair, and I felt my heart beating a frantic, arrhythmic pulse against my ribs, as if it sought to escape the prison of my chest.

In the hall below, the sound of boots on stone was distinct and heavy. I knew the weight of them. They belonged to Mr. Harcourt. He was coming to see me, to confirm what was already decided, to polish the final details of a bargain that had been struck in the cold, hard light of his ledger, not in the warm, glowing light of love.

My father sat at the dinner table, his head bowed, his hands clasped over a plate of untouched mutton. He looked small, shrunk into his chair by the magnitude of his debt and the enormity of his sacrifice. He had sold me. I used the word in my mind, sharp and jagged, refusing to soften it with euphemism. He had traded his daughter for the interest on a loan that was eating his estate alive, for the quiet dignity of a roof that would not leak, for the safety of a name that would not be dragged through the mud of insolvency.

"Elara," he said, his voice thick, not looking up. "He is coming."

"I know," I replied. My voice was steady, though my hands, hidden in the folds of my shawl, trembled violently. "I hear him."

"He is a good man, Elara. He has been generous beyond reason. He could have foreclosed. He chose instead to offer us a way out."

"Generosity," I repeated, tasting the word. It was a cold stone, smooth and hard, offering no warmth. "Is that all he is? Generous? A purse with a face? A bank of coin that dispenses alms to the deserving poor?"

My father’s head snapped up. His eyes were wet, but his gaze was hard, filled with the old, bitter pride that I knew too well. "You are ungrateful. You are blind to the suffering you would inflict upon us if you refused. You would see your mother in the workhouse. You would see your sisters cast out into the world with nothing but their wits and their shame. Is that the duty you owe your family? To feed your own vanity at the cost of their survival?"

"Duty!" The word burst from me, fierce and uncontrolled. I turned from the window, the cold glass no longer a barrier but a mirror reflecting my own wild eyes. "You call this duty? To bind me to a man I do not know, a man who speaks of his investments as others speak of their souls? I am not a cow to be shod and sold for the best price! I have a mind. I have a heart. I have a will."

"Your will is a luxury you cannot afford," he said quietly. "And your heart is a fool. It will break if it is not anchored to something solid, something that will not change with the seasons or the moods of the wind. Mr. Harcourt is solid. He is stone. He is earth. He will not leave you. He will not die of a fever in a foreign land. He will give you a home, and he will give you security. Is that not enough? Is the soul not satisfied by bread and shelter?"

I walked to the door. The latch was cold under my fingers. I looked back at him, this man who was my father, my protector, my jailer. "Bread and shelter," I said, my voice dropping to a whisper, yet loud enough to fill the room. "You speak as if I were an animal. As if I have no need for air, no need for light, no need for a companion who looks into my eyes and sees me, not my dowry. I would rather starve in a garret than live in a palace where I am invisible."

He flinched, as if struck. "You do not know what you say. You do not know the weight of the world. Once the contract is signed, there is no undoing. And I will not have you cast off as a broken thing, ashamed and alone."

"Then let me be broken," I said. "Better broken than bent. Better to fall into the abyss than to be nailed to a cross I have not chosen."

The handle turned. The door opened.

Mr. Harcourt stood there. He was a large man, broad in the shoulders, with a face that was handsome in a broad, featureless way, like a plain field. He wore a coat of deep blue, and his hat was tucked under one arm. He looked at me, then at my father, and then back at me. There was no malice in his eyes, only a polite, distant curiosity, as one might look at a piece of furniture one had agreed to purchase.

"Miss Elara," he said. His voice was deep, resonant, and utterly devoid of passion. "Your father speaks highly of you. I trust the arrangements meet with your approval?"

I looked at him. I searched his face for a spark, a flicker, a sign of the human soul beneath the merchant’s mask. I found nothing. There was only the calm, flat surface of a lake, beneath which lay the dark, silent depths of a life devoid of surprise, of danger, of love.

"Mr. Harcourt," I said. I straightened my spine. I felt a cold clarity descend over me, like the frost that was settling on the moor outside. "I accept."

He blinked, seemingly surprised by the speed of my answer, or perhaps by the lack of protest. "You are... decisive."

"I am practical," I said. "And I am tired of fighting the wind. It is cold tonight. I would rather go inside."

He stepped aside, gesturing with a hand that was large and heavy, the fingers thick with rings of gold and onyx. I walked past him into the warmth of the hallway. The smell of his cologne was strong, cloying, masking the scent of the storm outside. It smelled of money. It smelled of safety. It smelled of a tomb.

As I passed the mirror on the wall, I saw my own reflection. It was pale, drawn, but there was a fire in my eyes that did not belong to the woman who had just agreed to be a wife in name only. I looked at the man following me, his steps heavy and measured, and I knew then that I had made the right choice, and the wrong one, simultaneously. I had saved my family. I had condemned myself.

I walked up the stairs, my footsteps echoing in the empty house. The wind continued to scream outside, tearing at the eaves, but I did not hear it. I was listening to the silence within myself, a silence that was vast and terrible, and in that silence, I began to build the walls of my prison, brick by brick, with the mortar of my own resignation. I was twenty years old. I was a wife. I was free. I was a slave. And the night stretched out before me, long and dark, and I had nowhere to go but forward, into the dim, yellow light of a life that was not my own.
