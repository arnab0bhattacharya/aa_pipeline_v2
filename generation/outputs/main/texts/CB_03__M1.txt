I was summoned to the salon at a quarter past eight, the hour when Madame Vandenbrande usually took her chocolate and reviewed the day’s follies. The summons was brought by Sophie, the parlour-maid, who would not meet my eye, but merely jerked her thumb towards the corridor and vanished into the gloom of the back stairs. A cold dread, heavy and familiar as the stone flags of the courtyard, settled upon my heart. I smoothed my black bombazine dress and followed the command.

The salon was a vast, chill chamber, overlooking the canal. Outside, the Flemish sky was the colour of slate, weeping a ceaseless, drizzling rain that blurred the gables of the city into grey ghosts. Inside, the air was stifling with the scent of beeswax and the suffocating presence of Madame Vandenbrande. She sat in her high-backed armchair, a monolith of black silk and rigid morality. Beside her stood Mademoiselle Isabelle de Vos, a pupil of sixteen, whose beauty was as cold and hard as a diamond, and whose character was equally sharp.

"Miss Vance," Madame began, her English precise and cutting, though her tongue stumbled slightly over the vowels. "You are accused of a breach of trust so base, so low, that I scarce know how to look upon you."

My blood ran cold, but I forced my voice to remain steady. "Of what am I accused, Madame?"

Madame Vandenbrande did not answer immediately. She turned to the girl. "Speak, Isabelle. Do not be afraid. The truth is a shield."

Isabelle stepped forward, her eyes flashing with a malice I had long suspected but never seen so nakedly displayed. "She read my letters, Madame. My private letters from my mother in Brussels. I found the seal broken, the paper crumpled. She was reading them in the classroom yesterday when I entered. She hid them, but I saw."

A silence fell, broken only by the ticking of the great clock and the hiss of the rain. I looked from the accusing face of the girl to the stony visage of the headmistress. I saw no hope of sympathy there; I saw only the rigid wall of continental prejudice against the English governess, the friendless stranger.

"It is a lie," I said. The words were plain, but they felt as heavy as lead. "I have never touched your correspondence. I know the sanctity of a letter; it is a sacrament between souls. To violate it would be to violate my own conscience."

"Conscience?" Madame sneered. "You English have strange consciences. Isabelle is a lady. She does not invent such things. Why should she?"

"Because she hates me," I cried, my restraint beginning to fray. "Because I corrected her vanity yesterday, and she vowed to be revenged. Is this not the truth, Isabelle? Look at me and say it is not!"

Isabelle dropped her eyes, a faint flush rising to her cheeks, but she did not recant. "She is violent, Madame. See how she looks? Like a wild thing."

"I am passionate," I admitted, my hands clenched into fists to stop their trembling, "for I am innocent. You believe her word against mine because she is your countrywoman and I am an alien. You believe her because she is rich and I am poor. But before God, Madame, I am clean of this sin."

Madame Vandenbrande rose. She was a tall woman, and she looked down upon me with a disdain that was meant to crush. "Your protestations are useless, Miss Vance. The evidence is the broken seal. Isabelle saw you. I have no choice. You are dismissed. You will leave this house within the hour."

"To be turned out?" I gasped. "Into the street? In this city where I have no friend, no protector, and cannot even speak the tongue of the people to beg for bread?"

"That is your misfortune, not my fault," she replied coldly. "You should have thought of the consequences before you pried into the secrets of my pupils."

Injustice, that fiery stimulant, surged through my veins. The meekness required of a governess fell away from me like a shed garment. I stepped closer to her, close enough to see the pulse beating in her temple.

"You speak of consequences," I said, my voice dropping to a low, intense register. "But consider the consequences of a lie. You cast me out to starve on the word of a girl who is known for her cruelty. If there is a God in heaven—and I believe there is, though He seems silent in this room—He will not hold you guiltless. You are not judging a servant; you are judging a soul. Remember that when you sleep tonight, Madame. Remember the Englishwoman you cast into the dark because you were too proud to investigate, too prejudiced to hear the truth."

I saw a flicker of doubt in her eyes, a shadow of the superstition that haunts the Catholic mind, but it was gone in an instant, replaced by a harder resolve.

"Go," she said, pointing to the door. "Pack your trunk."

I did not weep. I would not give Isabelle the satisfaction of seeing my tears. I turned on my heel and walked out of the room, my head held high. As I passed Isabelle, I stopped and looked her full in the face.

"You have won a victory today," I told her. "But you have bought it with a lie. May it bring you peace."

I ascended the stairs to my narrow attic room. The wind howled round the gables, rattling the casement with a sound like the rattling of chains. I looked around the meagre room that had been my only shelter. It was cold, bare, and ugly, yet leaving it felt like a kind of death.

I began to gather my few books and my writing box. My hands shook, not with fear, but with a suppressed, burning energy. I was alone in a foreign land, penniless and dishonoured. The world was a wide, tempestuous ocean, and I was a wreck upon it. Yet, as I folded my shawl, a strange defiance rose in me. I was not a thief. I was not a liar. My conscience was clear. And so long as a human soul possesses the truth, it is not entirely destitute.

I took my pen and a scrap of paper. I wrote a single line to the British Consul: *Miss Eleanor Vance, wrongfully dismissed, seeks aid.*

Then, shouldering my small bag, I descended the stairs. I walked past the salon door, which was shut fast. I walked through the empty hall and opened the heavy front door. The grey rain blew in my face, cold and biting, but I stepped out into the storm. I did not look back. I walked toward the city, toward the unknown, armed only with my integrity and the fierce, unquenchable fire of my own spirit.
