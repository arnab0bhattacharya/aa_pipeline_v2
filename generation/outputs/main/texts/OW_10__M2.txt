Lord Arthur Pendennis had always possessed the rare gift of being perfectly insignificant, which was, he knew, the first essential of a great artist. For ten years he had composed little waltzes that smelled faintly of lavender and slightly of regret, pieces that were so charmingly trivial that they could be played at a dinner party without offending the guests, yet so vaguely melancholy that they might be mistaken for profound. He was the darling of the drawing-rooms, the darling of the parlours, and the secret darling of the women who wept over his melodies while pretending to be bored.

Then, on a Tuesday morning that was grey with the sort of rain that seems designed specifically to dampen the spirits of the optimistic, Lord Arthur announced to his publisher, Mr. Greville, that he was going to write serious music.

Mr. Greville, a man whose soul was as thin as the paper on which the sheet music was printed, dropped his teacup. It shattered on the parquet floor, a sound that was far more dramatic than anything Lord Arthur had ever composed.

"You cannot do this, Arthur," Mr. Greville said, his voice trembling with a panic that was almost musical in its dissonance. "The public does not want to be serious. They want to be amused, flattered, and confused in equal measure. If you write a symphony, they will think you are ill. If you write a sonata, they will think you are mad. But if you write a waltz that makes a lady feel she has lost a love she never had, they will buy it."

"But I am tired, Greville," Lord Arthur replied, smoothing the cuffs of his velvet smoking jacket. "I am tired of being a decorative object. I wish to be a monument."

"Monuments are cold, Arthur. Decorative objects are warm. And warmth sells better."

Lord Arthur left his publisher’s office in Soho with a lightness of heart that was deceptive; it was the lightness of a man who has just committed a social suicide and is too polite to realize it. He walked home to his rooms in Chelsea, past the grey river and the grey sky, thinking that perhaps Mr. Greville was right, but then thinking that he was wrong, and then thinking that it did not matter, because truth is always stranger than fiction, and fiction is always more profitable than truth.

When he arrived home, the door was already open. Standing in the hall, looking at the portrait of his grandmother with the detached curiosity of a man examining a new piece of furniture, was Julian Vane.

Julian Vane was the only critic in London who had never written a favorable word of Lord Arthur’s work. He had called his *Suite for Strings* "a sonic representation of a faintly sweet disease," and his *Nocturne for the Grieving Widow* "an insult to the concept of grief, which requires at least the dignity of silence." Lord Arthur had invited him to dinner once, out of a sense of duty to the intellectual elite, and Julian had eaten the pheasant, praised the wine, and then spent the rest of the evening explaining why Lord Arthur’s music was the direct cause of the decline in moral standards in the city of London.

"You are early," Lord Arthur said, looking at him with a mixture of surprise and disdain. "I did not send for you."

"I did not come for the music," Julian said, removing his hat. He was a tall man, sharp-featured, with eyes the color of old ice. "I came because I heard the news. I came to offer my condolences."

Lord Arthur laughed. It was a dry, brittle sound, like a twig snapping under a boot. "Condolences? For what? My death?"

"Your death is unlikely. You are too good at surviving on the adoration of others. But your artistic death is certain. You have shot yourself in the foot, Arthur, with a very elegant shoe."

"I have shot myself in the heart," Lord Arthur corrected. "That is the only place from which true art can come."

Julian smiled. It was a cold smile, devoid of warmth, but it had a certain precision. "You are being dramatic. You are always dramatic. That is your only talent. You are a professional tragedian in a comic world. Now, let us sit down. I brought a bottle of claret. It is a 1889 vintage, which means it is exactly as old as your last waltz, and therefore, I trust, it has mellowed."

They sat in the library, a room lined with books that Lord Arthur never read and vases that Julian Vane never noticed. The rain tapped against the window pane, a rhythmic, impatient sound.

"Why have you come?" Lord Arthur asked, pouring the wine. The liquid was the deep, dark red of a bruise. "If you are here to mock me, you are a little late. The public has already done that. I heard them laughing in the street. They are laughing because they think I am joking. They think I am writing a new waltz, but a waltz with a deeper sorrow. They do not believe I can change."

"No one believes in change," Julian said, taking the glass. "Change is too expensive. It requires effort. People prefer the familiar, even if it is tedious. They prefer the known ugliness to the unknown beauty."

"But beauty is the only thing that matters," Lord Arthur said.

"Beauty is a lie," Julian countered. "It is the lie we tell ourselves to make the world bearable. Your waltzes were beautiful lies. They soothed the soul by numbing it. But now you want to tell the truth? The truth is ugly, Arthur. The truth is a naked man in a dirty room. It has no melody. It has no rhythm. It is just noise."

Lord Arthur looked at him. In the dim light of the gas lamp, Julian’s face looked like a mask of marble. "Then why are you here?"

"Because I am bored," Julian said simply. "I have critiqued you for ten years. I have written three thousand words on your work. I have analyzed every note, every rest, every silence. I know your music better than you do. And I am bored. I want to see if you can break the spell. I want to see if you can be serious. I want to see if you can fail."

"I will not fail," Lord Arthur said. "I will be great."

"Greatness is a dangerous thing," Julian said. "It is heavy. It crushes the spirit. Most men are too light to carry it. They float. You have floated for ten years, Arthur. You have drifted on the surface of things. But now you want to dive. The water is cold. The water is deep. And there are things at the bottom that do not like to be disturbed."

Lord Arthur drank his wine. It was excellent, smooth and rich, but it tasted of ash. "What do you think I will write?"

"I think you will write nothing," Julian said. "Or, if you do write, it will be terrible. Because you are not a serious artist. You are a decorative artist. You are a jewel box. You are made to hold small, shiny things. You are not made to hold the sun. You will try to hold the sun, and you will break."

Lord Arthur stood up. He walked to the window and looked out at the rain. The street below was empty, save for a single cab that splashed through a puddle, the water flying up in a spray that caught the light from the streetlamp.

"You are cruel," Lord Arthur said.

"I am honest," Julian replied. "And honesty is the only virtue that is not expensive."

Lord Arthur turned back to him. He looked at Julian with a sudden, sharp clarity. He saw the boredom in his eyes, the desire for spectacle, the need to be the first to witness the fall. And he realized that Julian was not there to help him. He was there to watch. He was there to see if the bird would fly or fall.

"Sit down, Julian," Lord Arthur said. "I will play you a piece."

Julian raised an eyebrow. "A waltz?"

"No," Lord Arthur said. "A serious piece. But I have not written it yet. I will improvise. It will be bad. It will be ugly. It will be the truth."

He sat down at the piano. The keys were ivory and ebony, smooth and cold. He placed his hands on them. He did not look at Julian. He looked at his own hands.

He began to play.

It was not a waltz. It was not a melody. It was a series of dissonant chords, clashing and jarring, like stones thrown against glass. There was no harmony, no resolution. There was only the sound of the keys being struck, hard and fast, with a violence that seemed personal.

Julian watched. He did not move. He did not blink. His face was still, but his eyes were wide.

Lord Arthur played on. He played until the notes bled. He played until the music became a scream. He played until the silence that followed was louder than the sound.

Then he stopped.

The room was quiet. The rain had stopped. The air was still.

Julian Vane stood up. He buttoned his coat. He looked at Lord Arthur with a new expression. It was not admiration. It was not pity. It was fear.

"It is terrible," Julian said.

Lord Arthur smiled. It was the first genuine smile he had worn in ten years. "Yes," he said. "It is terrible. But it is mine."

Julian put on his hat. "I will not write a review," he said. "I will not speak of this. But I will remember it."

"Goodbye, Julian," Lord Arthur said.

"Goodbye, Arthur."

Julian left. The door closed behind him. Lord Arthur sat alone in the library, listening to the silence. He reached out and touched the keys. They were cold. He closed the lid of the piano.

He knew, with a sudden, terrible certainty, that he would never write another waltz. He knew that he had broken the spell. He knew that he was free. And he knew that he was alone.

He picked up his pen. He opened a fresh sheet of paper. He began to write.

It was the beginning of the end. And it was beautiful.
