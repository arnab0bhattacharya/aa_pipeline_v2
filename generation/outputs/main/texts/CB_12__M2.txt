The rain lashed against the leaded windows of the parsonage, a relentless, hissing percussion that seemed to seek entry into the very soul of the house. It was a November evening in the Pennines, where the mist clung to the moorlike hills like a shroud, and the wind moaned through the chimneys with a voice half-human, half-beastly. Inside the study, the fire crackled and spat, casting long, trembling shadows across the walls. I sat in my usual chair, the one with the worn velvet, my hands folded over the shawl that always covered my lap, though the room was stiflingly hot. Across from me, in the armchair by the grate, sat Mr. Harker, a man of letters and reputation, whose presence in our humble abode felt like a sudden, violent intrusion of the outer world.

Beside him, almost invisible in the corner, stood Elara. She was thin, her face pale as the linen of her nightgown, her eyes large and dark with a fever that had kept her in this house for three years. She held a small, bound manuscript in her hands, her fingers trembling slightly. She had written poems. I knew this only because I had heard her murmuring verses at night, when I could not sleep, and the sound of her voice, low and strange, had haunted me. I had never seen the pages. I had assumed them to be the delirious scribblings of a mind fraying at the edges, a harmless distraction for a girl who could no longer walk, let alone work.

Mr. Harker had been speaking of the state of contemporary verse, his voice loud and authoritative, slicing through the damp air. He spoke of "dullness" and "lack of spirit," words that stung me even though they were not directed at me. I had given up my spirit long ago, buried it beneath the linens of the sick, the ledgers of the household, and the silent endurance of my duty. I was the anchor, the stone that held the house together while the waters rose.

Then, Mr. Harker noticed the book in Elara’s hand.

"What is that?" he asked, leaning forward.

Elara looked at me. I could see the question in her eyes: *May I? May I show him?* I felt a sudden, violent spike of something cold and hard in my chest. Jealousy? No, the word was too small, too petty. It was a terror. A fear that if I let this thing out, if I let her shine, the structure of our lives would shift, and I, who had sacrificed everything, would be revealed as having sacrificed for nothing.

"Show him," I said, and my voice was steady, though my heart hammered against my ribs like a trapped bird. "It is only verses, Mr. Harker. Childish things."

But Elara was already turning the pages, her movements slow and deliberate. She read. The sound of her voice changed. It was no longer the whisper of a patient, but the clear, sharp tone of a musician striking a chord. She read of wind on the moor, of stones that remembered the age before man, of a soul locked in a cage of bone, screaming for the sky.

I listened, and I hated her. I hated the ease of it, the way the words flowed from her without the labor of my daily grind. I had spent my youth studying, planning, dreaming of a life beyond the parsonage, only to be called back by the collapse of our father’s health and the frailty of my sister. I had become the caretaker, the manager, the silent sufferer. And now, this girl, this invalid who consumed our resources, who took up space in my home, possessed a gift that I had only ever imagined.

Mr. Harker was silent. The fire popped. The rain intensified.

When she finished, the room was still. Mr. Harker looked at her, and I saw a light in his eyes that I recognized. It was the same light I had seen in the eyes of the young men who had once admired my intelligence, before I had let them see the dust of the hearth and the weariness in my own.

"Extraordinary," he said softly. "You have a voice, Miss Elara. A true voice. It is not pretty, but it is true. It has the ring of iron."

"Thank you," Elara said, her face flushing with a color I had never seen before. It was a dangerous color, a color of life.

I looked down at my hands. They were rough, stained with the ink of the accounts, the soot of the fire. They were the hands of a servant. I had given up my own ambitions to care for the family, to keep the roof over our heads, to ensure that Elara had her medicine and her quiet. I had told myself it was holy work, a form of love. But in that moment, sitting in the warmth of the fire while my sister received the praise of a man of the world, I felt a bitter, acidic taste in my mouth. It was the taste of betrayal, not by her, but by the fate that had chosen her to be the artist and me to be the nurse.

"Mr. Harker," I said, and my voice was sharper than I intended. "It is a pity, is it not? That such a talent should be confined to a sickroom. It is wasted on the walls of a country parsonage."

Mr. Harker looked at me, surprised. Elara flinched.

"It is not wasted," Elara said quietly. "It is what I have. It is all I have."

The words hit me like a physical blow. *All I have.* I looked at her, really looked at her, and saw not a rival, but a mirror. We were both trapped. She by her body, I by my duty. She had found a way to break the glass of her cage; I had simply learned to live within the bars.

I stood up. My legs were stiff from sitting. I walked to the window and looked out at the dark, churning moors. The rain was a curtain between me and the world, separating me from everything I had ever wanted.

"Mr. Harker," I said, my back to them, "will you stay for tea? The rain is likely to continue until morning."

"I would like that, if it is not an imposition," he said.

"It is no imposition," I said. "We have a great deal of time. And I have a great deal to learn."

I turned back to them. Elara was watching me with an expression of pity that I found unbearable. I walked to the tea table, my movements precise and controlled. I would not let her see me break. I would not let her see the fury that burned in my belly, the rage that I felt toward a God who had dealt out such uneven cards. I would make the tea. I would pour the cups. I would be the host.

But as I placed the sugar bowl on the table, I felt a strange, cold calm settle over me. It was the calm of the deep sea, where the pressure is immense and the light does not reach. I had my life, my duty, my suffering. And she had her verses. Let her have them. I would have the silence. And in that silence, I would find a strength that was not of this world, a strength that would hold me together when the rest of me crumbled.

"Here is the tea," I said. "It is weak, but it is warm."

Elara took the cup. Her hand brushed mine. I did not pull away. I could not. We were sisters, bound by blood and by the heavy, unspoken weight of our shared fate. The fire crackled. The rain fell. And in the quiet of the parsonage, we sat, two women in the dark, each holding a different kind of fire, burning, but never quite consuming.
