In the town of Grimshaw, where the very air was a grey mixture of coal-dust and damp wool, and the sky seemed to be a low-hanging lid of soot that pressed upon the chimneys of a thousand houses, there lived a woman named Martha Entwistle. She was the wife of a spinner, a man whose own life was spun out in long, wearying threads at the looms of the great Whitworth Cotton Mill. Martha was not old, but she had the look of a woman for whom the calendar had been folded and creased until the years were indistinguishable from one another. Her face, once as fresh and fair as the unbleached calico that rolled from the mill’s machines, was now a landscape of fine lines, mapped by care and fatigue; her hands, which had once been smooth and clever at the needle, were reddened and chapped by the ceaseless round of washing, scrubbing, and mending, and by the raw Lancashire air that bit at them like a stray dog.

Her husband, Caleb, was a man built of the same stern stuff as the mill itself. He was tall and lean, with a stoop in his shoulders that was a monument to his years bent over the machinery, and a jaw set as firmly as a bolt on a loom. He was proud of his independence, that glorious, hollow boast of the poor man who would rather starve on his own terms than sup at another’s table. His pride was his coat of arms, and he wore it against the world, a thin and threadbare garment that offered no real protection from the biting winds of poverty.

For poverty, that unwelcome and persistent guest, had long since made its home with the Entwistles. The children—three small, pale-faced creatures with eyes too large for their gaunt little faces—knew its presence intimately. They knew it in the thinness of the gruel that was their supper; they knew it in the hollowness of their mother’s cheeks as she watched them eat; they knew it in the silence that fell upon their father when he returned from the mill, his hands shaking not from the cold, but from the gnawing emptiness within him. The eldest, a boy of nine named Tom, had taken to standing outside the baker’s shop, his small nose pressed to the cold glass, conducting a silent and sorrowful symphony of longing with the golden-brown loaves that sat like sleeping lions on the shelves.

It was into this grey world of want and weary pride that a light, as unexpected as a sunbeam piercing the mill-smoke, was suddenly cast. The Whitworth Mill was owned by Mr. Josiah Whitworth, a man of iron and industry. But he had a brother, a Mr. Elias Whitworth, who was a creature of another sort altogether. Elias was a scholar and a gentleman, now widowed, who had come to live in a quiet, handsome house on the edge of the town, a place of books and gardens that seemed a world away from the clatter and clamour of the mill. He was a man of a gentle and melancholy disposition, his kindness a soft, deep well in a land of shallow, bitter streams.

Mr. Elias Whitworth, observing the quiet dignity and worn patience of Martha Entwistle as she went about her errands in the town, and hearing whispers of the family’s great need, sent for her one afternoon. His house was an island of peace; the air smelled of beeswax and dried lavender, not of oil and damp wool. He offered her, with a soft and stumbling courtesy, the post of housekeeper. He spoke of a fair wage, of comfortable rooms, and of a home for her children, where they might have their fill of good food and see the sun on green grass.

Martha’s heart, a tired bird long caged in the ribs of her chest, gave a wild and hopeful flutter. To see her children fed, to lay her own weariness down in a clean, quiet room—it was a vision of paradise. She returned home that evening, her step lighter than it had been in years, the news a warm coal in her pocket.

She found Caleb at the table, staring into the black mouth of the empty grate. She told him everything, her voice trembling with a joy she could not suppress. She spoke of Mr. Elias’s kindness, of the wage, of the children. And as she spoke, she watched the light in his eyes change, and then go out, like a lamp extinguished by a cold draught.

“Housekeeper?” he said, and the word was a stone he dropped onto the table between them. “You, Martha? A servant in a gentleman’s house?”

“He is kind, Caleb,” she pleaded, her voice a thin thread. “And it is for the children. Think of Tom’s face.”

“I think of it every hour of every day!” he burst out, his voice rising to a hoarse cry. “And I think of what it means to be a man! To hold up my own head. Not to have my wife go cap-in-hand to my employer’s brother for a crust of bread! To have the town say that Caleb Entwistle cannot provide for his own. It is charity, Martha. It is a beggar’s bowl, dressed up in a housekeeper’s apron!”

“It is work, Caleb! Honest work! And he is a good man!”

“He is a Whitworth,” Caleb said, and the name was a brand. “And I am an Entwistle. We may be poor, but we are not beholden. My children shall not eat the bread of pity. You will not take the place.”

His voice was final, a door slammed shut and bolted from the inside. He would hear no more. The hope that had flown into Martha’s heart was now a dead thing in her hands. She looked from her husband’s proud, unyielding face to the small, silent faces of her children, who had crept to the doorway and were watching, their eyes wide with a fear they understood better than the words. That night, the silence in the small house was a heavier blanket than ever before, and the gruel in the pot seemed thinner still. The light had been offered, and her husband, in his terrible, stubborn pride, had put it out. And in the darkness, the gnawing in their bellies was a thing of sound and fury, a hungry ghost that would not be appeased by a man’s honour.
