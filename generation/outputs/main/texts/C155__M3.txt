The rain had not ceased for three days. It beat against the parsonage windows with a flat, relentless determination that matched the mood within. Edmund Ashby stood at the casement of his small study, watching the water channel through the orchard below, carving little rivers between the roots of the apple trees his father had planted thirty years before. The glass was cold against his forehead. He had been standing thus for a quarter of an hour, and his mother had not spoken again since her last appeal, which hung in the air between them still, unresolved and heavy.

"You must think of your future, Edmund. You have always been a sensible boy."

He turned. Mrs Ashby sat in the chair by the fire, her hands folded in her lap, her cap slightly askew. She was not a woman given to sentiment, and the firmness of her mouth, though softened by age and the flickering light, told him she would not easily be moved from her position. She had come into the study that morning with the letter from Sir Clement Bohun, and she had not left.

"I have read it three times," she continued, nodding toward the letter on the desk. "The terms are generous beyond anything we might have hoped. Five hundred a year, the living at Amberley, the use of the carriage, and a house that has eight rooms. Eight, Edmund. Your father never had more than four."

Edmund crossed the room and took up the letter again, though he knew its contents by heart. Sir Clement Bohun of Delacote Park, the principal landowner of the parish and a man whose influence extended across half of Gloucestershire, wrote in a hand that was large and unhesitating. The living of Amberley would fall vacant at Michaelmas upon the retirement of the Reverend Dr Thorne. Sir Clement was empowered to present it to whomsoever he chose. He chose, he wrote, to offer it to Edmund, who had served the parish these two years since his father's death with "credit and diligence." But there was a condition, stated with a plainness that left no room for misinterpretation. Sir Clement's niece, Miss Margaret Bohun, had lately come to reside at Delacote Park. She was twenty-three years of age, possessed of a fortune of four thousand pounds, and in need of a husband. Sir Clement did not say that the living was the price for marrying her. He did not need to.

"I have never seen Miss Bohun," Edmund said, setting the letter down.

"You saw her in church last Sunday. She sat in Sir Clement's pew, two rows ahead. The short young woman with the brown hair and the grey pelisse."

Edmund recalled her. She had been small, neither handsome nor ill-favoured, with a complexion that spoke more of modesty than of health. She had sung the hymn in a voice that was tuneful if not remarkable. He had not looked at her twice.

"She is not clever, I suppose," he said, without conviction.

"She is a good girl, by all accounts, and she will have four thousand pounds. That is clever enough for most people." Mrs Ashby paused and looked at her son with an expression that was not unkind but permitted no softening. "You are five-and-twenty. You have nothing but this parsonage, which goes with the curacy, and the curacy goes when Sir Clement decides it goes. If you refuse him now, he will not offer again."

Edmund knew this. He knew it with the part of his mind that dealt in facts and arrangements, the part that had studied at Oxford and taken orders and learned to live on seventy pounds a year without complaint. But there was another part of him, less tractable and more insistent, that would not be governed by arithmetic.

"I have an understanding with Lucy," he said.

Mrs Ashby did not flinch. "I know it."

"An understanding, Mama. I have spoken to her. I have spoken to Mr Ferrars. I have given my word."

"Your word to a girl with no portion and no connections, whose father keeps a school in Cirencester and whose mother was a farmer's daughter. Your word, Edmund, against the word you gave to God when you took orders, to serve His church and live as a gentleman. These are not the same things, and you know it."

He did know it, or he feared he did. The argument had been turning in his mind for a week, ever since Sir Clement had first hinted at the arrangement over a glass of sherry in the dining room at Delacote Park. Edmund had listened, had smiled, had said he would consider, and had ridden home through the February dark with the wind in his face and Lucy Ferrars's last letter in his coat pocket. She had written of her sister's new baby, of the frost on the schoolroom windows, of how she had played a new piece on the pianoforte and thought of him. She had signed it, "Yours, as ever," in her clear, slanting hand, and the words had seemed to him then a kind of promise more binding than any contract.

He had walked to the schoolhouse the following morning and found Lucy in the garden, wrapping a laurel against the cold. She had looked up at him with those quiet grey eyes that always seemed to see more than she said, and he had not told her. He had spoken of the parish, of the rain, of the new curate at Hartpury, and he had gone away again carrying his secret like a stone in his chest.

"I cannot marry Miss Bohun," he said now, standing by the desk with his hand on the letter. "I do not love her. I love Lucy."

"Love is a fine thing," Mrs Ashby replied, "but it does not pay for a new roof. This parsonage has a damp that will be the death of you if you stay here another winter. Your sister is in service at Cheltenham because we cannot keep her here. Your brother is at sea. You are the eldest, Edmund, and the only one who can do anything for this family."

There was a silence. The fire shifted and sent up a small shower of sparks. Outside, the rain eased for a moment, then resumed.

"What would you have me do?" he asked, though he knew.

"Go to Sir Clement. Accept the living. Pay your addresses to Miss Bohun. Write to Lucy Ferrars and tell her that you must break the understanding, that you have no choice."

"I have a choice."

"You have the appearance of one. That is not the same thing."

He sat down at last, heavily, in his father's chair. The leather was cracked and the cushion was flat. He thought of his father, who had been curate of this parish for eight-and-twenty years and had died owing the butcher nine pounds. He thought of his mother mending the same black gown every spring. He thought of Lucy in her garden, her hands red with cold, her face turned up to his.

And he thought of Amberley. He had been there once, as a boy. A substantial stone rectory with a walled garden and a stable. A church with a painted ceiling and a peal of six bells. A village that would look to him for guidance, for schooling, for comfort in sickness and in death. A life of purpose and consequence. A life, he thought, that might be worth the sacrifice of a private hope.

He picked up the letter and folded it carefully, then placed it inside his pocketbook.

"I will go to Sir Clement tomorrow," he said.

Mrs Ashby nodded once. She did not smile. She rose, and came to him, and put her hand on his shoulder for a moment. Then she left the room, and he heard her go down the passage to the kitchen, where she would put the kettle on and begin to prepare the evening meal, as she had done for thirty years, without complaint.

Edmund sat for a long time in the gathering dark. When at last he took out a clean sheet of paper and dipped his pen, he wrote only one line before he stopped. The line read: "My dear Lucy." He looked at it, and the words seemed to him already to belong to someone else, someone who had lived in a smaller, poorer, happier world. He finished the letter before the candles guttered. He did not read it over. He sealed it and addressed it and set it on the hall table for the post. Then he went upstairs to his cold room and lay awake, listening to the rain, until sleep came for him at last, merciful and dreamless.
