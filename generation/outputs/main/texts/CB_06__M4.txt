I had been three months in the house of Messrs. Vandelaur & Company, of Antwerp, before the senior partner conceived the design of engaging me to instruct his two sons in the English tongue. The proposal came to me in the counting-house, one wet November afternoon, when the gas was already lit and the fog from the Scheldt hung yellow at the windows. Mr. Vandelaur spoke with that bland and rounded courtesy which sits so easily upon men of business, and which I had already learned to distrust, as one learns to distrust the velvet of a trap.

“You are a scholar, Mr. Hartley,” said he, leaning back in his chair and pressing the tips of his fingers together. “It is a pity your talents should be buried in ledgers. The boys—Émile and Théodore—they are good boys, but the English, it is not their affair. A little instruction in the evening, an hour, two hours; it will be a kindness to me, and I shall not forget it.”

I might have refused. I was not hired for pedagogy; I was hired to keep accounts, to copy letters, to endure the long dullness of the counting-house from eight in the morning until six at night. But there was in Mr. Vandelaur’s manner a something which made refusal difficult—a bland assumption that what he proposed must be agreeable, that what he commanded must be right. And I was young, and I was proud, and the notion of exercising my English upon these young Flemings flattered a vanity I did not then know I possessed.

So I consented. I was to present myself at the merchant’s house in the Rue des Peignes at seven o’clock, and there to remain until nine, instructing the boys in grammar, in pronunciation, in the reading of such books as I should judge proper.

The house was a tall, narrow building of dark brick, with a courtyard behind it and a garden beyond, where a few stunted limes grew, and where, in summer, I afterwards learned, Madame Vandelaur would sit with her embroidery and her little dog, and her air of a woman who has been beautiful and intends to remain so. She received me on the first evening in the salon—a room of heavy mahogany and green rep, with a portrait of the late Madame Vandelaur, the first wife, hanging above the chimneypiece, and a crucifix in the corner, and a smell of wax and stale tobacco.

She was a small woman, still handsome, with black eyes that moved quickly and a mouth that smiled without warmth. She spoke French to me, though I answered in English, and she smiled at my English as though it were a child’s trick, amusing but not quite respectable.

“Ah, you are the young Englishman,” said she. “My husband tells me you are very clever. But you must not be too severe with my boys. They are delicate. Émile, he has a weakness of the chest; Théodore, he is nervous. You will be gentle with them, monsieur.”

I said I would be as gentle as was consistent with instruction.

She looked at me a moment, and her smile did not change, but something behind it hardened.

“Consistent with instruction,” she repeated. “Yes. That is very well. But you must remember, monsieur, that they are not accustomed to—to application. Their father is too busy; I am not a scholar. They have been allowed to run wild a little. It is not their fault.”

I bowed. I was already beginning to perceive that in this house the fault would never be the boys’.

The boys themselves I found to be exactly what I had expected—two lads of fourteen and twelve, with their mother’s black eyes and their father’s heavy jaw, idle, indifferent, and possessed of that peculiar insolence which is the birthright of children who have never been contradicted. Émile, the elder, could read a little English, after a fashion; Théodore could not read at all, and had no intention of learning. They received me with a sort of tolerant contempt, as they might have received a performing dog.

The first evening was a farce. I set them to the conjugation of the verb “to be,” and Émile recited it with a fluency that suggested he had learned it by rote and understood nothing. Théodore yawned, and drew figures on his slate, and when I reproved him, looked at me with a surprise so genuine that I saw he had never been reproved before.

“But it is so dull,” said he. “Monsieur, why must we learn English? Everyone speaks French. Papa speaks French. Maman speaks French. The servants speak Flemish. It is only the English who cannot understand.”

“The English,” I said, “are your father’s best customers. It is for their money that he keeps his counting-house. If you cannot speak to them, you cannot sell to them.”

He stared at me. “But Papa sells to them,” said he. “Papa does not need us.”

I had no answer to that. I set him again to his alphabet, and he wept, and Madame Vandelaur appeared at the door to inquire what was the matter, and I was obliged to explain that her son had been asked to learn his letters, and she took him in her arms and comforted him, and looked at me over his head with a smile that said, plainly, “You see how delicate he is.”

This was the pattern of the weeks that followed. I would come at seven, and the boys would be late; I would set them their tasks, and they would dawdle; I would reprove them, and they would complain to their mother; and Madame Vandelaur would find occasion to pass through the room, or to send a servant with a message, or to call one of the boys away upon some trivial errand, until the two hours were consumed in a manner that left me weary and ashamed. I was not used to being defeated, and I was being defeated nightly by a woman who smiled and a pair of children who yawned.

At the end of six weeks I had made no progress. Émile could still recite his verb by rote and could not write a sentence of his own. Théodore had learned the alphabet, but he held his pen as though it were a snake, and his copybooks were a disgrace. I had been patient; I had been gentle; I had been, I think, more than patient. But I was not a saint, and I was not a fool, and I saw that I was being used—that I was brought there to give the appearance of instruction without its substance, so that Mr. Vandelaur might say to his English customers, “My sons, they are learning English; I have a young Englishman in the house,” and so be thought a man of cultivation.

The crisis came upon a Thursday. Mr. Vandelaur had returned from a journey to Ghent, and he sent for me to his study—that same room where I had first been engaged, with its shelves of ledgers and its map of the Scheldt and its smell of tobacco and sealing-wax. He was in good humour, and he offered me a glass of Schiedam, which I declined.

“Well, Mr. Hartley,” said he, “and how do the boys? They make progress, eh? They will soon be able to talk with the English customers, and then I shall be a proud father.”

I was silent a moment. I had rehearsed this scene a hundred times in my own mind, and always I had spoken plainly, and always Mr. Vandelaur had received my plainness with gratitude. But now, face to face with him, I felt the difficulty. He was not a bad man; he was a busy man, a man who loved his sons and believed in them, and it seemed a cruel thing to say to him, “Your sons are idle, and your wife encourages them in idleness, and I have wasted six weeks in a vain attempt to teach them what they do not wish to learn.”

And yet—and yet—what was I? I was a bookkeeper, a young man of no fortune, dependent upon my salary for my bread. If I spoke, I might lose my situation. If I did not speak, I should be false to my trust, and I should be false to myself, and I should be, in my own eyes, a contemptible creature, a flatterer, a parasite, a man who sells his silence for his dinner.

“Sir,” said I, “I must speak plainly. Your sons have made no progress. They do not apply themselves. They are intelligent enough—Émile especially—but they have no habit of attention, and they are encouraged in idleness by—” I stopped. I had almost said, “by their mother.”

Mr. Vandelaur’s face changed. The bland courtesy went out of it, and something harder came in—something that was not anger, but the coldness that comes before anger.

“By whom?” said he.

“By the whole arrangement, sir,” said I. “The hours are late; the boys are tired; they are allowed to absent themselves; they are not required to prepare anything between lessons. I cannot teach against the current. If you wish them to learn, they must be made to learn. If you do not wish it, then I am wasting your money and my time.”

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he rose, and went to the window, and stood with his back to me, looking out at the fog.

“You are very young, Mr. Hartley,” said he at last. “You are very young, and you do not know the world. You think that because a thing is true, it should be spoken. But there are truths that are better left in silence—especially in a house where there is a mother who loves her children, and a father who is much away.”

“I have spoken no more than the truth, sir,” said I. “If you wish me to continue, I will continue. But I will not pretend that I am succeeding when I am not.”

He turned. His face was not unkind.

“You are an honest young man,” said he. “I like you the better for it. But you must not speak to Madame Vandelaur of this. You will leave that to me. And you will go on with the lessons. I will see that the boys are more—more regular. And you will say nothing to them of what has passed.”

I bowed. I was dismissed.

I went out into the fog, and walked along the Rue des Peignes toward the river, and I was not sure whether I had done well or ill. I had spoken the truth, and I had kept my situation; but I had also agreed to a silence that sat uneasily upon my conscience. The boys would be “more regular,” perhaps, for a week or two; and then the old idleness would return, and I should be again where I was. And Madame Vandelaur would smile at me, and I should know that she knew, and she would know that I knew she knew, and there would be a wall between us that no courtesy could break down.

But I was young, and the night was cold, and the lights of the town were beginning to shine through the fog, and I thought that after all I had done what I could. I had not lied. I had not flattered. I had spoken the truth as I saw it, and if the truth had not been welcome, that was not my fault. I would go on with the lessons; I would do my duty; and if the boys learned nothing, it should not be because I had failed them.
