In the city of Aurelia, where the river ran as slow and deliberate as a well-fed bishop, there stood in a crooked street behind the cathedral a shop whose sign bore no name at all, but only the painted figure of a clock with its hands frozen at a quarter past three. It was the workshop of Master Ambrosius Vane, a clockmaker of such formidable reputation that the gentry whispered his name in the same breath as their prayers, and the poor crossed themselves when they passed his door, as though they had glimpsed something both holy and unholy at once. He was a small, neat man with hands like white kid gloves and eyes that seemed perpetually fixed upon some distant mechanism only he could see; and it was said—though he never confirmed it—that he had once mended the great clock of the cathedral so perfectly that it had kept time for forty years without a single tick being heard, a feat the archdeacon pronounced miraculous and the verger pronounced suspicious.

Into this workshop, on a morning of pale gold and silver mist, came King Ludovic the Fourth, called the Perpetual by his courtiers and the Tedious by his subjects, a monarch who had outlived three wives, seven chancellors, and every single one of his own enthusiasms. He came without ceremony, attended only by a single equerry who was instructed to wait outside, and he stood among the hanging pendulums and the breathing escapements and said, without preamble, "I am told you can stop time."

Master Vane set down his loupe. "Your Majesty is misinformed. I can persuade it. There is a difference."

"Persuade it, then," said the King. "I wish to live forever. I wish this moment—this very moment, with its excellent light and its tolerable temperature—to be prolonged indefinitely. I have consulted physicians, alchemists, and a woman in the fish market who reads entrails, and all of them have failed me. You are my last appointment."

The clockmaker regarded him with the mild, pitying expression of a man who has been asked to repair a watch that has been trodden upon by a horse. "Immortality, Your Majesty, is not a thing one can simply wind up and set upon the mantelpiece. It is a transaction. Every clock that stops for one man must, by the laws of equilibrium, run backward for another."

"Then let it run backward for another," said the King. "I am the King. The equilibrium is my concern, not yours."

"Very well," said Master Vane, and he went to the back of the shop, where there stood a clock unlike any other—a tall, slender thing of black lacquer and silver filigree, its face blank and pale as a drowned woman's, its pendulum a single thread of hammered gold. "This is the Horologium Perpetuum. It has no hands because it has no need of them. When I set it, time in Aurelia will cease to advance. The river will not flow, the bells will not ring, the bread will not rise, and Your Majesty will not age. But the clock will require winding."

"Then I shall wind it," said the King.

"You misunderstand," said Master Vane gently. "The clock winds itself upon the years of others. For every day Your Majesty lives beyond your appointed span, a day must be taken from someone within the city walls. The clock chooses. I do not. It has a taste for the young, I am sorry to say, and a particular fondness for those who are happy."

The King laughed, a sound like coins dropped into a velvet purse. "You speak as though I were a common murderer. I am a king. The lives of my subjects are already mine to spend. I spend them on roads and on wars and on the occasional public fountain. This is merely a different sort of fountain."

"Fountains, at least, are visible," said the clockmaker. "This one is not. But Your Majesty has been warned."

"I have been bored," said the King, "which is worse. Set the clock."

And so Master Vane, with a sigh that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than his lungs, drew from his waistcoat a small silver key and wound the Horologium Perpetuum seven times. The golden pendulum swung once, twice, and then hung still. Outside, the mist stopped moving. A pigeon that had been descending toward the cobblestones remained suspended in the air like a feathered thought. The river ceased its slow deliberation. The cathedral bells, which had been about to strike the hour, held their breath.

King Ludovic walked out into a city of statues. The equerry stood frozen with his mouth half-open, a fly suspended before his nose. The King went to the fish market, where the woman who read entrails sat with her hands cupped over a haddock, and he laughed at her and took an apple from a stall without paying, and the apple did not rot, and the stallholder did not blink. He went to his palace and found his courtiers arranged in attitudes of flattery so perfectly preserved that he could inspect them at his leisure and found them, for the first time, ridiculous. He had never been so happy. He had never been so alone.

For a week, he was content. For a month, he was amused. In the second month, he began to notice that the city smelled faintly of stagnant water, and that the pigeons in the square had not moved, and that the expression on the face of the fish-woman was one of such profound and permanent disapproval that it followed him everywhere. In the third month, he sent for Master Vane.

"The clock is not working properly," said the King. "Nothing is happening."

"Nothing is precisely what is happening, Your Majesty," said the clockmaker. "That is the nature of the mechanism. You asked for a moment prolonged. You have received it. It is the same moment. It will always be the same moment."

"But I am bored," said the King.

"Indeed," said Master Vane. "Boredom is the one thing that time cannot touch. It is the only true immortality."

The King commanded that the clock be stopped. Master Vane explained, with the patience of a man explaining arithmetic to a stone, that the clock could not be stopped; it could only be wound further, and each winding would take more days from more subjects, and the King would grow no older, and the city would grow no livelier, and the moment would stretch on and on like a held note that never resolves. The King raged. The King wept. The King offered Master Vane a dukedom, a pension, and the hand of his niece, all of which the clockmaker declined with the same mild courtesy with which he had declined the King's first commission.

"There is one thing," said Master Vane at last, "that might be done. The clock may be given a new master. It requires a keeper. If Your Majesty were to pass the key to another, the years would return to you, and the moment would end, and you would be old—older than you have ever been—in the space of a single breath."

"And who would take such a key?" asked the King.

"I would," said Master Vane. "I have been keeping time for forty years, Your Majesty, and I have never once been late. It is the only sin I have ever wished to commit."

And so the King, who had never in his life surrendered anything without a struggle, surrendered the key. Master Vane wound the Horologium Perpetuum seven times backward, and the golden pendulum swung, and the pigeon completed its descent to the cobblestones, and the cathedral bells struck the hour, and the river resumed its slow and deliberate progress toward the sea. King Ludovic the Fourth aged forty years in the space of a breath, and was dead before he reached the door, and was buried with great pomp in a ceremony that lasted precisely as long as ceremonies should.

The clockmaker kept the key. He kept it for many years, and the city prospered, and the pigeons flew, and the fish-woman read her entrails, and no one ever noticed that the clock in the crooked street behind the cathedral had begun, very quietly, to tick backward. Master Vane, when asked about it, would only smile and say that all clocks are honest in the end, and that time, like a well-bred guest, always knows when to leave.
