It was in the autumn of the year 183—, that a strange and fearful tempest, such as I had never before witnessed, even in that wild western region, descended upon the town of Wexbury. The rain came not in drops, but in sheets of living water, driven before a wind that howled like a spirit in torment, scouring the ancient stones of the assize town and rattling at the windows of my solitary lodging as if it would force an entrance to my very soul. For three days and nights the storm had raged without ceasing, and I, who had presided for a week over the courts of justice in that place, found myself a prisoner to its fury, and to a tempest of a far more dreadful kind that raged within my own breast.

I had passed my life in the calm, austere pursuit of the law. From my youth, I had revered its forms and its principles as the bulwark of society, the only sure guide through the labyrinth of human passions and errors. I had risen, by a steady and unblemished course of conduct, to the high office I now held, and I was conscious of no stain upon my character. My judgments, though they were sometimes severe, were ever, I believed, the dictates of an unclouded conscience, informed by the strictest evidence. I had ever held that the majesty of the law must be maintained, that its terror was as necessary as its mercy, and that the judge, in the solemnity of his duty, must become the impersonal instrument of that justice which is divine in its origin and terrible in its application. In this faith I had lived, and in this faith I had condemned men to death; and though my heart, being of a sensitive and thoughtful nature, would sometimes recoil from the finality of the sentence, my reason ever assured me that I did but fulfil the high and painful office allotted to me.

Among those whom I had thus condemned, some eight years prior, was a young man of the name of Elias Thorne. The case against him was, in the eyes of the world, and in my own, complete. He was a man of a fiery and ungovernable temper, and had been seen in a state of violent agitation by the murdered man, a wealthy farmer named Croft, only a few hours before the deed was done. A quarrel, the particulars of which were sworn to by credible witnesses, had taken place between them concerning a sum of money. Thorne was found the next morning, not far from the scene of the crime, his clothes stained with blood, and in his possession was a silver watch, the property of the deceased, which he could give no satisfactory account of how he had obtained. His protestations of innocence were wild and vehement, but they were those of a desperate man, and were interpreted by the jury as the last struggles of detected guilt. The evidence, though circumstantial, formed a chain so strong that I did not hesitate to pronounce the sentence of death; and I remember, even now, the cold, hard feeling of duty as I did so, the stern satisfaction that the law was not to be mocked, and that society was to be purged of a dangerous member. Elias Thorne died upon the scaffold, protesting his innocence to the last, but his words were lost upon the indifferent air.

This man, this Thorne, whose fate had been for years but a sombre memory, a single dark thread in the vast tapestry of my official life, was now risen from the grave to haunt me. It was on the second night of the tempest, when the wind shrieked with a more than mortal voice, that a messenger, drenched and trembling, brought to my door a letter from the governor of the county gaol. A man lay dying there, a wretch named Silas Blackwood, who had been a vagrant and a petty thief, and who now, in the final hour of his earthly existence, desired to unburden his conscience. His confession, the governor wrote, concerned the murder of the farmer Croft; and he named Elias Thorne as the innocent victim of a false accusation.

My blood ran cold as I read the words. A fearful hope, which was yet a greater fear, seized upon my heart. I ordered my carriage and, braving the fury of the storm, drove to the prison. The scene that awaited me in that dimly-lit, pestilential chamber, will never be erased from my memory. The dying man, his face a ghastly mask of yellow and grey, his breath coming in short, agonizing gasps, lay upon a pallet of straw. His eyes, which had been closed, opened at my approach, and fixed upon me with a terrible intelligence. In a voice that was but a hoarse whisper, yet which each word fell upon my soul with the weight of a hammer-blow, he told his tale. It was he who had quarrelled with Croft, he who had followed him and murdered him for his gold, he who had planted the watch upon the unconscious Thorne, whom he had found senseless in a ditch from an excess of drink, and whose clothes he had smeared with the blood of his victim.

When the dreadful recital was finished, I stood as one turned to stone. The world seemed to reel around me; the flickering lamp cast monstrous shadows on the wall, the wind howled without, and within my own bosom was a silence more awful than any sound. I was a murderer. My judgment, which I had deemed the pure emanation of justice, was in fact the instrument of a most foul and cruel injustice. I, who had prided myself on my spotless conscience, was now marked with a stain that all the waters of the earth could not wash away. I had looked upon an innocent man, and, with the authority of the law, I had condemned him to a shameful and an agonizing death. The forms of justice, the solemnity of the court, the weight of my own reputation—all rose up before me in a hideous mockery.

What was I now to do? The truth, if made public, would shatter the faith of the people in the very institution I served. It would bring ruin and disgrace upon my own head, and upon the name I had cherished as a synonym for integrity. My career, my honour, my peace of mind—all must be sacrificed upon the altar of a truth that could bring no comfort to the dead, and would only plunge the living into chaos. Or, I might bury this secret in my own breast, and let the world still believe that justice had been done. I might continue to sit upon the bench, a respected and honoured man, while this worm of guilt gnawed at my heart for ever. To conceal it, would be to make myself a party to the crime, a hypocrite of the worst kind; to reveal it, would be to proclaim myself a fool and a butcher. I, who had ever been the stern upholder of the law, was now its most wretched victim.

I returned to my lodging as the first grey light of dawn struggled through the storm-wracked clouds. The tempest was abating, and a strange, unnatural calm had fallen upon the world. But for me, there was no calm. The storm had passed from the heavens only to take up its eternal dwelling in my soul. I looked at my own reflection in the mirror—a face I had known and respected for sixty years—and saw a stranger, a man accursed. The judge and the murderer were become one. And in that moment, I understood that there is no tribunal so terrible as that of a man's own conscience, and no sentence so final as the one it pronounces in the silent watches of the night.
