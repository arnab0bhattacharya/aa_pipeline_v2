Mr. Halstead’s arrival at the grammar school in Little Marston had been attended with that quiet satisfaction which greets any change in a settled order, provided the change is not too violent. He was a man of a mild and studious aspect, whose chief recommendation to the trustees had been a university degree of some distinction and a reputation for thoroughness; but what that thoroughness was to be turned upon, they had not inquired with any very painful minuteness. To them, a schoolmaster was a functionary whose duty it was to keep boys at their desks, to hear the Latin grammar repeated until it was as familiar as the Lord’s Prayer, and to return them to their parents at the appointed holidays with their learning visibly increased, like a field that has been properly manured. Mr. Halstead, however, had brought with him from his college a conviction as firm as any religious faith: that the mind of a child is not a vessel to be filled, but a soil to be cultivated, and that the first and most vital part of this cultivation is to teach it to look, to observe, to connect the thing seen with the thing understood. He had, therefore, after a few weeks of the prescribed curriculum, begun to introduce what he called ‘natural studies’ into the school-day. The desks were cleared of their Ovids and their multiplication tables, and in their place were laid out the common treasures of the hedgerow and the stream: a nest of the harvest mouse, with its exquisite woven roof; the iridescent wing-case of a cockchafer; the stratified layers of a piece of shale from the quarry, each one holding the fossilized story of a world immeasurably older than the town of Little Marston itself. The boys, who had been accustomed to regard their lessons as a form of penance, were at first bewildered, and then, in a few cases, profoundly excited. They learned to tell a swallow from a martin by the cut of its wing, and to understand the slow chemistry by which a fallen leaf becomes the dark, rich earth in which its own tree is rooted. To Mr. Halstead, this was the very essence of education; to the parents of Little Marston, it was a bewildering and alarming waste of time. The first murmurings began at the market cross, where Mr. Pumble, the corn-factor, complained that his son could no longer parse a simple sentence but could discourse at length on the habits of the water-snail. Soon, the complaint became a general rumour, a current of opinion that flowed from the kitchen door to the drawing-room, gathering force as it went. The boys, it was said, were being turned into idle naturalists; they were learning nothing that would help them in the world, nothing that would fit them for the counting-house or the law. The matter was brought to a head at a meeting of the school trustees, a body of sober men whose principal concern was the sensible application of funds. They sat in the board-room of the Wheatsheaf Inn, the air thick with the fumes of port and disapproval. Mr. Pumble, a man whose face was the colour of a ripe plum and whose opinions were as solid as his ledger, spoke with the voice of practical grievance. ‘We send our sons here, sirs,’ he declared, thumping the table, ‘to be made into men of business, not into grub-worms. What use is it to a boy to know the name of a beetle, if he cannot cast an account? This new master, with his fancies and his ferns, is turning the school into a fair for idlers.’ The other trustees nodded; their own sons had reported with some amusement the master’s lectures on the metamorphosis of the tadpole. It was agreed that Mr. Halstead must be spoken to, and if he did not at once return to the ancient and proven methods, he would have to be replaced. It was on the evening of this meeting that the solitary defender of the new regime made his appearance. He was a boy of about twelve, named Silas Glegg, the son of the town’s glovemaker, a silent and observant child who had hitherto passed through the school as unnoticed as a shadow. He had been the first to feel the stirrings of a new life under Mr. Halstead’s instruction. The world, which had been a flat and uninteresting expanse of words and rules, had suddenly opened its secret doors to him. He began to collect, not with the random greed of a boy, but with the patient precision of a born naturalist. His pockets were filled with the chitinous armour of his finds, and in a drawer at home he kept his specimens, each one carefully labelled in his small, neat hand. When he heard of the trustees’ decision, a cold dread seized him. He knew, with the clear certainty of a child who has found the one thing that gives his life meaning, that if Mr. Halstead were to go, the door would be shut forever. He presented himself at the Wheatsheaf, a small, determined figure against the backdrop of the inn’s solid prosperity. He was shown into the board-room, where the trustees regarded him with a mixture of surprise and impatience. He did not speak at first, but laid upon the polished mahogany a large, black beetle, its elytra gleaming like polished jet. ‘This is a Great Diving Beetle, sir,’ he said to Mr. Pumble, his voice thin but clear. ‘Mr. Halstead taught us how it carries a bubble of air beneath its wings to breathe under water. He showed us the hollow in its back where the air is kept. It is a wonder of engineering, sir.’ The trustees stared, nonplussed. Mr. Pumble, however, was not a man to be moved by such trifles. ‘A wonder of engineering, is it?’ he snorted. ‘And what will it engineer for you, boy? A place in the workhouse? You should be at your Latin, not playing with insects.’ But Silas was not to be daunted. He spoke of the slow work of the bees, of the architecture of the spider’s web, of the way the frost splits the stone in the quarry, a process of change and decay that was the very history of the world laid bare. His speech was not eloquent; it was halting and simple, the speech of a boy, but it carried the weight of a profound and personal discovery. He was not defending a theory; he was defending the very colour and substance of his life. When he had finished, he stood for a moment in the silence, and then, with a quiet dignity that was more moving than any plea, he took up his beetle and left the room. The trustees were left looking at one another. They had heard the practical complaints of a dozen fathers, but they had not expected a defence from the very source of the mischief. The boy’s passion had been so earnest, his wonder so genuine, that it had momentarily pierced the thick hide of their common sense. It did not, however, change their minds. The decision stood. Mr. Halstead, they felt, had led the boy astray, and it was their duty to correct the error. The next morning, the schoolmaster was summoned. He listened to their verdict with a calm, sad resignation. He had known, of course, that his methods were a heresy in Little Marston. As he was packing his books, Silas Glegg came to the door of the classroom. The boy did not speak, but his face was a mask of desolation. Mr. Halstead laid a hand on his shoulder. ‘The world is wide, Silas,’ he said gently. ‘You have learned to see its wonders. Do not let anyone teach you to be blind to them again.’ He left Little Marston on the afternoon coach. As it rumbled out of the town, he saw the small, solitary figure of the boy standing by the milestone, watching him go. In the years that followed, Mr. Halstead’s brief tenure became a local anecdote, a tale of a well-meaning but impractical man who had tried to teach boys about beetles instead of business. Silas Glegg grew up to take over his father’s trade, and his collection, it was said, was the finest in the county. He never spoke of his old master, but sometimes, in the quiet of the evening, he would take out a specimen and look at it for a long time, his finger tracing the perfect, intricate line of its form. It was a small, private act of remembrance, a silent testament to a lesson that had been dismissed as useless, but which had, in its own way, given a single soul its true and lasting education. It is the fate of many a prophet to be without honour in his own country, and the sower of new ideas must often be content with the knowledge that the seed, however scattered, has here and there found a patch of fertile ground. The mind of a boy is a strange and unpredictable field, and the harvest is not always the one that the world expects.
