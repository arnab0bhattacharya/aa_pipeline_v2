The rain fell in long, mournful streaks against the windows of the assembly chamber, and I stood at the casement watching the city dissolve into a grey and weeping blur, my heart heavier than the clouds that hung so low above the Thames. It was the eve of the great debate, and all London—nay, all England—held its breath, waiting to see whether the man whom we had once loved as a brother, and whom the people now worshipped as a deliverer, would be crowned with the laurel of a dictator or struck down by the very laws he had sworn to defend. I had known Rufus Ashcombe when he was but a captain of dragoons, fiery and generous, with a laugh that rang like a bell across the parade ground and a hand ever open to the unfortunate. We had shared bread and danger, hope and sorrow; I had stood beside him when his young wife perished of the fever in the camp before Delhi, and I had held him while he wept like a child, his great frame shaken by a grief too vast for words. And now that same man, returning in triumph from the Eastern wars, his brow bound with the bloody laurel of a hundred victories, stood poised upon the precipice of ambition, and I—his oldest friend, his confidant, the keeper of his dearest memories—was called upon to decide whether I should raise my voice against him in the assembly and lose forever the friendship that had been the solace of my life.

I had sought him that very morning, hoping to reason with him in private, to appeal to the better angel of his nature before the madness of popular acclaim should seal his doom. His lodgings in Whitehall were thronged with officers and place-seekers, with flatterers whose tongues dripped honey and whose eyes gleamed with the cold fire of self-interest. I waited long in an antechamber, listening to the roar of voices within, the clink of glasses, the boisterous laughter of men who had tasted blood and found it sweet. At length the door opened, and he came forth to greet me—my Rufus, my friend—yet how changed! His face, once so open and frank, now wore the mask of command; his eyes, which had once melted with pity at a comrade's wound, now gleamed with a hard and restless fire, as though they sought ever for new worlds to conquer. He embraced me warmly, it is true, and called me by the old familiar name; but there was a something in his manner—a condescension, a consciousness of power—that struck me to the heart.

"Horace," said he, "you have come to congratulate me, I trust, upon the voice of the people. They call me to the helm; they bid me steer the ship of state through the tempest that threatens to engulf her. And I—I who have never yet refused a duty, however perilous—I must not now be found wanting."

I gazed upon him with an anguish I could scarce conceal. "Rufus," I replied, "the people are a many-headed monster, and their voice to-day is not the voice of to-morrow. You have served the republic nobly; your name is written in letters of gold upon the page of her history. Do not stain that page with the record of a usurpation. The laws are our safeguard; the assembly is the guardian of our liberties. Return to it, I implore you, as a citizen among citizens, and let your glory be the brighter for your moderation."

He laughed—a laugh that had in it more of bitterness than mirth. "Moderation!" he cried. "Moderation is the virtue of those who have never been tempted. Do you think that I have toiled and bled and conquered for my own aggrandisement? It is for England—for the England that I love—that I would assume the burden of power. The assembly is a nest of vipers, a den of faction and intrigue; it cannot govern, it can only wrangle. The people cry out for a strong hand, and I—I alone—can save the state from anarchy."

I saw that he was lost—lost in the labyrinth of his own self-love, blinded by the dazzling vision of his own greatness. I pleaded with him, I reasoned, I wept; but my words fell upon ears deafened by the roar of popular applause. He turned from me at last with a cold and stately courtesy, and I knew that the friend of my youth was gone forever.

That night I could not sleep. The wind howled mournfully around the house, and the rain beat against the panes like the fingers of the dead. I thought of the days when Rufus and I had wandered together by the banks of the Isis, discoursing of liberty and virtue, of the great men of old who had died for their country's good; and I asked myself whether those days had been but a dream, and whether the man who now grasped at absolute power was indeed the same being whom I had loved and honoured. I thought of his wife, that gentle creature whose memory he had once cherished as a sacred thing, and I wondered whether, in the giddy height of his ambition, he had forgotten the lessons of humility and self-abnegation which her love had taught him. And I thought of England—my England—the land of Shakespeare and Milton, of Hampden and Sidney—and I asked myself whether she was to bow her neck to the yoke of a military despot, and whether the blood of her martyrs had been shed in vain.

The morning came, pale and sorrowful, and I rose with a heart resolved. I would go to the assembly; I would speak, though my voice should break with emotion; I would raise my hand against the friend of my youth, and though I knew that in doing so I should sever forever the tie that bound us, I would do my duty to my country and my conscience. The debate was long and stormy. The partisans of Rufus, flushed with victory and confident of success, thundered forth his praises; they spoke of his triumphs in the field, his genius in council, his boundless popularity, and they urged the assembly to confer upon him the supreme authority, to make him, in all but name, the master of the state. I listened in silence, but my heart burned within me. At length I rose. The assembly was crowded; the galleries were thronged with eager faces; a breathless stillness reigned. I spoke—I know not how—of liberty, of law, of the sacred rights of the people; I spoke of the danger of military rule, of the corruption that waits upon absolute power, of the fate of those republics of old that had fallen beneath the sword of their own generals. I spoke of Rufus himself—of the Rufus whom we had known and loved—and I appealed to him, in the name of our ancient friendship, in the name of all that we had suffered and hoped together, to pause ere he plunged his country into the abyss of despotism.

When I ceased, a murmur ran through the assembly—a murmur of mingled admiration and dismay. The friends of Rufus answered me with angry taunts; they called me traitor, ingrate, the enemy of my country's saviour. But I saw that my words had not been wholly vain; I saw that some faces, before flushed with confidence, now wore an expression of doubt and fear. And then Rufus rose. He stood before the assembly, calm, majestic, terrible in his wrath. He spoke of my ingratitude, of the friendship that I had betrayed, of the dagger that I had sought to plunge into the bosom of him who had been my benefactor. He turned upon me the full blaze of his eloquence, and I felt my soul shrivel beneath the scorching fire of his reproach. But I did not quail. I met his gaze firmly, though my heart was breaking, and when he had ended, I said, in a voice that I strove to steady: "Rufus, I have loved you well; but I love my country better. If I have erred, may God forgive me; but I have done my duty."

The vote was taken. The motion for the dictatorship was defeated by a narrow majority. Rufus Ashcombe, the conqueror of the East, the idol of the people, the man whom I had loved as a brother, was rejected by the assembly of his countrymen. He left the chamber in silence, and I saw him no more. That night I received a letter in his hand—a letter cold and brief, in which he bade me farewell forever, and declared that the friendship of years was ended. I read it by the flickering light of my taper, and the tears fell fast upon the paper, for I knew that I had lost the dearest friend of my heart, and that the wound which I had inflicted upon him would never heal. Yet even as I wept, I felt a strange and solemn peace steal over my soul; for I had done my duty, and the voice of conscience whispered that the sacrifice was not in vain.
