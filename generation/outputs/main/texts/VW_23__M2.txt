The air in Pall Mall was thin, a pale, watery substance that seemed to offer no resistance to his stride, yet it pressed against his face with a cold insistence that made him pull his collar higher. It was that peculiar hour before the sun had fully committed itself to the day, when the city was suspended between the dream of the night and the machinery of the morning. Sir Arthur Pendelton walked, his shoes striking the cobblestones with a rhythm that was less a march than a pulse, a steady, mechanical throb that seemed to emanate from his own chest rather than the ground beneath him. The black taxis were already lining the curb, their engines idling with a low, resonant growl that vibrated through the soles of his feet, a sound that was both familiar and alien, like a hymn heard in a church where the words have been forgotten.

He was going to the House. He was going to speak. The thought arrived not as a decision but as a fact, as immutable as the stone of the buildings that rose on either side, their facades washed in a greyish light that offered no shadows, only a uniform, flat illumination that made the world look like a photograph left out in the rain. The speech was in his mind, a bundle of papers held together by a clip, but more importantly, it was in the muscles of his tongue, in the weight of his voice, a physical thing he had carried for weeks. It was about the tariffs, about the coal, about the cost of living, words that were heavy and dry, stones thrown into a dry well. Yet as he turned the corner, the light changed. It caught the wet pavement, reflecting the gas lamps that were still burning, their flames wavering in the chill, and for a moment the world was doubled, inverted, a city of light floating above a city of shadow.

And in that doubling, she appeared. Not as a ghost, for she was not dead, but as a presence, a warmth that radiated from the memory of a face seen twenty years ago in a drawing room in Kensington, where the air had been thick with the scent of lilies and the hum of conversation that he had never quite managed to join. Elsie. The name surfaced, soft and liquid, cutting through the bureaucratic rigidity of the morning. He had loved her, or thought he had, with a fierce, burning intensity that seemed now to be a foreign language, one he had once spoken fluently but had since forgotten the grammar of. What had happened to that love? Had it dissolved like sugar in hot water, leaving only a sweet, empty taste, or had it hardened into the very structure of his character, the iron spine that held him upright in the chamber?

He paused, leaning against the cold stone of a building, and watched a woman in a grey coat hurry past, her umbrella held high against a drizzle that had begun to fall, fine as mist. She did not look at him. She was invisible to him, as he was invisible to her, two atoms passing in the vast, indifferent space of London. He thought of his wife, Margaret, who was now lying in bed, perhaps waking, perhaps still asleep, her face turned away from him as it often was in the early mornings, a sign of a distance that was not physical but spiritual, a chasm that yawned between them, filled with years of duty, of children who had grown and left, of words that were spoken but not heard. Did she know what he was thinking? Did she know that he was carrying within him this double image, the woman of the past and the woman of the present, and that he was trying to reconcile them with the speech he was about to give? She would know, perhaps, not the specifics, but the weight of it, the heaviness in his step, the slight stiffness in his jaw. She was a woman of intuition, of quiet observation, who could read the weather of his mind by the way he held his hat, by the set of his eyes.

The fog was lifting, or perhaps it was rising, it was difficult to tell. The buildings of Westminster began to emerge from the haze, the dome of St. Paul’s a pale dome in the distance, a symbol of permanence that mocked the transience of the human drama unfolding in the streets below. He straightened his tie, a gesture of automatic propriety, and continued walking. The air was cooler now, sharper, biting at his cheeks. He felt a sudden, sharp pain in his left side, a twinge that reminded him of his age, of the body that was a vessel for the mind, a machine that required maintenance, that was slowly wearing out. He would not last forever. The thought was not frightening, but it was clarifying, a cold clarity that stripped away the pretensions of the moment. What mattered? The speech? The vote? The applause, or the hissing? Or was it only this: the sensation of the air on his skin, the sound of the traffic, the memory of Elsie’s laugh, which was a sound he had never quite captured, a melody that played in his head when the world was quiet, when the noise of politics and duty fell away.

He reached the gates of the Palace of Westminster. The guards stood like sentinels, their faces impassive, their red coats a splash of color in the grey morning. He passed them, his feet carrying him forward, and the world changed. The smell of the river, brackish and ancient, mingled with the smell of damp wool and the faint, metallic scent of the buildings. The walls were thick, stone upon stone, laid down by hands that were long since dust. He walked through the corridors, the sound of his footsteps echoing in the high vaults, a sound that was both his and not his, a echo of a time that was no longer present. He could feel the eyes of his colleagues on him, a mixture of hostility and curiosity, a forest of eyes that watched his every move, waiting for him to stumble, waiting for him to reveal the crack in the armor.

But he did not stumble. He walked with a steady, even pace, his face a mask of calm, his mind a whirlwind of memory and anticipation. He thought of Elsie again, not with regret, but with a kind of detached appreciation, as one appreciates a beautiful painting that one has seen only once, and knows one will never see again. She was part of the landscape of his life, a tree that had grown in a field he had since plowed under, its roots now deep in the soil, unseen but essential. And Margaret, his wife, who was waiting for him at home, who would ask him how it had gone, who would listen to his account with a polite interest that was also a form of love, a love that was quiet and enduring, like the light of the morning, which did not demand attention but was there, steady and reliable.

He entered the chamber. The noise hit him, a wave of sound that was almost physical, a roar of voices, of papers rustling, of chairs scraping against the floor. He found his seat, and for a moment, he closed his eyes, and in the darkness behind his eyelids, he saw the two women, Elsie and Margaret, standing side by side, their faces blurred, their features merging into a single, indistinct image of womanhood, of love, of life. And then he opened his eyes, and the world was sharp and clear, the green benches, the red leather, the faces of his enemies and his friends, and he stood up, and he began to speak, his voice clear and strong, a sound that cut through the noise, a sound that was his own, and yet was also the voice of the past, the voice of the future, the voice of the man who was walking through the morning, who was alive, who was here, in this moment, in this place, in this time.
