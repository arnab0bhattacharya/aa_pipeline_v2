The rain in London had a way of getting into a man’s bones, a damp, insinuating chill that was nothing like the dry, biting cold of the passes he had once known. Dr. Alistair Finch sat by the fire in his consulting room in Bloomsbury, a glass of sherry untouched at his elbow, and listened to the hiss of the gas lamps. The practice was quiet, respectable, and a world away from the field hospital at Fort Lockhart, where the air had smelled of carbolic and blood and the sun had baked the canvas until it cracked. He had spent ten years building this quiet, and he found it suited him. The tremor in his left hand, a souvenir from a skirmish near the Khyber, was the only part of him that refused to forget.

It was this quiet that the stranger shattered. Mrs. Pemberton, his housekeeper, announced him with a sniff of disapproval. The man was waiting in the hall, she said, and would not state his business, only that he must see the doctor. Finch, weary and curious in equal measure, told her to show him in.

The man who entered was lean, with a face tanned to the colour of old leather, a stark contrast to the grey London light. He wore a decent but well-worn greatcoat, and his left leg dragged slightly, a hitch in his gait that spoke of an old injury. He stood, hat in hand, and his eyes, a pale, startling blue, fixed on Finch with an unnerving intensity.

“Dr. Finch,” he said. His voice was low, with a burr to it that wasn’t quite Scottish. “You don’t remember me.”

Finch studied him. He had a surgeon’s memory for faces, for the map of a man’s bones and the particular way flesh knit together. He would have remembered this man. “I’m afraid you have the advantage of me, Mr…?”

“Croft. Elias Croft.” He offered a small, tight smile. “It’s no surprise you don’t recall. It was a busy time. ‘Eighty, it would have been. The fort at Gul Khel.”

The name was a ghost from a past life. Gul Khel. A mud-brick stain on a godforsaken plain, a place of heat and dust and sudden, brutal violence. Finch’s mind, long disciplined to forget, threw up a single, sharp image: a young subaltern, his face grey, a bullet lodged near his spine. He had operated for six hours. The boy had lived. He was sure of it.

“I remember the action,” Finch said carefully. “But your face… I’m sorry, Mr. Croft. I don’t recall you as a patient.”

“You wouldn’t,” Croft said, his gaze unwavering. “I wasn’t the officer. I was his orderly. You patched me up after, though. A splinter of shrapnel in the leg. You were quick about it. Said I’d have a limp to remember you by.” He tapped his left thigh. “And so I have.”

Finch felt a flicker of something—not quite memory, but a sense of plausibility. The chaos of that day, the sheer volume of wounded… it was possible. He gestured to a chair. “Please, sit. What can I do for you?”

Croft sat, his posture rigid. “It’s not for me, Doctor. It’s for what I left behind. There’s something buried at the old fort. Something that belongs to my family. I need to get it back.”

“Buried at the fort?” Finch frowned. “The place was abandoned years ago. It’s in the middle of tribal territory. You can’t simply stroll back in and dig it up.”

“I know. That’s why I need you.” Croft leaned forward, his voice dropping. “You have influence. You know people in the Political Service, men who could arrange a pass, an escort. More than that… you were there. You know the ground. I have a map, but it’s old. I need a man who’s seen it with his own eyes.”

The request was preposterous. Finch had spent a decade erasing the North-West Frontier from his life. He had no intention of returning, not for a stranger, not for a buried trinket. “I’m a physician now, Mr. Croft, not a soldier. I can’t help you.”

Croft’s expression did not change, but a coldness entered his eyes. “You can. And you will. Because you owe a debt, Doctor. We all do. You patched me up, but you left others. You made choices.” He stood, the movement abrupt. “I’ll call again tomorrow. Think on it. The item is a small, brass-bound chest. It was buried beneath the third pillar of the north-east bastion. You’ll remember the one. It had a crack in the shape of a lightning bolt.”

He was gone before Finch could protest, the click of the door echoing in the sudden silence. Finch sat for a long time, the sherry forgotten. The third pillar. He did remember it. He remembered the crack. He remembered the desperate, bloody business of that day, the smell of cordite and fear. But he did not remember Elias Croft. The man’s certainty, his thinly veiled threat, felt like a trap, a snare laid with details too precise to be pure invention.

The next morning, Finch did something he had not done in years. He sent a wire to an old colleague, now retired from the Intelligence Branch, and then he took a hansom cab to a modest office off the Strand. The brass plate on the door read “A. Vance, Enquiries.”

Augustus Vance was a small, meticulous man with the quiet air of a librarian and the eyes of a hawk. He had once been a sergeant in the Military Intelligence Department, and his mind was a filing cabinet of the Empire’s dirty secrets. Finch laid out the story.

Vance listened without interruption, his pen making occasional notes on a pad. When Finch had finished, he steepled his fingers. “Elias Croft, you say. The orderly.” He closed his eyes for a moment. “Gul Khel. The name is familiar. Not for the battle, but for what came after.” He rose and went to a large, green filing cabinet, his fingers dancing over the labels. He pulled out a thin folder.

“Here it is,” he said, returning to his desk. “A report from the Political Officer in ‘81. A small party went missing near the fort. A surveyor, a native guide, and an orderly. The orderly’s name was… Croft. Elias Croft.” He looked up. “They were presumed killed by raiders. Their bodies were never recovered.”

Finch felt a chill that had nothing to do with the London damp. “Presumed killed. But he’s here.”

“Indeed,” Vance murmured, his eyes scanning the report. “It says here the surveyor was carrying a quantity of gold coin, intended as payment for a local chief. It was never found. A small, brass-bound chest.” He tapped the file. “The official conclusion was that the raiders took the gold and the men were killed. But there was a note in the margin, added later by the officer’s superior. It suggested a different theory. That the orderly, this Croft, may have engineered the ambush, buried the gold for himself, and fled.”

The pieces clicked into place with horrifying clarity. The man was not a ghost from Finch’s past, but a scavenger of it. He was using the doctor’s memories, his guilt, as a key to unlock a fortune he had stolen and been forced to abandon. The story of the family heirloom was a lie. The threat was real.

“He’s not looking for a keepsake,” Finch said, his voice hollow. “He’s looking for his loot.”

“And he needs you to get it,” Vance said, closing the file. “He can’t show his face in that part of the world without a reason, and a former army surgeon with connections is a very good reason. He’s counting on your sense of duty, or perhaps your fear of the past, to make you his accomplice.”

That evening, Finch sat in his chair, the fire burning low. He thought of the young subaltern he had saved, and the men he hadn’t. He thought of the choices he had made in that dusty hell, and the choices that had been made for him. He had spent ten years running from the man he had been at Gul Khel. Now, it seemed, that man had been found. He looked at his left hand. The tremor was gone. It was perfectly steady. He knew what he had to do. When Elias Croft called again tomorrow, he would be ready. He would not be digging for gold, but for the truth, and he would bury the ghost of Gul Khel for good.
