The dust motes danced in the slant of afternoon light, rising and falling as if the air itself were breathing, and Eleanor dipped her brush into the cracked cup of turpentine. It smelled sharp, acrid, like the smell of dead leaves burning in autumn, or of something being stripped away; and she wiped the bristles on a rag that was already stiff, already caked with the colours of yesterday and the day before. The attic window was small, and grimy, and let in a light that was thin and watery, as if it had passed through a great depth of sea before arriving here; but it was her light, it was the light she had claimed for herself when she dragged the easel up the three flights of stairs, and swept the floor, and laid down the old newspaper that now crinkled beneath her shoes. Beneath her, the house went on being a house; the bell rang, the door opened and shut, and her mother’s voice rose from the hall, clear and bright and pointed, like a needle threading its way through the fabric of the afternoon. Mrs. Thorne had come to call. Eleanor could hear her name spoken once, twice, and then her mother’s laugh, which was like a string of small glass beads dropped one by one onto a plate.

For she was to be married. That had been decided, or rather it had been allowed to settle the way dust settles, the way decisions settle in families—not by anyone saying, at any one moment, you must, but by the slow accumulation of glances and silences and the turning of pages in a book that was not being read. Her father had mentioned it at breakfast, looking not at her but at the marmalade jar, turning it slightly on the cloth so that the label faced him; and her aunt had written from Cheltenham, and the letter lay on the hall table with its black-edged envelope looking, Eleanor thought, like a small dark bird that had blundered in through the window and could not find its way out. The man’s name was Henry. Henry Whitmore. He had a house in Wimbledon, and a position in the Board of Trade, and when he came to dinner he sat very straight in his chair and spoke of India, of the export of cotton, with a kind of careful authority that made Eleanor feel she was being measured for something—not a dress, not a life, but a shelf, a shelf in a cabinet where she would sit, behind glass, arranged.

And Mr. Crane had said it too, though in different words. He had stood behind her at the easel in that studio off the Tottenham Court Road with its smell of linseed and stale tobacco, and he had looked at her canvas, and then he had looked at her, and his eyes, which were small and red-rimmed and watery as if they had been washed too often, had filled with something that was not quite pity but was certainly not interest. He had said, there is feeling, yes, but feeling is not enough; and he had said, one must have a sense of structure, of form; and he had said it in the way one says a thing that is final, a thing that closes a door, and she had stood very still and heard the door close, and had thought of the sea, of standing on a beach and watching a ship go out, and the ship becoming smaller, and the deck and the masts becoming lines, and the lines becoming a dot, and the dot becoming nothing, and then there was only the sea, and the sky, and the sound of gulls, which is a desolate sound if one listens to it alone. But she had not stopped painting. That was the thing. She had not stopped.

The canvas before her now was small—she could not afford large ones, and there was something in the smallness that she liked, a kind of intensity, as if the image were being compressed, were being held under pressure, and might at any moment break open. She was painting the view from the window, which was rooftops, and chimney pots, and a slice of sky that was pale, that was almost white, but which held in its pallor, if one looked, if one truly looked, the faintest stain of green, like the green at the edge of a shell, or the green that gathers in the hollow of a wave just before it breaks. And the rooftops were not brown, as rooftops are supposed to be, nor grey, but were violet, and rose, and a deep, bruised purple where the shadows fell; and the chimney pots were red, but not the red of bricks, rather the red of embers, of the inside of a mouth, of the heart of a flower that has been pressed in a book and forgotten. She mixed the colour on the palette, breathing through her mouth so that her breath would not fog the surface, and the brush touched the canvas and the colour spread, and it was right, it was what she had seen, it was the thing itself.

But who would see it? Nobody. The paintings were stacked against the wall of the attic, face in, like children playing a game of hide-and-seek, and nobody came up here except herself, and the occasional mouse, and the wind when it found its way through the gap in the window frame and made the newspaper flutter. She did not show them. She had taken one to Mr. Crane, once, and he had looked at it and turned away, and she had brought it home and put it with the others, and that had been the end of that. But it was not the end, because she painted another, and another, and each one was a kind of speech, a kind of calling out, as if she stood at the edge of a vast dark field and called into it, and the sound went out and was swallowed, and she called again, and the sound went out again, and was swallowed again, and still she called, because the calling was the thing, the calling was the life, and the silence that answered was not nothing, was not emptiness, but was the world listening, perhaps, the world holding its breath.

Downstairs the bell rang again. She heard her mother say something about the garden, about the roses, and Mrs. Thorne’s voice answering, high and bright, and then the sound of the door closing, and the voices moving away, and the house settling back into its afternoon quiet, which was not really quiet but was the sound of a house breathing, the creak of stairs, the tick of the clock in the hall, the distant murmur of the kitchen where the kettle was being put on, all of it woven together like a fabric, like a web, and she was inside it, she was caught in it, and yet here, in this attic, with the dust and the turpentine and the thin light, she was outside it too, she was free.

She stepped back from the canvas. The paint was wet, and it shone in the light, and the rooftops and the sky and the chimney pots were there, they were hers, she had made them. And it seemed to her, standing there with the brush in her hand and the rag stained with colour hanging from her fingers, that this was enough; that if no one ever saw them, if they remained stacked against the wall of the attic like a secret, like a hoard, it was still enough; because she had seen the world, and she had put it down, and the world was in them, and they were in the world, and the silence that surrounded them was not the silence of failure but the silence of a bell that has been struck and is still vibrating, still ringing, ringing in the empty air, ringing and ringing, though no ear is there to hear.
