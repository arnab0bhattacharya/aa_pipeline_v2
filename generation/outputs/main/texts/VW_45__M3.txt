The light that fell through the high windows of the bookshop in Charing Cross Road was not the raw, indiscriminate light of the street, but strained, as if through greenish water, catching the millions of floating motes and illuminating the scuffed spines of the quartos. Eleanor stood behind the counter, her fingers still holding the delicate, dried texture of an eighteenth-century binding. The smell was of old paper, of wood shavings, of the quiet dust that settles over decades in a room where people speak only in murmurs. She had been wrapping the volume in brown paper, her thumb pressing the rough twine, when the bell above the door rang, admitting a sudden, violent roar of an omnibus and the heavy, scented presence of Mr. Vance.

He entered the shop as he always entered it, bringing with him the crisp, unmistakable aura of new money, of wide, immaculate pavements, of hansom cabs and hot-house flowers. He did not see the books; they were merely the scenery for the drama of his own arrival, the necessary shadow against which his grey spats and his pearl tie-pin might flash into prominence. He looked at Eleanor. He saw a young woman in a dark dress, pale, rather tired perhaps, but possessed of a grave, still beauty that attracted him precisely because it was so utterly unlike the women of his own class, who seemed always to be fluttering, like brightly colored birds in a cage, demanding seeds.

“I have been thinking,” he said, laying his gloves on the counter, on top of a first edition of Marvell, “that you must let me take you out of this place. Just for an hour. The sun is quite extraordinary; it turns the very mud to gold. We could drive to the Park.”

His voice was kind, undeniably kind. It possessed the assurance of a man who has never been denied, who believes the world is constructed of doors waiting to be opened by the golden key of his smile. Eleanor looked at the gloves, the soft, expensive leather, and then out the window, where the light did indeed strike the wet pavement and make it shine. She thought of her own room in Bloomsbury, the damp patch on the ceiling that spread like a dark continent, the gas fire that coughed and spluttered, the cold meat on the table. She thought of how easy it would be to be carried in that luxurious tide, to be wrapped in fur, to have the damp ceiling replaced by a plaster medallion, to be safe. The temptation was a physical thing, a sudden melting in the bones, a desire to lay down the heavy burden of her own independence and let him bear it.

She was about to speak, to yield perhaps, or to fashion some polite evasion, when the door opened again, and Julian entered. He brought with him the chill of the autumn air, the smell of rain and stale tobacco. His coat was thin at the elbows, his hair unbrushed, and his eyes, as always, had that burning, distracted look of a man who is perpetually pursued by the phantoms of his own verses. He saw Mr. Vance immediately, and the large, beautiful gloves lying on the Marvell, and his face, which had been animated by some thought he was carrying in from the street, closed up, becoming hard and bitter.

“Julian,” she said, feeling a sudden rush of color, as if she had been caught in some act of betrayal.

“Am I interrupting?” Julian asked, his voice thin, his eyes fixed on the wealthy customer with a mixture of contempt and despair. He saw the whole of it in a flash: the shop, the girl, the wealthy patron. He saw the age-old tragedy of the artist and the world, the eternal selling of the spirit for the sake of the body’s comfort.

Mr. Vance, oblivious to the tension, nodded pleasantly. He was not a man to be ruffled by the presence of a shabby poet. “Not at all,” he said, turning back to Eleanor. “Think about what I said, Miss Vance—I mean, Miss Royce. The offer stands. Tomorrow, perhaps?” And with a gentle, proprietary smile, he gathered his gloves and left, the door banging shut behind him, leaving a silence in the shop that was thick and heavy with unspoken things.

Eleanor stood very still, her hands resting on the counter. She felt the familiar weight of Julian’s judgment, his gaze burning into her, and she was suddenly, fiercely angry. “You must not look at me like that,” she said. “You have no right.”

“Like what?” he asked, stepping closer, his voice trembling. “Like a man who sees a bird walking into a cage? Eleanor, he knows nothing. He would buy you as he buys a painting, to hang on his wall, to show to his friends. He does not see you. He sees a pretty object, a thing to be possessed.”

“And what do you see?” she flashed, the anger making her words sharp. “A martyr? A saint? Someone to starve in a garret so that you may feel superior? I am tired, Julian. I am tired of the cold, and the damp, and the endless struggle. I am tired of counting pennies. Is it so terrible to want warmth? To want safety?”

He looked at her, and his anger seemed to deflate, leaving only a profound, weary sadness. “Safety,” he repeated, as if the word were a foreign coin, of uncertain value. “There is no safety, Eleanor. There is only the integrity of the soul, the private vision. If you go with him, you will be comfortable, yes. You will have fires and carriages and a house in the country. But you will be dead. You will be selling yourself.”

The words hung in the air between them, vibrating with the intensity of his belief. Eleanor felt them strike her, felt them sink into the soft, yielding part of her that wanted to yield, that wanted to say yes to Mr. Vance and his easy world. She looked at Julian, at his thin, passionate face, his eyes that seemed to see through the veil of the material world into some other, more real dimension. She thought of his poems, which were difficult and beautiful, and which no one bought. She thought of the long evenings they had spent together, talking in low voices in the British Museum, surrounded by the ghosts of dead civilizations, their minds touching and leaping together in a dance that was entirely free of the world of gloves and omnibuses.

But she also thought of the damp patch on the ceiling, the cold meat, the tired ache in her feet after standing all day. She thought of the future, stretching out before her, an endless repetition of this counter, these books, these murmurs. Was it selling oneself, to want to escape? Or was it simply surviving?

The light had shifted, moving from the quartos to the floor, leaving the shelves in shadow. The shop was very quiet. Outside, the traffic of Charing Cross Road roared on, indifferent to the small human drama being played out among the books. Eleanor picked up the brown paper and the twine, and began, mechanically, to tie the parcel again, her fingers moving with a precise, deliberate grace. She did not look at Julian.

“I don’t know,” she said at last, her voice barely above a whisper. “I don’t know what I am. But I know that I am cold, and he is warm.”

Julian watched her fingers, the way the twine bit into the soft brown paper, the way she tied the knot with a final, tight pull. He felt a sudden, terrible pity for her, and for himself, and for all the world, which was so beautiful and so difficult, and which demanded such impossible choices. He wanted to say more, to argue, to plead, but the words seemed suddenly useless, mere sounds in the vast, indifferent hum of the city. He turned and left the shop, walking rapidly into the crowd, his thin coat flapping in the wind, his head bowed against the sudden, icy rain that began to fall, washing the streets, making the pavements shine again, turning the mud to a dark, treacherous gold.

Eleanor stood alone in the shop. The rain tapped against the window, a steady, insistent rhythm. She looked at the space where Julian had been, and then at the door through which Mr. Vance had gone. She felt the two worlds pulling at her, the world of poetry and cold rooms, and the world of warmth and dead, beautiful objects. The light faded, the shadows deepened, and the books stood in their rows, silent witnesses to her indecision, their old, wise faces turned away, keeping their own counsel, offering no guidance, only the vast, accommodating silence of the printed word.
