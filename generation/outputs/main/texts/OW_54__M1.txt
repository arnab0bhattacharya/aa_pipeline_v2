In the city of Veridia, which was built of white marble and blue shadows and lay upon the coast of a violet sea, there was born upon a single night two sons. The one was the child of Aurelius the Merchant, whose coffers were heavier than the stones of the citadel and whose name was spoken with the reverence usually reserved for the Deity or the Demon. The other was the son of a fisher, whose nets were empty and whose hut was thatched with the sad green weeds of the marsh.

When the news of these births reached the ears of the Fairy Zephyra, who dwelt in the heart of a great emerald and loved only things that were rare and unreasonable, she descended from her jewel to look upon the infants. She found the rich man’s son wrapped in swaddling clothes of Tyrian purple, embroidered with pearls, while the poor man’s son lay upon a heap of dried rushes, shivering in the draft of the open door.

‘It is a cruel world,’ said the Fairy, ‘and men are cruellest of all things in it. I will give these children gifts, that they may be unlike the rest of their kind, and thus be loved, or hated, or pitied, as it pleases the gods.’

So she waved her wand of carved ivory over the sleeping babe of the Merchant, and said: ‘I give thee Beauty. Thy face shall be a garden where women shall lose their reason, and thy eyes shall be like the deep wells in which men drown. Thou shalt be the Adonis of thy age, and all who see thee shall be made glad or mad by the sight.’

Then she turned to the child of the fisher, who was crying for hunger, and she smiled with a strange melancholy. ‘And to thee,’ she whispered, ‘I give a Kind Heart. Thou shalt feel the pain of others as thy own, and thy soul shall be a mirror of the world’s sorrow. Thou shalt be poor, and despised, and unhappy, for he who feels for all must bear the burden of all. Yet it is a gift more precious than gold, though no man will know it.’

And with that she vanished into the emerald, leaving the world to its vanity and its pain.

Twenty years passed over Veridia like a shadow passing over a sundial. The city changed not, for it was too beautiful to change, and its citizens changed not, for they were too vain to learn. In this time, the son of the Merchant grew to be the wonder of the age. His name was Lysander. His skin was like the petal of a white rose, his hair was the colour of the sun when it sets behind the hills of gold, and his mouth was like the red heart of a pomegranate. He was a poem written in flesh, a living work of art. He dressed in silks that rustled like dry leaves in autumn, and he carried a fan of peacock feathers merely to display the whiteness of his hands. He spoke little, for his beauty spoke enough, and when he did speak, it was only to utter some charming vanity.

The son of the fisher grew to be the shadow of the city. His name was Corvin. He was thin and pale, with eyes that were too large and hands that were too soft for a labourer. He worked in the dye-houses, and the purple of the royal robes stained his fingers a permanent bruise. He had no mirror in which to see his face, for he could not afford one, nor did he desire to see it. He spent his nights wandering the slums, binding the wounds of the drunkards and giving his bread to the beggars. He was known as a fool, for he gave away what he did not have, and he wept for strangers who had never done him a kindness.

On the evening of the Festival of the Lotus, when the whole city was illuminated with lanterns of rose-coloured silk and the air was heavy with the scent of jasmine and myrrh, these two sons of the night met at the gate of the Royal Palace.

Lysander stood by the marble pillars, leaning against the cold stone as though it were a lover. He wore a doublet of sea-green velvet slashed with silver, and his beauty was so great that the passers-by stopped to stare, forgetting their own errands and their own sorrows. He was bored, for he was always bored; the world had nothing left to show him that was fairer than his own reflection.

Corvin came running up the avenue of cypress trees. He was clad in a tunic of coarse grey wool, and he was weeping, for he had just seen a child die of a fever in the alleyways, and the injustice of it sat upon his heart like a stone. He was looking for a physician, hoping to beg a draught for another sick man, and in his haste he stumbled against the velvet-clad legs of the rich man’s son.

‘I crave your pardon, my lord,’ said Corvin, bowing low. ‘I did not see you, for my eyes are dim with tears.’

Lysander looked down at the shivering creature with the disdain of a god looking at a worm. He saw the dirty tunic and the stained hands, and he shuddered. Yet, as he looked closer at the face of the poor man’s son, he felt a strange irritation. It was not a handsome face, but it was a living one, etched with lines of suffering that made it more interesting than any statue in the palace.

‘You are clumsy,’ said Lysander, his voice like the tinkling of silver bells. ‘And you are ugly. It is a sin to be ugly in a city where the flowers are so fair. You should have been born a toad, that you might dwell in the mud without shaming the sunlight.’

‘You are right, my lord,’ said Corvin, with a sad smile. ‘I am ugly, and I am clumsy. But I have a sick friend who is dying, and I seek a physician. Have you seen one pass this way?’

‘Physicians are tedious creatures,’ said Lysander, examining his fingernails. ‘They smell of chemistry and despair. I have seen no one. Why do you care if your friend dies? Death is the only reality; life is merely the dream that precedes it.’

‘Because he is in pain,’ said Corvin. ‘And his pain is mine. If I could bear it for him, I would. Is that not the nature of love?’

‘Love is a malady,’ said Lysander, yawning. ‘I have been loved by duchesses and princesses, and it has given me nothing but a headache. But tell me, little beggar, why do you weep? It makes your face even more grotesque.’

‘I weep because the world is full of sorrow,’ said Corvin. ‘And because I have no power to heal it. I have a kind heart, sir, but a kind heart is a heavy burden when the hands are empty.’

Lysander laughed. It was a cruel, musical sound. ‘A kind heart! What a useless ornament. It is like a jewel on a beggar’s finger—valuable in itself, perhaps, but serving only to attract the thief. I possess beauty, which is the coin of the realm. I can buy love, wine, and silence with a smile. What can your heart buy?’

‘It buys me the knowledge that I am not alone,’ said Corvin softly. ‘Though I am poor, I am never lonely, for I feel the presence of all who suffer. But you, my lord, who are so beautiful, you look very lonely to me.’

Lysander started. The arrow had struck the painted target of his vanity and found the flesh beneath. For a moment, the mask of the exquisite slipped, and he looked at Corvin with eyes that were suddenly old.

‘Loneliness is the price of perfection,’ said Lysander, recovering his composure. ‘One cannot mix with the common clay without being soiled. I live in a tower of ivory; you live in the gutter. It is natural that we should be different.’

‘Yet we were born on the same night,’ said Corvin. ‘I have heard the story. The fairy gave you beauty and me a kind heart. It seems she played a cruel trick on us both.’

‘She gave me the world,’ said Lysander.

‘She gave me the world’s pity,’ said Corvin.

They stood in silence for a moment. The lanterns swayed in the breeze, casting long, dancing shadows across the white stones. From the palace came the sound of music, the laughter of the courtiers, and the clinking of crystal glasses. It was a world of surfaces, of glitter and gold, and Corvin was as out of place in it as a bruise on a peach.

‘Go back to your gutter, little beggar,’ said Lysander, though his voice had lost its edge of contempt. ‘Your tears will rust the marble. And I must go in and be admired. It is my profession.’

‘Farewell, my lord,’ said Corvin. ‘I hope that one day you may find something in the world more beautiful than your own face.’

‘There is nothing,’ said Lysander.

‘There is kindness,’ said Corvin. ‘And it is the only thing that does not fade.’

Corvin turned and walked away into the shadows of the cypress trees, his grey figure melting into the darkness like a ghost. Lysander watched him go. He raised his hand to his face, touching the smooth, perfect curve of his cheek. He felt the coldness of his own skin. For the first time in his life, he wondered if the mirror had lied to him, and if the man in the rags, who wept for strangers, had not been the richer of the two after all.

Then he sighed, adjusted the peacock feather in his hand, and stepped into the light of the palace, where the music was loud and the wine was sweet, and where no one would ever ask him to feel.
