The lamp in the lodging-room off the Edgware Road burned with the steady, yellowish flame of a wick that had been trimmed too often and too anxiously, and by its light Vera Ilyinichna watched her husband's hands. They were broad, capable hands, scarred at the knuckles from some forgotten labour of his student days, and they lay now upon the table, quite still, beside the teacups, and yet they seemed to her—as all things seemed to her in these last days—to be engaged in some secret and strenuous work of their own, some labour of the spirit from which she was for ever excluded. He had been silent a long while. The clock on the mantelpiece, a cheap thing with a florid brass figure upon it, ticked with a kind of insolent regularity, and beyond the window the raw November drizzle made a faint, persistent hissing against the panes, so that the silence within the room was doubly enclosed, doubly impregnable.

"Alexei," she said at last, and her voice, though she had meant it to be firm, came out with a little catch in it, a small betrayal of the long evenings she had spent alone, "you are going away to-morrow, and yet you sit there as though you were already a thousand miles from me. What is it? What is it that keeps you so far off?"

He raised his eyes to her then, and she saw in them that peculiar, veiled look which she had come to dread more than any anger—the look of a man who stands at a window watching something in the street which the woman beside him cannot see. He was a man of middle height, lean, with the hollow temples and the fine, nervous mouth of one who has been nourished upon ideas rather than upon bread; and there was about him, even in the shabby gentility of his London clothes, the air of a man who carries a burden that is not visible to the ordinary eye.

"Far off?" he repeated, slowly, as though he were tasting the words and finding them strange. "Yes. Perhaps. It is a long journey, Vera."

"Do not play with me," she said. "You know what I mean. It is not the journey. It is something else. For a month now—no, for longer—you have been like a man walking in his sleep. You speak to me, and your voice comes from a great distance. You touch me, and your hand is not there. I have asked myself—" She stopped, and the colour rose into her pale English cheeks, the colour of a woman who has been bred in a vicarage and has married a stranger from a land she does not understand. "I have asked myself whether there was someone else."

He did not smile. That, perhaps, was the most terrible thing about it—that he did not smile, that he did not hasten to reassure her with the easy, tender falsehoods which men keep for such occasions. He looked at her with a grave and pitiful attention, as a physician might look at a patient whose disease he has already diagnosed and knows to be mortal.

"No," he said. "There is no one else. Upon my honour—such honour as I have—there is no one else. That I can swear to you."

"Then what?" She leaned forward across the table, and her small hands, so unlike his own, white and delicate and useless, clasped themselves together with a kind of desperate entreaty. "Alexei, I am your wife. I have left everything for you—my father's house, my country, my—my God, perhaps. I have asked nothing of you but that you should let me share your life. And now you are going away, and I feel that I have never known you at all. What is it that you are hiding from me? What is it that you are going to do?"

The rain hissed against the window. The clock ticked. And Alexei Fyodorovich sat motionless, and in his face there was a struggle which she could see but could not comprehend—the struggle of a man who has trained himself for years in the discipline of silence, and who finds at last that the discipline has become a second nature, a prison-house from which he cannot escape even when he would.

"You ask me what I am going to do," he said at last, and his voice was very low and very careful, as though he were picking his way among words that might explode at a touch. "I am going to Russia. I am going to—to see certain men. There is a work there which must be done. It is not a pleasant work, Vera. It is not a work of which one speaks to women—to anyone. It is—" He paused, and she saw his throat move as he swallowed. "It is necessary."

"Necessary," she echoed, and the word fell between them like a stone dropped into a well, and they waited for the sound of it to come back from the depths, and the sound did not come.

"Necessary," he repeated. "You must understand—you must try to understand—that there are things in the world which cannot be mended by talk, by petitions, by the slow and comfortable methods of your Englishmen. There are things which can be mended only by—by fire. And those who carry the fire must not ask whether their hands will be burned. They must not ask anything. They must simply carry it."

She looked at him, and in her eyes there was a dawning horror, the horror of a woman who has lived all her life in the pleasant, well-ordered garden of English decency and who suddenly perceives, beyond the hedge, the dark and trackless forest where her husband has his being.

"You are speaking of—of killing," she said. It was not a question.

He did not answer at once. He rose from the table and went to the window, and stood there with his back to her, looking out into the wet, blurred darkness of the Edgware Road, where the gas-lamps made smudges of yellow light upon the pavement and a solitary cab rattled past with a sound like the rattling of dry bones. When he spoke again, his voice was muffled, as though he were speaking not to her but to the night, to the rain, to the indifferent city.

"I am speaking of a man," he said, "who sits in a high place and sends other men to die in the snow, in the mines, in the prisons, by inches, by years, by the slow corruption of everything that makes a man a man. I am speaking of a man whose death would be—would be a kind of mercy, Vera, a mercy to thousands who have never heard his name and never will. I am speaking of a thing which I have been chosen to do because—because I am the only one who can do it without flinching. That is what I am. That is what I have been made. And that is why I cannot—" He turned from the window and faced her, and his face was grey and drawn in the lamplight. "That is why I cannot let you come near me. Not now. Not until it is finished."

She had risen too, and she stood with her hands pressed against the edge of the table, as though she needed its support. The teacups rattled faintly under her touch. She was not weeping. She was beyond weeping. She was looking at him as a woman looks at a stranger who has suddenly revealed himself in the likeness of her husband.

"You are going to murder a man," she said, slowly, "and you did not tell me. You let me think—you let me think it was only politics, only meetings, only speeches. You let me believe that we were—that we were like other people."

"We are not like other people," he said. "I thought—I hoped—that you might never need to know. I hoped that I might keep you—" He made a gesture with his hand, a small, helpless gesture, as though he were trying to shape in the air something that could not be shaped. "I hoped to keep you in the light. It was a folly. It was the folly of a man who wants to have his cake and his conscience too. Forgive me."

She shook her head, slowly, as though she were trying to shake off a dream. "I do not know what to forgive," she said. "I do not know who you are. I thought I was married to a man. I find I am married to—to a purpose. To a thing that has no face. To a—"

She stopped. The words would not come. And in the silence that followed, there was only the rain, and the clock, and the distant, mournful sound of a train whistling somewhere in the darkness beyond the houses, a sound that seemed to come from a great way off, from the very edge of the world, from the cold and trackless spaces where men go to do the things that cannot be spoken of.

He came towards her then, and took her hands in his, and she felt the warmth of them, the human warmth, and it seemed to her a terrible thing that a man's hands could be so warm and his purpose so cold.

"I leave to-morrow," he said, "from a place on the coast. I cannot tell you which. I cannot tell you when I shall return. I may not return at all. But I want you to know—I want you to believe—that whatever I do, I do it not because I have ceased to love you, but because I have loved something else more—something that is not a thing at all, but a—a necessity. A duty. A fire in the blood. You will hate me, perhaps. I cannot help that. But I shall carry your face with me into the dark, and it will be—it will be the only light I have."

She did not answer. She stood quite still, with her hands in his, and her eyes, wide and dry and stricken, fixed upon the cheap brass clock upon the mantelpiece, whose hands had come round at last to the hour of midnight, and whose ticking seemed to her, in that moment, the sound of a world falling to pieces, quietly, inexorably, in a shabby London lodging, while the rain fell and fell upon the indifferent streets.
