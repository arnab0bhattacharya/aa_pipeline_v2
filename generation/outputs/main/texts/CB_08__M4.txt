The lamp had burned low, its flame shrinking and swelling like a thing in pain, and by its wavering light I sat and watched the slow, hard breathing of my aunt. Three months it had been since the physician, Mr. Hesketh, had shaken his head and spoken of the winter as a bridge she could not cross; and now the snow lay drifted against the mullioned windows of Withering Heights, and the wind came off the moor with a sound that was almost human in its desolation. I had nursed her through it all—through the nights when she raved of my uncle dead these fifteen years, through the days when she lay so still I must hold the mirror to her lips to know her living. I had not slept a whole night in all that time, nor eaten a meal I did not take standing, nor spoken to a soul save the doctor and the two servants, who were old and slow and did not love me.

And to-day the lawyer had come.

Mr. Farrow had been shown into the parlour, where the fire was kept up for the sake of the invalid above, and I had gone down to him with my hands still smelling of the camphor I had been rubbing upon her chest. He was a dry, precise man, with a mouth like a trap and eyes that never softened. He had spread his papers on the table, and he had told me—with no more feeling than if he were reading a list of goods—that my aunt, in a codicil executed but six weeks since, had revoked the bequest of the farm and the little sum in the York bank which had been promised me since I was a child, and had left all to my cousin, Mr. Percival Ashby, of whom I had heard her speak but once, and who had never in his life set foot within her door.

I had stood there, I remember, and looked at the fire, and felt the room turn round me slowly, like a wheel. I had said something—I know not what—and Mr. Farrow had answered that he was but the instrument of the law, and that he trusted I would do nothing unseemly. Then he had folded his papers and gone out into the snow, and I had heard his horse's hoofs ring hollow on the frozen road, and I had gone back up the stairs to my aunt's bedside and sat down in my old place, and taken up my knitting, and gone on with it as though nothing had happened.

But she knew. I saw it in her eyes when she woke at dusk—a flicker of something that was not pain, and was not peace. She moved her hand upon the coverlet, and I took it, and she said, in that whisper which was all the voice she had left:

"Thou'lt not curse me, Ruth?"

I could not answer. My tongue was dry in my mouth. I thought of the years—the long, grey, loveless years—and of how I had given them all to her, and of how she had let me give them, and of how she had known, all the while, that she meant to leave me nothing. I thought of the little hoard I had saved from my own earnings, which would not keep me a twelvemonth. I thought of the world, and of how it looked to a woman of four-and-twenty with no home and no portion and no beauty to recommend her.

"I did it for the best," she whispered. "Percival is a man. He will maintain the name. Thou art but a girl, and girls marry."

I rose up then, and went to the window, and stood with my forehead against the cold glass, and looked out at the moor. The snow was falling fast, and the last light was going out of the sky, and the whole world was white and black and terrible. I felt my heart swell within me like a sea, and I set my teeth, and I said to myself: I will not weep. I will not give her that.

But when I turned back, she had fallen into a sleep that was more like a swoon, and her face was grey and sharp, and I saw that she would not trouble me long. And I sat down again, and the anger went out of me, and left me cold.

It was on the third day after that—for she lingered, as the dying will—that there came a knock at the door, and the servant brought me a letter. It was from Mrs. Ellwood, of High Scar, the widow of a manufacturer, a woman of substance and of a cold, correct religion, who had known my mother, and who had once said to me, at a funeral, that she feared I had not the gift of a meek and quiet spirit. She wrote that she had heard of my situation—news travels fast on the moors—and that she was in want of a governess for her two daughters, and that she would be willing to receive me, at a salary of twenty pounds a year, upon the understanding that I was not to expect to be treated as one of the family.

I read it twice, and then I folded it and put it in my pocket, and went about my duties. But all that day the words kept rising in my mind like a refrain—not to be treated as one of the family—and I thought of the cold parlour at High Scar, and the cold faces of the children, and the cold pride of the woman who wrote it, and I thought: is this the world, then? Is this what a woman's life comes to—to be cast off by the dead and hired by the living, and to be grateful for the hiring?

That night my aunt woke, and knew me, and asked for water. And when I had given it her, she said:

"Ruth, I have been thinking. Thou hast been good to me—better than I deserved. I have done thee a wrong. But I cannot mend it now. Only—if thou wilt take my counsel—go not to High Scar. I know Mrs. Ellwood. She is a hard woman. Thou wouldst fret thy heart out there."

"And what would you have me do?" I asked. "You have left me nothing. I must go somewhere."

She was silent a long while. Then she said, very low:

"Thy mother's sister—in Leeds—she would take thee."

"She is poor, and she has seven children, and she has not spoken to you these twenty years."

"Nevertheless," said my aunt, "she would take thee."

I looked at her—at the sunken face, the wasted hands, the eyes that were already looking beyond me into the dark—and I felt a great pity rise up in me and drown my anger. For she was dying, and she was afraid, and she had done a mean thing, and she knew it.

"Peace," I said. "Peace, aunt. I will do what I can."

She died in the morning, before the light came. And I was alone in the house with the two old servants, and the snow, and the silence.

For a week I stayed, and saw to the burial, and put the house in order, and wrote to Mr. Farrow, and received from him a cold and formal answer, and wrote also to Mrs. Ellwood, and said that I thanked her, but that I had other prospects. And then I packed my box—it was a small one—and put on my cloak and bonnet, and took my mother's Bible, and went out of the door of Withering Heights for the last time.

The morning was clear and hard, and the snow lay crisp underfoot, and the sky was a pale, fierce blue, and the moor stretched away on every side, white and trackless and free. I stood a moment on the threshold, and the wind came and took my breath, and I felt my heart lift within me—not with joy, but with a strange, stern exultation. I was penniless, and homeless, and alone; and I was not afraid.

For I had looked upon death, and upon the meanness of the human heart, and upon the cold charity of the world; and I had found that I could bear it. And I said to myself, as I turned my face toward the road that leads down into the valley: I will not be a governess, and I will not be a dependent, and I will not be a beggar. I will be a woman, and I will make my own way, and if the world will not help me, then I will help myself.

And I set my foot upon the frozen road, and went down into the valley, and the snow was white on the hills behind me, and the sun was cold and clear above.
