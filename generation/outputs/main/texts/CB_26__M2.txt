The letter lay upon the desk, a small, pale square of paper that seemed to burn against the dark wood like a brand. I had read it three times; I had read it until the ink blurred, until the words *died* and *funeral* and *brother* lost their distinct shapes and merged into a single, throbbing pain. My brother, Arthur. My only kin. My protector. My companion in those long, silent years when the world was vast and I was small. He was gone. The thought did not arrive as a sudden blow, but as a slow, cold tide rising in my chest, swallowing my breath, drowning my voice.

I sat in the small, sparsely furnished room that served as my study in this foreign city of Veldt. The rain lashed against the windowpane, a relentless, rhythmic drumming that mocked the stillness within. Outside, the streetlamps flickered and hissed, casting long, distorted shadows across the wet cobblestones. The air in the room was thick with the scent of damp wool and old books, a familiar comfort that now felt like a suffocating shroud.

There was no one here to comfort me. The headmistress, Madame de Rieux, was a woman of exquisite manners and rigid propriety; she would offer a polite inquiry about my health and perhaps a glass of wine, but her eyes would remain fixed on the horizon, avoiding the dark depth of my grief. The other teachers, a mixture of French and German women, moved through the corridors like ghosts, concerned only with their lessons and their reputations. They knew me as Miss Elmsworth, the strict, efficient English instructor of mathematics and history. They did not know that I had been raised by a sister who died of a fever when I was twelve; they did not know that Arthur had been the only man who had ever looked at me and seen a person, not a duty. They did not know the weight of my loneliness, or how it had settled into my bones like winter damp.

I looked at the clock on the mantle. It was half past eight. I had an hour and a half before the first period began. I could not stay here. I could not remain in this room with the letter and the silence. I had to go to the classroom. I had to stand before the girls and explain the properties of triangles and the dates of battles, while my heart lay broken in my hands.

The thought was absurd, yet it was the only thing I could do. To break down now would be to admit defeat, to allow the grief to spill over and destroy the facade I had built so carefully. I was a teacher; I was a woman of reason. I would not be a victim of emotion.

I rose from the chair, my legs stiff and trembling. I walked to the washstand and splashed cold water on my face. The shock of it sent a jolt through my nerves, clearing the fog for a moment. I looked at my reflection in the tarnished mirror. My face was pale, my eyes red-rimmed, but my expression was hard, set in a line of determination. I straightened my collar, smoothed my dress, and picked up my book.

The walk to the main building was a journey through a landscape of grey stone and wet leaves. The rain had ceased, leaving the air heavy and cold. I walked quickly, my boots splashing in the puddles. The school building loomed ahead, its windows glowing with a warm, yellow light that seemed impossibly cheerful given the state of my soul. I entered through the side door, slipping into the shadows of the corridor.

I could hear the murmur of voices from the classrooms ahead. The sound of young girls laughing, arguing, turning pages. It was a normal, ordinary sound, yet it struck me with a force that was almost violent. How could they be so light, so carefree, when the world was capable of such sudden, terrible loss? I felt a surge of anger, hot and fierce, rise in my throat. It was an anger at the indifference of the universe, at the cruelty of death, at the fragility of life. I clamped my jaw shut, forcing the anger down, converting it into a cold, steady resolve.

I entered the classroom just as the bell rang. The girls looked up, their faces bright with the excitement of the new day. I walked to the front of the room, my steps measured and precise. I placed my book on the desk and looked out at the sea of young faces. There was no sign of recognition in their eyes; they saw only Miss Elmsworth, the authority figure, the keeper of knowledge.

"Good morning, ladies," I said. My voice was steady, clear, and devoid of any tremor. I was surprised by its strength.

"Good morning, Miss Elmsworth," they replied in unison.

I opened my book to the page on geometry. "Today we shall begin our study of the properties of the equilateral triangle. Please open your notebooks to page forty-two."

For the next forty minutes, I spoke of angles and sides, of proofs and theorems. I moved around the room, correcting errors, answering questions. My mind was a blank slate, a void in which the words flowed without effort. I was a machine, a conduit for information. The pain was still there, a dull ache behind my ribs, a constant background hum, but it was contained, locked away in a dark room in my soul. I did not let it out. I did not let it touch my face or my voice.

When the bell rang, the girls gathered their books and filed out. The room fell silent, the silence heavy and oppressive. I stood at the desk, my hands resting on the wood. I felt a sudden wave of exhaustion, a physical weakness that threatened to buckle my knees. I closed my eyes and breathed in, the air sharp and cold.

I had survived the first period. There were three more to go.

I thought of Arthur. I thought of the last time I had seen him, the way he had laughed at my joke, the warmth of his hand on my shoulder. The image was vivid, sharp as a blade. It cut me, and I bit my lip to keep from crying out. I would not weep. I would not show them weakness. I was alone, but I was not defeated. I would carry this grief as a burden, a heavy stone in my pocket, and I would continue to walk, to teach, to live.

I picked up my book and walked out into the corridor. The next class was waiting. I straightened my back, lifted my chin, and stepped into the light. The rain had started again, tapping softly against the high windows, a gentle, persistent sound that accompanied me as I went to face the day.
