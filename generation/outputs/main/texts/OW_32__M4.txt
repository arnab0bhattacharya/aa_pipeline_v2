The ladies of the Kensington Committee for Rational Living were, as they themselves would have been the first to admit, entirely without illusions concerning the nature of the world, and it was precisely this absence of illusion which they prized most highly. They had abolished the decorative in their drawing-rooms, the ornamental in their dress, and the merely pleasant in their conversation, and had replaced all three with what they called, in their circulars, the Solid and the True. Mrs. Hargreave-Pell, their president, was a woman of formidable principle and even more formidable corsetry, who held that a rose existed for the purpose of being classified and not for the purpose of being smelled, and who had once been heard to remark, at a garden party, that she could see no use in flowers whatever, since they were neither nourishing nor instructive. It was this very lady who conceived the brilliant notion of inviting Mr. Lucian Vane to address the committee upon the subject of Plain Living.

Mr. Vane was, at that period, the most talked-of and least-understood figure in London. He was a poet who wrote very little, a critic who praised very much, and a talker who never stopped. He had furnished his rooms in the Albany with peacock-feathers, old Venetian glass, and a single enormous sunflower in a jar of blue china, and had been known to declare, at a dinner-table in Mayfair, that the only really moral thing in the world was beauty, and the only really immoral thing was a bad colour. To invite him to lecture against the committee was, Mrs. Hargreave-Pell explained to her colleagues, a stroke of strategy worthy of a general. He would come, he would be brilliant, he would be wrong, and he would be refuted. The committee would sit in the front rows, in their sensible serge and their sensible boots, and would listen to his nonsense with the calm superiority of those who have never in their lives been guilty of a decoration.

The lecture was announced for a Thursday evening in the small hall behind the Kensington Institute, and the committee assembled in full force, twenty-three strong, each lady equipped with a notebook and a pencil, and each resolved to take down the worst of it for future refutation.

Mr. Vane arrived precisely seven minutes late, which he afterwards said was the exact interval required to make an entrance neither too eager nor too rude. He was dressed in a suit of soft grey, with a buttonhole of two white violets, and he carried no notes whatever. He looked about the room with an expression of gentle melancholy, as though he had expected a garden and found a gymnasium, and then he began.

"Ladies," he said, "I am told that you have invited me here to speak against you. I confess I had intended to do so. I had intended to stand before you as the advocate of everything you have spent your lives in condemning—of the useless, the delightful, the exquisitely unnecessary. I had intended to tell you that your plain living is merely a poverty of the imagination, and that your high thinking is a species of intellectual housekeeping. But I find, on entering this hall, that I cannot do it. I am overcome. The spectacle of twenty-three ladies in identical serge, each with a notebook, each with a pencil, each with a mind made up—it is too beautiful. It is a picture. It is a pattern. It is, in its way, a work of art."

The committee exchanged glances. Mrs. Hargreave-Pell wrote down the word "art" and underlined it twice.

"You have achieved," continued Mr. Vane, "what the greatest painters of the Renaissance could not achieve. You have made monotony sublime. You have taken the dullest colour in the spectrum—I refer, of course, to the colour of duty—and you have spread it over twenty-three human beings with a consistency that is positively heroic. I am told that you object to ornament. But look at yourselves! You are ornament. You are a frieze. You are a chorus. You are the most decorative thing in Kensington."

Here a slight rustle passed through the front row, and Miss Ada Quilter, the secretary, who had a secret fondness for ribbons, was observed to touch her collar with a guilty hand.

"But I must not flatter you," said Mr. Vane, with a wave of his white fingers. "Flattery is the homage that mediocrity pays to those who are not mediocre. Let me rather speak to you of the great truth which you have so nobly missed. You believe that plain living is a virtue. It is not a virtue. It is a misfortune. It is the misfortune of those who have never seen anything beautiful enough to want. The man who lives plainly because he cannot afford to live otherwise is merely poor. The man who lives plainly because he thinks it right is a prig. And the woman who lives plainly because she thinks it right—ah, ladies, she is something worse. She is a philanthropist."

Mrs. Hargreave-Pell wrote down "prig" and "philanthropist" and placed a small exclamation mark beside each.

"You tell me," he went on, "that beauty is superficial. Of course it is superficial. It is on the surface, and it is precisely there that it does its work. The surface of a peach is not the peach, but it is the only part of the peach that tells you what the peach is like. The surface of a woman is not the woman, but it is the only part of her that the world will ever see, and it is therefore the only part she has any right to neglect. You have neglected it. You have dressed yourselves as though the body were a shameful secret, and the soul a public meeting. You have made your houses into barracks and your gardens into cemeteries. You have banished the rose because it does not preach, and the peacock because it does not pray. And what have you put in their place? You have put in their place the notebook and the pencil, and the lecture on the virtues of plain living, and the committee meeting at half-past four."

There was a silence. Miss Quilter's pencil hovered. Mrs. Hargreave-Pell's pencil had stopped.

"Let me tell you a story," said Mr. Vane, in a softer voice. "There was once a woman who lived in a house of grey stone, and she had a grey carpet and grey curtains and a grey cat, and she was very good and very useful and very much respected, and when she died they put a grey stone over her and a grey text upon it, and nobody ever visited the grave. And there was once another woman who lived in a house of red brick, and she had a garden full of yellow roses and a room full of blue china and a parrot that screamed at the postman, and she was not very good and not very useful and not at all respected, and when she died they put a white marble angel over her and a line of poetry upon it, and people came from miles away to look at the angel and to wonder who she was. Which of the two, ladies, do you suppose had the better of it?"

The committee did not answer. The committee was, in fact, looking at Mrs. Hargreave-Pell, and Mrs. Hargreave-Pell was looking at the floor.

"I see that I have shocked you," said Mr. Vane, with a smile of exquisite courtesy. "That is the first step. The second step is to persuade you. And the third—the third is to convert you. I shall not attempt the third to-night. I shall merely leave you with a thought, and the thought is this: that the plain living which you commend is only admirable when it is a choice, and it is only a choice when there is something splendid to choose instead. The ascetic who has never seen a jewel is not an ascetic. He is merely a man who has never seen a jewel. And the committee which has never allowed itself a ribbon is not a committee of saints. It is a committee of ladies who have never allowed themselves a ribbon. I thank you for your attention. I hope you will allow me to send you, as a small token of my esteem, a quantity of white violets for your next meeting. They will not improve your minds, but they will improve the room."

He bowed, and sat down, and the committee sat quite still for a moment, and then, with one accord, it began to clap. It clapped for a long time. It clapped, indeed, until Mr. Vane was obliged to rise again and bow once more, and then it clapped again, and then Mrs. Hargreave-Pell was heard to say, in a voice that trembled slightly, that she had never spent a more instructive evening, and that she thought, if the committee had no objection, they might consider the question of a small subscription for the purchase of a few flowers for the hall.

The subscription was carried unanimously. The serge was, within the year, replaced by silk; the notebooks by fans; and the Committee for Rational Living, having changed its name to the Society for the Appreciation of Beautiful Things, gave, in the following spring, a garden party at which Mr. Vane was the guest of honour, and at which Mrs. Hargreave-Pell wore a dress of pale yellow chiffon and a hat with three ostrich feathers, and was heard to remark, as she surveyed the lawn, that she had always secretly believed that a rose was a more convincing argument than a pamphlet, though she had never before had the courage to say so.
