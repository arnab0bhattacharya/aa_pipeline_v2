It is a common error in the distribution of our human sympathies to suppose that the keeping of a village hostelry necessitates a coarse conscience or an eye strictly mercenary. Mrs. Letty Byatt, who for fifteen years had presided over the sign of the Wheatsheaf in the Leicestershire village of Millby Parva, was, in truth, a woman whose mind had been narrowed by circumstance rather than impoverished by nature. She possessed a mental vision that could take in the remote consequences of an action, and a quiet shrewdness that often passed for mere taciturnity among the loose-tongued gossips who lingered over her tap-room ale. It was this very faculty of seeing far, of holding in suspense the immediate impulse to speak while she weighed the eventual balance of things, that now sat heavily upon her as she polished the brass spigot of her best barrel on a chill November morning in the year eighteen hundred and twelve.

For Mrs. Byatt held a secret, and a secret, however quietly lodged in the mind, is a living organism that demands sustenance and threatens to outgrow its chamber. It concerned Mr. Amos Bellamy, the senior churchwarden of Millby Parva, a man of such substantial respectability that his very gait seemed to rebuke the irregularities of the lower orders. Mr. Bellamy was the pillar of the vestry, the superintendent of the Sunday school, and the unyielding advocate for that strictness of parochial discipline which he believed was the only barrier between English society and the encroaching deluge of French infidelity. It was he who had lately distinguished himself by heading a zealous campaign among the magistrates to suppress the Wheatsheaf, declaring it a seminary of drunkenness and a blot upon the moral landscape of the parish. His words, delivered with a ponderous righteousness that carried great weight among the neighbouring gentry, had a terrible practical force, for the licensing sessions were but a fortnight away, and Mrs. Byatt knew well that her livelihood hung by a thread.

Yet the woman who was thus threatened with ruin knew a thing concerning Mr. Bellamy which would have made a fine, crackling bonfire of his reputation. She knew that for the past three years, on the evenings preceding the quarterly vestry meetings, the churchwarden, under cover of the deep dusk, had entered her premises by the low-browed side-door that opened into the back lane. There, in the dim little parlour she reserved for such quiet occasions, he would sit alone and consume a prodigious quantity of strong Jamaica rum, drawing it from a private flask he brought concealed within his capacious greatcoat, though he paid her handsomely for the use of the room and the pure water she provided to temper the spirit. She knew, too, that this periodic indulgence was not the coarse toping of a harvest-field, but the desperate, trembling relief of a man whose flesh was subject to some inward mastery, a hidden appetite that was as much a disease as a sin.

Letty stood at the tap, the cloth arrested in her hand, her grey eyes resting on the damp, yellow leaves that plastered the cobblestones of the yard outside. The temptation to speak was strong, for the instinct of self-preservation is a sharp spur, urging us to unhorse our adversaries with whatever weapon lies nearest to our hand. It would be a simple thing to go before the magistrates and declare that the man who sought to close her doors was himself a slave to the very bottle he denounced. But Letty was not a fool, nor was she a woman of merely retaliatory passions. She knew the topography of the human heart well enough to understand that exposing hypocrisy does not always advance the cause of truth, and that the destruction of a neighbour’s standing may bring down a ruin that spreads wider than the original offence.

She was roused from her meditations by the heavy tread of boots in the passage, and presently her son, Jem, a lad of nineteen with a broad, open face and hands already hardened by the plough, entered the kitchen, bringing with him a sharp odour of damp earth and turnips.

“Mother,” he said, leaning against the oak settle and rubbing his knees, “I met Parson’s clerk on the brook-bridge, an’ he says Mr. Bellamy’s been round to Squire Lester’s this morning, gettin’ his hand to a paper for the suppression o’ the Wheatsheaf. They say there’s six magistrates as’ll sign it come the sessions. It’ll go hard wi’ us, Mother, if we lose the license. I mun go to work in the fields then, an’ you’ll have to take in washin’.”

Jem’s voice had the bluntness of a man who feels the pressure of an impending ill but has no remedy to offer, and his face wore the resigned, bovine expression of one accustomed to the weather’s caprice.

Letty looked at her son, and a wave of maternal feeling, warm and turbulent, surged through her, making the secret in her breast seem a small price for his future comfort. “Nay, Jem,” she said, in a tone of forced quietness, “I’m not for giving up the Wheatsheaf while there’s breath in my body. Mr. Bellamy’s a powerful man, but he’s not the only one as has a tongue in his head.”

But as she spoke, she felt the hollowness of the threat. To use her knowledge would be to strike a blow at the very foundations of a man’s life, and though he was her enemy, the moral imagination which was Letty’s chief endowment would not allow her to contemplate such a deed without a profound shrinking. She saw him as he was: a man labouring under a heavy yoke, wearing the mask of virtue because the alternative was a social death. To tear off that mask would not cure him; it would merely scatter the fragments of his life among the rabble, who would trample them in their eagerness for a spectacle. And she asked herself, with that searching candour which is the austere companion of a solitary life, whether her own hands were so entirely clean that she could afford to act as a judge over the hidden weaknesses of another.

It is in these moments of inward debate, when the mind oscillates between the claim of personal justice and the wider, vaguer obligations of human fellowship, that the deepest tragedies of provincial life are enacted. The world’s history is written not only in the clash of armies, but in the silent struggles of obscure women in village kitchens, who hold the keys to reputations and must decide whether to lock or to throw them away.

Letty Byatt’s decision, though it cost her a bitter pang, was in keeping with the tenor of her thoughts. She would not speak. She would go to the licensing sessions, and if she must fall, she would fall with the consciousness that she had not bartered a man’s hidden misery for her own security. It was a resolution that had in it something of the grandeur which belongs to all renunciation, however obscure. She would bear the consequence of Mr. Bellamy’s zeal, leaving him to the higher tribunal of his own conscience, and to the slow, sure retribution which time brings upon all inconsistencies.

“Mother,” Jem said, watching her face with a perplexed air, “you’re not going to let him shut us up without a word, are you? You’re not one to bear a grudge and hold your tongue.”

“I’m one to know when speech does more harm than silence, Jem,” Letty replied, turning back to her barrel. “There’s them as thinks they’re doing God’s work by closing the alehouse, and maybe they are. But I’ll not be the one to bring a man’s private sorrow into the open market to be judged by them as has no understanding of it. We’ll go to the sessions, and we’ll say our piece, and what’s to be will be.”

The lad stared at her, dimly perceiving that she was moved by some reason beyond his reach, and then, with the easy acquiescence of the young in the decisions of their elders, went out to see to the horses.

Letty remained alone. The fire burnt low in the grate, and the damp November wind rattled the casement. She took up her cloth again and resumed her polishing, her heart heavy with the weight of a knowledge she had chosen to keep, and with the sombre prescience that the path of self-denial is often the most lonely and the least understood.
