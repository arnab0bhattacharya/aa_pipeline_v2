It was upon a raw, foggy morning in the latter days of November, 1894, that my friend, Mr. Havock Thorne, found himself engaged in a matter which combined the technicalities of modern science with the older, more universal science of human greed. I had accompanied him to Clerkenwell, to a narrow, greasy thoroughfare known as Vellum Mews, where the smoke of a hundred small forges mingled with the yellow winter mist.

Our client, a Mr. Silas Grier, met us at the door of his workshop. He was a thin, pale man, with the stoop of the desk-worker and the nervous, twitching hands of one who sleeps little and thinks incessantly. He wore a frock-coat which had seen much better days, and a silk hat which he turned ceaselessly in his grasp.

“I am greatly obliged to you for coming, Mr. Thorne,” said he, as he led us through a yard strewn with metal shavings. “It is the lamp, you see. My new electrical incandescent lamp. The design has been stolen, and I am a ruined man.”

Thorne said nothing, but his sharp, grey eyes were darting to right and to left, taking in every detail of our surroundings. We entered a long, draughty workshop, lit by the single, grimy window which faced the mews. At the far end stood a heavy, iron-clamped safe, its door still securely closed. Beside it was a workbench littered with coils of wire, glass bulbs, and the brass fittings of the electrical trade.

“You say the design was stolen from that safe?” Thorne asked, pointing his cane towards the iron box.

“It was, sir. The only complete set of drawings for my new vacuum filament. They were there at five o’clock yesterday afternoon. I locked them away myself. This morning, at eight, they were gone.”

“And the safe was forced?”

“No,” Grier admitted, a flush rising to his sallow cheeks. “That is the devil of it. The safe was opened with a key, or so it would seem. There is no mark of a pick or a drill.”

Thorne walked to the safe, examined the lock through a small lens he drew from his pocket, and then turned abruptly to face our client.

“You suspect someone, Mr. Grier. Pray, who is it?”

“It is Josias Crane,” Grier cried, with a sudden, fierce bitterness. “He has a workshop across the yard. He has been my rival these ten years past. He has long sought to perfect a similar lamp, but his filaments would always burn out in a matter of hours. He knew that I had succeeded where he failed.”

Thorne stepped out into the yard, Grier and I following closely at his heels. The fog was thickening, and a fine, cold drizzle had begun to fall. We crossed the cobbles to a low, brick building on the opposite side of the mews. The door was ajar.

Thorne pushed it open with his stick. The interior was in a state of wild confusion. A bench was overturned, papers were scattered across the floor, and a half-eaten meal of bread and cheese lay upon a stool. A small travelling-bag stood open upon a chair, partially filled with clothing, as though the owner had been interrupted in the act of packing.

“A hurried departure,” Thorne observed, his voice calm and measured. “He has gone, and he has gone in a hurry. You say he has disappeared, Mr. Grier?”

“None of the men in the mews have seen him since yesterday evening. He has fled, Mr. Thorne. He has my drawings, and he has fled to patent the design before I can.”

Thorne was kneeling upon the floor, his lens moving systematically over the scattered papers. He picked up a small, folded square of tracing-paper, which he unfolded and examined with the utmost attention.

“I perceive,” said he, “that this is a rough sketch of a glass bulb. It is unlabelled. You do not miss this from your safe, Mr. Grier?”

Grier glanced at it and shook his head. “No, that is not mine. It is a poor thing. It shows the old, flawed design of a carbon filament inside a nitrogen atmosphere. Crane was working on that last month, before he abandoned it.”

Thorne folded the paper carefully and placed it in his pocket-book. He then examined the floor, the window-sill, and the lock of the door. He paused at the fireplace, where a fire had recently been extinguished. With his stick, he raked through the cold ashes.

“You have not told me, Mr. Grier,” said Thorne, without looking up, “of the relations between yourself and your apprentice. Who is the young man who works the bellows?”

“My apprentice? That is young David Locke. He is a good lad, though somewhat dull of wit. He has been with me these two years past.”

“Is he here now?”

“No, I gave him leave to go to his mother in Greenwich. He was overwrought this morning, weeping and wailing. He is a nervous boy.”

“I should be nervous, too,” said Thorne, rising to his feet and brushing the ash from his knees, “if I had sold my master’s secrets. Come, Grier, let us return to your workshop. I wish to see that safe once more.”

Grier stared at him in astonishment. “You do not mean to say that David—”

“I mean to say, sir, that the truth is very often hidden in the ashes, both literally and figuratively. Now, let us see the safe.”

We returned to the first workshop. Thorne requested that Grier open the iron door. He did so, and Thorne leaned inside, running his long, sensitive fingers over the interior of the box and examining the mechanism of the lock.

“The key,” said Thorne, “was not a duplicate. It was the original. You have it, Grier?”

“I have it here,” said Grier, producing a small, brass key from his waistcoat pocket. “I carry it always.”

“Then you lent it to your apprentice?”

“Never!”

“Then how,” said Thorne, his voice suddenly sharp and commanding, “did this young David Locke manage to open the safe yesterday evening, whilst you were at your supper in the parlour? I observe, Grier, that the lock is of an old and simple pattern. A smear of tallow on the key, followed by an impression in soft wax, would easily provide a mould. The boy has been with you two years. He has had ample opportunity.”

Grier’s face went white. “Good heavens, Mr. Thorne, you cannot be serious!”

“I am never more serious than when I am examining a cold hearth,” said Thorne. “In Crane’s fireplace, I found the remains of a letter, partially burned. It was addressed to Crane, and it was in the handwriting of your apprentice. In it, the boy offered to sell the drawings of your lamp for fifty pounds. Crane, it seems, was to pay the money this morning.”

“But the drawings!” cried Grier. “They are gone from the safe!”

“They are gone, but they are not with Crane. When we entered Crane’s workshop, you saw his travelling-bag. It was half-packed. He was preparing to flee, yes, but he was fleeing without the drawings, because he had not yet received them. The boy, Locke, was to bring them this morning. But the boy, it seems, was overwrought. You said he was weeping. He had a fit of conscience, or he raised his price, and Crane, fearing the game was up, packed his bag and ran. But he left his rough sketches behind, and he left his half-eaten supper.”

“But where are my drawings now?” Grier demanded, his voice trembling.

“They are in young David Locke’s pocket,” said Thorne, with a grim smile. “Or, more likely, they are hidden beneath the mattress of his bed in Greenwich. The boy could not bring himself to part with them, and he could not bring himself to return them. He was caught between his greed and his fear. He wept, and he ran to his mother.”

Grier sank into a chair, his face buried in his hands. “This is a terrible thing,” he murmured. “The boy was like a son to me.”

“He was an apprentice,” said Thorne, coldly. “And apprentices, in my experience, are no more immune to temptation than dukes or dustmen. The police must be sent to Greenwich immediately. I have the address from the parish register, which I consulted this morning before coming to you. I fancy we shall find the drawings intact, and young Locke in a state of pitiable terror. As for Crane, he is a rogue, but he is a rogue without his prize. He has fled from a shadow.”

Thorne turned to me, his grey eyes twinkling beneath the brim of his hat.

“Come, Doctor,” said he, “I think our work is done here. We have caught the thief, and we have cleared the rival. It is a two to one chance that the police will have the drawings before nightfall. And now, if you please, I have a hansom waiting, and I think the fog is thick enough to excuse a glass of hot brandy and water at our club.”
