It was upon a raw, foggy morning in the latter days of November, 1894, that the matter of the Harley Street surgeon was brought to my attention. I had risen early and was standing by the window of my friend’s rooms, watching the yellow fog roll down the street, when a hansom clattered up to the door and a young man, muffled to the ears in an ulster, sprang out. A moment later our landlady ushered him in.

He was a slight, pale fellow, with the anxious air of a student, and his left hand, which grasped his wet hat, bore the tell-tale ink-stain upon the middle finger.

“Mr. Vance,” said my friend, glancing at the card which had been sent up. “Pray take a chair. You are from the house of Dr. Stephen Hartley, if I am not mistaken.”

Our visitor started. “Yes, sir. I am Arthur Vance, the junior assistant. But how—”

“The mud upon your boots is of a peculiar yellowish clay, to be found in only one district north of Oxford Street just now, owing to some drainage works. Again, there is a faint but unmistakable odour of iodoform about your coat, which suggests the operating theatre rather than the mere consulting room. But you have not come here at eight in the morning to discuss my methods. What is amiss at Hartley’s?”

Our visitor’s face grew very grave. “Dr. Hartley is lying unconscious in his locked consulting room, sir. And I fear there has been foul play.”

In a few hurried sentences he laid the strange case before us. Dr. Stephen Hartley, a surgeon of high reputation, had been discovered at six that morning by his housekeeper. His consulting room, situated at the back of the house on the ground floor, was locked from the inside. The key had been turned in the lock, and the heavy bolt drawn. Upon forcing the window, they had found the surgeon stretched upon the Turkish rug, breathing stertorously, with all the marks of some powerful narcotic. Beside him lay his surgical instrument case, thrown open, and it was speedily noticed that one of the smaller scalpels was missing.

Mr. Hubert Leslie, the senior partner, who shared the house, had been immediately summoned. He had examined the room and declared that nothing else was missing—neither the silver, nor a considerable sum of money in the desk, nor the patient’s fees that had been collected the day before. The affair was a profound mystery, and Leslie was for sending at once for the police. Vance, however, had begged for an hour’s delay, and had come straight to my friend.

“One moment,” said my friend, as Vance paused for breath. “You say Mr. Leslie insists that nothing was taken but the scalpel. Do you share that opinion?”

The young man hesitated. His fingers twitched upon his knee. “I do not, sir. I have my own reasons for thinking that something else has been tampered with.”

“And that is?”

“The records, sir. The case-books in the surgery. I have charge of them, as a rule, and I know every entry. Last night, before I left, there was a particular entry concerning a patient—a Lady Mowbray—who is to undergo an operation on Tuesday. This morning, when I looked at the book, the page had been altered. The dosage of the anaesthetic had been changed. It was cleverly done, but I know the ink of the original entry, and I can see where a fresh line has been drawn.”

My friend nodded briskly. “Come,” said he, snatching up his overcoat. “We must not lose a moment. Watson, I shall be glad of your company, for I may need a medical opinion. Vance, we will follow you in another hansom.”

It was but a short drive to the handsome, pillared house in the quiet square. We were ushered into a hall where the air was heavy with the scent of carbolic. A tall, grey-bearded man, with a heavy gold chain across his waistcoat, came forward to meet us. It was Mr. Leslie, the partner. His manner was bluff and masterful.

“So you have brought a detective, Vance?” said he, with a shade of annoyance in his voice. “It seems to me a great deal of fuss about a very unpleasant accident. Hartley has been overworking himself. He has taken a dose of some preparation of opium, either by accident or in a fit of depression, and in his stupor he has knocked over his instrument case. The whole thing is clear enough.”

“Perhaps,” said my friend, smoothly. “But we must examine the room.”

The consulting room was a large, handsomely furnished apartment. Dr. Hartley had been carried to his bed, and the room was empty save for ourselves. My friend walked swiftly to the door, examined the lock, and then turned his attention to the window, which had been forced. He next went down upon his hands and knees and examined the rug where the surgeon had been found. Presently he rose, with a curious smile upon his keen face.

“Well, Mr. Leslie,” said he, “your theory of an accident is, I fear, untenable. If Dr. Hartley had taken an opiate himself, he would have had no reason to lock and bolt his door before lying down. A man does not bolt himself in to go to sleep in his own consulting room. Again, the window was secured by a heavy catch, which is still in place. There is, then, no entrance from without. The room was a sealed chamber. If the narcotic was not self-administered, and no one entered, how came he by it?”

Leslie shrugged his heavy shoulders. “He may have locked the door because he wished to be private, and then taken the dose.”

“Then why is the scalpel missing? A man who swallows a drug does not usually dispose of a small surgical instrument. No, no, the scalpel is the key to the mystery. Let us see the case-book, Vance.”

The young assistant produced the volume. My friend turned to the page and examined it for a long time through his lens. “Yes,” said he, at last, “the entry has been altered. The figure denoting the amount of chloroform has been touched up. The original writer used a pen with a very broad nib, the man who altered it used a fine one. The ink, too, is different. Now, who had access to this book?”

“I have it in my charge,” said Vance. “But it is kept in the surgery, which is common ground. Mr. Leslie or Dr. Hartley might have consulted it at any time.”

My friend turned suddenly upon the senior partner. “Mr. Leslie,” said he, “I observe that you have a small cut upon the second finger of your left hand.”

Leslie started visibly. “I cut it last night, trimming a bandage,” said he, shortly.

“Trimming a bandage does not usually leave a cut of that peculiar shape,” said my friend. “It is a clean, thin incision, such as a very sharp blade would make. Indeed, I should say it was made by a surgeon’s scalpel.”

The man’s face went white. “What do you mean?” he cried.

“I mean that this case is simple enough when one traces the chain of events. Dr. Hartley discovered, last night, that you had altered the case-book. He sent for you, or you came to his room. He taxed you with it. You are to operate upon Lady Mowbray on Tuesday, and you have increased the dose of anaesthetic. Why? Because you are in desperate need of money, and Lady Mowbray has a life insurance policy, in which you are interested. You knew that if the dose were fatal, it would be put down to an accident at the operation. Dr. Hartley saw through your plot. He was a man of strict honour. He told you that he must expose you. You had your instrument case with you, for you had been preparing for the operation. In the struggle, you snatched out a scalpel, not to use upon him, but to dispose of it, for it was the instrument with which you had sharpened the pen-nib to alter the record. You had used the scalpel as a pen-knife. Then, with the chloroform which you had brought for your fell purpose, you overpowered him. You locked and bolted the door to give yourself time to escape and to make it appear that he had shut himself in. You threw the scalpel out of the window into the area below, where I have no doubt it now lies among the laurel bushes.”

“By heaven, you are right!” cried Vance, who had rushed to the window. “Here it is!”

Leslie sank into a chair, his face livid, his hands clenched. “You have guessed it,” he muttered. “He guessed it last night. I thought I could persuade him. But he was adamant. I had the chloroform in my pocket. I did not mean to use it, but when he said the word ‘police’ I lost my head. The rest is as you say.”

“And the altered record?” I asked.

“It was the one flaw in my plan,” said Leslie, dully. “I did not know that the boy knew the ink so well. I thought I could make it appear that Hartley himself had made a mistake in the entry.”

“Well, the matter is now clear,” said my friend, turning to me. “ “You see, Watson, it all hung upon the missing scalpel. A man does not throw away a valuable instrument unless he has a strong reason. The reason was that it had been used for a purpose for which it was never intended, and which would betray the user. The rest was merely a question of observing Mr. Leslie’s hand and putting two and two together.”

He rang the bell for the police, and as we walked away, he turned to me with a shrug. “A common case enough, Watson, in its essentials. Greed, a struggle, a locked door. But it shows, does it not, that there is nothing so unnatural as the commonplace?”
