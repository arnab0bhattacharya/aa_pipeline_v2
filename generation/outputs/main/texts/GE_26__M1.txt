It is a truth, sufficiently attested by the annals of our provincial history, that the most violent revolutions are not always those which overturn thrones and alter the maps of empires, but rather the slow, creeping alterations that unmake the small, familiar world of the individual. For the cottage of old Amos Pargeter, that low-roofed dwelling of brick and timber which had stood at the end of Draper’s Lane since before the memory of the oldest inhabitant, was as much a world to him as England is to an Englishman; and the new railway, that iron serpent whose promised course lay directly through his garden, appeared to him not as a harbinger of progress, but as a visitation of barbarian hordes, intent upon the desecration of a sacred ground.

Amos, a man whose seventy years had been spent in the patient, rhythmic labour of the loom, possessed that particular form of obstinacy which is born not of ignorance, but of a deep, unyielding attachment to the past. To him, the threads of his life—the warp of his father’s toil and the weft of his own silent days—were woven into the very floorboards of that cottage. The offer of the railway company, a sum that would have seemed a fortune to him in his youth, was to his mind a piece of impertinence, a vulgar attempt to purchase a soul with coin. He sat in his doorway, his shuttle idle in his lap, and watched the surveyors with a gaze of such profound, silent hostility that even the boldest of them felt a chill, as if they were disturbing the sleep of some ancient, earth-bound spirit.

The resentment of his neighbours, however, grew with the speed of the coming railway itself. The men of the village, who had been offered fair prices for their own plots and had accepted with varying degrees of reluctance, now saw in Amos’s refusal a threat to their own prosperity. It was whispered in the public-house, and confirmed at the pump, that the company might yet divert the line, leaving their sold land worthless and their cottages standing beside a field of weeds. "He doan't think of nobody but 'isself," grumbled Seth Hodges, the blacksmith, his voice thick with ale and grievance. "A stubborn old fool, 'e is. Makin' trouble for all on us, 'e is." The common sentiment, which is so often a compound of self-interest and a vague sense of injustice, solidified against the weaver, turning him from a respected elder into a public nuisance.

It was in this atmosphere of rising discord that the Reverend Mr. Thorne was prevailed upon to act. Mr. Thorne, a man of gentle disposition and considerable learning, whose sermons were more noted for their classical allusions than for their evangelical fire, viewed the conflict with a philosopher’s weary sympathy. He saw the clash not of right and wrong, but of two legitimate, yet irreconcilable, claims: the claim of the past upon the heart of an old man, and the claim of the future upon the livelihood of a community. Nevertheless, he was a man of duty, and so, one crisp autumn afternoon, he set out for the weaver’s cottage, his mind already composing the gentle, persuasive arguments he would employ.

He found Amos at his loom, the rhythmic *thump-thump* of the heddles filling the small, dark room with a sound that was as much a part of the place as the smell of wool and beeswax. The weaver did not stop his work at the parson’s entrance, but merely inclined his head, his eyes fixed on the growing bolt of grey cloth.

"A fine piece of work, Master Pargeter," Mr. Thorne began, his voice soft. "It is a pleasure to see such craft preserved."

Amos grunted, the sound lost in the clatter of the loom. "It'll be the last on it," he said, his Warwickshire accent broad and flat. "When they've torn the 'ouse down, there'll be no loom, an' no cloth, an' no Amos Pargeter."

"That is precisely the matter I wish to discuss," the parson said, taking a seat on a low stool. "The railway is an unstoppable force, a manifestation of the age's great engine of change. To resist it entirely is like a single reed trying to turn back a flood. It is a noble sentiment, your love for this place, a sentiment I deeply respect. But consider the consequence of your refusal. It is not merely your own fate that hangs in the balance, but the peace and prosperity of your neighbours. You have been offered a sum that would secure your comfort for the rest of your days. Is it not a form of charity, then, to accept it, and so allow your friends to reap the benefit of their own arrangements?"

Amos stopped the loom. The sudden silence was more profound than the noise had been. He turned slowly, his face a mask of ancient sorrow and defiance. "Charity?" he said, the word a bitter pill on his tongue. "They calls it progress. I calls it theft. They're stealin' my ground. My father, an' 'is father afore 'im, they lies in the churchyard, but their sweat's in this soil. It's not a piece of land, Parson. It's a book, an' every furrow's a line writ by God an' my kin. You talk of money. What's money to a man who's losin' 'is 'istory? You can't buy that back. An' these neighbours... they'd sell their own grandmothers for a new coat, they would. They don't understand. It's not about the money. It's about what's right."

Mr. Thorne felt the inadequacy of his prepared speech. He was arguing the calculus of social good against the poetry of a man's soul. He saw that for Amos, this was not a matter of negotiation, but of faith. To surrender the cottage would be to admit that the life he had lived, the values he had inherited, were of no account in the face of a new and louder world. The parson sighed, the sound of a man who understands that there are truths which cannot be reconciled. He saw the slow growth of feeling in the old man's eyes—not anger now, but a deep, settled grief, the grief of one who knows he is the last of his kind.

"I see," Mr. Thorne said at last, his voice barely a whisper. "I see that I have come on a fool's errand. Forgive me." He rose to leave, and as he did, he placed a hand briefly on the old weaver's shoulder. It was a gesture of profound, helpless sympathy, an acknowledgment of a tragedy that neither of them could prevent. The weaver did not respond, but turned back to his loom, and the rhythmic *thump-thump* began again, a solitary, defiant heartbeat in the gathering dusk.
