I had been three months in the pay of Mr. Vane at his cramming establishment in Surbiton. It was a dismal house of dingy yellow brick, semi-detached, with bay windows and a front garden path composed of clinkers, and my function was to prepare four young men of sluggish intellect for the entrance examination into the Royal Military Academy at Woolwich. They were, I may say without unfairness, singularly unteachable young men. Their minds were as resistant to knowledge as a duck’s back to water, and they sat before me with a blank, patient imbecility that was, I assure you, profoundly discouraging to a man who had lately entertained ambitions in the direction of original research.

I had come to this pass through my own indiscretion. I had been an assistant master at a large and flourishing private school in the west of England, where I had imparted the rudiments of physical science with considerable success, until the headmaster—a narrow, clerical person with a talent for amassing money—discovered that I had been setting before my boys certain elementary facts relating to the descent of man. I had spoken of the Neanderthal skull, of the implements of the river-drift, and of the biological principles set forth by Mr. Darwin. I had done so with restraint, and with a proper regard for the susceptibilities of the local clergy, but the thing was discovered and made a scandal of. I was given to understand that my teaching was of a character to unsettle the religious mind, and my engagement was terminated. I came up to London with very little money, a degree from London University, and a profound contempt for the ordinary conventions of schoolmastering. The post with Mr. Vane was the only one that offered itself immediately, and I took it.

My pupils were the sons of prosperous but unimaginative parents. They were destined for the artillery, and their sole concern was to scrape through the minimum standard of elementary mathematics and fortification. I plied them with textbooks, with Euclid, with French verbs, and with the barest outlines of English history, but it was like pouring water into a sieve. They sat and stared at me, or made surreptitious notes upon the margins of their textbooks, and once a week Mr. Vane himself, a large, red-faced man with a moustache and an air of harassed respectability, came in and heard them recite, and went away shaking his head.

It was in the second week of October that the girl first appeared. I had given my four dullards a problem in vulgar fractions to wrestle with, and was sitting at my desk near the window, staring out at the dreary street, when I became aware of a young woman standing at the garden gate. She was looking towards the window with a direct, inquiring gaze that I found somewhat disconcerting. She was, I observed, a person of singularly alert and intelligent appearance, with a pale, rather eager face and dark hair simply done. She carried a bundle of books under one arm. After a moment’s hesitation, she opened the gate, walked up the clinker path, and rang the bell.

I heard Mrs. Vane, the crammer’s wife, answer the door, and then a low, pleasant voice inquiring something. Presently Mrs. Vane put her head into the schoolroom and informed me that a young person wished to speak with me. I went out into the narrow hall and found her standing there with an air of perfect self-possession.

“You are Mr. Carruthers,” she said. It was not a question, but a statement. “My name is Eleanor Penton. My brother is one of your pupils.”

I acknowledged this. Young Penton was perhaps the most hopeless of my four, a stolid, pink-faced youth who stared at me with the eyes of a bewildered ox.

“I have been looking at his textbooks,” she went on, with a disarming directness, “and I found among them a copy of Huxley’s *Lay Sermons*. I believe it is yours. It has your name on the flyleaf.”

I admitted that it was. I had lent it to Penton in a moment of misguided optimism, hoping it might awaken some flicker of intellectual curiosity in him. He had returned it to me the next day with the remark that it was “rather stiff reading.”

“I have read it,” said Miss Penton, “and I have read the *Man’s Place in Nature*, and I have read Mr. Darwin’s *Origin of Species* twice. I wondered whether you would lend me something more.”

I stared at her. It is a curious thing, in the profession of teaching, how one comes to associate intellectual curiosity entirely with the male mind, and how the appearance of it in a woman strikes one as something extraordinary and almost improper. Here was this young woman, standing in the narrow hall of a suburban cramming establishment, asking for books on biology with an earnestness that I had never encountered in any of my male pupils. She was dressed neatly in a dark skirt and a plain jacket, and she held her bundle of books with a kind of reverence that I found touching.

“I should be very glad to lend you anything I have,” said I. “But I must warn you that my library is a small one. I am a poor man, and my books are mostly technical.”

She smiled at that, a quick, bright smile. “Technical books are precisely what I want,” she said. “I have exhausted the popular ones.”

From that beginning there sprang up an odd and, I suppose, unconventional acquaintance. She came to the schoolroom on two afternoons a week, while her brother was at his mathematics, and sat at the back of the room reading whatever I had lent her. She read with extraordinary speed and concentration, and she asked questions that were so shrewd and so much to the point that I found myself, for the first time since my dismissal, engaged in intellectual conversation of a quality I had supposed no longer accessible to me. She had read Tyndall on heat, and Clifford on the nature of things, and she had even struggled through a German pamphlet on embryology with the aid of a dictionary. Her mind was quick, receptive, and singularly free from that vague sentimentality which one is accustomed to associate with the female intellect. She wanted facts, and she wanted them precisely.

I found myself, as the weeks went on, looking forward to her visits with an eagerness that rather surprised me. The dull routine of the schoolroom, the stupid faces of my four pupils, the depressing atmosphere of suburban respectability—all these were made tolerable by the prospect of an hour’s talk with a mind that was genuinely alive. We discussed the formation of coral reefs, and the evidence for the existence of a continent in the Pacific, and the latest discoveries in spectroscopy, and the bearing of all these things upon the general conception of the universe. She sat opposite me at my desk, with her books spread out before her, and her eyes—very bright and dark and attentive—fixed upon my face, and I forgot the clinker path and the bay window and the smell of damp serge and ink that pervaded Mr. Vane’s establishment.

I was walking home one evening in December, through the gas-lit streets of Surbiton, turning over in my mind a question she had raised about the variation of species under domestication, when it suddenly occurred to me that I had not, in all these weeks, seen her brother exhibit the slightest interest in any subject whatsoever. Young Penton sat before me day after day, a monument of stolid indifference, and his sister, who had been denied the education that was being wasted upon him, came furtively to borrow books and ask questions. The irony of the thing struck me with considerable force. Here was the educational system of England in miniature: the dull boy crammed at great expense for an examination he would probably never pass, and the bright girl left to pick up knowledge as best she might in back parlours and schoolroom corners.

I mentioned this to her once, tentatively, for I was never sure how far one might go in such matters. She looked at me with a faint amusement.

“Oh, I don’t complain,” she said. “I have had a far better education than my brother, in the things that matter. But nobody knows it, and nobody would care if they did know it. That is the advantage of being a woman in England. One may learn as much as one pleases, provided one does not pretend that it is of any use.”

I laughed, and she laughed too, and then we went back to the question of the recapitulation theory, which was what we had been discussing when I made my unfortunate observation.

It was about this time that I began to notice a change in young Penton. He had always been a heavy, lethargic boy, given to staring out of the window and eating buns surreptitiously during the preparation of French exercises. But now he began to show a certain uneasiness, a tendency to look at me furtively, and once or twice he broke out into a kind of clumsy jocularity that was quite foreign to his usual manner. I suspected at first that he had discovered my lending his sister books, and that he was embarrassed by it. But the truth, when it came out, was far more absurd.

He stayed behind one afternoon, after the others had gone, and stood before my desk with an air of sheepish bravado.

“I say, Carruthers,” he said, “I suppose you know you’re making rather an ass of yourself over my sister?”

I confess I was taken aback. I stared at him.

“Making an ass of myself?” I repeated.

“Well, you know what I mean. Coming to the house, and lending her books, and all that sort of thing. The mater’s been talking about it. She says it’s not the thing at all.”

I was, I think, more amused than angry. The notion that my intellectual intercourse with Miss Penton could be construed as a courtship seemed to me exquisitely ridiculous. I told him, with what restraint I could command, that his sister came to borrow books and to discuss scientific questions, and that there was nothing in the least improper about it.

“Oh, I dare say,” he said, with a knowing leer that was extraordinarily disagreeable on his pink, stupid face. “But you know what people say. A fellow in your position can’t be too careful.”

I dismissed him shortly, and sat for a long time staring at the fire in the grate, thinking of the girl’s bright, eager face, and of the narrow, stuffy world in which she lived, and of the monstrous absurdity of a social system that could turn a mind like hers into a subject for tittle-tattle, and a dullard like her brother into its guardian.
