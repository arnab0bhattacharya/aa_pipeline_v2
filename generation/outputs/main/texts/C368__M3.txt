The rain fell in a steady, soot-blackened drizzle upon Whitehall, turning the paving stones of Old Scotland Yard to a slick, treacherous grease. Inside the cramped, gas-lit offices of Vance Inquiries, Mr. Arthur Vance was listening to a young woman whose red-rimmed eyes spoke of sleepless nights. 

"He swears he did not do it, Mr. Vance," Eleanor said, her gloved hands clasped so tightly in her lap that her knuckles shone white. "Edmund is many things—he is proud, and he is ambitious—but he is not a traitor. They say he sold the contents of the Harwich Fleet Deployment report to a foreign agent. If he is dismissed, he will not only lose his position at the Admiralty; he will stand trial at the Old Bailey."

Vance, a man of methodical habits and a quiet, piercing gaze, leaned back in his leather chair. "The evidence against Mr. Hollis appears damning, Miss Eleanor. He was the only junior clerk entrusted with transcribing the report. He was seen leaving the building with the document in his dispatch case, and by the time he returned it to the registry, the contents were known to an embassy across the Channel."

"He took his lunch in St. James’s Park, as he always does," Eleanor pleaded. "He swears the case never left his sight. He ate his sandwich upon a bench, watching the nursemaids, and then he returned. He did not speak to a soul."

Vance sighed, picking up his umbrella. The Admiralty was a fortress of red tape and brass buttons, but a fortress nonetheless. Intrigue within its walls was rarely a matter of grand espionage; it was usually born of petty grievances and overlooked details. "I will need to see his office, Miss Eleanor. And I will need to observe his colleagues."

The following morning, Vance presented himself at the Admiralty under the guise of an auditor from the Treasury, dispatched to review the expenditure of stationery—a subterfuge that granted him access to the lower clerical floors. The room where Edmund Hollis had worked was a vast, cavernous space, echoing with the scratch of steel nibs and the occasional clearing of throats. Rows of high desks stretched toward the high windows, beneath which sat junior civil servants, their sleeves stained with ink, their collars frayed.

Vance’s attention, however, was drawn not to the desks, but to a corner of the room where a new copying machine had been installed. It was a heavy, cast-iron contraption, a mimeograph that required a dedicated operator. The Admiralty had recently hired a man specifically for this role, a copying clerk by the name of Mr. Silas Crook.

Crook was a thin, sallow-faced man with a permanent stoop, as though he had been shaped by the very machine he tended. His hands were perpetually stained a deep, indelible violet from the ink used in the stencils. He worked with a rhythmic, mechanical precision, turning the large drum and feeding sheets of paper through the press. But what caught Vance’s ear was not the clatter of the machine. It was the sound that accompanied it.

Crook was whistling.

It was not a jaunty tune, nor a popular music-hall melody. It was a strange, atonal sequence of notes, rising and falling in a pattern that seemed to have no beginning and no end. It was a sound that wormed its way into the consciousness, subtle yet persistent. As Vance moved through the room, ostensibly checking ledger books and counting reams of foolscap, he found himself pausing near the copying machine, listening.

"Mr. Crook," Vance said, raising his voice over the clatter. "A diligent worker, I see."

Crook looked up, his small, dark eyes blinking behind a pair of steel-rimmed spectacles. "I do my duty, sir," he said, his voice flat and colorless. "The machine requires constant attention."

"And does it require whistling?" Vance asked, a faint smile on his lips.

Crook’s mouth clamped shut. "A habit, sir. Helps the rhythm of the work."

Vance nodded, making a note in his ledger, and moved on. But the whistling resumed almost immediately, that same peculiar, tuneless drone.

Over the next two days, Vance conducted his inquiry with meticulous patience. He interviewed the porters, the messengers, and the other clerks. He learned that Edmund Hollis had indeed taken the Harwich report to St. James’s Park. He had sat on a bench, his dispatch case resting beside him, while he ate his lunch. A foreign agent, it was rumored, had been seen in the vicinity, a man with a heavy gait and a furled umbrella.

But Vance could not shake the sound of Crook’s whistling. It was a code, he was certain of it. A method of transmitting information without drawing attention. But if Crook was the leak, how had he obtained the report? Hollis had sworn the document never left his sight.

On the third evening, Vance returned to the Admiralty after the clerks had departed. The building was quiet, save for the footsteps of the night watchmen. Vance made his way to the copying room and examined the mimeograph. He ran his fingers over the drum, noting the residue of violet ink. He opened the waste drawer beneath the machine and found a heap of discarded stencil sheets, their wax surfaces scarred with the impressions of typewritten text.

He carried a stack of these stencils to the gas lamp and examined them closely. Most were routine correspondence—requisitions for coal, requests for leave. But near the bottom of the pile, he found a stencil that made his breath catch.

It was the Harwich Fleet Deployment report.

The text was incomplete, fragmented. Words were missing, phrases were broken. But the essential details—the ship names, the dates, the coordinates—were there, pressed into the wax. Vance held the stencil to the light and saw that the missing words were not merely absent; they had been deliberately obscured, as if the stencil had been made from a document that had been partially concealed.

He sat back on his heels, his mind racing. The stencil had not been made from the original report, nor from a complete copy. It had been made from a reflection.

Vance returned to the copying room the next morning, arriving before the clerks. He positioned himself near the window, where the morning light fell across the rows of desks. When Crook arrived, Vance watched him closely. Crook hung his coat, donned his ink-stained apron, and began to prepare the machine. Then, as was his habit, he began to whistle.

Vance walked to the desk where Edmund Hollis had sat on the day of the alleged theft. He examined the surface, the inkwell, the blotter. Then he looked at the window. The glass was old, slightly warped, but it was not the window that interested him. It was the brass inkwell, highly polished, sitting upon Hollis’s desk.

Vance picked up the inkwell and turned it in the light. The curved brass surface acted as a mirror, albeit a distorted one. But a mirror nonetheless. If a document were placed upon the desk, face up, the brass inkwell would reflect its contents to anyone sitting at the adjacent copying desk—provided they had a line of sight.

Vance turned slowly and looked at Crook’s machine. From that vantage point, the angle was perfect. Crook, sitting at his mimeograph, could see the reflection of any document placed upon Hollis’s desk in the polished brass of the inkwell.

But a reflection was reversed, and at a distance, the text would be difficult to read. That was where the whistling came in.

Vance observed Crook throughout the morning. Whenever a senior clerk placed a sensitive document upon Hollis’s old desk—a practice that had continued in Hollis’s absence—Crook’s whistling would change. The pitch would rise and fall, the rhythm would shift. It was not a tune. It was a cipher. A method of encoding the reversed, fragmented text he could see in the inkwell’s reflection.

Crook was not memorizing the documents. He was transcribing them, in real time, into a series of whistled notes. And somewhere, in another building, perhaps even across the street, another agent was listening, transcribing the whistled code into text, which was then sold to the highest bidder.

Vance smiled, a thin, cold smile. He had seen enough.

He left the Admiralty and made his way to the office of Sir Gerald Wenthworth, the Permanent Secretary. He laid out his findings with the precision of a military map. The brass inkwell, the angle of reflection, the whistling cipher, and the discarded stencil in the waste drawer.

Sir Gerald listened in silence, his face growing darker with each word. When Vance finished, the Secretary rose from his desk and summoned the head of building security.

Within the hour, Silas Crook was escorted from the Admiralty by two unsmiling men from the Special Branch. His violet-stained hands were cuffed behind his back, and his mouth, for the first time in weeks, was silent.

Edmund Hollis was exonerated three days later. The charges of espionage were dropped, and he was restored to his position, though he was transferred to a different department, away from the copying room and its treacherous angles of reflection.

Vance received a letter of gratitude from Eleanor, written in a flowing, elegant hand. She and Edmund were to be married in the spring, she wrote, and they would be honored if Mr. Vance would attend the wedding.

Vance read the letter twice, then folded it neatly and placed it in his desk drawer. He rose, took his umbrella, and stepped out into the rain-soaked streets of Whitehall, where the only sounds were the clatter of horse hooves and the distant, mournful call of a foghorn on the Thames.
