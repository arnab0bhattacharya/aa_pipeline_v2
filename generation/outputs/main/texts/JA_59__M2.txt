The arrival of Mr. Arthur Penhallow at the gates of Pemberley Vale was an event that, by all rights, ought to have been received with the measured relief appropriate to a brother returned from the rigors of Eton. Yet the household of the Penhallows found it difficult to suppress a certain collective grimace, for Arthur had not merely returned; he had returned with a fervor that bordered on the theatrical, and a secret that he was already proving ill-equipped to keep. It was not his academic prospects, nor his health, that commanded the immediate attention of his sister, Miss Elara Penhallow, but the fact that he had not once, in the three hours since his arrival, ceased to stare into the middle distance with an expression of such profound and misplaced rapture that Elara began to suspect him of a fever, or perhaps a sudden and dangerous loss of his wits.

It was only when the family had settled into the drawing room, surrounded by the comfortable chaos of unpacking luggage and the clinking of tea-cups, that the mystery was laid bare. Arthur, who had previously been a model of reserved propriety, rose from his chair with a gravity that belied his nineteen years. He addressed his father, a man of considerable property and limited patience, with a solemnity that Elara found both alarming and, against her better judgment, faintly amusing.

“Father,” said Arthur, his voice trembling slightly with the weight of his own conviction, “I must confess to you a state of mind that I have struggled to conceal, but which is now impossible to hide. I am, in the most literal and irrevocable sense, in love with Miss Halloway, the governess at the residence of our neighbor, Mr. Thistleton.”

The silence that followed was not the silence of shock, but the silence of a room in which several people are simultaneously deciding whether to faint, to laugh, or to intervene with a stern rebuke. Mr. Penhallow, who had been in the act of pouring himself a second glass of port, paused, the bottle hovering in mid-air. His eyes widened, not with surprise at the sentiment, but with horror at the implication of the social calculus. Mrs. Penhallow, seated by the fire, looked up from her embroidery, her needle poised like a dagger above a piece of silk.

“Miss Halloway,” repeated Mr. Penhallow, as if tasting the words and finding them sour. “The woman who instructs the Thistleton children in geography and French? Arthur, are you aware of what you are saying?”

“Perfectly, Father,” replied Arthur, with a stubbornness that Elara knew well was inherited from their late uncle. “I have observed her conduct, her intelligence, and her virtue. She is the only person in Kent who speaks to me as if I were a rational being, rather than a pocketbook with legs. I find her presence to be the sole source of my happiness.”

Elara, who sat near the window, allowed herself a small, internal smile. She had known Miss Halloway for several years, having engaged in long and stimulating conversations about the state of the country’s roads and the merits of different authors. She knew the governess to be a woman of sharp wit and considerable pride, a quality that made her ill-suited to the servile station in which she was placed, but well-suited to the company of those who could appreciate her intellect. It was not that Elara disapproved of the match; indeed, she suspected that Miss Halloway might be more suitable to Arthur than any heiress of equal station, provided one could overlook the disparity in their social standing. However, she was well aware of the prejudices that governed their circle. To marry a governess was not merely to descend in rank; it was to invite ridicule from every aunt, cousin, and neighbor for the rest of one’s natural life.

Her father, however, did not seem inclined to appreciate the nuances of the situation.

“You are young, Arthur,” he said, his voice dropping to that low, dangerous register that Elara had learned to fear. “You are impressionable. A woman who earns her living by teaching children to conjugate verbs is not a suitable companion for a gentleman of your standing. This is a folly that will bring disgrace upon our name. You will not see her again.”

Arthur’s face flushed, but he did not bow. “I cannot command my feelings, Father. And I doubt whether you can command mine. If I am to be denied, then I must ask you to consider that my happiness is not a trivial matter to be dismissed with a wave of the hand.”

Mrs. Penhallow let out a small, strangled sound. “Arthur! How dare you speak to your father in such a manner! It is quite unbecoming. And what about the Thistletons? How do you suppose they will take this? They are respectable people, though not of our exact circle, but to have their employed person taken from them by a young man who has just been at school—it is the height of indiscretion.”

Elara, who had been watching the scene with the detached interest of a spectator at a play, felt a sudden pang of sympathy for her brother. He was foolish, certainly, and his timing was abysmal, but there was a raw honesty in his attachment that she found refreshing in a world so often governed by calculation. She looked at Miss Halloway’s portrait, which hung unnoticed in the hallway, and thought of the last time they had walked together in the garden. They had discussed the price of grain and the quality of the local clay, and Arthur had listened with an intensity that Elara had previously reserved only for his cricket matches. It was not a grand, sweeping romance, but it was grounded in a mutual respect that Elara, in her own experience of the marriage market, had found to be rare.

“Arthur,” said Elara, breaking the tense silence with a calmness that surprised them all. “Perhaps you are being too hasty in your declarations, and too harsh in your refusals. But Father is right that the situation requires delicacy. You cannot simply announce your intentions without considering the consequences for Miss Halloway. If she is to be removed from her position, it must be done with care and compensation, not as a punishment for your infatuation.”

Mr. Penhallow looked at his daughter as if she had suggested they sell the estate to pay for a new carriage. “Elara, are you taking his side? Are you encouraging this madness?”

“I am taking the side of good sense,” Elara replied, turning her gaze to her father. “If Arthur is truly in love, and if Miss Halloway is a woman of merit, then to forbid the relationship without addressing the practicalities is merely to create a scandal. If, on the other hand, Arthur is merely infatuated, then a period of separation will cure him. But you cannot cure infatuation by shouting. You must be patient. And you must be polite to the Thistletons. I suggest that you wait until the term is over before you make any formal decision. It is the end of the month, after all. There is time to think.”

Arthur looked at his sister with a mixture of relief and adoration. “You are a friend, Elara. I did not know whether to trust you or not.”

“Trust me,” she said, with a smile that held a hint of warning, “and do not write to her tonight. I suspect you have already started a letter, and if I find it, I shall be forced to burn it.”

Arthur’s face fell, and he looked down at his hands, which were indeed stained with ink. Elara felt a surge of affection for the boy who was no longer quite a child. He was making a mistake, perhaps a large one, but it was a mistake made from the heart, not from greed or vanity. She looked at her father, who was still fuming, and at her mother, who was dabbing her eyes with a handkerchief, though she did not appear to be weeping. The drawing room was quiet, save for the ticking of the clock and the distant sound of a carriage passing on the lane. Elara knew that the battle was not over. There would be more lectures, more sighs, and more visits from the aunt who had recently arrived from London to oversee the household. But for now, the immediate crisis was averted. Arthur would remain at home, and Miss Halloway would continue to teach the Thistleton children their sums. And Elara, who had always prided herself on her ability to navigate the treacherous waters of social expectation, found herself wondering if, in the end, the most radical act of all was simply to allow a young man to be foolish, and to hope that his foolishness might lead him to something true. She poured herself a cup of tea, the steam rising in delicate curls, and decided that she would write to Miss Halloway herself, not to intervene, but to inquire after her health, and to see if, by any chance, she had also been listening to the distant sound of a young man’s heart breaking against the stones of Pemberley Vale.
