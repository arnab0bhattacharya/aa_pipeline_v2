It is a melancholy truth, ingrained in the very fibres of my heart, that the loftiest aspirations of the human intellect are oftentimes allied to the deepest degradation of our moral nature; and that the same ardent thirst for knowledge which elevates the soul above terrestrial concerns may, by a singular perversion, render us oblivious to the sacred and delicate affections which constitute the true charm of existence. Such was the reflection that forced itself upon my mind as our vessel ploughed the desolate waves of the Northern Ocean, bearing us toward that island of fire and frost whose subterranean commotions I had so long burned to investigate. I had sought in the terrific grandeur of Icelandic volcanoes a distraction from the pangs of an overwrought and disappointed spirit; and my young wife, Clara, had implored, with a fervency I could not withstand, to share the dangers of my expedition. Against my sober judgment, yet yielding to the impassioned eloquence of her appeals, I consented; and now, as I gazed upon her fragile form exposed to the terrors of the tempestuous deep, I felt that I had madly interwoven her fate with the phantom of my own ambition.

The voyage was protracted and perilous. The elements seemed to have conspired against our feeble bark, and the billows, upheaving their dark and crested heads, roared with a voice of thunder that appalled even my seasoned heart. Yet Clara stood upon the heaving deck, sustained by an enthusiasm that appeared to emanate from a source superior to mortal endurance. Her cheek, though pale, was lit by a spiritual fire; and her eye, fixed upon the tumultuous scene, betrayed no fear, but rather a deep and solemn exultation. I, who had boasted of my philosophical fortitude, retired to my cabin, overcome by physical suffering and mental gloom; while she, whom I had deemed too delicate for the rough encounters of the world, remained the unshrinking observer of the warring elements.

It was during the long and weary nights of that navigation that the humiliating conviction of her superiority forced itself upon my reluctant acknowledgment. Confined to my berth, I strove to arrange the scattered materials of my scientific inquiries, but my thoughts were chaotic, wandering, and devoid of that lucid arrangement which science demands. Clara would sit beside me, her pencil tracing upon the paper those very phenomena which my disordered senses had failed to apprehend. She noted the varying hues of the phosphoric light that played about our vessel's prow; she marked the direction of the currents, and the flight of the oceanic birds that skimmed the crests of the waves; and when she read aloud her observations, I listened with a mingled feeling of astonishment and shame. Her mind, instinct with a clear and penetrating intelligence, grasped the truths of nature with a firmness and precision to which my own excited and wayward imagination could never attain. I had deemed myself a philosopher; I was but an enthusiast, pursuing shadows, while she, with a steady and unpretending hand, gathered the flowers of truth that bloomed along the rugged path of discovery.

We approached the coast of Iceland, and the scenery that presented itself to our view was of a character singularly wild and sublime. The black and barren mountains, whose summits were veiled in mist, frowned upon the desolate shore; and the vast glaciers, stretching their icy arms into the sea, reflected the cold and melancholy light of the northern sun. We disembarked near a small and wretched hamlet, inhabited by a few hardy fishermen, whose countenances bore the stamp of their inhospitable climate. Here, amid scenes of the most profound desolation, we established our abode, preparing to penetrate into those interior regions where the volcanic fires yet struggled for mastery with the frosts of winter.

The ascent of Mount Hecla was the object of our expedition, and we undertook it with a zeal that perhaps partook more of rashness than of wisdom. The toil was immense, the cold intense, and the danger imminent; but Clara pressed onward, her spirits rising with the difficulty of the undertaking. When we reached the summit, and looked down into the crater, whence issued clouds of sulphureous vapour and the low murmurs of subterranean agitation, a scene of terrific grandeur broke upon our sight. The surrounding country, covered with ashes and lava, presented the aspect of a world ruined by some convulsion of nature; and the icy plains, stretching far away toward the horizon, gleamed with a ghastly whiteness that filled the soul with awe. I stood mute and overwhelmed, feeling the insignificance of human power in the presence of such mighty energies; but Clara, with a calm and steady gaze, examined the strata of the volcanic rock, and noted the peculiarities of the boiling springs that issued from the mountain's side. Her observations were marked by an acuteness and a comprehensiveness that left my own crude and imperfect notes far behind; and I felt, with a pang of self-reproach, that the glory of the discovery belonged less to me than to her.

We returned to England after some months of laborious and perilous research, and I found myself surrounded by the comforts of civilized life, with the task of arranging my materials for the Royal Society before me. I sat in my study, surrounded by the specimens we had collected, and by the voluminous notes that Clara had transcribed with her own hand. The paper which I was preparing was to be the crowning achievement of my philosophical career; it was to establish my reputation among the learned of Europe, and to vindicate my enterprise in the eyes of those who had deemed it chimerical. But as I cast my eye over the manuscript, I perceived that the most important and original portions were the results of Clara's observations. Her clear and logical deductions, her accurate descriptions of the volcanic phenomena, and her theory of the formation of the basaltic columns which we had discovered on the coast, formed the very soul of the work. To present it as my own would be to rob her of the honour due to her exertions; to place her name beside mine was an act of justice which my pride and my false notions of scientific proprietorship urged me to withhold.

I revolved these ideas in my mind with an anguish that I cannot describe. I reflected upon Clara's devotion during the voyage, upon her courage amid the perils of the storm, and upon the unflagging zeal with which she had pursued our inquiries amid the desolation of Iceland. I called to mind the gentle and uncomplaining patience with which she had endured the hardships of our journey, and the cheerful and animating smile which had lighted up her pale features even in the midst of the most terrific scenes. And was I now to defraud her of the meed of her toils? Was I to appropriate to myself the fruits of her intellect, and to consign her to the obscurity of a mere companion of my travels? The thought was revolting to every principle of honour and gratitude; yet a strange and perverse reluctance held me back. I feared the ridicule of my associates; I dreaded the suggestion that I had leaned upon a woman's understanding; and I shrank from the acknowledgment that my own share in the discovery was inferior to hers.

In this conflict of feelings I passed several days of intense mental suffering. I avoided Clara's society, and shut myself up in my study, striving to remodel the paper in such a manner as to diminish the prominence of her contributions. But the attempt was vain; the fabric of her reasoning was so interwoven with the whole work that to remove it was to reduce the paper to a mass of disjointed and unmeaning fragments. At length, unable to endure the torment of my own thoughts, I rushed from my study and sought Clara in her apartment. She was sitting by the window, her eyes bent upon the landscape, and her countenance wearing that expression of serene and thoughtful melancholy which was natural to it. As I approached, she turned toward me her mild and inquiring gaze, and the sight of her gentle and confiding face smote my heart with a pang of remorse.

"Clara," I said, my voice trembling with emotion, "I have done thee a grievous wrong. The paper which I have prepared for the Royal Society is, in truth, more thine than mine; and yet I have hesitated to place thy name beside mine upon the title-page. My pride, my unworthy and selfish pride, has struggled against the dictates of justice; but the struggle is over. I acknowledge thy superiority, and I will not rob thee of the honour which is thy due. Thy name shall stand with mine; and the world shall know that I am indebted to thee for the most valuable discoveries contained in this work."

She looked at me for a moment with an expression of surprise and gentle reproach, and then, with a sweet and unassuming modesty, she replied, "My dear Edward, I have never sought fame; my only ambition has been to aid thee in thy pursuits, and to share thy dangers and thy toils. If my observations have been of service to thee, I am amply rewarded; and I ask no higher honour than to be remembered as thy faithful companion. Yet, if thou thinkest it just that my name should appear with thine, I will not refuse the tribute which thou wouldst pay to my exertions; for I value thy good opinion beyond all the applause of the world."

I seized her hand and pressed it to my lips, while the tears started from my eyes. The load that had oppressed my heart was removed; and I felt that the consciousness of having acted justly was a sweeter reward than all the fame which scientific discovery could bestow. The paper was read before the Royal Society, and Clara's name appeared beside mine upon the title-page. The applause which it received was perhaps less grateful to me than the reflection that I had not abused the confidence of my gentle and high-minded wife; and I learned, by this lesson, that the truest philosophy consists not in the pursuit of abstract knowledge, but in the cultivation of those benevolent and just affections which bind human beings together in the ties of mutual respect and love.
