The door of the Athenaeum closed upon him with a soft, upholstered thud, shutting out the smell of leather and stale cigar smoke, and releasing him into the grey, wet air of Pall Mall. It was not yet ten o'clock, yet the light seemed already exhausted, a thin wash of silver that lay upon the wet pavement and the polished flanks of the waiting motorcars. Edward Marchant stood for a moment on the step, adjusting his umbrella—a nervous gesture, for there was no rain, only a fine, suspended mist that clung to the collar of his coat and dampened the crepe of his mourning band, which he wore for a colleague long dead.

As he began to walk, the pavement stretched before him, a river of grey stone flowing toward the white, pockmarked towers of the Abbey. The city was waking, but slowly, reluctantly; the sounds of the morning—the clip-clop of a cab horse, the distant, grinding shriek of a train—seemed to reach him through a layer of cotton wool. He felt very old this morning, and very brittle. The speech lay in his pocket, a weight of paper that seemed to pull at the lining of his coat, but it was not the weight of the paper he felt, but the weight of the words themselves, heavy, dense, and utterly, hopelessly inadequate.

For how could one speak of Empire and the debt, of the reconstruction of Europe and the price of coal, when the light fell in this particular way, turning the wet stones to slate and the sky to the colour of a pigeon’s wing? The words in his pocket were dry, hard things—*fiscal responsibility, the national interest*—but the world outside was fluid, shifting, dissolving even as he looked at it. He thought of the House, that great, hollowed-out vessel of noise and tradition, and he felt a sudden, violent urge to turn back, to retreat into the silence of the club library where the newspapers lay folded like shrouds. But his feet, trained by thirty years of habit, carried him onward, past the red brick of the Palace and the iron railings of the park.

And then, because the rhythm of his walking was steady and the light was soft, his mind drifted, as it always did in these moments of transit, away from the present and into the pale, sun-drenched rooms of the past.

It was not his wife he thought of first, though Helen sat in his mind like a piece of solid, comfortable furniture. Helen was at home, in Kensington, arranging flowers, or perhaps reading the paper, her mind occupied with the practicalities of the day—luncheon, the price of butter, the health of the dog. She was a good woman, a solid woman, and he loved her with the dull, reliable affection one has for a warm hearth in winter. But she was not the one who haunted him. Helen was the present; she was the chair he sat in, the bed he slept in. The one he thought of now was the ghost, the girl who had lived not in Kensington but in the imagination, in a summer that seemed to have no end.

Margaret. The name rose like a bubble, clear and round, and burst in the grey air. He had not seen her for twenty years—thirty perhaps?—and yet she was more real to him at this moment than the stone lions of Trafalgar Square. She was there, in the garden of that house in Sussex, where the bees had droned in the lavender and the light had fallen through the leaves in trembling, golden coins. He remembered the texture of her dress, a light muslin that smelled of starch and sunshine, and the way she had turned her head, laughing at something he had said—a joke, perhaps, or a foolish observation about the clouds.

What had they spoken of? It did not matter. The words were gone, lost in the drift of years, but the feeling remained: a sharp, piercing sweetness, a sense of being utterly alive, of standing on the precipice of a life that was to be vast and significant. They had believed, in that garden, that their love was the central fact of the universe, that it would change the shape of things. And now? Now she was a middle-aged woman in Yorkshire or was it Devon, married to a country squire, with children and grey hair, and he was a politician walking to Westminster to argue about the price of coal.

The absurdity of it struck him, not with a crash, but with a slow, sinking sensation. All that passion, all that intensity, had it all been for nothing? Had it simply evaporated, like the morning mist, leaving no trace? He looked at his hand on the handle of the umbrella. It was a politician’s hand, manicured, heavy with rings, the skin loose over the knuckles. It was not the hand that had trembled when it touched Margaret’s waist. The hand had aged, the passion had cooled, and the world had gone on turning, indifferent to their small drama.

A bus rumbled past, its red flank streaked with mud, and the vibration shook the pavement. Edward stepped aside to let a group of clerks pass, their heads bowed against the damp. He felt a sudden, sharp pang of envy for them, for their youth and their ignorance. They had not yet learned that the speech does not matter, that the debate is a play performed for an empty house, that the only thing that is real is the light on the wet pavement and the memory of a girl in a muslin dress.

But then, as he crossed into Parliament Square, the great stone mass of the Palace of Westminster rose up before him, vast and enduring, indifferent to the weather and to the moods of men. The clock face of Big Ben glowed palely in the gloom. It was a fortress of stone, a monument to the collective will, to the slow, grinding machinery of history. It was solid. It was real. And Edward Marchant, who had felt so fluid and so fragile a moment ago, felt himself hardening again, contracting back into the shape of the man he was required to be.

He touched the papers in his pocket. The words were there. They were dry and hard, but they were the tools of his trade. He would stand up in the Chamber, and the sound of his voice would fill the air, and the Members would listen, or pretend to listen, and the business of the nation would proceed. It was not love, and it was not beauty, but it was something. It was the task at hand.

The mist seemed to lift slightly, or perhaps he only imagined it. He straightened his shoulders, fixed his gaze upon the Members’ Entrance, and walked on, leaving the ghost of Margaret in the garden, and the silence of the club, behind him in the grey morning.
