It was upon a Tuesday evening in October that the singular meteorite shower descended upon the moors above Slackness. The sun had set in the usual murk of mill smoke, and the gas-lamps in the high street were just flaring into yellow life, when the heavens began to fall. It was not a shower of shooting stars, but a volley of iron and stone. Great masses of heated rock, trailing smoke and a dull, ruddy fire, struck the dry heather and the peat bogs with the heavy, thudding impact of artillery. Within a quarter of an hour, three separate fires were blazing on the dark slopes, and the wind, which blew steadily from the west, drove the flames in long, roaring lines toward the town.

Mr. Samuel Grindrod, the principal mill owner of Slackness, was eating his cold mutton in the substantial bay window of his house on the hillside when his under-manager, a nervous man named Jukes, came running up the drive. Grindrod was a man of large, solid build, with a heavy, clean-shaven face, and a mind entirely devoted to the regularity of his machinery and the margins of his profit. He listened to Jukes’s breathless report with an impatient clicking of his tongue.

“The moor is alight, Mr. Grindrod! The whole eastern slope is blazing. The heather is as dry as tinder. If it crosses the beck, it will take the drying-sheds, and perhaps the mill itself.”

Grindrod pushed his plate away. “Then we must put it out,” he said, with the fatigued practicality of a man who is forced to organize a fire brigade after a long day’s accounts. “Ring the bell at the engine-house. Turn out the hands from the lower shed. Tell them to take their coats and shovels. And be quick about it, Jukes. I will come myself.”

By the time Grindrod reached the edge of the town, the spectacle was extraordinary. The moor, which had been a shapeless black mass, was now a jagged, crawling furnace. The flames leapt twenty feet into the air, throwing a fierce, orange glare upon the brick fronts of the mills and the grimy faces of the operatives who were assembling in the street. The heat was palpable even at a distance. It was a savage, primeval thing, this fire from the stars, contrasting violently with the neat, mechanical life of the manufacturing town below.

The mill hands, mostly men and boys from the weaving sheds, were gathered in a disorderly, murmuring crowd. They were sullen, weary after their day’s toil, and utterly undisciplined. Grindrod, in his heavy overcoat and tall hat, pushed through them, issuing commands with the autocratic certainty of a man accustomed to being obeyed.

“Now then, lads, no loitering! You, Bates, take a dozen men and get up to the left flank. Beat out the edges. You, Hodgson, go for water. Form a line!”

But the men did not move with the required alacrity. They stood in heavy, resentful groups, blinking in the heat. A murmur went among them. The fire was too large, they said, and the wind too strong. They were not paid to fight stars. There was a disposition to hang back, to leave the danger to the owner and his foremen.

It was at this moment of hesitation that a young man stepped out from the crowd. His name was Arthur Fenwick. He had been a mechanic in the spinning-room until he had led an abortive strike for shorter hours some three months before. Since then, he had been blacklisted by every mill in the valley. He was lean, dark, and very dusty, with the sharp, restless eyes of an idealist and a thin, intelligent face. He wore a threadbare jacket, and his hands were stained with oil.

“Mr. Grindrod is giving you the wrong way of it,” said Fenwick, in a clear, carrying voice that surprised the crowd into silence. “Beating the edges is no use against a peat fire. It burns below the roots. You want to trench. You must cut a break ahead of the fire, right down to the bare clay, and let it burn itself out against the barrier.”

Grindrod turned upon him, his face reddening. “Who asked your opinion, Fenwick?” he said, angrily. “You are an idle, blacklisted agitator. Get out of the way. This is work for men, not for those who shirk their duty.”

Fenwick did not move. He looked at the owner with a calm, almost scientific curiosity. “Look at the wind, Grindrod,” he said, ignoring the prefix. “It shifts a point every ten minutes. If your men go up the left flank now, they will be caught in the open when the wind veers. You will lose the sheds, and perhaps a life or two. I have seen a peat fire before, in the Yorkshire wolds. I know the trick of it.”

There was a low murmur of assent from the crowd. The men knew Fenwick. They knew he was clever with his hands, and that he had a way of being right, even when he was inconvenient. Grindrod glared at the young mechanic, his pride battling with his common sense. The fire was roaring nearer. The heat was scorching their faces. The old order of command and obedience was breaking down in the face of a common danger.

“Give us the shovels, then,” said a heavy, grizzled man from the front rank. “I’m for trenching. Let the lad show us where.”

Grindrod hesitated. He was a man of the eighteenth century, one felt, living in the twentieth; a believer in castes and classes, in the divine right of employers. To take orders from a blacklisted striker was an outrage against the whole social fabric. But the drying-sheds were full of valuable cloth. The mill was uninsured against fire from the heavens. He swallowed his pride with a visible effort.

“Very well,” he said, stiffly. “If you think you can do better, Fenwick, take command. But I will hold you responsible for the sheds.”

Fenwick nodded, and instantly the scene changed. He became, in a flash, the directing intelligence of the crowd. His voice, which had been merely argumentative, took on a note of sharp, practical authority.

“Right,” he said. “Hodgson, take twenty men and get to the top of the ridge, where the bare rock shows. Cut a trench from the rock to the old stone wall. Make it six feet wide. The rest of you, follow me. We will make a second break below, at the edge of the gorse. We must work in pairs, one to dig, one to beat out the flying sparks. Quick, now! The wind is freshening.”

The men, galvanized by his energy, snatched up their shovels and spades and ran up the dark, smoking hillside. Fenwick led them, his thin figure darting hither and thither, pointing, directing, encouraging. Grindrod, left standing by Jukes, felt a sudden, profound sense of isolation. He was the owner of the mill, the master of two thousand hands, and he was standing idle while a discredited agitator saved his property. It was a ridiculous, an ironic situation. He almost laughed.

The next two hours were a desperate, confused struggle. The fire roared and leapt, and the heat was intense. The sky was a pall of smoke, through which the flames shot up in jagged, fantastic shapes. The moor was a wilderness of fire and shadow. But Fenwick’s tactics were sound. The trench, dug frantically by the sweating, grimy men, held. The fire, reaching the bare clay, stopped, hesitated, and then, finding no further fuel, died down into a sullen, smoking line.

It was past midnight when the danger was over. The men, exhausted and blackened, lay about on the scorched earth, panting. Fenwick, his face streaked with sweat and soot, his threadbare jacket singed at the edges, came down to where Grindrod was standing.

“It’s out,” he said, briefly. “The sheds are safe. The fire will burn itself out in the bog by morning. You can send the men home, Mr. Grindrod.”

Grindrod looked at him. He felt a curious, unwilling respect. The young man had shown a capacity for leadership, a practical intelligence, which the owner himself lacked. He was no longer merely a striker; he was a man who had done a man’s work. The old social categories seemed suddenly inadequate.

“You have done well, Fenwick,” he said, slowly. “I am obliged to you.”

He paused, and his eyes, resting on the tired, expectant faces of the men about him, held a new, thoughtful expression. The world was changing, he perceived. The hands were no longer a mere unthinking mass, to be ordered hither and thither. They had leaders, and those leaders had brains. The meteorite shower had brought down fire from the stars, but it had also revealed, in the glare of the burning moor, the outlines of a new social order.

“Come and see me tomorrow,” he said, at last. “We will talk about that place in the spinning-room. The mill needs a man who can think.”

He turned and walked slowly down the hillside toward the town. The gas-lamps still flared in the high street, and the smoke still drifted above the moors. The stars, those remote, incalculable bodies, had descended to earth, and in their descent had wrought a minor revolution in the mind of a mill owner.
