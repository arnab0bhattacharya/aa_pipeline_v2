The trading station at Mbota consists of a dozen corrugated iron roofs baking in the sun, a long jetty of African oak, and my dispensary, which clings to the edge of the river bank. It is a lonely, feverish spot. I am the only Englishman within a hundred miles, and my practice consists chiefly of dosing the Kroo boys with quinine and binding up the minor injuries they sustain in the palm-oil sheds. It was here that Wickham found me.

He arrived one Tuesday morning in a surf-boat, paddled by six silent men from the Mpongwe tribe. He was a tall, lean man, spectacled, with the desperate, hungry look of the fanatic. His baggage consisted entirely of two leather trunks, which he handled with extraordinary care. He introduced himself as a biologist attached to the Royal College of Science, and produced a letter from my agent at Sierra Leone, requesting I extend him every courtesy. 

His project, he explained over a glass of trade gin that evening, was the study of a local affliction—or rather, a local immunity. The natives called it the "Sleeping Death," though it was no such thing. It was a specific parasite, transmitted by the bite of a local black fly, which attacked the nervous system. The acute phase was a brief fever. The chronic phase was far stranger. It severed, completely and permanently, the brain's capacity to register physical pain.

"Think of it, Vane," he said, leaning forward in the lamplight, his eyes shining behind his spectacles. "Absolute analgesia. A man could thrust his hand into a furnace and feel nothing but the warmth. He could walk on a broken leg. It is a physiological marvel. I have come to isolate the parasite and culture it."

I was immediately uneasy. I had seen the "Sleeping Death." I had seen Kroo boys with the affliction ignore a festering wound until the gangrene killed them. Pain, I told him, is a necessity. It is the body’s burglar alarm. To remove it is to invite catastrophe.

Wickham waved a dismissive hand. "Necessity is a relative term, Vane. Pain is a primitive mechanism, a brutal survival instinct suited to the cave-man. In a civilised society, pain is an anachronism. Imagine the surgical possibilities! Amputations without chloroform. Laparotomies while the patient reads the morning paper!"

"Imagine the surgical mortality," I retorted. "A man doesn't know he is being cut, but he still bleeds."

Wickham merely sniffed, as though I had brought up a triviality. He was, he said, going upriver to a village where the parasite was endemic. He required my assistance in securing specimens.

We went upriver the following morning in my steam-launch. The village was a wretched collection of mud huts, but the people were docile enough. Wickham set up his apparatus in the shade of a great baobab tree. He had brought a microscope, an array of glass slides, and a set of surgical instruments that looked distressingly sharp.

His method of obtaining specimens was direct. He offered the chief a quantity of brass wire and a bolt of Manchester cotton in exchange for blood. But Wickham was not merely after blood. He wanted to observe the parasite's life cycle in living tissue. He wanted to observe the analgesia.

I was called away to treat a woman with a dislocated shoulder in another part of the village. When I returned to the baobab tree, I found Wickham in a state of high excitement. He had a young man, perhaps twenty years of age, sitting on a stool. The man was staring blankly at the ground.

"I have confirmed the infection," Wickham said, rubbing his hands together. "The parasite is in the blood. Now, to test the nerve response."

He picked up a long, slender needle from his case. It was a surgical probe, but he had blunted the tip slightly, and attached it to a small galvanic battery. He explained, in broken English, to the young man that he was going to touch his arm. The man nodded, uninterested.

Wickham touched the needle to the man’s forearm. The man did not flinch. Wickram increased the current. Still nothing. He pressed harder, until the skin indented. Not a flicker of the man’s expression. 

"You see?" Wickham breathed. "Complete insensibility."

"Quite," I said, feeling a profound distaste for the whole business. "You have proved your point. Let the man go."

"Not yet," said Wickham. "I must test the deeper tissues."

Before I could stop him, he had picked up a small, sharp scalpel. He took the man's forearm and made a swift, clean incision, about an inch long. Blood welled up immediately. The young man looked down at the cut with mild curiosity, as though he were watching a fly walk across a table. He did not cry out. He did not withdraw his arm. He simply stared.

"Good God, Wickham!" I cried. "What are you doing?"

"Observing," said he, entirely calm. "The subject feels nothing. The capillary action is normal. The parasite has entirely blocked the nociceptive pathways. This is a triumph, Vane!"

I seized his wrist and forced the scalpel down. "It is a brutality," I said. "The man is a patient, not a specimen."

Wickham laughed—a short, sharp bark. "My dear Vane, you are too squeamish. The man is unharmed. He doesn't mind. Do you?" he asked the young man.

The young man smiled vacantly and shook his head.

I dressed the wound, bandaged it firmly, and sent the man away with a few beads. Wickham was furious at my interference, but he had what he wanted. He spent the afternoon at the microscope, making smears and sketches. He was completely absorbed, muttering to himself and occasionally letting out a little hiss of satisfaction. He was, I decided, quite mad.

That night, as we lay in the launch, sweltering under the mosquito nets, I asked him what he intended to do with his discovery.

"I intend to culture it," he said, his voice floating through the darkness. "I will take the parasite back to London. I will infect a colony of guinea pigs. I will isolate the toxin that blocks the pain. And then, Vane, I will synthesize it."

"And then?" I asked.

"I will give it to the world," he said. "A world without pain. Think of the hospitals. Think of the battlefields."

"Think of the slaughterhouses," I said. "Think of the factories. Think of the mines. A man could be worked to death and never feel a thing."

"Sentimental nonsense," Wickham snapped. "You see only the worst. Pain is a tyrant. I intend to depose it."

I did not argue further. It was useless to argue with a man who had the conviction of a prophet and the conscience of a vivisectionist. I lay awake for a long time, listening to the drone of the insects and the distant roll of the river. I thought of the young man with the cut on his arm, smiling vacantly at his own blood. I thought of the brass wire and the Manchester cotton. I thought of the world without pain, and I found it a terrible prospect.

We returned to Mbota the next morning. Wickham spent three more days in the village, taking more blood, making more smears. I refused to accompany him. He went alone, with his trunks and his instruments, and returned each evening in a state of feverish exaltation. He had, he said, made great progress. He had the parasite. He had the toxin. He was ready to leave.

On his last evening, he showed me a small glass vial. It contained a clear fluid, slightly yellowish.

"There," he said. "The active principle. A few drops of this in the bloodstream, and a man is insensible to pain for a month. I have only enough for a few dozen subjects, but I will synthesize more in London."

I looked at the vial. It seemed a small, innocent thing to hold such a terrible possibility. I thought of asking him to leave it with me, to throw it in the river. But I knew he would not. And I knew, too, that he was in earnest. He would go back to London, and he would give it to the world.

He left the next morning, paddled by his silent Mpongwe men. I stood on the jetty and watched him go. He waved his hat once, and then the boat rounded the bend in the river and was gone. I went back to my dispensary and dosed a Kroo boy with quinine. The sun beat down on the corrugated iron roofs. The river rolled on. And somewhere, in a small glass vial in a leather trunk, the future was being carried away.
