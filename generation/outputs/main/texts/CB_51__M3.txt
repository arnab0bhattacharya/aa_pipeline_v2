The frost had bound the Hollow in a grip of iron. It was a season that tried the soul as sharply as the wind tried the flesh, piercing the meagre clothing of the poor and driving them to desperate hearths. I stood at the lattice of Hollow’s Edge, looking down upon the mill I had bought—the ruined mill of Thornebeck, which I had meant to raise from its desolation into a temple of industry and daily bread. The western wind brought a keen scent of snow down from the moors, and the sky lowered, sullen and leaden, as though the very heavens frowned upon my ambition.

They had all warned me. Squire Bolingbroke, a man of substance and narrow sympathies, had ridden over from his warm, sheltered hall to prophesy my ruin. He had sat in that very parlour, his heavy face flushed with a mixture of patronage and contempt, and told me plainly that a woman of independent means, however ample her purse, could not stem the tide of commerce or mend the broken fortunes of a parish. When I remained resolute, he had withdrawn his suit. I was no longer a fitting bride for a man of his standing, and he left me to my "Quixotic charities," as he termed them. I had felt the wrench of it—what woman does not feel the sting of a withdrawn devotion?—but my spirit was not broken. I had looked into his eyes, and seen there the cold complacency of a man who views the suffering of others as an unalterable law of nature, and I had known that my path lay apart from his.

But now, in the biting grip of January, the trial had come. The new machinery, imported at vast expense from a foundry in distant Staffordshire, had failed. The great cog-wheel had cracked under the sudden frost, and the looms stood idle. The men who had looked to me for salvation now looked at me with eyes of hollow doubt. I could read their thoughts: they had trusted a woman, and a woman had led them into deeper want.

A knock came at the parlour door. It was not the timid rap of a servant, but a firm, somewhat imperious summons. I bade them enter.

It was Mr. Leighton, my suitor who had withdrawn—not the Squire, but the other, the darker and more exacting spirit. Arthur Leighton was a man of fierce intellect and cold passions, a clergyman whose faith was more of the rod than the crutch. He had parted from me in anger when I refused to abandon my scheme, declaring I was courting ruin with a fool’s rashness. Yet here he stood, his black coat whitened with driving snow, his dark eyes burning with an intensity that seemed to thaw the very frost of the room.

"You are obstinate, Eleanor," he said, without preface. His voice was deep, and it carried the resonance of the pulpit, yet it trembled with a restrained urgency. "I hear the mill is dead. The men are murmuring. Will you not now confess your error and withdraw your hand from this plough before it cuts you to the bone?"

"I shall confess nothing, Arthur," I replied, turning from the window to face him. I felt my own pulse quicken. "The machinery has failed, but the purpose has not. The men shall not starve while I have a shilling left."

"Your shillings will soon be as dust," he returned, stepping closer. "The men do not want your money; they want bread earned by their own labour, and you cannot give it them. You have built a castle on shifting sand."

"I have built on the rock of necessity," I cried, the blood mounting to my cheeks. "If the machinery is broken, we shall mend it. If it cannot be mended, we shall find another way. I will not be beaten by a piece of iron and a frost!"

He looked at me long and searchingly, and a strange fire kindled in his eyes—not anger now, but something more profound, a sort of fierce admiration struggling against his better judgment.

"You are a strange woman," he murmured. "There is something in you that I cannot withstand, though I know it is madness."

"It is not madness; it is duty. If God has given me means, He has given me responsibility. I will not stand by while children cry for bread and men are driven to desperation for want of work. You, of all men, should understand that."

"I do understand it," he said, and his voice dropped to a lower key. "I understand it too well. That is why I am here. I came to tell you that you are ruined—but I see you will not hear it."

"I will not."

"Then I will tell you something else." He paused, and the firelight played across his pale features. "I have a friend in Leeds, an ironmaster. He could cast a new wheel. It would cost money—more than you have, perhaps."

"Then I will find it."

"You are impossible." He almost smiled, a rare and fleeting thing, a gleam of sunlight on a winter’s day. "I will write to him to-night. The letter shall go by the morning post."

I could not speak for a moment. The sudden rush of gratitude and something deeper—something perilous and unspoken—choked my utterance.

"You are very good, Arthur," I said at last.

"I am not good. I am a selfish man, Eleanor. I came here hoping to find you humbled, so that I might offer my hand again. Instead, I find you more unbending than ever. You will be my ruin yet."

He turned abruptly and left the room. I heard the front door close, and through the lattice I watched him stride into the white storm, his black figure swallowed quickly by the driving snow. I was left alone with the fire and the wind, and the weight of all that lay upon me.

The next day brought the men. They came in a deputation, led by old Jonas Bray, whose grey hair and weather-beaten face spoke of fifty years’ labour in the mills. They stood in the yard, a haggard, gaunt band, their eyes full of a sullen suspicion that was harder to bear than anger.

"We mun speak wi’ ye, Miss," said Jonas, his voice rough but not unkindly. "We’re gratefu’, and we’ll not deny it; but t’ mill’s stopped, and t’ childer cryin’ for food. Is it to go on? Can ye mend it, or mun we seek elsewhere?"

I stood before them on the frozen flags, the wind cutting through my cloak, and I felt a strength rise in me that was not my own.

"Friends," I said, "I will not deceive you. The wheel is broken. But I have sent for help. A new wheel shall be cast, and while we wait, there shall be work—manual work, repairing the mill, clearing the leat, building the storehouse. You shall not want for bread while I have means to provide it. I ask only for your patience and your trust."

There was a silence. Then a young man at the back, a fiery, gaunt fellow named Seth Booth, spoke up: "And if t’ new wheel fails, like t’ old one?"

"Then I will sell Hollow’s Edge," I answered. "I will sell my plate, my furniture, my very clothes, before I see you and your families destitute. I have taken this burden, and I will bear it, with God’s help, to the end."

A murmur went through the crowd—not of applause, but of something deeper and more quiet. Jonas Bray pulled off his cap, and the others followed his example.

"God bless ye, Miss," he said simply. "We’ll stand by ye."

They dispersed, and I was left alone in the biting air. The snow was falling heavily now, blanketing the ruined mill, softening its harsh outlines, covering the frozen earth in a mantle of white. I looked up at the lowering sky, and I felt, despite the cold and the dread, a strange and solemn peace. His will be done. The trial was not over—perhaps it had scarcely begun—but I would not falter. The fire in my heart burned still, and it was not yet quenched.
