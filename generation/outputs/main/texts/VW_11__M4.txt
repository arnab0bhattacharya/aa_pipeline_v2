The chairs, of course, would not do. They stood in their serried, expectant rows, each one a little island of polished wood and horsehair, and Clara, standing at the back of the hall with her hands pressed together, saw that the whole pattern was wrong. It was a question of the aisle—the aisle was too narrow, or perhaps too wide; it made the rows look either crowded or forlorn, and Mrs. Ashby, who was to play the Schumann, required space for her pages to turn without brushing the elbows of the front row. “A little to the left,” Clara said to the man with the trolley, and then, “No, no, the left, the other left,” and the chairs scraped and groaned against the floorboards, and the sound was like a sigh let out by the building itself, which had been a temperance hall once, and a drill hall, and now was the Perivale Rooms, and smelled of beeswax and old programmes and the faint, sweetish ghost of spilled lemonade.

She had been on her feet since seven. At eight the florist had arrived with the lilies, which were too tall, and at nine the piano-tuner, who had a cold and spoke through a handkerchief about the damp, and at half past ten Mrs. Ashby herself, in a hat like a small brown bird’s nest, who had sat down at the Bechstein and played four bars and stopped, and played four bars again, and said, “It is the pedals, Clara. It is always the pedals,” though the pedals were blameless. And Clara had soothed her, had brought her tea in the green cup with the chip, had said, “Of course, my dear, of course,” and had thought, all the while, of the letter in the drawer of the escritoire at home, the letter from the bank, which she had found on Tuesday and had not mentioned, and would not mention, and which lay in her mind now like a stone at the bottom of a clear pool, visible whenever the surface stilled.

For Arthur had not told her. That was the thing. He had come in on Monday with his face all smooth and cheerful, had asked about the concert, had said the weather would hold, and all the time—she knew it now—he had been carrying it, the knowledge, the ruin of it, tucked somewhere behind his waistcoat, and she had poured his tea and passed him the marmalade and never guessed. And how was she to speak of it? To say, I know? To watch his face come apart? So she said nothing, and arranged the chairs, and the stone lay in the pool, and the afternoon went on.

By four the platform was a bower of white and green, the programmes were stacked in neat battalions, and the soprano, Miss Ferrers, a girl with a long neck and frightened eyes, had been persuaded not to withdraw. “My throat,” she kept saying, touching it. “It is like a little dry leaf, Mrs. Ellison. A little dry leaf.” And Clara had taken her hands—such cold hands—and had said, “You will sing like a bird, my dear. You will open your mouth and the bird will fly out,” which was nonsense, and Miss Ferrers had laughed, and had gone away to dress, and Clara had stood a moment alone in the empty hall, in the failing light, and had thought: the bird will fly out, and the stone will lie at the bottom of the pool, and the chairs will be filled, and no one will know.

The hall filled. It filled slowly, in twos and threes, with a rustle of silk and the tap of sticks and the low, comfortable murmur of people who have known one another for twenty years. Clara stood at the door, receiving them, and her face ached with smiling, and she watched them come, and it seemed to her that she saw them all at once and each one separately, as though the years were a fan spread open. There was old Colonel Whitby, who had lost his son in the war and who came now to every concert, sitting in the third row, his hands upon his knees, his eyes fixed on the platform as though someone might at any moment step out from behind the curtain and be his boy. There was Mrs. Ashby, in the front row, arranging her music with the air of a woman who has never in her life been uncertain of anything. There was the vicar, and the vicar’s sister, and the two Miss Trents, who always came together and always left together, and who had been, Clara remembered, quite pretty once, in a thin, startled way, and were now like two pressed flowers in a book. And there was Arthur, at the back, by the door, standing with his hands behind his back, looking at her across the heads of all those people, and his face was the face of a man who is drowning quietly and does not wish to make a fuss.

The lights went down. The first chord of the Schumann sounded, and Mrs. Ashby’s hands moved over the keys like two white mice, and the hall was very still. Clara sat in her place at the side, and let the music come over her, and thought: he is at the back, and he is drowning, and I am here, and I know. And the music went on, and it was like water, and the stone at the bottom of the pool was turning over and over, and she thought of the letter, and of the mornings, and of all the mornings to come, and of how one went on, how one always went on, arranging chairs, soothing sopranos, pouring tea. Miss Ferrers rose, and her throat was not a dry leaf; the bird flew out, and the audience breathed, and Clara, watching the faces—Colonel Whitby’s, the Miss Trents’, the vicar’s—saw that they were all, every one of them, carrying something, some letter, some loss, some unspoken thing, and that the music was for them, and that they had come to be together in the dark and to forget, for an hour, the stone at the bottom of the pool.

When it was over, when the clapping had died and the chairs were being stacked and the lilies were being carried out, Arthur came to her. He stood beside her, and he did not speak. She looked at him, and his face was grey and tired, and she saw that he knew that she knew, and that he could not say it, and that she could not say it either, not here, not with the men stacking chairs and the smell of beeswax and the last of the audience drifting out into the Kensington evening.

“It went well,” he said at last.

“It went well,” she said.

And they stood together in the emptying hall, among the chairs, and outside the lamps were being lit, one by one, down the long street, and the stone lay at the bottom of the pool, and the water above it was very still.
