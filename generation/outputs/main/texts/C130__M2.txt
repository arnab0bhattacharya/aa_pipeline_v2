The air in Port Valiente tasted of salt and diesel, a sharp, metallic tang that clashed with the damp, earthy scent of the mountains I had spent seven years carving through. I stood at the edge of the platform, my hands clasped behind my back, feeling the roughness of my leather gloves against the skin. The heat of the midday sun pressed down on the coastal plain, but a cold wind swept in from the Pacific, ruffling the hem of my tweed coat. I had not slept properly in three days. My mind was a labyrinth of gradients, tonnage, and curve radii, a mental architecture I had built brick by brick, rail by rail, to connect the isolated hamlets of the Sierra de Oro to the modern world. I believed, with a fervor that bordered on religious conviction, that this line would be the artery of the republic. I believed that the copper and silver in the veins of those mountains would flow out like blood, carrying wealth, education, and dignity to the villages of San Mateo, El Puente, and Los Altos. I believed that the train would carry farmers’ produce down and factory goods up, knitting the fractured fabric of our nation together.

But as the whistle blew, high and shrill, tearing through the humid air, I felt a vibration in my chest that was not the promise of commerce, but the threat of judgment.

The locomotive emerged from the tunnel cut into the cliff face, a black beast belching smoke that turned gray against the bright blue sky. It was the *Andean Star*, the finest engine the company could afford, imported from Scotland with brass fittings that gleamed like new coins. I watched it decelerate, the pistons churning with a rhythmic, heavy thud that I had once loved the sound of. Now, it sounded like a heartbeat slowing to a stop. The first car was a mail coach, its windows dark. Behind it came a second car, then a third. My eyes narrowed, straining against the glare. The windows of the second car were barred. Not with wire, but with iron, thick and rusted, as if they had been in use for decades.

A murmur began among the few civilians on the platform—merchants with crates of tobacco, a group of women in shawls, and the station master, old Manolo, who looked up from his ledger with a face drained of color. I did not move. I could not. My feet seemed rooted to the wooden planks.

The train came to a halt with a screech of brakes that echoed off the stone walls. The doors of the mail coach opened first, and a postal worker stepped out, stretching his legs, looking bored. Then, the doors of the second car swung open.

They were not farmers. They were not merchants. They were not the children of the mountain villages who had waited for this day with such eager anticipation.

Soldiers.

They wore the khaki uniforms of the national army, their caps brimmed with stiff leather. They stood in the doorway, not stepping out, but looking down at us with a detached, weary expression. There were dozens of them, filling the car, and more in the third, which was a troop transport, its wooden slats barely able to contain the mass of men. Their rifles were slung across their backs, the wooden stocks polished to a shine by nervous hands.

I looked at Manolo. He had lowered his ledger. His lips moved, forming a word I could not hear, but I knew it. It was the word every man in the republic had been whispering for the last six months, ever since the General had seized the capital and declared the "Restoration of Order."

*Rebellion.*

The soldiers began to disembark. They moved with a practiced efficiency, forming a line along the edge of the platform. I watched a young soldier, no older than twenty, step down. He looked around, his eyes wide and confused, seeing the tropical palms and the white sand for the first time. He looked at the crowd, then at me. For a second, our eyes met. I saw not hatred in his gaze, but a profound, crushing sadness. He was a boy from the capital, I realized, sent to the ends of the earth to kill men who looked just like him.

A general stepped out of the first car. He was a tall man, thin as a rail, wearing a white tunic that was already stained with sweat. He carried no weapon, only a cane. He walked to the center of the platform, directly in front of me. The crowd parted, or perhaps they were pushed aside by the soldiers who had formed a perimeter. The general’s gaze swept over the port, over the ships in the harbor, over the mountains in the distance. Then, it settled on me.

"Engineer," he said. His voice was calm, devoid of the heat that usually accompanied his orders. "You are the one who built this."

I nodded, my throat tight. "Yes, sir."

"Did you know?" he asked. "When you laid the last tie, did you know that the first load it would carry was not ore, but men?"

I looked at the mountains. I thought of the tunnels I had dug through solid granite. I thought of the workers who had died in the collapses, their names carved into stones that now lay in the mud of the valley. I had told myself that their deaths were for a purpose. That the line would bring life. That it would bring justice.

"I built it for the people," I said, my voice sounding hollow in my own ears.

The general smiled, a thin, cruel expression. "And the people are what is dying, are they not? The rebels in the Sierra. They use the same roads you built to move their supplies. They use the same tunnels to hide. Your prosperity has become their shelter."

He tapped his cane on the platform. *Click. Click.*

"You thought you were building a bridge to the future," he continued. "But you only built a highway for the war. You connected the villages to the army. You made it possible for us to reach them. For them to reach us. You did not bring prosperity, Engineer. You brought logistics."

I looked at the young soldier by the train. He was looking at his boots, his shoulders hunched. I looked at the third car, where more soldiers were waiting. I looked at the tracks, the perfect, straight lines I had obsessively maintained, the symbols of order and progress that now led directly into the heart of the conflict.

The train began to move again, reversing slowly, its whistle blowing a low, mournful note. The soldiers would stay. They would march inland, up the grades I had calculated with such precision, into the valleys I had surveyed with such care. They would go to the villages I had dreamed of helping. They would bring the smoke and the fire that I had tried to prevent.

I stood on the platform, watching the train disappear into the tunnel, the smoke trailing behind it like a ghost. The heat of the sun seemed to intensify, baking the platform into a furnace. I felt a profound sense of failure, a weight heavier than any rock I had ever moved. I had given my health, my youth, and my soul to this line. I had believed in the sanctity of the work, in the noble purpose of connection. I had been wrong. In the end, the railway was not a lifeline. It was a noose.

I turned my back on the tunnel and walked toward the sea. The waves crashed against the shore, relentless and indifferent, eroding the land just as surely as the train had carved the mountain. I did not look back. I knew that if I did, I would see not the promise of the future, but the shadow of the past, long and dark, stretching all the way to the capital. The engine’s sound faded, replaced by the roar of the ocean, a sound that had been there before the first rail was laid, and would be there long after the last soldier had died. I was alone on the platform, the engineer of a dead dream, standing in the ruins of a hope I had built with my own hands.
