It was with some hesitation that Mrs. Beatrice Croft presented herself at my chambers in the autumn of 1893. She was a young woman of perhaps five-and-twenty, of the fashionable Hampstead set, and her bearing was one of quiet refinement. Yet a certain tremor in her gloved hands and the anxious glance she cast about the room spoke of a distress which she was at great pains to conceal.

“Mr. Hargreaves,” she began, her voice low but clear, “I am told you are a man of discretion.”

“That is the first requirement of my profession, madam,” I replied, gesturing her to a chair. “I may be of service to you?”

“It is a matter of the utmost privacy,” she said, and I saw her jaw set with a resolve that belied her nervous manner. “My husband, Mr. Julian Croft, is in the wine trade. His firm, Croft & Son, is well-established in the City. He is a man of unimpeachable reputation, and I would not, for all the world, be the cause of a scandal that might tarnish it.” She paused, drawing a breath. “That is my primary concern. Not the truth of the matter, but the prevention of its public airing.”

I nodded, encouraging her to proceed.

“For some weeks,” she continued, “I have observed certain… discrepancies. Julian is a man of regular habits. He leaves for his office at half-past eight and returns for dinner at seven. But recently, there have been evenings when he is delayed by an hour or more. He offers plausible enough explanations—business at the docks, a late client, a committee meeting. Yet there is an air about him, a sort of furtive weariness, that I cannot account for.”

“And these discrepancies,” I prompted gently. “Are they confined to his hours?”

She shook her head. “No. Two days ago, while searching for a misplaced letter in his study, I chanced upon a second ledger. It was hidden beneath a loose floorboard. It is an account book, identical in form to the one he uses for his business, but the entries are… different. They refer to properties, to rents, to sums paid to persons I have never heard of. And then there is the key.”

From her reticule she produced a small, heavy object wrapped in a silk handkerchief. She laid it upon my desk. It was a solid brass key, of a pattern common enough to London’s many doors, but with a distinctively worn head and a complex, five-lever bit. “I found this in his waistcoat pocket. I have tried it upon every door in our house, in our garden shed, and at his office, which I visited under the pretext of bringing him luncheon. It fits none of them.”

“A most curious problem,” I remarked, examining the key. The wear on the head suggested it was used frequently. “And you wish for me to discover where this key belongs?”

“I wish for you to discover the nature of my husband’s second life,” she said, her voice firming. “I must know the truth for my own peace of mind, but I must know it quietly. If he is… if he has established another home, I must be prepared to act, to contain the situation before it can explode and ruin him. My own position is secondary to that.”

The case was presented with a clarity and a purpose that I found compelling. It was not a matter of marital jealousy, but of damage control, a far more complex and delicate operation. I assured Mrs. Croft of my complete discretion and, having taken the key for my own examination, promised her a report within the week.

My first step was to observe Mr. Julian Croft. He proved to be a man of medium height and build, impeccably dressed in the sober style of a successful merchant, with a neat moustache and a placid, unremarkable face. For three evenings I followed him from his offices near the Monument. On two occasions he went directly home to Hampstead. On the third, however, he did not. At a little after six, he hailed a hansom and directed the driver towards the Strand. I followed in another cab, alighting a discreet distance behind him as he entered a narrow turning off the main thoroughfare, a street of respectable, if unremarkable, lodging houses.

I did not see him enter a particular house, but I noted the street—Craven Passage—and marked its character. It was not the sort of place one would expect a man of Mr. Croft’s standing to frequent, unless for a purpose he wished to keep entirely separate from his respectable life.

The following day, I turned my attention to the key. I began with the locksmiths in the vicinity of Mr. Croft’s business, a method of inquiry which is often fruitless but occasionally yields surprising results. At a fourth establishment, a dusty little shop in a backwater of Finsbury, a wizened old man with spectacles perched on his nose examined the key with a professional squint.

“Ah, yes,” he said, with a knowing nod. “That’s one of mine. A bit of an old pattern, that. Haven’t cut one like it in five years or more. It’s for the old Chubb ‘Victory’ lock. Very popular with the landlords down by the river, back in the fifties.”

“And do you recall cutting this particular key?” I asked.

The locksmith chuckled. “I should say so. It was for a Mr. Davies. A nervous little fellow, a clerk of some sort. He had lost his and was in a terrible state about it. Paid me a handsome sum to come to the premises and open the door. That was… oh, it must be six months ago now. A Tuesday, I remember, for I was closing for the half-day.”

“Mr. Davies,” I mused. “And the premises?”

“A house in Craven Passage, off the Strand. Number fourteen. I remember it well. A gloomy sort of place.”

The pieces began to fit together with a gratifying precision. I thanked the locksmith and made my way directly to Craven Passage. Number fourteen was a four-storied brick house, its windows grimy, its brass door-knocker tarnished. I tried the key in the lock. It turned smoothly and silently. I was inside.

The hall was narrow and ill-lit, smelling of dust and boiled cabbage. A flight of stairs rose before me. I stood for a moment, listening. From the floor above came the faint, rhythmic sound of a metronome, and with it, the scraping of a bow upon strings. It was a cello, and the player, whoever he was, was practising scales with a grim, repetitive determination.

I ascended the stairs as quietly as I could. The music came from a room at the front of the house. The door was slightly ajar. Peering through the crack, I saw a scene of such startling incongruity that for a moment I could not reconcile it with the placid wine merchant of Hampstead.

The room was sparsely furnished, with a narrow bed, a table, and a single chair. A heavy curtain was drawn across the window. Seated on the chair, his back to me, was a man in his shirtsleeves, hunched over a cello. It was Mr. Julian Croft. His face, seen in profile as he turned a page of music, was a mask of intense concentration. The respectable moustache was there, the neat collar, but the man himself was transformed. He was no longer the merchant; he was an artist consumed by his craft.

On the table beside him lay a second ledger, identical to the one his wife had found. But this was no record of illicit rents or secret payments. I could see the columns clearly from my vantage point. They were headed ‘Subscription’, ‘Patronage’, ‘Benefactor’, ‘Anonymous Gift’. Beside the names were sums of money, some quite large. Beneath the ledger was a pile of correspondence, and on the topmost envelope, I could make out the crest of the Royal Academy of Music.

I withdrew as silently as I had entered, my mind in a whirl. The second life, the secret house, the hidden accounts—they were not what Mrs. Croft had feared. There was no other woman, no scandalous liaison. There was only this: a respectable man, burdened by the respectable life he had built, who came to this grim little room to pursue a secret, passionate, and perhaps unrequited, ambition. The payments in the book were not expenses; they were donations. He was not hiding a fortune; he was funding a cause. Perhaps he was a patron to other struggling musicians, or perhaps he was paying for his own tuition and the hire of this very room from some mysterious benefactor.

I made my report to Mrs. Croft the next afternoon. I did not tell her everything. The image of her husband in that bare room, pouring his soul into the music, was a private thing, not for sharing.

“The key belongs to a lodging house in Craven Passage, off the Strand,” I began. “Number fourteen.”

Her face paled. “And the ledger? The money?”

“The ledger is a record of charitable donations,” I said, choosing my words with care. “Your husband is a benefactor to a number of young artists and musicians. It is a cause he has evidently chosen to support in private, without any desire for public recognition.”

She stared at me, her expression a mixture of relief and utter bewilderment. “Musicians? Artists? But… why the secrecy? Why a second house key?”

“I believe,” I said, “that he wished to keep this aspect of his life entirely separate from his business and his home. Some men compartmentalise their passions in such a way. The house in Craven Passage is merely an address for his correspondence and a place where he conducts this private philanthropy. There is no scandal, Mrs. Croft. Your husband’s reputation is, as far as I can ascertain, as secure as you could wish.”

She was silent for a long time, looking out of the window at the bustling street below. The anxiety had left her face, replaced by a pensive, thoughtful calm.

“A patron of the arts,” she said softly, as if tasting the words. “To think of it. All this time.” She turned back to me, and a small, genuine smile touched her lips. “Thank you, Mr. Hargreaves. You have given me not only peace of mind, but something more besides.” She pressed my fee into my hand and took her leave, a woman no longer burdened by fear, but by a new and curious understanding of the man she had married. I never saw her again, but I have no doubt she found a way to bring her husband’s secret music into the light of their shared home.
