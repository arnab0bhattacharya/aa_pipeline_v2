If there was one thing upon which the whole neighbourhood of Laburnum Crescent was united—and it was a neighbourhood of a thousand warring factions, from the cat that sang upon the garden wall at midnight to the retired major who fired off his opinions like so many blank cartridges at the world in general—it was that Mr. and Mrs. Honeywood were the pleasantest young couple in Kentish Town. Mr. Aubrey Honeywood was a gentleman of such airy and expansive optimism that he regarded a bill as a mere polite intimation from a tradesman that the universe intended to be paid at some remote and convenient season; and Mrs. Clara Honeywood, his wife, was a lady of such winning and helpless grace, such a pretty confusion when she consulted her purse, such a charming little shriek of surprise when she discovered it empty, that the butcher, the baker, the grocer, the draper, the cheesemonger, the upholsterer, and the man who sold the little French rolls, all felt themselves elevated into a species of chivalric brotherhood, whose noble office it was to keep the Honeywoods going.

The villa itself was a perfect expression of their characters. It was called "The Nook," though it stood upon a corner so exposed that the wind whistled through it from every quarter of the compass, and it had a bow window in front, a little conservatory at the side, and a garden behind that was chiefly remarkable for a broken summer-house and a pump. Within, everything was bright, everything was new, and everything was unpaid for. The chairs were of the most cheerful pattern, the carpet was of the most inviting texture, and the piano, which had a voice of its own and a way of tinkling in the night, was the wonder of the Crescent. There was a small library, consisting principally of the covers of books, and a what-not in the corner laden with shells and coral, and a drawing-room so full of little tables that a visitor was obliged to thread his way among them as through a miniature archipelago.

"Upon my word, my dear," said Mr. Honeywood, as he sat at breakfast one fine morning in the autumn of the year, "I do not know how it is, but the world seems to grow more obliging every day. Here is a note from Mr. Tallow, the butcher, expressing a hope that the little account may be settled at my perfect convenience; and here is one from Mr. Grindem, the baker, who is so good as to say that he would not for the world disturb me. I call that handsome, Clara—I call that truly handsome."

"Delightful!" said Clara, who was making toast and burning it with the most engaging air of abstraction. "And the new curtains, Aubrey—do you not think the new curtains are beautiful? They were so obliging at the warehouse as to send them home without any application at all."

"Beautiful!" cried Aubrey. "Everything is beautiful. We live in a beautiful world, my love, and among beautiful people. I protest I never was so happy in my life."

It was upon this very morning, while the toast was burning and the coffee was boiling over, that there came a double knock at the door—a knock so sharp, so peremptory, and so entirely unlike the apologetic taps of the tradesmen, that the little servant girl, who had been hired at a wage that was never paid, turned pale and dropped the toast-rack. And presently there entered, without waiting to be announced, a lady of a certain age, dressed in a brown stuff gown of the most uncompromising severity, with a bonnet that looked like a helmet, and a pair of spectacles upon her nose through which she surveyed the apartment and its occupants with an expression of the most withering disapproval.

"Aunt!" cried Mr. Honeywood, starting up. "Aunt Dorothea!"

"Precisely," said that lady, seating herself in the most comfortable chair with the air of a general taking possession of a fortress. "Your aunt Dorothea. I have come to stay."

"To—to stay!" faltered Clara, with her prettiest confusion.

"To stay," repeated Miss Dorothea Honeywood. "I have heard—for such things are heard, sir, though you may fancy they are not—that you are living here upon the credit of the whole parish, that you owe money to every shopkeeper in the Crescent, that your furniture is unpaid for, your piano is unpaid for, and your very breakfast this morning, I make no doubt, was eaten upon the understanding that it should be paid for at some future period which will never arrive. I have come to take charge."

"Take charge!" said Aubrey, with an attempt at a laugh. "My dear aunt, we are getting on famously—famously!"

"You are getting on to ruin," retorted Miss Dorothea, "at a pace which I never saw equalled except in the case of a gentleman who rode a blind horse down a steep hill, and who was good enough to observe, as he went, that he had never gone so fast in his life before. I have brought my boxes. I have brought my account-books. I have brought a little money, which I intend to lay out to the best advantage. And I have brought a firm determination to pay every man his due, and to put this house upon a footing that shall not make it the laughing-stock of the neighbourhood."

It is impossible to describe the consternation that reigned in The Nook for the next week. Miss Dorothea rose at six, inspected the larder at seven, and by eight had made out a list of every creditor in the parish, with the amount of his debt and the date of his last application. She wrote letters—such letters!—to Mr. Tallow and Mr. Grindem and the upholsterer and the man who sold the little French rolls; and she went in person to the warehouse and demanded to know upon what principle of justice they had allowed a young couple to run up a bill of such magnitude. The tradesmen, who had never before considered the matter in that light, were quite overcome; and when Miss Dorothea added that she intended to pay every farthing, they were more overcome still.

As for Aubrey and Clara, they wandered about the house like two disconsolate ghosts, forbidden to order anything, forbidden to buy anything, forbidden even to look at the piano without a permission which was never granted. The pretty little dinners gave place to plain and wholesome fare; the new curtains were returned to the warehouse; the what-not was sold, and the shells and coral with it. Clara cried a good deal, and Aubrey whistled a good deal, and both of them thought their aunt the most tyrannical old lady that ever lived.

But by-and-by, when the bills were all paid, and the creditors were all satisfied, and the account-books showed a balance on the right side, and the house was furnished with things that were honestly their own, there came a change. Miss Dorothea, who had a heart beneath her brown stuff gown, began to soften. She bought Clara a little work-box, and she gave Aubrey a lecture upon the sin of living upon credit that lasted an hour and a half, and was so full of good sense and good feeling that Aubrey never forgot it. And when at last she announced her intention of returning to her own house, the two young people clung to her with a gratitude that was quite affecting.

"You will come again, aunt?" said Clara.

"I will come again," said Miss Dorothea, "if you want me. But I hope you will not want me. Pay as you go, my dears—pay as you go—and you will find that the world is not half so obliging as you fancied, but that it is a great deal more comfortable."

And so she went away in her brown stuff gown and her helmet bonnet, and The Nook settled down into a quiet, honest, comfortable little villa, where the butcher was paid on Saturday, and the baker was paid on Monday, and the piano was played on Sunday evenings by Clara with a clear conscience, and Aubrey smoked his pipe in the garden and reflected that, upon the whole, there was no investment in the world so profitable as a maiden aunt.
