When the Reverend Mr. Aldous Pennyman came to the perpetual curacy of St. Botolph’s, in the market-town of Marrish, he brought with him a valise of books, a conscience newly burnished by ordination, and that perilous enthusiasm which belongs to men who have formed their opinions in the seclusion of a college and have not yet learned how much of human conduct is determined by the price of coal. He was eight-and-twenty, with a pale, eager face, and a habit of thrusting his hand into his hair when he spoke of improvement, so that he presented to the congregation on a Sunday a somewhat ruffled appearance, as of a man who had been wrestling with an angel and had not altogether prevailed.

The town of Marrish, in those years before the first Reform Bill had done its work upon the boroughs, was a place of some three thousand souls, of whom perhaps a third were employed in the weaving-sheds of Mr. Josiah Barmby, a manufacturer whose name was spoken with that mixture of respect and resentment which the English poor have always felt for the men who give them bread and take their liberty. Mr. Barmby was a Dissenter by birth, a Churchman by policy, and a philanthropist by that species of calculation which is not the less sincere for being exact. He had built a school, endowed a almshouse, and subscribed to every charity in the parish, and it was his custom to say, in the hearing of the clergyman, that he thanked God he was but a poor instrument, whose wealth was held in trust for the good of the town. The instruments of Providence, as Mr. Pennyman was to discover, are apt to keep a strict account of the trust.

It was in the October of his first year that the vicar conceived the design of a lending library for the working men of Marrish. He had observed, in his visits among the cottages, that the younger weavers would spend their evenings in the tap-room of the Blue Boar, not because they preferred beer to knowledge, but because the Blue Boar had a fire and a light and a newspaper, and their own homes had neither. A library, therefore, with a good fire and a few shelves of sound and entertaining books, seemed to him the very ark of civilization launched upon the flood of ignorance. He spoke of it in the vestry after evening prayer, with his hand in his hair and his voice trembling with that generous warmth which is so becoming in a young man and so inconvenient in a committee.

The first to answer was Mr. Barmby, who sat in the front pew with his hands folded upon his gold-headed cane, and who said that he had long felt the want of such an institution, and would be happy to give the first fifty pounds towards it, and the room over the old malt-house rent-free for a twelvemonth. The vicar was so much affected by this munificence that he shook the manufacturer by the hand, and declared that the age of Christian merchants was not yet past. Mr. Barmby bowed, and observed that he hoped the books would be of a character to make the men better workmen as well as better Christians; for it was his opinion that a man who read of the improvements in machinery would be less likely to break his looms in a fit of temper.

The old curate, Mr. Ezekiel Tredgold, who had served the parish for forty years and had never been promoted, sat in the corner and said nothing until the meeting was on the point of dispersing, when he remarked, in a dry, creaking voice, like a gate that had not been oiled since the Restoration, that he had seen libraries before, and that the books were generally the last things thought of. He had, he said, no objection to the men reading, provided they read the Bible and the Prayer-Book; but he had observed that when a man once got a notion into his head from a printed book, it was ten to one he would get another, and there was no knowing where it would end. The vicar replied, with some heat, that he hoped the day was past when the Church was afraid of knowledge; and Mr. Tredgold, with a smile which had in it more of sorrow than of malice, answered that he had no fear of knowledge, but a great fear of the persons who purveyed it.

The library was opened in the November, with a speech from Mr. Barmby, a prayer from the vicar, and a distribution of tickets to such of the workmen as could produce a certificate of good conduct from their foreman. It was here that the vicar first began to perceive the delicate filaments which attach themselves to every gift. The certificates were to be signed by the foremen, and the foremen were the servants of Mr. Barmby; so that the library, which was to have been a free fountain in the wilderness, became in some sort a reward of industry, and the man who had broken his loom in a fit of temper found himself excluded from the consolations of literature. The vicar remonstrated, mildly, with Mr. Barmby, who replied that he was the last man to interfere with the clergyman’s province, but that he had observed that a man who could not keep his hands from mischief would not long keep his hands from the books; and that he had, moreover, promised the foremen that their recommendations should be respected. The vicar, who had not yet learned that a promise to a foreman is a fact of more specific gravity than a principle, yielded the point, and contented himself with observing that the library would still be a great good.

It was not long before other obligations began to disclose themselves. Mr. Barmby sent down a selection of books from his own shelves—sermons, tracts on the sin of idleness, and a volume entitled *The Weaver’s Guide to Contentment*, which had been printed at his own expense. The vicar, who had hoped for something of history and travel, thanked him with a faltering voice, and placed the volumes on the shelf. Then came a request from the manufacturer that the library should be open on Sunday afternoons, since that was the only time the men had to themselves; and the vicar, who had a horror of Sunday labour, and had preached against it, found himself obliged to consent, because the room was Mr. Barmby’s and the fire was Mr. Barmby’s, and the men would have gone to the Blue Boar if they had not come to the malt-house. Then came a hint, conveyed through Mrs. Barmby, that the vicar might do well to mention the library in his sermon, and to speak of the manufacturer as a man raised up by Providence; and the vicar, who was not yet a saint, mentioned it, and felt the words stick in his throat as he did so.

The old curate watched all this with a silence that was more eloquent than speech. One afternoon, when the vicar was arranging the shelves, Mr. Tredgold came in, and stood leaning on his stick, and looked at the books with his dim, patient eyes.

“You have got a fine room here, sir,” he said, “and a fine fire, and a fine many books. I hope the men will be the better for it.”

“I hope so, indeed,” said the vicar, warmly. “It is a great thing to have the countenance of such a man as Mr. Barmby.”

“It is a great thing,” said the curate, “to have the countenance of any man who can pay for the room. But I have observed, in my forty years, that when a man pays for the room he generally expects to choose the company. You will find, sir, that the men will come, and will read, and will be very grateful; and by-and-by, when there is an election, or a strike, or a question of wages, they will remember who gave them the room, and they will vote as he bids them, and they will think they are thinking. That is the way of the world, sir, and I do not see that a library will alter it.”

The vicar was silent, for he felt the truth of the old man’s words, though he would not confess it. He looked round the room, at the fire, at the shelves, at the men who were beginning to come in with their caps in their hands and their eyes on the books; and he saw that every gift in Marrish came with a string attached to it, and that the string was held by some hand which was not his own. He had meant to be a benefactor, and he found that he was only a steward; and the discovery was not the less bitter because it was made in a warm room, with a good fire, and a copy of *The Weaver’s Guide to Contentment* lying open on the table.
