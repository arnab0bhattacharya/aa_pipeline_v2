In the kingdom of Alabastria, where every street was a corridor of white marble and every garden a symphony of green and gold, there stood a single tower of lapis lazuli, so tall that its gilded dome was wont to tickle the chin of the morning clouds. And in this tower, which had no name because it needed none, the Prince Calian was brought up as one brings up an orchid: in a climate of perfect artifice, shielded from the rude gusts of reality. For eighteen years his world was a circle of painted vistas, his companions were the silent philosophers of the tapestry, and his only knowledge of the earth was derived from the exquisite, and therefore entirely inaccurate, descriptions in his books.

On the morning of his eighteenth birthday, the King, whose own world was scarcely larger than his son’s, ascended the winding stair of amethyst to grant the youth his one desire. The King was a man of magnificent raiment and vacant expression, a living monument to the fact that one may be draped in gold and starved of meaning. ‘My son,’ he said, his voice echoing in the dome like a bell rung in a tomb, ‘the world is a fruit, and to-day I pluck it for you. Ask, and it is yours. A diamond the size of a pigeon’s egg? A horse with wings of spun silver? The hand of the fairest princess in the seven kingdoms?’

The Prince, who had been contemplating a single, perfect rose of the colour of crushed strawberries, turned his gaze upon his father. ‘I desire no jewel, for light is more beautiful when it is not imprisoned. I desire no winged horse, for the sky is a garden I have never been permitted to walk in. And as for princesses, I am told they are all painted, and I have had my fill of paintings.’

The King blinked, his regal composure momentarily ruffled. ‘Then what, in the name of all that is opulent, do you want?’

Calian looked out through the crystal window at the city of Alabastria, a city of towers and gardens where not a single leaf was out of place. ‘Father,’ he said, his voice soft but clear, ‘I have read of a creature called a poor man. The books say he is a man who lacks things. It seems a most peculiar state of being. I should like to see one.’

The King was silent for a moment, the concept striking him as one might strike a discordant note in a flawless symphony. ‘A poor man?’ he murmured. ‘What a singular and distressing curiosity. But your wish is my command.’ He descended the tower and summoned his three chief ministers: the Chancellor, who was wise in the ways of commerce; the Chamberlain, who was wise in the ways of etiquette; and the Minister of Gardens, who was wise in the ways of colour. ‘Go forth,’ he commanded, ‘and find me a poor man. I have a fancy to inspect one.’

And so the three ministers, resplendent in their robes of watered silk, descended into the perfect city. They looked for a poor man in the Grand Plaza of Fountains, but there they found only merchants selling perfumes in crystal vials and sculptors chipping at blocks of flawless marble. They sought him in the Alabaster Arcade, but there were only ladies in gowns of moonlight and gentlemen discussing the subtle shades of a sunset. They searched the terraced gardens, where the roses were so perfect they seemed to be made of velvet and the nightingales sang only in harmonious keys. Nowhere was there any sign of this creature of lack. The very architecture of Alabastria, with its soaring spires and immaculate boulevards, seemed designed to repel the very idea of want.

‘It appears,’ said the Chancellor, adjusting his spectacles of smoked quartz, ‘that the city is entirely devoid of poor men. Perhaps it is a matter of definition. Is a man poor who has but one gold-leafed carriage instead of two?’

‘A preposterous notion,’ scoffed the Chamberlain. ‘Such a man would be merely eccentric. Poverty must surely be a matter of sartorial distress. A tear in the brocade, perhaps? A stain upon the velvet?’

But the Minister of Gardens, a man of a more contemplative nature, said nothing. He merely looked at the flawless faces in the crowd and felt a strange disquiet. At last, as the sun began to cast long, violet shadows, he led his companions to the very edge of the city, where the last marble terrace ended and the wild, untended forest began. And there, sitting upon a mossy stone, was a man.

He was not dressed in silk or velvet, but in a simple tunic of undyed wool. His hands were not soft and pale, but brown and calloused. He was eating a piece of coarse bread, and beside him sat a small, brown dog, its ribs faintly visible beneath its coat. The three ministers stared, aghast. This was not a creature of lack, but a creature of essence. His poverty was not an absence of luxury, but a presence of something else entirely.

The Chancellor, ever practical, approached him. ‘You there,’ he said, his voice ringing with authority. ‘Are you, by any chance, a poor man?’

The man looked up, his eyes the colour of the earth after rain. ‘I have a roof of thatch when it rains,’ he said calmly. ‘I have bread when the grain is milled. My dog is my companion. What more is there to want?’

‘A great many things!’ cried the Chamberlain, horrified. ‘Silk! Jewels! A name that is known! You are the very picture of destitution.’

The man smiled, a slow and gentle thing. ‘I have never seen a jewel, and so I do not miss it. My name is known to the sun and the wind. Am I poor because I do not know your names?’

The ministers, unable to comprehend this strange logic, decided to bring him before the King. They led the man and his dog through the immaculate streets, and the people of Alabastria stared as if at a ghost. The man, whose name was Doran, seemed not to notice, his gaze fixed upon the sky or the intricate patterns of the cobblestones.

When he was brought into the throne room, the King regarded him with a mixture of fascination and revulsion. The Prince Calian, however, descended from his dais and stood before the man. He looked at Doran’s weathered face, his simple clothes, his honest hands. He saw not a creature of lack, but a man whole and entire, a man whose very being was a form of wealth that Alabastria had never known.

‘You are the poor man,’ the Prince said, his voice filled with wonder.

‘So I am told,’ replied Doran.

‘What do you lack?’ Calian asked, his heart aching with a new and terrible curiosity.

Doran considered this. ‘Perhaps,’ he said slowly, ‘I lack the knowledge of what it is to be you. To be kept in a tower of lapis lazuli, to see the world only through painted windows. It must be a great and lonely poverty.’

A silence fell upon the throne room, a silence more profound than any music. The King looked at his son. The ministers looked at the floor. And the Prince Calian, for the first time in his life, felt the chill of a true and terrible want. He had asked to see a poor man, and in one sentence, the man had shown him the poverty of his own gilded soul. The world was not a fruit to be plucked, but a bitter and beautiful mystery, and he, the Prince of Alabastria, was its most destitute citizen. He looked out the great window, past the perfect towers and gardens, and saw not a kingdom, but a cage of exquisite design. And for the first time, he longed for the wild, untended forest beyond.
