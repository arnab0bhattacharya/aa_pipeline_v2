The letter had lain open on the narrow lectern for an hour, and I had read it until the black characters swam before my sight like creeping insects on the parchment. I had locked the door of my bedchamber against the noises of the foreign corridor—the clatter of wooden shoes, the harsh guttural of the city tongue, the jangling of the convent bell that called us to our daily slavery. I had locked the door, and standing alone in the centre of my barren cell, had read my financial ruin. The English bank had broken. The small, hard-earned hoard, scraped together by years of uncomplaining toil in a land not my own, was swallowed up in the crash. I was penniless.

I folded the sheet with a steady hand, though every pulse in my wrist beat with a wild, choked violence. I knelt by the bare boards of my bed, but no words of supplication would come. I only looked up into the gray, unfurnished void of the ceiling, and a fierce, burning rebellion awoke in my heart. Why was the bread of the diligent thus snatched away? Why must the solitary worker be stripped to the bone, while the oppressors who gambled with her substance slept securely in their padded English carriages? I was a stranger in a Catholic city, bound to an alien race by the iron chain of necessity; and I was left to face the coming winter with nothing but the daily pittance my employers doled out, and the pride that would not let me own my want.

I rose and washed my face with cold water, binding the frosty restraint of my exterior manner over the seething turmoil within. When I descended to the long, low schoolroom, my step was measured, my eye tranquil, my voice severe. I took my accustomed place before the dull, clouded window, through which the murky light of the northern autumn barely struggled. I heard the barbarous jargon of my pupils—the guttural French of the Walloon city, intermingled with the mongrel patois of the lower streets. They were fifty in number, the daughters of small traders, of bankrupt citizens, and of grasping manufacturers. I looked down the rows of uninteresting faces, and a sudden, bitter scorn took possession of me. They were as nothing to me; I was as nothing to them; yet the iron bond held us, master and slave, chained to the wheel of routine.

"Proceed with your translation, Mademoiselle Elise," I said, as a heavy, staring girl paused in her stumbling rendition of a French classic. My voice did not tremble. I carried my hidden wound with a rigid, stoical silence. I was resolved that no soul in the establishment of Madame Héger should know the destitution that menaced me. I would not be an object of pity, nor worse, of cold, calculating charity.

When the long day of mechanical instruction was over, and the dusk gathered thickly in the narrow street below, shutting out the grim, irregular roofs of the old town, I sat alone in the schoolroom, calculating the dark arithmetic of my future. I possessed exactly three pieces of silver, and a few copper coins, the remainder of my last quarter’s salary. My lodging and board were deducted in advance from my quarterly wages by Madame Héger’s sharp, unsparing pencil. How many weeks could I sustain life on the pittance remaining? Three weeks of absolute starvation I might endure; but starvation, I knew, would bring illness, and illness would betray me. I must buy bread. I reckoned the cost of the blackest, coarsest loaf at the baker's stall in the Rue de la Loi. I calculated the pence. I could live, if I denied myself fire, and lived on crusts, for six weeks. In six weeks, the next quarter's payment would be due.

But between now and then lay a gulf of hazard. What if the lingering cold, the damp fogs rising from the great river, should strike me down with fever? I had no money for a physician; I had no funds for medicine. I must not fall ill. I gripped the edge of the oaken desk until my knuckles whitened, staring into the gathering gloom. I would not ask Madame Héger for an advance. The very thought brought a flush of burning humiliation to my cheek. I knew her—her cold, Belgian eye, her calculating, unsentimental soul. She would look upon my request as a weakness, a fault in the machine of her economy. She would suspect me of extravagance, of vice, perhaps, to have wasted my substance in a foreign land. I would die in the streets before I would expose my naked poverty to her appraising, worldly gaze.

The days that followed were a harsh, unrelenting crucible. I moved through the icy corridors of the establishment like a specter, sustained by a fierce, internal fire. I ate my solitary portion of dry bread in my cell, breaking it with rigid economy, telling myself that the spirit must conquer the flesh, that the soul was more than the body. I taught with a desperate, concentrated energy that startled even myself. I drove my pupils through their insipid lessons with a severity that made them shrink and pale, for I saw in their sluggish ignorance a personal affront, a mockery of the intellectual fire that consumed me. I was hungry, and their apathy was an insult to my hunger. I labored under a pressure of physical and mental strain that threatened every hour to snap the slender thread of my endurance.

One afternoon, a week later, the director, Monsieur Vandenhouten, entered the schoolroom. He was a man of heavy build, and calculating, cold blue eyes, possessing the sluggish, material nature of his race. He stood by the stove, warming his hands, while I conducted a class in English grammar. His presence always weighed upon me like a physical burden; his heavy, phlegmatic gaze seemed to reduce all effort to a question of francs and centimes. When the hour concluded, he detained me with a gesture.

"Mademoiselle Leigh," he said, in his deliberate, guttural accent, "you push these children too hard. They are not machines of iron, like your English. They have nerves, they have temperaments. You make them nervous and rebellious. You must soften your methods."

A sudden, fierce retort rushed to my lips. I longed to tell him that they were dolts, that their temperaments were sluggish clay, that they needed the iron rod of discipline to rouse them from their native torpor. But I restrained myself. I stood before him, pale, slender, my hands clasped behind me, my eyes steadily meeting his heavy, cold gaze. I felt the sharp pangs of hunger gnawing at my vitals, the exhaustion of sleepless nights pressing upon my brain, and beneath it all, the fierce, unquenchable pride that would not bend.

"I shall endeavor to adapt myself to the requirements of the establishment, Monsieur," I said, in a level, measured tone. "But I cannot teach without exertion. Knowledge does not enter the mind by mere passive reception."

He grunted, turning away. "You are too intense, Mademoiselle. It is not healthy. It disturbs the equilibrium of the school."

He left me, and I sank into my chair, trembling with a reaction I dared not betray. The equilibrium of the school! What was its equilibrium to me, standing on the edge of an abyss? I looked out into the darkening street, where the rain was beginning to fall in long, slanting lines against the ancient gables. The wind moaned around the corners of the building, a fitting voice for the desolation that had settled upon my soul. I thought of England, of the gray, rain-swept moors I had known in childhood, of the freedom that had once seemed my birthright. I thought of the God who had made me, and I wondered if He had forgotten me in this foreign land. But even as the despair pressed upon me, a stubborn flame of resistance flickered up in my heart. I would not yield. I would not ask. I would endure.

That night, as I knelt by my bed in the freezing darkness, the convent bell tolled the hour of midnight. The sound vibrated through the still, heavy air, a deep, solemn note that seemed to echo the emptiness of my condition. I pressed my face against the cold, hard wood of the floor, and a sob broke from my lips. It was the first sign of weakness, and I strove to choke it back. I clung to my silence, to my solitary, unshared burden. I was alone, utterly alone, in a city of strangers. But I was still myself. The iron had entered into my soul, but it had not broken it. I would rise again on the morrow, and take up my cross, and walk the narrow, thorny path to the end.
