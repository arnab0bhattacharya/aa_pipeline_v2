The sun had already sunk behind the western cordillera when I came at last into the town of San Jerónimo de la Cruz, and the valley lay in that profound and luminous dusk which is peculiar to the high countries of the Andes, where the air is so thin and clear that the departing light seems to linger upon the very stones with a kind of obstinate tenderness, as if reluctant to abandon a world it could no longer warm. I had ridden since dawn, and my mule was spent, and I was myself in that condition of bodily fatigue which, in men of my profession, produces a strange lucidity of mind—a detachment, as it were, from the flesh, in which the impressions of the day arrange themselves into patterns that the waking and active man would never perceive.

Below me, in the hollow of the hills, the roofs of the town were already losing their outline, and the river—the Rio Turbio, which the Indians call by a name I shall not attempt to render—caught here and there a gleam of the vanished sun and held it, motionless, like a thread of molten metal laid across the darkening land. Beyond, the mountains rose in vast, sombre masses, their summits still faintly rosy with the afterglow, and among them, invisible now, lay the passes through which my railway was to go—or rather, through which it was not to go, for the survey had stopped at the foot of the first great barrier, and the question of the passes had become, in the six weeks of my residence in this country, something more than a question of gradients and tunnelling.

It was at the house of Don Anselmo Reyes, the chief engineer of the projected line, that I learned, that very evening, how matters stood. Don Anselmo was a man of some fifty years, grey, spare, with the manners of a grandee and the eyes of a tired priest; he had been educated in London, in the days of the first great railway fever, and he spoke English with a precision that was almost painful, as though each word had been separately examined and approved before being permitted to pass his lips.

“You have heard,” he said to me, when we were alone upon the verandah, and the servant had brought coffee and the inevitable aguardiente, “you have heard of General Barrientos.”

I had heard of him. Who, in that republic, had not? He was the commander of the rebel forces in the sierra, the man who held the passes, the man whose name was spoken in the capital with a shrug and in the villages with a whisper. He was, they said, half Indian and half Spaniard, and wholly a soldier; he had been in arms, in one cause or another, since he was fourteen years old; he had killed men with his own hand, and had seen his own men killed, and had learned, in that hard school, a philosophy which he was fond of expressing in a phrase that had become proverbial among his followers: that civilization, like charity, begins at home.

“He has sent for you,” said Don Anselmo, without looking at me. “He desires to see the English engineer who is to build the railway. He has, he says, a great respect for the English. He wishes to discuss with you the question of the passes.”

I asked what answer Don Anselmo had returned.

“I have returned no answer,” he said. “I am not, in this matter, the person to be consulted. You are the engineer of the company. The company has its arrangements with the government of the republic. The government of the republic has its arrangements—or its difficulties—with General Barrientos. I am only the servant of the company, and my business is to build the railway where I am told. But I am also, my friend, a man who has lived long enough in this country to know that a railway is not built with rails and sleepers alone. It is built with—how shall I say?—with understandings.”

He was silent for a moment, and then he added, with a certain reluctance, as though the words cost him something:

“The government has promised you its protection. The government has also promised you—I do not say it is a promise that can be kept—the sum of fifty thousand pesos for the completion of the survey. General Barrientos has promised you—what? He has promised you that if you build the railway through the passes, he will not oppose you. He has promised you that the railway will bring civilization. He has promised you—and this, I think, is the most significant of his promises—that he will make you a colonel in his army.”

I confess that I laughed. The idea of myself, a railway engineer, with a colonel’s commission in the forces of a rebel general, appeared to me then, as it appears to me now, in the highest degree absurd. But Don Anselmo did not laugh. He looked at me with those tired, priest-like eyes of his, and he said:

“You laugh. That is very English. But I assure you, my friend, that General Barrientos does not laugh. He is a man who has a great sense of the fitness of things. He sees you—a foreigner, a man of science, a man who has built railways in other countries—he sees you as a force, as a power, as something that may be used. And he sees the government—the government of his enemies—as something that may be used also. He does not care for the railway. He cares for what the railway can do. He cares for the men who will come with the railway, and the money that will come with the men, and the influence that will come with the money. He is not a fool, my friend. He is a very clever man.”

The night had come on while he spoke, and the valley was now a gulf of darkness, in which the river was no longer visible, and the mountains were only a deeper blackness against the stars. Somewhere in the town a dog barked, and another dog answered it, and then a woman’s voice, high and querulous, called out a name, and was silent. The smell of the earth, of the dry, aromatic scrub of the hills, came to us on the night wind, and with it, faint and intermittent, the sound of a guitar, played with that melancholy and monotonous insistence which seems to be the natural music of these high and lonely places.

“And what,” I asked at last, “does the government say?”

Don Anselmo shrugged his shoulders.

“The government,” he said, “says what governments always say. It says that the railway is a great work of civilization. It says that the passes are the key to the country. It says that General Barrientos is a bandit, and that his followers are bandits, and that they will be dealt with in due course. It says that you are to proceed with your survey, and that you will be protected. It says—and this is the most important thing—it says that it has no money.”

He paused, and then he added, with a certain dry bitterness:

“The government has no money, but it has promises. General Barrientos has no money either, but he has the passes. And you, my friend, have neither money nor passes. You have only your science, and your English conscience, and your—how do you say?—your sense of duty. I do not envy you.”

I sat for a long time in silence, looking out into the darkness. I thought of the line I had surveyed, of the cuttings and the embankments, of the bridges that were to span the torrents, of the tunnel that was to pierce the mountain’s heart. I thought of the men who would come to build it—the Irish navvies, the Italian masons, the Indian labourers—and of the men who would come after them, the merchants and the miners and the settlers, and of the towns that would grow up along the line, and of the civilization that would follow in its wake. And I thought of General Barrientos, in his camp among the passes, with his Indian soldiers and his mestizo officers, and his dream—for it was a dream, however brutal—of a new order in this old and troubled land.

And I understood, in that moment, with a clarity that was almost painful, that I was caught between two forces, each of which claimed me, each of which promised me its protection, each of which demanded my loyalty; and that neither of them cared, in the least, for the railway itself, or for the civilization it was to bring, except as a means to an end—the end being power.

The guitar had ceased. The dogs were silent. The night was very still, and the stars burned with that cold and steadfast brightness which they have in the high countries, where the air is clear and the earth is very far away. I rose at last, and said good-night to Don Anselmo, and went to my room, where a candle was burning before a little image of the Virgin, and a jug of water stood on the table, and the bed was narrow and hard, as beds are in countries where men have other things to think of than their comfort.

I did not sleep. I lay awake, listening to the silence, and thinking of the morrow, when I was to ride up into the passes, and meet the man who held them, and hear what he had to say. And I confess that I was afraid—not with the fear of a coward, but with the fear of a man who sees, suddenly and clearly, the abyss that lies beneath the smooth surface of the world, and knows that he must walk upon it, and that there is no turning back.
