The village of Ashbury Hollow lies in a fold of the downs where the Thames, in its younger course, winds sluggish and brown between banks of willow-herb, and the beechwoods climb close upon the cottages as if to reclaim them. It was there, in the third year after the fever had passed through the valley like a scythe through standing corn, that I came to dwell in the small house my husband had built, and there that I resolved to live out my days in the solitude which had become my only consolation.

The pestilence had taken from me my two children—my Edmund, who was nine, and my little Catherine, who had but turned four when the fever kindled in her cheeks and consumed her in a fortnight. My husband, who had gone to Reading to fetch the physician, returned to find the door of our cottage sealed with the mark of the sickness, and he stood in the lane all that night, calling my name through the boards, until at dawn the constable led him away. He did not sicken; but the grief of that night entered his bones, and before the frost came he was laid beside our children in the churchyard on the hill. Thus was I left alone in the world, and I desired no other company than my memories, which, though they pierced me, were yet the only companions I could endure.

It was in the autumn of that year that the woman called Margaret Hale came to my door. She was the widow of the parish clerk, a bustling, warm-hearted creature, whose charity had outlived her discretion, and who had taken upon herself the care of such orphans as the fever had left scattered through the county. She stood upon my threshold with her shawl drawn tight against the wind, and in her eyes that look of gentle importunity which I had learned to dread.

“Mrs. Ellwood,” said she, “I would not trouble you, knowing your affliction; but there is a boy, a little silent creature of six years, whose mother died in the pest-house at Newbury, and whose father none can find, living or dead. He has been passed from house to house, and no one will keep him, for he does not speak, and they say he is simple. But I have seen him, and I think his silence is not want of wit but excess of sorrow. You have a room that stands empty, and a heart that has known loss. I ask you to take him.”

I answered her with a coldness that I remember now with shame. “I have no room,” I said, “for any child. My house is a tomb, and I am its keeper. Take him elsewhere.”

She urged, and I refused; she pleaded, and I grew bitter. “Do you think,” I cried, “that because I have lost my own, I am fit to raise the offspring of strangers? The sight of a child is to me a knife turned in the wound. I will not have him. Let the parish do its duty.”

Margaret Hale went away sorrowing, and I shut my door and sat down in the dark, and wept until I had no tears. Yet I was not at peace; for in the days that followed, whenever I went to the well or to the baker’s cart, I saw the boy. He was a slight, grave creature, with hair the colour of damp straw and eyes of a pale, still grey, and he stood at the edge of the green and watched me, not with the bold stare of childhood, but with a patient, questioning look, as though he waited for a word that he had long since ceased to expect. I turned my face from him and hurried by.

But he would not be dismissed. On the third day he was at my gate; on the fourth, upon my very doorstep; and when I opened the door to bid him begone, he did not run, but stood looking up at me, and held out his hand, in which lay a small thing—a button of bone, such as a child might treasure, or a pebble worn round by the river, or, as I saw when I took it, a little wooden bird, rudely carved, its paint all worn away. I knew it at once; my Edmund had carved such birds, and given them to his sister, and one of them I had placed in the earth with him. The sight of it smote me, and I cried out and thrust it back upon the child, and bade him never come again.

He came again. He came every day, and every day he brought some trifle—a feather, a sprig of yew, a horse-chestnut polished by his thumb—and laid it upon my step, and went away without a word. The neighbours began to remark upon it; some said the boy was bewitched, and some that I was cruel to drive him off; and Margaret Hale came once more, and said, “He will not be kept from you. He has chosen you, though he cannot say why. Take him in, I beseech you, before the winter takes him.”

I do not know what it was that at last overcame me—whether the sight of the frost whitening the downs, and the thought of the child sleeping in a barn; or the memory of my Catherine’s hand in mine; or that strange, unspoken claim which the little carved bird had laid upon my soul. But on a bitter evening in November, when the rain beat against the panes and the wind howled in the chimney, I heard a faint knocking at my door, and I opened it, and there he stood, drenched and shivering, with a sprig of holly in his hand, and I took him by the arm and drew him in, and set him before the fire, and dried his hair with my own towel, and gave him bread and milk; and when he had eaten, he looked at me, and his lips moved, and he said, in a voice like a whisper of wind in dry leaves, “Thank you, ma’am.”

That was all. But from that night he abode with me, and I called him Thomas, after my father, and he grew, and was silent still, but not sullen; and in time I came to love him, not as I had loved my own—for that love lies buried in the churchyard on the hill—but with a love that was tempered by sorrow and made patient by remembrance. And often, when the evening fire burned low and he sat at my feet with his wooden bird in his hand, I thought of the words of the preacher, that the heart of the mourner is not shut forever, and that even in the ashes of our desolation there may be found a seed of comfort, if only we have the grace to receive it.
