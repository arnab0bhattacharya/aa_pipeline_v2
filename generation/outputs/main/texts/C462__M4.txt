The rain had been falling since morning, a soft, persistent drizzle that turned the London streets to a slick of grey mud and made the air smell of wet stone and horse. Julian Ashworth, standing at the window of his cousin’s townhouse in Half Moon Street, watched a hackney coach rattle past and felt the full weight of his own insignificance. He was a country parson’s son, in town on the charity of his mother’s grander connections, and every stitch of his good blue coat felt like a borrowed skin.

His cousin, the Honourable Horatia Finch, had taken him up as a project. “You have a pleasing countenance, Julian,” she had declared on his arrival, “and a certain rustic earnestness that the ladies may find refreshing. We shall make a gentleman of you yet.” This chiefly involved dragging him to assemblies where he stood by the wall, and to promenades where he was jostled by people of far greater consequence.

It was at one such promenade, in the genteel wilderness of Hyde Park, that he first saw Miss Clara Bellamy. She was walking with a party of fashionable young ladies, her pelisse a startling shade of emerald green, her bonnet framing a face of such lively, unpretentious beauty that Julian’s heart performed a small, painful somersault. Horatia, ever alert to the social barometer, leaned close. “That is Miss Bellamy. Her father is a baronet, you know. Quite the heiress. They say she has ten thousand a year.”

Ten thousand a year. The figure was so astronomical to Julian, whose entire annual allowance was a modest two hundred pounds, that it seemed less a sum of money than a distant, glittering star. He watched Clara Bellamy laugh at something her companion said, and felt a gulf open between them as wide as the Serpentine.

His opportunity came, as such opportunities do, by accident. A week later, at a crowded subscription ball in a stifling room on King Street, he was pushed by the press of bodies and trod squarely on a small, satin-clad foot. He turned, mortified, to apologise, and found himself looking into the very face that had haunted his dreams. Miss Bellamy, her foot smarting, looked up at him with an expression of pained surprise that quickly melted into amusement.

“I do beg your pardon,” Julian stammered, his ears burning. “I was clumsy. The crush is abominable.”

“It is,” she agreed, her voice a pleasant, musical alto. “And my foot will forgive you, sir, if you will procure me a glass of ratafia. I am quite parched.”

He returned with the drink in record time, his heart hammering. They fell into conversation, and Julian, to his own astonishment, found it easy. She was witty and warm, with none of the affected airs he had dreaded. She asked him about himself, and he, desperate not to appear the country nobody, spoke of his cousin’s house, of the season’s diversions, and of his “interests” in a manner so vague as to suggest a man of leisure. When she asked where his family seat was, he named his father’s parsonage in Devonshire, but added, with a wave of his hand, “It is a small estate, but the country is fine.” He saw her eyes light with interest, and a terrible, thrilling vanity took hold of him.

For her part, Clara Bellamy was equally captivated. Here was a young man of genuine feeling, not a fop or a fortune-hunter. He was handsome in a serious, unassuming way, and he spoke to her as a person, not as a prize to be won. When he alluded, however vaguely, to his “estate” and his “interests,” her imagination, fired by the romantic novels she devoured, supplied the rest. He was clearly a gentleman of property, perhaps a younger son of a noble house, choosing to be modest. Her own circumstances, which she was too proud to explain, only made his apparent wealth more reassuring.

The misunderstanding, thus planted, flourished in the hotbed of the season. They met at Almack’s, where he was permitted a single dance; at the theatre, where their eyes met across the pit; and on repeated walks in the park, where their conversation grew more intimate. He told her of his love for the poetry of Wordsworth, and she confessed her secret passion for the novels of Mrs. Radcliffe. He thought her the most natural, unaffected creature in London. She thought him the most sincere and discerning man she had ever met. He imagined a future where he might, by some miracle, be worthy of her ten thousand a year. She imagined a future where his wealth would save her from a fate she dared not name.

The truth, of course, was quite different. Clara’s father, Sir Percival Bellamy, was a baronet whose fortunes had been squandered on a disastrous investment in a canal scheme and a fondness for the gaming tables. The ten thousand a year was a fiction maintained by his wife, a woman of formidable social ambition, who believed that a good marriage for her daughter was the only means of salvaging the family from ruin. Clara, far from being an heiress, was the principal asset in a desperate financial gamble. Her emerald pelisse was a cleverly altered gown of the previous season, and her pin money was a thing of the past.

The crisis came on a Tuesday afternoon in June. Julian, emboldened by a letter from his father hinting that a living might soon be vacant in the gift of a distant cousin, determined to speak to Clara’s father. He would confess his love, and his modest prospects, and hope that Sir Percival might value sincerity over wealth. He called at the Bellamy house in Grosvenor Square, his heart in his throat.

He was shown into the library, a room lined with books that looked as though they had never been opened. Sir Percival, a florid man with a nervous tic in his left eye, listened to Julian’s halting declaration with an expression of mounting bewilderment.

“But, my dear fellow,” Sir Percival interrupted, “I was given to understand you were a man of considerable property. My wife said… that is, we were led to believe…”

At that moment, the library door opened and Lady Bellamy swept in, her face a mask of strained politeness. She had been listening. “Mr. Ashworth,” she said, her voice like broken glass, “I think there has been a grave misunderstanding. My daughter is not… that is to say, we are not in a position to entertain an offer from a gentleman of such… limited means.”

The floor seemed to open beneath Julian’s feet. “Limited means?” he echoed, his face draining of colour. “But I was told… Miss Bellamy’s fortune…”

Lady Bellamy’s laugh was a brittle, terrible sound. “Her fortune? Sir, you have been misinformed. It is we who were misinformed about you.” Her eyes narrowed. “Are you telling me you have no estate? No income beyond a parson’s pittance?”

The full, humiliating truth crashed over him. He was not a catch; he was a fraud. And they thought him a fraud of a different sort. He stammered his apologies, his explanations dying in his throat, and fled the house, his borrowed coat feeling like a suit of motley.

He did not see Clara, who was listening from the top of the stairs, her hand pressed to her mouth. She had heard everything. Her own deception, her family’s desperate gamble, was laid bare alongside his. He was poor. He was not her rescuer, but another struggler in the same harsh world. And he believed her to be a rich woman who had rejected him for his poverty.

For two days, Julian did not leave Half Moon Street. Horatia, sensing a disaster, left him to his gloom. On the third day, a letter arrived, written on a single sheet of paper in a hand he knew.

*I am not an heiress. I have not a penny to my name but what my mother allows me for ribbons. I am sorry to have deceived you. I did not mean to. I thought you were rich, and I was afraid to tell you the truth. – C.B.*

He read it three times, his heart a riot of shame and relief and a wild, impossible hope. He seized his hat and ran all the way to Grosvenor Square, arriving breathless and dishevelled on the Bellamy doorstep.

Clara was in the small garden at the back, a patch of green hidden from the street. She was sitting on a stone bench, her hands in her lap, and she did not look up when he approached.

“I have ten thousand a year,” he said, and she started, looking at him as if he were mad. “It is a lie. I have two hundred, and a father who might find me a living. I am a parson’s son, and I have nothing to offer you but a very small house and a great deal of affection.”

She stared at him for a long moment, and then, to his astonishment, she began to laugh. It was a shaky, tearful laugh, but it was real. “I have a baronet for a father,” she said, “and a mother who has spent my dowry three times over on hats. I have nothing to offer you but a very bad temper and a fondness for novels.”

He sat down beside her on the bench, the damp stone seeping through his trousers. “That sounds,” he said, his voice unsteady, “like a very fair exchange.”

The rain had stopped. A weak sun was breaking through the clouds over Mayfair, and in the small, hidden garden, two penniless people, both thoroughly embarrassed and entirely relieved, began to laugh at the absurdity of it all.
