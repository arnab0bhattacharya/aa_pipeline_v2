The rain on the windowpane was not water, but a smearing of the world outside, a grey blurring that made the streetlamps look like bruises on the skin of the night. Arthur stood in his office, the silence of the room pressing against his eardrums with a physical weight, heavier than the paperweights on his desk, heavier than the unopened mail. It was 1915, or perhaps 1916; the years had lost their distinct edges, becoming a single, elongated stretch of beige. He looked at the clock. The hands had stopped at four o’clock, a small rebellion of the mechanism, or perhaps a mercy. He did not wind it. To wind it would be to acknowledge the passage of time, and time was the very thing that had stolen from him. He thought of Edmund, who had gone to Flanders with a lightness that seemed then like a grace, a boyish dash, but which now, in the memory, felt like a severing. A clean cut. Arthur’s life had been divided into *before* and *after* the cut, and he lived entirely in the after, a hollow space where the boy used to be.

Across the city, in a room that smelled of carbolic and damp wool, Elara sat by the bed of a girl who was not her sister, but whom she watched with the same ferocity of attention she once gave to her own hands. The girl was asleep, her face a map of small, white scars. Elara’s own hands lay in her lap, still, the fingers slightly curled as if holding an invisible, fragile bird. She had been sent here not for her body, which was whole, but for the noise in her head, the shelling that echoed when there was no shell. She remembered the letter from home, the ink smudged by tears or rain, the mother’s handwriting shaky, asking only if he had written, if the post had come. It had not. The letters crossed in the air like birds in a storm, missing each other by inches, by days, by months. She had written to Edmund, to Arthur, to the empty chair, and the words had gone out into the void, returning only as silence.

At home, in the house on the hill that had begun to lean slightly, as if tired of standing upright, Margaret stood before the mirror. She was not looking at her face, but at the space behind it, the years that had accumulated there like dust. She was fifty, or sixty; it did not matter. What mattered was the sound of the clock in the hall, ticking, ticking, a relentless metronome counting down to a moment that might never arrive. She had kept the house in order, the silver polished, the curtains drawn at dusk, a ritual of containment. If the house was perfect, perhaps the world outside was not so broken. She had cooked for three, though only two remained, and the third place setting was for the ghost of the son, the empty chair that occupied more space than the furniture. She waited for the postman, a man in a blue uniform who moved with the speed of a hawk, but whose hands were always empty, or held only the bills that confirmed the debt of living.

The war had not just killed men; it had killed the language between them. They had spoken in the old way, before the trenches, before the gas, before the letters became brief, coded things. *All well. Sentiments to all. E.* That was all. No description of the mud, the cold, the screaming. Just *all well*, a lie so large it could house the whole truth inside it. And at home, Margaret had written back: *The roses are early this year. We hope for your return.* A lie, too. She did not hope; hope was a luxury, a bright thing that burned the eyes. She waited. That was all. She waited for the sound of the front door, the creak of the stairs, the voice that would say, *Mother, I am here.*

In Flanders, the mud was not brown, but a living, sucking thing, a mouth that swallowed boots and bones alike. Edmund lay in the trench, his face pressed against the earth, the smell of it filling his nostrils, sweet and rotting. He thought of his mother’s roses. He could see them, red and vivid against the grey sky of his memory, but they were fading, losing their color, becoming sketches in pencil. He tried to write a letter, but the pencil snapped. He looked at the sky, a thin strip of blue above the barbed wire. It was the same sky as over London, over the hospital, over the office. It was the only thing that connected them, a cold, indifferent vault that covered them all. He closed his eyes. In the darkness behind his lids, he saw his father’s face, not the stern, silhouetted figure of authority, but the man who had once carried him on his shoulders. He saw his sister’s eyes, bright with a fever that was not hers, but his. He saw his mother’s hands, trembling as she held the empty plate. They were all there, in the dark, separated by miles and miles of silence, bound together by the thread of blood that pulled tight, taut, ready to snap.

The war ended in November, but the silence did not. It lingered, a fog that settled into the lungs. Arthur went to the station, his coat buttoned to the chin, his face a mask of neutrality. He waited for the train that would bring him home, or perhaps the train that would bring him nowhere. The whistle blew, a long, mournful note that cut through the air. The train came. He looked at the faces, looking for the one he knew, the one he had lost. But the faces were new, or old in a way he did not recognize. They were strangers, all of them. He boarded the train, not because he knew where it was going, but because staying still was impossible. The motion began, a gentle lurch, then a steady hum. He looked out the window, watching the fields scroll by, green and grey, indifferent to the history of the men who watched them.

At home, Margaret opened the door. The air rushed in, cold and sharp. She stood in the hallway, listening. There was no sound. No footsteps on the stairs. No voice. Just the clock, ticking, ticking. She walked to the room where Edmund had slept, the bed made, the pillow still holding the shape of his head. She touched the linen. It was cold. She sat on the edge of the bed, her hands in her lap, still, the fingers slightly curled. She thought of the letters, the ones she had written, the ones she had received, the ones that had crossed and missed. They were all in the waste paper basket, a pile of white squares, a monument to the things unsaid. She looked at the window. The rain had stopped. The sun was breaking through the clouds, a pale, thin light that did not warm, but only illuminated the dust motes dancing in the air. They were suspended there, caught in the light, invisible, insignificant. She was one of them. She was a mote in the sunbeam, suspended between the earth and the sky, between the past and the future, between the living and the dead.

Arthur arrived at the house an hour later. He stood at the door, the key in his hand, the metal cold against his palm. He did not turn it. He looked at the house, the leaning walls, the dark windows. It looked like a ship on a stormy sea, waiting to capsize. He thought of Elara, in her hospital room, watching the scars on another’s face. He thought of Margaret, in the hallway, listening to the silence. He thought of Edmund, in the mud, smelling the earth. They were all there, in the house, in the office, in the hospital, in the trench, but they were not together. They were separated by the war, by the years, by the silence that had grown between them like a wall of stone. He turned the key. The door opened with a creak, a sound like a sigh. He stepped inside. The house was empty. The clock in the hall was still stopped at four o’clock. He looked at it. He reached out, and with a trembling hand, he wound it. The hands began to move, the second hand ticking, a small, precise sound, marking the time that had been lost, the time that would never be regained. He sat down in the armchair by the fire, the coal glowing red, a small, dying light. He closed his eyes. In the darkness, he saw them all, standing in a line, facing away from him. They did not turn. They did not speak. They were strangers, bound by blood, by memory, by the terrible, quiet weight of what had been. And the clock ticked on, counting the seconds, one by one, into the night.
