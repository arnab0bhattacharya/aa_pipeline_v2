The house in Nettlebury’s Mill Lane, a tall, narrow tenement of grey brick, with a fanlight over the door like a single sober eye, had for twelve years been the dwelling of Mrs. Agatha Pemberton, widow of the late Dr. Pemberton, who had attended the better families of the town through two influenzas and one contested election, and had left his relict a modest competence, a mahogany sideboard, and the habit of considering herself a woman of substance. Since the doctor’s death, Mrs. Pemberton had let her upper floor to a succession of single gentlemen—a curate, a traveller in seeds, a retired exciseman—all of whom had departed with the ordinary complaints of damp chimneys and early breakfasts, and none of whom had left behind them so much as a legend. But in the Michaelmas of 1834 there came to her door a person of another species, a foreign gentleman by the name of Herr Albrecht Dürnberg, who spoke English with a guttural precision, paid a quarter’s rent in advance in gold that had the mintage of no kingdom Mrs. Pemberton could name, and required only that his rooms should not be entered after ten o’clock in the morning, and that his meals should be left upon a tray outside his door.

It is a fact well known to those who have lived in small towns, that a mystery is a commodity which the town will not consent to leave in the hands of its owner. Nettlebury was a place where every curtain had its appointed twitch, and where the butcher’s boy, who carried the gossip from the shambles to the Rectory, was the true historian of the age. Within a fortnight it was established at the Crown and Mitre that the foreign gentleman was a spy of the French; within three weeks, that he was an alchemist who had fled his own country with a secret for turning pewter into silver; and by the end of the month, that he was nothing less than a prince in disguise, who had taken refuge in Mill Lane from the machinations of a usurping uncle. Mrs. Pemberton, who was a woman of strict veracity, and who felt the dignity of her position as the widow of a professional man, contradicted these reports with a severity that only served to confirm them. She said that Herr Dürnberg was a gentleman of independent fortune, engaged in literary pursuits; and the town, which had never known a literary pursuit to keep a man from his bed before two in the morning, smiled and said no more in her hearing.

But the person most affected by the presence of the lodger was Mrs. Pemberton’s daughter, Miss Harriet, a girl of two-and-twenty, who had been educated at a seminary in Warwick, and who had returned thence with a smattering of French, a talent for water-colour, and a restless intelligence which had no proper employment in a household of two servants and a weekly wash. Harriet had nothing to do. She dusted the whatnot, she copied out her mother’s receipts, she practised her scales upon the piano until the lodger’s door was heard to open and shut; and she had read all the novels in the circulating library, including those which the librarian, a timid man, kept under the counter. It was inevitable that her mind should fasten upon the upper floor as a blank space in the map of her existence, and that she should begin to fill it with conjectures. She had seen Herr Dürnberg but twice: once upon the stairs, where he had bowed with a foreign courtesy that seemed to her more elegant than the clumsy salutations of the Nettlebury young men; and once at his window at dusk, where his profile, dark against the last of the light, had the look of a man in a portrait, removed from the common air.

It was in the second month of his tenancy that Harriet, being sent up with a letter that had come for him by the evening post, found his door ajar, and, receiving no answer to her knock, was betrayed by that curiosity which is the natural daughter of idleness into a step within the room. The apartment was in the greatest disorder. Books lay open upon the table in a dozen languages, or in characters that were not languages at all; a map of the county was pinned to the wall, with certain villages marked in red chalk; and upon the hearth, half consumed, lay a heap of papers, the charred edges of which still showed a fine, close handwriting. Harriet had but a moment to take in these particulars before she heard a step upon the stair, and had barely time to retreat to the landing and assume the countenance of a person who has knocked twice and waited with patience, when Herr Dürnberg appeared, and, seeing the letter in her hand, thanked her with a gravity that had in it something of a rebuke. His eyes, she observed, were of a pale grey, and had the trick of seeming to look past the person addressed, as if the person were a transparency.

That night Harriet could not sleep. She told herself that she had done nothing wrong, that the door was open, that a letter is a public trust; but the image of the red-chalked map would not leave her. She rose before dawn, and, wrapping herself in her mother’s old pelisse, went out into the garden, which sloped down to the mill-stream, and there, upon the frozen grass, she found a scrap of paper that the wind had carried from the burnt heap and dropped beneath the wall. It was scorched at one corner, but the writing was legible, and it was in English, though written in a foreign hand: *—the fourth of the month, at the sign of the Green Man, and let no one be told, for the town is full of eyes.* Harriet read it three times, and then, with a beating heart, she folded it small and hid it in her bosom.

From that morning her investigation proceeded with the stealthy ardour of a campaign. She watched the lodger’s hours and found them regular in their irregularity; she noted that on Tuesdays he went out at dusk and returned at midnight, and that on Fridays he received no letters. She made friends with the girl who came to char the grates, and learned that Herr Dürnberg’s linen was marked with a cipher that was not his name, and that his boots were made in a fashion unknown to Nettlebury. She even, in the boldness of her zeal, contrived to be in the passage when the postman came, and to examine the superscriptions of his letters, which were directed in various hands, some of them with a foreign seal. And all the while she said nothing to her mother, partly because she feared to be laughed at, and partly because she had begun to feel that peculiar pleasure which attends the possession of a secret—a pleasure which is not the less sweet for being a little guilty.

But the end of these discoveries was not what she had expected. One evening in March, as she sat at the window with her work, she saw Herr Dürnberg come out of the house and cross the lane toward the mill, and, obeying an impulse she could not govern, she took her cloak and followed him. He went by the water-side, where the willows were just breaking into bud, and turned into the path that led to the Green Man; and there, in the little sanded parlour of that inn, she saw him sit down with a man in a fustian coat, whom she knew for a Nettlebury man, a quiet, dull fellow who kept the toll-bar and was reputed to be a Methodist. The two talked low for half an hour; and once the toll-bar man took out a paper and showed it to the foreigner, and the foreigner nodded and made a note in his pocket-book. Harriet, standing in the shadow of the opposite hedge, felt her grand romance shrink suddenly into something small and sordid. She had imagined a prince, a conspirator, a man of mystery; and here was a man of business, transacting with a toll-bar keeper the dull affairs of a land-agent or a corn-factor. The red-chalked map, the burnt papers, the cipher on the linen—all these were doubtless the common furniture of a commercial traveller, and she had been a fool.

She went home ashamed, and for a week she would not look at the upper window. But on the eighth day, as she passed the lodger on the stairs, he stopped her and said, with his grave courtesy, “Miss Pemberton, I think you have dropped something of mine.” And he held out to her the scrap of scorched paper that she had hidden in her bosom, and which she had that morning missed from its place. Harriet turned scarlet and then pale; but the foreigner only smiled, a little sadly, and said, “You have been very good to take an interest in a stranger. I am not a prince, and I am not a spy; I am a collector of old songs, and the toll-bar man knows the ballads of the county. But I thank you for your kindness.” And he bowed and went up the stairs. Harriet stood upon the landing with the paper in her hand, and felt that she had learned a lesson which no seminary in Warwick could have taught her: that the world is not divided into the mysterious and the common, but that every life, if we look closely, has its red-chalked map and its burnt papers, and that the most romantic thing in Nettlebury was the simple fact that a foreign gentleman should care for the old songs of a toll-bar man. She put the paper in the fire, and went down to help her mother with the receipts.
