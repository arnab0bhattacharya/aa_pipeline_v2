The morning had come up over St James’s with a kind of grey reluctance, the light not so much falling as filtering, thinning the fog that hung in the streets into a pale vapour that smelt of wet stone and the exhaust of motor-cabs. Colonel Arthur Merridew, who had walked from his rooms in Ryder Street, found the steps of the club already damp, and the brass of the door-handle cold to the hand, and the hall beyond—that vast, dim, brown hall with its marble columns and its clock that ticked so slowly that one might have thought the hours themselves were reluctant to be spent—received him into its particular silence, which was not the silence of emptiness but of many men not speaking.

He took his usual chair. It was understood, without anyone having ever said so, that the chair by the third window was his; he had sat in it for eleven years, and the leather had taken the shape of him, and the arm on the left had a small worn place where his thumb rested. He unfolded The Times. The paper crackled, and the sound was enormous in that hush, and old General Fotheringay, three chairs away, lowered his own paper by a quarter of an inch, and looked over the top of it, and raised it again. That was the whole of their conversation, and it was sufficient; it had been sufficient for years.

The words on the page did not so much enter his mind as drift across it, like the shadows of clouds crossing a field—a strike in the north, a debate in the House, the price of something or other, a divorce, a death, a cricket score. His eye caught, and held, on a photograph of a girl in a white dress standing beside an aeroplane, and for no reason at all he thought of his wife, dead these fifteen years, standing in the garden at Byfleet with the secateurs in her hand, saying something about the roses that he had not listened to, and would now give a great deal to hear again. The thought came and went. He turned the page.

At one o’clock he rose, folded the paper, and went in to lunch. He ate his lunch alone at a small table by the wall—the soup, the cold beef, the Stilton, the glass of claret—and the waiter, whose name was Perkins and who had a daughter at a school in Streatham, said, as he always said, “Cold again today, sir,” and the Colonel said, as he always said, “Cold again, Perkins,” and that too was sufficient. He ate slowly, and the dining-room filled and emptied around him, and the talk of other tables rose and fell—something about the Derby, something about a man called Hargreaves who had got himself into a scrape, laughter at the far end where the young fellows sat—and it all washed past him, a sea of voices, and he was a rock in it, or perhaps only a pebble, worn smooth.

After lunch he did not go back to the chair by the window. He went instead to the smoking-room, where the fire was, and sat with his hands folded on his waistcoat and his eyes half closed, and let the afternoon come down. The clock ticked. Somewhere a coal settled in the grate with a soft collapse. He was thinking—if it could be called thinking, this slow drift and eddy of images—of the boy. Of the boy at seven, in a sailor suit, at Weymouth, holding a bucket and refusing to go into the water; of the boy at eighteen, thin and furious, on the platform at Waterloo, going back to the Front; of the boy, last Christmas, at the flat, saying very little, smoking too much, standing at the window with his back to the room. There had been a girl’s name, he thought. Or perhaps not. He could not remember. He had not asked, and the boy had not told him, and the silence between them had grown to be a thing with a shape and a weight, a third presence in the room, which neither of them knew how to put out.

It was perhaps four o’clock when Perkins came to him, carrying a small silver salver, on which lay a telegram. The Colonel took it, and turned it over, and looked at it, and did not open it at once; for a telegram, in his experience, was never anything but bad news, and there was a moment—a small, absurd, human moment—in which he would have given anything not to know what was inside it. Then he tore it open.

*Married this morning,* it said. *Alice Prendergast. Very happy. Letter follows. — Hugh.*

He read it once. He read it again. He turned it over, as though there might be something on the other side. *Alice Prendergast.* He did not know the name. He did not know the girl. He sat quite still, with the telegram in his hand, and the firelight moved on the ceiling, and the clock ticked, and the whole enormous machinery of the club went on around him—the rustle of papers, the murmur of voices, the far-off closing of a door—and he was aware of none of it. He was aware only of a strange, hollow, ringing sensation, as though a bell had been struck somewhere deep inside him, and the note would not die away.

*Married this morning.* This morning, while he had been reading about the strike, and the price of something or other, and the girl in the white dress beside the aeroplane, his son had been standing in a church, or a registry office, saying words, taking a hand, becoming a husband. This morning. And he had known nothing of it. He had not been told. There had been no asking, no permission sought, no letter of explanation, no invitation—nothing but this little scrap of paper, this flimsy yellow thing that weighed nothing in the hand and yet had the weight of a world in it.

He folded the telegram very carefully, and put it in his waistcoat pocket, and patted the pocket, and looked about him. General Fotheringay was asleep in his chair, his paper fallen to the floor. The fire burned. The clock ticked. Nothing had changed. And yet everything had changed; for he was a man who had a daughter now, a daughter he had never seen, a daughter called Alice, and somewhere in London—or perhaps not in London, perhaps in a church in the country, with bells, with flowers, with a white dress and a breakfast and a great deal of laughter—somewhere there was a young woman who had taken his son’s name and his son’s life into her hands, and he did not know her face, did not know her voice, did not know whether she was kind, or clever, or foolish, or good.

He got up, and went to the window, and stood looking out. The fog had thickened again, and the street-lamps were coming on, one by one, small yellow blurs in the grey; and a hansom went by, the horse’s hooves muffled, the driver a dark shape; and beyond, in the Park, the trees were black and bare against the fading light. He stood there a long time. He thought of writing a letter, and could not think what to say. He thought of sending a wire, and the words would not come. *Congratulations.* *God bless you.* *Who is Alice Prendergast?* The last was the only one that was true, and it could not be sent.

Presently he turned away, and went back to his chair by the fire, and sat down again, and took out the telegram, and read it once more. *Very happy.* Well. That was something. That was, perhaps, everything. The boy was happy. The boy had chosen, and had acted, and had not asked his father’s leave—and why should he? Had he himself asked leave, in 1888, when he had married? No; he had written a letter, and his father had been angry, and the anger had lasted a year, and then there had been a christening, and the anger had been forgotten. It was the way of the world. The young went their own way, and the old were left to their papers and their fires and their memories; and the clock ticked, and the fog thickened, and the lamps came on one by one.

He put the telegram away. He rang the bell, and Perkins came, and he ordered a whisky and soda, and Perkins said, “Cold again today, sir,” and he said, “Cold again, Perkins.” And then, because he could not help it, because the words had to go somewhere, he said: “My son is married, Perkins.”

“Indeed, sir,” said Perkins, who had a daughter at a school in Streatham. “I wish him every happiness, sir.”

“Thank you, Perkins,” said the Colonel. “Thank you.”

And he sat with his whisky and soda, and the fire burned, and the clock ticked, and the night came down over St James’s, and he thought of the boy at seven, in the sailor suit, at Weymouth, holding the bucket, and of the girl in the white dress beside the aeroplane, whom he did not know, and who was now his daughter; and the two images floated together in the dark of his mind, and would not be separated, and were, in some way he could not have explained, the same.
