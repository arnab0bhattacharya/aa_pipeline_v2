The fever came upon me in the night, a thief in the blood. One moment I was shivering in my narrow bed in the cold attic, the next I was lost in a burning wilderness, my own voice crying out to me from a great distance. I knew only the rough hands that pressed me down, the bitter draught forced between my lips, and the ceaseless, maddening drip of water from a cloth upon my brow.

When at last the fire in my veins subsided to a dull ember, I found myself in my own bed still, but the room was changed. A fire, small but real, crackled in the grate. The air, which was wont to hold the chill of a stone tomb, was warm. And in the chair by the hearth, her back straight as a ramrod against the faded chintz, sat Mrs. Corbin.

She was the housekeeper of Fellwood Hall, a woman of severe countenance and severer principles, who had regarded my presence here these three months past as an affront to the natural order. She had measured my portions of tea and sugar with a niggard’s eye, and her lips had thinned to a line of granite whenever I failed to perfectly instruct my young charges in their French verb-conjugations. I had never known her to offer a word of kindness. Now, she was my nurse.

“You are awake,” she stated, not a question but a pronouncement. Her voice was as I remembered it, dry and hard, like two stones rubbed together.

“Yes,” I managed, my own voice a stranger’s rasp. “I am… better.”

“You were not better yesterday. Or the day before. You were at death’s door, and it is not a door I have ever seen a governess knock upon with such enthusiasm.” She rose and came to the bedside, her shadow falling over me. She laid a cool, rough hand on my forehead. “The fever has broken. You will live to see the children neglect their Latin, I do not doubt.”

There was no pity in her words, only a statement of fact. Yet the simple act of that hand upon my brow sent a strange, unwelcome pang through my heart. It had been a long time since a human touch had been anything but a correction.

In the days that followed, she was my gaoler and my saviour. She brought me broth, thin but hot, and sat with me while I drank it, her knitting needles clicking a relentless rhythm in the quiet. She did not speak of her own life, nor did she ask of mine. Our conversations were practical, clipped.

“The fire needs more coal,” I would say.

“It does not,” she would reply, and continue her knitting.

“The children—”

“The children are with the master’s sister. They are not your concern until you can stand without trembling like a birch in a gale.”

Her hardness was a wall I could not breach. But I began to see cracks in it. One evening, a wild wind drove the rain against the casement with the fury of a thrown handful of gravel. I watched her face as she stared into the fire, and for the first time, the granite seemed to soften. A flicker of something—memory, perhaps, or pain—crossed her features.

“You do not like the wind, Mrs. Corbin?” I ventured.

“It has a voice,” she said, her eyes still on the flames. “It speaks of things best left unsaid.”

“What things?”

She turned her gaze upon me then, and it was so fierce I flinched. “Of promises made in shelter and broken in the storm. Of youth that believes itself invincible. Of a girl who came to a house like this one, thinking she was to be a companion, an educator, a friend.” She spat the last word as if it were a curse. “She was a fool.”

I held my breath. The room was suddenly very still, save for the crackle of the fire and the shriek of the wind. “A governess?” I whispered.

Her laugh was a short, ugly sound. “What else? We are all of a kind, are we not? The educated poor, hired to polish the minds of the rich, until we are worn down to a dull, useless stub.”

She rose and took a small, locked box from the mantelpiece. With a key from a chain at her waist, she opened it. From it, she drew a folded piece of paper, brittle with age. She did not give it to me, but held it in her hand as she spoke.

“She was clever, this girl. She could read Homer in the original, and she played the pianoforte with a feeling that made the master’s wife weep. She thought this gave her value. She thought her mind was her own.” Her voice grew hard again, all weakness scoured away. “The master’s son was home from Oxford. He found her mind… stimulating. He quoted poetry to her in the library. He told her she was not like the other women, that she had a soul. And the fool, the great, dreaming fool, believed him.”

I saw it all then, in the firelight. The clandestine meetings, the whispered words, the slow, sweet poison of a hope that was never meant to be.

“What happened?” I asked, my own heart aching with a terrible premonition.

“The mistress happened. The family happened. They saw the attachment, and they saw only one thing: a threat. A governess is a servant, Miss Vance. She may teach the children their declensions, but she may not aspire to sit at their table. She may polish their minds, but she must not touch their hearts. When the son was sent away, and the girl was left weeping, the mistress came to her. She did not shout. She was worse. She was calm. She told her she was a disgrace to her profession, a viper in the bosom of a Christian home. She told her that her education was a curse, that it had filled her with vanity and worldly ambition. And then, she told her that she would be dismissed without a character, that no respectable family would ever hire her again, and that her name would be ruined.”

Mrs. Corbin’s hand, holding the paper, trembled. “She broke her. Not with a whip, but with words. She took her cleverness, her feeling, her very hope, and she called it sin. The girl left that house with nothing. She had to take a place as a scullery maid, because it was all that was left to her. She scrubbed floors and blacked grates until her hands were raw and her mind was numb. She learned that a sharp tongue and a hard heart were better armour than a knowledge of the classics. She learned to be… me.”

She finally held the paper out to me. I took it with a shaking hand. It was not a letter. It was a sonnet, written in a fine, elegant script. The ink was faded, but the passion in the words was still clear. It was a poem of adoration, of a mind finding its equal.

“It is his,” I said, my voice thick.

“It is all I have left of the girl I was,” she said, and her voice was once more flat and hard. “Keep it. Burn it. It is of no matter. The fever is gone. You must get well. There is work to be done.”

She turned away, her back once more a ramrod of iron. But I saw her lift a hand to her face, a swift, secret gesture to dash away a tear. And I understood. I was not merely her duty. I was her ghost. In my illness, in my position, in the quiet desperation I had tried so hard to hide, she had seen the girl she had been. She had nursed me not out of kindness, but out of a fierce, painful necessity to save, this time, what had once been lost. The fire crackled, the wind howled, and in the quiet of that attic room, a terrible inheritance had passed from one governess to another. I was no longer alone. I was haunted.
