The morning was so fine, and the proposed excursion to Thornberry Dell so universally desired, that Miss Helena Vane could not but look forward to the day’s engagements with an elevation of spirits she was seldom willing to acknowledge. She was the eldest of three sisters, and though her sense and temper were more uniformly praised than her beauty, she was not entirely without a proper consciousness of her own worth. A gentleman, however, was coming—Mr. Elliot, of Enscombe House, whose recent tenancy of the adjoining estate had given a new and very lively interest to the neighbourhood. He was sensible, gentlemanlike, and possessed of that easy address which distinguishes the well-bred man from the merely well-informed; and Helena, though she had known him but a fortnight, had already discovered that her satisfaction in his society was greater than she could account for on the mere grounds of neighbourhood and civility.

The party was to consist of her father, Mr. Vane; her two younger sisters; and Mr. Elliot. The arrangements had been settled with that harmonious concurrence which sometimes blesses a family when no one’s particular vanity is concerned, and Helena was anticipating the pleasure of a quiet walk through the woods, with all the advantage of conversation, when an event occurred to throw the whole design into confusion. A carriage was heard on the gravel; and on Mrs. Vane’s going to the window, she exclaimed, with a look of mixed surprise and resignation, that it was Great-aunt Meridew.

Great-aunt Meridew was a widow of considerable fortune and inconsiderable temper, who divided her time between visiting her various relations and finding fault with the manner in which they received her. She was eccentric in the particular way which is least tolerable to those who value regularity—that is to say, her eccentricities were not amusing, but merely inconvenient, and she had a way of insisting on her own pleasure which left no room for the preference of anyone else. She was known to carry about with her a grey parrot, the companion of her solitude and the torment of her acquaintance, which she had acquired from a sea captain, her nephew by a former marriage; and from this bird she was seldom separated.

“Dear me!” said Mrs. Vane, turning from the window. “I had not the least notion she was in the county. We must make the best of it; but I am afraid the picnic must be given up.”

Helena, who had been employed in tying her bonnet, paused with a look of concern. “Given up? Surely not, Mama. We may perhaps contrive to accommodate her in the carriage.”

“My love,” said Mrs. Vane, “you do not know your aunt. If she discovers there is a party of any kind, she will not rest till she has made herself one of it; and then we shall have the parrot, and she will insist upon directing everything, and Mr. Elliot will be disgusted.”

This last consideration had more weight with Helena than she would have owned. She had been building, with that quiet hope which is the most dangerous kind of expectation, on the opportunity the excursion would afford of knowing Mr. Elliot’s mind better. To have the whole scheme deranged by an eccentric old woman and a profane bird was a vexation she could not contemplate with calmness.

But it was already too late to deliberate. Great-aunt Meridew was in the house; and in a few moments she had made known her intention of accompanying them. She had heard of the picnic from the gardener, she said, and was determined not to be left behind, for she was very partial to a wood, and had not seen a proper Dell these twenty years. The parrot, whose name was Captain, was on her shoulder, and regarded the company with that air of impudent intelligence which is peculiar to his species.

Mr. Vane, who was the most good-natured man living, and who had long since given up all resistance to his aunt’s inclinations, only said he was very happy, and that the more the merrier. Mrs. Vane smiled with an effort; and Helena, who saw that her younger sisters, Clara and Lucy, were in transports of mischievous delight, endeavoured to compose her features and her feelings.

Mr. Elliot arrived while these matters were in discussion. He listened to the arrangement with perfect composure, and said he was very glad to find the party enlarged. Whether he observed any change in Helena’s countenance, or whether he was merely governed by his own good-breeding, he said nothing which could betray his thoughts; and Helena, who had feared a look of disappointment, was relieved, though she could not be satisfied.

The party set forth. Great-aunt Meridew was established in the carriage with Mrs. Vane and Clara, while the rest preferred walking. The day was everything that could be wished, and the woods of Thornberry Dell were looking with their best effect; but Helena found her enjoyment considerably diminished by the consciousness of what was behind them. She walked with Mr. Elliot, however, and exerted herself to be agreeable; and he, on his side, was so far from silent or absent, that she began to think the presence of the aunt might be endured, provided the bird could be kept in subjection.

In this, however, she was undeceived. When they reached the Dell, and were assembled on the chosen spot, Captain, who had been removed from his mistress’s shoulder and placed on the branch of an adjacent oak, no sooner observed the company seated than he began to exert those powers of conversation which he had learned in the society of sea captains. What he said was not, indeed, of a kind to be repeated in polite society; but it was delivered with such an air of authority, and with so perfect an imitation of the tone and manner of a commanding officer, that Mr. Elliot could not forbear laughing. Great-aunt Meridew was rather pleased than otherwise, and declared that Captain was a creature of wonderful parts, and that what he said was no worse than what might be heard on board any man-of-war.

Mr. Elliot, whose good-humour was equal to his good breeding, bore it all with that ready complaisance which distinguishes the well-regulated mind; but Helena’s mortification was excessive. Every word the parrot uttered seemed to her a fresh insult to her own dignity, and she could see, or fancied she could see, in Mr. Elliot’s eye, a lurking amusement which cut her to the quick. She endeavoured to turn the conversation, to engage his attention to the scenery, to appeal to his taste and his sense; but Captain was not to be silenced, and his remarks, however unsuitable, had the advantage of being brief, emphatic, and perfectly audible.

“Belay that!” cried the parrot, at a moment when Helena was endeavouring to point out a particularly fine view, and Mr. Elliot, with a suddenness which he could not command, laughed again.

Helena was silent. She felt that she had been injured by a bird, and that there was no redress.

Mr. Elliot, however, who had been watching her with a degree of attention which she had not suspected, presently addressed her in a tone of gentleness and concern. “Miss Vane,” said he, “I fear this excursion has been less agreeable to you than I could have wished. I must confess I have been wanting in respect, and I beg your pardon. The truth is, I have been so much struck with the excellence of the bird’s education, that I forgot the feelings of his audience.”

Helena, who had not expected such a speech, coloured deeply, and could only say that she was not at all offended.

“I am glad of it,” said he, “for I should be very sorry to lose your good opinion on account of a parrot. I have been reflecting, that if I can be so much diverted by the conversation of a sea captain’s bird, I might find equal entertainment, and perhaps more instruction, in the conversation of the sea captain’s relation.”

This was said with a look which Helena could not misunderstand; and she found, to her great surprise, that her vexation was vanishing, and that a very different feeling was taking its place. She made no reply, but her silence was of that kind which speaks more eloquently than words; and Mr. Elliot, who was quick in perception, was not slow to profit by it.

Great-aunt Meridew, who had observed nothing of what passed, and who was wholly occupied in feeding Captain with biscuit and assuring him of her affection, declared, as they returned, that she had not spent so pleasant a day for years, and that the Dell was a charming spot, and that Mr. Elliot was a very sensible young man. In this last opinion Helena entirely concurred, though she did not say so; and if the parrot’s discourse had been the means of bringing about a better understanding between them, she could almost forgive him his profanity.
