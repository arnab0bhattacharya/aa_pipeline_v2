The garden of the King of Omara was a place where Art had conquered Nature and then, with a courtier’s tact, allowed her to believe that she had never been defeated. Here the pomegranate trees let fall their blossoms like drops of blood from a wounded sunset; there the fountains, weary of their own silver eloquence, plashed in a manner that was almost a whisper. It was a garden designed for the eye, and the eye, having been so long and so exquisitely pleased, had grown a little weary of its own satisfaction.

In the midst of this perfection, upon a terrace of malachite that overlooked the lily-ponds, stood the peacock. His name was Ambrosius, though no one had ever thought to give it to him, for he was the sole and undisputed sovereign of the garden’s lesser kingdom. He was a creature of such overwhelming and deliberate beauty that the very flowers seemed to bow their heads as he passed, not in reverence, but in a kind of floral despair. His train, when he deigned to display it, was a tapestry of a thousand eyes, each one a jewel that had been taught to see. He would spread it wide against the setting sun, and the air would fill with a shimmering as of a thousand small and gilded mirrors, and he would turn his head slowly, this way and that, to admire the effect, for he was a connoisseur of his own magnificence and found it always beyond reproach.

“To be beautiful,” he would murmur to his reflection in the still water of the pond, “is to have said everything that need be said. The lilies are white, and therefore dull. The roses are red, and therefore common. I alone have solved the problem of existence, which is to be a spectacle that is perpetually worth beholding.”

And the lilies, who were of a forgiving nature, said nothing. The roses, who were of a more passionate disposition, said nothing either, but they said it with a certain thorny emphasis.

It was at this juncture, when the garden had attained a perfection of silence that was almost a vice, that there arrived a stranger. He was a plain bird, of a dun and dusty grey, with a beak that was neither curved nor crimson, and a tail that was, to speak frankly, a scandal. He alighted upon the low branch of a sycamore tree that grew beneath the King’s own window, and there, without a by-your-leave or a single glance at the malachite terrace, he began to sing.

The song was not a thing of ornament. It was not a trill, nor a flourish, nor a cadenza. It was a melody so simple and so pure that it seemed to have been born of the silence and to be returning to it, having in the meantime made the world a little more comprehensible. It rose and fell like the breathing of a sleeping child. It spoke of the morning and of the dew, of the long afternoon and the shadow of the mountain, of the evening star and the quiet that comes after. It was, in short, the sound of a heart that had nothing to hide.

The King, who had been weary of governing, and wearier still of being governed by the opinions of his court, heard the song and was silent. He rose from his divan of cloth-of-gold, and he went to the window, and he looked down at the plain grey bird, and a tear, which he did not trouble to conceal, stood in his eye.

“That,” said the King, “is the voice of truth. I have heard it before, but I had forgotten. Let the bird be fed with the finest grain, and let his nest be built of the softest down, and let him sing to me every evening, for he is the only one in this garden who does not lie.”

The news travelled through the garden with the speed of a scandal. The lilies, who had always been a little envious of the peacock’s theatricality, began to whisper among themselves. The roses, who had never forgiven him for calling them common, nodded their heads with a certain grim satisfaction. And Ambrosius, who had been preening upon the malachite terrace, felt the first cold breath of a new and terrible emotion.

He went to the sycamore tree. He did not spread his train. He stood before the plain grey bird and looked at him for a long time in silence. The bird, who was eating a modest supper of millet, looked back with an eye that held no malice and no fear.

“You have,” said Ambrosius, at last, in a voice that was meant to be icy but which trembled a little at the edges, “a very singular method of attracting attention.”

“I had not thought of it as a method,” said the bird. “I was merely singing.”

“Merely,” repeated the peacock, and the word fell from his beak like a drop of poison. “There is no such thing as ‘merely.’ You have stolen the King’s ear with a trick of sentiment. You have made a virtue of your poverty. You have turned your lack of beauty into a kind of reproach. It is the oldest trick in the world, and I am surprised that a bird of your obvious intelligence should stoop to it.”

The grey bird considered this for a moment. “I do not understand,” he said, “how one can steal what is given freely. The King heard a song and was pleased. I did not ask for his pleasure. I did not even know he was listening. I sang because the evening was beautiful, and because I was alive, and because the two things together made a kind of joy that would not be contained.”

“And what,” demanded the peacock, his voice rising to a pitch that was almost a shriek, “is the use of a joy that is not contained? What is the use of a beauty that is not seen? You are a plain, drab, insignificant creature, and you have made the King prefer you to me. It is not a question of art. It is a question of politics. You have flattered him. You have told him that simplicity is better than splendour, and he, being a king and therefore tired of splendour, has believed you. You are a demagogue in feathers.”

The grey bird ate another grain of millet. “I have never,” he said, “thought of myself as a demagogue. I have thought of myself as a singer. And as for beauty, I have never claimed to possess it. But I have noticed that when I sing, the flowers seem to grow a little brighter, and the air a little softer, and even the moon, when she rises, seems to pause for a moment as if she were listening. I do not know if that is beauty. It may be only a kind of agreement.”

“An agreement!” cried the peacock. “An agreement with what? With the vulgar? With the common? With the great unwashed multitude of things that have no eyes and no ears and no appreciation of a well-turned phrase or a well-spread tail? I tell you, I would rather be a jewel in a casket that is never opened than a candle in a window that is seen by every passer-by.”

“And yet,” said the grey bird, gently, “the candle gives light, and the jewel, in its casket, gives nothing but the hope of a thief. I do not say that you are wrong. I only say that I have found a different way of being. You are the most beautiful creature in this garden. I am the plainest. But the King, who is neither beautiful nor plain, but only tired, has chosen the song. I do not think he has chosen it because it is better. I think he has chosen it because it is different. And difference, to a tired man, is the only kind of beauty that still has power to move.”

The peacock was silent for a long time. The sun had set, and the garden was filling with the blue and silver shadows of evening. The fountains had ceased their whispering and had begun to dream. And then Ambrosius did a thing that he had never done before. He did not spread his train. He did not turn his head to admire the effect. He simply stood, a little humbled, a little puzzled, and looked at the plain grey bird who had, in a single evening, made him question the one thing he had never thought to question: the sufficiency of his own beauty.

“I do not like you,” he said at last, “but I think I shall have to listen to you. And that, for a peacock, is the beginning of wisdom.”

And the grey bird, who was not a philosopher, but only a singer, said nothing, and sang.
