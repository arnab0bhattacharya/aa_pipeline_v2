The scent of the shop was the scent of their father: beeswax and lavender, the dry, papery smell of bolts of linen and the faint, sharp tang of tallow from the candles kept behind the counter. For ten years, this scent had been the backdrop to Arthur and Elspeth’s lives. They had inherited the draper’s shop in the quiet Lincolnshire town of Alford, along with the town’s expectations and their father’s meticulous ledgers. They had run it with a quiet, synchronized grace, a partnership forged in grief and necessity. Now, that partnership was fraying at the edges, unraveling like a cheap seam.

It began, as it always did, with the name. “I spoke with Mrs. Gable today,” Arthur said, his voice carefully neutral as he sorted a new delivery of muslin. “She says the weather is to turn by the weekend.”

Elspeth, who was mending a tear in a bolt of damask with the kind of fierce concentration usually reserved for surgery, did not look up. “And did you tell her we have a fine new shipment of wool shawls to keep the ladies of Alford warm?”

“I did,” he said, a pause hanging in the air like dust in a sunbeam. “I also mentioned that I would soon have news of my own to share.”

The needle in Elspeth’s hand stilled. She looked up, her eyes, the same grey as the North Sea in winter, meeting his. “What news, Arthur?”

He straightened, a man of thirty, already carrying the stoop of his trade. “I intend to ask for Miss Clara Finch’s hand.”

The name fell into the quiet shop with the graceless clatter of a dropped spool. Elspeth’s face, usually soft with a weary kindness, became a mask of stone. “No.”

“Elspeth, you cannot simply say ‘no’.”

“I can and I do. Clara Finch is… she is not suitable.” Elspeth’s voice was low, but it carried the force of a winter gale. “She is a magpie. All chatter and shiny baubles. She collects compliments as another woman collects stamps. She has never had a thought in her head that did not concern the cut of a bonnet or the colour of a ribbon.”

“You judge her unfairly,” Arthur countered, his own voice rising. “She is kind. She is cheerful. She brings light into a room.”

“She brings light into a room because she is the only thing shining in it,” Elspeth retorted, jabbing her needle back into the damask. “She is shallow, Arthur. She will want things. New dresses, trips to Lincoln, a life beyond this shop, beyond this town. A life beyond *us*.”

“There is no ‘us’ in the way you mean it,” he said, the words a quiet blow. “There is you, and there is me. And I wish for there to be a ‘we’ with Clara.”

“And where will this ‘we’ live?” Elspeth gestured around the shop, at the shelves stacked with calico and flannel, the drawers of buttons and ribbons. “This business can support one household. Our household. It cannot support two. You know that as well as I do. Father’s books are not full of fortunes.”

“Then we will make it support two,” he insisted. “I will work harder. We will expand. I was thinking of taking on a line of ready-made garments.”

“With what capital? With what time? With whose hands?” Elspeth stood, her tall, slender frame seeming to fill the small space between the counter and the door. “My hands, Arthur. That is the answer. The same hands that have stitched every seam, counted every yard, and balanced every account since Father died. I have given ten years of my life to this shop so that you would not have to bear it alone. So that *we* would not have to bear it alone. And you would throw that away for a woman who thinks ‘plaid’ is a type of pastry?”

“That is not fair!” he roared, the sound shocking in the peaceful shop. “You think you have sacrificed? What about me? I have stayed here, in this small town, selling needles and thread to the same faces I have known since I was a boy. I have foregone my own ambitions, my own chance at a life, because I was your brother, your partner. I thought I was doing it for your happiness.”

“My happiness?” Elspeth laughed, a bitter, brittle sound. “My happiness is a full ledger and a quiet conscience. It is knowing this shop is secure, that our father’s legacy is safe. That is my happiness. It is not a thing of… of bonnet strings and giggles.”

“And my happiness?” he pleaded, his anger deflating into a raw desperation. “Does that not matter to you? Clara makes me happy, Elspeth. For the first time in a decade, I see a future that is not just more of the same. A home of my own. A wife. Children. Is that so much to ask?”

“It is too much to ask of this shop,” she said, her voice hard again. She saw it all so clearly: the division of their meagre profits, the arguments over stock, the slow, inevitable decay of their father’s life’s work. And beneath it all, a deeper fear: that Arthur would be swallowed by this flighty girl, his quiet diligence lost to her frivolous demands. She would break him. Elspeth was certain of it. “You are a good man, Arthur. A steady man. She will not appreciate that. She will want you to be something you are not.”

“And you want me to be something I am not!” he shot back. “You want me to be our father. A man who never laughed, who never loved anything more than a well-woven twill. I am not him, Elspeth. I cannot live his life. I want my own.”

The bell above the door jingled, and both of them fell silent, their faces smoothing into the polite masks they wore for the world. Mrs. Gable bustled in, her face pink with the cold, and the siblings dissolved into their roles, Arthur helping her with a shawl, Elspeth discussing the merits of different wools. But the air between them was thick with unspoken words, with the ghosts of a shared past and the terrifying spectre of separate futures.

That night, after the shop was closed and the day’s takings were counted in the flickering lamplight of their small sitting room, the quarrel resumed. It was worse now, stripped of the pretence of public civility.

“You would see me trapped here, an old maid, for the sake of your own comfort,” Elspeth whispered, her hands twisting a handkerchief.

“And you would see me a bachelor forever, chained to this counter, for the sake of your own sense of duty,” he replied, his voice weary. “Each of us believes the other’s happiness depends on our own sacrifice.”

The truth of the words hung between them, stark and undeniable. Elspeth looked at her brother, really looked at him, and saw the lines of fatigue around his eyes, the slump of his shoulders that was not just from his trade. She saw the longing in him, a deep, quiet ache for a life she could not give him. And in that moment, her certainty wavered. Was her protection a cage? Was her sacrifice a selfishness?

Arthur, in turn, looked at his sister. He saw the pinched tightness around her mouth, the way she held herself, always braced against the next small disaster. He saw the ten years of quiet, thankless labour etched onto her face. He saw that her fierce opposition was not born of malice, but of a desperate, terrified love for him, a love that had curdled into control. She was not just protecting the shop; she was protecting him, as she had always done. And in doing so, she was suffocating him.

“I do not wish you to be unhappy, Elspeth,” he said, his voice softer now.

“And I do not wish you to be miserable, Arthur,” she replied, her own voice cracking. “But I fear what this will do to us. To this place.”

They sat in silence for a long time, the only sound the ticking of the clock on the mantelpiece. It was a clock their father had bought, a sturdy, reliable thing. Outside, the wind rattled the windowpanes. The shop was dark and silent behind the wall, waiting for them in the morning. It was their shared anchor, and now it was the millstone around their necks. He saw a future with Clara, bright and uncertain. She saw a future with him, secure and slowly dying. Each believed they were offering the other a kindness, and each knew, with a dreadful certainty, that the other saw only a sacrifice. The chasm between them was no longer about Clara Finch. It was about two lives that had been woven together for so long, they no longer knew how to be unravelled without tearing each other apart.
