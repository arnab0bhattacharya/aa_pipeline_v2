The letter lay on the table between them—her father’s letter, really, though she had written it, as she wrote them all, her father’s hand having grown tremulous and his patience shorter than his charity. It was a letter about a bell. The bell in the north tower of St. Cuthbert’s, which had cracked in the spring and had been silent since, and which the parish, in its slow, grumbling way, had decided it could not afford to mend. She had written, therefore, to the Archdeacon, to the Rural Dean, to a firm in Bristol that cast bells, and now to this young man, this Mr. Anstruther, who had come down from London with his pale clerical collar and his long, thin hands, to read to her father in the afternoons because the bishop’s eyes, like his patience, had begun to fail.

He had come upon her in the garden, where the lavender grew leggy and the cathedral, enormous and grey, seemed to lean its weight against the sky. She had the letter in her hand. He had asked—with that air of his, half deference, half challenge, as though he were testing the ice of her—what she was about.

“It is about the bell,” she said. “They say it cannot be mended. They say the metal is flawed. So it must hang there, dumb, and the parish must make shift with the harmonium, which Mrs. Pettigrew plays as though she were beating a carpet.”

“And you think,” said Mr. Anstruther, “that the bell should be mended.”

“I think,” she said, and paused, for she was not used to saying what she thought, only to writing what her father thought in a hand that was not quite her own, “I think it is a sin to let a thing be silent when it was made to speak.”

He looked at her a moment. The rooks were clamouring in the elms beyond the wall; the light came slanting through the branches and lay in bright coins upon the grass. She felt, absurdly, that she had said too much, and that he was weighing it, and that she did not care—no, that she did care, that was the strange thing, she cared very much what this young man with the thin hands should make of her.

“But a bell,” he said at last, “is only a bell. It is not a soul. If it is flawed, it is flawed. You cannot argue the metal into wholeness by wishing it.”

“No,” she said. “But you can argue the parish into wholeness by mending it. That is the point, Mr. Anstruther. They have forgotten that the bell is not for them. It is for God. They think of it as an expense. They think of everything as an expense—the candles, the vestments, the poor. And so the church grows colder and smaller, and the bell hangs there, and no one hears it, and they say it is the times, they say it is the war in Europe, they say it is the cost of coal.”

She stopped. Her heart was beating in a way that was ridiculous, and she felt the colour in her cheeks, and she thought: I have said too much; I have been unladylike; I have been—what was the word her father used?—vehement. But Mr. Anstruther did not look shocked. He looked, rather, as though he had found something in the garden he had not expected—a stone, perhaps, that was not a stone but the edge of something older and larger.

“You are a bishop’s daughter,” he said, “and yet you speak like a revolutionary.”

“I speak like a woman who has answered her father’s letters for six years,” she said, “and has seen what people say when they think they are being polite. They say, ‘The bell is a great pity,’ and then they go home and put another lump of coal on the fire. They do not mean to be wicked. They are merely comfortable. And comfort,” she said, and the word came out of her with a heat that surprised her, “comfort is the great silencer. It silences the bell. It silences the poor. It silences everything but the ticking of the clock and the rattle of the tea-cups.”

The sun had gone behind a cloud; the garden darkened; the rooks were quiet. She saw him standing there, and she saw herself, as though from a great distance, a tall woman in a grey dress with a letter in her hand, and she thought: this is what it is to speak. This is what it is to have a thought and to send it out into the air, and to have it met, not with a nod, not with “yes, dear,” not with the gentle, terrible patience of her father, but with contradiction, with argument, with a mind that pushed back.

“You are right,” he said, “and you are wrong.”

“That is a very clerical thing to say.”

“It is a very human thing to say. You are right that they are comfortable. You are wrong that comfort is the enemy. Comfort is what they have instead of the bell. They have lost the bell, and so they hold on to the coal. You cannot mend the bell by scolding them. You must give them back the sound, and then they will remember what it was for.”

“And how,” she said, “does one give back a sound to people who have forgotten how to listen?”

He smiled then—a quick, unexpected smile that changed his whole face, that made him look younger and less like a curate and more like a boy who had once climbed trees. “One begins,” he said, “by writing letters. You write them very well, I think. You have a way of putting things that is not quite like anyone else. You put the bell in the letter as though it were a living thing. That is a beginning.”

She looked down at the letter in her hand. The ink was her father’s ink, the paper her father’s paper, and yet the words—the words about the bell, about the sin of silence, about the parish that had forgotten—those were hers. She had written them without knowing she was writing them. She had thought she was only being her father’s hand.

“I have never,” she said slowly, “argued with anyone before. Not really. Not about anything that mattered.”

“And now?”

“And now,” she said, “I find that I am rather angry. And that I like it.”

The cloud passed; the sun came out again; the lavender sent up its scent, sharp and clean, and the cathedral, enormous and grey, seemed to settle its weight more easily against the sky. Somewhere in the close a door opened and shut. A dog barked. The ordinary sounds of the afternoon came back, and with them the knowledge that she must go in soon, must see to her father’s tea, must answer more letters, must be again the daughter of the bishop, quiet and useful and unheard.

But she stood a moment longer in the garden, and she looked at Mr. Anstruther, and she thought: he has contradicted me, and I am not diminished. I am—larger. I am a bell that has been struck, and though the metal may be flawed, the sound is still there, waiting.

“I shall write to the firm in Bristol,” she said. “I shall tell them that the bell is not an expense. I shall tell them that it is a voice, and that voices must not be allowed to die.”

“And I,” said Mr. Anstruther, “shall read to your father this afternoon, and I shall tell him that his daughter is a better theologian than the Archdeacon.”

She laughed—a sound she had not heard from herself in years, a sound that rang out across the garden and startled the rooks from the elms—and she turned, and went in, and the door of the close shut behind her, and the afternoon went on, ordinary and extraordinary, as afternoons do.
