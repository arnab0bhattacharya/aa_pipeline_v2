The affair of the Larkins’ marriage had been canvassed through the whole of Fennell St Mary for above a fortnight before Miss Harriet Ellwood could be brought to pronounce upon it, and when at last she did pronounce, it was with that species of satisfaction which accompanies the discharge of a duty long delayed. She had been from home, it is true, when the wedding took place—three weeks at her aunt’s in Sevenoaks, where the society was so confined that she had returned to the village with an appetite for company which only the most vigorous exercise of candour could satisfy.

“I own I am not an admirer of the match,” said she, seated in Mrs. Ashby’s drawing-room, where a small party had assembled upon a Tuesday morning, ostensibly for the purpose of examining patterns for a new sofa, but in reality for the discussion of their neighbours. “Miss Larkin is a pretty-behaved young woman, I have nothing to say against her, but a man of Mr. Larkin’s sense to marry a girl who has not a word to say for herself, and who brings him nothing but a pair of fine eyes and a very pretty curtsey—for I am told the settlement was nothing at all—it is what I cannot understand, and what I shall never pretend to understand.”

Mrs. Ashby, who was a widow of moderate fortune and great discretion, observed that Mr. Larkin was old enough to know his own mind, and that a wife who did not talk much was perhaps no bad thing for a man of a certain temper.

“Oh, certainly,” cried Harriet, “if a man wishes for a wife merely to sit opposite to him at table and smile, he might as well marry a picture. But I have always understood that Mr. Larkin was a man of understanding, and a man of understanding, in my opinion, requires a companion who can enter into his views and return him an answer. Miss Larkin—Mrs. Larkin, I should say—may be very amiable, but amiable people are to be met with everywhere, and when a man of property marries, the neighbourhood has a right to expect something more than amiability.”

There were four ladies present, and of the four, three were of Harriet’s opinion, or said they were, and the fourth was silent. Harriet, thus encouraged, proceeded to observe that she had heard it said that the bride’s family were not at all pleased with the match, that there had been some coolness between the two houses, and that Mrs. Larkin’s brother, who was a man of large fortune in another county, had refused to attend the wedding.

“I do not know that there is any truth in it,” said she, “but I have it from a very good quarter, and I am not one of those who pretend to be blind. If people will marry imprudently, they must expect to be talked about.”

It was at this point that Mrs. Ashby’s niece, a quiet young woman who had been sitting at the window with her needlework, looked up and said, in a voice of great composure, that she believed the brother in question was a Mr. Ferrers of Whitbourne, and that he was a man of a very retired disposition, who never went anywhere.

“I daresay,” said Harriet, “but a brother is a brother, and a wedding is a wedding, and if Mr. Ferrers could not make an effort upon such an occasion, I must say I think it was a very great want of proper feeling. However, I do not speak from any personal knowledge of the gentleman. I only know what I have heard.”

The conversation then turned to other matters, and Harriet, having delivered herself of her opinion, was in excellent spirits, and talked a great deal about the necessity of speaking one’s mind and the folly of a false delicacy, and said that she thanked Heaven she had never been afraid to say what she thought, and that she hoped she never should be.

It was upon the following Thursday that she had occasion to call at the Rectory, and upon entering the drawing-room, was surprised to find a gentleman standing at the fire, a stranger, a man of about thirty, of a very agreeable person and countenance, whom Mrs. Langford immediately introduced as Mr. Ferrers.

Harriet coloured, she hardly knew why, and Mr. Ferrers, with great civility, hoped he did not interrupt her; he had been walking over from Whitbourne, he said, and had come in to rest himself and to pay his respects to Mrs. Langford, who had been so good as to promise him some account of his sister.

“Your sister, sir?” said Harriet.

“My sister, Mrs. Larkin,” said he. “I am but just arrived in the country, and I have not yet had the pleasure of seeing her since her marriage.”

Harriet felt the blood rush to her cheeks. She endeavoured to say something about the happiness of the match, but the words came out in confusion, and she was conscious that she had said nothing to the purpose. Mr. Ferrers, however, did not appear to notice her embarrassment. He spoke of his sister with great affection, of her good sense and sweetness of temper, and of the satisfaction it gave him to see her so well settled; and Harriet, as she listened, became sensible that she had been guilty of a very great impertinence, and that the man before her was the very brother whose absence from the wedding she had so lately censured.

“I had not the pleasure of being at the wedding,” said he, in answer to a question from Mrs. Langford. “I was unfortunately detained in the north upon business of a pressing nature, and I could not reach Kent in time. It was a great disappointment to me, for I have a very high opinion of my new brother, and I should have been glad to have shown it.”

Harriet, who had by this time recovered a little of her presence of mind, said that she hoped the neighbourhood was sensible of the acquisition it had received in Mrs. Larkin.

“You are very good,” said Mr. Ferrers, with a bow. “I believe my sister will be happy here. She is not a woman of many words, but she has a very good understanding, and a heart that cannot fail to make her beloved.”

Harriet felt the reproof, though it was not intended for her, and she would have given a great deal to have been able to recall what she had said in Mrs. Ashby’s drawing-room. She looked at Mr. Ferrers, who was talking to Mrs. Langford with an air of perfect ease and good humour, and she thought she had never seen a man whose countenance expressed so much benevolence and good sense. He was not handsome, perhaps, but there was something in his manner which was infinitely more agreeable than beauty, and Harriet, who had always laughed at the idea of love at first sight, began to think that there might be something in it after all.

“I am sorry,” said Mr. Ferrers, turning to her again, “that I have not the pleasure of knowing you better, Miss Ellwood. I have heard your name mentioned by my sister, who I believe has the honour of your acquaintance.”

“Mrs. Larkin is very obliging,” said Harriet, “but I fear I have not deserved her good opinion. I am afraid I have been so unfortunate as to say something—”

She stopped, and Mr. Ferrers looked at her with some surprise.

“I am sure,” said he, “my sister would never speak of you but with kindness. She is not given to censure, and she has a great respect for your family.”

Harriet was silent. She was conscious of a feeling of shame which she had never experienced before, and she began to perceive that frankness, when it is not accompanied by good nature, is very apt to be mistaken for ill-nature. She thought of the many things she had said about Mrs. Larkin, of the many people to whom she had said them, and she felt that she had been guilty of a meanness which she could not excuse to herself.

“I hope,” said Mr. Ferrers, as he rose to take his leave, “that I shall have the pleasure of seeing you again, Miss Ellwood. I am but a poor hand at visiting, but I shall be in the country for some weeks, and I shall hope to improve my acquaintance with my sister’s neighbours.”

He bowed and was gone, and Harriet remained seated by the fire, in a state of mind which it would be difficult to describe. She had been used to pride herself upon her frankness, and she had always thought it a virtue; but she now saw that it was possible to be frank and yet to be unkind, and that the truth may be spoken in a spirit which is not the spirit of truth. She resolved, from that moment, to be more careful of her words, and to remember that the character of a woman is not to be estimated by the brilliancy of her conversation, but by the goodness of her heart.

Whether she succeeded in this resolution, and whether Mr. Ferrers ever became better acquainted with her, it is not for me to say. But I have been told that she never again spoke ill of Mrs. Larkin, and that she was heard to observe, upon more than one occasion, that a silent woman was not always a fool, and that a man of understanding might very well choose a wife who did not pretend to be clever.
