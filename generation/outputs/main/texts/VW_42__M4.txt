The tea was lukewarm and the room too small for four, so that their knees, beneath the little round table, made a kind of silent conversation of their own, nudging and withdrawing, while above the china the talk went on. Ellen Marsh, who was to be married in the autumn, poured, and the steam rose and hung a moment in the slanted light from the window and was gone. “Sugar, Dora? You always took two.” And Dora, who had taken one these five years, said two, because it was Ellen’s party and Ellen’s sugar, and the saying of it carried her back, somehow, to a green bench under a wall at school, where the same three had sat in a row, and the fourth, Beatrice, had come late with her hat awry and her hair coming down, always late, always a little breathless, as though life were a train she was forever catching. Now Beatrice, at the window, was speaking of a picture she had seen—the light in it, she said, the light on the water, like nothing so much as the light on the Cam at six in the morning when one had been up all night. “You never were up all night,” said Dora, and Beatrice laughed, and the laugh was the old laugh, but the hands that held the teacup were a woman’s hands, with a ring on them. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked, and outside a barrel-organ ground out its tune, and the afternoon, which had seemed at three o’clock to stretch before them like a country, narrowed and grew shallow, and Ellen, watching the tea-leaves settle, thought: this is the last of it, the last of us four as we were; and she was not sad, exactly, but the thought lay in her like a stone in a stream, with the water of the talk running over it.

The wedding was in June, and the church was full of lilies, and Beatrice, standing at the back in a dress the colour of claret, watched Ellen come up the aisle on her father’s arm and thought how small she looked, how astonishingly small and pale, and how the veil made a mist of her face so that one could not be sure, from where one stood, whether she was happy or frightened or only very tired. Dora, beside her, was crying quietly, with a handkerchief crushed in her fist, and Beatrice, who could not cry, put her hand on Dora’s arm and felt the fine tremor of it, and thought: she is crying for all of us, for the green bench and the tea and the afternoons that will never come again. The organ swelled; the words were said; the ring went on. And afterwards, in the long marquee on the lawn, with the sun making a dapple of the white cloth, Beatrice found herself beside a man with a red face who talked of the colonies, and she nodded and smiled and looked over his shoulder at Ellen, who was laughing now, actually laughing, with her head thrown back, and the laughter came to Beatrice across the noise of the tent like something remembered out of a dream. She thought: she is gone from us now; she belongs to that man, to that house in Surrey, to the children she will have; and the thought was not envy, but a kind of wonder, as though she had watched a door close softly in a house she had once lived in. Dora came up, her eyes red, and said, “Isn’t it lovely?” and Beatrice said, “Lovely,” and they stood together with their glasses in their hands, and the afternoon wore on toward evening, and the shadows of the elms lengthened across the lawn, and the little band played a waltz, and nobody danced.

The funeral was in November, and it was Ellen’s husband who had died, suddenly, of a thing that had no name until the doctor gave it one afterwards. The three of them stood at the graveside in the raw wind, and the earth was heaped beside the hole, and the parson’s voice went on and on, and Dora, who had come down from Yorkshire, thought: how strange that we should be here, we four, and one of us gone, and the others standing in our good black coats with our hands folded, as though we had rehearsed it. She looked at Ellen, who was very upright and very still, and whose face had a look she had never seen on it before, a look of having been somewhere and come back, and of not being able to say what she had seen. Beatrice, on the other side, was thinking of the wedding, of the lilies and the laughter, and how the two days were the same day, really, the one the other’s shadow; and she wanted to say something to Ellen, something that would be true, but the words that came were the old words, the words from the green bench, and they were no use now. Afterward, in the house, with the fire lit and the blinds drawn, they sat with their cups and spoke of small things—the trains, the weather, the price of coal—and Ellen said, once, “He liked the garden,” and then was silent, and the silence was not awkward but heavy, like a room full of furniture in the dark. And when they left, at the gate, Beatrice took Ellen’s hand and held it a moment, and Ellen said, “You’ll come again,” and Beatrice said, “Yes,” and the gate clicked, and they went down the lane in the dusk, the three of them, and did not speak.

Years after, in the great roaring cavern of a railway station, with the trains sighing and the porters shouting and the light falling in dusty shafts from the high roof, Beatrice, waiting for the 4.15, saw a woman on the far platform and knew her at once, though she had not seen her for a decade, by the way she held her head, a little to one side, listening to something no one else could hear. Dora. She was older, of course, and thinner, and there was grey in her hair, and she carried a bag and a bundle and a little fur tippet that had seen better days. Beatrice crossed the bridge over the tracks, and the trains below smoked and shunted, and when she came down the steps on the other side Dora was looking at her, and for a moment neither spoke, and then they both began, and the words tumbled over each other, and they laughed, and Dora said, “You haven’t changed,” and Beatrice said, “Nor have you,” and both knew it was a lie and both were glad of it. They found a bench, and the 4.15 came in and went out again, and they sat on, and Dora spoke of her husband, who was dead now too, and of her son, who was in India, and Beatrice spoke of her painting, which had come to nothing, she said, or to very little, and Dora said, “You always had the light,” and Beatrice looked at her and thought: she remembers the light. The station clock moved its hand; the light in the shafts grew golden and then grey; the porters called the trains, and the trains went out, and the two women sat on the bench with their parcels beside them, and the years fell away and the years came back, and it seemed to Beatrice that they were all there, the four of them, in the noise and the smoke and the coming and going, Ellen and Dora and herself and the other one, the one who was gone, and that the station was not a station but a place where everything that had ever happened was happening still, all at once, under the great roof, in the fading light. “I must go,” said Dora at last. “My train.” And she gathered up her bag and her bundle, and Beatrice said, “Write to me,” and Dora said, “I will,” and went off down the platform, a small figure in the crowd, and did not look back. And Beatrice sat on the bench until the lights came on, one by one, along the platform, and the station grew quiet, and the last train went out into the dark.
