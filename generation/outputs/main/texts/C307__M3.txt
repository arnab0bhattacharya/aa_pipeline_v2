The wind coming off the Channel at Saltmere carried the taste of salt and rotting kelp, a flavor Mr. Elias Thorne found oddly agreeable as he sat at the window of the Tidecock Inn. It was late October, the season long dead, and the village offered nothing but chalk cliffs, a pebble beach, and an endless susurration of grey waves. This isolation was precisely what he required. The front parlour of the Tidecock was cold, the fire in the grate reduced to a sullen glow, but Thorne paid it little mind. His attention was fixed upon the small phial in his waistcoat pocket, and upon the patch of bare, stony earth in the inn’s backyard. 

He had synthesized the compound—a viscous, amber-tinctured distillation of nitrates, a newly isolated alkaloid derived from charred bone meal, and a trace of an experimental organometallic catalyst—in his cramped attic room over the course of three days. He called it Formula IX. Theoretical chemistry suggested it would aggressively stimulate cellular mitosis in flora. The previous eight formulas had resulted only in blackened leaves and a terrible stench. Formula IX, however, had remained stable.

On Tuesday evening, beneath a drizzling rain, Thorne had stolen into the backyard and poured a diluted measure of the amber liquid over a patch of dead soil. He had scattered a handful of common ryegrass seeds and three dormant dahlia bulbs into the muck. By Wednesday morning, the results were so profound that Thorne, a man of empirical restraint, had been forced to sit down on his bed and breathe into a handkerchief. The grass had already sprouted two inches. The dahlias had broken the soil, their stalks a vivid, almost violent green.

By Thursday, the grass was knee-high. By Friday, it had consumed the yard. 

It was now the following Tuesday, precisely a week since the initial application, and the Tidecock’s backyard had become an impenetrable jungle. Thorne watched from his window as the landlady, Mrs. Agnes Lott, stood at the edge of the patio, her hands clutched to her chest. The dahlia stalks had risen to the height of a man, their leaves broad as dinner plates, though they bore no buds, only an endless proliferation of vegetative matter. The ryegrass had tangled into a thick reed-bed, swaying heavily in the coastal wind. The brick border had been displaced by the sheer force of expanding roots, the mortar cracked and crumbling.

Mrs. Lott was a woman constructed entirely of stiff upper lips and brisk skepticism, but Thorne could see the fear in the set of her shoulders. She did not understand what was happening in her own garden. She had raked that very soil a fortnight ago. Now, the encroaching wilderness brushed against the inn’s back door, leaving smears of green sap on the whitewashed wood. 

She turned and looked up at Thorne’s window. He offered a curt, reassuring nod, but she did not return it. She retreated indoors. A moment later, he heard the heavy tread of her boots on the stairs, followed by the insistent rapping at his door.

"Mr. Thorne," she said, entering without waiting for his invitation. Her face was pale, her eyes wide. "There is a sickness in the ground. It must be. My father’s dahlias never grew so. It is unnatural."

"It is merely the mild autumn, Mrs. Lott," Thorne said, keeping his voice perfectly level. "The coastal microclimate is remarkably forgiving. The damp has simply encouraged a spurt."

"A spurt?" she echoed, her voice trembling. "The baker’s boy refused to deliver the bread this morning. He says there are adders in the grass. He says the weeds have swallowed the coal shed. I can hear the roots at night, Mr. Thorne. I hear them scraping against the foundation."

Thorne offered a thin smile. "Plant respiration can create quite a chorus in the still air. I assure you, it is perfectly natural."

Mrs. Lott did not look assured. She looked at the small chemical apparatus on his washstand—the glass retorts, the Bunsen burner, the stains of sulfuric acid on the wood. "You are a chemist, sir. You deal in unnatural things. Have you put something in my soil?"

"Absolutely not," Thorne lied smoothly. 

She left, but the damage was done. By the afternoon, the village constable arrived. 

Constable Bowden was a heavy, unhurried man with a mustache like a worn hearthbrush and a deep suspicion of anyone who paid for their lodging in advance. He did not knock. He simply opened the gate to the backyard—which was now concealed behind a curtain of trailing ivy that had grown six feet in two days—and stood staring at the botanical chaos. 

Thorne went downstairs to meet him. The parlour was empty; Mrs. Lott had barricaded herself in the kitchen. 

"Mr. Thorne," Constable Bowden said, turning his cap in his hands. "Mrs. Lott says you’ve been pouring something on her garden."

"Mrs. Lott is mistaken," Thorne replied, stepping out onto the patio. The air smelled intensely of crushed chlorophyll, a thick, heady scent that made his eyes water. "I am merely a lodger. I admire the flora."

"Flora," Bowden repeated, gesturing to the towering dahlias. Their stalks were now as thick as saplings, the leaves obscuring the upstairs windows. "This isn't flora, sir. This is a plague. The vicar was by earlier. He says the brambles from your yard have crossed the property line and are strangling the churchyard yew."

Thorne felt a tightness in his chest. The compound was too volatile. The coastal rain had washed it deeper into the water table. He had not anticipated the speed of the subterranean spread. "Nature is merely reclaiming the space," Thorne attempted, though the excuse sounded hollow even to his own ears.

Constable Bowden stepped closer, his heavy boots crushing a carpet of newly sprouted moss. He looked at Thorne with a gaze that was more weary than angry. "I’ve sent a wire to the agricultural board in Maidstone, Mr. Thorne. They’ll be here on the morning train. But I’ve a few questions for you before they arrive."

"Ask away," Thorne said, keeping his hands clasped behind his back to hide their trembling.

"What is in them phials in your room?"

"Reagents for a textile dye. Harmless."

"If it’s harmless, why is the landlady’s dog missing?"

Thorne blinked. "The dog?"

"Aye. The terrier. Went out last night to relieve itself. Didn’t come back. Mrs. Lott found its collar this morning, caught in the thicket by the coal shed. The buckle was snapped clean off. There were stains on the leather. Green stains."

Thorne felt the blood drain from his face. He thought of the organometallic catalyst, the untested nature of the alkaloid. He thought of the aggressive mitosis, the uncontrolled cellular division. If the compound could affect the dog—if the dog had ingested a root or licked a leaf—

"It is likely the dog simply wandered off," Thorne said, his voice slightly higher than intended. "Terriers are prone to chasing rabbits."

"There are no rabbits in Saltmere, Mr. Thorne," Bowden said grimly. "Not anymore. I haven’t seen a bird in the garden since Thursday. The place is too thick. It’s alive, sir. Mrs. Lott says she saw the ivy moving in the night, reaching for the drainpipe."

Thorne looked out at the garden. The wind had dropped, but the leaves were still rustling. There was a wet, tearing sound as a root pushed through the patio stones, splitting the slate. A thick, green tendril, pulsing with unnatural sap, curled out of the crack and began to inch its way toward the constable’s boot.

Thorne took a step back. He was a man of science, a man who believed that nature was merely a mechanism to be understood and manipulated. But looking at the pulsing stalk, feeling the oppressive humidity radiating from the mass of leaves, he felt a primal, superstitious dread clawing at his throat. 

"You had best come with me to the station, Mr. Thorne," Constable Bowden said, taking a step backward as the tendril brushed his ankle. "I think it would be unwise for you to remain on the premises. The agricultural men will want to speak with you, and I daresay Mrs. Lott will want you gone."

Thorne nodded slowly. He knew he could not answer the questions that were coming. He could not explain to the agricultural board that he had sought to master the fundamental engine of life and had instead unleashed a monster. He looked up at his window, where the amber phial sat on the washstand. 

"Very well, Constable," Thorne said. "I shall fetch my coat."

He turned and went back inside, the green smell clinging to his clothes. As he climbed the stairs, he heard a loud crack from the backyard. He did not look back to see what had broken. He only knew that whatever he had created, it was no longer his to control.
