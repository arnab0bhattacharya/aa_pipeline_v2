Lord Havelock Ashworth had always maintained that the soul was merely a servant to the body, and that the body, in its turn, was a vehicle for the soul’s amusement. He was a young man of exquisite taste, possessing a face that looked as if it had been carved by a sculptor who was particularly fond of irony and a wardrobe that suggested he had been dressed by a committee of jealous gods. His life had been a symphony of silks, a gallery of glances, and a library of lies told with such charming fluency that they had mistaken truth. He had never done a good deed, for good deeds are the currency of the poor and the pious, and Havelock considered both groups to be slightly tedious.

It was on a Tuesday, a day of peculiarly dreary grey light, that Dr. Sterling, a man whose face was as expressionless as a blank cheque, broke the spell. They were seated in the drawing-room of Havelock’s club, the air thick with the scent of stale cigars and expensive regret.

“My dear Havelock,” said the doctor, adjusting his spectacles with a precision that suggested he was measuring the distance between himself and the abyss, “your liver is playing a rather aggressive game of chess, and I am afraid it has just checkmated you. You have, I am led to believe, one year. No more. No less.”

Havelock did not weep. He did not rage. He merely adjusted his cufflinks, which were made of moonstone, and said, “That is inconvenient. I had booked a table at Savoy for the winter season. But I suppose one must make the best of a bad hand. Tell me, Doctor, is there anything I should avoid? Or perhaps, is there anything I should do? I find that one tends to neglect the finer points of existence when one assumes one has an infinite supply of it.”

“Do what you please,” said Dr. Sterling, his voice devoid of sympathy, for he was a man who viewed death as a clerical error. “Just don’t let it surprise you.”

Havelock left the club with a lightness of step that belied the weight of his sentence. He walked into the fog that hung over London like a wet shroud, and for the first time in his life, he felt the cold. It was a shocking sensation. He realized then that he had never truly been alive, only occupied. The diagnosis was a gift, terrible and brilliant, a window through which he could finally see the world without the distortion of his own vanity.

He resolved to spend his last year in the service of others. It seemed the only logical conclusion to a life that had been so thoroughly selfish. If he was to die, he would die as a saint. Or at least, as a gentleman who had tried.

He began at the slums of Whitechapel, where he donated his fortune to a home for the destitute. The director, a woman with eyes like polished flint, thanked him with a smile that did not reach her face. “You are a generous man, Lord Ashworth,” she said. “But generosity, like perfume, is quickly evaporated. What you offer is money. We need time. We need love. We need someone who will sit with us and listen to our sorrows.”

Havelock had no time for sorrows. Sorrows were heavy, unglamorous things that stuck to one’s clothes. He tried to volunteer at a hospital, where he spent three hours holding the hand of a dying child. The child looked at him with eyes that were not sad, but terrified, and asked if he was going to take him away. Havelock, unaccustomed to the raw, unadorned truth of human pain, stammered, “No, I am only here to be pleasant.” The child’s hand slipped from his grasp, not in death, but in rejection. Havelock fled the hospital, his heart beating with a strange, unfamiliar rhythm.

He found that doing good was physically exhausting and spiritually repulsive. To be kind was to be vulnerable, and vulnerability was the enemy of style. To forgive was to admit that one was wrong, and to be wrong was to be boring. He tried to reconcile a woman with her husband, only to find that their marriage was held together by a web of such intricate, petty deceptions that to pull one thread would unravel the whole. He tried to feed the hungry, but the hunger of the soul was a beast that no amount of roast beef could sate.

He traveled to the Riviera, seeking the sun and the sea, hoping that the warmth might thaw his frozen conscience. He rented a villa in Menton, a place of lemon trees and blue water, where the air smelled of salt and decay. He hired a nurse, a stern woman named Madame Gault, who watched him with the suspicion of a cat observing a mouse that had suddenly learned to play the piano.

“You are trying to be good,” Madame Gault said one evening, as they sat on the terrace, watching the sunset bleed into the Mediterranean. “It is like wearing a suit that does not fit. It is pinching you.”

“I am preparing for eternity,” Havelock said, though he did not believe in eternity. He believed in the next moment.

“Eternity is not a dress,” she replied. “It is a room. And you are trying to decorate it with furniture that does not belong there. You are trying to be a saint, my lord. But you are not a saint. You are a man. And men do good things because they are afraid, or because they are in love, or because they are bored. Not because they have a deadline.”

Havelock looked at the sea. It was calm, a sheet of hammered silver. He realized that his attempts at goodness were merely another form of vanity. He was performing his death, turning his final year into a spectacle for an audience of one. He was not doing good; he was posing for it.

The next morning, Dr. Sterling arrived. He had come to check on his patient, expecting to find a broken man, a figure of pathos. Instead, he found Havelock sitting in the garden, wearing a white suit that had been tailored to perfection, his hair combed back, his eyes bright with a clear, cold amusement.

“My dear Havelock,” said the doctor, checking his pocket watch, “I have some news. I have reviewed your charts again. There has been a mistake. Your liver is perfectly healthy. You have no illness. You have as many years to live as you choose to live them.”

Havelock did not smile. He did not weep. He simply looked at the doctor, and for the first time, the doctor felt a chill that had nothing to do with the morning air.

“A mistake,” Havelock said softly. “How tedious. I had just begun to understand the difference between acting and being. I had just learned that the mask is the face, and the face is the mask. But now, I must go back to being myself. And I am afraid I have forgotten how.”

He turned to Madame Gault, who was standing by the door.

“Madame,” he said, “cancel the charity dinner. I have nothing to give but my own mediocrity, and I find that I have no taste for it. Pack my bags. I am going back to London. I have a dinner at the Opera, and I believe I have forgotten how to lie with elegance.”

He stood up, the fabric of his suit rustling like dry leaves. He walked toward the house, his shadow long and thin on the stone path. He did not look back at the sea, or the lemon trees, or the doctor who stood frozen in the garden. He knew, with a certainty that was both terrifying and liberating, that he would live a long life, and that it would be a masterpiece of indifference. He had saved his soul, if he had one, by losing it. And in the end, he suspected, that was the only good deed he would ever perform.
