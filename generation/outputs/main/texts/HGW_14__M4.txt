The trouble, as I have come to understand it, was not so much the poison itself as the extraordinary fuss that followed it, and the way in which a perfectly straightforward question of professional competence became entangled with questions of propriety, loyalty, and the position of women in the pharmaceutical trade. But I run ahead of my story.

My name is Miss Eleanor Vane, and I had the misfortune to be born with a good head for chemistry and the additional misfortune to be born a woman. At the Pharmaceutical Society’s examinations I had acquitted myself creditably—so creditably, in fact, that one of the examiners remarked to another, within my hearing, that it was a pity I could not be registered, as I clearly knew more about the materia medica than half the young men who passed through their hands. But registered I could not be. The Society, in its wisdom, had determined that the dispensing of medicines was a matter for the stronger sex, and no amount of Latin nomenclature or careful titration would alter their view. I might qualify till the cows came home; I might memorise the entire British Pharmacopoeia; I might, I daresay, have discovered a cure for the common cold—none of it would have the slightest effect upon the granite conviction of the Council that women were constitutionally unsuited to the weighing of powders and the mixing of tinctures.

And so I found myself, at the age of twenty-six, in the position of what the world politely calls a “lady help” and what I privately called an underpaid drudge, in the establishment of Mr. Algernon Prout, dispensing chemist, of Cresswell Road, Holloway. Mr. Prout was a large, florid man with a walrus moustache and a manner of ponderous geniality that he reserved chiefly for his more prosperous customers. He had purchased his business some fifteen years previously with money inherited from a maiden aunt, and had conducted it ever since with a degree of competence that might charitably be described as adequate. He could make up a simple prescription without too much difficulty, provided it did not involve anything more exotic than Epsom salts or the simpler tinctures; but the moment a physician sent in a mixture requiring careful calculation, or a powder demanding exact proportion, Mr. Prout’s brow would furrow and his manner would become distinctly less genial.

It was into this breach that I stepped. I had answered an advertisement for an “assistant with some knowledge of dispensing,” and after a brief interview in which Mr. Prout satisfied himself that I was quiet, respectable, and unlikely to demand anything so vulgar as a written agreement, I was engaged at a wage that would have been derisory had it not been so frankly insulting. My duties, as he explained them, were to attend to the shop when he was otherwise engaged, to keep the books, to dust the shelves, and to “help out” in the dispensary when things were busy. What this meant in practice was that I did nearly all the dispensing, while Mr. Prout took the credit, the fees, and the occasional commendation from the local practitioners.

For three years I laboured in this fashion, and I will confess that I derived a certain grim satisfaction from the knowledge that my mixtures were invariably correct, my powders accurately divided, and my labels legible and properly affixed. Mr. Prout, for his part, grew increasingly confident in his own abilities, and would occasionally venture upon a prescription of his own composition—usually a cough mixture or a liniment—which he would press upon his customers with the air of a man bestowing an inestimable boon. These concoctions were, I need hardly say, entirely useless, but as they were also entirely harmless, I let them pass without comment.

Then came the affair of Mrs. Harbottle’s medicine.

Mrs. Harbottle was the wife of a prosperous builder in the neighbourhood, and she suffered from a variety of complaints which she described with great particularity to anyone who would listen. On this occasion her physician, a Dr. Merridew, had prescribed a mixture containing tincture of digitalis, and had been at some pains to impress upon Mr. Prout the necessity of accurate measurement. “The smallest excess,” he had said, tapping the prescription with his forefinger, “may produce symptoms of a most alarming character. I rely upon your discretion.” Mr. Prout had assured him that he might do so with perfect confidence, and had bowed him out of the shop with the air of a man who has never made an error in his life.

It happened that on the afternoon in question I was engaged in the back room, compounding a rather complicated lotion for a customer who was waiting in the shop. Mr. Prout, therefore, undertook to make up Mrs. Harbottle’s mixture himself. I heard him rattling about in the dispensary, and thought nothing of it until, some twenty minutes later, I emerged to find him standing by the counter with a bottle in his hand and an expression of profound satisfaction upon his face.

“There,” he said, “that’s done. Mrs. Harbottle’s mixture. I’ll just run across the road and leave it with her, as she’s waiting in.”

I took the bottle from him and glanced at it—a habit I had acquired, and one which Mr. Prout tolerated because it flattered his vanity to have his work inspected and approved. The mixture was a dark brown liquid, as it should have been, and the label was correctly written. But something about the colour troubled me. I held it up to the light, and my heart gave a sudden lurch.

“Mr. Prout,” I said, as steadily as I could manage, “did you use the tincture of digitalis from the green bottle or the blue?”

He stared at me blankly. “The green, of course. Why?”

“Because,” I said, “the green bottle contains tincture of strychnine. The digitalis is in the blue.”

For a moment he did not seem to comprehend. Then his face went a curious mottled colour, and he snatched the bottle from my hand and held it up to the light himself. “Nonsense,” he said, but his voice lacked conviction. “Nonsense. I know perfectly well—”

“The digitalis,” I repeated, “is in the blue bottle. You moved them yourself last week when you were rearranging the shelves.”

He set the bottle down upon the counter with a hand that trembled slightly. “Good God,” he said. “Good God. What are we to do?”

“We are to make up a fresh mixture,” I said, “immediately, and destroy this one. And then you are to take the fresh one to Mrs. Harbottle and say nothing about what has occurred.”

He looked at me with an expression of the most profound relief. “Yes,” he said. “Yes, of course. You’re quite right. I’ll—I’ll make it up at once.”

But even as he reached for the blue bottle, I saw his hand shake, and I knew that he would never manage the measurement with the necessary precision. “Give it to me,” I said, and took the bottle from him. I compounded the mixture myself, with a steady hand and a mind that seemed curiously detached from the whole affair, and when it was done I sealed it and labelled it and handed it to him without a word.

He took it and went out, and I stood alone in the dispensary, staring at the bottle of strychnine on the shelf. And I thought of Mrs. Harbottle, who would have taken that mixture four times a day, and who would have been dead by morning—dead, and no one the wiser, for who would have thought to question Mr. Prout’s dispensing? And I thought of myself, and of the three years I had spent in that shop, and of the fact that not one of those correct and carefully compounded mixtures had ever borne my name.

When Mr. Prout returned, he was almost himself again. “Well,” he said, rubbing his hands, “that’s that. No harm done. I’m much obliged to you, Miss Vane, for your—your vigilance. I shan’t forget it.”

“No,” I said. “I don’t suppose you will.”

He looked at me sharply, but said nothing more, and the matter was never mentioned between us again. But I have thought of it often since, and I have come to a conclusion. The next time—and there will be a next time, for men like Mr. Prout do not learn from their mistakes—I shall not be quite so accommodating. I have the address of a solicitor, and I have kept a careful record of every prescription I have compounded in this shop for the past three years, with dates and times and the names of the patients. It is not, I confess, a very Christian thing to do. But then, I have observed that Christians are seldom called upon to weigh out strychnine.
