It was said of Lady Agatha St. John that she had never committed a sin, except, perhaps, the sin of omission; for she had omitted to marry, omitted to repent, and omitted to grow old. She had, however, achieved one triumph in the art of memory: she kept a diary. It was not, mercifully, a record of her own emotions—she had none, or at least none that had not been carefully selected to match the seasons—but rather a meticulous catalogue of every compliment ever paid to her.

The volume was bound in a rather excessive shade of violet morocco, clasped with gold, and bore upon its first page the inscription: *The Mirror of My Perfection*. It was, she would tell her friends, her only literature. For a woman to write down her own virtues is tedious; to record the admiration of others is merely the highest form of social history. She carried it with her everywhere, as a priest carries his breviary, or a gambler his lucky coin.

The garden party at Richmond, given by the charming but entirely vulgar Mrs. Vandeleur, was an affair of considerable aesthetic merit. The lawns sloped down to the Thames in a manner that suggested a water-colour by a dying artist, all soft blues and fading greens. The azaleas were in a state of almost scandalous bloom, and the tea-cups gleamed like small pools of amber on the white cloth. Society was present in its entirety, or at least that part of society which was sufficiently bored to travel so far from Mayfair.

Lady Agatha moved among the guests like a white peacock through a parterre, her diary resting upon a small marquetry table near the orchestra. She had placed it there, trusting, as the sun trusts the morning. It was a fatal error. A sudden gust of wind, which had been waiting all afternoon for its cue, sent a parasol spinning across the table, and in the ensuing confusion the violet volume vanished.

It was Lord Gervase Endicott who discovered its absence, though not its whereabouts. He observed Lady Agatha’s distress, the sudden pallor beneath her rouge, the way she clutched at the empty table as though it were a lost lover. He observed, too, the strange ripple of alarm that passed through the party. Every face turned pale, every hand trembled about its teacup. The Duchess of Wexford dropped a cucumber sandwich with an expression of profound moral anguish. Sir Reginald Molyneux, who had told Lady Agatha only the previous Tuesday that her eyes were like twin stars navigating the difficult seas of his heart, went the colour of an overcooked beetroot.

The truth was, every guest was terrified. They had all paid their tribute to the shrine of Agatha, and they knew, with the terrible clarity of the guilty, that their compliments had been recorded. To have one’s finest aphorisms read aloud at a garden party in Richmond would be social ruin. A compliment, once written down, ceases to be a social grace and becomes a confession. It is the nature of admiration to be fleeting; to preserve it is to embalm it, and embalmed things are always a little horrifying.

Lord Gervase, who had no interest in compliments either given or received, watched the panic with the detached amusement of a man who has long since given up expecting anything of human nature except its absolute predictability. He was young, extraordinarily handsome in a languid sort of way, and possessed of a mind so thoroughly cultivated that it had grown quite barren. He had never paid Lady Agatha a compliment in his life. He found her beautiful, naturally—everyone found her beautiful—but to tell her so would have been to admit that she did not already know it, and Lord Gervase was far too well-bred to insult a woman’s intelligence.

It was he who found the diary. He had wandered to the far end of the garden, where the roses grew in a thicket of crimson and gold, and there, upon a stone bench, lay the violet volume. He picked it up, turned it over in his hands, and felt the weight of a thousand flatteries. He did not open it. He had no desire to read the follies of his acquaintances, partly because he already knew them, and partly because he suspected they would be very badly spelled.

He carried it back to the lawn, where Lady Agatha stood surrounded by her anxious courtiers. They parted before him like the Red Sea before Moses, each face a study in terror. Sir Reginald looked as though he were about to flee into the azaleas and never return. The Duchess of Wexford had gone quite grey.

"I believe," said Lord Gervase, with a slight bow, "that you have mislaid your library, Lady Agatha."

She seized the volume with a cry of relief, pressing it to her breast as though it were a recovered child. The guests exhaled collectively, a great sigh of relief that stirred the petals of the nearby roses and set the tea-cups rattling.

"You have saved me," she declared. "I should have been quite desolate without it. But where did you find it?"

"Upon a bench by the rose garden. It had been abandoned, I suppose, by whoever borrowed it. A careless thief, to leave such treasure unattended."

Lady Agatha smiled her slow, beautiful smile. "I must reward you," she said. "What would you have? Name it."

"I would have," said Lord Gervase, "the privilege of reading a single page."

A gasp went up from the assembly. The Duchess of Wexford closed her eyes. Sir Reginald Molyneux took a step backward, as though preparing to deny everything.

Lady Agatha regarded him with surprise. "You wish to read my diary?"

"Only a page. Any page you choose. I have never paid you a compliment, Lady Agatha. I have watched you for an entire season and said nothing. I should like to know what you have written about me in the absence of my admiration."

There was a silence. The orchestra, with exquisite timing, ceased playing. Lady Agatha opened the diary, her fingers turning the pages with the deliberation of a priest turning the leaves of a missal. She searched for a long while. The guests watched in agony.

At last she found the entry. She looked up, and there was something in her face that Lord Gervase had never seen before: a faint, almost imperceptible blush.

"You appear," she said, "under the heading of *Silences*."

"Silences?"

"Yes. I have written: *Lord Gervase Endicott said nothing to me today. He looked at me as one looks at a sunset—with wonder, but without the need to speak. It is the finest compliment I have ever received.*"

Lord Gervase bowed. The guests exchanged glances, uncertain whether to be relieved or humiliated. Sir Reginald, realising that his twin stars had been eclipsed by a silence, quietly helped himself to another sandwich and contemplated the absolute failure of language.

"You see," said Lord Gervase, as he walked with Lady Agatha toward the river, "the only true compliment is the one that is never spoken. Words diminish everything they touch. To name a thing beautiful is to reduce it to the level of the ordinary, for what can be named can be compared, and what can be compared has already lost half its magic."

"That is a paradox," said Lady Agatha.

"It is the truth," he replied. "And the truth, as you know, is simply a paradox that has not yet been forgiven."

They walked on in silence, while behind them the azaleas blazed their futile crimson, and the guests resumed their tea with the quiet desperation of those who have been reminded that the most dangerous thing a man can do is say nothing at all.
