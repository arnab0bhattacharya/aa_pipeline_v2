It was in the wet autumn of the year ’94 that I found myself once more in the company of Mr. Alistair Finch, a gentleman whose singular powers of observation had served me well upon more than one occasion. Our destination was the cathedral city of St. Editha, in the west of England. The morning was grey and chill, and a persistent drizzle slicked the cobbles of the close as we were ushered into the Deanery.

Our host was the Very Reverend Dr. Thorne, a man whose features were as finely wrought as the carved oak of his library. He was of a nervous disposition, his long, pale fingers constantly worrying at the folds of his clerical apron. With him was Mr. Silas Croft, the organist, a man of a more robust and ruddy complexion, though his eyes, when they met mine, held a distinct look of alarm.

“You will understand, gentlemen,” the Dean began, his voice a low, cultivated murmur, “that this matter is of the utmost delicacy. The reputation of the cathedral is at stake, and I have taken the liberty of engaging you, Mr. Finch, rather than the city constabulary, in the hope of a quiet resolution.”

Finch, who had been observing the rain-streaked window with an air of polite boredom, turned his sharp, grey eyes upon our host. “I shall be glad to hear the particulars, Doctor.”

It was the organist who spoke first, his words tumbling out in a great rush. “It is the organ, sir! For the past three nights, as I have been leaving the organ-loft after the final practice, there has been a sound. Not the wind, nor the settling of the great timbers. It is a cascade of notes, clear as a bell, from the Swell division. A little, tinkling arpeggio, no more than four or five notes, and then silence. It is most unnerving, I assure you.”

“And the choir?” Finch inquired, his tone mild.

“They call it the ‘Phantom of the Pipes’,” Mr. Croft admitted with a grimace. “Young men and boys are prone to such fancies. They say it is the shade of old Prebendary Gifford, who was said to have written a secret mass and hidden the score within the organ case itself.”

The Dean waved a dismissive hand. “A folktale, Mr. Finch, nothing more. But there is a second, and far more serious, matter. It concerns the great chalice and paten of St. Editha, which have been kept in the cathedral treasury for four hundred years.”

He led us to a small, iron-bound strongroom adjacent to his study. From a velvet-lined case he produced a magnificent silver-gilt chalice. It was heavy in the hand, the bowl engraved with scenes from the Gospel, the knop of the stem a marvel of intricate metalwork. “Exquisite, is it not?” the Dean said softly.

Finch took the chalice, holding it to the light of the single gas-jet. He turned it over and over, his long, sensitive fingers tracing the engraved figures. He then took the paten and subjected it to the same silent scrutiny. Finally, he set them down with a faint click.

“A very fine copy,” he stated.

The Dean and the organist both stared at him. “Copy?” cried Dr. Thorne. “But this *is* the chalice! It has been locked in this room, which has only two keys, one of which I keep upon my person at all times.”

“A copy, none the less,” Finch insisted, his voice losing its languor. “The detail is superb, but the chasing is a trifle shallow, lacking the confident depth of the original work. And here,” he said, pointing to the base of the paten, “is a tiny, almost imperceptible seam where the gilt has been applied over a silver base of a slightly different alloy. The original was solid silver-gilt throughout. Your treasure, Doctor, has been replaced.”

The colour drained from the Dean’s face. He sank into a chair, his hand at his throat. “Good heavens. It is impossible. Who…?”

“That,” said Finch, “is what we must ascertain. I should like to see the organ-loft, if you please.”

We made our way through the vast, echoing nave, our footsteps swallowed by the gloom. The cathedral was a forest of stone pillars, rising into a darkness where the great vaulted roof was lost to sight. The air was cold and smelled of damp stone and old incense. As we passed the high altar, Finch paused for a moment, his eyes scanning the shadows of the choir stalls before moving on.

The organ-loft was a cramped chamber high above the north transept, dominated by the great silent pipes of the instrument. The console, with its bewildering array of keys and stops, stood in the centre. Finch’s investigation was swift and methodical. He examined the bellows, the trackers, the very air itself. He ran a hand over the polished wood of the console and sniffed his fingers. He knelt and examined the floorboards.

“Nothing,” he murmured to himself. Then, his gaze fell upon a small, open music-stand beside the organist’s stool. On it lay a sheet of paper, not printed music, but a hand-drawn sketch of the cathedral’s interior. It was a fine, precise drawing, but one detail struck him as odd: a section of the choir screen, near the pulpit, was marked with a small, neat cross.

“Mr. Croft,” Finch said, his voice suddenly sharp. “Who has access to this loft?”

“Only myself, the Assistant Organist, and the head verger, Mr. Judd. And of course, the Dean.”

“And the key?”

“Is kept in the vestry. The verger has charge of it.”

“I see.” Finch turned to the Dean. “I should like a word with your head verger, if I may. And then, I believe, we shall have our ghost.”

A few minutes later, we were in the cathedral office, a small, cluttered room smelling of floor polish and beeswax. The head verger, Mr. Judd, was a man of formidable appearance, with a bristling grey moustache and the disciplined posture of an old soldier. He stood before us, his hands clasped behind his back, his expression one of stern forbearance.

Finch laid the sketch upon the desk. “This was found in the organ-loft, Mr. Judd. A competent piece of work. I notice it marks a particular section of the choir screen.”

Judd’s eyes flickered to the drawing, then back to Finch’s face. “I am not a draftsman, sir.”

“No,” Finch agreed pleasantly. “But you are a man of considerable ingenuity. The ‘ghost’ in the organ is a simple matter of a thin silk thread, weighted with a bead, looped around a few of the Swell keys and attached to the great West Door. When you leave the cathedral at night, you close the heavy door behind you. The vibration, or perhaps a slight tug as you secure it, is enough to pull the thread and sound the notes. A clever device, designed to distract and to frighten.”

The verger’s face remained impassive, but a muscle twitched in his jaw.

“The motive, however, is less whimsical,” Finch continued, his voice hardening. “The chalice and paten were replaced with gilt copies. The original is solid silver-gilt; the copy is gilded silver. An expert job, but not perfect. It would require a quiet place to work, a knowledge of metallurgy, and, most importantly, access to the treasury key. The Dean’s key never leaves his person. But the verger’s key, which opens the outer door to the treasury corridor, is left in the vestry each night.”

He paused, letting the silence stretch. “The final clue was the smell. In the organ-loft, there was a faint but distinct odour of nitric acid, the sort used in assaying and metalwork. A curious smell for a house of God.”

Mr. Judd stood rigid as a post. For a long moment, the only sound was the ticking of a grandfather clock in the corner. Then, his shoulders slumped, and the iron discipline seemed to melt from him.

“My son,” he said, his voice a low rasp. “My boy, in Bristol. He got into debt. A great deal of debt. They were going to ruin him. I have served this cathedral for forty years. It has been my life. But my boy is my blood.”

He looked at the Dean, his eyes filled with a weary shame. “I thought… I thought I could sell the plate and replace it before anyone noticed. The copies were made by a man I knew in the foundry. I only needed a few months. The ghost story… it was to keep folk away. To make them think the place was haunted, so no one would come snooping at night.”

The Dean rose to his feet, his face a mask of sorrow and disappointment. “Judd… after all these years.”

“There is nothing more to be said, Doctor,” Finch interjected quietly. “The original plate is, I presume, hidden within the hollow space behind the pulpit, as marked on your sketch. The matter is concluded. I believe we have a train to catch.”

And so it was. As we stood on the platform of St. Editha station, waiting for the London express, Finch lit a cigarette and gazed out at the rain, which had not ceased.

“A sad business, Finch,” I remarked.

“A necessary one,” he replied, exhaling a plume of smoke. “Theft is theft, Watson, whether it is motivated by greed or by a misguided sense of paternal duty. The facts were plain for any who cared to look for them without the interference of superstition. The ghost was the least of it. It was the human element, as always, that proved to be the most predictable and the most tragic.”
