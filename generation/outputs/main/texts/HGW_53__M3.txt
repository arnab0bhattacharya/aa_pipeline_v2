My father was a man of ironmongery, and he held the art of painting in a contempt that was only matched by his regard for tempered steel. He was a square, sturdy man with hands like cracked leather, and he viewed the world entirely through the prism of utility. That I, his only son, should wish to capture the shifting light of the Kentish skies upon a bit of stretched canvas seemed to him a mild form of lunacy, to be stamped out with a firm hand and an apprenticeship to the grindstone.

I was indentured to him at fourteen. By the time I was nineteen, I could forge a hinge, temper a chisel, and sell a ploughshare with the best of them in the market town of Wattleborough. But the curse of the artist was upon me. I saw colours in the dull black coals of the forge; I saw the drama of life in the sweat and grime of the farriers. And so, in the evenings, I would steal away to the attic above our shop, light a tallow candle, and paint. I ground my own pigments, using linseed oil from the chemist and minerals begged from the railway navvies. My canvases were coarse, but the brush was my true tongue.

It was a miserable, secret business. I was dreadfully cramped for space, and the attic smelled of damp straw and rusting nails. But I persevered, driven by that impatient folly which is the chief characteristic of youth. I painted the market square on a wet Tuesday, the gloom of our own cellar, and the portraits of the drunken old men who loitered about the Ram's Head public house. 

It was one of these portraits that proved my undoing. I had painted old Gudgeon, the sweeps' master, in a fit of maudlin despair, his face a map of soot and sorrow, lit by the flare of a gas-jet. It was a crude thing, perhaps, but it had a vigour in it. I had left it to dry on a settle in the taproom, having no mind to carry it home through the rain, when who should walk in but Mr. Pellew.

Mr. Pellew was a London art dealer. He had come down to Wattleborough to value some furniture at the Manor, and he was drinking a glass of bitter ale. His eye, wandering over the room, caught the canvas. I was sitting in a corner, nursing a mug of tea, and I saw him start, set down his glass, and go to it. He examined it closely, then turned and asked the landlord who had painted it. The landlord, with a jerk of his thumb, indicated me.

Mr. Pellew was a short, energetic man, with a bald head and a pair of very bright, impatient eyes. He came over to my table and sat down opposite me without ceremony.

"You did that?" he said, pointing a stubby finger at the picture.

"I did, sir," I said, feeling a sudden hot flush creeping up my neck.

"How old are you?"

"Nineteen, sir."

"And you're an ironmonger?"

"Apprenticed to my father, sir."

He made a sound between a grunt and a whistle. "It's a monstrous thing," he said, "that a lad with your grip on a brush should be selling door-knockers. It's a waste, I say. A positive waste. Look at the smudge on that old fellow's chin! It's living, that's what it is. You've got the trick of it."

He leaned forward, and his manner became intensely practical. "Look here," he said. "I've a gallery in Cork Street. I deal in pictures that mean something. I don't want your pretty watercolours of village greens. But I'll tell you what I'll do. If you can bring me a dozen canvases of this stamp within six months, I'll give you an exhibition. And if you sell, well, you'll make a living. If you don't, you can go back to your nails and tacks. What do you say?"

I said nothing for a moment. The thing was too vast, too sudden. I had a vision of myself in London, free from the smell of hot iron, living among men who understood. Then I thought of my father.

"I'll try, sir," I said. "I'll try."

He left his card on the table and went out. I sat there, staring at the card, and the taproom seemed to swim about me. I knew my father. He was not a bad man, but he was as rigid as the iron he worked. To tell him I wished to leave his trade for an uncertainty in London would be, in his eyes, a betrayal.

I went home and faced him in the back parlour. He was reading the local paper, his spectacles low on his nose. The gas-jet hissed above him.

"Father," I said, "I've had an offer."

He looked up, mildly interested. "An offer? For what? A contract for spades?"

"For myself," I said. "A London man has seen my painting. He wants me to send him a dozen pictures. He says I might make a living at it."

He took off his spectacles and polished them slowly on his waistcoat. It was a trick he had when he was displeased. "A living," he repeated. "A living by daubing canvas. And what sort of a living, I'd like to know? A precarious, shiftless, Bohemian living. I've heard of them artists. They starve in garrets, and they're proud of it."

"It might not be so," I urged. "This man is a dealer. He means business."

"And what of the business here?" he asked, his voice rising. "I've built up this trade. I've slaved to make it what it is. And you, my only son, you're to throw it over for a whim? For a brush?"

"It's not a whim, father. It's my life."

He stood up, and his face was heavy and set. "You're a fool," he said. "A sentimental fool. You can't eat paint, and you can't pay the rent with pictures. You're an ironmonger, and an ironmonger you'll stay. I'll have no more of this nonsense. If you go to London, you go with my curse, and not a penny of my money."

He turned and went out into the shop. I heard the ring of his hammer on the anvil, sharp and decisive. He had spoken, and he considered the matter closed.

But the thing was not closed. The fever was in my blood. For three days I went about my work in a dream. I weighed the certainty of ironmongery against the uncertainty of art. I thought of the shop, with its dull, orderly rows of pots and pans, its smell of oil and metal. I saw myself at forty, bald and square, weighing out nails. It was a respectable fate, but it was death to the soul.

On the third night, I packed my trunk. I took my canvases, my brushes, and the few shillings I had saved. I left my indentures on the table in the parlour, torn in half. It was a symbolic act, I suppose, a piece of youthful melodrama, but it satisfied me. I crept down the stairs, past the sleeping shop, and out into the street.

The morning train to London was a local, stopping at every station. I sat in the third-class carriage, with my trunk beside me, and watched the Kentish fields roll by. I felt a strange exhilaration, mingled with a cold dread. I was free, but I was alone. I had burned my boats.

I found a lodging in a back street near the Tottenham Court Road, a room that smelled of dust and stale cabbage. I set up my easel and began to work. It was a hard struggle. Mr. Pellew was exacting, and the London fog played havoc with my colours. I lived on bread and tea, and sometimes I walked the streets at night, hungry and despairing. I thought often of my father's warm parlour and the hissing gas-jet.

But I stuck to it. I had that impatience with convention, that disregard for the prudent way, which is the only thing that carries a man through such times. I painted the London poor, the costermongers, the slatternly women, the grimy children. I painted them as I saw them, with a hard, practical eye and a certain angry pity.

At the end of six months, I took my canvases to Cork Street. Mr. Pellew looked at them for a long time. He pulled at his lip and frowned.

"They're rough," he said. "Damned rough. But they've got guts." He gave me a cheque for ten pounds, a sum that seemed to me a fortune.

I went back to my lodging and sat on the edge of my bed, staring at the slip of paper. I had won. I had escaped the iron. But I thought of my father, and I wondered if he knew, or if he cared.

I went down to Wattleborough a week later. I walked into the shop. He was serving a customer, weighing out some screws. He looked up and saw me, and his face went grey. He finished his transaction in silence, and then came round the counter.

"Well?" he said.

I showed him the cheque. He looked at it, and then at me. His expression did not change. "So you've sold your soul for ten pounds," he said.

"No, father," I said. "I've sold my pictures."

He turned away. "You'd better go," he said. "I've no use for you here."

I went out into the street. The market square was bright with the afternoon sun. I had chosen my path, and I knew that I should never turn back. The iron was behind me; the colours were before me. And though the future was uncertain, I felt that I had at last come into my own.
