The light was going; it drained from the garden in a slow tide of grey, leaving the lawn a sullen, unfamiliar colour, and the elms at the far end of the park stood up like a wall against the last, bruised flush of the west. From the drawing-room at Halloway, the last of the day was a matter of softening edges, of the great mahogany table losing its gloss, of the silver on the sideboard ceasing to glint and becoming merely a dull, pale shape in the gloom. Clara sat by the empty fireplace, her hands in her lap, a figure of such perfect repose that she seemed a part of the room’s own stillness. She was beautiful in the way a well-kept garden is beautiful, each element in its proper place, the whole an assurance of order and grace.

He had loved that order once. He had loved the certainty of Clara, the clear, untroubled blue of her eyes, the way her voice never rose in agitation but flowed on, a placid river of common sense and gentle feeling. It was a love he had built for himself, brick by careful brick. And then, for a moment, the door had opened and the wind had come in, and the whole edifice, so solid a moment before, had trembled. The wind was named Miss Vance.

Clara looked up. “The light is quite gone,” she said, her voice a soft murmur that did not disturb the settling dust. “You will want your lamp, Edward.”

“Yes,” he said. But he did not move. He was watching the window, where the glass had become a black mirror, reflecting only the pale oval of his own face and the deeper shadow of Clara behind him. Where was she now? Not in a room like this, with its scent of beeswax and dried lavender. She would be in a cluttered little room in Bloomsbury, perhaps, with books piled on the floor and the gaslight hissing, a pen in her hand, her brow furrowed not with the placid contemplation of a future, but with the sharp, immediate struggle of a sentence. He could see it so clearly: the ink-smudge on her finger, the impatient way she would push a strand of dark hair from her eyes. And the laugh. It came back to him not as a sound but as a sensation, a sudden, sharp crack in the smooth surface of things.

“Do you remember,” he began, and then stopped. How could he speak of it? “That day in the National Gallery.”

Clara’s reflection smiled. “The Turner? Oh, yes. It was very bright. A little overwhelming, I thought.” She saw the sun on the water, the great wash of gold and white. She remembered Edward’s hand on her elbow, guiding her through the crowd.

He remembered Miss Vance, standing before it, her head cocked. “It’s not a picture of a storm,” she had said, not to them, but to the canvas itself. “It’s a picture of the *idea* of a storm. All noise and no substance. Very clever.” And then she had turned and looked at him, and her eyes, which were not blue but the colour of wet slate, had held a flicker of something—amusement, perhaps, at his earnest face, at the pretty, silent girl on his arm. She had laughed then, a short, private sound that seemed to mock the very grandeur of the painting, and in that instant, the gold of the Turner had seemed to him a cheap and obvious thing. He felt the heat rise in his cheeks now, here in the darkening room, as if she had laughed at him again.

“She is very… unconventional,” Clara said, as if she had followed the track of his thought and found it leading, as she knew it must, to the same place. “But I am sure she is very clever. She must be, to write for those magazines.”

It was a statement of fact, delivered without malice, and it was this very lack of malice that wounded him. To Clara, Miss Vance was a category, a type—the clever, working woman, to be admired from a safe distance, like a ship seen through a telescope. She could not know that the telescope had been turned round, and that he now saw the whole familiar landscape of his life—this room, this house, Clara herself—as something distant, small, and strangely unreal. He felt a desperate urge to make her see, to explain the tremor that the other woman’s presence induced in him. But how could he explain a tremor? It was like trying to describe the colour of a sound.

“Her laugh,” he said, the words feeling foolish and heavy in his mouth.

“Her laugh?” Clara’s tone was one of mild, polite inquiry.

“It is so…” He searched for the word. *Unkind*? No. *Free*? Perhaps. It was a laugh that held no hostages, that asked for nothing, that simply broke from her at the absurdity of a thing and was gone again, leaving the air cleaner than it was before. It was a laugh that made him feel, by contrast, like a man who had spent his whole life speaking in a whisper. “It is so very modern,” he finished lamely.

A silence fell between them, deeper than the one before. It was filled with all the things he could not say. It was filled with the memory of a walk by the Serpentine, when Miss Vance had pointed out the foolishness of the swans, and he had felt a wild, secret joy. It was filled with the sight of her hands, stained with ink, as she argued about Ruskin. It was a silence that had texture and weight, and he felt he would suffocate within it.

Then, from the hall, came the sound of the gong for dinner, a deep, resonant boom that shattered the quiet. Clara rose, smoothing her skirt. The moment was broken, sealed over. The real world, with its silver and its schedules, reasserted its claim.

“Come, Edward,” she said, holding out her hand. Her hand was cool and soft, and he took it. He let her lead him from the room, out of the encroaching dark and towards the brightly lit hall, where the footman stood waiting. He was going in to dinner with the woman he was to marry. It was all so easy, so simple. But as he walked, he felt the other presence, the ghost of a laugh in the silent air, the shadow of a pair of slate-grey eyes. It followed him, a question he could not answer, a fire he did not know how to put out. And he knew, with a certainty that was both a terror and a strange, thrilling liberation, that he did not want to.
