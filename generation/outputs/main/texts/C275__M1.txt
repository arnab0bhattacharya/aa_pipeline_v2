The rain had not stopped for three days, a steady, grey Sussex downpour that turned the gravel drive at Blackwood Manor into a soupy mire and blurred the ancient oaks into skeletal shapes. Inside the drawing room, the air was thick with the scent of beeswax, wet wool, and the faint, medicinal tang of old age. Sir Alistair Blackwood, a man whose skin was as thin and pale as parchment, sat slumped in a wingback chair before a fire that did little to chase the damp chill from his bones. His hands, gnarled with arthritis, trembled slightly as he held a silver locket.

Opposite him, perched on the edge of a chaise longue, sat Mr. Julian Croft. Croft was a man of perhaps thirty, with a lean, weathered face and eyes the colour of the storm-tossed sea outside. He was well-dressed in a tweed suit that spoke of London tailors, yet he carried himself with a restless energy, as if he were more accustomed to the deck of a ship than a drawing room.

“It is the eyes, you see,” Sir Alistair said, his voice a reedy whisper. He looked from the locket’s miniature portrait to Croft’s face. “They are exactly as Edmund’s were. The same intensity. The same… hunger.”

“I never knew my father, Sir Alistair,” Croft replied, his voice smooth and cultured. “But my mother always said I had his look. She said it was the one thing of his he left me, besides his name.”

From the doorway, a sharp, disapproving cough cut through the room’s sepulchral quiet. Mrs. Thorne, the housekeeper, stood framed in the oak archway. She was a tall, severe woman in black bombazine, her iron-grey hair pulled back in a merciless knot. For forty years, she had been the keeper of Blackwood Manor’s keys and its secrets.

“Sir Alistair,” she said, her tone brooking no argument. “Your tea will be cold.”

“In a moment, Thorne. In a moment. We are discussing my brother. My Edmund.” Sir Alistair waved a dismissive hand. “Mr. Croft has come all this way. From the Cape.”

Mrs. Thorne’s gaze swept over Croft, lingering for a moment on his polished shoes, which had left a faint trail of mud on the Persian rug. “Indeed. A long journey. And a remarkable tale.” She did not sound as though she found it remarkable in a positive sense.

“It is no tale, Mrs. Thorne,” Croft said, turning to face her. “It is the truth. My father, Edmund Blackwood, died of fever in Simon’s Town in 1871. My mother, a woman of good Dutch family, raised me. Only on her deathbed did she give me this letter and tell me to seek out my English family.”

He produced a creased, yellowed envelope and handed it to the baronet. Sir Alistair’s trembling fingers fumbled with the seal. Inside was a single sheet of paper, covered in a spidery script. “It is his hand,” the baronet breathed. “By God, it is Edmund’s hand.”

Mrs. Thorne stepped forward. “Sir Alistair, with respect, a letter can be forged. I was at this house when Sir Reginald received the news of Master Edmund’s death. The captain of the *Sea Serpent* himself brought the report. He spoke of no wife. No child.”

“The captain would not have known,” Croft countered smoothly. “It was a brief, private marriage. My mother was… discreet. She feared the family’s disapproval.”

“And yet, she sends you here to claim an inheritance,” Mrs. Thorne said, her voice like ice.

“Not an inheritance,” Croft corrected, looking directly at the baronet with a disarming sincerity. “A family. I have my own means from my mother’s estate. I seek only to know my father’s people. To see the house he grew up in. To sit in his chair.”

The appeal was masterful. Sir Alistair’s watery eyes filled with tears. “A Blackwood,” he whispered. “After all these years. To think, I might have a nephew. An heir.” He looked at Mrs. Thorne. “You see, Thorne? He is not grasping. He is family.”

Mrs. Thorne’s lips thinned. “Or he is a confidence man of exceptional skill, sir. He knows the family secrets, you say?”

“Indeed!” Sir Alistair was animated now, the frail old man momentarily vanished. “He knew about the silver cup Edmund won at Harrow. He knew about the scar on my left elbow from when Edmund pushed me from the apple tree. He knew… he knew the name of my mother’s pug. ‘Barnaby.’ How could he know that? It was never written down anywhere.”

“People talk, Sir Alistair,” Mrs. Thorne insisted. “Old servants, people in the village. The past has a way of leaking. This man is an imposter, come to prey on a lonely old man in his final years.”

The accusation hung in the air, ugly and sharp. Sir Alistair flinched. “That will be all, Thorne. Please, see to the fire in the library. Mr. Croft and I have much to discuss.”

Defeated for the moment, the housekeeper gave a stiff nod and retreated, her shadow seeming to linger in the doorway long after she was gone.

The following day, a Mr. Finch arrived from London. He was a small, unassuming man from a discreet firm of investigators, recommended by Sir Alistair’s solicitor. He was given a small study to work in and spent the first day doing little more than observing. He watched Croft charm the baronet with stories of the Cape—of Table Mountain wreathed in cloud, of vineyards sprawling across sun-drenched valleys, of the wild, untamed beauty of a land Sir Alistair only knew from his brother’s letters. Croft’s knowledge was vast, his details precise. He spoke of the specific scent of the fynbos after a rain, the taste of a particular brandy they had shared in a tavern in Cape Town. It was a performance of impeccable authenticity.

On the second day, Finch requested an interview with Mrs. Thorne. They sat in her immaculate, sparse sitting room.

“He is very good,” Finch admitted.

“He is a liar,” she replied, pouring tea with a steady hand. “Edmund was a wild boy, a romantic. He wrote letters full of grand plans, but he was not a man of substance. He would not have married a woman of good family. He would have found some tavern girl or a farmer’s daughter. This man… he speaks of a world Edmund could never have inhabited.”

“Yet he knows the small things,” Finch mused. “The name of a dog. The details of a childhood scar.”

“Gossip,” she insisted. “Or perhaps… research. A clever man could learn a great deal from a village like this, if he knew what questions to ask.” She paused, her gaze distant. “There is one thing. One thing only I and Sir Alistair know. A thing from their last summer together, before Edmund sailed. A secret they shared, a foolish boyhood promise.”

Finch leaned forward. “And what is that, Mrs. Thorne?”

She shook her head. “It is not my secret to tell. But it is a test. Ask him. Ask Mr. Croft about the starling’s nest.”

Finch found Sir Alistair and Croft in the library, poring over old maps of the African coast. The baronet was glowing, a new light in his eyes. Finch waited for a lull in the conversation.

“Mr. Croft,” he began, his tone casual. “Sir Alistair was just telling me of his brother’s adventurous spirit. It reminds me of a story I once heard, about a promise made to a starling’s nest. Do you know anything of that?”

A flicker of confusion crossed Croft’s face. It was there and gone in an instant, replaced by a thoughtful smile. “A starling’s nest? No, I’m afraid that is not a story my mother ever told me. Perhaps it was after my father left for the Cape.”

Finch’s eyes met Mrs. Thorne’s across the room. She gave a slow, almost imperceptible nod. It was the answer she had expected. The wrong answer.

Later that evening, as Finch prepared to leave Blackwood Manor, he found Mrs. Thorne in the hall. The rain had finally stopped, and a sliver of moon was breaking through the clouds.

“It was not about a nest,” she said quietly, not looking at him. “It was about a boat. A little boat they built, and named the *Stargazer*. They launched it in the pond, and swore an oath that whoever returned to the house first after a long journey must leave a smooth, grey stone from the pond’s edge on the mantelpiece in this hall. A sign that they had come home.”

Finch looked at the mantelpiece. It was bare, save for a clock and a silver tray for calling cards. “And Edmund? Did he leave a stone before he sailed for the Cape?”

“He did,” Mrs. Thorne said. “It is there still. Tucked behind the clock. Sir Alistair does not see it anymore, but I do. Every day.”

The next morning, Sir Alistair’s solicitor was summoned. The will was to be changed. Mr. Julian Croft, son of Edmund, was to be named as the sole heir to the Blackwood baronetcy and its estates. As the papers were being prepared, Croft stood by the grand fireplace, a picture of quiet humility. His hand rested on the mantelpiece, his fingers idly tracing the dust. They brushed against something small and hard, tucked away behind the ormolu clock. Curious, he drew it out. It was a smooth, grey, water-worn stone. He looked at it for a long moment, a flicker of genuine puzzlement in his sea-grey eyes, before placing it back exactly where he had found it.
