Mr. Cecil Arden, curate of the parish of Deane St. Lawrence in Hampshire, was a young man of moderate fortune, unexceptionable character, and a very strong regard for the eldest daughter of his neighbour, Sir William Maitland. Miss Maitland was in her one-and-twentieth year, possessed of a lively mind, and a countenance that, if it did not declare absolute beauty, declared at least a great deal of good humour and intelligence. Mr. Arden had admired her, with the silent and unvarying constancy of his nature, for above a twelvemonth; but as his admiration had never yet proceeded beyond the bounds of a respectful bow or a casual remark on the weather, he had very little reason to suppose his sentiments were either suspected or returned.

It was therefore with no small degree of surprise that he received a note from Sir William requesting the favour of his company at the Manor that morning, to consult on a matter of some delicacy. Mr. Arden went directly, his mind revolving all the possible subjects of such a communication, from the repair of the parish poorhouse to a proposed new carriage. He was conducted into the breakfast parlour, where he found Sir William alone, stirring his coffee with an air of considerable self-importance.

"Mr. Arden," said Sir William, without any preamble, "I have a proposition to lay before you. My daughter Sophia has a mind that requires direction. Her mother, poor woman, was never a great reader, and I confess myself unequal to the task of guiding her studies. She has read all the novels in the house, which I consider no very good thing, and now she is forever pestering me for histories and poetry, of which I know nothing. You are a scholar, sir. I wish you to advise her on her reading."

Mr. Arden expressed his willingness to be of service, though he felt the request to be rather unusual. He had never before heard of a young lady's education being committed to the nearest clergyman, and he was not entirely certain whether the arrangement was a mark of Sir William's confidence or of his indolence. He rather suspected the latter.

"I shall send her to you at the parsonage twice a week," Sir William continued. "Tuesdays and Fridays. You may begin with whatever you think proper. I leave the whole entirely to your discretion. Only mind that she does not read any of that French nonsense. I will not have a daughter of mine reading Voltaire."

Mr. Arden gave his assurance that Miss Maitland's reading should be of the most unexceptionable kind, and took his leave, not without some inward trepidation. The prospect of sitting alone with Sophia Maitland, in his own parlour, for an hour at a time, was not one which he could contemplate with entire composure. He was a man of strong feeling and weak utterance, and he foresaw that the task of maintaining an appearance of calm instruction, while his heart was beating with a violence that must have been audible across the room, would require all the powers of self-command he possessed.

The first lesson took place on the following Tuesday. Miss Maitland arrived precisely at eleven o'clock, attended by the Maitland footman, who carried a parcel of books. She was dressed in a morning gown of pale muslin, and carried herself with an ease of manner which Mr. Arden, who was at that moment engaged in arranging his own neckcloth before the looking-glass with a degree of attention that he would afterwards have been ashamed to own, found exceedingly formidable.

"I am come to be improved, Mr. Arden," said she, as she took the chair he offered her. "Papa has given me over to your care entirely, and I am to be made into a blue-stocking, or a philosopher, or something of that kind. I am not quite certain which, but I daresay you know what is best for me."

Mr. Arden replied that his endeavour should be to direct her reading to such subjects as were both improving and agreeable, and that he hoped she would find their meetings not wholly tedious. He then opened the first volume of a work on moral philosophy which he had selected with great care, and began to read aloud. His voice, which was in general a good one, faltered somewhat on the opening sentence, which happened to contain the word "affection," but he recovered himself and proceeded with a steadiness that did him credit.

Miss Maitland listened with an attention so profound and so uninterrupted that Mr. Arden was quite at a loss to determine whether she was genuinely interested or merely exercising a degree of patience beyond what the subject deserved. He glanced at her once or twice, and observed that she was looking at the book with a fixedness of gaze that might have denoted deep reflection or a total absence of thought. He could not discover which, and the uncertainty made him nervous.

When he had finished the chapter, he paused and looked at her. She looked at him. There was a silence of some duration.

"Miss Maitland," said he at length, "perhaps you would wish to offer some observation on the passage we have just read?"

"I am sure it is very instructive," she replied. "The author's sentiments on the subject of self-denial appear to be everything that could be wished."

Mr. Arden could not but feel that this was rather a tame response from a young woman whom he had always considered to be possessed of considerable quickness. He ventured to put a question to her on the author's distinction between the passions and the affections, and she answered it correctly but without any of that play of fancy or independence of thought which he had hoped to elicit. The lesson concluded with a brief discussion of the relative merits of history and biography, in which Miss Maitland expressed a decided preference for the latter, on the grounds that she liked to know what people had for dinner, which history, in her experience, very seldom mentioned.

Mr. Arden smiled, but checked himself immediately, as though he had been guilty of some impropriety. He bowed, and said that biography was certainly a branch of literature which admitted of much amusement as well as instruction. Miss Maitland then rose to depart, and the footman was summoned. At the door she turned and said, with a look of something like amusement, "I shall look forward to Friday, Mr. Arden. I find moral philosophy surprisingly entertaining."

She was gone before he could determine whether she was laughing at him or at the subject. He sat down in his chair and took up the volume again, but he read nothing. He was wholly occupied in wondering whether Miss Maitland's gravity had been real or assumed, and in reproaching himself for the warmth of feeling which her presence had occasioned. He resolved that on Friday he would conduct himself with a composure that should admit of no question. He would be the clergyman, the instructor, nothing more. He would not smile at her remarks on biography, nor would he allow his voice to falter on the word "affection."

On Friday he succeeded tolerably well. He read a passage from a history of the Roman Republic, and asked her several questions on the character of the Gracchi, which she answered with a readiness and intelligence that surprised and pleased him. She appeared to have read with attention, and to have formed opinions of her own, which she expressed with a modesty that did not conceal their originality. Mr. Arden forgot his resolution in the interest of the discussion, and found himself talking with an animation that he had not known he possessed. He even went so far as to dispute one of her positions, which she defended with a spirit and good humour that left him nothing to desire but the continuation of the argument.

When she rose to leave, he was conscious of a degree of regret which he knew to be foolish, and which he therefore took care to conceal. He handed her to the door with a civility that was, perhaps, more formal than the occasion required, and she departed with a grave courtesy that matched it exactly.

They walked down the path to the gate in silence, the footman following at a discreet distance. At the gate she paused and said, "I must thank you, Mr. Arden. I have enjoyed our discussion excessively. I had not thought the Gracchi could be so interesting."

"Nor I," said he, with a warmth which he could not repress.

She looked at him for a moment, and he thought he saw something in her expression that was not altogether composure. But she said only, "Tuesday, then," and passed through the gate and into the carriage.

Mr. Arden returned to the parsonage and sat down to write his sermon. He found that he had written the word "Sophia" three times before he observed it, and was obliged to begin the page again.
