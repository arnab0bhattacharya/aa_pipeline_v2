It was in the summer of 1904, I believe, that young Anstruther began to build his flying machine in the old tithe barn at the bottom of Mr. Pemberton’s meadow, and from the first there was a feeling in the village that no good would come of it. I was at the time a boarder at the vicarage, recuperating from an attack of influenza that had left me with a great impatience for quiet and a corresponding appetite for any small excitement, and I confess that the hammering and sawing that proceeded from the barn of an afternoon drew me down the lane with a curiosity that was not entirely disinterested.

Anstruther was a thin, brown-faced young man of perhaps six-and-twenty, with the hands of a workman and the eye of a visionary, and he had come down from London with a small legacy and a great quantity of wire, canvas, and bamboo, and had taken the barn on a quarterly tenancy. He was, I soon discovered, an engineer by training and an enthusiast by nature, and he had conceived the notion—which he explained to me at some length on my second or third visit—that the problem of flight was not, as the newspapers would have it, a matter of great engines and vast expense, but of a proper understanding of the principles of the kite and the gliding bird. He had built, in fact, a machine that was part box-kite and part aeroplane, with a light petroleum motor of his own devising, and he proposed to launch it from the crest of the Downs above the village, where the west wind blew steadily and the ground fell away in a long, smooth slope that he declared was providentially designed for the purpose.

The vicar, Mr. Cardew, was not pleased. He was a man of the old school, who saw in every innovation a symptom of moral decay, and in Anstruther’s machine he detected at once a blasphemous attempt to usurp the prerogative of the angels. He preached against it on the following Sunday with a good deal of vehemence, taking as his text the story of Babel, and comparing the young engineer, not altogether unjustly, to those who sought to build a tower into heaven. The squire, Sir John Harrowell, who was a practical man and a justice of the peace, took a different view. He came down to the barn one afternoon in his dog-cart, inspected the machine with a great deal of condescension, and offered Anstruther two hundred pounds for it on the spot, with the evident intention of presenting it to the War Office or of exhibiting it at the Crystal Palace, I could not quite determine which. Anstruther declined with a politeness that was a shade too elaborate, and Sir John drove away in a huff, remarking to me as he went that the young man would break his neck and serve him right.

But the person who interested me most in the whole affair was Miss Eleanor Blunt, the daughter of the retired colonel who had taken the White House for the summer. She was a tall, straight girl, with a clear eye and a manner that suggested she had never in her life been afraid of anything, and she had come down to the barn one morning while I was there, ostensibly to inquire about a stray dog, but really, I am convinced, to look at the machine. She asked Anstruther a great many questions, which he answered with a kind of stammering eagerness, and she examined the canvas wings and the little motor with a practical interest that I found quite admirable. When he had finished his explanation she said simply: “I should like to fly in it,” and I saw Anstruther’s face flush with a pleasure that was almost painful to witness.

The first trial was fixed for a Saturday afternoon in early September, and by that time the whole village was in a state of the most intense excitement. The machine had been carted up to the Downs in sections and assembled on the crest, and a crowd of perhaps forty or fifty people had gathered to watch, including the vicar, who had come to see fair play, as he said, but who I think had come to see the young man punished for his presumption. Sir John was there too, with a pair of field-glasses and an expression of great scepticism. Anstruther made a short speech, which was mostly inaudible, and then climbed into the machine and started the motor, which made a noise like a gigantic sewing-machine and emitted a puff of blue smoke. The machine ran forward along the turf, wobbled, lifted perhaps ten feet into the air, and came down again with a crash that sent a wheel spinning into the crowd. Anstruther was thrown out but not hurt, and he got up and laughed in a rather shaky way, and said that the angle of the wings was wrong and that he would try again next week.

It was at this point that Miss Blunt stepped forward. She had been standing at the edge of the crowd with her hands in her pockets, watching the proceedings with the calmest interest, and now she walked up to Anstruther and said, in a voice that carried to the furthest ranks of the spectators: “You promised me the first ride, Mr. Anstruther. I am ready when you are.” There was a murmur from the crowd, and the vicar stepped forward with his hands raised in protest, and Sir John said something about the impropriety of the thing that was fortunately lost in the general confusion. But Miss Blunt was not to be moved. She had, she said, every confidence in Mr. Anstruther’s machine, and she was not going to be prevented from flying by a lot of old women, and if the vicar objected he might object to the bishop. Anstruther, who had gone very pale, tried to dissuade her, but she silenced him with a look, and after a moment’s hesitation he set to work to repair the damaged wing with a feverish energy that I have seldom seen equalled.

The second trial took place on the following Wednesday, with a smaller crowd but a better machine, and this time the thing actually flew. It rose to a height of perhaps a hundred feet and travelled a distance of some three hundred yards before it came to earth in a somewhat undignified manner in a field of turnips. Miss Blunt, who had been strapped into the seat beside Anstruther, stepped out with a flushed face and a smile that I can only describe as radiant, and declared that it was the most wonderful experience of her life and that she would go up again directly if the machine were not broken. Anstruther, who had cut his hand on a wire, was too happy to speak, and the crowd, which had been prepared to see a tragedy, gave a cheer that echoed across the Downs and brought the vicar hurrying from the village in a state of great agitation.

The sequel was not long in coming. Sir John Harrowell, who had watched the flight from a distance with an expression of profound astonishment, came down to the barn the next morning with a cheque-book and an offer of five hundred pounds, and this time Anstruther, after a long conversation with Miss Blunt, who had taken to spending her mornings at the barn, accepted. The machine was crated up and sent to London, where I understand it was exhibited at the Crystal Palace and subsequently purchased by a firm of motor manufacturers, and Anstruther went with it as consulting engineer. He married Miss Blunt in the following spring, and the vicar, who had preached a very moving sermon on the sin of presumption, was induced to perform the ceremony, and to admit, in the course of a rather halting address, that the ways of Providence were not always to be understood by the mere parish priest.
