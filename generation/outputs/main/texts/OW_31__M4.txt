The thing had been accomplished with a discretion that was almost insolent. There was no scene, no letter discovered, no tearful confession wrested from a servant—only a line in the bank-book, a sum of four hundred pounds entered in her husband’s own hand, and, beneath it, the name of Mrs. Verena Quill, a name which Lady Agatha Lorne knew as she knew the shape of her own looking-glass, though she had never in her life spoken it aloud. It was a name that hung in the air of certain drawing-rooms like the scent of a too-generous perfume, and it belonged to a woman who had buried two husbands and outlived the mourning of both, and who was said to receive her guests at midnight and to have a wit that was the terror of the merely respectable.

“You have paid her debts,” said Lady Agatha.

“I have,” said her husband.

They were standing in the morning-room, a place of pale green silk and white narcissi, and Lord Alfred Lorne was arranging his cravat before a glass with the air of a man who has all the time in the world and intends to spend it.

“Four hundred pounds.”

“You have the figure admirably.”

“And you will not tell me why.”

“I will not.”

“You do not deny that it is—singular.”

“My dear Agatha,” said he, turning at last, “I deny nothing. Denial is the resource of the man who has something to hide. I have merely something I do not choose to show. There is a difference, though I observe that the world has never learned it.”

She looked at him for a long moment. He was a handsome man, with the beautiful indolence of one who has never been obliged to be anything but charming, and it was precisely this indolence which now enraged her. A guilty man, she thought, would have been eager; an innocent one, eager also. Only a man who was neither, or both, could stand there selecting the precise shade of a button with the calm of a connoisseur.

“Then I shall find out for myself,” she said.

“I should be sorry to deprive you of the pleasure,” he replied, and bowed, and went out, and she heard him humming something from the new piece at the Lyceum as he descended the stairs.

The invitation to Mrs. Quill’s ball had lain upon the mantelpiece for a week, a thing of gilt and cream, and Lady Agatha had put it there as one puts a wasp under a glass—to look at, and to know that it was alive. Mrs. Quill’s balls were famous. They were famous for the audacity of their hour, for the excellence of their music, and for the curious mixture of the very great and the very nearly ruined who assembled there. To go was to be talked about; to refuse was to be talked about differently. Lady Agatha had refused. Now she sent a note by hand to say that she had changed her mind, and she spent the intervening days in a state of exalted misery, choosing her gown as a general chooses his ground.

She went in silver, with a rope of pearls about her throat and no colour anywhere but the high flush of her own purpose. The house in Belgrave Square was ablaze; the windows of the second floor were open, and the night air carried out into the street the sound of a waltz played as though it were a secret. She gave her name and ascended, and at the head of the stairs stood her hostess.

Mrs. Quill was not beautiful. She was something better worth looking at. She was tall and very pale, with hair of a red so dark it was nearly brown, and she wore black velvet and a single emerald, and she had the eyes of a woman who has listened to a great many confessions and believed none of them. She greeted Lady Agatha with perfect composure and a smile that contained no surprise whatever.

“Lady Agatha. How good of you. I had begun to think you disapproved of me.”

“I did,” said Agatha.

“And now?”

“Now I have come to see for myself.”

“That is the only honest reason for going anywhere,” said Mrs. Quill. “Let me give you some champagne; it is the one thing in this house that is above reproach.”

The rooms were full, and the talk was of the sort that is best described as brilliant and worst described as kind. Agatha moved through it like a woman wading a river, and all the while she watched her hostess, who moved among her guests with the ease of a woman who owns the water and has never once been obliged to swim. Twice Agatha saw her laugh at something a bishop said; once she saw her lay her hand for an instant on the sleeve of a man who was known to be ruined and to be charming about it. She did not see her look at Lord Alfred, who had come, of course; he had come, and he was standing by the window with a glass in his hand and an expression of the most exquisite boredom, and he had not spoken to his wife since she entered.

At the height of the evening Agatha found herself alone for a moment in a small room off the greater one, a room of dark red walls and a single lamp, and there Mrs. Quill came to her.

“You have been watching me all night,” said Mrs. Quill, “with the devotion of a detective and the manners of a martyr. It is very fatiguing for us both. Shall we be comfortable instead?”

“I came,” said Agatha, and her voice was not quite steady, “to ask you a question.”

“Ah. The question. I wondered whether you would have the courage. Most wives send their husbands. Or their solicitors. The solicitors are the worst.”

“My husband has paid your debts.”

“He has.”

“Four hundred pounds.”

“You are exact. I like that in a woman; it is so rare, and it saves so much time.”

“Do you deny,” said Agatha, and now she was very white, “that there is an understanding between you?”

Mrs. Quill looked at her for a long moment, and something in her face—amusement, or pity, or a little of both—was worse than any cruelty.

“My dear Lady Agatha,” she said, “I have never in my life denied anything, and I do not propose to begin now. It is such a middle-class virtue. But I will tell you a story, if you will sit down. You are standing like a woman in a play, and the carpet is better than the play.”

Agatha did not sit. But she did not go.

“There was a girl,” said Mrs. Quill, “who was foolish, and who married a man who was worse than foolish, and who found herself, at three-and-twenty, with a name that no one would speak and a brother who would not be seen with her. She had no money. She had, however, a certain talent for being talked about, which is a capital in this city, and she made of it a competence, and then a fortune, and then a position, which is the most amusing of all, because it is the one thing the virtuous cannot forgive. Now, this woman had a brother. He was a good man—I assure you they exist—and he loved her, and he could not bear that she should want. But he was proud, and he would not be seen to give. And so he found a friend, a man of no particular principle and of a great deal of money, and this friend paid the debts quietly, and took no receipt, and asked no thanks, and never once in ten years mentioned the matter, because—and here is the part you will not believe—because he thought it a small thing to do, and he was rather ashamed of how small it was.”

The room was very still. The waltz had ended somewhere beyond the red walls.

“Your husband,” said Mrs. Quill, “is my brother’s oldest friend. He is also the only man of my acquaintance who has never once looked at me as though I were a subject for his own good opinion. I do not expect you to believe me. Belief is a gift, and you have not the habit of it. But I will tell you this, and then I shall go and see about the supper, which is the only real work of the evening. You have a husband who is silent because he thinks a promise is a thing to be kept even from his wife, and you have come here tonight to confront a rival who does not exist, in a gown that must have cost you a week of sleep. And I—” she smiled, and it was almost kind, “—I have a ball to give, and a bishop to whom I have promised the pleasure of the next waltz, and I find that I am sorry for you, which is the last thing I intended.”

She went out. Agatha stood alone for a moment in the red room, and then she went out too, and down the stairs, and into the street, where the night air was cool and the linkmen were calling cabs. Her husband was waiting by the door, and he had her cloak over his arm.

“You are leaving early,” he said.

“I am leaving,” she said, “with my husband.”

He put the cloak about her shoulders, and for a moment his hands rested there.

“I might have told you,” he said.

“You might have.”

“I thought,” said Lord Alfred, “that you would prefer a mystery to a brother-in-law. I see now that I was wrong. It is the only error of judgment I have made this year, and I shall endeavour to survive it.”

She laughed—a small, unwilling laugh, the first she had given in a fortnight—and took his arm, and they went out together into the lamplit square, where the last of Mrs. Quill’s guests were arriving, and the music had begun again, and the night, which had promised so much and delivered so little, was still young enough to be forgiven.
