The sun fell in long, pale blades across the grass, cutting the shadows of the elms into geometric slices, and Eleanor, sitting upright on the green iron chair, felt the morning strike her through her thin spring jacket. It was a little too early for the nurses; the gravel paths were nearly empty, swept by a wind that tossed the new leaves into a green, shivering tumult. Beside her, Leo had already escaped, a flash of grey serge and bare knees, galloping toward the pond with that strange, loping stride that belonged neither to man nor beast, but strictly to the very young. He was gone; he was absorbed into the morning; and she was left with the rustle of the paper bag, the sticky warmth of a half-eaten bun, and the sudden, startling quiet of her own mind.

How odd it was, she thought, crossing her ankles beneath the chair—how profoundly, bewilderingly odd—that one could be reduced to this; to sitting on a chair in Kensington Gardens, watching a small boy throw breadcrumbs to ducks, and yet feel simultaneously that one was everywhere, scattered, diffuse, spread thin over the surface of years. For she was not here, not entirely. The trees, with their wet, dark trunks, pulled her downward, backward, into the damp earth of another spring, another garden, where her mother had stood in a grey shawl, clipping deadheads from the rose bushes with a decisive, sharp snap that echoed now in Eleanor’s mind as clearly as it had echoed then.

Her mother, who had died in the autumn of 1918, just as the world was reassembling itself into something stiff and formal and unrecognizable—her mother, who had never once sat on a public chair, who had moved through life with a kind of glacial certainty, leaving grooves in the air behind her. Eleanor could see her now: the sharp line of her jaw, the way she held the secateurs, the faint, permanent stain of earth beneath her thumbnail. She had been a woman of precise opinions, of rigid, beautiful silences, and Eleanor had spent her youth chafing against the smooth, hard surface of that certainty, only to discover, now, at thirty, that she had inherited the very thing she had despised: the feeling that life was something happening elsewhere, just beyond the hedge, just out of reach, while one stood pruning.

Leo was shouting at a pigeon. His voice, high and clear, cut through the ambient hum of the city—the distant grind of omnibuses on the Bayswater Road, the muffled clop of a horse, the long, wavering note of a distant siren—and Eleanor felt the sound enter her body and settle there, in her chest, alongside the other things: the book she had not written, the paragraphs abandoned in notebooks that lay in a drawer in the flat, growing yellow at the edges, their ink fading into the pale blue of old letters. She had meant to write about light, she remembered; about the way light fell through a window in the late afternoon and made everything bearable, even beautiful, even grief. She had meant to write about the interior life of women, the vast, uncharted continents that lay behind their eyes, but the words had dried up, or rather, had been swallowed, absorbed into the daily round of feeding, washing, soothing, the endless soft repetition of domestic hours that wore one smooth as a river stone.

And now here was Leo, running back to her, his face flushed, his cap askew, trailing a stick like a sceptre.

"Mummy, there’s a man with a dog that looks like a rug," he announced, breathless, leaning against her knee. His hair smelled of soap and outdoors and the faintly metallic tang of childhood itself, that particular scent that clung to small boys like an aura.

"A rug," she repeated, smoothing his cap. "What sort of rug?"

"A brown one. With a hole in it," he said, grinning, and was gone again before she could answer, pulled back into the current of his own fierce, darting attention.

She watched him go, and the love she felt was so sudden, so physical, that it winded her; it was like being struck in the ribs by something invisible and winged. And yet, even as she watched him, she was aware of how inadequate it was—how inadequate all feeling was—against the brute, luminous fact of existence. The world was so full; so teeming; and she was so small, sitting in her green chair, a woman of thirty with a child and a husband and a flat in Maida Vale and a drawer full of unwritten books, and somewhere, beneath all that, a girl who had once stood in a garden in Surrey and believed that life would be a clear, bright, singular thing, a single note sustained without wavering.

The girl was gone. Or rather, the girl was here, buried inside the woman like a fossil in rock, preserved but compressed, her outlines blurred. Eleanor thought of Margaret, her dearest friend from those days, who had gone to Paris to paint and had married a Frenchman with a black beard and a collection of African masks, and who now wrote letters on thin blue paper that smelled of Gauloises and turpentine, full of phrases that Eleanor read with a mixture of longing and something that was not quite envy but was its close and uncomfortable neighbour. *The light here is extraordinary*, Margaret wrote, *it breaks everything into facets, like a diamond. You would understand. You always understood.*

But did she? Eleanor wondered. Did she understand now, sitting here in the thin English sun, with the grass stain on her son’s knee and the crumb of bun still stuck to her thumb? She had understood once, or had believed she did, and the belief had been enough; it had carried her along like a current, through school, through the early years of the war, through the terrible, bewildering months when the news came in waves of black-edged envelopes and the world seemed to be unmaking itself with a horrible, methodical efficiency. She had survived; they had all survived, in one fashion or another; but the girl who had understood light was not the woman who now sat in Kensington Gardens, watching her son poke a stick into the mud at the edge of the pond.

The husband, Robert, was at his office. He was always at his office now, bent over ledgers and contracts, his pen scratching in the quiet, ordered room that smelled of ink and wood polish and the faintly mineral scent of his ambition. He had come back from the war thinner, quieter, with a new way of looking at her, sideways, as if he were trying to determine whether she was the same woman he had left, or some version of her, a copy with slight but perceptible differences. They did not talk about the war; they talked about rent and groceries and Leo’s new shoes, and sometimes, late at night, lying beside each other in the dark, they reached for one another with a desperation that was also tenderness, a kind of blind, fumbling need that was not quite love but was the closest thing to it that remained.

A cloud passed over the sun, and the garden shifted, darkened, the greens deepening to something richer, more sombre. The pond was a black mirror now, reflecting the elms and the sky and the small, darting figures of children. Leo was crouching at the edge, his stick trailing in the water, and Eleanor watched him with the intensity she sometimes felt for ordinary things—the way the light struck the rim of a cup, the way a shadow fell across a page—as if by looking hard enough she could pierce the veil of the everyday and see through to something beneath, something luminous and essential, the thing itself, the irreducible core.

But the veil held. The world remained opaque, charming, heartbreakingly particular. A nurse pushed a pram along the path; an old man sat reading a newspaper, turning the pages with a slow, deliberate crackle; a girl in a yellow coat walked past, her heels clicking on the gravel, her face composed and distant, and Eleanor thought, with a pang, *She is the girl; she is the one who believes.* And for a moment, she was that girl, and the woman in the green chair was a fiction, a story she might have written once, in another life, in the drawer of unwritten books.

Then Leo ran back, mud on his fingers, and the morning resumed its shape, its ordinary, unrepeatable shape, and she took his hand and felt the small, warm weight of him, the living pulse, and the sun came out again, and the leaves flashed silver, and the city hummed its vast, indifferent, beautiful song.
