Among the numerous advantages which the village of Chaddleworth in Hampshire possessed over the neighbouring parishes, was not the least the fact that it contained but one family of consequence, and therefore could be entirely free from those rivalries and jealousies which so frequently disturb the tranquility of a small neighbourhood. The Mississons of Chaddleworth Park had long held this distinction, and the present representative of the family, Mrs. Mississon, was a woman who understood the value of consequence and had no intention of diminishing it by any imprudence of conduct.

Her eldest daughter, Clara, had been for two years engaged to Mr. Edward Wyncote, a man of unexceptionable character, whose estate at nearby Bramley St. John was moderate but well-managed, and whose income, though not large, was such as to promise comfort, if not splendour. The match had been arranged with the full approbation of Mrs. Mississon, who had considered Mr. Wyncote’s steady habits and respectable connections a sufficient compensation for his lack of vivacity. Clara, who had been but nineteen at the time of the engagement, had received the addresses of Mr. Wyncote with a composure which her mother interpreted as prudence, and which her sister Margaret, a girl of sixteen with more observation than experience, privately suspected was indifference.

Mr. Wyncote’s circumstances, however, were such as to require him to wait for the living of Bramley, which was then in the gift of his uncle, a Baronet of some sixty years, whose health was spoken of in terms which encouraged expectation without producing satisfaction. The engagement, therefore, had been long, and during its progress, Clara had found occasion to observe her lover with a degree of attention which had not, perhaps, been bestowed upon him in the first warmth of their acquaintance.

It was on a fine morning in April that Clara found herself walking alone in the shrubbery at Chaddleworth Park, her mind occupied by reflections which had grown too troublesome to be dismissed. Mr. Wyncote had called the preceding evening, as he called every Tuesday and Friday, and had sat for the usual hour in the drawing-room, conversing with Mrs. Mississon upon the price of wheat, the health of his uncle, and the prospect of rain before the week was out. Clara, who had sat opposite him, had found her eye wandering to the window, and had caught herself wishing that he would say something — she did not know what — which might distinguish this visit from the twenty visits which had gone before it.

She was not so unreasonable as to require perpetual entertainment. She knew that Mr. Wyncote was good, and she had been taught, by a mother who valued goodness above all other qualities, that a woman who could not be happy with a good man did not deserve to be happy at all. Yet she could not but feel, as she walked between the damp borders, that there was a deficiency somewhere — a deficiency not in Mr. Wyncote’s conduct, which was everything it ought to be, but in her own heart, which refused to respond with the warmth that his devotion merited.

She had attempted, more than once, to convince herself that this was the natural cooling of affection which followed upon long engagement, and that when they were married, and settled in the parsonage at Bramley, she would find contentment in the performance of her duties. But contentment, she feared, was not happiness; and she was not certain that she wished to exchange the remainder of her youth for a composure which resembled nothing so much as the composure she already possessed.

She was interrupted in these reflections by the appearance of Margaret, who came towards her with a letter in her hand and an expression of some importance.

“Mama wishes you to come in,” said Margaret. “Mr. Wyncote has written, and Mama thinks you ought to see the letter directly.”

Clara took the letter and opened it. It was, as she had expected, a communication of the most regular and unexceptionable kind. Mr. Wyncote wrote to inform her that his uncle had been taken ill on the previous Sunday, that the physician had been called, and that there was now every reason to suppose the living of Bramley would be vacant within the year. He expressed himself, throughout, with a steadiness which did credit to his feelings, and concluded by requesting permission to wait upon Mrs. Mississon the following morning, in order that the arrangements for the marriage might be finally settled.

Clara read the letter twice, and then stood in silence, looking at the spring flowers which had begun to appear along the border, as though she expected them to offer some counsel which her own mind could not supply.

“Well?” said Margaret, who had been watching her with a curiosity which she did not think it necessary to conceal. “Is it not what you wished?”

“I do not know what I wished,” said Clara; and then, recollecting herself, added, with a composure which she was far from feeling, “It is, of course, exactly what was to be desired. Mr. Wyncote writes with his usual — correctness.”

Margaret said nothing, but regarded her sister with an expression which, had Mrs. Mississon observed it, would have been thought impertinent.

Clara returned to the house, and delivered the letter to her mother, who received it with a satisfaction which was, perhaps, the more complete for being unaccompanied by any display of tenderness. Mrs. Mississon was a woman who had long since determined that feeling was a luxury which could not be indulged without some sacrifice of judgment, and she had trained herself to regard all events with an equanimity which her acquaintance mistook for coldness.

“This is precisely what I expected,” said she, laying the letter upon the table with an air of finality. “Sir Henry’s health has been declining these two years, and I have always said that Mr. Wyncote’s patience would be rewarded. You must begin to think about your preparations, Clara. There is the linen to be considered, and the furniture for the parsonage, which I am told is in a very indifferent state. I shall write to your aunt Harding today, for she has excellent taste in such matters, and will be of more use to you than I can be, having been so long out of the way of weddings.”

Clara listened in silence. She observed that her mother spoke of the event as though it were already accomplished, and that there was no room, in Mrs. Mississon’s account of the matter, for any hesitation or doubt. She was to marry Mr. Wyncote; the living was to be vacated; the parsonage was to be furnished; and her aunt Harding was to be consulted upon the linen. There was a completeness in this arrangement which left her nothing to do but submit, and she was beginning to feel that submission was the only course which lay open to her, when Margaret, who had been sitting in the window with an appearance of great indifference, suddenly spoke.

“I suppose,” said she, “that Clara is very happy.”

Mrs. Mississon looked up, as though surprised that anyone should think it necessary to state so obvious a fact. “I have no doubt of it,” said she. “Mr. Wyncote is a man of excellent principles, and Clara has always shown a discretion which does her credit. I have never had any anxiety about her.”

Margaret said no more, but she looked at Clara with an expression which her sister found it difficult to endure. It was the look of a person who sees what others will not see, and who is restrained from speaking only by a consciousness that the truth, once spoken, cannot be recalled.

Clara passed a sleepless night. She lay awake, reviewing the whole of her acquaintance with Mr. Wyncote, from the first evening he had dined at Chaddleworth Park to the visit of the preceding Tuesday, and she found that the catalogue of his virtues, when she had finished it, left her precisely where she had begun. He was good, he was steady, he was devoted; and she felt towards him a gratitude so entirely free from passion that she could not, in honesty, call it love.

She thought of what her mother would say, if she were to declare that she could not marry Mr. Wyncote. She thought of the astonishment of the neighbourhood, the concern of her friends, and the pain which she must inevitably inflict upon a man who had done nothing to deserve it. She thought of the parsonage at Bramley, furnished under the direction of her aunt Harding, and of the long years which would follow, years of correctness and composure, in which she would perform her duties with a propriety which would satisfy everyone except herself.

And yet, when she rose in the morning, and came down to breakfast, and found her mother already seated at the table with an air of business which seemed to imply that the marriage was as good as settled, she could not bring herself to say the words which would put an end to it. She sat in silence, turning her cup in her hand, and it was not until Mrs. Mississon began to speak of the wedding clothes that she found voice enough to interrupt.

“Mama,” said she, “I believe I must speak to you. I am not certain that I can marry Mr. Wyncote.”

The silence which followed was of a kind which Clara felt rather than heard. Mrs. Mississon set down her cup, looked at her daughter with an expression in which surprise and displeasure were mingled, and said, after a pause which seemed to last an age:

“You are not certain?”

“No, Mama.”

Mrs. Mississon drew herself up in her chair. “And pray, Clara, when did this uncertainty begin? Has Mr. Wyncote done anything to occasion it?”

“No. He has done nothing.”

“Then I am at a loss to understand you. You have been engaged these two years, the living is within your reach, and everything is settled. What can you mean by such a speech?”

Clara looked at her mother, and then at Margaret, who had raised her eyes from her plate with an expression of hope which she could not entirely conceal. She felt the weight of the moment, and knew that whatever she said now would determine the course of her life. She took a breath, and spoke.

“I mean, Mama, that Mr. Wyncote is everything that is good, and that I have the highest regard for him — but I do not believe that regard is sufficient. I have tried, for these two years, to feel what I ought to feel, and I find that I cannot. I should be deceiving him, and myself, if I married him with my heart as it is.”

Mrs. Mississon was silent for a full minute. When she spoke, her voice was controlled, but there was a tightness about her mouth which Clara had learned to recognise as the sign of anger held firmly in check.

“You are very young,” said she, “to know so much about your own heart. I was married at twenty, and I did not think myself qualified to pronounce upon the nature of love. I trusted to my mother’s judgment, and I have never had cause to repent it.”

Clara said nothing. She knew that her mother’s marriage had been, by all accounts, a fortunate one, and that Mrs. Mississon had never regretted her compliance. But she knew, also, that what had been right for her mother might not be right for her, and that the willingness to be guided was not the same as the capacity to be happy.

The discussion continued, with much that was said on both sides, and nothing that was conceded. Mrs. Mississon spoke of duty, of prudence, and of the opinions of the world; Clara spoke of honesty, of feeling, and of the injustice of marrying a man she could not love.
