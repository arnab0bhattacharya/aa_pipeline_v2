The funnel coughed, and the gull, startled, swerved on a chord of air; and Eleanor, leaning against the damp rail, felt the whole fabric of the ship shudder and right itself beneath her, as if it were a creature breasting a dark wave. There was the smell of soot now, and of salt; and the spray, flung up, dotted her grey sleeve with beads that swelled and broke. The coast of England, a mere bruise on the horizon at dawn, had thickened, grown solid, and was now breaking into green and brown; into chalk cliffs that resembled the flanks of vast, sunken animals; into fields that held, she knew, the wet, heavy dew of an English autumn. They were creeping up on it; the ship seemed to be drawing the land towards her, or perhaps she towards it; and the water, no longer the immense, blinding sapphire of the Arabian Sea, was grey, shot with violet, heaving with a sullen, familiar chill.

She had been away a year. A year, which had folded itself up, like a brightly coloured shawl, and was now packed in the trunk in her cabin, smelling of sandalwood and damp. She had seen the sun strike the marble of the tomb at Surat until it was blinding, a pool of liquid fire; had watched the crows cawing in the tamarind trees; had felt the white dust settle on her ankles, and the slow, languid pulse of the heat. There was the old woman by the river, her sari the colour of marigolds, dipping her brass vessel; the smoke of the evening fires rising blue against the violet dusk; the smell of spices in the narrow streets; the blind beggar holding out a hand like a withered claw. All this she had in her; it beat in her veins; it was lodged in her mind like a bright, unmovable stone. And now the cliffs were white; the grass was green; and a tramp steamer, passing, hooted abruptly, shattering the stillness with a hoarse, familiar cry.

What would she say? She thought of them gathered in the drawing-room at Datchet; her mother pouring tea, the silver kettle sending up a little plume of steam; the firelight catching the edges of the chairs; the curtains drawn against the damp. Clara would be there, perhaps, holding her cup awkwardly, her hair frizzed; and Eleanor could see their faces, their pale, expectant faces, turning to her. "Tell us," they would say. "What was it like? The heat, and the jungles, and the temples?" And she would have to speak.

But how could one speak? How could one take the sound of the temple bell at evening, when the sky was the colour of a bruised plum, and the priests were chanting, and the smell of incense hung heavy in the air, and put it into words that would not sound merely absurd, merely picturesque, in a drawing-room at Datchet? She could see them, their eyes wide, their mouths a little open, trying to imagine a heat that melted the candles and turned the leather of one's boots to mush; a sun that was a tyrant, not a friend. They would say, "How too extraordinary!" and "How very exotic!" and the words would fall between them like little pebbles, dry and hard, and the thing itself, the thing she had felt, would be lost, would be dissolved, would be swept away in the tide of their polite incomprehension.

She looked at the water, heaving, greenish, with patches of purple; the foam, creamy and flecked, sliding past the hull. The ship rolled slightly; a bell rang below; a steward passed, carrying a tray of teacups, which rattled. Life on the ship, which had been so intense, so immediate, was already fading. The man with the red beard who had told her about the oranges he was importing; the woman in the deck-chair, reading a novel, her face hidden by a veil; the captain, standing on the bridge, his hands behind his back, his eyes fixed on the sea—all of them were receding, were becoming shadows, figures in a play that was over. And India, too, was receding; it was slipping away, like a ship in the night, its lights growing dimmer and dimmer, until it would be nothing but a memory, a dream, a bright stain on the fabric of her mind.

She would tell them nothing. She would say that it was hot, that the food was curious, that the journey was long. She would give them facts, dates, names; she would describe the house they had lived in, the garden, the servants. But the rest—the essence, the soul of it—she would keep. She would fold it up and put it away, like a letter in a drawer, to be taken out and read in silence, when she was alone. For it was hers, this year; it belonged to her, not to them. They could not have it. They would not understand.

The wind freshened, and she drew her coat closer. The cliffs were nearer now; she could see the grass on the top, and a few sheep, white dots against the green. A lighthouse appeared, standing on a rock, its white face staring blankly at the sea. The world she was returning to was solid, unyielding; it was made of stone and earth and water; it was the world she had been born in, and it was closing around her like a shell. She felt a sudden pang of loss, as if she were leaving something behind, something precious, something she could not name. But the ship went on, cutting through the waves, and the land grew nearer, and the gulls, crying, wheeled above the stern.

There was a smell of earth now, mixed with the salt; a smell of wet fields and burning leaves; a smell that was England, that was home. The funnel coughed again; the deckhands were coiling ropes; the noise and bustle of arrival began. Eleanor stood motionless, her hands on the rail, her eyes fixed on the approaching shore. She was coming back; she was Eleanor Vance again, the girl they knew, the girl they expected. But inside, she was someone else; she was someone they did not know, and would never know. And that, she thought, was as it should be. The secret was hers; the silence was hers. She would keep it.
